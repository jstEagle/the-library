## 2. Admission Charged by Legend

The tide had gone out further than Ada had ever seen it go, and it had done so without asking anyone's permission.

They stood at the top of the cliff path with a shell knife in one pocket and a thermos of cold coffee in the other, watching the sea withdraw from the land like an animal pulling in its breath. The mudflats below gleamed the color of old pewter, stitched with channels that caught the early light and held it. Gulls walked the exposed sand on their stiff red legs, picking at whatever the water had surrendered, and the whole valley smelled of iodine and cold stone and something older underneath—something like the inside of a sealed room.

Ada had lived at the Rivergate for eleven months now, long enough to know the tide's moods the way you learn a difficult person's moods: by attention, by failure, by keeping notes. They kept a small waterproof book in the boathouse, columns ruled in pencil. *Height. Hour. Wind. What came in. What went out.* Most days the entries were dull as communion wafers. But this morning's entry had made them stand up from the table so fast their chair went over.

*Lowest I have ever seen. Lowest anyone has ever seen?*

The old men in the town would say it was the moon, or the wind off the northeast, or God stretching His legs. But Ada had spent six years digging up other people's garbage for universities that paid by the bucket, and they had learned one thing above all others: when the ground gives you something it has never given before, you do not waste the hour being sensible. Sensible people's middens stay unexcavated. Sensible people's mothers stay missing.

Ada took the cliff path down.

* * *

The going was treacherous. The path was really only a suggestion—a sheep track worn into the chalk, narrowing to a ledge where the rock had sheared away sometime in living memory. Below, the newly bared seabed spread out in terraces, each one dripping. Pools sat in the hollows like coins left in a dead man's pockets, and in them Ada saw anemones with their arms furled, crabs the size of thumbnails, a school of stranded sprats turning as one body. Everything was holding its breath. Everything knew the water would come back.

They were forty minutes working along the base of the cliffs, boots sucking in the grey silt, before they saw it.

At first it looked like nothing—a shadow in the cliff face, a fold of darker dark. But shadows don't have edges cut by tools. Ada stopped, wiped their glasses on their shirt hem, and looked again. There, where the chalk met a band of harder greenstone, a mouth opened in the rock: an arch perhaps two meters high, its rim smoothed and beveled, its threshold worn into a shallow dish by what could only be feet. Many feet. Centuries of feet.

A cave. Below the high-tide line. Below even the storm line, which meant that whoever had carved that entrance—and it *had* been carved; Ada could see the chisel scars under a skin of barnacle, fine as stitching—had known the sea's schedule better than the sea did.

Their heart was doing something unprofessional. Ada stood there a moment, letting the cold air fill them, and made themselves think like a digger instead of like a person who had not been invited anywhere in a year. Tide tables. The lowest ebb had passed; they had maybe three hours, four if the weather held off the southwest. The floor of the cave rose inward—that much was visible from outside—which meant dry ground beyond the reach of all but the highest springs. It was, they thought with a strange lurch, almost exactly the kind of place they'd have chosen themselves.

"Admission charged by legend," they said aloud, because talking to oneself was another habit of the invisible, and stepped inside.

* * *

The dark took a moment to give up its shapes. Ada clicked on their headlamp and stood still while the beam wandered: walls of wet greenstone veined with white quartz, a ceiling low enough to touch, and a floor of packed dark earth rising in a gentle ramp toward the interior. The air was cold and mineral, tasting of iron and salt and, faintly, of smoke—old smoke, the ghost of ten thousand fires, soaked so deep into the stone that no rain would ever wash it out.

People had lived in here. Not sheltered—*lived*. You could smell the difference.

Ten paces in, the lamp found the first marker.

It was set into the wall at shoulder height: a rectangle of stone about the size of a hymnal, its face carved with lines. Ada brushed away grit with a fingertip and felt the grooves—deliberate, regular, cut with something finer than a nail. A grid of nine squares, and in each square a symbol: a wave, a fish, a bell, a key, a ladder, a door, a sun split by a line, a boat, and in the center square, larger than the rest, a spiral coiling inward like water going down a drain.

Toll-markers. That was the word that arrived in Ada's head unbidden, wearing the certainty of something remembered rather than deduced. They had never seen these symbols before, and yet some deep stratum of them—the part raised on a mother's stories, on bedtime tales of river-folk who paid their way in songs and secrets—recognized the grammar. Pay here. Declare yourself. The center symbol was the price: give the water your name, and it will carry you.

There were more markers deeper in. Seven in all, spaced at intervals down the ramp, each one different, each one worn smooth at its lower edge where generations of hands must have touched it—touched it the way you touch a church door, or a coin you're about to spend. Ada photographed each one with the cracked phone they used for fieldwork, hands steadier than they had any right to be. The flash lit the cave like lightning trapped indoors.

Between the fourth and fifth markers, the lamp caught the lantern.

It hung from an iron bracket driven into a seam in the wall, and it was rusted nearly to lace—but the rust had preserved rather than destroyed, sealing the metal under a skin of orange scale. Ada reached up and turned it carefully. Stamped into the base, clear as the day a die struck it: a crest. Two interlocking rings around a stylized gate, and beneath the gate, letters too corroded to read entire but shaped unmistakably like *RIVERGATE TRUST*.

Ada stood very still.

The Rivergate Trust was everywhere in the valley once you knew to look for it—the way rot is everywhere in a wall once you find the first soft spot. Their crest was on the ferry timbers and the weir gates and the brass plate outside the bank. It was on the charity envelopes the town pushed through your door every quarter, asking for maintenance of the flood defenses, as if the floods were weather and not policy. Everyone paid the Trust. Nobody asked the Trust for receipts.

And here was its mark, rusting in a cave that officially did not exist, hanging beside toll-markers carved centuries before any trust was founded. Which meant either the Trust had found this place and claimed it—or the Trust had *always* known it was here, and had let the sea keep the secret for them.

Ada's fingers were cold. They noticed this the way you notice weather: distantly, as fact.

They were reaching for the lantern again, meaning to work it free of the bracket, when the lamp swung across the floor and found the boot prints.

* * *

They were pressed into the damp earth of the ramp, sharp-edged, barely softened by seepage. Tread pattern: deep lugs, commercial grade, the kind sold in the outdoor shop in town for eighty pounds a pair. Size large. And—not two days old. Possibly not two hours. The edges hadn't slumped. No snail had crossed them. In a cave where the air moved like syrup, two days was ancient history; two hours was now.

Someone else had stood exactly where Ada was standing, recently, and had hung that lantern back on its bracket—or taken one down and left this one—or simply paused, the way Ada was pausing, to read the markers.

Ada crouched and put their own hand flat beside the print. Bigger than theirs. Heavier heel-strike. Someone in no hurry, or someone confident the cave was theirs.

The professional instinct said: photograph, measure, leave. The instinct underneath that—the one with teeth—said: *they came looking for something, and my mother came looking for something, and those might be the same thing.*

Ada followed the prints.

They went up the ramp past the sixth and seventh markers, past a low shelf where someone had stacked flat stones into a rough table, and ended at the back wall of the cave—or where the back wall should have been. Instead the prints stopped before a slab of greenstone set flush into the rock, taller than a door, its surface covered edge to edge with carving so dense it looked at first like weathering. Ada raised the lamp close.

Not weathering. Writing—or the ancestor of writing—the same nine symbols from the toll-markers, repeated in rows, spiraling inward toward the center of the slab where the drain-spiral motif dominated everything, cut deep, rubbed with something dark so the lines still showed black after however many hundreds of years. Around the spiral's outermost coil, smaller marks: tallies. Hundreds of them. A count of something, kept over centuries, by people who had come down to this door at low tide and paid their admission and gone home again.

Ada pressed their palm against the slab. Cold came through it like a current, colder than the cave, colder than the sea outside, a cold with direction—as if something on the far side were exhaling through the stone. And beneath their hand, faint but unmistakable, they felt it: a vibration. Slow. Rhythmic. Like a pulse traveling up from somewhere far below, or far inland, following the river's buried spine.

The source. The word arrived without permission. *Ingrid's word.* Their mother had spent the last decade of her career chasing it—had given lectures about "the hydrological mythos of the upper valley" that made her colleagues smile into their beards, had filled notebooks with sketches of weirs and sluices and drowned steeple silhouettes, had missed birthdays and funerals and finally, three years ago, had missed Ada entirely, walking out of a hotel bar in Gallowford mid-argument and leaving a half-finished pint sweating on the table between them.

*The river has a source, Ada. Everything else is plumbing.*

Ada's throat hurt. They took their hand off the stone.

That was when they heard the sea come back.

* * *

It didn't sound like waves. It sounded like a door opening at the end of a long corridor—a long, low exhalation rolling up the cave from the entrance, followed by a hiss, and then the particular gurgle of water finding old channels and filling them greedily. Ada spun, lamp beam swinging, and saw light at the cave mouth change quality: the pewter flats outside vanishing, the terraces drowning terrace by terrace, the sea returning over ground it had lent out that morning and now intended to collect.

They checked their watch and swore. The ebb had turned early—or they had lost time in the dark, which amounted to the same thing. Three hours had become forty minutes.

Ada ran.

The ramp was slicker going down, the packed earth greasy with condensation, and they took the slope in sliding strides, one hand trailing the wall, passing the seventh marker, the sixth, the lantern swinging on its bracket and knocking out a hollow iron note like a small mad bell. Behind them the sound of the incoming tide grew a second voice, a deeper register, water shouldering into the lower chambers with a weight that made the rock hum.

The fifth marker. The fourth. And then the floor changed underfoot without warning—from earth to bare greenstone polished by ages of feet, and Ada's leading boot skated sideways, and the world tilted.

They went down hard on one knee, lamp cracking against stone, and slid. The floor here dipped—they hadn't mapped it coming in, hadn't seen the shallow basin sunk into the rock just past the fourth marker, a basin now brimming. Ada's momentum carried them to its rim and over, and for one full second they were falling into a tidal pool the temperature of held breath, black water closing over their boots, their thighs, their waist—

—and their hand hit the rim of the basin and held.

Chalk grit under fingernails. Quartz seam biting the palm. Ada hung there, half in and half out, the pool tugging at them with patient enormous strength, not violent, just certain, the way the Trust was certain, the way debts were certain. Their lamp, cracked, strobed. In its stuttering light they could see the bottom of the basin: a carved spiral, identical to the one on the great slab, glowing pale through the water like an eye opening.

"Admission charged," Ada gasped, to nobody, to the pool, to the spiral, and hauled.

Their knee came over the rim. Then their hip. They crawled clear on hands and knees, soaked to the ribs, shivering so hard their teeth chattered out a rhythm, and behind them the basin finished filling and went still and deep and patient, waiting for the next traveler who misjudged the sea's hours.

Ada got to their feet at the third marker and looked back once. Water was already sheeting across the threshold of the cave mouth, silver in the last of the daylight, climbing the worn dish of the entrance step. In twenty minutes the door would be shut until the next anomalous ebb—and there was no telling when the sea would make that offer again.

But they had seen the boot prints. Large. Recent. Confident. Someone else had read the great slab, touched the same cold stone, heard—if they'd listened—the same pulse from below. And the lantern wore the Trust's crest, and the Trust did not send its crest anywhere it wasn't prepared to defend.

Ada waded out through thigh-deep water into the bruised evening, climbed to the cliff path, and stood there dripping while the cave drowned behind them and the first stars came out over the Rivergate.

They had come for shells. They were leaving with a mystery that had fresh footprints in it.

Back at the boathouse, drying off by a stove that gave more smoke than heat, Ada opened their waterproof notebook and wrote beneath the morning's tide entry:

*Cave below east cliffs. Toll-markers ×7. Slab with spiral + tallies — writing? Lantern: TRUST CREST. Boot prints — recent, size 12ish. Someone got there first.*

Then, after a long moment, the pencil hovering:

*Mum's word for it was "the source." Start there.*

Outside, the sea knocked softly against the hull, all night, like something asking to be let in.
