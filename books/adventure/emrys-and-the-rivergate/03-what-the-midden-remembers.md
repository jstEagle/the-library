## 3. What the Midden Remembers

The boathouse had one working lamp and Ada had carried it to the far end of the room, away from the window that faced the water, because light on the Rivergate at night was a thing you gave away for free to anyone with eyes and a boat. The bulb was forty watts at best, the color of weak tea, and it turned the overturned rowboat in the corner into the ribcage of some beached animal. Ada worked beneath it the way they'd learned to work in the field: elbows in, breath slow, hands doing the thinking.

They had laid everything out on a sheet of canvas scavenged from the boathouse loft—a tarp stiff with old varnish, smelling of pine resin and decades of damp—and they had divided it into quadrants with a grease pencil, the way Dr. Okafor had taught them in their second year, before Ada stopped being anyone's student. *Context is everything,* Okafor used to say, tapping the table with two fingers. *An object out of its soil is just a thing. In its soil, it's a sentence.* Ada had pulled all of this out of its soil in a hurry, with the tide coming back like a debt collector, so every object here was a sentence with its words scrambled. They would have to unscramble it by lamplight, alone, with salt drying white in the creases of their knuckles.

Quadrant one: the toll-markers. Six of them, carved from a dense grey stone that wasn't local—Ada had checked their mental catalogue of the valley's geology twice, then a third time, because the answer kept refusing to change. Basalt, maybe, or something basalt-adjacent, hauled from a long way off, which meant someone had wanted these markers badly enough to pay for the hauling. Each one bore the same incised symbol: a vertical line crossed by three horizontals, like a ladder lying on its side, or a river crossing a road three times. Below the symbol, worn nearly smooth but still legible if you tilted them into the light, ran a band of lettering in no alphabet Ada recognized—not runic, not ogham, not anything from the survey courses. The strokes were confident. Whoever cut them had cut many.

Toll-markers. That was what the shape of them said—the flat top where something might once have sat, the deliberate placement marks on their undersides, the way each base was dressed to fit a socket rather than the ground. Someone had charged admission to this river. Not the Trust with its laminated permits and its cheerful signage at the car park—someone older, someone who had made travelers stop and acknowledge, in whatever tongue that lettering spoke, that the water did not belong to them.

Ada turned one of the markers under the lamp and found, near the base, a second inscription: shallower, later, cut by a less patient hand. Latin. *QUI NON SOLVIT,* it said. *Who does not pay.* And beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic: a date. 1891.

Two years before the Trust's charter. Two years before the maps changed. Someone had stood in that cave with a chisel and amended an older law with a newer one, the way you'd paste a tax stamp over a shrine.

"You were a border," Ada told the marker quietly. Their voice came out rough; they hadn't spoken aloud since morning, except to gull. "Then somebody made you a receipt."

* * *

Quadrant two: the lantern. It was rusted past function, its glass fogged to the opacity of sea ice, but the stamp on its base plate came up clean when Ada worked a pencil rubbing of it onto the back of a tide-table page. A circle. Within the circle, a weir—three arches over flowing water—and around the rim, in small serif capitals: THE RIVERGATE TRUST, EST. 1893.

Ada sat back on their heels and looked at it for a long time.

The Trust's crest was everywhere in the valley, so ubiquitous it had gone invisible, the way a name said too often stops meaning anything. It was on the drainage grates in town. On the water company's bills. On the little brass plaques on the benches along the promenade, each one thanking some long-dead benefactor whose fortune, if you followed the genealogies Ada had followed out of boredom one winter, traced back to the same five or six families who signed the founding charter. The crest was wallpaper. It was the texture of ordinary life.

It should not have been stamped on a lantern inside a cave that appeared only at anomalous low tides, in a chamber whose oldest stones predated the Trust by centuries.

Unless the Trust had known about the cave. Unless the Trust had been coming to the cave.

Ada picked up the lantern again, more carefully now, and this time they looked at it not as an artifact but as evidence—which was a different discipline entirely, one they'd learned not from any professor but from watching their mother work a site. Ingrid Eriksen had a way of holding things, turning them, asking questions of them that weren't archaeological questions. *Who touched this last? What did they want? What were they afraid of?* She'd been accused once, gently, by a colleague, of anthropomorphizing her finds. She'd laughed and said that objects were people who had stopped talking, and it was rude to assume silence meant nothing to say.

The lantern's hinge was freshly rusted—rusted in a bright orange bloom over older brown corrosion, which meant it had been flexed recently, opened and closed within the last few months. The wick was charred. Not old char, soft and crumbling, but hard char, fused, recent. Someone had burned this lantern. Inside the cave. Within the last season.

And quadrant three held the reason Ada's hands had started, very slightly, to shake.

Boot prints. Ada had seen them in the silt floor of the inner chamber as the tide groaned its warning through the rock, and they'd had time for exactly three photographs before the water came up their shins and drove them out. But three photographs were enough. Size eleven, maybe eleven and a half. Vibram-style lugs, deep tread, a pattern sold in every outdoor shop in the county. The prints were crisp-edged, unfaded, undisturbed by dripwater—which meant they were days old at most. Weeks, if the chamber had stayed dry.

Someone else had stood where Ada stood. Had held a light up to those markers. Had walked out again, and told someone, or planned to tell someone, or was told to go in the first place.

Ada looked at the lantern's fresh hinge-rust and let the two facts lie down next to each other on the canvas like animals deciding whether to fight.

* * *

They made tea on the single burner and drank it standing at the window, looking at the black seam of the river, and tried not to think about the fourth quadrant. But the fourth quadrant was the whole reason the lamp was lit, and eventually Ada went back to it the way you go back to a door you know opens onto a cold room.

The child's shoe first, because it was easier.

Leather, black, a buckle shoe of a style that had gone out before the war—the First War, Ada corrected themselves, running a thumb along the cracked vamp. Small. Absurdly, terribly small, the size of something you could cup in two hands. The sole was worn unevenly, more on the outer edge of the left foot, which meant a child who walked with their weight rolled outward, a child who maybe ran along walls, who scuffed, who dragged a foot the way children do when they're being called home and don't want to come.

It had been in the mud a long time. The leather had cured to the consistency of bark, and there were barnacle scars along the toe cap—barnacles need years, decades, to grow and die and leave their calcareous ghosts. This shoe had been underwater since before Ada's grandparents were born. Since roughly 1893, if you wanted to be precise about it, and Ada was trained to want to be precise about it.

A child's shoe, in a tidal cave below cliffs that overlooked a stretch of river where the maps showed nothing at all. No village marker. No ford. No ferry crossing. Just water, and the blank paper space that cartographers leave when nobody asks them questions.

But the ledger page asked questions.

Ada unfolded it for the third time—it was tissue-thin, water-stained amber, and they handled it with the flat of a palette knife because their fingers were still salty and salt eats paper the way regret eats sleep. It was a fragment, torn vertically, half a page of a bound volume, the stitching holes visible along the broken edge. The handwriting was copperplate, clerk-trained, beautiful in the impersonal way that institutional handwriting is always beautiful.

Most of the entries were sums. Payments, dated quarterly, running down the left margin in a column: names Ada didn't recognize attached to figures that would have been a laborer's yearly wage in the 1890s, paid out twice, three times, four times to the same names. Beside several of the entries, in the same hand, a notation in abbreviated form: *acc. reloc.* — accepted relocation. One entry had a different notation, pressed harder, the nib nearly tearing the paper: *declined.* And beside *declined*, in a different ink, added later, a word Ada had to hold to the lamp and read three times before believing it:

*Emptied.*

The fragment named places. That was the part that made the cold walk up Ada's arms and settle in their shoulders. Because the payments weren't abstract—they were tied to parishes, to hamlets, to specific clusters of human lives, and Ada knew the modern map of this valley the way they knew their own teeth, and none of these places were on it. *Merrow St. Cuthbert's. Halvard's Reach. Tarn Beg. Wetherlow. Ashen Ferry.* Five names. Five communities, each with quarterly disbursements logged against them, each notation marching with terrible bureaucratic patience toward the same final entry, repeated five times down the torn edge of the page, in the clerk's flawless hand:

*Submersion complete.*

Halvard's Reach. Ada set the knife down. Bryn Halvard poled her ferry out of the lower landing, and her family had worked the river as far back as anyone in town cared to remember, which was a phrase the town used a great deal and examined never. *As far back as anyone remembers.* Ada had assumed it was modesty, or vagueness, the usual fog of oral history. Now the phrase sounded different. It sounded engineered.

Five villages. Drowned. Not by flood—by *entry in a ledger*. By quarterly payments and a word like *submersion*, written the way you'd write *harvest complete* or *accounts closed.* Somewhere upstream, a bound volume held the other halves of these pages, and the halves before them, and the halves after them, and Ada understood—with the particular nausea of a person whose training has just been turned into a weapon against them—that somewhere was a place worth killing to keep quiet.

That was when they allowed themselves to open the envelope.

Not an envelope, really—a oilcloth packet, folded and tied with waxed cord, the kind of wrapping that means *this must survive water*, which was either irony or intention given where Ada had found it: wedged deep in a fissure at the back of the inner chamber, above the high-tide line, placed there deliberately by hands that wanted it found by someone patient enough to dig. Ada had almost missed it. It was Ingrid's voice in their head that had made them check the fissure—*the midden remembers, look where the deposit layers break*—and it was Ingrid's habit of concealment that had shaped the packet's hiding place, and Ada had known, had *known*, with a certainty that preceded thought, whose hands had put it there. Before they opened it. Before they saw what was inside.

Inside was a photograph.

Glossy stock, standard print size, the kind any chemist's shop or village post office would process. It showed a stretch of riverbank Ada recognized instantly—the willow-hung bend two miles downstream of the cave, where the current slowed and the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a wading bird. Autumn light. And walking along the bank, mid-stride, caught glancing back toward whoever held the camera, was Dr. Ingrid Eriksen.

Her hair was shorter than Ada remembered. Greyer at the temples, though the photograph's colors were slightly shifted, cold-biased, the way cheap processing goes. She wore the green field jacket she'd owned for twenty years, the one with the torn pocket Ada's father had mended with orange thread before he left, before everything, back when mending was still something that happened in their house. She was carrying a sample case. She was squinting against low sun. She looked—this was the detail that broke something loose behind Ada's sternum—she looked *happy*. Focused, absorbed, lit from inside by the particular pleasure of a question taking shape. Ada knew that expression from a hundred memories they had spent seven years trying not to polish. It was the face their mother wore when the ground was telling her something.

On the reverse, in the photo shop's stamped format—date, reference number—was a processing date.

Three weeks ago.

Three weeks ago, Ingrid Eriksen had stood on a riverbank ten miles from the town that still talked about her, and someone had stood close enough to photograph her, and she had glanced back at them without alarm, and the photograph had been developed and printed and then carried—by whom? by her?—into a tidal cave and sealed in oilcloth above the waterline, in a hiding place built like a message.

Ada put the photograph face-down on the canvas.

They stood up. They walked to the window. The river lay out there black and indifferent, moving seaward with the patience of something that has outlasted every attempt to own it, and Ada gripped the sill and breathed the way Okafor had taught them to breathe when a find hits too close—four counts in, six counts out—because grief in the field is dangerous, grief makes you dig wrong, grief makes you keep things you should record and record things you should keep.

Their mother had been here. Three weeks ago. Which meant the estrangement—seven years of silence, of forwarded letters returned unopened, of a birthday card Ada had mailed to a university address and gotten back stamped *NOT AT THIS INSTITUTION*—had ended, or paused, or transformed, without Ada knowing. Ingrid had come home to the valley. Ingrid had come to the cave. Ingrid had hidden a photograph of herself where only a digger would find it, and Ada's mind laid the inference out with horrible, clean efficiency, each step load-bearing:

She wanted it found. By someone who could find it. By me.

She hid it above the tide line, which means she knew the cave flooded, which means she'd studied it, which means she'd been here more than once.

She left no note. A photograph instead of a note. A picture of herself alive, walking, looking back. Not a goodbye. A *trailhead*.

And she had not come to the boathouse. Ada's address was no secret; the town made sure everyone knew where the archaeologist's strange child lived, off-season, in the old boathouse, digging shell middens for pub money. If Ingrid had wanted to knock, she'd had three weeks in which to knock.

So she couldn't. Or wouldn't. Or—

Ada's eyes went to the lantern on the canvas. Fresh hinge-rust. Fresh char on the wick. Boot prints days old, size eleven.

Ingrid wore size eight.

Ada turned around slowly and looked at their scattered quadrant of evidence, and the fear arrived all at once, total, adult, unsentimental—the fear that replaces the ache of abandonment the way a flood replaces a stream, filling the same channel with something vastly larger. Their mother had walked into this valley asking questions about drowned villages, and someone with a Trust-stamped lantern had walked in behind her, and the trail of her went into that cave and did not come out of it. *Submersion complete.* The clerk's handwriting swam behind Ada's eyes.

They thought about Vane before they knew his name—thought about the shape of the threat generically, the way you brace for weather: *they will come and ask what I found, and I will have to decide, very quickly, what kind of liar I am.*

Ada had spent seven years practicing invisibility. It turned out invisibility was mostly lying—lying to landlords, to the dole office, to gulls and neighbors and themselves—and they were good at it, and they hated that they were good at it, and tonight the skill settled over them like a tool belt settling onto hips: heavy, familiar, ready.

Because here was the thing the grief became, once it finished breaking and began to set. Ada picked up the photograph and looked at their mother's face one last time—the squint, the field jacket, the glance backward, the invitation—and felt the loneliness of seven years compress into something with an edge on it. Not sorrow. Direction.

Ingrid had followed the river upstream. Every clue pointed upstream: the toll-markers facing inland, the ledger's payments flowing from some central source, the lantern's owners coming and going toward the head of the valley, the photograph taken on a bank that faced north. The river knew. Rivers always knew; they were the valley's longest memory, and everything the Trust had buried, it had buried in the water.

"Okay," Ada said aloud, to the lamp, to the boat ribs, to the woman in the photograph. "Upstream."

They began, methodically, to pack.

* * *

Outside, far down the dark river, a light moved where no boat had business moving at two in the morning—steady, unhurried, sweeping the banks with the thoroughness of a search rather than the wandering of a fisherman. Ada watched it from the unlit side of the window until it passed out of sight beyond the point, and then they blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with their packed bag between their knees, listening to the tide turn, counting the hours until dawn made the river safe to travel.

Three weeks, the photograph said. The boot prints said: someone is still looking.

Ada intended to get to the source first.
