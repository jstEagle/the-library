## 4. The Trust Sends Its Regards

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was wrong on two counts. Nobody knocked at the boathouse—nobody had knocked there in the eleven months Ada had lived behind its salt-blistered door—and nobody who meant well arrived before eight.

Ada knew this because they had learned the river's schedule the way other people learned prayer: low tide at 6:40, gulls working the mudflats by seven, the first ferry horn from Bryn Halvard's crossing at 7:15, a sound like a sick goose complaining about the weather. The knock fell between the ferry horn and the gulls, an intrusion into a liturgy that had no room for visitors.

They stood very still inside, one hand on the kettle, and listened.

Three knocks. Measured. Courteous, even. The kind of knocking that expected to be answered because it had always been answered, everywhere, by everyone, all of that man's life.

Ada set down the kettle without letting it clink against the hob. The boathouse was one room and a loft, and every inch of it was catalogued in their head: the finds from the cave wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboard by the stern cleat—the child's shoe, the ledger page, the photograph; the trowel and screens stacked to look like ordinary midden-digging gear; the second pair of boots drying by the door, still crusted with cave clay that no shell midden could explain.

*Don't answer*, said the part of them that had survived eleven months of being nobody. *Wait him out. Whoever he is, he'll go away.*

But the photograph was under the floorboard three feet from where a stranger would stand if Ada let him in, and something colder than caution whispered that a man who knocked politely at a shuttered boathouse at half past seven already knew more than a stranger should.

Ada opened the door.

The man on the step was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, wearing a waxed jacket too clean for the weather and shoes that had never touched mud. He held a flat parcel under one arm and an expression of pleasant, unhurried interest, like a man admiring a view he already owned.

"Miss Eriksen." He pronounced it correctly—*AIR-ik-sen*, the Norwegian way, not the flattened local version—and that was the first wrong thing. "Aldous Vane. Rivergate Trust." He did not offer a card. Men like him didn't need cards; the name did the work. "I hope I'm not intruding."

"You are," Ada said.

His smile deepened by a millimeter, as though rudeness were a flavor he'd been hoping to sample. "May I?" He gestured past them into the boathouse without waiting for permission, and stepped inside as if the threshold were a formality observed for other people's benefit.

He looked around with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory. Ada watched his eyes move—the screens, the buckets of sorted shell, the cot, the kettle—and understood, with a lurch, that he was reading the room the way Ada read a midden: layer by layer, oldest evidence at the bottom.

"You've been digging the middens," he said. "North bank, I believe. And the spit below the cliffs after the spring storms." Not questions. "The Trust has always taken an interest in careful hands. We fund most of the legitimate archaeology in this valley, you know. Have done since the twenties."

"I don't dig for the Trust."

"No," Vane agreed mildly. "You dig for cash, I imagine. Off-season, out of season, out of—" he glanced around the boathouse, and the glance contained the whole town's gossip compressed into a single courteous flicker, "—out of things generally. It must be difficult. Your mother being who she is. Was." He let the verb hang between the two tenses like a man testing which one made Ada flinch.

Ada flinched at neither, and hated how much effort not flinching took.

"What do you want?"

"To make you an offer. A standing offer, really." He set the flat parcel on the sorting table, squaring its edges to the table's edge with two small adjustments, precise as a man laying a cornerstone. Inside the brown paper, when he folded it back, lay banknotes in a rubber-banded brick, and above them a printed card:

**THE RIVERGATE TRUST WELCOMES REPORTS OF ITEMS RECOVERED FROM THE SHORELINE. FAIR RATES PAID. ALL ENQUIRIES DISCREET.**

"The sea gives and the sea takes," Vane said, "and lately it's been giving rather generously. An anomalous tide last week—lowest in nine years, the harbor office says. Exposed all manner of things. Caves, ledges, the bones of old Merrow showing through the sand like knuckles." He said the village name lightly, carelessly, the way you'd say a word you'd said ten thousand times and never once meant. "Anything recovered from the shoreline is technically Trust property. Old charter rights. But we're not unreasonable. Fair rates. No paperwork. No questions about how items came into your possession." His eyes came up from the card and rested on Ada with the gentle weight of a hand on the back of the neck. "We'd simply hate for history to end up in the wrong hands. Sold to collectors. Sensationalized. Misunderstood."

"Misunderstood by whom?"

"By everyone." He smiled again, and for a moment the courtesy thinned enough to show the machinery underneath, oiled and patient. "History is a load-bearing wall, Miss Eriksen. You'd be surprised what comes down when amateurs start chipping at it."

Ada looked at the money. It was a lot of money—more than a season of middens, more than a year of middens—and it sat on the table like bait sits on a hook, which is to say obviously, insultingly, and effectively anyway, because eleven months of cold kettles and counted coins looked at that brick of notes and felt something shameful stir.

"I haven't found anything," Ada said.

It came out smooth. That was the terrible thing. No catch in the throat, no heat in the ears, no telltale glance toward the floorboard. Just a flat, easy sentence delivered with the mild boredom of someone discussing weather, and Ada heard their own voice do it and thought: *where did you learn that?* Because it wasn't from Ingrid, who was constitutionally incapable of a lie, whose face broadcast every thought like a noticeboard. This ease lived somewhere else, some older stratum, dug up fresh.

Vane studied them for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he seemed to file it.

"No," he said pleasantly. "Of course not. The tide keeps its own hours, and so do we. If anything should... surface..." He produced a pen—a heavy, dark pen—and wrote a telephone number on the back of the card in a small, exact hand. "Any hour. Day or night. We're very responsive."

He rewrapped the money, considered it, and left it on the table. "Consider it a retainer against future honesty. People find that keeping it costs less than returning it." At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back with genuine, almost paternal warmth. "Your mother was a brilliant woman. Brilliant people see patterns that aren't there. It's a kind of illness, pattern-seeing. The Trust does try to look after its own." A beat. "She was ours, you know. Funded her first three seasons. Did she never mention us?"

"She mentioned nothing," Ada said, "for eleven years."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes—not surprise; confirmation. "Well," he said softly. "Then we have that in common, she and I. Both of us wondering where she went." He tipped his head, courteous to the last, and went down the steps onto the mudflat path, his clean shoes finding the driest line without ever appearing to look for it, and walked away toward the town without once looking back.

Which was, Ada realized only after the door closed, the most frightening thing he'd done. Innocent men look back.

* * *

Ada did not touch the money for a full minute. Then they picked it up with two fingers, carried it to the stove, and held it over the burner—and stopped, because burning it was a performance for an audience of one, and the audience already knew everything the performance would say.

Instead they put it in the tin biscuit box under the cot, unburned, unspent, a retainer against future honesty sitting there like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

Then they ate breakfast, because paranoia was no excuse for bad field discipline, and while the porridge cooked they made themselves review the morning like evidence.

He'd known their name, pronounced correctly—which meant records, or the mother's papers, or the town gossip refined into intelligence. He'd known the dig sites, north bank and cliff spit—which meant observation, someone watching, days or weeks of watching. He'd used the word *caves*. Plural. Ada hadn't mentioned any cave. The harbor office might talk about anomalous tides, but *caves* was a specific word, chosen, seeded, dropped to see what grew.

And he'd said *she was ours.*

Ada washed the bowl and tried to feel nothing, and succeeded mostly in feeling the shape of the nothing.

* * *

The light changed in the middle of the afternoon. Ada noticed it without understanding it for several minutes—the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know by heart. The boathouse had a particular afternoon slant, sun through the east window laying a bright bar across the floorboards, and the bar was in the wrong place, or rather the dust in it was wrong, disturbed, swirled in patterns that settled air doesn't make.

Ada went very still, then moved through the room the way they moved through a site: slow, systematic, reading the surface before disturbing it.

The oilcloth bundle under the floorboard was untouched. The knot was exactly as they'd tied it—they checked twice, memorizing the loop's orientation against a scratch in the board. Whatever else had happened here, whoever had come had not found that. Small mercy. The only one on offer.

Everything else had been read.

The screens had been restacked—neatly, but in the wrong order, coarse mesh where fine had been. The drawers of the sorting table had been opened and closed with care, but the shell drawer sat a quarter inch proud, its runners catching where they'd never caught before. The cot blanket had been folded back and replaced, but the fold faced the wrong way; Ada always folded toward the window, away from the damp, and someone had folded it toward the wall. The tin biscuit box—the empty one, the decoy full of old letters and a dead gull's skull kept for exactly this eventuality—had been opened. They'd gone through the letters. Ada could tell because the ribbon around them had been retied left-handed.

Even the boots by the door had been turned over. Cave clay brushed, sampled, or photographed—no way to know. The second pair, the ones Ada actually wore digging, were missing from their peg.

No. Not missing. Moved. Hanging on the far peg, where Ada never hung them, a correction so subtle it might have been accidental, might have been nothing—except that nothing in that room had been accidental all day.

Ada stood in the middle of the boathouse while the light died off the water outside, and felt the walls change nature around them. For eleven months this place had been invisible—a shed at the edge of the town's attention, worth gossip but not effort. Now it was a stage set, examined, measured, and left standing so the occupant wouldn't know the audience had walked through it. The message wasn't in anything broken. The message was that nothing needed breaking. *We can be here whenever we wish. You will not see us. You will keep living here, and we will keep knowing.*

They found the last touch at dusk, checking the boat itself, the old skiff upside down on trestles by the slip. Scratched into the hull's underside, in the same small exact hand as the phone number, so faint it would vanish under a week of weather:

**WE LOOK FORWARD TO HEARING FROM YOU.**

Ada sat on the slipway until full dark, listening to the tide come in over the flats, filling the channel, climbing toward the cliffs, covering the cave mouth that was the whole reason any of this had started. Nine years until the next tide like it. The sea would keep its secret; the Trust would keep its watch; and somewhere upstream, past drowned villages and polite men with clean shoes, a river ran from a source somebody had killed to own.

Eleven months of being nobody, ended in a single morning by a man who pronounced their name correctly.

Ada went inside, took the ledger page from its oilcloth, and read it again by lamplight—the payments, the village names, the dates going back further than they'd yet been able to follow. Then they took out the map of the valley, the cheap tourist one, and spread it flat, and for the first time looked not along the river but up it, tracing the blue thread from the sea to the blank space at the head of the valley where the cartographers stopped drawing and the legends started.

Upstream was a direction. It was even, possibly, a plan.

In the tin box, the Trust's money sat in the dark like a ticking thing. Ada decided they'd spend every note of it—on supplies, on passage, on distance—and leave the empty wrapper on the table for the next visitor, with a word written inside in their own hand.

They fell asleep planning the shopping list, and woke once in the night certain they'd heard footsteps on the mudflat path, and lay listening until the gulls started, and told themselves it was the tide.

It was probably the tide.
