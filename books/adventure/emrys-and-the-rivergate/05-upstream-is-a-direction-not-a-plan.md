## 5. Upstream Is a Direction, Not a Plan

The decision did not arrive all at once. It came the way the tide came—slowly, in increments too small to argue with, until the boathouse floor was wet and there was no longer any pretending the sea had not reached you.

Ada spent the night after Vane's visit awake on the cot, listening to the hull of the old skiff knock against its mooring cleat like something asking to be let out. The searched boathouse smelled different now. Someone else's hands had been among their things; the air held a faint residue of tobacco and shoe leather that was not theirs. Ada had put everything back exactly where the searcher had left it, which was almost exactly where Ada had left it, and that was the worst part—the violation was invisible unless you knew your own clutter the way you knew the lines of your own palm. Someone had lifted the false bottom of the ammunition tin where Ada kept the ledger page. Had read it. Had put it back.

They know I have it, Ada thought, staring at the rafters where the swallows used to nest before the season ended and the town shuttered itself like a shell. And they let me keep it. That was either arrogance or a trap, and Ada had learned from their mother's textbooks that arrogance and traps often wore the same coat.

Ingrid's notes lay open on the crate that served as a desk. Ada had gone through them four times now, and each pass yielded something new, the way a midden yields—layer by layer, patience rewarded. The notes were written in that cramped, slanting hand Ada knew better than their own, the hand that had signed school permission slips from hotel stationery in nine countries. Most of it was hydrology: flow rates, silt loads, seasonal variations, all of it annotated with a growing irritation. *Figures don't reconcile. Upstream gauges report LESS water than downstream. Impossible. Someone is lying with numbers.*

And then, on the last sheet, in pencil, pressed hard enough to groove the paper: *The source. If the river is being managed, someone manages it from somewhere. Follow the water to its head. Everything upstream of Gallowford is Trust land "for conservation." Conservation of WHAT?*

Follow the water to its head.

Ada said it aloud to the dark boathouse, testing the shape of it. It sounded less like a plan than a direction, and Ada had never been good at plans anyway. Plans required a future you believed in. Directions only required the next step, and Ada had been taking those alone for a long time.

The tide turned around four in the morning. Ada knew without looking—the pressure in the ears, the change in the slap of water against the pilings, that subsonic shift the body learns when it lives close to the water's moods. Somewhere out under the black water, the cave was closing its mouth again, keeping its secrets on the sea's schedule. Ada thought about the boot prints in the cave silt, size eleven, recent. Thought about Aldous Vane's soft voice and softer handshake. Thought about the photograph: their mother, three weeks ago, squinting into the sun on the Rivergate quay, looking—Ada had studied this for an hour—looking *hurried*.

Ingrid Eriksen did not look hurried. Ingrid Eriksen looked like she had all the time in the world and intended to spend every second of it on her own terms. Hurried meant pursued, or pursuing. Either way, she had come here, to Ada's stretch of river, and then she had gone upstream, and then she had not come back.

"Well," Ada said to the dark, and the word came out steady, which surprised them. "Upstream, then."

* * *

Bryn Halvard's ferry was moored at the old stone quay a mile downriver, past the row of shuttered holiday cottages whose windows had been boarded for the winter like the eyes of sleeping animals. Ada walked there in the grey first light with the ledger page sewn into the lining of their coat—Vane's men had searched the boathouse, so the boathouse was no longer a safe place for anything that mattered—and a mental list of what they'd need.

The Halvard ferry was the last one working the lower Rivergate out of season. Everyone else had hauled up for winter. Bryn was the kind of person who hauled up for no one.

She was on her deck when Ada arrived, mending a net with the quick, economical motions of someone who had done ten thousand times, and she looked up without surprise, which Ada would think about later. "Eriksen," she said. Not a question. Everyone in the valley knew the name; it was said the way you'd say a word that had been in the family too long.

"Halvard."

Bryn went back to her net. Ada stood on the quay and let the silence do its work. This was a technique from the digs—people filled silences, and what they filled them with told you more than questions would. But Bryn Halvard did not fill silences. She mended her net, and the river moved under the hull, and a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a monument.

"I need supplies," Ada said finally.

"Mm."

"Rope. Dried food. A tarp. Lamp oil if you have it. And I need to know the river—shallows, weirs, where the Trust's people watch."

"Mm," Bryn said again, and pulled a knot tight.

Ada had prepared a story. It was a good story, detailed and mostly true, about surveying work, about a contract, about measurements needed above the weir. They had rehearsed it on the walk down. But standing on Bryn's quay, Ada found the story sitting in their throat like a stone, and beside it, unexpectedly, the memory of Vane's courteous smile, and the two things would not fit in the same mouth. Ada had lied to Vane smoothly and hated how easy it had been. They found they did not want to do it again before breakfast.

"I'm going upstream," Ada said. "After my mother."

The net-mending did not stop. But Bryn's eyes came up then—grey eyes, river-coloured, with the flat assessing look of a woman who read water for a living and had learned to read people the same way, by current and undertow. She looked at Ada for a long moment, and whatever she saw, she did not name it.

"Dr. Eriksen," Bryn said. "The one on the telly."

"The one who's missing."

"Is she missing, then? Or just not where you are?" There was no cruelty in it. It was a genuine question, the kind the river asks.

"Three weeks," Ada said. "No letters. Her notes ended here." They did not mention the cave, the photograph, the ledger. Some things you kept in your lining, sewn in, where searches couldn't reach.

Bryn set down the net at last. She was perhaps sixty, built like the bollard at the quay's end—short, dense, immovable—and her hands were the hands of someone who had argued with rope and river for fifty years and won more than she'd lost. "I don't ask questions," she said. "Questions are like hooks. Once they're in, something bleeds."

"Then I'm not asking you any."

"Good." She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and jerked her chin toward the cabin. "Come and see what I've got. And Eriksen—" Ada paused at the gangplank. "Whatever you're after up there. The river's been owned a long time. Owners don't like visitors."

"I'd noticed."

"Mm." And that was all, but Ada had the distinct, uncomfortable sense of having been weighed, measured, and filed away somewhere behind those grey eyes, to be retrieved later at a moment of Bryn's choosing.

The barter took an hour. Bryn had a store of everything in her cabin, organized with a rigidity that bordered on devotion—tinned food by year, rope by grade, tarps folded to knife-edge squares. Ada traded the only currency they had: labour. They re-caulked a seam on the ferry's portside hull while Bryn supervised with the silence of a foreman, and mended two nets, and split a cord of firewood until their palms blistered and their shoulders burned. Bryn watched all of it. That was the thing about her—she watched everything, continuously, without appearing to. Ada caught her gaze on them a dozen times and each time it slid away as if it had never landed, like light off moving water.

"You dig middens," Bryn said at one point, not as a question. "Down the point. Off-season."

"Off-season," Ada agreed.

"Found anything good?"

"Shell. Bones. The usual." The lie was smaller than the one they'd told Vane, and Ada hated it less, which was its own kind of information.

Bryn grunted. "My grandmother used to say the middens down the point went back before the churches. Before the maps." She handed Ada a coil of rope to test. "Before the Trust."

"Did she." Ada kept their voice level with an effort. *The maps say never existed.* They thought of Old Merrow, the name they'd found in the ledger page among the drowned villages, and filed the grandmother away with everything else.

"Did," Bryn said, and offered nothing further, because offering things was apparently against her religion.

By noon Ada had a kit: forty feet of good rope, a tarp, a fortnight of food in a waterproof roll, a storm lantern with a pint of oil, a folding shovel Bryn threw in without being asked—"diggers always need shovels," she said, and Ada decided not to wonder how she knew what Ada was—and a secondhand river chart of the upper valley, its paper soft as cloth from handling. The chart showed the river running up through the fens, past Gallowford, into country marked with the Trust's green hatching: *RIVERGATE TRUST LANDS — PERMIT REQUIRED — CONSERVATION AREA.* Above that, near the top edge of the map, the river's source was marked with nothing at all. A blank. A white space where water came from.

"Nobody goes up there," Bryn said, watching Ada's finger on the blank.

"Somebody does. My mother did."

"Mm." Bryn looked out at the river for a while. "I can't take you past the weir. There's wardens, and my ferry's licensed. License is worth more than my opinion." A pause, and then, gruffly, as if it cost her: "There's a footpath on the east bank from the old mill. Old right-of-way. Trust says it's closed. Trust says a lot of things." She pressed a folded paper into Ada's hand—hand-drawn, decades old, the ink faded to the colour of weak tea. A route. "Grandmother's. She walked it when she was a girl, when walking was still allowed."

Ada took it. "Why are you helping me?"

Bryn's eyes came up, flat and grey and unreadable. "I'm not helping you. I'm trading. You caulked my hull better than I'd have paid for." She turned back to her net. "Go on, then. River won't wait and neither will I."

* * *

Ada set out that afternoon, and the first day on the river was so beautiful it felt like a lie.

The Rivergate in autumn, above the town, was a green-gold corridor of willow and alder, the water running shallow and quick over gravel beds where the light broke into moving coins. Kingfishers stitched blue along the banks. The air smelled of leaf-rot and cold stone, and the footpath Bryn's grandmother had walked was still there under the bramble—narrow, stubborn, a right-of-way the Trust's fences had to bend around, and did, grudgingly, in two places where the old law was stronger than the new money.

Ada walked with the pack riding high and the shovel lashed across it, and for the first few miles they did the thing they always did with landscapes: read them. It was the one inheritance from Ingrid that had never hurt to carry. The east bank was old alluvium, stable, the willows rooted in a century of quiet deposition. The west bank showed fresh slumping, raw scars of erosion where the water had been allowed—or made—to bite. Ada noted it without understanding it yet, the way you note a symptom before the diagnosis.

The river got deeper and slower as the afternoon went on. The valley widened. And the drowned villages began—not the villages themselves, not yet, but their margins: a line of foundation stones at the water's edge, a fence post standing in a foot of water with no fence attached, a row of willows planted, Ada was almost certain, along a street that no longer existed. Once, on a gravel spit, they found a doorstep. Just a doorstep, blue-painted, facing the water, with no house and no road and no reason, except that there had been both, once, and someone had painted the step blue because it was theirs.

Ada stood looking at it for a long time.

The light went long and copper, and mist began to come off the water in slow ribbons, and the first day's walking turned out to be exactly as easy as Bryn had implied it would not stay. Ada made camp in a hollow above the bank, tarp strung low, cold camp because smoke would carry. They ate tinned food and watched the river run silver-dark under the first stars, and they took out Ingrid's notes one more time and read the pencilled line by lantern light: *Follow the water to its head.*

"I'm doing it," Ada told the page. "In case you wondered. In case you're—" They didn't finish the sentence. Dead, or captive, or simply gone, the way she had always been gone, and the word for all three was the same word, and Ada was tired of it.

The river made its sounds in the dark. Water over gravel, water against bank, and underneath, very faint, so faint Ada might have imagined it—though they were almost sure they didn't—a sound like a bell. One note, low, from somewhere upstream in the fog. Then nothing.

Ada lay awake a while, listening, and told themselves it was a buoy, a mooring chain, a trick of current over a sunken pipe. The explanation was reasonable. The explanation was almost certainly right.

They slept anyway, because the body is honest even when the mind is managing itself, and slept deeply, and did not dream—or dreamed of blue-painted doorsteps, which amounts to the same thing.

In the morning the mist lay on the river like a held breath, and Ada broke camp, and shouldered the pack, and looked upstream to where the valley narrowed into the fens and the white space on the map began.

The point of no return, Ada thought, is a thing people imagine as a gate. But it isn't. It's just a morning when you keep walking.

They kept walking. Behind them, far downstream, faint on the wind, a ferry bell rang twice—the signal, Bryn had said, for *all well*—and Ada chose to believe it was meant for them.
