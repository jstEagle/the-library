## 6. Drowned Steeple, Rising Fog

The fog came up the valley before the sun did, and Ada walked into it like walking into a room where someone had just been crying.

It wasn't river mist, not exactly. River mist sat on the water and stayed there, polite and shallow. This rose from the ground itself, from the drowned fields on either bank, and it carried things in it: the smell of cold silt, of rotting willow, of something older underneath—stone, maybe, or lime mortar gone soft with a century of submersion. Ada had dug enough middens to know that water keeps what land forgets. The land above the tide line gives its dead back to the air. But drowned ground holds everything. Every foundation stone, every fence post, every bone of every animal that didn't make it to higher ground when the water came.

The maps said nothing was here.

Ada had checked them again at dawn, all three—the ferry company's chart, the Ordnance sheet from 1971, and Ingrid's own hand-drawn survey, the one with the obsessive little annotations in her upright architect's script. The Ordnance map showed open pasture. The ferry chart showed *deep water, keep mid-channel*. And Ingrid's map showed nothing at all, which was stranger than showing something, because Ingrid annotated everything. She'd labeled individual boulders. She'd once drawn an arrow pointing at a cow field with the note *good flint scatter, ask farmer's permission first*.

Here, between the second bend and the weir pools, her pen had simply stopped. A blank stretch of riverbank, no notes, no arrows. As if she'd lifted the pencil and decided some places were better left unrecorded.

Or as if someone had made her lift it.

The fog thickened until Ada's world shrank to ten feet of towpath and the sound of their own boots. They counted bends by feel, the way Bryn had taught them the night they'd bartered for supplies—*the river talks if you let it*, she'd said, poling her flat-bottomed boat through the dark with an ease that looked like laziness but wasn't. *Count the pulls against your pole. Two hard pulls means the current's found rock. Three soft ones means you're in the slack water behind a point.* Ada used the same logic on foot now, reading the ground through their boots the way they read a midden through a trowel: pressure, resistance, the difference between packed clay and something hollow.

Which is how they knew, before they saw it, that the village was there.

The towpath began to change underfoot. Gravel became cobbles—worn, rounded, laid by hands that had expected cart wheels and got instead a hundred years of floodwater smoothing them further. Then the cobbles gave way to something worse: flagstones, wide and square, running off at an angle into the fog on both sides. A street. Not a path that happened to be old—a street, with the ghost-geometry of doorways opening off it, thresholds still legible as slight rises in the stone where steps had been worn down by generations of feet going in and out of houses that were now entirely underwater.

Ada stopped. Stood very still. Let the fog move around them.

Off to the left, out in the flooded field where the river had spread itself fat and slow, there was a shape. At first it was only a darkness in the murk, a suggestion. Then the fog thinned for a moment—one of those openings, like a curtain twitching—and Ada saw it whole.

A church tower. Standing in eight feet of water, straight as the day it was built, its upper windows clear of the surface. The steeple's cap was green with weed below the waterline and silver-grey above it, and the weathervane on top—Ada could just make it out—was not a cockerel but a fish, turning slowly, obediently, in a wind Ada couldn't feel.

Old Merrow.

Except Old Merrow did not exist. Ada knew this the way they knew their own name. There was no Old Merrow in the gazetteer, no Merrow of any kind, no parish record, no ruin marked, no *site of*. The Trust's handsome historical plaques dotted the whole valley—*RIVERGATE TRUST, PRESERVING OUR SHARED HERITAGE SINCE 1893*—and there was no plaque here. No sign saying *no fishing*. No sign saying anything. Just a village that had been erased so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased.

And yet here were its streets under Ada's boots, and here was its church standing in the water like a man up to his chest who refuses to sit down.

Ada took out the notebook—the cheap one, not Ingrid's—and wrote: *flagstones, N-S alignment, street width suggests market town not hamlet. Tower: dressed stone, C15 probably, tower intact = deliberate flooding, slow rise, people had time to leave OR time was taken from them slowly.* They wrote the last part and then stared at it. *Time was taken from them slowly.* That was not an archaeological observation. That was an accusation wearing an archaeologist's coat.

They were still staring when the bells rang.

There was no wind. Ada checked—looked up at the motionless reeds along the bank, at the fish weathervane hanging dead-still, at the fog itself, which moved only the way fog moves, in slow internal tides. Nothing. And then, from under the water, muffled and round and wrong, the bell in the drowned tower swung once and struck.

One note. It went through the water first—that deep, pressurized thrum Ada felt in the soles of their feet through the flagstones—and then up through the fog after it, arriving late and softened, like a voice calling from another room in a house you grew up in.

Ada stood absolutely still. Their heart was doing something complicated.

The bell struck again. Twice more. Four strokes total, evenly spaced, and then silence, and the fog closed over the tower again as if it had never shown itself.

Bryn's voice, from three nights ago, at the ferry landing, while she'd lashed Ada's pack down without being asked to: *If you ever hear a bell out on that water and there's no boat and no wind, you get off the path and you wait. You don't go toward it. My grandmother had a rule about it.*

*What rule?*

*That the ringing isn't for you.*

Ada got off the path. Sat down on the dry bank above the floodline, among the nettles, and waited, and hated waiting, and understood why Bryn had said it. Because every instinct they had—the digger's instinct, the instinct that had put them in that tide-cave with the Trust's lantern stamped in rust on its handle—every instinct said *go to the tower, there's a doorway at the waterline, there might be a crypt, there might be records, there might be—*

But the bell wasn't ringing for them. The bell was ringing for whoever had lived here. Four strokes. Ada found themselves counting it like a pattern, because patterns were the one language they trusted, and somewhere in the counting a thought arrived that they would carry for the rest of this journey, though they didn't know it yet:

*Someone taught that bell its number. Bells don't ring themselves. Even drowned ones.*

They ate half a oatcake and waited for the fog to do what fog does, which is either thin or thicken, never hold. It chose thin. By mid-morning the sun had burned a hole in it downstream, and Ada stood, brushed off the nettles' argument, and kept walking upstream along the drowned street, because the street went the same direction they were going, and there was a grim rightness in following a road the water hadn't managed to delete.

They had gone perhaps half a mile when they heard the pole.

The soft knock-knock of a wooden pole finding bottom, rhythmic, unhurried, coming upriver behind them. Ada didn't turn around. They knew the rhythm. Everyone on the Rivergate knew Bryn Halvard's rhythm; she was the only ferry left working the upper water, and she poled the way other people breathed.

Her boat slid out of the last of the fog like something surfacing. She shipped the pole, let the current carry her alongside the bank, and looked up at Ada with her flat grey river-keeper's eyes.

"You're a mile past where I told you to stop," she said.

"I don't remember you telling me to stop."

"I told you where the safe camps were. Everything past the second weir pool isn't a camp, it's a dare." She glanced out at the water, at the place where the tower's weathervane caught the new sun. "You heard it, then."

"The bell. Yes."

"Four strokes?"

"Four."

Bryn nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis. "Grandmother said four meant the children. Morning bell, school bell—it rang four times to call them in from the fields." She said it the way she said everything, level and unsentimental, but her hand had gone still on the pole. "Village full of kids, and somebody rings their bell a hundred years after the water took the schoolhouse, and everyone downstream says it's gas pockets, settling stone, thermal whatever-you-like." She looked at Ada. "You're a digger. What do you say it is?"

Ada thought about lying, and found, to their surprise, that they didn't want to. "I say someone remembers the number."

Something moved in Bryn's face—not a smile, nothing so generous. More like a lock turning. "Aye," she said. "That's what Grandmother said too. Before she stopped saying anything about Merrow at all. Took me till I was grown to understand she hadn't forgotten. She'd been paid to forget."

She said it plainly, watching Ada the whole time, and Ada felt the words land somewhere below the ribs. *Paid to forget.* The ledger fragment in the bottom of their pack, wrapped in oilcloth, seemed suddenly heavy. Payments since 1893 to families who accepted relocation. They hadn't told Bryn about the ledger. They hadn't told anyone.

"Bryn," Ada said carefully, "why are you here? Gallowford's six miles downstream and you don't pole six miles for weather."

"No," Bryn agreed. "I pole six miles because a stranger bought rope and lamp oil off me four days ago and asked questions about the headwaters, and then Vane's men came asking the same questions the next morning, offering silver for answers I'd already sold cheap." She rested both hands on the pole. "I've been ferrying this water twenty-two years, Eriksen. I know what the Trust pays for silence, because I've taken it. And I know what it costs, because I've seen what's left of the people who wouldn't take it." She nodded upstream, toward the fens, toward the white space on every map. "You're walking into the one place in this valley where the water has a memory and the owners have a gun. So. I'm going to be on this river, going where you're going, whether you like company or not. You can pretend I'm not here. Most people manage it."

"Why?" Ada asked. It came out rougher than intended. "What do you want out of it?"

Bryn looked at the drowned tower for a long moment. The fog was nearly gone now; the church stood naked in the bright water, absurd and undeniable, its fish weathervane glinting.

"My grandmother was born in that village," she said. "In a house on the street you've been walking on all morning. She was six years old when they moved her out. She remembered the smell of the bread oven and the number of the school bell and nothing else, because she was six, and because forgetting was the price of the relocation payment, and her father signed." Bryn shipped the pole and held out one work-roughened hand, palm up, not quite a request. "I want to see what they bought with our signature. That's all. That's the whole of my price. Now—are you getting in the boat or making me pole beside you all day like a nursemaid?"

Ada looked at the hand. Looked at the water, which lay over the street they'd been walking, over the schoolhouse, over the bread ovens, patient as a creditor.

Then they shouldered the pack and stepped down into the boat, and the river took their weight the way it takes everything—without comment.

* * *

Bryn poled from the stern, and Ada sat amidships with the pack between their knees, and for a long while neither of them spoke, because the water required attention. The channel narrowed as they passed the drowned village's far boundary, and the current quickened, and twice Bryn called a warning—*rock, port side*—in the tone of someone narrating a conversation she'd been having with the river for decades.

It was Ada who broke the quiet, because keeping quiet had never once in their life prevented them from thinking, and the thinking needed somewhere to go.

"When you said paid to forget," they said. "How much? Do you know?"

"Grandmother's family got eleven pounds a year," Bryn said. "Paid quarterly, in person, by a man from the Trust who came to the new cottage and asked if there was anything else the family needed. Same man for thirty years. Grandmother said he aged slower than the payments did." She adjusted the pole. "Eleven pounds bought a house worth forty, and a living worth more, and a graveyard they weren't allowed to visit. And it bought the quiet. That was the real purchase. The land was almost incidental."

Ada's hands tightened on the gunwale. "You know that how? The quiet being the purchase?"

"Because the families that took the money stopped talking to the families that didn't," Bryn said. "Simple as arithmetic. My grandmother's sister married a man whose family refused payment—held out two years, made speeches, wrote to newspapers that never printed the letters. After the wedding, the sisters didn't speak for forty years. Not a quarrel. Just—" she searched for it— "a shame that worked like a wall. The Trust never had to enforce anything after the first year. The money did the enforcing. Silence is cheaper than wardens, Eriksen. Remember that, wherever you're going."

*I have a ledger in my pack,* Ada thought, *that proves every word you're saying, and I'm sitting here deciding whether to trust you with it.* Aloud they said, "And the bell? Did your grandmother ever explain—"

"She explained it the way she explained everything, in a song." Bryn's voice changed slightly, taking on a cadence that wasn't hers, older, worn smooth by repetition. "'Ring four for the children, ring five for the loaves, ring six when the water comes up to the eaves.'" She let the verse hang there. "Six, Eriksen. If you ever hear six, that's not memory anymore. That's instruction."

"Instruction for what?"

"That's the thing about songs," Bryn said. "They keep the tune and lose the meaning, and then one day the meaning comes back and finds nobody ready for it." She nodded ahead, where the valley bent north and the fens began—a vast grey-green flatness of reed and black water, threaded with channels that appeared and vanished, and somewhere in all of it, according to a pencil mark inside a wardrobe in Gallowford, a route that only a bell-keeper knew. "We'll make the fen mouth by dark if the wind holds. Sleep in the boat tonight. Tomorrow we find out what your mother's map is worth."

Ada started. "I never told you about my mother's map."

"You never told me anything," Bryn agreed, without heat. "But you talk in your sleep, digger, and you sleep like a scholar—flat on your back, arms crossed, guarding something." She poled them around a snag, easy as turning a page. "I don't need your secrets. I just need you to know I can see the shape of them. Makes for cleaner traveling."

The fens opened before them, and the light went long and gold across the reeds, and somewhere behind them, fainter than memory, Ada thought they heard the bell ring once more—but when they turned, there was only the tower, and the water, and the evening beginning to gather.

Five strokes, Bryn had sung. Five for the loaves.

Ada counted what they'd heard.

It was five.

* * *

*Bryn had known the stranger was trouble from the first hour—trouble being her word for anyone who asked about the headwaters, and she'd known plenty—but trouble usually traveled loud, with engines and permits and that particular Trust-bought confidence. This one traveled quiet, slept like a scholar, and lied badly about why they were on the river, which Bryn privately respected. Bad liars were honest about everything else.*

*She'd gone back for them, though she'd have denied it under oath. Gone back because of the bell, if she was honest, and she tried to be, these days. Four strokes. Her grandmother had hummed that number into her before she could walk, the way you teach a child a prayer, and Bryn had spent twenty-two years poling past Old Merrow telling herself it was gas in the stones. Hearing the stranger confirm the count—four, steady, no drama—had done something to a door she'd kept painted shut.*

*So now she poled them both into the fens with a stolen ledger sleeping in a stranger's pack, and a debt of her own she hadn't confessed yet, and the last gold light going flat on the water, and she thought: Grandmother, you'd laugh. All those years of silence, and it ends because a half-starved digger counts bells.*

*At the fen mouth she shipped the pole and lit the small lamp, and the reeds stood black around them, and from deep in the wet dark, faint but unmistakable, came a sound she had never once heard in twenty-two years on this water.*

*A bell. Ringing six.*
