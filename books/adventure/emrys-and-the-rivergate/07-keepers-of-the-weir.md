## 7. Keepers of the Weir

Ada smelled the weir before they saw it—coal smoke and wet stone and something underneath both, a faint mineral tang like the inside of an old kettle. The river narrowed here between shoulders of grey rock, and the water, which had been running easy and brown-green all morning, suddenly gathered itself and went quiet in the way that deep water goes quiet, the way it does when it is being held back by something that was not made by God.

They had been on the river two days now. Two days of reed beds and herons lifting off like folded laundry, of banks soft enough to leave boot prints a mile long behind them, of sun that felt almost like permission. Ada had begun to think—dangerously, they would later admit—that Bryn's warnings were the usual river-folk theater, the kind of talk ferrymen and ferry-women kept in stock to make a flat journey feel like a passage.

Then the trees opened, and there it was.

The weir spanned the river like a jaw. Great blocks of dressed granite, each one taller than Ada, fitted without mortar into a curve that turned the whole Rivergate through ninety degrees and dropped it through three iron sluice-gates into a churning white basin below. Above the gates, the water lay flat as poured pewter, so still it held the sky upside down. A walkway crossed the top of the structure, planked and railed, and at its midpoint stood a small stone hut with a slate roof, chimney smoking.

And standing on the walkway, in uniforms the colour of river fog, were four men.

Ada stopped at the treeline and did what Ingrid Eriksen had taught them to do before they could ride a bicycle: they looked without looking. Counted. Four wardens, one at each end of the walkway, two at the hut. Rifles slung but hands free—that was a choice, and choices meant rules, and rules meant someone above them cared about appearances. A rowboat tied below the sluices. A signal pole with three flags furled. A wire fence running off from either end of the weir into the trees, following the high-water line, topped with nothing worse than strung tin cans that would rattle.

*They're not guarding the weir,* Ada thought. *The weir guards itself. They're guarding the road past it.*

Because that was the thing about a river valley: you could follow the water or you could follow the land, and the Trust owned both, but ownership showed differently in each. On the water there were wardens and boats. On the land there were fences and signs—KEEP OUT, TRUST PROPERTY, BY ORDER—and then, beyond the signs, the older paths, the drovers' roads, the ways that predated deeds and charters and men named Vane. The paths nobody maintained because nobody official remembered them.

But somebody local remembered them. Somebody always did.

Ada withdrew into the alders and sat down on their heels among the leaf mould, and for a long time they simply watched the weir the way they'd once watched a promising midden: patiently, letting the site come to them.

* * *

The afternoon taught them the shape of the place.

A cart came down the north bank track around two o'clock—a farmer with a load of turnips, presumably, though the sacks were lashed tight—and was stopped at the fence gate by one of the wardens. There was no drama. The warden lifted the bar, the farmer handed over a paper, the warden read it, nodded, wrote something in a ledger he carried under his arm, and waved the cart across the walkway. The whole exchange took less than a minute and had the smoothness of a ritual performed ten thousand times.

That was the detail that chilled Ada more than the rifles: the courtesy. The Trust didn't bark at people. It *processed* them. It had forms where other powers had fists, and somehow that was worse, because a fist you could hate, but a form you just signed.

Toward evening a second party arrived from downstream—the direction Ada had come—and this one Ada watched with total attention. Two travelers, a man and a woman with backpacks, the kind of casual river-walkers who'd been common on the first day's banks. The wardens met them at the gate. Even at two hundred metres Ada could read the conversation's grammar: the travelers gestured upstream, wanting passage; the warden shook his head, pleasant, immovable; papers were consulted; heads bent together; and finally the couple turned around and walked back the way they'd come, their body language carrying the particular slump of people who have been refused politely and don't know why they feel ashamed.

*Turned back,* Ada thought. *Just like that.* No sign said the river was closed. No notice, no proclamation. The closure existed only in the bodies of four men on a walkway, and it worked because everyone downstream had grown up believing it should work.

Ada ate cold oatcake in the alder shade and watched the light go amber on the pewter-flat water, and thought about their mother's field notes, the ones they'd memorized until the paper itself seemed worn soft in their memory. *When a site is guarded,* Ingrid had written, in the margin of a report on a looted barrow in Jutland, *the guard is the map. Watch what they watch, and you will find what they fear.*

What did the wardens watch? The walkway. The gate. The north bank track. The water immediately above the sluices.

What did they not watch?

Ada waited for full dark to answer the question.

* * *

Night came up out of the ground rather than down from the sky, the way it did in valleys, rising like floodwater through the trees until the last grey went out of the west. The hut's window glowed. One warden walked the beat along the walkway, boots ringing on planks, and the rhythm of it became, after an hour, almost soothing—a heartbeat belonging to someone else's body.

Ada stripped off everything that rattled. Pack cinched tight, buckles wrapped in strips of shirt, the tin cup buried under a root to be collected after. They rubbed mud on anything pale: the knife handle, the buckle of the strap across their chest, the strip of white ankle showing above their left boot. Digger's habits. You learned fast on a site that the eye catches movement and contrast and nothing else, and that the person who looks like the ground belongs to the ground.

Then they went to read the terrain, because that was the real work, and it was work they loved even now, even frightened, and hating themselves a little for loving it.

The fence ran east from the weir's abutment along what Ada's feet told them was the old high-water terrace—a gravel spine laid down centuries ago when the river ran higher and freer, before anyone dreamed of holding it. Gravel drained. Gravel didn't hold footprints. Whoever had routed the fence had followed the easiest line, which meant the driest line, which meant the line the river itself had chosen first. And fences, like rivers, eroded at the outside of curves.

Ada moved along the fence line slowly, hands out, reading with their fingers as much as their eyes. Forty metres east of the abutment the terrace curved, and at the outside of the curve a seep had been working for years—you could smell it, that cold cabbage-and-iron smell of moving groundwater—and the fence post there leaned drunkenly, its footing rotted, the bottom rail sprung loose from the next post like a rib gone wrong.

Under the sprung rail, the ground told the rest of the story. Mud, yes—but mud with a tunnel through the bramble at its edge, a tunnel worn smooth, greased with use. Not by Ada. By animals, probably, badgers maybe, or by people who had used this gap before and often. The brambles had grown back over it, but they'd grown back *bent*, trained along a habitual line, and a bramble that bends has been bent by something patient.

Ada lay flat and went under the rail the way they'd once gone under fallen turf at the Merrow excavation, breathing shallow, elbows doing the work, the mud taking their weight with a cold indifferent grip. A can on the wire rattled somewhere above them and Ada froze, heart slamming, counting seconds—one, five, ten—until the night sounds resumed and no torch beam swung their way.

On the far side, they knelt in the dark and let their pulse settle, and allowed themselves ten seconds of pure animal satisfaction. Then they stood, oriented on the sound of the water—which here came muffled and strange, from below rather than beside—and began to climb.

Because that was the part the wardens didn't watch: the abutment itself. The great granite shoulder where the weir anchored into the hillside was sheer on its river face but stepped on its landward side—old construction scaffolding ledges, courses offset for the builders who'd raised the thing a century ago, now furred with moss and ivy. Nobody climbed the weir because nobody was supposed to climb the weir, and the Trust's power, Ada was beginning to understand, rested entirely on the word *supposed.*

They went up slow, testing every hold the way they'd test a baulk of buried timber—weight on gradually, always ready to take it back. The granite was slick with dew. Twice their fingers found holds that crumbled, old mortar rotting to grit, and twice they redistributed and found better ones, colder and deeper-set. Halfway up they paused in the shadow of an offset course and looked down at the hut, close enough now to see the warden inside dozing over a newspaper, his rifle propped in the corner like a broom.

Above him, chalked faintly on the lintel of the hut door, Ada saw letters. Old ones, weathered, half-erased: **M.R.W. 1893**. And beneath, smaller, in a different hand: *the river remembers its name.*

Their breath caught. Their mother's handwriting? No—older, different. But the sentence—

A plank creaked on the walkway. The night warden, making his round. Ada pressed into the ivy and became a shadow among shadows, close enough to hear the man humming under his breath—an old tune, modal, descending, the kind of melody that gets into stone—and then his boots receded and Ada climbed.

The top course was the worst: a metre of exposed wall with nothing above it, wind pushing at their back like a hand deciding whether to help or shove. Ada went over it the way water goes over a lip—all at once, committed—and came down on the planked walkway on the far side, crouched low, in the blind spot behind the hut's chimney.

From there it was thirty metres of open walkway to the north end, moonless, the wardens' lanterns making islands of light that Ada threaded between like a cat crossing a lit kitchen. At the north abutment they found the steps—of course there were steps, on the far side, for the wardens' own use—and went down them into the dark of the north bank, and did not run, because running was noise, and noise was death, and only when the trees closed over them and the weir's lanterns were swallowed did they let themselves breathe like a living person again.

They had done it. They were through.

That was when they reached for the pack strap to check their supplies and found the strap hanging loose, bitten through, and the pack itself—gone. Torn away somewhere on the climb, snagged on the ivy or dropped into the dark below the walkway, lost to the river side of the weir, to the Trust side, to the men who would find it at dawn and know exactly what it meant: someone had passed, and passed going *upstream*, and knew how to move like the ground.

Ada stood in the black under the trees and took inventory by touch, and the inventory was short. The knife. The oilskin pouch at their breast with the ledger page and the photograph, which they had never once let leave their body since the cave. Half a candle stub. Three oatcakes in a pocket, damp. That was all. The rest—the food, the tarp, the spare socks, the little spirit stove, the rope—was on the wrong side of a granite jaw guarded by four sleeping men.

They could go back. The thought arrived and was examined and dismissed in the space of a single breath. Going back meant climbing down in the dark with the camp now stirring, meant leaving prints, meant gambling everything already won for things merely useful. Ingrid's voice, dry as site dust: *You never re-enter a trench for your hat.*

"Right," Ada whispered to the dark, and their breath steamed. "North bank. Cold. Hungry."

They laughed—quietly, wildly—at the absurd arithmetic of it: they had beaten the Trust's fence and its rifles and its polite, form-stamped menace, and what actually defeated them was gravity and a rotten ivy stem. Somewhere behind them the river went over the sluices with its endless held-breath roar, and Ada turned their back on it and walked north along the bank, eating one of the three oatcakes in small, deliberate bites, making it last, learning the new lesson all the way down into their bones:

Competence got you through the gate. It did not feed you on the far side. Every passage cost, and the river kept its own books.

Dawn found them walking, stiff with cold, the valley opening ahead into mist—and there, moored at a ruined landing stage half-swallowed by reeds, a boat. A familiar boat, patched and tar-black, and standing in it with a boathook and an expression caught between fury and relief, a broad-shouldered woman with grey in her braids.

"You," Bryn Halvard called across the water, "are the worst cargo I ever agreed to carry."

She threw them a line, and a bundle that smelled, impossibly, of hot bread.
