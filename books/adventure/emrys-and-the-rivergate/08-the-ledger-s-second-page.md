## 8. The Ledger's Second Page

The hollow tree found Ada before Ada found it, which was how the best shelters always worked—revealing themselves only to someone too tired to be looking. It stood a half-mile back from the weir, off the warden path, an oak so old that lightning or rot had gutted its heart centuries ago and the tree had simply gone on living around the absence, bark sealing over the wound in slow gray ridges. The opening faced south, away from the river, away from the road. Inside, the cavity was wide enough for a person to sit with their knees drawn up, back against wood that smelled of rain and old smoke and something faintly sweet, like the ghost of sap.

Ada had lost the pack at the weir. That was the arithmetic they kept returning to as the light failed: one pack, containing the tent, the spirit stove, most of the food, the spare socks, the trowel wrapped in oilcloth that had been Ingrid's before it was anyone's. What remained was what Ada carried on their body—the ledger fragment folded into eight squares inside their shirt, the photograph, a knife, a water bottle, half a loaf of bread gone hard as chalk, and a handful of shellfish dug from the mudflats below the weir in the last gray hour before dark, eaten raw because there was no fire to cook them. The shells were still in their pocket. They could feel the edges through the canvas when they shifted.

Cold came up out of the ground the way truth came up out of water—slowly, then all at once.

Ada pulled their knees tighter and unfolded the ledger page by the light of a storm match, cupping the flame the way their mother had taught them never to do, because cupping a flame meant you cared more about keeping it than reading by it, and Ingrid Eriksen had held that a researcher should always be willing to lose the light. *Read fast,* she used to say over excavation trenches at dusk. *The dark is also data.*

The page was heavy stock, cream-colored, water-stained along one edge where decades of damp had bloomed it brown. The header read, in copperplate so fine it looked printed:

**RIVERGATE TRUST — ANNUAL DISBURSEMENTS — REGISTER OF CONSENT**

And beneath that, columns. Names. Sums. Dates going back further than Ada had expected, further than made comfortable sense.

*1893.*

Ada had assumed—had built the whole architecture of their anger on the assumption—that the Trust was recent, a development consortium, the same bland corporate muscle that bought up coastlines everywhere. But 1893 was three generations deep. 1893 was great-grandparents. 1893 was the year the maps changed.

They read.

---

*Halvard, Maren — £4 — accepted relocation — paid in full, Michaelmas.*
*Osei, Thomas & family (6) — £11 — accepted relocation — paid in full.*
*Crowe, Edith — refused first offer (£3) — second offer (£9) accepted relocation — paid in full, Lady Day.*
*Bellamy, Josiah — declined all offers. Note: see Appendix F.*

Appendix F did not exist on this page. Whatever had happened to Josiah Bellamy, who declined all offers, lived somewhere else in some other file, and Ada sat in the hollow of a dying tree feeling the cold come through their coat and understood, with the particular clarity that exhaustion grants, that *see Appendix F* was how a sentence said *we made him disappear* without ever having to say it.

The sums were obscene. Not large—obscene in their smallness. Four pounds. Eleven. Nine, after Edith Crowe held out for a season. Ada tried to make the numbers mean anything. A house. A field under plow for a hundred years. A churchyard where your dead lay in rows you could name. Four pounds. You could not buy a good boat for four pounds. You could barely buy a winter's coal.

And these weren't wages. Wages were written differently; Ada had catalogued enough estate ledgers in their mother's shadow to know the grammar of labor from the grammar of surrender. These entries said *accepted relocation*, and the phrase repeated down the column like a litany, like a prayer, like the response spoken in a church whose congregation had been sold its own pews:

*Accepted relocation. Accepted relocation. Accepted relocation.*

Page after page of it—or rather, this one page, but the handwriting continued past the torn edge, and the dates continued, and Ada turned the fragment over and found the record resumed on the reverse, and here was the thing that stopped their breath:

The payments didn't stop in 1893. They didn't stop in 1900, or 1920, or after either war. They ran on. Same names, generation after generation—Halvard, Osei, Crowe, Bellamy appearing again and again like a tide table—smaller sums now, adjusted for currency, marked not *relocation* anymore but *maintenance*. Maintenance. As if the families were bridges. As if the silence itself required annual upkeep, like pointing a roof, like dredging a channel.

*Halvard, R. — maintenance — £2/10 — paid in full.*

Bryn Halvard. Or Bryn Halvard's father. Or her grandmother Maren, whose name sat at the top of the whole rotten column, first family on the list, four pounds for everything under heaven.

Ada thought of Bryn's face on the ferry deck, watching too carefully, asking no questions. Thought: *she takes their money.* And then, immediately, the correction arriving like a slap: *four pounds. Nine. Two-ten. What would you have done?*

That was the mechanism. Ada saw it now, saw it whole, the way you finally see the shape of a fish trap when you're already inside it. The Trust hadn't needed violence—not much of it, not after the beginning. It needed only arithmetic applied to desperation, year after year, until the paying out became as natural as rain and the taking became as shameful as theft, so that no one downstream ever spoke of it, because to speak of it was to admit you'd been bought, and no one could afford that admission. The valley hadn't been conquered. It had been *purchased in installments*, and every installment had been small enough to swallow, and the swallowing had become inheritance.

---

The match died. Ada struck another, hands not quite steady.

Outside, the river spoke to itself in the dark, that low continuous syllable of water over stone that you stopped hearing after a while and then heard again all at once, the way you stop and start hearing your own heartbeat. Somewhere upstream, the weir held back whatever the wardens held back. Ada thought about the drowned steeple of Old Merrow rising out of the fog, bells chiming without wind, and thought about the word *maintenance*, and felt the two facts click together like strata settling after a dig.

Villages didn't drown themselves. Rivers didn't rise on schedule. Someone had decided where the water would go, and someone had paid the people to stop saying otherwise, and the ledgers—the beautiful, meticulous, self-satisfied ledgers—recorded it all in copperplate because the men who wrote them had never imagined a future in which anyone would read them as evidence. That was the arrogance of archives. They preserved everything, including guilt, certain that guilt would only ever be read as bookkeeping.

Ada's mother had spent her life teaching students to read material culture—the humble record, the discarded thing, the object that outlived its owner's intentions. *The archive lies,* Ingrid liked to say, *but the midden tells the truth.* And here Ada sat inside a tree, holding a piece of the lie, understanding for the first time why Ingrid had left. Why she'd walked out of a career, a house, a child, chasing a river's source. She hadn't abandoned them. She'd followed the same instinct Ada had followed into the cave at low tide: the professional refusal to let a false record stand.

It didn't forgive her. Ada held onto that with both hands, the way you hold a rail in rough water. It explained her; it did not excuse her. A person could be right about a river and wrong about a daughter, and both facts stayed true at once, and grief was just the space between them.

But it did mean Ada wasn't crazy. It did mean the cave, the toll-markers, the lantern stamped with the Trust crest, the boot prints of whoever else had come looking—it all meant something, and the something had a paper trail, and the paper trail began in 1893 with four pounds and a woman named Maren Halvard accepting relocation.

*Accepted.* Ada mouthed the word. Even the language was the crime. Nobody accepts drowning. You accept a delivery; you accept an apology. What the families had done was submit, or endure, or grieve—and the ledger had renamed their grief *consent*, filed it under *Register of Consent*, and closed the book.

Ada folded the page back into its eight squares and put it inside their shirt, against the skin, where it seemed to give off a faint cold of its own, like a stone kept in a cellar.

---

Sleep came in shallow pulls. Twice Ada woke to sounds—the slap of water, the far-off engine note of a patrol boat on the night current—and twice lay still in the tree's hollow heart, listening the way diggers listen, sorting threat from wind. Once, between the second waking and dawn, they dreamed of their mother at a trench edge, offering a trowel handle-first, saying *read fast, the dark is also data*, and woke with wet eyes and no memory of crying, which was somehow worse than the alternative.

Dawn came up gray-green through the canopy, the light moving through leaves like it moved through shallow water. Ada ate the heel of the bread, chewing slowly, making it last, and took stock with the flat honesty of morning: hungry, cold, packless, hunted, and one page closer to knowing exactly what kind of machine owned the valley.

The question was no longer whether to go on. The question was what to do with knowing. Because knowledge, Ada realized, sitting in the wreckage of a tree older than the crime, was also a ledger entry. The Trust had paid the valley to forget. Every year, two pounds ten, maintenance of silence. Which meant that remembering was the one transaction the Trust had never budgeted for—and that anyone who carried the memory upstream, unreported, unpaid, was committing a kind of fraud against the whole arrangement.

Ada smiled at that, alone in the hollow, and it was not a warm smile.

Then, through the trees, down toward the water: the sound of oars. Unhurried. Familiar. The particular creak-and-dip rhythm of someone who had rowed these currents since childhood, and Ada's chest tightened with a suspicion they couldn't yet name—hope or warning, or both wearing each other's coats.

A voice carried across the morning water, low and carrying, calling up through the trees:

"Ada Eriksen. I know you're in there. Come out slow—I've brought tea, and I've brought something you'll want to see."

It was Bryn.
