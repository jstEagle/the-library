## 9. Bryn's Bargain

Bryn Halvard had been poling the Rivergate since she was nine years old, and in all those years she had learned one thing above every other: the river kept accounts. Not the way the Trust's ledgers kept them—ink and arithmetic and men in dry boots—but the way water keeps them, in silt and driftwood and the slow, patient settling of everything that ever fell into it. Sooner or later, the river presented its bill.

She had felt hers coming for three days now, ever since she'd poled the Eriksen kid upstream past the drowned steeple of Old Merrow and watched their face change when the bells chimed under the fog.

She found Ada's camp at dusk on the fourth day, in the willow brake below the second weir, where the river bent north and the ground went soft as old bread. She smelled the smoke before she saw it—green wood, badly dried, someone burning what they had instead of what they should—and she grounded her boat against the bank with the particular silence her grandmother had taught her, hull kissing mud without a sound.

Ada sat with their back against a willow trunk, knees drawn up, turning something over in their hands. A page. Even in the failing light Bryn knew what it was by the way they held it: carefully, the way you hold a thing that is either worth nothing or worth everything, and you haven't decided which.

"You're burning wet wood," Bryn said.

Ada didn't startle, which Bryn noted and filed away. The kid just looked up, face smudged, eyes red-rimmed but level. "You followed me."

"The river follows you. I just use it." She stepped off the boat and came up the bank, boots sinking. "You lost your pack at the weir."

"Trust wardens took it." Ada said it flatly, but Bryn heard the cost underneath—the particular hollowness of someone who has eaten their pride along with half their food. "Two of them. Polite as priests. They said the upper reaches were closed for maintenance."

"Maintenance." Bryn almost laughed. She sat down across the small fire, uninvited, and held her hands to the thin heat. "My grandmother used to say the Trust maintained the valley the way a coffin maintains a body. Everything kept exactly where it was put."

Ada looked at her then, really looked, and Bryn saw the question forming before it was spoken. She had been waiting for it. That was why she'd come.

"You knew I was coming upriver," Ada said. "At the ferry landing. You asked no questions, but you packed me extra rope. You told me which side of the weir the footing holds. And now you've tracked me two days through fen country to tell me about your grandmother." Their voice was careful, the care of someone who has recently learned that care is necessary. "Why?"

Bryn fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch. This was the part she had rehearsed on the water, alone, saying it to the current because the current doesn't flinch. Now, with a person in front of her—a young person, hollow-cheeked, holding a dead mother's ledger page like a talisman—the rehearsal meant nothing.

"Because I'm paid to know where strangers are on this river," she said. "And I never reported you."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the fens, a bittern boomed, that low gulping note like a bottle being emptied underwater.

"Paid," Ada repeated. "By whom?"

"Who do you think?" Bryn met their eyes. "The Rivergate Trust sends a man to my landing twice a year. Aldous Vane did it himself, the last four years, though before that it was some clerk from Gallowford. There's a book at the mill office with my name in it going back—" she calculated, and hated the arithmetic even now— "twenty-two years. Since I was nineteen and my boat needed a new hull and my grandmother needed medicine she couldn't afford." She turned the stick in the fire. "I report strangers on the river. Names if I have them, descriptions if I don't, direction of travel always. In return, the Trust leaves my mooring rights alone and my fuel costs appear to shrink. It's not a fortune. It's enough. That's worse, isn't it? Enough is what makes people complicit. Fortunes make them villains, and villains get caught."

Ada hadn't moved. The ledger page had gone still in their hands. "You're telling me you're a spy."

"I'm telling you I'm a toll," Bryn said. "The Trust charges admission to this whole valley, and I'm one of the booths. There are others. The lock-keeper at Tarn Mill. The woman who runs the tea room at Gallowford bridge. Old Petch, who claims to be blind and sees everything on the towpath. We're not an organization. We're a habit. The Trust learned a hundred years ago that you don't need soldiers if everyone downstream owes you something."

"And me." Ada's voice had gone very quiet. "You reported me."

"No."

"Why not?" It came out sharp, almost angry, and Bryn understood why. It was the anger of a person who had built a wall around themselves brick by brick—invisibility, anonymity, the shuttered boathouse, the shell middens dug at dawn before the town woke—and discovered that the wall had a door in it all along, and someone had simply chosen not to open it. Being spared by accident was luck. Being spared deliberately was being *seen*, and being seen was dangerous, and being seen kindly was unbearable.

Bryn answered honestly, because anything else would have been an insult to both of them.

"At first? Because you were nobody. A digger kid buying rope and hardtack, heading upstream into country where strangers vanish. Reporting you would have been like reporting a heron." She smiled without much humor. "Then you came back from Old Merrow looking like you'd seen your own grave, and I thought: this one's after something the Trust buried. And I thought about my grandmother, Sefia Halvard, born in Old Merrow in 1881, drowned out of her own village at eleven years old by men with charts and dynamite, who spent seventy years in Gallowford never once saying the village's name aloud except when she was dying, and then she wouldn't stop saying it." The firelight moved on her face. "So no. I didn't report you. Twenty-two years of taking their money, and the first time it mattered, I kept my mouth shut. I don't know what that makes me. Probably nothing good."

* * *

They sat with it a while. The fen darkened around them, and the sounds came out—the water ticking against the reeds, a distant dog, the great soft breathing of the marsh. Bryn got bread and smoked eel from her boat and they ate without ceremony, and Ada ate the way hungry careful people eat, making it last, and Bryn pretended not to notice and set the second portion down within reach.

"My mother's name is in that ledger," Ada said finally. "Ingrid Eriksen. Dated three weeks ago. She was in the cave below the cliffs, and then she was here—" they tapped the page— "asking about Headwaters House. And then she wasn't anywhere." A pause. "You knew her. When she came through. You ferried her."

"I ferried her as far as Tarn Mill," Bryn said. "She walked from there. She asked me about the Headwaters House and I told her what everyone tells strangers: it's private land, it's not on any map worth trusting, and the road that goes there doesn't exist officially so neither does the road back." She shook her head slowly. "She thanked me. Paid double. Stood on the deck a moment looking upstream like she was reading something written on the water. Then she went." Bryn looked at the fire. "That's the last I saw of her. But I'll tell you what I've wondered every day since. The Trust pays me to watch this river. Nothing moves on it that I don't hear about inside a day. And Dr. Ingrid Eriksen—famous, recognizable, asking loud questions in Gallowford—walked up to the source and disappeared, and the Trust says nothing, and Vane comes sniffing down to the coast asking about shoreline finds." She let that sit. "They know where she is, Ada. Or they know what happened to her. And they're not looking, because you only search for something you've lost."

Ada's jaw tightened. Bryn watched them swallow whatever rose in their throat, watched them fold it down and store it somewhere deep, the way her grandmother had folded down seventy years of grief until it killed her quietly. These Eriksens, she thought. They carry things like pack animals and call it strength.

"So what do you want?" Ada asked. "You came all this way. You confessed. People don't confess for free."

"No," Bryn agreed. "We don't." She wiped her hands on her trousers and made herself say it plainly, because the plainness was the only honesty she had left to offer. "Here's my bargain. My boat can take you to Gallowford in a day and a half, down the fen channels the wardens don't watch because they think the fens are empty. They're not empty. They're full of us—people the Trust pays too little and watches too closely, people who remember songs about villages that officially never existed. I can get you to Gallowford, get you to whoever you need, stand between you and trouble as far as my word counts for anything. Which is more than it used to be, but less than you need."

"And in exchange."

"I want to see the ledger. All of it. Every page you have." Bryn's voice stayed steady but her hands didn't; she pressed them flat against her knees. "Twenty-two years I've taken their coin and told myself it was survival, that survival is clean because staying alive is neutral. Then you show up carrying proof that the Trust emptied five villages and wrote the drowning into law, and I find out my grandmother's village wasn't lost—it was *filed*. Catalogued. Paid off annually like a debt someone was proud of servicing." She looked at the ledger page in Ada's hands, at the columns of figures she could almost read by firelight. "I need to see it. Not for the Trust. For me. For Sefia Halvard, who died in a town that wasn't hers, believing her village was a dream she'd invented to survive losing it. If I'm going to spend whatever's left of my name helping you, I need to know exactly what I'm spending it on."

Ada was silent a long time. The fire burned low. Bryn let the silence be; she'd spent her life around water and water taught you that pressure applied too fast cracks what it means to move.

"It's evidence," Ada said at last. "Against them. If you look at it, you're in it. Whatever happens to me happens to you. Vane already came to my boathouse wearing gloves and a smile. He'll come to your landing next, and he won't wear gloves."

"He's been coming to my landing for twenty-two years," Bryn said. "He just didn't know he was visiting a spy who'd turned." She shrugged. "You're not offering me danger, Eriksen. I've been marinating in it since before you were born. You're offering me a reason for it. Those aren't the same thing, and I've waited a long time for the second one."

Ada looked down at the ledger fragment. Firelight moved across the paper, across the faded ink, across the names of families who had accepted relocation, and Bryn saw the kid's thumb press against the edge of the page—not reading, holding. Anchoring.

"There's a page about Old Merrow," Ada said quietly. "Third entry. Payments beginning 1893. The amounts go up the year the church bell stops ringing in the record." They looked up. "Your grandmother's family name might be in it. I haven't been able to make myself check mine yet. I understand if you can't either."

"I can," Bryn said, and found that it was true, and that the truth of it hurt in the specific way of a splinter coming out—pain that is also relief. "That's the difference between us right now, maybe. You're still hoping your mother left you treasure. I stopped hoping for anything from that family of money a long time ago. All I want is the truth, and I want it in ink, in their handwriting, dated and signed, so that no one—not Vane, not the clerks, not me in my worst doubting hours—can call it a story."

"Stories are what we have," Ada said. "Someone told me the source wants to be understood, not found. Maybe the same is true of the past."

"Maybe," Bryn said. "But understanding travels faster by boat."

She stood, joints cracking, and offered her hand across the dead fire. Ada looked at it—at the rope-scarred palm, the missing half-finger, the hand of a woman who had sold twenty-two years of small betrayals to buy a boat and a quiet life—and then took it. Her grip was strong. Theirs was desperate. Between those two grips, something was agreed that neither of them would have signed in daylight.

"We leave before first light," Bryn said. "There's a channel behind the fen islands the dredgers can't follow, and a woman at the far end who owes me and hates the Trust more than she fears them, which these days is the only currency worth anything. Sleep. Eat the rest of the eel. And Ada—" she paused at her boat, one hand on the gunwale— "the page you couldn't make yourself check. Bring it tomorrow. Whatever name is in it, you shouldn't have to be the only one who reads it."

She pushed off into the dark water and let the current take her a little way downstream, to where she could sleep with one eye on the willow brake and the other on the black mirror of the fen. Above her, stars came out between clouds, indifferent and clean. Below her hull, somewhere in the cold dark, the river carried on its long accounting, patient as ever, adding column to column.

And in the morning, when she poled back to collect her passenger, Ada was waiting on the bank with the ledger pages wrapped in oilcloth against their chest—and behind them, far down the fen track, a lantern was moving. Steady. Deliberate. Following the channel toward Gallowford, the way a man walks when he already knows where he is going and is in no hurry, because everything ahead of him already belongs to him.

Bryn counted the lanterns. There were two.

"Get in the boat," she said. "Now."
