## 10. Gallowford's Polite Hunger

Gallowford announced itself by smell before sight: cut timber, wet stone, and beneath both, faint as a watermark, the sweetness of Trust money. Ada came down off the fens' last ridge in late afternoon light, boots crusted with dried mud, pack lighter than it should have been, and stopped where the road bent to look.

The town sat folded into a bend of the Rivergate like something the river had set down and decided to keep. Mills lined the water in a long gray row, their wheels turning slow and patient, each one flying a small green pennant from its gable—the crest of the Rivergate Trust, that same interlocked circle-and-wave Ada had seen stamped into rusted tin and embossed on cream paper. The pennants were everywhere. On the church. On the schoolhouse. On the lampposts, little fabric flags that turned lazily in the wind, so that walking down the high street would be like walking through a procession of small green hands waving hello, hello, hello.

*Prosperity through stewardship*, said the sign at the town boundary, freshly painted, the letters gold-leafed enough to catch the low sun.

Ada pulled the collar of their coat up and became someone else.

It was easier than it should have been. Years of living at the edges of towns had taught them how people read strangers: they looked at the hands first, then the eyes, then the story. So Ada had rubbed graphite dust into their fingertips until they looked like a person who handled instruments all day, and they carried Bryn's borrowed leather tube slung across their back the way a surveyor carries one, and when the first man on the road—a millhand with sawdust in his eyebrows—nodded and asked if they'd come about the weir works, Ada heard themselves say, "Aye. Upper reaches," in a voice flat and bored and entirely convincing.

The lie settled over them like a coat that fit too well.

*You hate this*, they reminded themselves, walking on. *Notice that you hate this.* But even as they noticed, some efficient part of their mind was already cataloguing: which windows had curtains drawn against gossip, which doorways held watchers, which of the millhands glanced toward the largest building—Trust House, obviously, granite-faced and three stories tall, standing at the head of the high street like a headstone for something not yet dead.

* * *

The inn was called the Weir & Wheel, and it was warm inside in the way of places that could afford peat without counting it. Ada paid for a room with coins counted out slowly, apologetically, the way a working surveyor would count them, and asked for bread and cheese and whatever stew was going, and ate alone at a corner table with their back to the wall and their eyes doing quiet work.

The taproom filled as the mills let out. Ada listened the way they dug a midden—carefully, in layers, letting the loose material fall away from what held shape underneath.

Layer one: ordinary talk. Prices of wool. A wedding. Someone's cow.

Layer two: the Trust, mentioned the way weather is mentioned. The new sluice gates downstream—"fixed quick, I'll say that for them." A bonus paid before winter. A widow rehoused after her cottage took water. Gratitude, mostly genuine, worn smooth by repetition.

Layer three, deeper, quieter, spoken only when the speaker thought no one listened: the old names. A woman at the bar telling the landlord, in the tone of a joke that wasn't one, that her son had asked why the family prayed for "the drowned ones" every Michaelmas when there hadn't been a flood here in living memory. The landlord's answer, low: "Because we're not fools, Nell, whatever else we are."

And then, threaded through all of it, the thing Ada had come for—or rather, the absence of it. No one mentioned a woman archaeologist. No one mentioned Dr. Eriksen. Which meant either she hadn't passed through, or she had passed through quietly, or—

"Surveyor, are you?"

Ada turned. A man had taken the stool beside theirs without seeming to arrive. Fifty-ish, comfortable, with the kind of face that had been chosen for him by committee: pleasant, forgettable, with eyes that were none of those things. He wore good wool and no pennant, which itself was a kind of uniform.

"For the Trust?" he added, when Ada didn't answer fast enough.

"Aye." Ada kept the boredom in their voice like ballast. "Upper reaches. Water levels."

"Mm." He ordered a cider he didn't drink. "Funny season for it."

"Funny season for everything."

That earned a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Name's Corrigan. I do a bit of liaison work. If you find anything odd up there—old stonework, boundaries that don't match the maps—you'll report it, of course. Standard practice."

"Standard practice," Ada agreed.

"There was another one through here." Corrigan turned his glass a quarter-turn, watching the room in its reflection rather than directly. "Woman. Older. Very thorough, by all accounts. Asking after the upper valley. Headwaters House, specifically." He paused, delicately. "You wouldn't know her?"

The name of their mother rose in Ada's throat like floodwater. They swallowed it down and shrugged. "I work alone."

"Mm," Corrigan said again, and Ada understood that the sound was the whole conversation—that *mm* meant *I don't believe you* and also *I'm not going to say so yet* and also *we will speak again*. He left coins on the bar, more than the cider cost, nodded pleasantly to the room at large, and went out into the dark.

Ada sat very still and finished their stew, tasting nothing, and thought: *she was here. Weeks ago. Asking exactly what I'm asking. And they've been asking behind me ever since.*

* * *

The rumors came easier after that, because Ada learned the shape of the asking.

They spent two days in Gallowford being boring. They walked the towpath with a notebook, making marks that meant nothing, measuring things that needed no measuring. They chatted with lock-keepers about gauge readings. And everywhere, in the gaps between talk, the town gave up its pieces.

From a ferryman's boy, hauled out of the water and scolded within an inch of his life while Ada waited nearby: Headwaters House stood a week's walk upstream, past the salmon ladders, where the valley narrowed to a throat. Nobody went there. Nobody *wanted* to go there. The house had its own people, its own lights, and once a year a cart came down with salt fish and went back up with crates that nobody asked about, because asking was a habit the town had been gently cured of.

From the landlady at the Weir & Wheel, while she changed Ada's linens and talked because Ada didn't stop her: the woman surveyor had stayed in room four. Paid cash. Kept odd hours. Left early one morning without settling her account—which wasn't like her, the landlady said, because she'd seemed the careful sort—and the Trust had covered the bill anyway, unasked, which the landlady found "polite in a way I didn't care for."

From the blacksmith, over a repaired buckle: that the mills ran on schedules set from Headwaters House. That the water level in the whole valley was set from Headwaters House. That his grandfather used to say the river used to have a different name, before the Trust, but he couldn't remember what it was, only that his grandfather had gone quiet whenever he tried.

*Emrys*, Ada thought, hearing Bryn's voice say it. *The river had a name, and they took the name along with everything else.*

On the second evening, Ada bought a round for the taproom—cheap insurance, expensive habit—and let the room loosen around them. An old millwright, three ciders deep, leaned close and confided that his father had worked a season at Headwaters House as a joiner, back in the twenties, and had come home thin and strange and never spoke of it except once, drunk, when he said the house had more rooms below than above, and that the below-rooms weren't cellars, they were *kept*, the way you keep documents or prisoners or anything else you mean to hold onto.

"And your father?" Ada asked. "What happened to him?"

"He got better," the millwright said. "Got better the moment he stopped asking questions. That's the cure for most things here." He tapped the side of his nose, winked, and wandered off into the warm fog of his own evening.

Ada sat with the words and felt the cat-and-mouse of it all tightening around their ribs. Ingrid had been here. Ingrid had asked these questions, slept in that bed upstairs, walked this street wearing whatever disguise she'd chosen, and somewhere behind her—behind *them* both now—Corrigan and his kind moved at their own pace, unhurried, certain, closing like water finding its level.

Ada finished the cider and went up to room four.

* * *

The room was small and clean and faced the river. Ada closed the door, set the candle on the sill, and did what they'd been wanting to do since the landlady's gossip: searched it.

Not frantically. Methodically, the way they'd dig a test pit—in layers, topsoil first. The mattress seams. Under the floorboard by the window, loose, replaced slightly wrong. Nothing. The fireplace chimney, cold. Nothing. Ada was kneeling at the wardrobe, running fingers along its inner walls, when they felt it: a faint irregularity under the paint, near the bottom, where the wood swelled with damp.

Pencil. Scratched deep, then painted over by some conscientious owner—but the grooves caught light when the candle angled right.

Ada fetched the candle close and read.

Lines. Precise, confident, drafted by a hand that had drawn a thousand site plans: a rough map fragment showing the river above Gallowford, the valley narrowing, and—set apart from the river itself, connected by a dotted line—a rectangle labeled in small capitals: **H.H.** Below the rectangle, a second line curved away eastward into the wardrobe's corner and ended in a mark Ada recognized from their mother's field notes, the symbol she used for *access*: a culvert, a way in.

And beside it all, one word, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn the wood:

**ASK THE BELL-KEEPER.**

Ada knelt there a long time, candle trembling, reading their mother's handwriting the way you'd read a letter from the dead.

She'd been here. She'd sat in this exact spot, in this exact light, hiding a map in the only place she could be sure no one would repaint before spring. She'd known she might not come back for it. She'd left it anyway—for whoever followed. For *her*, maybe. Some part of Ingrid Eriksen, however buried under years of silence and distance, had believed her child might one day come looking.

Ada put their forehead against the cool painted wood and breathed until the burning behind their eyes receded. Not yet. There would be time for grief later, or there wouldn't; either way it wouldn't help tonight. Tonight there was a bell-keeper to find, and a culvert to trace, and a house full of someone's careful cruelty waiting a week upstream.

They straightened, memorized the lines until they could see them with their eyes shut, and blew out the candle.

In the dark, the river murmured past the window, steady and patient, carrying its stolen name toward the sea. Somewhere downstairs, a door opened and closed softly—too softly, at an hour when honest guests were asleep—and footsteps crossed the taproom below with the unhurried confidence of a man who had already decided how this would end.

Ada lay still on top of the covers, listening, and understood that Corrigan had not come to knock.

He had come to confirm which room she'd slept in.

And tomorrow, when Ada walked out of Gallowford wearing their surveyor's boredom like armor, they would walk out ahead of the answer—because the question had just become a race, and the bell-keeper, whoever she was, would only stay hidden for those who asked politely.
