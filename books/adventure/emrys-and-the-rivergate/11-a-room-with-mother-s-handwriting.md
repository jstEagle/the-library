## 11. A Room With Mother's Handwriting

Gallowford announced itself the way all mill-towns do: by sound before sight. Ada heard it from the bend above the weir—a low, patient grinding, like a beast chewing stone—and then the smell arrived on the wind, wet flour and machine oil and woodsmoke braided together into something that was not unpleasant but was unmistakably *industrial*. The river narrowed here, funneled between stone embankments into a race of white water that turned wheel after wheel after wheel, and every wheel fed a building, and every building belonged, one way or another, to the Trust.

Ada had walked most of two days since losing the pack at the weir, hungry in the way that sharpens rather than dulls, wearing boots gone soft at the seams. Bryn's coin—three pieces of it, pressed into their palm at the last landing with instructions to say nothing about where they'd come from—sat heavy in their pocket. *Buy a bed,* she'd said. *Eat something hot. You look like something the river coughed up.* Then she'd poled back downstream toward Gallowford's lower quay, where her boat could pass without questions, promising to find them when she found them, which was Bryn's way of saying she would be watching whether or not she appeared.

The town itself rose along both banks in tiers of grey slate and brick stained orange by decades of smoke. It was handsome in the way a well-fed animal is handsome—healthy, groomed, and entirely aware of its own comfort. Window boxes overflowed. The gutters ran clean. Children in good coats chased each other across a bridge built of pale granite, and on the bridge's parapet, set into the stone like a seal into wax, gleamed the crest Ada now knew too well: the interlocked R and T, the motto beneath it worn nearly smooth by hands touching it in passing.

*Flumina servantur* — the rivers are kept.

Ada touched it too, briefly, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it still hurts.

The inn sat at the top of the high street, three storeys of black timber and white plaster called the Weir's Rest, its sign creaking on iron brackets shaped like leaping fish. Inside, the air was warm and close, thick with peat smoke and the smell of roast mutton that made Ada's stomach clench so hard they nearly doubled over. They hadn't eaten properly since the hollow tree.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with forearms like hams and an expression of professional neutrality that Ada recognized instantly as the local dialect of caution. Her eyes did the arithmetic of Ada's appearance—mud, salt-stiff clothes, no luggage, ferry-woman's coins—in about four seconds.

"Room?" she asked.

"Room," Ada said. "And whatever's hot."

"Surveyor, are you? We've had surveyors through." Something flickered behind the neutrality—not hostility, exactly. Attention.

"Hydrographic," Ada said, which had the advantage of being almost true. "Mapping flow rates for the Trust." The lie came out smooth and easy and Ada hated it, hated how little friction it met coming out of their mouth, hated that Vane's visit had taught them this fluency. The innkeeper nodded, satisfied or pretending to be, and pushed a ledger across the counter.

"Name?"

"Eriksen." Ada watched the pen hover. "First name Aldous," they added, because if the name was going to be spoken aloud in this town anyway, better it should be spoken wrong.

The room they were given was number six, at the back, overlooking the yard. Ada carried up bread and stew on a tray, ate standing at the window like an animal that didn't trust its surroundings, and told themselves firmly that the job—the only job—was to ask about Headwaters House in the morning, quietly, in the way surveyors ask things, and be gone before Vane's people caught the scent.

They did not expect room four.

* * *

It happened the second evening. Ada had spent the day doing genuine, tedious fieldwork as cover—pacing the embankments with a borrowed measuring tape, sketching the race, letting the word *surveyor* settle over them like camouflage—while gathering rumors the way driftwood gathers at a piling. Ingrid had been here. Everyone agreed on that much, though no one agreed on anything else. A tall woman, grey braid, foreign-sounding name. Asked after Headwaters House at the chandler's. Again at the wool merchant's. Bought maps from the stationer, old ones, the kind kept in drawers under the counter for collectors. And always, everywhere, the same question trailing behind her like a dog: *did she say where she was going? Did anyone see which road she took?*

Two men in good boots and bad manners had been asking the same questions since Tuesday. Ada saw one of them from across the market square and felt the skin of their scalp tighten. They turned down a side lane, walked without hurry, and let the crowd of market-day swallow them.

Which is how they came to be back at the Weir's Rest at mid-afternoon, with hours to kill and nowhere safe to stand still, and heard the innkeeper telling her pot-girl that room four wanted fresh linens before Thursday's party arrived.

Room four.

Ada stood in the corridor outside it for a long moment, hand raised to knock on a door where no one would answer, feeling foolish and frightened and drawn forward all at once, like a person wading into cold water by inches. Ingrid had stayed here. Ingrid had slept here, weeks ago, while Ada was digging middens and not writing letters and telling themselves the silence between them was permanent and fine.

The door wasn't locked. Inn doors in working towns rarely were, between guests.

The room was small and clean and utterly ordinary: iron bedstead, washstand, a chair with a rush seat, a window looking out over the high street where the evening light was beginning to go amber. Someone else's suitcase stood against the wall—Thursday's party had left it early, or the innkeeper stored things there. Ada closed the door softly behind them and simply breathed for a moment, breathing in a room their mother had breathed in, knowing it and not knowing it, the way you can stand in a field where someone once stood and feel nothing and everything.

Then they saw the wardrobe.

It was a plain thing, pine, older than the inn's current paint, with a rail inside and a shelf above and a mirror on the door that had gone cloudy at the edges like breath on glass. Ada opened it to hang up their coat—an ordinary gesture, housekeeping, nothing more—and the light from the window fell across the inside of the left-hand panel.

Pencil marks.

Faint, silver-grey, the graphite half-worn by time and oilcloth, but unmistakable to a person who had grown up watching that exact hand draw site grids in the margins of newspapers. Ada knew their mother's drafting the way they knew their own heartbeat: the small confident arrows, the north-point rendered as a single elegant stroke, the habit of labeling distances in chains rather than meters because Ingrid Eriksen had learned surveying from a man born in 1901 and never saw reason to update.

There was a map fragment drawn directly on the wood.

Not large—perhaps the size of a spread hand—but dense. A section of waterway, rendered in Ingrid's quick sure lines: a river bending east around a shaded block labeled *GF* — Gallowford — then continuing north into country marked with hatchwork that meant fen or marsh. Two features were circled. The first, downstream of the town, was annotated *weir — warded*, with a tiny skull beside it that would have been grimly funny if Ada hadn't lost their pack climbing past that very weir. The second circle sat well upstream, past the fens, where the map fragment ended raggedly as if torn—or as if Ingrid had run out of wall.

Beside the second circle, in letters smaller than the rest, pressed hard enough into the pine to leave grooves:

*Headwaters House.*

And below that, underlined twice, the words that stopped Ada's breath:

*Ask the bell-keeper.*

Ada read it four times. Five. Their fingers hovered over the marks without touching, the way they'd been trained never to touch a surface before photographing it, except there was no camera, no kit, no protocol—only a wardrobe in a rented room and their mother's handwriting speaking to them across weeks of silence, from wherever she was, or wasn't.

*Ask the bell-keeper.*

Gallowford had a church—Ada had seen its squat tower from the embankment, wedged between the mill offices and the Trust's granary like an afterthought. Churches had bells. Bells had keepers. It was the simplest sentence in the world and it rearranged everything, because it meant Ingrid had known she was being followed, had known her notes might be searched, had chosen the one hiding place no searcher would think to open: the inside of a wardrobe in a town full of wardrobes. Had trusted the finding of it to chance, or to Ada specifically—to the one person alive who would recognize that hand, who would open that door, who would understand that *ask the bell-keeper* was not a suggestion but a route, a key, a message in a bottle thrown backward through time.

She had been leaving signs for them. Or signs for whoever came after. The distinction mattered less than Ada expected.

They copied the map into the back pages of their notebook, line for line, arrow for arrow, pressing hard enough to make their own grooves. They measured nothing, photographed nothing; they simply memorized, the way diggers memorize a section before the tide takes it. Then they closed the wardrobe door gently, the way you close a coffin or a letter, and stood in the middle of the small clean room while the light went from amber to grey.

They should leave. Every instinct honed since Vane's visit said: don't sleep where you've been found, don't return to a place twice, don't give the pattern a shape. The men in good boots were asking questions within a quarter mile of this building.

But the bed was right there. The iron bedstead with its thin mattress and starched sheet, and somewhere underneath the freshness of new linens, Ada could not stop thinking, was the ghost-shape of where their mother had lain, and what dreams she'd had, and whether she had slept at all or lain awake drawing maps on furniture in the dark.

Ada paid the innkeeper again for the night—"changed my mind about the back room, prefer the street"—and watched her face for suspicion and found none, or none she showed. Then they climbed the stairs to room four and shut the door and did not leave it until morning.

* * *

Sleep came slowly and left early.

Ada lay in the dark listening to Gallowford's night sounds: the mills running on, because the mills always ran; a drunk singing somewhere near the bridge, badly, a song with a rising chorus that snagged at Ada's ear with its familiarity before the wind took it; and beneath everything, fainter than all of it, the sound of the river being held—gates and sluices and wheels, the constant negotiated mutter of water that was not permitted to run as it wished.

In the morning they would find the bell-keeper. In the morning they would follow the penciled arrow north into the fens. In the morning the fear would be useful again, converted into motion. But the night was long, and the night was for the other thing.

Ada lay on their side facing the wardrobe and let themselves remember, which they had not permitted in years, because remembering was a door that opened outward onto a drop.

Their mother had smelled of chalk and pipe tobacco she never smoked herself—she bought it for the smell, claimed it cleared her head. She had a way of kneeling at a trench edge that put her whole weight on her heels, hands loose between her knees, utterly still, watching soil the way other people watched fire. She had taught Ada to read stratigraphy before teaching them to ride a bicycle. *"The ground keeps every appointment,"* she used to say. *"It's only people who break them."*

And then she had broken every appointment there was. The missed birthdays blurred into missed years. The postcards stopped. The last phone call—Ada had been twenty-two, furious, fluent in the language of grievance—ended with Ada saying something they could not now reconstruct and could never unsay, and Ingrid saying, quietly, in the voice she used for findings she was certain of: *"You'll understand when you see what I've seen."*

Ada had hung up on her.

That was the fact that lived at the bottom of everything, smooth and dark as a stone in a pool. Not that Ingrid left—Ada had rehearsed that grievance so long it had worn thin, translucent, almost comfortable. But that the last thing their mother ever heard from them was the dial tone.

The tears came without drama, which was somehow worse. No sobbing, no heaving—just a slow leak, a warm sting at the corners of their eyes that became a steady overflow running sideways into the pillow, while Ada lay rigid and stared at the wardrobe and thought: *she was in this bed. She drew those arrows. She underlined it twice because she wanted me to find it, she believed I would find it, she trusted me with a route through the fens after I hung up on her, after five years of nothing, she—*

They wept for their mother in her own bed, in a stranger's room, in a town that belonged to the people who had taken her, and the crying had no dignity in it at all and no performance either. It was just the body finally doing what the body had refused to do for years, the floodgate cracking the way old gates crack—suddenly, all at once, and then in long shuddering pulses that seemed to come from below the floor of the self.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Ada realized they weren't only grieving. Grief alone doesn't sharpen; it dissolves. But what was moving through them had a cutting edge to it, cold and bright. Because the ledger page in their coat—the one naming villages that officially never existed—was real. The boot prints in the cave were real. The lantern with the Trust crest was real, and Vane's courteous offer was real, and the men in good boots were real, and somewhere upstream, past the fens, their mother had circled a house and pressed her pencil so hard into pine that the grooves would outlast them both.

*Ask the bell-keeper.*

Whatever Ingrid had found, it had cost her everything—her freedom, maybe her life, certainly her daughter. She had not sent money. She had not sent apologies. She had sent a map.

That was an apology, Ada understood, lying in the dark with a soaked pillow. That was the only kind their mother had ever been capable of making: not words, but a way through. *Here is the ground. Here is the route. Keep your weight on your heels. Watch the soil.*

The mills ground on. The drunk went quiet. Near dawn, the first cart rattled up the high street, and Ada rose, washed their face in the basin until the evidence was gone, and stood before the cloudy mirror looking at a face that was Ingrid Eriksen's face younger and harder-used, and said aloud, to the glass, to the room, to the woman who had slept here:

"I'm coming. And I'm bringing everything you found into the light."

It sounded less brave aloud than it had in their head. They said it anyway.

* * *

Downstairs, the inn was waking—smell of bacon, clatter of crockery, the pot-girl hauling ash. Ada ate standing at the counter, paid, and asked the question they'd prepared overnight, casual as a yawn:

"The church tower—I'm surveying sightlines. Who keeps the bell these days?"

The innkeeper's ladling hand paused, just slightly, over the porridge pot.

"Marta," she said. "Old Marta Fenwick. Half-blind as a mole and twice as stubborn." A beat, and then, with the careful flatness of a woman choosing not to know something: "You'll find her mornings at the tower. Evenings she sits by the river gate, feeding gulls." She looked up, and her professional neutrality cracked just enough to show something underneath—not warning, not encouragement. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who had cleaned room four's linens and seen what was written inside the wardrobe and said nothing for weeks.

"Mind how you go," the innkeeper added, turning back to her pots. "Town's full of strangers lately. Asking questions."

Outside, the morning was bright and cold, and the church tower stood up against it, grey and squat, its louvred windows dark. Ada crossed the high street with their notebook against their ribs like a second heartbeat, and as they passed the granite bridge they noticed, freshly chiseled over the worn old motto, a new plaque the Trust had mounted not long ago:

*THE RIVERGATE TRUST: ONE HUNDRED YEARS OF KEEPING THE VALLEY SAFE.*

One hundred years. Since 1893. Since the payments began.

Ada climbed the tower steps two at a time, and from somewhere above, before they even reached the bell chamber, came the sound of an old woman's voice—cracked, unhurried, singing something low and minor that made the hairs rise on Ada's arms, because beneath the words they couldn't yet make out, the melody was the same rising phrase the drunk had sung on the bridge last night.

The whole town knew it. The whole town had forgotten they knew it.

Ada stepped up into the light of the bell chamber, and the singing stopped, and a voice like dry leaves said: "I can hear you thinking, young Eriksen. Come in, and mind the rope."
