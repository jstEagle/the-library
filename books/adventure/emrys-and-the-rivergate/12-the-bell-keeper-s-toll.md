## 12. The Bell-Keeper's Toll

The bell tower of Gallowford stood where the town's three streets collided like arguments that had never been resolved—a squat stone finger rising from a knot of crooked houses, its shingled spire green with age, its bell mouth dark as a held breath. Ada had walked past it twice the day before, following the wardrobe map's pencil ghost in their mind, and both times had felt the building watching them back. Not the bell. The window at its base, small and square and curtained in faded blue, behind which someone or something seemed to keep its own kind of hours.

The woman who answered Ada's knock was smaller than her voice.

"I know why you're here," she said before Ada could speak, "because everyone who knocks on this door wants something, and you've got the look of someone who's been wanting for a long time." She was ancient in the way of river stones—worn smooth, dense, impossible to guess the age of. Her eyes were the trouble. The left one was clouded white as skimmed milk, but the right was sharp and brown and fixed on Ada with an accuracy that made the skin prickle. "Come in, then. Mind the step. It minds everybody."

* * *

Inside, the tower's ground floor was round and low-ceilinged, hung with ropes—not bell ropes, or not only; there were coils of hemp and sisal and one rope of braided leather gone dark with handling—and smelling of tallow, dust, and underneath it all, faintly, of river water. A stove ticked in the corner. Shelves climbed the curved walls, crowded with jars: nails, buttons, seeds, teeth, things Ada couldn't name in the dim light. And bells. Small ones, dozens of them, handbells and sheep-bells and ship's bells no bigger than teacups, arranged on every surface like a congregation waiting for a word.

"You're the bell-keeper," Ada said. "Marta."

"Marta Quill. Was. The Trust struck me off their rolls eleven years ago when I stopped being useful and started being old." She lowered herself into a chair by the stove with the economy of someone who had learned exactly how much body she had left and spent none of it extra. "Sit. You'll be the surveyor everyone's whispering about. The one asking after Headwaters House."

Ada kept their face still. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." Marta smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was not a fooled one either. "Surveyors don't sleep in room four of the Ferryman's Rest. Surveyors don't stand in the market holding a ledger page like it's a live coal. Surveyors—" she raised the good eye, "—don't have hands like yours. Digging hands. You've been in the mud your whole life, girl or boy or neither, I can't tell and don't care, but I can tell the earth knows you."

Ada looked down at their own hands as if seeing them through her eyes: the nails cut short, the callus ridge along the palm where the trowel sat, the old scar across the left knuckle from a shell midden six years ago. They thought of all the careful lies they'd built in Gallowford—the clipboard, the borrowed confidence, the flat professional voice—and how each one had been easy, and how much they hated that it had been easy.

"What do you want?" Ada asked. "Vane's people will come asking too. If they haven't already."

"They came in spring. A polite man with a polite envelope." Marta spat, delicately, into a brass spittoon that had clearly received many such tributes. "They wanted to know if anyone had come upriver asking about the source. I told them what I tell everyone: I keep a bell, I ring it for weddings and funerals and floods, and I'm too blind to see anything worth reporting." She leaned forward. "But you're not Vane's. Vane's smell of soap and certainty. You smell of—" she sniffed, theatrical, "—boathouse smoke and bad decisions. So. What do you want, digging-hands?"

"The way through the fens," Ada said. "To Headwaters House. My mother went that way and didn't come back."

Marta was quiet for a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the belfry louvres and made a sound almost like breathing.

"That's not what I asked," she said at last. "That's what you want. I asked what you want to give me. Nothing in this valley is free, child. The Trust taught us that lesson so well we teach it to ourselves now. Passage costs. Information costs. Even kindness has a price tag these days, though most folk have stopped reading them." She tapped the arm of her chair. "My toll is a story. A true one. Not the story you'd tell a warden, not the one you'd tell a ferry-woman to keep her from asking questions. The true one. About your mother, and about yourself, and why you're really walking up a river that has drowned better-prepared people than you."

"And if I lie?"

"Then I'll know, and you'll walk out of here with nothing but my blessing, which is worth precisely what you paid for it." She settled back. "And if you tell the truth, I'll know that too. Truth has a sound, did you know that? Like a bell. Lies ring thin. Truth rings long. Ring me something long, Eriksen."

Ada hadn't told her the name.

The realization landed cold, and Marta saw it land—the good eye glittering—and she laughed, a dry rustle like paper.

"Everyone in Gallowford knows Ingrid Eriksen's face," she said. "She stayed at the Rest five weeks ago, same room, same questions. And you've got her chin. Or she's got yours. One of those." Her voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "So. The truth. Take your time. The bell's not going anywhere. Neither am I. Probably."

* * *

Ada had rehearsed versions of themselves for so long that the rehearsal had worn grooves: the competent digger, the unbothered drifter, the person who had chosen distance rather than been exiled to it. They opened their mouth to begin one of those grooved stories and found, to their surprise, that nothing came out. The grooves were empty. Marta sat waiting with her ruined eye and her sharp one, and the stove ticked, and somewhere in the walls the old timber creaked like a ship, and Ada understood that they were going to have to build the sentence from raw timber, right here, in front of a stranger.

"My mother left when I was nineteen," they said.

It came out flat. They tried again.

"She left when I was nineteen, and I told everyone—including myself—that I didn't care. That she'd chosen her work over me the way she always did, that I was used to it, that I'd built a life she couldn't touch. I dug shell middens for tourists' pocket money and lived in a boathouse and called it freedom." Ada's hands had found each other in their lap and were gripping hard. "But that's not the truth either. The truth is I waited. For years. I checked the post even though I'd moved twice and never given her the address. I read her papers—I hated her papers, I read every one. When she won the Halloway Prize I got drunk alone in the boathouse and cried and told myself it was the cheap gin."

The words were coming faster now, pulled out of them like a splinter, and it hurt in exactly the way a splinter hurts—sharp first, then strangely relieving.

"Three weeks ago the tide went out wrong and showed me a cave under the cliffs, and inside it I found her lantern. Her photograph. Dated. Recent." Ada's throat closed. They pushed through it. "And I didn't cry then either. I catalogued the finds. I made a list. Because making lists is what I do instead of feeling things—it's the family trade, apparently, we file our griefs and index our dead—and I told myself I was going upriver for the archaeology. For the evidence. For the Trust's crimes." They looked up, and their eyes were burning, and they let them burn because looking away felt like another kind of lie. "But that's not why I'm here. I'm here because my mother is somewhere up this river, alive or dead, and I need to find her, and I need—" the word cracked coming out, "—I need her to explain. Why she went. Why she stayed gone. Why I wasn't enough of a reason to come home. And I hate that I need it. I hate that at twenty-six years old I'm still a kid standing at a window watching a road."

The silence afterward was enormous. The stove ticked. A jar lid contracted with a tiny ping. Ada became aware that their face was wet and did nothing about it.

Marta rose, slowly, joint by joint, and crossed to the shelves. Her fingers walked along the small bells without touching them, reading them like braille.

"There," she said softly. "There it is. Long ring." She turned back. "Do you know what I did, before I kept this bell? Fifty years ago? I was a sluice-keeper's daughter. Out past Old Merrow, before Merrow was Old." Her clouded eye caught the firelight and held it. "My father worked the gates for the Trust in the early years, when they still pretended the water was weather. He opened a gate one night in November because a man with a crest on his coat told him the downstream levels needed easing. My father couldn't read. The man read the order to him. What the order actually said, we learned later, was raise the upper weir. The water came into Merrow at two in the morning. We lost the church, the mill, and my brother Tomas, who was seven and slept lightest of any of us and woke first and went out to see what the noise was."

Ada couldn't speak. Marta didn't seem to require it.

"The Trust paid my father a relocation settlement and a pension he was too ashamed to spend. He died owing them his silence, and I inherited the debt, and for fifty years I paid it by keeping their bell and ringing what they told me to ring." She picked up one of the smallest bells—a dented thing of green bronze—and held it in her palm. "Then eleven years ago I found my father's copy of the real order, hidden in the church wall where he'd put it the night before the flood, because some part of him knew. And I stopped ringing their tunes." She set the little bell down with a click. "So when I say I know what a buried truth weighs, child, I'm weighing it in the only units I trust. Family units."

She came back to her chair and sat, and fixed Ada with the brown eye.

"Now. Your mother. Ingrid Eriksen came to me five weeks ago, and she paid the same toll you just paid, and her story rang true too, and I'll tell you what I told her, which is what I'll tell you." She leaned forward. "The fens between here and Headwaters House are not natural ground. Nothing in this valley is natural ground; you'll have worked that out by now if you're half the digger your hands say you are. The Trust dredges them every season and calls it maintenance, and what they're really doing is keeping the water high and the paths drowned, so nobody walks to the source who isn't carried there in a Trust boat. But there's a way. An old way. The drowned villages knew it, and the bell-keepers kept it, because a bell-keeper's job was never just noise—it was memory. We were the maps."

She took Ada's hand—her grip surprisingly strong, dry as bark—and pressed it flat against the table's scarred wood.

"Feel. This is the fen road, as I'll draw it for you." Her finger traced lines in the grain. "From Gallowford you go north-west along the levee until the third drain-cut—you'll know it because the culvert there has a keystone carved with a fish, and the Trust's masons missed it when they re-faced everything else, because it's below their sightline and above their interest. Through the culvert, half a mile of causeway that shows at low water only—and the Trust controls the water, so you watch the dredge-schedules; they run the pumps at dawn and dusk, and between the pumpings the level drops a hand's width, no more, but a hand's width is enough if you move. Then the causeway ends at a stand of alders growing in a ring. Alders in a ring are never accidental. Underneath them is the old Merrow well-house, roofed but hollow, and you can shelter there, and from its door the path rises along the fen edge to the sheep-walk, and the sheep-walk goes all the way to the ridge above Headwaters House." She released Ada's hand. "Three days, if the water behaves. Four if it doesn't. And it won't."

"Why tell me?" Ada asked. "You said yourself what the Trust did to your family. Helping me is dangerous."

Marta smiled again, and this time it reached the clouded eye too, warming it like light behind frosted glass.

"Because the Trust took our land," she said, "and our dead, and our names off the maps, and fifty years of my father's life. But they never got the tune." She tapped her temple. "It's all still in here. Every path, every gate, every song the village children sang to remember the sluice-order. They think they own the water. Let them think it. Water doesn't belong to anybody. It's just passing through, same as grief, same as us." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and began pulling things from the shelves: a coil of waxed cord, a tin of matches, a strip of dried eel wrapped in cloth. "Take these. Don't thank me, thanks cost extra."

At the door, with the evening coming down blue and cold outside, Marta caught Ada's sleeve. Her face had changed. All the dry humor had drained out of it, and what remained was older than the tower, older than the town.

"One more thing, and it's free, because it's not information, it's a warning, and warnings should never be sold." Her voice dropped. "Everyone who goes looking for the source thinks of it as a place. A spot on a map, a treasure, a door to be kicked open. It isn't. The source is the oldest thing in this valley and it has been listening to everything done above it for a hundred years—every gate closed, every village drowned, every secret filed away in the dark. It doesn't want to be found, child. Founders get claimed. It wants to be understood. There's a difference, and the difference is the difference between arriving and surviving." She patted Ada's cheek with a hand like a bird's foot. "Your mother understood that. I think it's why they kept her. Go carefully. And when you get there—if you get there—ring something. Anything. Let the valley know its own are walking."

* * *

Ada walked back through Gallowford as the lamps were lit, one by one, like a fever spreading through the town. The Ferryman's Rest glowed at the end of the street, and above it, in room four, the wardrobe waited with its pencil ghosts. Ada's pack was lighter by nothing and heavier by everything: a coil of cord, dried eel, a route drawn in memory in a blind woman's finger-trace, and a sentence that kept turning over and over like a stone in a current.

*It wants to be understood.*

At the inn's door, Ada paused. Down the street, beneath the gas lamp outside the Trust's Gallowford office, two men in wardens' grey were talking to the night-clerk, and one of them held a piece of paper up to the light, and even at this distance Ada recognized the posture of a man comparing a description to a face.

They slipped inside, took the back stairs, and began packing for the fens—dawn tide, third drain-cut, the fish-carved keystone. Three days, if the water behaved.

Marta had said it wouldn't.

Outside, far off toward the dark line of the fens, Ada heard—or thought they heard—the pumps start early.
