## 13. Fen Water Rising

The fens began where the river forgot it was a river.

Ada had read that line somewhere—in one of Ingrid's old field guides, maybe, or in a book left behind in the boathouse by some previous tenant—and had dismissed it as the kind of sentence people wrote when they wanted to sound wise. Standing now at the edge of the Halvard fen, boots sunk two inches into peat that breathed like something sleeping, they understood it completely. The water here did not flow. It *lingered*. It pooled in the low places and held its position with the patience of something that had outlasted every attempt to move it, and the reeds stood in it so thick and so still that the whole landscape looked like a photograph of itself, waiting for someone to notice the error.

Marta's route had been specific. *Follow the old causeway—stones under the waterline, you'll feel them through your boots. When you reach the leaning alder, turn north. The church tower at Marrow Fen will show you the high ground if the fog comes down.* She had said it the way she said everything, half-song, half-scold, her clouded eyes fixed on some middle distance where the past and present shared a table. *The Trust hasn't drained the fens because they can't. The fens remember how to be sea.*

Bryn had not liked the plan. Bryn had not liked any of the plan, if Ada was honest, but she had come anyway, poling her flat-bottomed skiff through channels she claimed to know from her grandmother's stories, channels that appeared on no map printed in the last hundred years.

"There," she said now, and her voice had gone flat in the way Ada had learned to distrust. "You see it?"

Ada saw it. Half a mile out, where the reeds thinned into open water the color of strong tea, a dredge sat anchored—a long low barge with a crane arm folded against its hull like a sleeping heron. On its side, in flaking white letters: *RIVERGATE TRUST — CHANNEL MAINTENANCE DIVISION.*

"Maintenance," Ada said.

"Mm." Bryn shifted her pole. "Maintenance dredges work the main channel. Deepen it. Clear it." She nodded at the barge, at the churned brown water spreading from its stern in a slow fan. "That one's not working the channel. It's working the *edges*. It's pushing water into the fen, not out of it."

Ada looked again, and the wrongness resolved into sense. The dredge's crane wasn't lifting silt out. It was dragging a chain along the fen floor, and where the chain passed, the water level crept. Ada could see it in the reeds—stems that had been green to a hand's height yesterday now submerged to the leaf. Could hear it, faintly, in the lap of water against peat that had been dry ground a week ago.

"They're raising the level," Ada said. "On purpose."

"Or they're raising it carelessly, and the paperwork will say so, and no one will lose their job." Bryn's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely bitter. "That's the trick of it, isn't it. You never need to prove intent when negligence will do."

The fog was coming down as they spoke. Not drifting in—*descending*, a grey ceiling lowering itself over the fen with the unhurried confidence of something that owned the air. Marta had warned of it. *Fen fog isn't weather, girl. It's the water remembering it used to be sky.*

"We should move," Ada said. "Now, while we can still see the causeway."

Bryn hesitated. Ada watched her do it—watched the ferrywoman's instinct war against something else, some older instinct that lived in the fen itself and spoke through her. Her grandmother had been born out here, in a village the maps said had never existed. Bryn had grown up on stories of this water. And stories, Ada was learning, were a kind of map too.

"Stay left of the alder," Bryn said finally. "If the water rises past your knee, get to high ground and *stay there*. Don't try to wade for me. I know this fen better than the Trust does, and better than you ever will." She jabbed the pole, and the skiff slid away into the reeds, and then the fog took her, and Ada was alone with the sound of water where there had been no sound before.

* * *

The causeway was real. That was the first mercy.

Ada found it by feel, as Marta had promised—stones under the waterline, each one placed with the deliberate spacing of human hands, worn smooth by centuries of feet. The people who built this had known the fen when it was drier, or when they had managed it better than the Trust did, or both. Ada walked with their weight forward, arms out, counting steps the way they counted stratigraphic layers in a cut: not because the counting mattered, but because counting kept the fear in its box.

The fog thickened until the world was ten feet wide. Then five. The reeds became grey ghosts, then nothing. Sound arrived from everywhere and nowhere: the distant groan of the dredge's engine, the whisper of water moving where water should have been still, the occasional plop of something diving—eel, or frog, or nothing at all.

Ada thought about the ledger page folded against their chest. *Payments accepted for relocation, 1893–present.* Five village names, four of them missing from every map. The fifth—Marrow Fen—marked only with a small cross and a note in a clerk's hand: *structure retained, tower only, survey point.*

A survey point. The Trust had drowned a village and kept its church tower the way a hunter keeps a trophy.

The water reached their ankles. Then their shins. It happened gradually enough that Ada might have missed the transition if their body hadn't been keeping its own count, the cold climbing their legs like a slow tide. Marta's voice in their head: *If the water rises past your knee—*

It passed their knee somewhere around the fortieth step.

Ada stopped. Stood in the fog with water pulling at their thighs, and listened. The dredge's engine had changed pitch—lower, more labored, the sound of a machine working against resistance. Or working *with* it. Pushing water into the fen's low places, filling basins that had been dry for a century, and the causeway was sinking under the effort.

*Don't try to wade for me.* Bryn's voice, clear as if she stood beside them.

*Get to high ground.*

Ada turned north. Or what they hoped was north. In the fog, with the water rising, with the causeway stones disappearing one by one beneath the flood like a sentence being erased, north was a faith rather than a fact. They waded. The peat sucked at each step and gave it back reluctantly. The cold worked inward, past muscle, toward bone. Ada thought, absurdly, of the child's shoe from the cave—a small leather thing, salt-stiffened, its laces still tied. Someone had tied those laces that morning. Someone had believed there would be an evening.

The tower appeared the way Marta had said it would: not gradually, but all at once, as if the fog had been holding it back and grew tired of the game. A grey shape resolving out of grey, and then the church tower of Marrow Fen stood before them, leaning—Marta had said *leaning*, and it was, canted perhaps five degrees east like a drunkard listening for a bell—and surrounded by water on every side.

The nave was gone. Submerged entirely, or collapsed into the peat; Ada could see only the suggestion of rooflines beneath the surface, dark geometries like a shipwreck's bones. But the tower rose a full forty feet out of the flood, its flint-and-limestone skin furred with lichen, its belfry windows empty eyes. A stone ledge ran around its base at the waterline—the remnant of a churchyard wall, perhaps, or a plinth. Above it, a door. The door was oak, black with age, and it stood ajar.

Ada waded the last twenty feet, water to their waist now, and hauled themselves up onto the ledge with arms that shook more than they wanted to admit. The door's gap was narrow—narrow enough that Ada had to turn sideways and press through, flint scraping their pack, and then they were inside, and the fen's noise cut off as if a hand had closed over the world's mouth.

* * *

The tower's interior was a single stone throat, rising.

Ada stood in the dark and let their eyes adjust, and what emerged was not emptiness but *architecture*: the spiral of a staircase clinging to the inner wall, most of its steps intact; the belfry above, open to the sky through arched windows; and everywhere, on every surface, the marks of the water. A tideline of dried silt at shoulder height, and above it another, and another—a stratigraphy of floods, each one a year the Trust's engineers had let the fen rise and the village drown a little further. Ada's professional eye read them automatically, the way it read middens and cut-faces: *this flood older, this one recent, this one catastrophic.* A century of neglect written in mud.

And lower—near the base, half-buried in debris—a pew. A single oak pew, wedged against the stair, its wood gone silver-grey. Carved into its end, small and careful, initials: *E.H. 1887.*

Ada sat down on it. It held.

The cold came for them then, the way cold does when you stop moving—first the shiver, then the deeper tremor, then the teeth. Ada opened the pack and inventoried by touch: the tin of matches, damp but viable; half a loaf wrapped in wax cloth; the ledger page; Ingrid's map fragment; the flask of tea Bryn had pressed on them that morning, still faintly warm. They drank the tea in small sips and ate a little bread and did not let themselves think about the water outside, which they could hear now that they listened—not rising, exactly, but *arriving*, finding new channels, filling new rooms of the fen with the patience of something that had all night and all the memory of the sea.

Bryn. Ada thought of Bryn out in that, poling channels her grandmother had sung to her, and felt something they hadn't expected to feel: not fear for themselves, but a specific, located dread with another person's name on it. Two days ago the thought of company had been a weight they carried out of obligation. Now the fog felt larger for her absence. The tower felt colder. Isolation, Ada was discovering, was a room you could furnish yourself—and they had furnished theirs so thoroughly, for so many years, that they'd mistaken it for the whole house.

They climbed the stairs.

The steps were sound for the first two turns and then crumbled to rubble at the third, so Ada climbed the last stretch by pressing back and shoulders against the opposite walls, chimney-style, boots finding purchase in the mortar joints. The belfry opened around them: four arched windows, a frame of blackened oak where the bell had hung—the bell itself long gone, carted off or drowned—and below, through the western arch, the fen.

The fog was thinning. Not lifting—thinning, the way a held breath eases, and through it Ada could see the water.

It was everywhere. The causeway: gone. The reed beds: islands now, small and drowning. Far to the south, the dredge's running lights burned through the murk, two orange points, and Ada watched them move—slowly, methodically, north. Toward the high ground. Toward the route Marta had given them. Whatever the dredge was doing, it was doing it along a line, and the line was not random.

Ada got out the map fragment and held it to the last grey light. Ingrid's pencil work—Ada knew the hand the way they knew their own, having grown up watching it move across excavation plans—traced the fen's channels as they had been, with annotations in her small exact script: *sluice, disused. Cut, 1904. Original course of the Emrys here.* The old river's name. The name the Trust had replaced on every sign and chart with *Rivergate*, as if the water itself had been rebranded.

*Original course.* Ada looked from the map to the flooded fen and back, and the thought assembled itself with the click of a finds tray seating into its foam: the Trust wasn't just raising the water. They were *routing* it. Pushing the fen's flood along a chosen path—perhaps to scour out the old channels, perhaps to bury them deeper, perhaps simply to make the crossing impassable for anyone following a bell-keeper's directions. Either way, the water was a tool tonight, and someone was holding the handle.

Below, at the tower's base, something moved.

Ada went still. The light was nearly gone now, the fog giving back the last of it in a grey wash, and at first they thought it was a heron—a pale shape wading the shallows near the drowned nave. Then the shape resolved: a boat. A long, low, motorized launch, its hull painted the flat grey of official indifference, a single figure standing in it with a lantern.

The lantern light swept the tower's base. Held. Swept again.

The figure raised something—not a weapon, Ada thought, though in the dark it was impossible to be sure. A speaking trumpet, maybe. Or just a hand, shading the lantern, looking up.

And then, through the belfry's western arch, across a mile of rising water, a sound. Faint, halting, unmistakable: a bell. Not this tower's bell—that was gone, drowned or stolen decades ago. Another bell, far off to the north, ringing a pattern Ada didn't recognize but their body did, the way a body recognizes a lullaby it can't place. Three strikes. A pause. Two. A pause. Three.

Somewhere out in the dark, Bryn was answering it.

The figure in the launch below turned toward the sound too, lantern swinging, and Ada pressed back from the arch into the shadow of the belfry frame, heart hammering, and understood two things at once with terrible clarity.

The first: that bell was a signal, an old one, and it was being rung for the fen's people—for the descendants of the drowned villages, the keepers of the sluice-songs, the ones the Trust had counted as scattered and silenced. And it was being answered.

The second: the man in the launch was not leaving. He had moored the boat against the tower's ledge, and his lantern light was climbing the outside of the stonework, and he was coming up.

* * *

Ada retreated down the belfry stairs as far as the collapsed section and stopped, wedged in the dark, listening. Below, the tower door—left ajar for a century—creaked wider. Boots on stone. The lantern's glow rising through the spiral like a slow dawn.

Then a voice, carrying up the tower's throat with the ease of a man who had done this before, who knew exactly how stone conducts sound:

"Miss Eriksen." A pause, courteous, almost gentle. "Or is it *Mx*? Forgive me—I've never been certain how you prefer it. Mr. Aldous Vane, Rivergate Trust. We met at your boathouse, though I admit the circumstances were less... atmospheric." The lantern light wavered, and Ada heard him set it down on stone. "The water will be over the causeway by midnight and over the churchyard wall by morning. I have a boat, a thermos of coffee, and a version of events I think you'll want to hear before you freeze. Your mother would want you to hear it."

Ada closed their eyes in the dark. The bell rang again, far off—three strikes, a pause, two—and the water rose against the tower's ancient stones with a sound like slow applause.

*Come up, then,* Ada thought, and said nothing at all.
