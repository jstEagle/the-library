## 14. Vane's Version of History

The church tower held water like a cup holds wine—patiently, at the level it chose.

Ada sat on the belfry ledge with their knees drawn up, watching the fen rise through the ruined windows. The flood had come up fast in the last hour, faster than any tide had a right to move, and now the lower reaches of the tower were drowned to the second storey. Below them, somewhere under that brown silk-smooth surface, lay pews, a font, a floor where generations of some vanished congregation had knelt. The water didn't care. It covered everything with the same indifferent gloss, and in the last light of evening it turned the drowned world into something almost beautiful, which was its cruelest trick.

Bryn was out there somewhere. Or not out there. Ada had lost sight of her boat in the reed-beds when the dredges came through—three flat black barges flying no flag, running no lights, their pumps churning the channels wider and deeper until the fen simply swallowed its own banks. Maintenance, the Trust would call it. Ada was beginning to learn what that word meant. It meant whatever the Trust needed it to mean that day.

Their pack was gone. Their food was gone. What remained was a tin of matches kept dry inside their shirt, half a candle stub, and the ledger page, which they had taken to sleeping with the way other people slept with knives.

The tower groaned around them as the water found new purchase. Pigeons had once lived in the belfry; their droppings still streaked the sill, and the bell above—small, green with verdigris, its rope long rotted—hung silent in a web of dust. Ada thought about ringing it. Laughed at themselves for thinking it. Who would come? Marta's warning ran through their head on a loop: *the source doesn't want to be found.* Well, the fens didn't want them either. They were a guest nobody had invited, and the country was making that clear.

Night came down the way it does over open water—not falling so much as seeping, the last grey draining out of the sky until the fen and the dark became one continuous thing. Ada lit the candle stub and cupped it against the wind that moved through the broken louvres. In its small light the tower felt less like shelter and more like a throat they'd climbed into.

They were dozing against the stone when they heard the engine.

It wasn't the churn of a dredge. It was quieter, more refined—a long low thrum, well-maintained, approaching without hurry. Ada pinched out the candle and pressed back against the wall, heart going hard. Through the louvre slats they watched a shape resolve on the black water: a flat-bottomed launch with a canvas canopy, a lantern at its bow burning steady and white, the kind of light that said *we are not afraid of being seen.*

The boat came alongside the tower and stopped. A figure stood in it, unhurried, and raised the lantern so its glow climbed the stonework and found Ada's face in the dark.

"Miss Eriksen," Aldous Vane called up, pleasantly. "You'll catch your death up there. Do come down. I've brought blankets."

* * *

Ada did not come down. Not immediately. They stayed on the ledge with their back to the cold stone and their voice level, and asked the only question that mattered. "How did you find me?"

Vane's laugh was soft, genuine-sounding, carried clearly across the water. "Because I arranged the water, and you arranged yourself around it. It's not complicated. My dredges raised the fen by eighteen inches this afternoon. There are exactly three places a person on foot can go when that happens, and two of them are underwater." He let that sit. "I'm sorry about your friend's boat, incidentally. We towed it to Gallowford. She wasn't aboard."

The relief hit Ada so hard they had to grip the sill. *She wasn't aboard.* Alive, then—or unaccounted for, which was different, but tonight Ada would take different.

"You could have drowned me," Ada said.

"We could have done a great many things." Vane settled himself on the canopy bench below, visible now in the lantern light: grey coat, neat hands, the face of a man who had never once raised his voice because he'd never needed to. "And I'd rather we talked about why we haven't. Come down, Miss Eriksen. I give you my word you'll step back onto this boat whenever you like. You have my word you can leave. That's worth more than you think—it's the same word I gave your mother."

That got Ada moving before pride could stop them. They climbed down through the flooded stairwell, water to the chest, gasping at the cold, and hauled themselves over the gunwale with hands that shook from more than temperature. Vane wrapped a blanket around their shoulders without touching them—an act of courtesy performed at a careful distance—and poured something amber into a tin cup. Whisky, or the smell of it. Ada held the cup and drank nothing.

The launch pulled away from the tower and idled out into the fen, and the drowned church sank behind them into the general darkness, one more thing the water kept.

* * *

"I imagine you hate me," Vane said, steering with one hand, the lantern swinging gently at the bow. "That's reasonable. But let me tell you what you actually know about me, rather than what you've decided. You know I offered to buy shell middens off a beachcomber. You know my wardens patrol a weir. You know a dredge or two inconvenienced your walk. That is the entire criminal record, Miss Eriksen, and I'd submit it's thinner than most saints'."

"The villages," Ada said.

"Ah." He nodded slowly, as if she'd raised a point he respected. "The villages. Old Merrow. Candlemere. The rest. Yes. Let's talk about those, since your mother has filled your head with them, and since you've been reading things you found in a hole in a cliff."

"They were drowned on purpose."

"They were *relocated*," Vane said, "on purpose, yes. In 1893, and again in 1911, and once more in '34. Paid, housed, moved to better ground than they'd ever farmed. And do you know what they were relocated *from*?" He didn't wait. "From a river that killed them. Every spring, Miss Eriksen, every thaw, the Rivergate rose and took what it wanted. Livestock. Children. Whole streets in wet years. There are parish registers older than the Trust that read like casualty lists. Five villages in the valley bottom, and every one of them buried someone in a flood before the Trust ever existed."

The launch slid between stands of reeds. Somewhere a bittern boomed, a sound like a door in an empty house.

"So we built," Vane went on. "Weirs, sluices, the reservoir works at the head of the valley. We took control of the water away from the weather and gave it to people who could be answerable for it. And yes—the engineering required flooding the lowest ground permanently. Yes, the maps were redrawn. Would you prefer the alternative map? The one with the crosses for graves?" He glanced at her, and his eyes in the lantern light were tired and sincere, and that was the frightening part. "Gallowford has a hospital because of Trust money. The ferry routes run because we keep the channels. When the drought came in '76, the valley ate because our reservoirs held water back for it. Order isn't free, Miss Eriksen. Someone pays for it, and someone administers it, and everyone forgets both until the river rises. Then they remember, and they're grateful, and then they forget again. That's not tyranny. That's maintenance."

"You sound like you believe it."

"I *do* believe it. That's the difference between me and the villains in your mother's books." Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile. "Dr. Eriksen sees the drowned villages and wants the truth dragged into daylight, consequences be damned. She calls it justice. I've read her work; she's brilliant; I don't say that to flatter you. But ask yourself what her justice looks like in practice. Gates opened. Records burned or scattered. A century of flood management dismantled in an afternoon. And then—" he spread his hands, elegant, helpless— "chaos dressed as truth. The water comes back, and it doesn't read charters. It doesn't care whose grandchildren lived where. It just rises. Your mother wanted chaos dressed as truth, and she'd have called the dressing honesty."

Ada looked down at the tin cup. The whisky trembled slightly with the engine's vibration, gold rings chasing each other across the surface, and they hated how much sense the man made, sentence by sentence, the way a con makes sense right up until the moment you remember what's missing.

"And me?" Ada said. "Where do I fit in your version?"

"You're the part I regret." For the first time his voice lost its polish, just slightly. "Ingrid came to us with questions. She could have walked away with answers and a grant and gone back to her career. Instead she kept digging, and now she's—somewhere safe, let's say—and you've come after her, wading into a country you don't understand, carrying papers you can't possibly evaluate. You're a child playing in machinery, Miss Eriksen. I don't want you hurt. Neither does your mother. That's the one thing, I suspect, on which the three of us agree."

"She's alive." Ada made it flat, a fact, refusing to make it a question.

Vane was quiet for a long moment. The reeds parted; ahead, far off, a single light burned on some distant shore—Gallowford, maybe, or one of the Trust's stations, keeping its small ordered flame against all that dark water.

"Yes," he said finally. "She's alive. And whether she stays that way depends a great deal on choices made in the next few days, by her and by you. Which brings me to my offer."

* * *

He laid it out plainly, and that was the worst of it—that he didn't dress it up.

The Trust would fund Ada's work. Properly fund it—a berth, equipment, access to collections no academic had touched in fifty years. The ledger fragments, the cave finds, everything: handed over, catalogued, preserved. In exchange, Ada signed nothing, said nothing, published nothing, and went home. And in time—when tempers cooled, when Dr. Eriksen concluded her translation work and saw reason—the family could be together again. There was even talk, Vane said carefully, of a formal role. The Trust valued legitimacy. A name like Eriksen, attached openly, would mean something.

"Or," Ada said.

"Or you keep walking upstream." Vane brought the launch about, slow and gentle, the bow swinging toward a shingle spit where a track climbed away from the water toward higher ground. "And the country keeps doing what it did today, except next time I won't be along to fish you out. I'm not threatening you, Miss Eriksen. I'm describing the river. It's the same description I'd give anyone."

The boat grounded softly on the shingle. Vane stepped out, helped Ada ashore with a dry courtesy that made their skin crawl, and then handed them a canvas bag—heavy, and smelling of bread.

"Food. Matches. A lamp. Blanket's in the boat if you want it." He paused, one foot on the gunwale, and looked back at them with something that was almost kindness. "Your mother left you an inheritance, I'm told. So do I. Mine's simpler: a warm meal and a road home. Most people, given the choice between a story and a supper, take the supper. Take a day. Think. If you walk east you'll reach Gallowford by tomorrow night, and no one will trouble you—I'll see to it personally."

Then the launch backed off the shingle, turned, and dwindled away into the fen with its white lantern burning, leaving Ada alone on a strip of gravel in the middle of a drowned country, holding a bag of bread like a bribe.

* * *

They didn't eat. Not at first.

They sat down on the shingle with the canvas bag between their knees and made themselves go through Vane's speech piece by piece, the way they'd learned to go through a stratigraphy—layer by layer, oldest lie at the bottom. And the terrible thing, the thing they would never say aloud to anyone, was how much of it held weight. The floods had been real. The parish registers, if they existed, probably did read like casualty lists. Gallowford's hospital had Trust money in its walls; Bryn's fuel had Trust money in its tank; Ada's own boots had been bought with coin earned digging the Trust's shoreline. The whole valley was soaked in Trust money the way the fen was soaked in fen water, and you couldn't wring it out of anything.

But underneath it, at the bottom of the column, beneath all the true layers, was the thing that didn't fit. Payments since 1893 to families who *accepted relocation*. Accepted. As though acceptance could be bought annually, year after year, generation after generation—as though a payment that never stopped wasn't a confession wearing a receipt. And the archive doors behind Headwaters House, sealed and humming. You didn't lock away records that proved your virtue. Nobody builds a vault for their own innocence.

And the photograph. Three weeks old. Their mother's face looking out of it, and the date stamped in the corner like evidence in a trial.

Ada thought about Ingrid—really thought about her, not the monument or the wound but the woman: the way she'd hum when she brushed soil from a rim sherd, the way she'd once spent an entire afternoon teaching eight-year-old Ada to tell a natural deposit from a human one. *People leave traces in order,* she'd said. *Lies leave traces too. They're just arranged differently.*

Vane's history was arranged very beautifully indeed.

Ada opened the bag. Bread, cheese, apples, a slab of chocolate, a lamp with oil in it—generous, calculated to be generous, calibrated to the exact weight of gratitude a starving person can carry. They ate, because refusing the food would have been theatre, and they were done with theatre. They ate and hated how good it tasted and understood, chewing, that this was the trap working precisely as designed: not the offer, but the comfort. The warm meal and the road home. The version of the story where everyone lives and nothing has to burn.

East lay Gallowford, and warmth, and silence, and a mother somewhere safe.

Upstream lay everything else.

Ada stood, shouldered the empty-weight of their lost pack out of habit, and looked north, where the fen gave way to darker country and the river ran on toward its source, invisible but utterly certain, the way a pulse is certain under a wrist.

"Bryn," they said aloud, to the dark, to the water, to whoever was listening. "Hold on. I'm coming."

They struck no light. They walked north.

Behind them, far out in the black fen, a bell began to ring—distant, irregular, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause—and Ada froze on the shingle with their heart slamming, because they knew that pattern now, had read it in a ledger and heard it in a song: the old alarm of the drowned villages, the signal that meant *come, come now, come while there's still time.*

Someone was calling them home.
