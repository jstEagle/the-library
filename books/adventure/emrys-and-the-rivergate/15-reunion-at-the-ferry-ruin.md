## 15. Reunion at the Ferry Ruin

The ferry ruin sat where the river widened into a slack brown pool, all that remained of a crossing older than any map Ada had ever catalogued. Two stone piers reached out of the water like broken teeth, and between them the current ran slow and greasy with fen silt. A roofless tollhouse leaned against the near pier, its timbers silvered to bone, its doorway framing nothing but river.

Ada had walked most of the night after the tower, wading the last mile through water that rose past their knees, and now they stood in the ruin's shelter watching the light come up gray and cold over the reed beds. Their clothes had dried crooked. Their hands still shook—not from fear anymore, but from the long chemical aftermath of it, the body's slow accounting. Vane's food sat untouched in their pack: wax-wrapped bread, a tin of smoked fish, two apples polished like offerings. They hadn't decided whether eating it was surrender or sense.

They heard the boat before they saw it—the particular knock of a pole against gunwale, rhythmic as a heartbeat—and then Bryn Halvard came around the bend of the reeds, standing in her flat-bottomed ferry like she'd been grown there, pushing upstream against a current that should have been too strong for one woman.

Ada's chest did something complicated. Relief first, sharp and stupid. Then, underneath it, the memory of the dredges rising behind them in the fen, the water climbing deliberate as a hand, Bryn's shout swallowed by engine noise—and the long dark hours of not knowing.

Bryn brought the boat alongside the old pier and made fast without a word. She looked at Ada standing in the tollhouse doorway, looked at the water line on their coat, and said, "You look like something the river gave back."

"Feels about right." Ada's voice came out rougher than intended. "I thought you were drowned."

"I thought you were." Bryn climbed onto the stone, moving stiffly, and Ada saw for the first time how tired she was—deeply, structurally tired, the kind that lives in a person's posture. "When the water came up I turned downstream thinking you'd run for the bank. Took me four hours to work out you'd have gone up. Toward trouble."

"Toward my mother."

"Toward your mother," Bryn agreed. She lowered herself onto the tollhouse step, forearms on knees, and stared at the water. The silence stretched until Ada could hear the reeds ticking against each other.

Then Bryn said, "I took money from them again."

Ada went still.

"After the fen. After we got separated. I put in at the Trust dock at Marlow Staithe to look for you, and there was a man there—I didn't know his name, gray coat, polite the way Vane's people are polite—and he knew who I was. Knew my grandmother's name. He offered me fuel. Diesel's dear this year, and my tank was down to fumes, and he filled it while I stood there deciding." Her jaw worked. "And I let him."

"How much?"

"The fuel. And forty pounds he called 'a courtesy.'" She said the word like spitting out a bone. "He asked where you'd gone. I told him I'd lost you in the fen. Which was true when I said it." She finally turned to look at Ada, and her eyes were wet and furious at the same time, furious mostly at herself. "But I took his diesel, Ada. I'm burning his diesel right now to come find you. You're sitting across from me on a boat the Trust paid to float."

* * *

The wind moved through the reed beds in long slow combers, and somewhere downstream a moorhen laughed its broken laugh. Ada found they couldn't look away from Bryn's hands—broad, scarred, knuckles like river stones, hands that had poled them safely past drowned Merrow and its impossible bells. Hands that had just held out a palm for Trust coin.

"You told me," Ada said slowly, "that you never reported me. Back at the start. When you admitted you were paid to watch the river."

"I didn't report you. That's still true."

"But you took the money anyway. Before, and now again." Ada heard their own voice go flat and cold, the voice they used on looters at dig sites, and hated it even as it came out. "Do you know what I've lost since I met you? My pack. Half my food. Nearly my life in that tower. And the whole time, the woman poling me upstream has been on their payroll."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ada agreed. "It probably isn't. But neither is drowning villages."

Bryn flinched as if struck. For a moment Ada thought she would stand up, get back in her boat, pole away downstream and out of their life entirely—and some small mean part of them braced for it, expected it, had spent a lifetime expecting people to leave.

Instead Bryn said, quietly, "You want to know what clean hands cost? Ask me what I ate last winter."

The question landed harder than any accusation. Ada sat down on the step beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and waited.

"My grandmother was born in Old Merrow," Bryn said to the water. "You know that. What I never told you is that my mother grew up in the resettlement houses at Gallowford, in a row the Trust built, and every house in that row was mortgaged to the Trust. My mother worked the mill. When the mills mechanized, the Trust bought the debt. When she died, the debt came to me. Forty years of interest on a house I never wanted, in a town that exists because five other towns don't." She turned her scarred palms upward, an offering or a surrender. "I take the ferry work they give me because refusing means losing the boat, and the boat is the only thing my grandmother's family still owns on this river. So yes. I take their money. Small amounts. Enough to survive. Not enough to be theirs." Her voice cracked on the last word. "That's the arithmetic I do every day. Every single day. And I hate it, and I do it anyway, because the alternative isn't purity. The alternative is being hungry and useless."

Ada thought of themselves, lying smoothly to Aldous Vane on the boathouse step, hating how easily the lie had come. Thought of Vane's food in their pack, untouched, and how long their resolve would last if the hunger got bad enough. Clean hands. They had judged Bryn from inside a body that had never once known real debt.

"I lied to Vane," Ada said. "At the boathouse. Told him I'd found nothing on the shore. It came out smooth, like pouring water. I've been telling myself it was survival too."

"Was it?"

"Yes. And I still felt filthy after." They looked at the river, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream eventually. "Maybe that's the difference between us and them. They feel clean doing filthy things. We feel filthy doing necessary ones."

Bryn made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been wrung out of her years ago. "Your mother would have understood that. From what you've said of her."

"My mother left me a photograph in a cave and a pencil map in a wardrobe and eleven years of silence." Ada heard the old anger rise and, for once, let it speak plainly instead of swallowing it. "I don't know yet what she'd understand. That's partly why I'm going upriver. To ask her."

"To ask her or to punish her?"

"Both. Maybe both." Ada exhaled. "Is that terrible?"

"It's honest." Bryn rubbed her face with both hands. "Honesty's the only currency out here that doesn't devalue."

* * *

They ate Vane's food after all. It seemed important, suddenly, not to waste good bread on a principle; Ada split the loaf and the fish and one apple, and they ate sitting in the ruined tollhouse with the river sliding past, and the food tasted like nothing and like mercy at once.

"We need terms," Bryn said at last, crumpling the wax paper into her pocket—she wasted nothing, Ada noticed, not paper, not fuel, not words. "If we're going on together, I won't have secrets sitting between us like ballast. So here's mine, stated plain: I will take Trust money again if it's fuel or food or keeping the boat. I'll tell you every time, same day. I won't take it for information about you, and I won't carry anything for them, and if it ever comes to choosing between their coin and your safety, I choose you—but you should know I'm a woman who needs telling, because I chose wrong once." Her voice dropped. "Years ago. Someone else came up this river asking questions, before your mother. I reported them. Just a name, just once, to pay off three months of the mortgage. I've never learned what happened to them."

The confession hung in the air between them, and Ada felt the weight of it settle—not forgivable exactly, not yet, maybe not ever fully, but *known*, which was different and perhaps worth more.

"And here's mine," Ada said. "I lie well when I'm frightened, and I'm likely to be frightened a lot from here on. If I lie to you, call it out. Don't let me get away with it just because we're tired. And—" they hesitated, then pushed through, "—when we find my mother, whatever happens, however it goes, don't let me walk away from her the way she walked from me. Even if I want to. Especially if I want to."

Bryn studied them for a long moment. "Those are strange terms for an alliance."

"They're the only terms I can honestly offer."

"Then they'll do." Bryn stood, joints cracking, and looked upstream where the river narrowed toward hills gone blue with distance. "Gallowford's a day and a half by water if the wardens don't stop us. Less if we run dark—no lamp, no fire, sleep in the boat."

"Run dark, then."

Bryn paused, one hand on the pier post, and something moved across her weathered face that was almost a smile. "My grandmother had a saying. *The river keeps no ledger.* She meant it as comfort. Lately I think she meant it as instruction."

She stepped down into the boat and held it steady against the stones, and Ada gathered their pack—their lighter pack, their poorer pack—and swung it aboard, and the motion felt like signing something.

As Bryn pushed off and the current took them, both of them scanning the banks, the reeds, the gray distance for the shape of watchers, Ada caught sight of it: chalked on the underside of the far pier arch, high above the waterline where only someone looking straight up from a boat would ever see it, a mark. Fresh. Days old at most.

A circle bisected by a wavy line—the same symbol stamped on the rusted lantern in the tide cave, the same crest Aldous Vane wore on his signet ring.

Someone had marked this crossing as watched. Or—as Ada looked longer, noting the small second stroke beneath it, a notation style Ingrid used on every site plan she'd ever drawn—as *passed through*.

"Bryn," Ada said quietly. "Stop the boat."
