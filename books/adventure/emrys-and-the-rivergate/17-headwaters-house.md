## 17. Headwaters House

They smelled it before they saw it.

Not smoke, not industry—something subtler. Cut grass. The green sweetness of watered lawns rising through the cold mountain air like a lie told in perfume. Ada stopped on the trail with one hand against a birch trunk and breathed it in, and the wrongness of it settled into their chest the way a wrong note settles into a song.

"Smell that?" Bryn said behind them.

"I smell summer."

"In October." Bryn came up beside them, her pole across her shoulders, her coat stiff with two days of river spray. "That's what money smells like when it's spent on keeping something alive that should be sleeping."

They climbed the last of the ridge through trees gone thin and silver, and then the forest simply gave up, the way it does at the edge of an estate, as if it had been paid to leave. And there it was.

Headwaters House sat in the fold of the valley like a hand cupped over a spring.

Ada had imagined it a hundred ways since Gallowford—a fortress, a mill, some squat administrative block with barbed wire and floodlights. They had not imagined this: a long manor of grey stone, three stories, its windows tall and dark and patient, its slate roof green with lichen that had been allowed, deliberately, Ada thought, to grow. It was beautiful the way a heron is beautiful—still, elegant, and built entirely for killing things smaller than itself. The river ran out from beneath it. That was the detail that made Ada's skin prickle. Not past it, not around it. *Out from under it*, emerging from a stone arch in the house's foundations as if the building had been lowered onto the water's throat and left there to drink first.

The gardens fell away from the house in terraces, and every terrace was green. Roses still bloomed along a low wall. A lawn stretched down toward the water, striped by mowing, soft-edged with mist. Somewhere below, hidden in the ground, pipes were carrying river water up to all of this while downstream—Ada thought of Old Merrow's drowned steeple, of the fen families stacking sandbags, of Gallowford's polite hunger—the valley went thirsty or drowning according to someone else's arithmetic.

"They irrigate," Bryn said flatly. She'd seen it too. Everyone from the river would see it first, before the architecture, before the beauty. "Look at the sluice marks on that weir. Look at the *grass*."

"I'm looking."

"Your mother walked into that." Bryn said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. She just stated it, the way she stated depths and currents, facts about water. "And didn't walk out."

"Not yet," Ada said.

Bryn looked at them then, a long look, and said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

They spent the afternoon watching from the treeline above the eastern terrace, the way Ada had once watched shell middens erode out of a cliff face—not rushing, not touching, letting the site tell them its own schedule. It was digger's patience, and it had kept them alive through weirs and wardens and fen water, and it served them now.

The wardens moved through the grounds on a rhythm. Ada counted them, marked their intervals against the face of their watch. Six visible, walking pairs, dark green coats that read almost black in the shade, radios held loose at their sides. They were not soldiers. That was the unsettling thing. They strolled. They paused to look at the roses. One of them, a heavyset man with grey at his temples, stopped at the terrace wall and stood for a full minute doing nothing at all, gazing downriver toward the valley he was paid to keep ordered, his face unreadable.

"They're local," Bryn murmured. She'd produced a battered pair of binoculars from somewhere inside her coat, and she passed them over without ceremony. "See the boots? Half of them are wearing their own. Trust kit on top, but the boots are theirs. Ferry-town boots. Fen boots."

Ada took the glasses and found the heavyset warden again. He was right; the man's boots were cracked leather, resoled twice, the boots of someone who'd grown up walking riverbanks, not parade grounds. Ada thought of the ledger's second page, of payments made annually to families who accepted relocation, and wondered what the going rate was now for standing in a garden holding a radio while your grandmother's village stayed underwater.

"Consent," they murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing." Ada passed the glasses back. "Keep counting."

By late afternoon they had the shape of it: the wardens circled the house and terraces but rarely came up to the ridge, and never into the trees. The house itself had servants' entrances on the north side, a coach road curving away east toward Gallowford, and—this was the detail Ada returned to again and again—a lower gallery. They'd spotted it by accident: a colonnaded walkway set into the hillside below the main floor, half-buried, its arches opening onto the rock face where the river emerged. Windows glowed there even in daylight, warm yellow rectangles in the stone, and Ada could not stop thinking about what needed light underground.

"The archives," they said quietly, as dusk began pooling in the valley. "Ingrid's notes said the founding documents were kept where the water couldn't reach. Below the spring line. That gallery is below the spring line."

"And guarded by six men who water roses."

"Seven. We missed one—he's on the coach road." Ada lay back against the moss and stared up through the birch branches at the first stars. Their body ached in the deep, honest way of two weeks on the river, and they were so tired that the tiredness had begun to feel like a personality. But underneath the exhaustion something else was running, steady as groundwater: *she's here.* Or she had been. The photograph in the cave was three weeks old. The innkeeper in Gallowford remembered her. Vane's agents were asking questions behind her. Every current in the valley bent toward this house, and Ingrid Eriksen had bent toward it too, alone, and Ada had spent their whole life learning what happened to people who faced the Eriksen stubbornness without backup.

"She might not want to see you," Bryn said. She said it the way she said everything, plainly, watching the water instead of watching Ada's face. "Mothers who leave don't always want finding."

"I know."

"You don't. You want her found. Those aren't the same thing."

Ada turned their head and looked at her. In the failing light her face was all angles and river-reflection, and there was no cruelty in it, only the honesty of a woman who poled strangers upriver for coin and asked no questions because questions cost more than answers.

"When my grandmother's village drowned," Bryn went on, "her mother told her to run ahead, to the high field, and said she'd follow with the baby. Her mother never came. My grandmother spent sixty years saying she'd have stayed if she'd known. Sixty years being angry at a woman who probably died trying to keep a promise." She shrugged. "I'm not saying your mother drowned saving you. I'm saying you don't know yet what she was doing. Neither does she, maybe, until you're standing in front of her."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you careful." Bryn folded the binoculars away. "Careful people live to be angry later."

* * *

Night came down the valley like water filling a lock, slow and total, and Headwaters House lit itself window by window until it hung above the dark gardens like a lantern ship at anchor. From the ridge it was almost lovely. That was the trick of it, Ada thought. Everything the Trust built was almost lovely—the weirs were almost natural, the relocations were almost voluntary, the order was almost worth the price. Almost was the load-bearing word of the whole century.

They ate cold food—what remained after the weir, eked with anything edible Bryn had scavenged—and took turns watching. Ada watched first, lying prone at the ridgeline with the binoculars, letting the house resolve itself out of the dark one lit window at a time.

It was near midnight when they saw the figure.

The lower gallery's windows had gone dim, most of them, banked down to embers of light—but one, far along the colonnade near the arch where the river emerged, still burned bright. And past that window, in the section of gallery open to the night air, someone was moving. Walking. Slowly, with a lamp, stopping at the arch, standing there a long time, then walking again. Pacing. The gait was familiar in a way that bypassed Ada's eyes entirely and seized something older, something in the chest, in the wrists, in the way their own feet suddenly wanted to move.

They knew that walk. They had watched it across excavation sites and train platforms and the length of a childhood kitchen. Shoulders slightly forward, hands clasped behind the back, head tilted down as if reading the ground. Ingrid Eriksen walked like a woman reading a sentence she hadn't finished.

Ada's breath went out of them.

"There," they managed. "Gallery. By the arch. Tell me you see—"

Bryn took the glasses. She was silent a long moment. When she lowered them her jaw was set hard.

"Might be anyone."

"It's her."

"Ada."

"*It's her.*"

"Then it's a trap with her inside it, or it isn't, and either way you go down that hill tonight and get caught, everything ends in a cell and she spends whatever's left of her life knowing you came for her and failed." Bryn's voice stayed level, but her hand closed hard on Ada's shoulder. "We do this like a dig. You taught me that, whether you meant to or not. We map it. We find the way in that nobody's watching. Then we go."

Ada made themselves breathe. Made themselves lower the glasses. The figure at the arch had stopped moving, stood with the lamp raised, and for one impossible moment seemed to look directly up at the ridge—at them—before turning back into the gallery's warm dark.

Hope and dread had stopped being separate things somewhere on the ridge that night. They had merged into a single weight behind Ada's sternum, dense as wet clay, and they understood, lying in the bracken with the river singing under the house that drank from it first, that there was no version of tomorrow in which they did not go down that hill. There was only the question of how, and whether Bryn came with them, and what would be left of them afterward.

"Bryn," they said finally. "How close can you get your boat to that arch?"

Her mouth curved, thin and wolfish, the first expression she'd worn all day that wasn't watchfulness.

"Close enough to make you swim the last bit," she said. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we read the water."

Above them, the lights of Headwaters House burned on, patient as a held breath, and beneath its foundations the river—their river, Ingrid's river, everyone's stolen river—went on flowing out into the dark, carrying toward the valley all the secrets it had been forced to hold.
