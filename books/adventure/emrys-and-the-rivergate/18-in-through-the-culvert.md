## 18. In Through the Culvert

They watched Headwaters House from the ridge until the light went from gold to pewter to nothing at all.

It sat in its valley like a thing that had grown there rather than been built—three storeys of pale stone, wings spread wide, a slate roof green with age. But Ada's eyes kept snagging on what was wrong with it. The gardens were too green for the season. The lawns drank from iron spigots that wept constant water while, downstream, whole fields stood brown and cracked. And beneath it all, if you lay flat on the ridge and pressed your ear to the ground, you could feel it: a vibration, low and ceaseless, like a heart beating under the floor of the world.

*The house is drinking,* Ada thought. *The house has always been drinking.*

"Your mother's map," Bryn said. She'd gone quiet over the last mile, her ferryman's ease folding away into something harder. "Show me again."

Ada unrolled the wardrobe rubbing they'd taken in Gallowford—the pencil marks Ingrid had left inside room four, transferred to paper by lamplight. Most of it was fragmentary: a contour line here, an arrow there, measurements in Ingrid's small, precise hand. But in the lower corner, almost hidden behind a flourish that might have been decorative, was a shape Ada recognized now because they'd spent three days learning to recognize shapes like it.

A culvert. An eighteenth-century drainage culvert, running from the river's edge into the hillside beneath the house's eastern wing. Ingrid had drawn it in section: a brick barrel-vault, shoulder-height, dry-laid, with a date scratched beside it—*1751*—and one word in her handwriting:

*Dry.*

"It'll be gated," Bryn said.

"Probably. But gates rust, and nobody guards a door they've forgotten exists." Ada rolled the map. "That's the whole point of a culvert. It's supposed to be invisible."

"And if your mother's wrong? If it's flooded?"

Ada looked down at the house, at the lights moving behind its tall windows like fish under ice. Somewhere inside those walls was Ingrid Eriksen—or whatever was left of her. Somewhere inside those walls was the answer to why the river ran backwards through their family's history.

"Then I get wet," Ada said.

* * *

They came down through the wood as full dark settled, moving the way Ada had learned to move through shell middens and spoil heaps: slow, weight on the outside of the foot, reading the ground before trusting it. The wardens patrolled on a rhythm—Ada had counted it from the ridge, two men, forty minutes between passes along the garden wall—and they slipped the wall at the gap where a beech root had heaved the stones apart, exactly where Ingrid's map said it would be.

*She walked this same route,* Ada realized, crouched in the shadow of the beech with their pulse loud in their ears. *Three weeks ago. Maybe standing right here.* The thought was strange comfort and strange grief at once, like holding something warm that had belonged to someone dead.

Except she wasn't dead. Ada held onto that. Whatever else was true, however angry they were going to be when they found her—she wasn't dead.

The river emerged from the hillside a hundred yards east of the house through a formal stone arch, all dressed up in carved keystones, the water coming out clean and cold and utterly controlled. That was the front door of the source, built to be admired. What Ada wanted was the servant's entrance.

They found it where the bank thickened with alder and bramble, downstream of the arch, half-swallowed by a century of growth. The culvert mouth was a round darkness in the earth, maybe four feet across, choked with leaf mold and the roots of an elder that had seeded itself in the mortar. Ada knelt and put their hand into the dark.

Cold air moved against their palm. Steady, faint, but moving—breathing inward.

"That's draft," they whispered. "Air's being pulled through. Means it connects to somewhere open on the other end." They looked back at Bryn, whose face was a pale smudge in the dark. "Your mother's map says it comes up in the cellars."

"I'm not your mother."

"No," Ada said. "But she drew it true so far. Every mark so far has been true."

Bryn was silent a moment. Then she reached into her coat and pressed something into Ada's hand: the stub of a candle, three inches long, wrapped in waxed cloth, and a box of matches worn soft at the corners. "Grandmother's," she said. "For the crossings. You don't go under the earth without light someone loved you enough to carry."

Ada closed their fingers around it. It was absurd how much the small weight steadied them.

"You're sure about staying out?"

"Somebody has to count the wardens," Bryn said. "And somebody has to be at the wall when you come back out running." She said *when*, not *if*, and Ada could have kissed her for the grammar. "Forty minutes between passes. If you're not back by the third pass after dawn, I'm ringing every bell between here and Gallowford and letting them hang me for it."

"They'd never let you get close enough to—"

"Then you'd better come back," Bryn said, and turned away to watch the house, conversation finished.

Ada scraped the leaf mold from the culvert mouth, lay flat, and went in feet first, the way diggers enter tunnels—because feet can feel what hands can't see, and because a body goes into the dark easier backward than forward.

* * *

The culvert was exactly what Ingrid had drawn: a barrel vault of hand-made brick, each one stamped with a maker's mark worn nearly smooth, laid dry without mortar in the old way so the water that once ran through it could seep through its skin. It was dry now. It smelled of cold stone and old air and, underneath that, something mineral and strange—a smell like struck flint, like the inside of a cave, like the source itself breathing through the bones of the hill.

Ada crab-walked forward on heels and palms, the candle unlit, saving it, counting their progress in body-lengths. Ten. Twenty. Behind them the circle of night sky at the mouth shrank to a coin, then a star, then nothing. Ahead: absolute black, and the hum.

They'd felt it from the ridge, but in here it was different. In here it wasn't a vibration in the ground—it was a sound, or the ghost of a sound, a note below hearing that lived in the teeth and the breastbone. Machinery. Something turning, somewhere ahead, endlessly turning. The hill itself had a pulse, and the pulse was manufactured.

At thirty body-lengths the culvert forked.

Ada stopped, heart hammering, and lit the candle.

The match flared and the dark flinched back. Two passages opened ahead—one continuing level, one descending by a shallow rake of steps cut into the brick floor, each step slick with condensation. On the wall between them, in chalk that had gone grey with age but was still legible, someone had written: *E. WING — 12 RISES — MIND THE DROP.*

The letters were large, practical, hurried. And though Ada had no proof—no photograph, no sample to compare—they knew the hand. Everyone who has ever been watched over by a parent knows their handwriting the way they know their own heartbeat. Ingrid had been in this tunnel. Ingrid had left a signpost the way she'd left pencil marks in a wardrobe: quietly, precisely, for whoever came looking.

*Mind the drop.*

Ada took the descending passage.

Twelve rises. They counted them, pressing each foot down before committing weight, and on the eleventh their heel found nothing and their stomach dropped and they froze, arms out, candle guttering, before their exploring toe located the twelfth step—set lower than the others, a trap for anyone counting carelessly. A drop of eight inches onto a landing where the culvert widened into something else entirely.

Ada breathed. Laughed once, silently, at nothing funny. Went down.

The passage opened into a gallery.

Not a cellar—a gallery, long and straight, its brick vaulting giving way after twenty feet to dressed stone, to actual architecture, as if the house above had sent roots downward and grown rooms in the dark. Iron pipes ran along one wall, thick as a man's waist, wrapped in felt insulation gone to rot, and from them came the hum, louder now, a chorus of pumps and valves somewhere deeper in. Along the other wall—

Doors.

Iron doors, set flush into the stone, ranked one after another into the distance, each with a heavy lock plate and a stenciled number. RT-001. RT-002. RT-003. The stencil was crisp, official, recent. Ada raised the candle to the nearest and read the smaller line beneath the number:

*RIVERGATE TRUST ARCHIVE — SEALED BY ORDER OF THE BOARD.*

Row upon row upon row. The gallery stretched further than the candlelight would follow, and every few paces another door, another number, another seal. Ada walked slowly down the line, boots silent on the stone, and did the arithmetic they didn't want to do. If each door held even one room of records—if these were the parish books, the deeds, the correspondence, the maps of five drowned villages and a century of managed floods—then the truth of the Rivergate wasn't hidden in the valley.

It was filed.

Alphabetized. Numbered. Kept at a constant temperature by machines that hummed day and night, guarded by men who policed the gardens above, owned by a board that signed its name in stencil. The Trust hadn't destroyed the evidence of what it had done. It had *curated* it. There was something worse in that—something Ada couldn't yet find the bottom of. Fire would have been mercy. This was a decision, renewed annually, to keep every stolen letter, every forged deed, every payment record, close enough to touch and locked away forever.

Their breath fogged in the cold. Their candle flame leaned steadily toward the far end of the gallery, toward the draft, toward whatever lay beyond the last door.

RT-047. RT-048. Then the doors stopped, and the gallery ended at a junction: stairs rising toward the house above, and a corridor continuing level toward the sound of water—real water now, audible past the machinery, a low rushing hush like the sea heard through a wall.

Taped to the wall at eye height, a piece of paper, fresh, white, deliberate.

Ada took it down with fingers that had begun, despite everything, to shake.

It was a single line, in pencil, in that same unmistakable hand:

*If you're reading this, you were supposed to turn back at the fork. Since you didn't—RT-012. Bring the light.*

Below it, a small drawing: a key, sketched in three strokes.

Ada stood in the humming cold with the note in their hand and felt the strangest sensation rise through the fear—not hope exactly, and not relief, but the fierce, furious tenderness of being *expected*. Of being known. Somewhere above or beyond these sealed doors, Ingrid Eriksen had stood at this wall and written instructions for a child she hadn't seen in years, betting everything on the certainty that the child would disobey the first instruction and follow the second.

*You shouldn't have come,* she'd say, when Ada found her. Ada already knew she'd say it. They were already composing the answer.

They turned back down the gallery, candle high, counting doors.

*RT-009. RT-010. RT-011.*

*RT-012.*

The lock plate hung open. The door stood an inch ajar, and from the gap came a thread of warm yellow light, and the smell of lamp oil and old paper—and, very faintly, under the hum of the machines, a voice. Low. Reading aloud. Practiced, patient, unhurried.

Ada knew the voice the way they knew their own heartbeat.

They put their hand flat against the cold iron, feeling it tremble faintly with the machinery's pulse, and understood that whatever waited on the other side of this door—anger, grief, reunion, ruin—there was no version of the world, now, in which they did not open it.

Ada pushed.
