## 19. The Archive of Drowned Things

The culvert spat me into a world that hummed.

Not the river's hum—that low throat-note I'd been hearing for days, the sound of water moving through stone with intent. This was mechanical, electrical, a vibration I felt in my back teeth before I heard it at all. The air was cold the way cellars are cold, cold the way hospitals are cold: temperature as policy. My breath came out in small ghosts and drifted toward a row of brass vents set into the wall like gills.

I stood in a corridor of dressed granite, dry as a sermon, though the river ran somewhere below me—I could feel it through the soles of Bryn's borrowed boots, a pulse in the floor. Pipes ran along the ceiling, fat and sweating, tagged with little enamel labels in a hand too neat to be anything but institutional. *G-4. G-5. SUPPLY, NON-POTABLE.* Someone had organized this underworld the way an entomologist organizes a drawer of beetles.

Ingrid's map fragment had ended at a penciled arrow and the word *below*, which was exactly the kind of precision I'd spent ten years resenting and thirty seconds being grateful for. The culvert had been on it, dated 1789, drawn from memory by someone who'd crawled it during some earlier century's trouble. Trouble, apparently, was cyclical here.

I killed my headlamp to a dull ember and listened.

Machinery, somewhere ahead—a rhythmic chuffing, patient as digestion. Footsteps? No. Just the house above breathing through its pipes. Bryn was outside in the dark with the boat, watching the warden patrol rotate past the boathouse slip, counting seconds between lanterns the way she counted everything. If she saw three flashes of her own lamp across the water, it meant come in after me. If she saw four—

Don't think about four.

I moved down the corridor, one hand trailing the granite. The walls were seamless, old work, older than the Trust—this part of the underbelly might genuinely have been a drainage system once, before someone looked at all this convenient darkness beneath their elegant manor and thought *storage*. The humming grew. A door appeared in the left wall, steel, painted the green of old filing cabinets, with a card frame beside it holding a typed label:

**ARCHIVE ANNEX — SALVAGE COLLECTIONS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

Salvage. The word sat wrong in my mouth even before I touched the handle. Salvage is what you call things pulled from wrecks when no one living claims them. It presumes the absence of owners. It presumes the sea got there first.

The door wasn't locked. That frightened me more than a lock would have. Locked doors say *we fear discovery*. Open ones say *no one has ever come looking*.

* * *

Inside was a room the size of a church, and it was arranged like one.

Shelving ran in long aisles where pews should have been, iron-framed, rising twelve feet into shadow. Each shelf held boxes—acid-free board, numbered, labeled—and each label carried a name that stopped my heart and restarted it out of rhythm.

**MERROW. PARISH RECORDS, 1801–1892.**

**MERROW. DOMESTIC EFFECTS, LOT 44.**

**HALLOW FEN. SCHOOL REGISTERS.**

**COLD HARBOUR. PHOTOGRAPHIC PLATES.**

Villages. Not the villages on any map I'd ever seen—the maps said these places had never existed—but the villages from the ledger page I'd fished out of a tide cave, the ones paid annually since 1893 to *accept relocation*. Here they were. Not relocated. *Collected.*

I walked down the first aisle with my lamp turned so low it barely lit my own hands, and I opened the nearest box because my body did it before my mind could vote.

A christening gown, folded with archival precision, yellowed to the color of weak tea. Under it, a wooden rattle with three teeth marks worn into the ring. Under that, a hairbrush with a strand of silver hair still caught in the bristles, kept because whoever catalogued this had decided even that was data. A tag hung from the box's corner in faded copperplate: *Merrow lot 44. Contents verified against inventory, March 1901. Condition: fair.*

Condition: fair. A dead child's rattle. Fair.

I put the lid back with both hands because one hand was shaking too much to do it alone.

This was what the Trust had done with five drowned villages—not just taken the land, not just rewritten the maps, but gathered up every object that proved people had lived there, sealed it underground, and filed it. They hadn't destroyed the evidence. Destruction would have been almost honest. They'd *curated* it, the way you curate anything dangerous: contained, catalogued, available only to those already convinced it meant nothing.

And the families? The ledger had told me what the families got. Annual payments, signatures, silence. You can't mourn properly when the grave goods are locked in a stranger's basement. You can't even prove your grandmother existed except by her signature on a release form.

My mother had known this. Ingrid Eriksen had stood in this exact cold air, or air like it, and seen these shelves, and that was why she hadn't come home. That was why three weeks ago she'd walked into a tide cave and then out of the world. She'd found the place where grief was stored, alphabetically.

I made myself keep walking.

* * *

The second room was photographs.

They hung in long glass-fronted cases like specimens in a natural history museum, and maybe that was the most accurate description anyone would ever manage, because what else do you call a hundred faces pinned behind glass, ordered by village and year, each with a typed caption?

*Mrs. Aldous Penhale, Merrow, b. 1841, d. 1893. Relocated to Gallowford.*

Relocated. As if she were furniture. As if dying the same year your village was dynamited into the riverbed were a change of address.

I found children in the third case. School photographs, the stiff formal kind where everyone looks like they're being arrested. Rows of small faces from Hallow Fen, 1888, and I caught myself doing the thing I do at digs—reading the image stratigraphically, noting the clothing, the building behind them, the chalkboard with its faint arithmetic still legible. Professional habit. Professional distance. The distance lasted until I noticed that the boy in the second row, fourth from the left, was making a face at the camera, cross-eyed and grinning while everyone else obeyed the lens, and something in my chest went over like a wheelbarrow.

He'd been somebody's problem. Somebody's joy. He'd grown up or he hadn't, but either way he'd ended up here, behind glass, in a basement, owned by men who measured the valley in kilowatts.

"You're not specimens," I said aloud, and my voice fell flat in the humming dark, and I hated how much it sounded like a prayer in an empty church—addressed to no one who could hear.

There was a desk in the corner of the photograph room, oak, tidy, with a visitor's log lying open. I shouldn't have looked. I looked. The last entry was three weeks and two days old, in handwriting I would have known blindfolded, upside down, dissolved in water.

*I.E. — research access granted per arrangement — V.*

Per arrangement. My mother had signed herself into this crypt like a guest. Had walked these aisles. Had maybe stood where I was standing, looking at these same faces, and traded something—I didn't yet know what—for the right to look.

I copied the date into my notebook with fingers that wouldn't quite steady, closed the log, and understood that I was now standing inside the reason she'd left me. Not the excuse. The reason. Some discoveries are so heavy you can't carry them and carry a child too, and Ingrid Eriksen had always been better at carrying discoveries than people.

The anger arrived on schedule, right on time, punctual as the tide. I let it. Anger was fuel and I was far underground.

* * *

The third room was the one that broke me.

It was smaller, deeper in, and colder—the vents here breathed directly onto the shelves, and everything wore the grey bloom of long refrigeration. Textiles. Books. Paper. The fragile things, kept at the temperature of deliberate forgetting.

I was three aisles in, cataloguing out of pure reflex now—my notebook filling with box numbers, dates, a running index that some future tribunal might thank me for—when I saw the spine.

Black leather, tooled in gold, the gold rubbed away along the edges where a century of hands had gripped it. A family Bible, the big old kind, the kind used as a lectern and a birth register and a weapon in arguments about who inherited what.

The tooling read: **ERIKSEN**.

No. No, that was coincidence, that was a common enough name, that was—

I took it off the shelf and the cold of it bit through my gloves, and I opened it to the front pages where such books keep their true contents, and there it was in fading iron-gall ink, in a hand I knew from a hundred letters that had stopped coming, in a hand I knew from birthday cards and margin notes and the flyleaf of every book she'd ever given me:

*Ada Wren Eriksen, born 14th of March, to Ingrid Solvang Eriksen.*

My name. My birth, recorded in ink, in a basement, in a box-lot, in a collection.

Above mine, generations. *Henrik Eriksen. Marta Solvang—* no, different Marta, an older one, the name cycling through families like a bell-note. And then, further up the page, in a stiffer hand, in ink gone brown as old blood, entries from the 1890s, and I read them the way you read a diagnosis, twice, because the first time doesn't take:

*Elias Eriksen, signatory to the Rivergate Charter, 1893.*

Signatory.

The word went through me like cold water through a breach. Not victim. Not neighbor. Signatory. My great-great-whatever, my blood, my name, written at the bottom of the document that had drowned Old Merrow and Hallow Fen and Cold Harbour and the rest—written among the founders, the dynamiters, the men who'd stood on the ridge and watched the valleys fill and called it progress and then collected the villagers' christening gowns and hairbrushes and Bibles and filed them in the dark.

The Bible in my hands was lot-tagged like all the others. *Eriksen family effects. Verified 1904.* They had catalogued their own. Or—one founder keeping his family's book safe in the archive he built over the drowning, the way a man keeps a trophy, or a confession, or both.

I sat down on the concrete floor with the Bible open in my lap, and the humming filled my ears, and I understood—with the total, useless clarity that only arrives after the damage is done—what Vane had offered my mother, and what she'd refused, or accepted, or was still deciding. He hadn't wanted her silenced. He'd wanted her *enrolled*. An Eriksen, a charter-signatory's descendant, a famous archaeologist, lending her name to the story the Trust told about itself. *Even the scholars agree. Even the descendants consent.*

And if she wouldn't enroll, well. The archive had room.

I pressed my forehead against the cold cover and let myself shake, quietly, the way you learn to cry in shared houses. My whole life I'd thought our name was just a weight—mother too famous, child too visible, the estrangement a private weather between two stubborn people. It had never occurred to me that the name itself was evidence. That I'd been walking around my whole invisible life carrying a signature in my blood, and that somewhere under a beautiful house, men with neat handwriting had known it all along.

Bryn's grandmother had been born in Old Merrow. Bryn had grown up on payments she half-knew the source of. And I had grown up downstream of my own family's crime without ever once thinking to ask why the water tasted of other people's houses.

We were all of us lot-tagged. All of us shelved.

I wiped my face on my sleeve, which is what diggers do instead of grieving properly, and I made a decision with the calm, terrible steadiness that comes after crying: this Bible was going back upstream with me. Not to be hidden. To be *read*. Aloud. In daylight. In Gallowford, in the fen towns, in every kitchen where someone's grandmother had gone quiet whenever the river came up in conversation.

You don't salvage a thing like this. You return it.

* * *

I was rewrapping the Bible in my spare shirt when I heard it.

Footsteps. Not machinery—footsteps, unhurried, on the granite corridor outside, accompanied by a thin scratch I couldn't place until it paused and resumed: a pen. Someone writing as they walked. Someone who belonged here so completely they annotated their own corridors.

The light under the archive door brightened—lantern-yellow, not electric—and a shadow crossed it, and stopped.

Through the gap beneath the door I could see two feet in worn leather shoes, and beside them, resting on the floor, a canvas field bag with a strap repaired in red twine.

I knew that repair. I'd watched my mother mend that strap at a campsite in the north country when I was nine, complaining that she cared more about her equipment than her luggage, meaning me, and she'd said, *the luggage carries the equipment, Ada, it deserves respect,* and we'd laughed, or she had, and I had pretended not to.

Three weeks and two days since that bag had last been logged in.

The feet shifted. The pen scratched once more—on the doorframe itself, I realized, adding something to the wood—and then the figure moved on, unhurried, deeper into the humming dark, and I sat alone in the Archive of Drowned Things with my family's Bible against my chest and my heart going like a fish in a net.

She was alive. She was *here*. And she was walking away from me down a corridor built by the people who owned us both.

I gave her ten seconds. Then I followed.
