## 20. A Voice From the Gallery

The figure moved the way water moves under ice—there and not there, a suggestion of motion in the lamplight at the far end of the gallery.

Ada pressed themselves flat against the cold stone between two sealed archive doors, heart hammering against the ribs like something trying to get out. The culvert air still clung to them, river-damp and mineral, and their breath came in shallow pulls they tried to make silent. Above, the house's machinery hummed its low constant note, so steady Ada had stopped hearing it minutes ago and now heard it again all at once, the way you hear your own pulse when you're frightened enough.

The figure passed through a spill of light.

Grey hair, cropped short. A canvas coat Ada knew the weight of, having carried it once over their shoulders across a rain-soaked campsite in a country whose name they'd had to look up afterward, because their mother had never bothered to say it aloud. The figure moved with Ingrid Eriksen's particular gait—unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who believed the ground would hold her because she had already decided it would.

Ada's first thought was not joy. Their first thought was *she's older*. Their second was a fury so sudden and complete it made their vision swim, and they had to grip the edge of an archive door to keep from calling out—because the word that rose in their throat was not *Mum* but *how dare you*, and shouting it here, in the humming underbelly of Headwaters House, would kill them both.

The figure stopped at a table beneath a caged lamp. Sat. Bowed her head over papers.

Ada counted to thirty. Then twenty more. The wardens they'd seen on the ridge were outside; the gallery, as far as they could tell from two hours of watching the culvert mouth and the service stair, belonged to whoever the Trust had put down here, and that appeared to be one person, one lamp, one table.

They stepped out of the shadow.

* * *

The stone floor gave away their footsteps—of course it did; everything in this house was built to announce intruders to itself—and Ingrid's head came up with the sharp economy of a heron striking. For one suspended second, mother and child looked at each other across forty feet of archive, and Ada saw the whole catastrophe of recognition happen in her face: the flinch, the narrowing, the terrible arithmetic of *how much did you see, how much do you know, how fast can this be undone.*

"You shouldn't have come."

Not *Ada*. Not their name, not even *hello*. Four words, pitched low and flat, and delivered with the same tone she'd used to tell them, aged eleven, that they'd mislabeled a stratigraphic layer. Disappointment. Correction. As if Ada were an error in the record.

"You're welcome," Ada whispered, and hated that their voice cracked on it.

Ingrid rose from the table. Up close, in the caged light, she was thinner than the photographs in Ada's head, and there was a tremor in her left hand that she folded into her coat pocket the moment she noticed Ada noticing. Her eyes did the thing they had always done—catalogued Ada head to toe, inventorying. Mud to the knee. A borrowed oilskin two sizes too big. Hands nicked and dirt-blackened from the culvert's rusted ladder rungs.

"You're alone?" she asked. Not *are you hurt*. Not *I've missed you*. A tactical question.

"Someone's outside."

"Who?"

"Bryn Halvard."

Something moved behind Ingrid's eyes—relief, maybe, or its opposite. "The ferry-woman. She takes Trust money."

"So do you, from the look of it." Ada gestured at the table: the lamp, the papers, the neat stacks of foolscap covered in Ingrid's small, ruthless handwriting. "What is this? What are you doing here, Mum? Three weeks. Three weeks you've been—" They couldn't finish it. The word *dead* sat behind their teeth like a stone. *Three weeks I thought you were dead. Three weeks I dug you out of a tidal cave with my bare hands, looking for you.*

Ingrid's jaw tightened. "Keep your voice down. The gallery carries sound up the light-wells. There are men on the floor above who would very much like to know how I got in and out of here, and I'd rather they keep believing I don't."

"Got in and out." Ada laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud, and they clamped a hand over their own mouth. "You make it sound like a dig site. Like a *permit*."

"Sit down," Ingrid said. "And be quiet, and I will explain exactly as much as you have time to hear."

"I don't want—"

"Ada." And there it was, finally, the crack in her voice, thin as a hairline in glaze, there and gone. "Please."

Ada had spent twenty-six years cataloguing their mother's tones the way other children learned lullabies. The imperious tone. The distracted tone. The tone she used on benefactors and the softer, rarer one she used on students who reminded her of herself. In twenty-six years, Ada could count on one hand the number of times Ingrid Eriksen had said *please* and meant it.

They sat.

* * *

The table was a scholar's table, absurdly domestic in the stone gallery: two lamps, a tin of pencils, a kettle on a hotplate, a camp cot folded against the wall with a blanket folded at its foot in perfect quarters. Ingrid's world, miniaturized and made portable, the way she had always made her life portable—Ada had grown up in a dozen rented rooms that looked exactly like this table, down to the chipped mug holding the pencils.

The papers were the shock.

Ada knew old documents. They'd grown up handling vellum and rag paper the way other children handled sand. What lay under Ingrid's hands was a charter—huge, brittle, its ink gone the brown of dried blood—and beside it, Ingrid's translation in progress, line after line of her cramped, exact hand.

"The founding charter of the Rivergate Trust," Ingrid said quietly. "1893. There are three copies in the world. One is in a vault in the capital. One is in the Trust's own museum, behind glass, where visitors read the sanitized first four pages." She turned the vellum toward Ada. "The fifth and sixth pages were cut out of that copy a long time ago. This one—" her hand rested on the document the way she'd once rested it on Ada's head, a lifetime ago, in a way that had felt like blessing and ownership at once— "this one is complete."

Ada didn't touch it. "How do you have it?"

"Because they need it read." Ingrid's mouth twisted. "The Trust has a problem, Ada. Their own founding documents are dangerous. The men who run the Trust today—the board, the families, the Vanes—they are fourth-generation inheritors of a lie, and most of them have never read past the sanitized pages themselves. But the old charter is the legal root of every water right, every land claim, every drowned village they've never had to answer for. And there are clauses in it. Obligations. Liabilities. Written in a legal Latin mixed with valley dialect that no one alive reads fluently."

"And you do."

"I'm the best in the world at what I do," Ingrid said, without a flicker of false modesty, and Ada felt the old familiar heat rise in their chest—that childhood fury at a mother who stated her own excellence as plain fact while never once stating her love. "They found me in the spring. I'd been following the water for two years by then. I'd found the cave. I'd found the ledger pages. I'd found enough to know that the 1893 charter was the keystone, and that it was kept here." She paused. "They were watching me the whole way. They let me get to the door, and then they opened it, and they made me an offer."

"What kind of offer?"

"Access in exchange for labor. I translate. They keep the translations. I live." Ingrid said it flatly, but her folded hand trembled in her pocket, and Ada saw it. "I am not a prisoner in the way you're imagining. There are no bars. I could walk up those stairs any day I liked and out the front door, and there would be a car waiting, and it would drive me anywhere I wanted to go, and somewhere along that drive the car would stop, and that would be the end of the story. That's how it's done now. No bars. Just weather you can't survive."

"And you stayed."

"And I stayed," Ingrid said, "because a translation can be slowed. A translation can be *wrong* in small ways that no one catches for years. I have been translating this charter for three weeks, and every page I finish is a page the Trust thinks it owns, and every page I finish is also—" she looked at Ada, and for the first time her voice lost its lecture-hall steadiness— "a page I have memorized. All of it. Every clause. Every name. Every signature. It's all in here." She tapped her temple. "They can burn the paper. They can't burn what I've kept."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, a door closed, and both of them went still as stone until the hum of the house swallowed the sound again.

Ada looked at their mother—the folded hand, the quartered blanket, the camp cot, the three weeks—and the fury came back up their throat, and this time they let a little of it out, in a whisper, because a whisper was all they could afford.

"I dug a midden looking for you. I dove a tidal pool. I read your notes until my eyes bled, and I followed a river through a drowned village and a weir and a fen, and I slept in a church tower, and I did all of it believing you were *dead*, Mum. Believing I'd find your body at the end of it. Do you know what that's like? Do you have any—"

"I know exactly what it's like." Ingrid's voice came out low and cracked clean through. "My mother walked out of a dig in '71 and was never seen again. I was nineteen. I spent eleven years looking for her, Ada, and I found her grave in a pauper's field in a country I couldn't pronounce, and I swore—" She stopped. Breathed. The tremor in her hand had escaped the pocket now; she pressed it flat against the table. "I swore my own child would never spend eleven years looking for me. And then I found what I found, and I understood that the only way to keep you safe was to be somewhere you couldn't follow, and I told myself that was different. I told myself a lot of things." She laughed, short and terrible. "It turns out I am exactly the kind of mother who makes her child dig through middens looking for her. The apple doesn't fall far. It just falls further away, and calls it protection."

Ada hadn't known their grandmother had vanished. Hadn't known about the pauper's field. Twenty-six years, and there were whole continents in this woman that Ada had never been shown, and the unfairness of it—*she got to know her mother's grave and I didn't get to know whether my mother was alive*—rose up and choked them.

"You could have written," Ada said. "A postcard. One line. *Alive. Don't look.*"

"A postcard is a trail," Ingrid said. "And then Vane came to your boathouse, didn't he. I know he did. It's in his schedule." She said it like a field observation, and Ada flinched, and Ingrid saw the flinch, and something in her face folded. "He offered you money for the cave finds."

"He searched my home while his car idled outside."

"He'll have taken nothing and left everything slightly wrong. Drawers a centimeter off. That's his signature. He wants you to know he was there. Fear is his preferred currency—it's cheaper than payment and it appreciates." Ingrid pulled the charter back toward herself, and her voice reassembled itself, brick by brick, into the lecture tone. "Which is why you cannot be here. He let me live because I'm useful and because I have no one. The moment he learns you exist—really exist, not as a shoreline scavenger but as *my* child, the child of a charter signatory's line—you stop being a stray and become leverage. And he will use you, Ada. Not cruelly. That's the worst of it. He'll use you kindly, reasonably, with every justification laid out in order, and you will find yourself agreeing to things, and it will take you years to understand what you agreed to."

"Then tell me what not to agree to." Ada leaned forward. "You said you've memorized it. All of it. So tell me. What did they do in 1893? What's on the pages they cut out?"

Ingrid went very still.

It was the stillness Ada had seen a hundred times at the edge of a trench, when their mother's trowel found something that changed the site's whole story—the stillness of a person deciding how much truth a moment could hold. Ada had hated that stillness their whole life, because it always meant the answer was being *managed*, portioned out, decided for them.

"Don't," Ada said. "Don't do the thing where you decide what I can carry. I carried the fen. I carried the weir. I carried three weeks of believing you were dead, and I'm still carrying it, and I'm still here. I can carry it."

"You can't," Ingrid said softly. "Not all of it. No one can. That's rather the point of what they built."

"Try me."

For a long moment the only sound was the kettle's ghost-heat ticking as it cooled, and the great muffled hum of the house above, and the river itself—Ada could hear it now that they listened, a deep pressure-sound beneath the floor, the source water moving through the dark under the manor like blood under skin.

Ingrid looked at the charter. Then at the stairs. Then, last, at Ada—and Ada watched their mother make a decision, and recognized it, because it was the same decision Ada had made on the mudflats when the tide was coming in and the cave was right there: the decision to stop being careful.

"Give me your hand," Ingrid said.

Ada gave it. Their mother's hand was cold and dry and it shook, and it closed around theirs with a grip that was stronger than it had any right to be, and Ingrid Eriksen drew one long breath and began, in a voice pitched for a whisper and carrying the weight of thirty years:

"In the spring of 1893, five men met at this house. They owned the mills, the ferries, the weirs, and the debts of everyone in the valley. And the river—the real river, the free one—rose out of a spring system under this hill, a dozen vents, braided, seasonal, flooding when it pleased. You couldn't mill it. You couldn't sell it. You couldn't insure against it. So the five men bought the hill, and they drilled, and they blasted, and they collapsed the natural vents and channeled the whole source into one controlled emergence under this house, and they built the reservoir gates, and they made the water *obedient*."

Her grip tightened.

"And the water, with nowhere to go, went down. Into the valley. And Old Merrow drowned in a single night, and the maps were redrawn before the funerals were finished, and the charter they wrote called it *improvement of navigation*, and the ledger called it *relocation*, and every flood since—every single one, Ada, every drowned field, every village emptied, every family paid off and scattered—has not been weather. It has been *policy*. It has been maintenance. The Trust doesn't protect the valley from the river. The Trust *is* the river. It has been holding the valley hostage for a hundred and thirty years, and charging it rent."

The lamp hissed. Ada's hand was still in their mother's, and they could not have said which of them was holding on harder.

"The source isn't forbidden," Ingrid finished, barely a breath. "It's *evidence*. The whole river, from here to the sea, is a crime scene, and the water has been washing the blood off it for a century. And I am the only person alive who has read the whole confession."

Ada opened their mouth—

And the light above the stairs changed.

A bar of lamplight from the floor above swept down the stairwell, and with it came footsteps—measured, unhurried, two sets—and Ingrid moved like a woman half her age, snuffing the second lamp, sweeping the translation pages into a canvas portfolio, pressing the charter flat under a stack of blank foolscap, and pushing Ada, with one hand flat against their chest, back into the shadow between the archive doors.

"Whoever it is," she breathed, "you are a surveyor's assistant who got lost. You came down for a records request. You know nothing. *Say nothing.*"

The footsteps reached the bottom of the stairs.

And the voice that came with them, warm and courteous and carrying perfectly in the cold stone, was one Ada had heard once across a boathouse threshold, offering money with a smile:

"Well now. How very interesting. It seems the gallery has acquired a second scholar tonight."

Aldous Vane stepped into the light.
