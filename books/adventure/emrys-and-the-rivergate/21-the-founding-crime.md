## 21. The Founding Crime

The document was heavier than it looked.

Ingrid felt that weight every time she lifted the charter from its case—not the paper itself, which had gone soft and foxed as an old leaf, but what lived inside it. Ninety-one years of weight. Five villages of weight. She carried it now through the lower gallery with both hands, the way you carry a sleeping child or a loaded thing, and Ada walked beside her in the lamplight without speaking.

The archive's underbelly hummed around them. Pumps somewhere below the floor, keeping the river where the Trust wanted it. Cold air moved through the corridor in slow breaths, smelling of machine oil and old vellum and, beneath both, the mineral sweetness of water that had never seen sun. Their footsteps rang on iron plating laid over stone. Somewhere above them, wardens made their rounds; their boots were a weather Ingrid had learned to read.

"Here," she said, and set the charter down on the reading table—a slab of green marble, absurdly elegant, bolted to the floor as if even furniture here feared being washed away.

Ada stood on the far side, arms crossed, and did not touch it.

Ingrid understood. She had spent three weeks in this room before she'd been willing to touch it herself.

* * *

Three weeks ago—no. Longer than that now. Six weeks, seven. Time moved strangely below Headwaters House, where there were no windows and the pumps kept a tide of their own.

She had come down the culvert on a Tuesday, if Tuesdays still meant anything up there, with a headlamp and her mother's field notebook and the certainty that she would be in and out before anyone knew. She'd found the first archive door by accident, following warm air where cold should have been, and the door had opened onto a room the size of a church: shelves floor to ceiling, and on them, boxed and labeled and catalogued in copperplate hand, everything the valley had ever been made to forget.

Parish registers. School photographs. A christening gown pressed flat under glass like a moth specimen. Deeds. Diaries. The entire paper body of five villages, exhumed and embalmed.

She had stood in the doorway for a long time, her breath loud in her own ears, and thought: *this is what happened to them. Not drowning. Filing.*

Vane had found her there at dawn. She'd heard his shoes on the iron plating—leather soles, unhurried—and had time to consider running and reject it, because running meant leaving, and leaving meant all of this went back into the dark with no one the wiser.

"Dr. Eriksen," he'd said, and offered her tea, which was somehow worse than a gun. "You've found our library."

"Your tomb," she'd said.

He'd considered that with genuine interest, tilting his head like a man appraising a painting. "Those are not mutually exclusive categories," he said. "Most of the valuable things in the world are kept in tombs. That's rather the point of tombs."

Then he'd shown her the charter.

Not because she asked. Because he wanted to. That was the thing about Aldous Vane that Ada would need to understand, Ingrid thought now, watching her child across the marble table—that he was not ashamed. He believed. There was no mask to strip away, no moment where the decent man underneath showed through. What you saw was the whole architecture, load-bearing walls and all.

"You're an archaeologist," he'd said, unrolling the document with white gloves and the tenderness of a priest. "You of all people know that history is not what happened. It's what we can prove happened. Sit down, Dr. Eriksen. Let me show you what we can prove."

* * *

Present again. The lamp between them threw their shadows huge against the archive doors.

"Read it," Ingrid said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Aloud. I need—I need to hear someone else say it. I've said it to myself too many times. It starts sounding like something I invented."

Ada unfolded their arms slowly. Crossed the room. Sat.

The charter's first page was a preamble in beautiful Victorian copperplate, all flourishes and civic virtue—the Rivergate Valley Conservation and Improvement Trust, established for the perpetual benefit of the valley's inhabitants, its waters, its works. Ada read it in a flat voice, and Ingrid let them, because the preamble was the armor. You had to get through the armor first.

The second page was different.

The handwriting changed. The flourishes fell away. This was the working text, the engineers' annex, and it described—in the calm, itemizing language of men who had purchased dynamite by the crate—the spring system.

"The source," Ada read, and stopped. Looked up. "It says *springs*. Plural."

"Say the rest."

"'The valley's flow issues from a natural system of seven principal springs along the northern escarpment, interconnected by subterranean channels of considerable antiquity...'" Ada's finger tracked the line. "'...which system, being irregular in its seasonal output, renders reliable management of downstream levels impracticable...'"

"Impracticable," Ingrid repeated. "That word cost four hundred lives."

Ada didn't answer. They read on, and Ingrid watched their face while they did it—the way their jaw tightened, the way they went very still in the particular way they'd gone still since childhood when something hurt too much to flinch at.

"'...it has therefore been resolved,'" Ada read, slower now, "'that the said spring system shall be consolidated by controlled demolition into a single managed emergence, the flow thereof to be governed by gates and reservoir works constructed at the Trust's expense...'" A pause. "'...the consequent raising of the water table in the southern lowlands having been assessed and found acceptable.'"

"Found acceptable," Ada said. "By whom?"

"By the six men who signed that page." Ingrid turned to the last leaf. "Look at the signatures."

Ada looked. And Ingrid saw the exact moment it landed, because Ada's hand came up off the table and pressed flat against their own sternum, as if something in there had shifted position.

"Eriksen," Ada said. "That's—"

"Tomas Eriksen. My great-grandfather. Your great-great-grandfather." Ingrid heard her own voice go distant, lecture-hall steady, the voice she used when feeling would otherwise stop her mouth. "One of six founding signatories. He owned the mill rights at Gallowford. The other five were the Vane interests, two farming magnates, and the man who drew the new maps."

"The maps." Ada's voice had gone thin. "The ones that don't show Old Merrow."

"The ones that never showed any of them." Ingrid pulled the second document from the case—an atlas, 1894, gorgeous, authoritative, the edition every school in three counties used for forty years. She opened it to the valley and laid it beside the charter. "Five villages on the southern lowlands. Merrow, Cael Ford, Hobb's Landing, Tarn Wick, and the little one nobody even names anymore because we lost the name—the one your bell-keeper's people came from, probably. All of them sitting on land the engineers' annex calls 'acceptable.' And here—" she touched the map, the clean blank green where communities had stood for nine hundred years, "—here they are after. Meadowland. Uninhabited. As if they'd never been."

"They drowned them on purpose." Ada wasn't asking. Ada was reading the annex again, lips moving, doing the arithmetic of it. "They blew up the springs to control the flow, and controlling the flow meant flooding the lowlands, and they *knew*, they wrote it down, they assessed it—"

"And then they paid for it." Ingrid slid the ledger fragment across—the one Ada had carried half the length of the river, the one they'd nearly died for in a cave the sea only opens sometimes. "You already have the third document. I didn't know that until Vane told me you'd been asking questions downstream. Annual payments, beginning 1893. 'To families accepting relocation.' Do you understand what that ledger actually is, Ada? It's not compensation. Compensation implies a wrong acknowledged. It's an annuity. Silence, purchased annually, indexed for inflation, renewed every year for ninety-one years. Some families took it for a generation and forgot why it came. Some families—" and here her voice did finally crack, just slightly, just enough, "—some families became ferry-women who are still being paid to watch the water."

Ada looked up sharply. "Bryn?"

"Bryn knows what she knows. That's hers to tell you, not mine. But the point is this—" Ingrid pressed both hands flat on the charter, over the signatures, over Tomas Eriksen's confident nineteenth-century loop. "Every flood since was policy. Every 'unseasonal' rise. Every village that lost a harvest, every cottage that went damp and was condemned, every family quietly bought out and moved uphill. The Trust controls the springs, so the Trust controls the water, so the Trust controls who stays dry and who doesn't. And everyone downstream believes they live under a moody sky. They believe in weather. There is no weather. There is only policy wearing weather's clothes."

The pumps breathed below them. The lamp flame leaned and righted.

"That's why the source is forbidden," Ada said slowly. "Not because it's dangerous."

"Because it's evidence." Ingrid nodded. "The original spring system is still down there, Ada. Under the reservoir works, behind the gates, in the dark. The old channels weren't destroyed—they were *capped*. Sealed, gated, managed. Which means the crime scene was never cleaned. It was curated. Vane keeps it the way you'd keep a body in a freezer. Proof of nothing, as long as no one ever sees it. Proof of everything, the moment someone does."

* * *

After Vane had shown her the charter, that first dawn, he had made his offer, and it had been so reasonable she'd almost admired it.

"You can walk out of here," he'd said, pouring the tea she hadn't asked for. "I'll have you driven to Gallowford myself. You'll publish nothing, because you'll have nothing publishable—you'll have seen a room full of old paper, and my solicitors will explain the rest. Or you can stay. Work with me. You are, frankly, the finest field archaeologist this valley has produced in fifty years, and the archive deserves a professional hand. Catalogue it. Conserve it. Give these drowned things the dignity of good scholarship." He'd smiled then, and it had been, horribly, a kind smile. "Your family helped build this, Dr. Eriksen. There's a symmetry in an Eriksen tending it."

"And if I refuse to do either?"

"Then you stay anyway, for a while, until I'm satisfied you're not going to become a problem. I'm told the guest rooms are comfortable." He'd set down the teapot. "I want to be clear that none of this is cruelty, Dr. Eriksen. I have read every page in this archive. I know exactly what was done and by whom and why. I simply believe that order is worth its price, and that the price was paid by people who are conveniently dead, and that the alternative—letting the valley return to the mercy of seven unruly springs—would drown more people in ten years than the founders drowned in one. Someone must own the water. The only question history ever asks is *who*, and we answered it. Badly, perhaps. But answered."

She had stayed. Not for his reasons. For hers: because the archive was here, and the charter was here, and proof that could end the Trust was sitting in a basement that only she, of all the people alive, had both the skills to find and the name to make the world listen.

And because some stubborn, shameful part of her had wanted to see her great-grandfather's signature with her own eyes before she burned it all down.

* * *

Now Ada sat across the marble table with the atlas open, the charter open, the ledger open, three documents fanned out like a hand of cards, and Ingrid watched her child assemble the argument the way she herself had assembled it, alone, over six weeks of lamplight.

"The flood that killed the shellfish beds at Rivergate proper," Ada said. "Two years ago. That was a gate decision."

"Almost certainly. There was a dispute that season over oyster leases. I've found correspondence."

"And the fen rising last week—the dredges—"

"Policy. They were flushing travelers out of the fen route ahead of you. I heard Vane arrange it." Ingrid held her daughter's—her child's—gaze. "I'm sorry. I heard it and couldn't stop it and didn't know yet whether you'd taken that crossing."

Ada absorbed that. Ingrid could see the anger arrive and be set aside, deliberately, like a tool laid on a bench for later use. It frightened her a little, how like her that was.

"So the plan," Ada said. "Whatever the plan is. It can't just be publishing this. Vane owns the newspapers' owners. It can't be taking it to London. By the time anything moved, we'd be gone and the archive would be ash."

"No," Ingrid agreed. "It can't be paper. Paper is his medium. He's had ninety years of practice winning with paper." She looked toward the corridor, toward the deep galleries, toward the sound of the pumps keeping the river in its cage. "The source isn't forbidden, Ada. That's the whole revelation. Everyone downstream thinks the headwaters are dangerous, sacred, cursed, closed—they think the Trust is protecting them from something. But the source is just *evidence*. Seven springs, capped and gated, still beating under this house like a heart someone decided to own. And evidence wants one thing."

Ada followed her look. In the silence, beneath the pump-hum, Ingrid could almost hear it—she'd learned to, down here, in the small hours: water, patient behind iron, remembering what it was before 1893.

"It wants to be understood," Ada said. "Marta's words."

"Marta's words." Ingrid allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "The bell-keepers sang the sluice orders for a hundred years. Did you know that? The old alarm patterns, the maintenance songs—half of them are instructions. The Trust took the land and the records and the names off the maps, but the songs went upriver and downriver and into children's heads, and songs can't be filed." She closed the charter gently, reverently, the way you close a casket. "We're going to need her, Ada. Whatever we decide. We're going to need the tune."

Ada was quiet a long moment. Then: "There's something else in the annex. Page four. I read it twice." They turned the engineers' pages back, and their finger stopped on a paragraph near the bottom, in ink darker than the rest, added later—added, Ingrid realized with a cold slide of recognition, in a hand she knew better than any signature in the building.

Her own mother's hand. Ada's grandmother's.

"What is this?" Ada whispered.

Ingrid leaned across the table, and read, and felt the floor tilt beneath ninety-one years of weight.

Because it was a warning. Dated 1961. Written by a woman who had gotten this far, into this room, sixty years before either of them—and who had written, in the margin of the founders' own annex, in pencil, in a hurry:

*They know I've seen it. If anything happens to me, the second gate is not what they claim. Check the second gate.*

Above them, faint through floors of stone and iron, a door closed that should not have been open.

Footsteps on the gallery stairs. Leather soles. Unhurried.

"He's early," Ingrid said, and reached for the lamp.
