## 22. Why She Left

The lamp between them threw two shadows on the archive wall, and the shadows did not look like mother and child. They looked like two strangers who had wandered into the same cave from opposite directions and were only now noticing each other's lanterns.

Ingrid Eriksen sat with her back against a shelf of sealed drawers, her coat still damp from the culvert crawl, her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a verdict. She had not touched Ada since the gallery. She had said *you shouldn't have come*, and then she had said nothing at all for a long time, and the silence had been worse than any argument they'd ever had across a kitchen table eleven years ago.

Ada's fury had been building since the photograph in the midden. Three weeks old. Her face turned toward the camera, alive, while Ada had spent those same three weeks eating cold beans in a boathouse and telling themselves their mother was dead, because dead was easier than gone. Dead didn't choose. Dead didn't pack a bag and leave a note that said *fieldwork* and mean *away from you*.

"Say it," Ingrid said quietly. "Whatever you've been carrying. Say it here, where no one else can hear it."

"You don't get to invite my anger like it's an appointment." Ada's voice came out low and rough. "You don't get to schedule it between the charter and the escape route."

"No." Ingrid didn't flinch. "I don't. But you're going to say it anyway, because if you don't, you'll carry it up every stair of this house and out the other side, and it will be heavier than anything the Trust ever built."

The machinery hummed behind the walls. Somewhere above them, wardens' boots crossed a floor in slow, unhurried rhythms, the sound of people who believed the building was theirs. Ada looked at their mother — the gray in her hair she hadn't known about, the new lines around her mouth, the way her left hand trembled slightly until she pressed it flat against her knee — and the words came up like water through cracked ground.

"I dug middens for three years." The first sentence was almost calm. The second was not. "I dug other people's garbage for cash money because I couldn't stand to dig in the town where everyone asked about you. Do you know what they said? That Dr. Eriksen finally found something worth more than her family. That's the joke, Mum. They were wrong about the treasure but right about the choice."

"I know."

"You don't *know*. You weren't there. You were never—" Ada's breath caught, and they hated it, hated the crack in their own voice more than they had ever hated their mother. "Eleven years. Birthdays. The flood that took the boathouse in '19 — I nearly drowned, and when they pulled me out I called your name before I called anyone else's, and you didn't come, because you were in some library in another country reading dead people's letters."

Ingrid's eyes were wet but steady. "The letters were yours."

"What?"

"The letters I was reading. The ones that started all of this." She reached into the inner pocket of her coat, slowly, the way you move around a frightened animal, and drew out a folded page gone soft as cloth with handling. "Your great-great-grandmother's. Hester Eriksen. I found her correspondence in a Norwegian archive six years ago. She wrote to her sister about the spring. About what her husband did to it."

Ada took the page because their hands moved before their mind agreed. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. The handwriting slanted hard, urgent, a woman writing fast by candlelight.

*They have blown the springhead. Aldous's grandfather and his friends, with powder and permission bought from men who never saw this valley. The water has nowhere to go now but over the low ground. Marta's grandmother says the bells of Merrow rang by themselves the night the valley filled. He tells me it is progress. He tells me our name is on the charter and that makes it right. I have hidden the true papers where the water cannot reach them, and I have taught the tune to the children so that someone, someday—*

Ada stopped reading. The room seemed to tilt, gently, like a boat taking on water.

"Our name," they said. "Eriksen. On the charter."

"Signatory number nine." Ingrid's voice was flat, stripped of everything, a field after harvest. "Aldous Vane's great-grandfather drew it up. Your great-great-grandfather signed it. Nine families drowned five villages and called it improvement, and one of them was ours."

* * *

The hum of the machinery filled the pause. Ada became aware, distantly, of details with terrible clarity: a drip of condensation falling from a pipe in perfect rhythm; the smell of cold stone and old paper and, beneath it, the green mineral breath of the river itself, moving somewhere below them in the dark, patient as it had been for a century.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Ada heard themselves ask. It wasn't the question that mattered. It was the doorway question, the one all the others stood behind.

Ingrid laughed — a small, broken sound with no humor in it. "Tell you what? That the name I gave you was bought with drowned villages? That the comfortable life we lived, the university fees, the house with the good roof — that it was interest paid on a crime?" She unfolded her hands and looked at them as if they belonged to someone else. "I found Hester's letters in the autumn. By winter I had traced the charter's existence to this collection. And I sat across the breakfast table from you, Ada, twelve years old, eating porridge, and I thought: *every morning this child says my name, and my name is a lie.*"

"So you left instead of lying. That was the alternative? Just — go?"

"Just go." She nodded. "It sounds insane when you say it back. It felt like the only honest thing I had left. I couldn't stay and pretend. I couldn't stay and tell you, either, because—" Her composure slipped, just once, a hairline fracture across the whole edifice. "Because you would have carried it. Don't you see? You were the one person in the world I could still keep clean. Everyone else I touched, I contaminated with what I knew. My colleagues, my funders — half of them took Trust money without knowing what fed it. But you. You were digging shell middens and naming gulls and you had no idea, and every day you didn't know was a day you were still innocent, and I couldn't bear to be the one who ended it."

"That wasn't your decision to make."

"No." Quietly. "It wasn't. It was the worst kind of arrogance — the protective kind. I decided your ignorance was a gift I could give you, when really it was a wound I couldn't watch myself make." She looked up, and her eyes were the same gray-green as the river in November, and Ada realized with a jolt that they had inherited them, had been looking at this color in mirrors their whole life without knowing where it came from. "So I went underground. Literally, eventually. I spent four years getting close enough to this collection to be useful to them. Vane thinks he recruited me. A translator for the founding documents — the originals are in Old Valley dialect, mixed Danish, and there are perhaps five people alive who can read it fluently. I made sure I was one of the five."

"You traded yourself."

"I traded my cooperation for access. One drawer at a time. I told him I wanted academic credit for authenticating the charter. He wanted legitimacy — a famous archaeologist's imprimatur on the Trust's founding myth. We used each other, and I told myself that made it clean." Her mouth twisted. "It doesn't. Nothing about this is clean. I have been living in this house for three weeks, translating the crime of my own blood, eating their food, walking their corridors, and telling myself every night: tomorrow I'll have enough. Tomorrow I'll have the whole charter photographed, the sluice records, the payment ledgers, the maps of what they flooded. Tomorrow."

"And then I showed up."

"And then you showed up." Something moved through her face — terror and love in the same expression, indistinguishable, like lightning inside a cloud. "Do you know what I felt when Bryn's message reached me? Not joy. Fear. Pure fear. Because I knew what they do to loose ends, and you were a loose end with my face."

Ada stood very still. The fury was still there — it hadn't gone anywhere, it was a live coal in their chest — but around it, unbelievably, other things were arranging themselves. The shape of those eleven years looked different from inside this room. Still unforgivable. But no longer senseless. Their mother had not abandoned them for ambition or another person or the simple cold arithmetic of preferring work to family. She had done it out of a love so warped by shame that it had become indistinguishable from desertion.

It didn't excuse it. Ada held onto that. Understanding was not absolution, and they would not let this room trick them into confusing the two.

"You should have let me carry it," they said at last. "That's what you never understood. I'm not — I was never porcelain, Mum. I grew up on the Rivergate. I know what water takes. What I couldn't survive wasn't the truth. It was the version of you that the truth would have replaced." They swallowed. "I spent eleven years grieving a woman who chose her work over me. That woman never existed. The real one chose her guilt over me, which is somehow worse and somehow — I don't know. More human. More *ours*."

Ingrid made a sound that might have been a sob strangled at birth. She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth.

"I'm not done being angry," Ada said. "I want that on the record. Years, probably. Maybe forever. But anger is a thing you can work alongside. I've worked alongside worse."

"What are you saying?"

Ada crouched down so they were level, eye to eye, the way you'd meet a spooked animal or a child or — they realized — exactly the way their mother used to kneel to meet them, a lifetime ago, before the leaving. "I'm saying we finish this together. Not because I forgive you. Because you can't read Old Valley dialect and I can't either, and neither of us can crank that gate alone, and because the Eriksen name is on that charter, which means the un-signing of it is ours to do." They held out their hand — not to shake, but palm up, open, the gesture of someone offering to help another person up off the ground. "Partners. Terms explicit. No more protecting me. No more deciding alone what I can bear."

Ingrid stared at the hand. Then at their face. Then, slowly, she reached up and took it, and her grip was stronger than Ada expected, callused from three weeks of forced labor with pen and crank and crowbar, and she pulled herself to her feet using their arm, and for a moment they simply stood there, holding on, two people in a drowning archive, forehead almost touching forehead.

"I watched you read Hester's letter," Ingrid whispered. "You read it the way I did. Looking for the hiding place."

"Old habit."

"It's a good habit." And there — briefly, crookedly, astonishingly — she smiled. "She hid the true papers where the water couldn't reach them. I've spent three weeks working out where that is. It's not in this building, Ada. It's under it. The original chamber — the springhead itself, before the founders dynamited it. There's a gate down there, a hand-cranked sluice from before their time, older than the Trust. Hester's papers are behind it. So is the proof of everything — and so, I think, is the mechanism that could end it."

"The source."

"The source." Ingrid squeezed their hand once and let go, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — the guilt burned off it, leaving something harder and brighter underneath, the voice of a field director with a site to open. "Vane knows I've taken everything I can carry. He's been watching me loosen for days; he's not a fool, whatever else he is. When he decides I'm finished being useful, he'll seal this level and we'll both become part of the collection." She glanced toward the corridor, where the boot-steps had stopped. "Which means we have until he stops hesitating. And Ada — he is not a man who hesitates long."

Above them, faint through stone and steel, a sound began. Distant, rhythmic, iron on iron.

A bell.

Not the drowned bells of Old Merrow. Not memory, not fog-work. A living bell, rung hard and fast, in a pattern that made Ingrid's head snap upward and the color drain from her face.

"That's the alarm pattern," she breathed. "The old one. The villages' signal. Nobody's rung that in a hundred years — who's ringing it?"

Ada already knew. Downriver, in Gallowford, in a church tower, someone was keeping an old promise with a rope and both hands.

"Bryn," they said. "Bryn's ringing the bell."

And beneath their feet, deep in the dark where the river waited behind a century-old gate, the water seemed — Ada would swear it, always — to lean forward and listen.
