## 23. Vane Closes the Trap

The sound came first—a deep, mineral groan that traveled up through the floor of the archive like the house clearing its throat.

Ada had been standing in the doorway of the room where Ingrid worked, watching her mother's hands move over a document older than any nation either of them could name, when the noise began. It was not mechanical, exactly. It was geological. The sound of stone settling against stone with intent.

Ingrid's pen stopped mid-word.

"That's the culvert," she said quietly. She did not look up. Her voice had the flat calm Ada had learned, over three weeks of following her trail, to recognize as fear wearing competence as a coat. "He's sealed it."

"You're sure it's him?"

"Who else?" Ingrid set down the pen with exaggerated care, the way she used to set down trowels at sites when something had gone wrong and she wanted no one to see her hands shake. "There are only two people in this valley who know that culvert exists. One of them is me."

*And one of them taught me,* Ada thought but didn't say. The map fragment in the wardrobe of room four—pencil marks, precise as everything Ingrid Eriksen had ever done—had been left for whoever was clever enough or stubborn enough to find it. Ada had followed it down through the house's underbelly, through humming machinery and cold corridors lined with sealed archive doors, and now the way back was closing behind them like a mouth.

They stood together in the gallery and listened to the house breathe. Somewhere below, water moved where water should not have been moving. The Rivergate—or Emrys, though the name still felt foreign on Ada's tongue, a word from someone else's childhood—ran beneath Headwaters House the way a spine runs beneath skin, and the Trust had spent a century learning every vertebra.

"He'll come now," Ingrid said. "Not the wardens. Not the polite machinery. Him."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been translating his founding documents for six weeks, Ada." She finally turned, and in the lamplight her face looked older than it had in any photograph, carved rather than aged. "I've read his great-grandfather's letters. They all end the same way. *The valley must be governed by those who understand it.* When Aldous believes he's losing control, he doesn't send soldiers. He comes to negotiate. He thinks negotiation is what strong men do."

"And if we refuse to negotiate?"

Ingrid's mouth did something that was almost a smile and almost grief. "Then you'll find out why five villages are underwater."

* * *

They came for them an hour after dawn, which surprised Ada until they understood it: Vane wanted them to see his face clearly. He wanted witnesses.

Two wardens flanked the doors of the long gallery—Ada recognized neither, though both wore the grey-and-green of the Trust with the ease of men who had worn it their whole working lives—and then Aldous Vane walked in alone, hands clasped behind his back, dressed as though for a parish meeting rather than a siege. He was smaller than Ada had expected. That was the trick of him, Ada realized. Everyone expected power to be large. Vane's power lived entirely in his certainty, and certainty takes up very little space.

"Dr. Eriksen," he said warmly, inclining his head to Ingrid first, as though she were the guest of honor. "Miss Eriksen. I hope the accommodations have been adequate."

"The accommodations," Ada said, "are a prison."

"A prison with central heating and a wine cellar." He said it without malice, almost playfully, and gestured to a chair near the tall windows that overlooked the gardens—the irrigated, impossibly green gardens while downstream the fens swallowed roads. "Please sit. Both of you. What I have to say will take some time, and I'd rather you heard it comfortably."

"We've heard enough," Ingrid said. "We found the charter."

Something flickered across Vane's face—not guilt, Ada noted. Something closer to satisfaction, as though the charter were a difficult child who had finally made something of itself.

"Of course you did. You're your mother's daughter." He nodded at Ada, and the compliment landed like a hand on the back of the neck. "Yes. The 1893 charter. The spring system. The villages." He recited it the way other men recited scripture, without shame, without apology. "You've been reading my family's sins for two days now, Dr. Eriksen, and I confess I'm curious what conclusion you've reached. Because here is mine: everything in that document was necessary."

"Necessary," Ada repeated.

"Five hundred people relocated. Five villages drowned. A river rewritten from source to sea." He counted them off on his fingers like items on a shopping list. "And in exchange: no flood has killed anyone in this valley since 1892. Do you know what happened in 1892? I do. I've read the coroner's reports. Forty-one dead in Merrow alone when the old river jumped its banks in March. Forty-one, including nine children, because the water went where it pleased and no one had the authority to tell it otherwise." His voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it never rose. "We told it otherwise. We have told it otherwise for a hundred and thirty years. Every farm that hasn't washed away, every mill that turns, every town downstream that sleeps soundly—that is our work. That is the crime, if you insist on calling it one, and I accept the name. I've always accepted the name."

"You dynamited a living river," Ingrid said.

"We engineered one. There is a difference, and the difference is forty-one graves that don't exist." Vane turned to the window, and for a moment his silhouette against the bright garden looked almost weary. "Your mother knows this better than anyone, Miss Eriksen. She's spent six weeks among my archives. Ask her whether the Trust's records show carelessness. They show the opposite. Minutes, surveys, compensation ledgers—we kept everything, because we believed we would one day be judged, and we wanted to be judged fairly."

"You kept everything," Ada said slowly, "because the records are the leash. You hold every family's history hostage. You decide what the valley remembers."

Vane smiled, and it was the smile of a man hearing his own argument returned to him in a slightly worse accent. "Or we simply remember what everyone else agreed to forget. Relocation was voluntary, Miss Eriksen. People signed. People took the payments."

"People signed," Ingrid said, "after the second flood came suspiciously early. After the dredges appeared. After the price of staying became drowning." She stepped forward, and Ada saw her mother stand the way she must have stood in lecture halls, in front of committees, in front of anyone who mistook politeness for agreement. "I've translated your charter, Mr. Vane. I've also translated the correspondence around it. Your founders called it 'encouragement.' I call it what it is."

"I don't imagine we'll resolve the vocabulary this morning." Vane turned back from the window, brisk again, a man with an agenda and limited patience for preamble. "So let me make you an offer. My last offer, and I mean that plainly."

* * *

He laid it out the way he probably laid out everything: in threes.

First, legitimacy. "The Trust is aging, gentlemen are dying, and the valley is changing whether we like it or not. What we need—what I need—is continuity with a new face. An archaeologist of Dr. Eriksen's reputation, publicly affiliated with the Trust. Your endorsement that our stewardship is historically sound. Your daughter—" a nod toward Ada, courteous, appraising, "—as a trustee in training. Young blood. Local roots. The Eriksen name beside ours on the new charter."

Second, access. "Everything you've seen in these archives stays available to you. Publish what you like—within reason. The charter can be studied. History can be debated. I am not afraid of historians, Dr. Eriksen; I employ several."

Third, silence as safety. "And in return, the engineering remains ours. The gates, the sluices, the flow. No publications about the founding methods. No interviews about the spring system. And no—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—no further exploration of the source itself."

The room was quiet. Through the window, Ada could hear the sprinklers ticking over the lawns, patient as insects.

"And if we refuse?" Ada asked, though they already knew. They had known since the culvert groaned shut in the night.

Vane's expression softened into something genuinely sorrowful, and that was the worst moment yet, because Ada understood it was sincere. He was not a man who enjoyed cruelty. He was a man who had organized cruelty so thoroughly that it no longer registered as cruelty—it registered as weather.

"Then you become a problem," he said gently. "And the Trust has a century of practice with problems. Your mother would have disappeared quietly—I want you to understand that was never my preference; I argued for her retention even after she broke into the west archive. But you've involved the ferry-woman. You've involved the bell-keeper. You've been asking questions in Gallowford, and Gallowford talks. Problems that spread require solutions that spread." He spread his hands, apologetic. "People vanish in this valley all the time. The river takes them. It always has. That's rather the point of a river."

"You'd kill us," Ada said flatly, "and call it hydrology."

"I'd fail to prevent an accident," Vane corrected, "and grieve sincerely at the service." He straightened his cuffs. "I don't say this to frighten you. I say it because I respect you both, and respect means telling people the truth of their situation. You have until tomorrow evening. Consider it seriously. Consider, especially—" and here he looked directly at Ada, and his eyes were pale and clear and utterly without doubt, "—what order costs, and what chaos costs, and which bill you'd rather your neighbors pay. Your mother wants truth. Truth is beautiful. Truth also floods valleys. Someone must be blamed when the water rises, Miss Eriksen, and I promise you: if it isn't the Trust, it will be whoever unlocked the gates."

He left the way he had come, unhurried, pausing at the door to say, "Dinner is at seven. The wardens won't disturb you. Think of tonight as a holiday," and then the doors closed and the lock turned with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke.

The gallery was beautiful in the morning light—Ada hated how beautiful, how much money had gone into making a prison feel like a library, oak shelves and brass lamps and tall windows framing gardens that drank while the fen drowned. Somewhere below their feet, the machinery hummed its one note. Somewhere beyond the walls, wardens patrolled grounds designed to be seen from above, so that escape would be visible from three directions at once.

"They'll do it," Ada said finally. "The accident. If we refuse."

"Yes."

"And if we accept, we spend the rest of our lives holding the lid on a grave and calling it stewardship."

"Yes." Ingrid sat down heavily in one of the leather chairs, and for a moment she looked less like the formidable Dr. Eriksen than like a tired woman in her late fifties whose knees hurt. "Welcome to the arithmetic I've been doing since March."

Ada paced. The floorboards gave slightly under their boots, and Ada thought: *even the floors are managed.* "Bryn's outside somewhere. If she's not already—" They couldn't finish it. If the Trust knew about Bryn, and Vane clearly knew about Bryn, then the river between here and Gallowford had gotten very small and very dangerous.

"She's resourceful," Ingrid said. "Her grandmother survived Old Merrow. Resourcefulness is genetic in that family, I suspect."

"You've met her grandmother?"

"I've read her grandmother's file." Ingrid's mouth twisted. "Relocation payment, 1893, accepted under protest. The protest is recorded. One line. *Signed under threat of the water.* They filed the threat, Ada. They catalogued their own extortion and shelved it between the rainfall records and the fish counts." She laughed, short and bitter. "That's when I understood there was no negotiating with them. You cannot negotiate with an institution that has already documented its own crimes and considers the documentation a virtue."

"So what do we do?" Ada stopped pacing. Their heart was going hard, but their mind—digger's mind, midden mind, the mind that read soil the way musicians read scores—was suddenly, horribly calm. "Tomorrow evening. That's our deadline. Which means tonight is our only night."

Ingrid looked up at them, and mother and child regarded each other across twenty years of silence and three days of shared captivity, and Ada watched their mother arrive at the same thought they were having, watched it land in her face like weather.

"The reservoir gates," Ingrid said quietly.

"The reservoir gates."

"You understand what that means. Downstream. The fens. Gallowford. If we breach without controlling the release—"

"Then we're no better than 1893, just with worse intentions." Ada crouched beside the chair, close enough to see the fine lines around their mother's eyes, the ink stain on her right middle finger that hadn't changed since childhood photographs. "But Marta said the sluices open in sequence. She said there's a song. *Every drowned-village child learned it,* she said. If we can get word to her—if Bryn can get word to her—"

"If." Ingrid exhaled. "We're locked in a house surrounded by wardens, and our only allies are a ferry-woman on a hostile river and a half-blind woman eighty miles downstream."

"Then we'd better be cleverer than we've ever been." Ada stood, and something in their chest had shifted—from fear to ferocity, the same transition they'd felt the day they'd decided to follow the river instead of running from it. "Mom. The culvert's sealed. How many other ways are there out of this house that the Trust has forgotten about?"

Ingrid stared at them. Then, slowly, the almost-smile returned—the real one this time, the one that had made strangers trust her with their oldest stories.

"You've been reading my pencil marks."

"Every one."

"Then you know I don't leave maps for people I expect to give up." She rose, crossing to the tall windows, looking out over the gardens—but Ada saw that she wasn't looking at the lawns. She was looking past them, at the ridge of dark hills beyond, reading the landscape the way Ada read it, the way their whole strange family read it: as a document written by water and amended by liars.

"There's a well shaft on the north side," she said. "Eighteenth century. Sealed in 1911 according to the maintenance ledger—which means sealed badly, because 1911 was a lazy year and the foreman was Vane's great-uncle." She turned back, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and for the first time since Ada had found her in this house, Ingrid Eriksen looked not like a prisoner but like a woman planning a dig. "It drops into the old spring channel. The channel runs under the west wall, past the first reservoir gate, and out through the fen cut. It will be dark, wet, and possibly collapsed."

"Sounds like home," Ada said.

Outside, the sprinklers ticked on over the perfect lawns. Below the floor, the river waited under a century of locks and lies, patient as only water is patient, remembering—Ada was certain of it now—remembering everything.

Tomorrow evening was the deadline.

Tonight, they had a well to open.
