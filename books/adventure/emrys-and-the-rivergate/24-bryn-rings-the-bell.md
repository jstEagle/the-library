## 24. Bryn Rings the Bell

The bell tower of Gallowford had been locked for eleven years, and Bryn Halvard knew this because she was the one who had picked the lock the first time, on a dare from a boy who no longer lived in the valley, and she had never told anyone, and the lock had been changed twice since.

She stood in the shadow of the churchyard wall with her hands in her coat pockets and the cold coming up through the soles of her boots like something rising through water, and she watched the wardens' lanterns move along the millrace road toward Headwaters House, three lights in a line, steady as a procession. They were going to seal Ada in. She didn't know how she knew it—only that the river had told her, in the way rivers tell things: by what they carry. All evening the water below the weir had been running wrong, too high, too fast, carrying silt that should have settled miles downstream. The Trust's dredges were working the upper reaches again. And dredges working at night meant someone upstream wanted the fens deep and the roads drowned and the story closed before morning.

Her grandmother had taught her one thing about nights like this, though her grandmother had taught it as a lullaby, which was the only way anything could be taught in a family that had learned to keep its mouth shut for four generations.

*When the water rises, ring the old way.*

Bryn had spent most of her life being paid not to remember that song. The Trust's money came every quarter, in a plain envelope, for reporting strangers on the river, and she had taken it because fuel cost money and honesty cost more, and she had told herself the arrangement harmed no one. Then she had met a thin, stubborn digger-child of Ingrid Eriksen who looked at her ledger page like it was a wound, and Bryn had understood that the envelope was not payment. It was a leash, and every quarter she cashed it, the collar fit a little tighter.

She took her hands out of her pockets. They were shaking. Good, she thought. Let them shake. Shaking hands can still pull a rope.

The churchyard gate didn't creak; she'd oiled it years ago for reasons she'd never examined until now. The grass was wet and silvered under a moon half-smothered by cloud, and the church itself rose black against the blacker sky, its squat Norman tower squatting over the town like an old dog that everyone assumed was asleep.

The vestry door gave to the crowbar she kept under the ferry seat for freeing fouled anchors. Inside, the air smelled of dust and hymnals and decades of candle smoke, and Bryn stood a moment in the dark letting her eyes adjust, listening to the building breathe. Old buildings breathed. Her grandmother used to say the drowned ones breathed loudest, down under Old Merrow's water, where the pressure of a century pushed the air out of the walls in slow bubbles you could see rising on a still day if you knew where to look.

Bryn knew where to look. Bryn had always known where to look. That was the whole trouble with her.

The tower stairs were stone, spiral, worn into shallow bowls by the feet of ringers long dead. She climbed with the crowbar in one hand and her heart going like a drum, and at the top, behind a door with a new padlock—the second change since her boyhood dare—she stopped and pressed her forehead against the cold wood and said, out loud, to no one, to everyone:

"Grandmother. I'm going to make noise now."

Then she broke the lock.

* * *

The ringing chamber smelled of hemp and pigeon droppings and old rope. Eight bells hung above her in the dark, but only the tenor mattered—the great one, the alarm bell, the one the Trust had ordered silenced after the last flood scare in 1961, when some clerk in some office decided that a bell that could call people together was a bell that could call them to *remember*, and remembering was bad for business.

The rope hung frayed and gray, thick with dust, and when Bryn took it in both hands it felt less like rope than like a hand—a big, patient hand that had been waiting eleven years for someone to take hold of it.

She had never rung properly. Ferry-women don't ring bells; they pole boats, read water, keep their heads down. But her grandmother had made her learn the pattern anyway, standing in the kitchen with two wooden spoons and a pot lid, tapping it out while the rain came down: *three and one, three and one, then nine even strokes, then silence, then three and one again.* Not a peal. Not celebration. An alarm, older than the church, older than Gallowford, older than the Trust and all its paper. A pattern the drowned villages had used to warn each other of rising water, passed mother to daughter down the generations, smuggled out of Old Merrow in the head of a girl of nine who walked out of the flood with her shoes in her hand and never once cried where anyone could see.

"They took our land," her grandmother had said, tapping the pot lid, "but not the tune."

Bryn wrapped the rope around her wrists the way you wrap a mooring line, took the strain, and pulled.

The first stroke went through her body like a wave through a hull. The sound was enormous—not loud so much as *large*, filling the tower, the churchyard, the town, rolling out over the rooftops of Gallowford and down the valley like weather. Dust fell from the beams. Pigeons exploded from the belfry windows in a clatter of wings. Somewhere below, a dog began to bark, then another, then a dozen, the whole town's dogs answering the bell the way dogs answer thunder.

Three and one. Three and one. Nine even strokes.

Her shoulders burned. Her palms split somewhere around the fourth repetition and she felt the rope go slick and warm and did not care. This was not a thing done gently. This was a thing done *fully*, the way you pole a ferry through white water, the way you commit to a current or drown. Each stroke she put her whole weight into it, hanging off the rope, giving the bell everything her grandmother had given her, everything the drowned villages had smuggled out in a child's head, everything the Trust's money had tried to buy quiet.

And the valley answered.

It started small. A lamp flared in a window across the churchyard—an upstairs window, the kind people light when they've been woken and are frightened. Then another, further down Mill Street. Then a cluster near the bridge, three windows at once, and a door opening, yellow light spilling onto cobbles, and a figure standing in it, looking up at the tower.

Bryn rang on. Three and one. Three and one. Nine even strokes. Silence—long enough to let the fear gather—and then three and one again, and in the silence she heard it: voices. Not panic-voices. *Recognition*-voices. An old man's voice, cracked and astonished, calling out a phrase in the old river dialect that the schools hadn't taught in sixty years, and another voice answering him, and then a woman saying, clear across the night air, words that made Bryn's arms nearly fail:

"That's the Merrow pattern. That's the *flood warning*. Who's ringing the Merrow pattern?"

Nobody had rung the Merrow pattern in a hundred years. Nobody alive should know it. And yet window after window lit, door after door opened, and people came out into the streets in their nightclothes and coats thrown over, faces turned up to the tower, and Bryn understood, ringing, weeping, hauling the rope with bleeding hands, that the song had not died. It had gone underground the way water goes underground. It had been running beneath the valley all this time, in kitchens, in lullabies, in the half-remembered nonsense rhymes grandmothers sang while shelling peas, waiting.

Waiting for someone to be stupid enough, and brave enough, and angry enough, to climb a locked tower and break a lock and make noise.

* * *

They came up the tower stairs while she rested between sequences—four of them, then more, a dozen, filling the ringing chamber with breath and lamplight and the smell of woodsmoke off their clothes. The old man with the dialect voice was named Tam Osgood, and he had a warden's coat over his pajamas, which he took off as he climbed, folding it over his arm like a man setting down a weight, and Bryn would remember that gesture for the rest of her life: a keeper of the Trust laying down his uniform in a bell tower because his body remembered a song his employers had forbidden.

"My gran was born in Old Merrow," Tam said, taking the rope beside her without asking, the way boat-people do things without asking. "She used to tap it on the table. I thought it was just a game." He looked at Bryn, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It wasn't a game."

"No," Bryn said. "It was a map. It was a warning. It was—" She stopped, because her throat had closed.

"It was us," said the woman from the bridge, whose name was Sorrel Fenn and whose family, Bryn now realized, had a name that appeared on the ledger page Ada carried—Fenn, relocated 1897, compensation paid, signature illegible. "It was all of us. They didn't drown the villages. They drowned the *telling*, and kept the villages as a reservoir."

Below, the town was waking in earnest. Lights everywhere now, the whole bowl of Gallowford pricked with flame and electric glow, and people gathering in the market square, and—this was the part that made Bryn's chest ache—people *listening*. When the bell spoke, they went quiet to hear it. When it paused, they counted the strokes aloud, the old ones counting, the young ones learning, children's voices picking up the pattern like they'd always known it and only just been reminded.

Tam Osgood took the rope for the next sequence, and his strokes were clumsy but true, and then Sorrel's son, a boy of maybe fourteen, took a turn, and the bell did not care about skill. The bell cared about continuation.

Downriver, Bryn knew, other towns would be hearing it. Sound carries on water; every ferry-woman knows that. The bell's voice would travel the Rivergate valley like a hand moving along a string of beads, touching each settlement in turn—Lower Merrow, Ashcombe, the fen-edge farms—and in each place, someone would wake with an old word in their mouth. Some would light lamps. Some would stand at their doors. And some, Bryn prayed, some would do more than listen.

Because the bell was not only memory. It was also, in the oldest grammar of the valley, a *call to the water*: come, look, the river is speaking tonight. And the river was speaking. Everyone downstream could feel it—the ground-hum of the dredges, the wrongness in the current, the smell of disturbed silt. The Trust was doing something enormous and secret up at Headwaters House tonight, and secrets need darkness, and the bell was burning the darkness off the valley like fog off a morning reach.

A knock at the tower door, heavy and official. Everyone stilled. Tam went down, and there was a low exchange, and he came back up grinning like a skull.

"Warden at the door says the bell is to stop, by order of the Trust, disturbance of the peace, safety ordinance whatever-number." He spat neatly into the corner, an old habit, unrepentant. "I told him the bell is the parish's, the parish is older than the Trust, and if he wants it stopped, he's welcome to climb up here and stop it himself. There's forty of us and one of him and a very narrow stair."

The warden did not climb. Through the lancet windows Bryn watched the lanterns on the millrace road—those three steady lights, the sealing party—and saw, with a ferocious joy that frightened her a little, other lights beginning to move *toward* them. Lanterns from the fen cottages. Lanterns from the bridge. People walking upstream in the dark, following the road their grandparents had been marched down in 1893, going the other way.

"Look," she said, and the ringing chamber emptied to the windows, and they all looked, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing to say that the sight didn't say better: the valley was turning around. The current of consent, which the Trust had dammed for a century, was reversing.

Bryn took the rope again. Her hands left red prints on the hemp. Three and one. Three and one. Nine even strokes. And in the pauses now she could hear the far-off sound she had been listening for underneath everything else, the sound she'd have sworn she imagined if her grandmother's ghost weren't standing at her shoulder: a second bell, faint, miles downriver, answering. Then a third. Out of rhythm, out of practice, but unmistakable—villages that had no towers left ringing whatever they had, forge hammers on rails, pots on fences, hands on anything hollow, the old pattern stumbling down the valley like a message relayed from house to house in the dark.

"They took our land," Bryn said aloud, to the bell, to the tower, to the drowned and the living, "but not the tune."

The bell swung back at her, huge and golden and merciless, and she laughed, and it came out half sob, and she pulled again.

* * *

Near dawn, a girl came up the tower stairs soaked to the waist, mud to the knees, hair full of reeds, and Bryn knew before she reached the top that it was one of Marta's fen-children, sent running through the dark with a message, because that was how messages traveled now—by foot, by water, by the old ways, since the telegraph office in Gallowford had a Trust man behind its counter.

The girl bent double, gasping, and got it out between breaths:

"Marta says—the sluices. She's coming upriver. Says tell the bell-ringer: the sequence has to start before the Trust floods the lower galleries, and she needs a boat that can take the weir at night, and—" the girl gulped air, "—and she says, *tell Bryn Halvard her grandmother's ferry-route still holds, if she remembers it.*"

The ringing chamber went quiet except for the bell, settling, humming its last low note into the stones.

Bryn looked at her ruined hands. At the people crowded around her, strangers and neighbors, wardens without their coats, a boy with rope-burns copying hers. Downstream, the answering sounds were still going, faint and stubborn, a valley talking to itself in a language it had been forbidden to speak.

Ada was inside Headwaters House. Ingrid Eriksen was inside Headwaters House. Marta was coming with the sequence that could open the old sluices and save the lowlands from the flood that must come. And between here and there lay twelve miles of managed river, one hostile weir, and the whole descending weight of the Trust's attention.

Bryn rolled her shoulders, felt something in her back give with a crack, and grinned at the room.

"I remember the route," she said. "Somebody wake my ferry."

She was down the tower stairs and into the wet street before the bell had finished fading, and the crowd parted for her, and behind her, unbidden, Tam Osgood's boy took up the rope and rang three and one, three and one, keeping the signal alive over the town while Bryn Halvard walked down to the water to go and steal her own boat back from the men who thought they owned the river.

The sky in the east was the color of wet slate. The water was high, and rising, and running the wrong way for anyone but her.

Perfect, Bryn thought. High water was exactly what her grandmother's route was built for.
