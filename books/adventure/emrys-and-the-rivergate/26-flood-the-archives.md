## 26. Flood the Archives

The corridor warden's name was Pell. He told them that much before he walked away down the gallery with his keys still swinging from his fist, and he did not tell them anything else, and somehow that was enough.

Ada stood in the unlocked corridor for a long time after the sound of his boots faded, listening to the house breathe around them. Headwaters House breathed wrong—there was no other word for it. The ventilation hummed through ducts older than anyone living, and beneath the hum came a deeper note, a pressure in the walls like a held breath, like something vast lying on the far side of the stone and waiting to be asked a question.

*The source*, Ada thought. *It's right there. It's been right there the whole time.*

They found Ingrid at the long table in the map room, where she had been working when Pell's bell-signal reached her—a single stroke, repeated three times, the old alarm pattern filtered through iron and distance until it sounded almost gentle. She had not gone back to her translating desk. She sat with her hands flat on the table as if steadying it, or herself, and when Ada came in she looked up with an expression Ada had never seen on her mother's face: not the public brightness, not the lecture-hall authority, not even the raw guilt of the reunion two days past. Something plainer than all of those. A woman waiting to find out what her child would do next.

"Bryn's message got through," Ada said. "Marta's coming upriver with the sluice song."

"I heard." Ingrid's voice was hoarse. "You rang a bell and half this valley remembered it was ever alive. Do you understand what you've started?"

"No," Ada said honestly. "That's why I need you."

They faced each other across the map table, and between them lay the great survey of Headwaters House itself—the one Vane kept locked in this room because it was the only document in the entire archive that told the truth about the building's bones. Ada had found it an hour ago while the wardens hesitated at their posts, torn between loyalty and the sound of their own grandmothers' villages ringing in a church bell downstream. The survey showed everything: the manor's foundations straddling the spring emergence like a cork in a bottle; the reservoir gates upstream, hand-cranked, built in 1893 by men whose names were carved into the charter; and below the house, threading through bedrock like veins through a wrist, the old sluices.

Five of them. One for each drowned village.

Ada laid both hands flat on the survey now, mirroring their mother without meaning to, and said the thing they had been carrying since the moment they understood what the archive was.

"We flood it."

Ingrid didn't move. "Say that again."

"The reservoir gates. We open them, and we breach the lower culvert so there's nowhere for the water to go but through. The source takes the house back. Every ledger, every charter, every box of stolen photographs and christening gowns and family Bibles—" Ada's throat tightened on the last item but they pushed through it, "—all of it goes under. The Trust's whole machinery of memory, drowned the way it drowned everyone else. Let it be weather, Mom. Let it be weather instead of owners."

For a moment the map room was silent except for the breathing of the house, that deep pressurized note, and Ada thought their mother was going to refuse—that after everything, after the cellars and the charter and the confession in the dark, Ingrid Eriksen the archaeologist would flinch from destroying evidence, would choose preservation over justice the way institutions always did, the way the Trust itself had dressed its crime in the language of stewardship for a hundred and thirty years.

But Ingrid said, "And downstream?"

And there it was. The question Ada had known was coming, had carried up from the spring chamber like a stone in their boot, had turned over and over through the sleepless hours since Pell unlocked the door.

"And downstream," Ada agreed quietly. "That's the trade-off. That's why Marta has to get here first."

---

They worked through the afternoon, and the work felt like fieldwork, which was the strangest mercy of it. This was the thing Ada knew how to do—read a landscape, read a structure, follow the logic of water through stone—and for the first time in their life they were doing it side by side with Ingrid, their heads bent over the same survey, their pencils making marks on the same tracing paper. Their mother talked the way she must have talked to students once, quick and precise, drawing cross-sections from memory.

"Look at the gradient." Her finger traced the river's fall from the reservoir gates to the valley mouth. "Vane's engineers rebuilt everything to serve the house. The gates hold back more than the spring makes—they hold back a century of managed surplus, released on schedule to irrigate these gardens while Merrow's fields went begging. If we open the gates full, that's not a flood, that's a detonation. It'll take out the house, yes. It'll also take out Gallowford."

"So we don't open them full."

"So we don't open them *first*." Ingrid pulled the sluice diagram closer. "The old system wasn't just control—it was choreography. Five sluices, opened in sequence, each one bleeding the surge into a different channel as it comes down. My grandmother's generation sang the order into children's heads because writing it down was dangerous. Marta knows it. Without the sequence, we're not freeing the river, we're aiming it at the people who survived the Trust. With it—" she exhaled, "—with it, the water comes down hard but honest. Spread across the channels it was always meant to take. Fields will flood. Roads will go. But houses can be climbed out of, if people know to climb."

"Which is what Bryn's bell already told them."

"Which is what Bryn's bell already told them," Ingrid agreed, and for a moment they looked at each other across the survey, and something passed between them that had no words in either of their vocabularies yet—an acknowledgment that they were planning destruction together, mother and child, and that neither of them could remember anymore exactly where the plan had stopped being Ada's and started being theirs.

Ada broke the look first, because looking too long at their mother still felt like standing too close to a fire they'd spent years learning to live without.

"There's another problem," they said. "The gates are hand-cranked. Iron gearing, 1893. I saw the mechanism when we came through the spring chamber. Even unresisted, that's hours of cranking. And it won't be unresisted. Vane knows we're inside now. He sealed the culvert behind us—he's not stupid, he's just certain, and certainty moves slower than panic but it does move. By tomorrow he'll have every warden in the valley up here, and the ones who wavered tonight will have been given reasons not to."

"Then we go down tonight," Ingrid said. "Before he finishes being certain."

* * *

They went down as the light failed.

The spring chamber again—the vast vaulted room where the river was born, where two days ago Ada had put their hands on the ancient gate crank beside their mother and felt the water groan behind the iron like a sleeping animal turning over. Lamplight caught the wet stone and made it gleam like the inside of a shell. The air tasted cold and mineral, older than any archive, older than any crime.

Ada stood at the base of the gate mechanism and looked up at it, really looked, for the first time with intent. Two cranks, geared together, mounted on a windlass the size of a cart wheel. The chain ran up into darkness where the gate itself sat in its slot, and rust had welded parts of the assembly into a texture like coral.

"It'll turn," Ingrid said, reading their silence. "It turned for me when they made me demonstrate the maintenance protocol. Slowly. It's meant to be slow—it's meant to be *contemplative*. The founders wanted opening the gates to feel like a ceremony, so no one would ever do it casually."

"Good," Ada said. "Let him feel contemplated."

They tested the cranks together, one on each handle, and the mechanism resisted, then groaned, then gave a quarter-turn with a sound like a ship changing its mind. Water shifted somewhere above them in the slot—a deep, muscular slithering. Both of them stopped. Listened. The house went on humming overhead, oblivious, elegant, wrong.

"Not yet," Ingrid said, and her hand was shaking, and Ada saw it and pretended not to, because some mercies were better delivered silently. "Not until Marta's here. Not until we know the sequence. If we open this gate and the sluices stay shut, we kill the valley we're trying to give back."

Ada let go of the crank. Their palms were printed with rust and their arms trembled with the effort of a quarter-turn, and they did the arithmetic anyway, because someone had to: a full opening would be hundreds of turns. Hours. Against wardens, against Vane, against the whole descending weight of a century of managed water.

"We need Bryn," they said. "And we need the sluice stations manned—all five, simultaneously, or near enough. Marta can't sing five places at once."

"Marta doesn't have to." Ingrid was already pulling the survey toward the lamplight, her pencil moving. "The sequence isn't five separate songs. It's one song with five verses. Each sluice station has a marker stone—the founders cut them, actually, granite posts with the verse numbers chiseled on, because even they needed reminders. If we can get word downriver to Bryn, and Bryn can put people at the stones—"

"People," Ada said. "Downstream people. People who've spent a hundred years being managed by this house."

"People who just heard a bell ring their grandmother's alarm," Ingrid said. "People who lit their windows. Ada—we don't have to do this alone. That was always the difference between us and them. They never believed anyone would help for free."

Ada looked at their mother—at the lamplight moving on her face, at the pencil steady in her hand now, at the woman who had vanished for a decade rather than hand her child a poisoned inheritance and who was now, in the wet cold of the river's birthplace, handing her child something else instead: a plan with room in it for other people.

"Then we send word tonight," Ada said. "Through the fen channels, if the water's high enough. Can you write the verses down?"

"No," Ingrid said, and smiled, tired and fierce. "Nobody writes them down. That's the whole point. But I can describe the marker stones well enough that Bryn will know them, and Marta will sing when she gets here, and the song will travel the way it always traveled. Mouth to mouth. Village to village." She tapped the survey, at the five points along the falling river where granite waited under a century of silt. "They took our land," she quoted softly, "but not the tune."

* * *

They wrote the message by lamplight in the map room—no names, nothing that would damn anyone if a warden found it. Descriptions of stones. Times. A request phrased as a question, because questions could be denied. Ada folded it into oilcloth and thought about how to get it downriver, and the answer arrived before they finished asking: the culvert was sealed, but the house had windows, and the house had a roof, and somewhere below on the black water a ferry-woman was waiting with a pole and a conscience she'd argued herself into twice.

Getting to the roof meant crossing the upper gallery, and the upper gallery meant passing the archive doors, and Ada made themselves walk slowly past them—one last inventory. Sealed steel, row on row, each stenciled with a catalog number. Behind those doors: the ledger pages naming payments to families who accepted relocation. The photographs. The salvaged shoes and spoons and Bibles, everything taken from the drowned villages and filed like specimens, the whole apparatus of a crime committed against memory itself.

*I'm sorry*, Ada thought, and was surprised to find they meant it—not sorry that the archive would die, but sorry that it had ever existed to be destroyed. Sorry for the hands that had catalogued other people's griefs so neatly. Sorry for the century of order purchased with forgetting.

Behind one of those doors was the Eriksen family Bible. Their name in it. Their mother's handwriting in the margins, maybe, young Ingrid's careful script beside births and deaths. Ada had seen it two days ago and had not been able to touch it, and now they stood outside the door that held it and understood, finally, what they were choosing.

Some inheritances you refuse. Some you drown. And some—the tune, the names, the story—you carry out through the water yourself, singing.

The roof was cold and enormous under the early stars. Ada found the north parapet above the boathouse landing, where the drop to the black river was shortest, and weighed the oilcloth packet in their hand. Below, faint as breath, a lantern swung at the end of a moored boat. Bryn. Keeping watch the way she kept everything—without promises, without clean hands, without leaving.

Ada struck a match and held it low, twice, the signal they'd arranged a lifetime ago in a hollow tree, and watched the lantern below swing once in answer.

Then they dropped the packet into the dark, and listened to the river take it, and turned back toward the stairs and the crank and the long night of arithmetic ahead.

Behind them, through the soles of their boots, they felt it begin: a change in the house's held breath, a deepening of the pressure in the stone, as if the source itself had heard the plan and was leaning in to listen for the song.

By dawn, Marta would be on the water. By dusk tomorrow, the first verse would be sung.

Ada put their shoulder to the future and started down the stairs.
