## 27. Marta's Sequence

Marta had not been on the river in sixty years, and the river knew it.

She felt it in the way the water carried Bryn's ferry—eager, chattering against the hull, the way a dog circles an old friend come home after too long. She sat in the bow with her hands folded over her stick and let the sound of it wash through her. Not the sight. Her eyes had given up on the world years ago, trading it for something truer: the texture of air, the temperature of stone, the particular music water makes when it is being held back by something that would rather be running.

That music was everywhere now. It came off Headwaters House in waves, a groaning beneath the ground like a great animal turning in its sleep.

"You feel that?" she said to no one in particular. To the boat. To her grandmother, dead seventy years, who was riding along whether anyone invited her or not.

"Feel what?" Bryn asked from the stern. The girl—no, woman; Marta corrected herself; everyone under fifty was a girl to her now—poled with the easy rhythm of someone born standing in a boat.

"The house is holding its breath." Marta turned her face toward the shore, where the fog hung thick as wool. "It knows why we've come."

* * *

They had come for her at Gallowford in the grey hour before dawn, and Marta had been ready, because she had been ready for forty years. That was the thing nobody understood about waiting: it was not passive. Waiting was work. Every morning since the Trust men had come to her father's door with their ledger and their polite voices, every morning since the water rose over the churchyard wall at Old Merrow and took the graves one by one until even the names were drowned, she had kept something ready. A song. A sequence. A key made of nothing but notes and numbers, carried in the only vault the Trust could never raid—the head of a half-blind old woman they'd long ago stopped considering worth watching.

Bryn's message had reached her through three hands, the way messages did in the valley, passed mouth to ear like contraband, which was exactly what it was. *The bell-keeper is needed upriver. Bring the old knowledge.* No signature. No explanation. But there was only one kind of trouble that needed the old knowledge, and Marta had spent her whole life hoping it would come and ashamed of hoping it.

"I brought my boots," she told Bryn as the ferry slid past the drowned steeple of Old Merrow. She heard it pass—felt the current lean, the way it always did there, where the village streets still ran beneath the water and pulled at anything floating overhead like hands tugging a sleeve. "The ones I was buried in, near enough. I told your grandmother once I'd walk into that village again if it killed me."

"She'd have liked that," Bryn said quietly.

"No," Marta said. "She'd have laughed at me and told me to wear the good ones." And then, because grief was a country she knew every road of: "Sing me the marker, child. Tell me where we are."

Bryn sang it out—*steeple port, fog bank starboard, weir two miles*—the ferry-woman's call, but underneath it Marta laid the older song, the one with the same bones. That was the secret of this valley, the thing the Trust had never grasped though they'd owned its water for a hundred years: everything they built, they built on top of something older, and the older thing kept singing through the cracks. Their channel markers sat on drowned fence posts. Their flood schedules followed the rhythm of a planting song. You couldn't drown a people's memory if you didn't know where they kept it, and they had never thought to look inside a tune.

* * *

Headwaters House announced itself before they saw it, the way all large cruelties do. First the smell—cut grass, coal smoke, money. Then the sound, that deep hydraulic groan Marta had been hearing since the fen, growing teeth. Then the light: windows glowing yellow through the fog, orderly and warm, a house pretending to be a home.

They put ashore below the ridge, in the shadow of a boathouse that had been built, Marta noted with grim satisfaction, directly over the foundations of a mill. She could feel the millstones under her boots, round ghosts in the mud. Her grandmother had ground barley here, or her grandmother's cousin had—one of them, one of the women whose name was in no ledger because the ledgers only recorded what was taken, never who it was taken from.

Ada Eriksen came down the path to meet them, moving fast, a lantern swinging wild shadows. Marta heard the footsteps before the voice—young, uneven, walking like someone carrying more weight than their body accounted for.

"Marta? I'm Ada. Ada Eriksen. Ingrid's—"

"I know whose you are." She reached out and found a thin shoulder, sharp through a wet coat. She held it a moment. "You have her jaw. The whole town used to say Ingrid Eriksen got her stubbornness from the river, but it wasn't the river. It was further upstream than that."

"We need the sluice order. Tonight. The gates—" Ada's breath was ragged. "There are seven sluices between the source and the lower valley. If we breach the reservoir without opening them in sequence, the surge takes Gallowford. Takes the low fields, the ferry landings, everything below the fen. But if they're opened right—" A pause. Marta heard the young person swallowing fear. "Marta, I don't know how to do this. Nobody alive knows how to do this."

Marta smiled. It was an old smile, worn smooth as a river stone.

"Nobody *alive*," she agreed. "But I'm not entirely alive, dear. Haven't been since 1931. I'm mostly memory, and memory is exactly what you need."

* * *

They gathered in the ruined mill—Ada, Bryn, and Marta herself seated on a fallen beam with the fog pressing at the broken windows and the house's machinery humming above them like a hive of brass bees. Someone had spread a map on the stones. Marta heard them smoothing it, heard Ada's finger tracing lines she could not see.

"Here's the reservoir gate," Ada said. "We open it first—that part's certain, Ingrid confirmed it from the charter documents. Then the water comes down the old channel. These six structures—" a finger tapping stone, tap, tap, tap, "—are marked on the original survey as *relief sluices*. Hand-cranked. They're supposed to bleed the surge into the side channels in order, so it arrives downstream as a rise, not a wall. But the order matters. Get it wrong and two channels meet mid-valley and we make our own flood, worse than the Trust ever managed."

"And you want the order from me."

"Bryn says the sluice sequence is in a song. A children's song."

"It is." Marta folded her hands. Let the silence stretch. Silence was a tool too; her father had taught her that, standing in the pulpit of the Old Merrow chapel before the water came. *Never give a hard truth quickly,* he said. *Let it arrive the way weather arrives, so folk can prepare.*

Bryn shifted. Ada waited—good, the girl could wait—and finally Marta spoke.

"Every child in the five villages learned it before they learned their letters. We sang it skipping rope. We sang it washing at the bank. We sang it at funerals, slow, and at weddings, fast, and nobody thought anything of it because that's what songs are for—they carry what can't be written down. The Trust burned the survey maps of the sluices in 1904. Did you know that? There's a minute of it in their own records, I'm told. *Redundant infrastructure documentation, destroyed for administrative tidiness.*" Her voice went dry as August grass. "Tidy. They drowned five villages and called the paperwork tidy."

"But the song survived," Ada said softly.

"The song survived because it was worthless to them. Because it sounded like nonsense." Marta began, then, very quietly, to sing.

*One for the spring where the cold water wakes,*
*two for the mill where the old wheel breaks,*
*three for the alder, four for the ford,*
*five for the churchyard, open the door—*

"That's it?" Bryn said. "That's just a counting rhyme. My gran sang that. I sing it poling the ferry sometimes. It doesn't mean anything."

"It means everything," Marta said. "That's the beauty of it. Listen again." She raised one hand, and the fog seemed to lean in. "*One for the spring where the cold water wakes.* The reservoir gate sits at the springhead—first, always first, the water must be woken before it can be guided. *Two for the mill where the old wheel breaks.* The second sluice is at the mill race—this mill, child, the one you're sitting in the bones of. Its wheel was removed in '98 and the sluice behind it sealed. *Three for the alder.* The third stands in the alder carr below the ridge; there's a tree older than the Trust growing straight out of its housing, and you'll have to cut it or climb it, and I'd climb it, because that alder has earned its keep. *Four for the ford.* Fourth at the crossing-stones. *Five for the churchyard, open the door.*"

Her voice caught on the last line. It always caught. Sixty years, and it always caught.

"The fifth sluice is in the Old Merrow churchyard. Under the yew. The Trust built their relief channel through consecrated ground and then wondered why the valley never forgave them." She drew a breath. "And the last two verses—the sixth and seventh—are the ones nobody sings anymore, because the places they name went under deepest. *Six for the ferry, seven for the shore.* The sixth is at the old ferry ruin where you two found each other—I know about that, don't look surprised, the fog carries gossip better than any telephone. And the seventh..." She turned her blind face toward the direction of the great house, toward the groan of held water. "The seventh is in the house itself. In the cellar. Under everything they built. They built their empire on top of the last key to undoing it, and they never knew, because the key was a nursery rhyme and they didn't listen to children."

Silence in the mill. The fog dripped from the broken rafters. Somewhere above, the machinery hummed its patient, terrible patience.

"They took our land," Marta said. "They took our houses, our graves, our names off the maps. They took the church bells and melted two of them down for munitions in the war we didn't start. They took my father's congregation and scattered it across three counties like seed. But they did not take the tune." She tapped her own temple, twice, sharp. "You cannot confiscate what lives in a song. Believe me. They tried everything else."

* * *

Afterward, while Ada and Bryn pored over the map, marking each sluice against the verses, Marta asked to be walked to the water's edge. Bryn took her arm—good girl, strong arm, smelled of rope and river mud—and stood beside her without speaking, which was the finest courtesy anyone had paid Marta in years.

"Do you understand what you're asking us to help do?" Bryn said at last. Low. "If this goes wrong—"

"If it goes wrong, the valley floods and we die blamed for it. If it goes right, the valley floods a little and we live blamed for it anyway." Marta shrugged. "I'm eighty-one. Blame weighs less than it used to."

"That's not funny."

"No. It's arithmetic." She listened to the river below the bank, running dark and quick, and for a moment she was eight years old again, barefoot on the shallows at Old Merrow, her grandmother's voice rising over the washing stones: *sing it while you work, Marta-girl, the work goes easier sung.* "Your grandmother sang that rhyme to you? Truly?"

"Skipping rope. On the Gallowford green." A pause. "I never knew what it was for."

"Neither did she, likely. Or maybe she did, and taught it to you anyway, the way you hand someone a lamp before they know they'll be walking in the dark." Marta squeezed the strong arm under her hand. "That's what inheritance is, child. Not the deed and the ledger. The lamp. Somebody hands it to you and trusts you'll find the door it's for."

From up the slope came Ada's voice, calling them back—there was movement at the house, lights in the lower gallery, and no time left to be tender. Marta let Bryn turn her by the shoulders, and she lifted her face to the great dark shape of Headwaters House, feeling its hum in her back teeth, in the old bones of her, in the graves of everyone she had ever loved.

"Well," she said to the house, conversationally, the way her father addressed difficult parishioners. "You should have learned the songs."

Then she took Bryn's arm and climbed toward the light, already singing under her breath—*one for the spring where the cold water wakes*—teaching it again, note by note, to whoever would carry it next.
