## 28. Opening the First Gate

The sluice shaft was behind the third archive door, the one with the brass plate worn nearly blank—only the ghost of a word remained, *Keeper's*, and beneath it a keyhole shaped like nothing Ada had ever seen. Ingrid had the key. Of course she had the key. She had spent eleven weeks in this house learning its grammar, and she drew the key from her coat pocket the way a person draws out a fact they've been carrying too long, carefully, with both hands.

"Are you ready?" Ingrid asked.

Ada almost laughed. "No."

"Good." Ingrid turned the key. "Neither am I. It's better that way. Ready people rush."

The door swung inward on a breath of cold air that smelled of stone and iron and something older—mineral, green, the smell of water that has never seen the sun. Beyond it a stair descended into the dark, cut not by the Trust but by hands two centuries gone, each step dished in the middle by feet that had worn them smooth. A rope handrail ran down the wall, its fibers stiff with age but sound. Ada touched it and felt the grooves of countless grips before theirs.

"Field notes," Ingrid said quietly, and Ada realized their mother was speaking to them—not at them, not over them, but to them, the way she'd once spoken to colleagues around a dig table in another life. "The stairs predate the house. The Trust didn't build this. They found it."

"They found everything," Ada said. "That was the crime. Not that they took the water. That they took it and pretended it had always been theirs."

Ingrid looked at them then, and in the lamplight her face was older than the last photograph, older than the news clippings, older than Ada's anger. "Yes," she said. "That's it exactly. I've been trying to say that for twenty years to people who didn't want to hear it. And my own child says it in one breath."

"Don't make it a compliment. It's an accusation."

"I know." Ingrid shouldered her pack. "Come on. We've a gate to open."

* * *

The spring chamber opened around them like the inside of a held breath.

Ada had imagined a cave—dripping stone, a pool, darkness. What they found instead was a cathedral of water. The chamber was vast, its ceiling lost in shadow above the reach of their lamps, its walls rising in courses of ancient masonry that might have been Roman, might have been older, each block fitted so precisely that two hundred years of the Trust's neglect had not loosened a single joint. And at its heart, the source.

It did not gush. It *emerged*—rising through a basin of black stone at the chamber's center, welling up silent and glassy, so clear that Ada could see the pale shapes of stones at impossible depths, and then not see them, because the water went down past the reach of light into a blue-black that was not darkness but depth. The surface did not ripple. It *breathed*. Slow swells moved across it, in and out, like the chest of something enormous sleeping.

Behind it, filling the eastern wall, stood the gate.

It was not what Ada had expected. They had imagined iron bars, a portcullis, something martial. This was a single slab of bronze-green metal, tall as a church door, set into a frame of granite, and it was *beautiful*—worked over every inch with interlocking patterns of fish and reeds and running water, the kind of ornamentation that isn't decoration but language. At its center, at the height of a standing person's chest, was the mechanism: two iron cranks, joined by a chain drive that vanished into the wall, each crank fitted with a wooden handle worn pale by generations of hands.

"Martа's song," Ada said. The words came out hushed, though there was no one to disturb but the water. "The sluice order. It was for this."

"For all of them," Ingrid said. She set her lamp on a stone ledge and its light made the bronze fish seem to swim. "There were seven gates once, on seven springs. The Trust dynamited six and built their reservoir over the wreckage. This is the only original gate left, because this is the master spring—the one that feeds all the others through channels under the valley. Open this in the right order with the downstream sluices, and the water goes where it always went. Open it wrong, or alone, and—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Ada had seen the fen drowning. They had climbed a church tower to escape water that had been raised on purpose, by men with dredges and schedules and no songs at all.

"Then we don't open it wrong," Ada said.

"No," Ingrid agreed. "We don't."

* * *

They worked the way archaeologists work, which is to say slowly, and with their hands.

First the mechanism. Ada knelt at the cranks while Ingrid held the lamp, and together they read the assembly like a text: the chain drive, the ratchet, the counterweight channel running up into the masonry, the grease ports—little brass cups, still threaded, meant to be filled with tallow or oil. Two hundred years old, and built to be maintained by ordinary people. Built to be *understood* by ordinary people. That was the thing the Trust had never forgiven the valley for: this gate assumed a world where water belonged to everyone who could work a crank.

"There's still grease in the cups," Ada said, touching one. It came away on their fingers, dark and waxy, smelling faintly of animal fat. "Someone kept this working. Recently."

"Vane's engineers," Ingrid said. "They maintain everything. They just never open anything." She crouched beside Ada, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a moment neither of them moved. "Your hands are steady," she said.

"Yours are shaking."

"I've been shaking for eleven weeks. It's a perfectly functional state." But she laughed—a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped without permission—and Ada found, to their surprise, that they were smiling. The chamber swallowed the sound and gave back only the slow breathing of the water.

They tested the cranks. Locked. A locking pin sat through the crank housing, iron, corroded at the head. Ada fetched the pry bar from their pack—the same bar they'd used to lever open shell middens on the Rivergate flats, the same bar that had pried up their first toll-marker in the cave, a lifetime and a hundred miles ago—and set its edge against the pin.

"On three," Ingrid said.

"You don't have to count. I know how to lever things."

"I know you do. I've read your site reports." Ada looked up sharply. Ingrid's face was unreadable and then, deliberately, readable: "Bryn showed me. She carried them upriver with the rest. You dig well, Ada. You always did. Even at eight, in the back garden, you'd brush the soil off a broken plate like it was a crown jewel."

"You watched."

"From the kitchen window. For years." Her voice did something complicated. "I was good at watching. It was the being *in* the room I couldn't manage."

Ada turned back to the pin. "Three," they said, and drove the bar down, and the pin shrieked out of its housing in a gout of orange rust, and the sound went up into the dark of the ceiling and came back down again, and the water in the basin shivered for the first time.

* * *

The cranks moved an inch and stopped.

Not jammed—*loaded*. Ada could feel it through the wooden handle, a deep, patient resistance, the way you feel a moored boat pulling at its line. The gate wasn't stuck. The gate was *holding back the spring*, and every inch they cranked it open would be an inch the water pushed to take back.

"Counterweight channel's clear," Ingrid reported from the wall, lamp raised. "The weights should be doing most of the work. They've either been locked off or they're not heavy enough anymore. Someone adjusted this."

"Vane."

"Vane." She said the name without heat, which was somehow worse. "He'd want the gate openable—in case the reservoir ever needed emergency discharge—but slow. Controllable. Nothing about this house was ever meant to happen quickly."

"Then we make it happen quickly."

"No." Ingrid came back and stood at the second crank, opposite Ada, and set both hands on the worn wood. "We make it happen *together*. If we rush it, the chain jumps the ratchet and we lose the whole travel, and we won't get a second attempt before the wardens come. Steady pressure. Full turns. Like raising a mooring stone." She looked at Ada across the width of the gate. "You've done this."

"Shell middens. Tide work." Ada reset their grip. "You learn to work against water. It always pushes. You don't beat it. You just refuse to let it win the inch you're working on."

Ingrid's eyes were bright in the lamplight. "That," she said, "is the whole history of this valley in one sentence."

They began to crank.

The first turn took everything they had. Ada's shoulders burned, their boots slipping on stone worn glassy by older feet, and the chain in the wall clattered link by link with a sound like a slow bell. The gate did not move. The second turn, the gate moved a finger's width, and behind it—through the bronze, through the granite, through the soles of their boots—Ada felt it: the groan. Deep, subsonic, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than heard. The water pressing. The water wanting.

"It knows," Ingrid said.

"Don't anthropomorphize the hydrology."

"I'm an archaeologist, not a hydrologist. I'm allowed." She set her weight into the third turn. "Marta told me something, when Bryn's message brought her up through the fens. She said her grandmother called this gate the *mouth*. Said the old people talked to it. Not worship—talk. Told it what they were doing and why, so it wouldn't be frightened."

"That's not how water works."

"No," Ingrid agreed. "But it's how people work. And this gate was built by people, for people, and every person who ever turned these cranks probably said something. So." She took a breath. "We're opening the mouth. We're letting the river go home. That's the whole of it."

Ada, who had spent a lifetime refusing their mother's rituals and then a month discovering they were made of them, found their own hands turning the crank in rhythm with hers, and found their own voice, low, joining the work:

"We're opening the mouth."

The fourth turn. The fifth. Sweat ran down Ada's spine; the lamplight swung with their effort and made the bronze fish leap. The groan behind the gate deepened, and now there was sound with it—a hiss, a rush, the sound of an enormous weight shifting on an enormous bearing—and the gate came up off its seat by a hand's width, and cold air breathed out of the gap beneath it, wet and ancient, smelling of the deep of the world.

"Ratchet's holding," Ada gasped. "Again. While it's moving—momentum's ours—*again*—"

They threw themselves into it, mother and child, four hands on two cranks, and the turns came faster now, the chain singing, the gate rising inch by groaning inch, and through the widening gap came the first tongue of the source—water sliding over the gate's lower edge, black and glassy, spreading across the chamber floor in a sheet that reflected their lamplight back at them like a second sky.

"Enough," Ingrid said suddenly. "Ada. *Enough.*"

Ada stopped, arms trembling, chest heaving. The gate stood open perhaps a foot. The water came through steadily, not a flood but a *current*, purposeful, filling the channel that ran from the chamber's floor toward the old sluice runs—Marta's sequence, seven gates downstream, the song every drowned child had known. The basin's breathing surface was dropping, slowly, the way a held breath releases.

"It's going," Ada said. They could hear it now, faint and far below: water finding old paths, old channels, the veins the Trust had pinched shut for a hundred and thirty years. "It's actually going."

"Yes." Ingrid sank down onto the stone beside the crank, and Ada saw that her hands, wrapped around the worn wood, were shaking again—and that she was weeping, silently, without any change in her posture, tears simply falling off her jaw onto the ancient stone. "It's going home. God help me, it's going home and I helped put it in a cage and now I've helped let it out and I don't—" Her voice failed.

Ada stood there, arms burning, water rising over their boots, cold and clean, and looked down at the woman who had left them, who had vanished, who had chosen a cage of documents over a home with a child in it, and who was now crying on the floor of the machine that had eaten her life.

They sat down beside her. The water was ankle-deep and rising, and it was the cleanest cold Ada had ever felt.

"You could have told me," Ada said. "Any year. Any one of them. You could have sent a letter that said *our family helped drown five villages and I can't carry it alone*."

"Yes."

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"Yes." Ingrid wiped her face with the back of her wrist, a child's gesture, absurd and devastating. "There's no defense, Ada. There's only the work. I thought if I translated enough of their charter, mapped enough of their crimes, I could hand you something clean instead of something rotten. It was vanity. It was cowardice wearing work clothes. And then they realized what I'd understood, and they kept me, and the longer they kept me the more it looked like choice, and—" She laughed, wet and terrible. "And then you walked into my prison through a drainage culvert with a pry bar and a stolen ledger, and you were so *angry*, and it was the best thing I'd ever seen."

"It wasn't anger. It was—"

"It was anger. It was allowed to be. It still is." She turned and looked at Ada fully, and the lamplight and the water-light mixed on her face. "But look at what you did with it. You followed the river. You read the ground. You found the gate. You didn't do it for me—you did it because it was *true*, and you are your father's child in your stubbornness and my child in everything else, and I have never in my life been so proud and so ashamed in the same breath."

The water reached their knees. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the house went on humming its machinery hum—but thinner now. Off its feed. Ada could imagine Vane hearing it change.

"We're not done," Ada said. "Six gates. Marta's sequence. And the reservoir's still full behind us—if we open downstream wrong, all of it comes down on Gallowford."

"Then we'd better be right." Ingrid stood, and held out her hand, and after a moment Ada took it, and their mother hauled them to their feet with a strength Ada had forgotten she had—the strength of a woman who had spent thirty years hauling herself out of trenches and trouble and her own silences. "Field notes," she said, and her voice had steadied into something Ada recognized from old photographs: the voice of a woman at a dig, at the edge of the important thing. "Gate one, open one foot. Flow established in the primary channel. Downstream sequence begins at first light."

"Or sooner," Ada said. "Bryn's watching the culvert. If Vane moves, she rings."

"Then we climb."

They gathered the lamps. At the foot of the stairs Ada paused, turned back, and looked once more at the gate—bronze and granite and the black water sliding through the gap beneath it, patient as truth, older than every lie told about it.

"We're opening the mouth," Ada said again, to the gate, to the water, to the child they had been in a garden brushing soil off a broken plate.

And from the deep beneath the chamber, faint and vast, something that was only pressure and physics and nothing else at all—and was, Ada decided, also a voice—answered.

Then the lamps guttered, all three at once, in a breath of cold air from the stairs above, and from somewhere overhead came a sound that stopped them both mid-step: the heavy, deliberate fall of the culvert grate, slamming shut.

Bryn's signal was a bell. This was not a bell.
