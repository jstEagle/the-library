## 29. Vane's Last Argument

The grate had fallen behind them, and the sound of it was still living in the stone—a flat iron syllable that echoed down the stairwell and died somewhere in the dark below the spring chamber. Ada stood with one hand on the crank housing, the other on Ingrid's sleeve, and listened to the water.

It had not stopped groaning. That was the thing Ada would remember afterward, in the years when the memory had softened into something almost bearable: the water had never stopped. All through what came next—the lamps, the voice, the gun, the argument that would decide the shape of a valley—it went on beneath everything, patient and immense, like a heart beating under a floor.

"Someone sealed the culvert," Ingrid said. Her voice was level, but her hand had come up over Ada's, gripping hard enough to hurt. Twenty years of fieldwork lived in that grip: *don't move, don't panic, think.*

"Bryn's outside," Ada said. "If she could—"

"If she could have stopped it, it wouldn't have closed." Ingrid turned toward the stairs, tilting her head, listening the way she listened to soil, to stratigraphy, to anything that kept its secrets in layers. "One person," she said. "Heavy tread. He wants us to hear him coming."

The lamps guttered again—not out, but low, their flames leaning toward the stairwell as though drawn by the draft of an opening door. And then the door itself: a slow swing of pale light from the gallery above, cut by the black rectangle of a descending figure.

Aldous Vane came down the stairs alone.

He had left his coat somewhere. That was the detail Ada's mind seized on first, absurdly—Vane without his coat, Vane in shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow like a man about to wash up for dinner, Vane looking for all the world like a neighbor who'd dropped by to borrow a tool. The pistol in his right hand ruined the picture only slightly, because he held it low, angled at the floor, the way a man holds a walking stick he has no real need of.

He stopped four steps from the bottom. The lamplight reached him in slices.

"You've been busy," he said.

Ada's mouth was dry. Behind them, the crank housing ticked faintly, metal cooling or metal waiting; Ada couldn't tell which. "You shut the culvert."

"I did." He said it without apology, the way he might confirm an appointment. "I also sent my men home. Did you notice? The wardens are gone. The house is empty except for the three of us—and whatever your ferry-woman thinks she's doing out there with a church bell." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "You've made quite a noise tonight, Eriksen. Downstream too, I'm told. Lights in windows. People standing in doorways."

"Good," Ada said.

"It isn't good." He said it gently, and that gentleness was worse than any shout. He descended the last steps and stood on the chamber floor, and Ada saw now how tired he looked—not defeated-tired, but the deep weariness of a man who has carried something heavy for a very long time and has finally been told he can put it down, and doesn't know who will pick it up. "It's the beginning of the worst night this valley has ever had. And I came down here alone, without witnesses, because I thought the least I owed your mother—" a small bow of the head toward Ingrid, courteous even now, even here, "—was the truth."

"We've read the charter," Ingrid said. "We know your truth."

"You know a document." Vane shook his head slowly. "Documents are what we keep so that no one ever has to remember. Let me tell you what I actually know. I was twenty-three years old when I took over the flow schedules. My predecessor walked me through these chambers—he was eighty-one, and his hands shook, and he showed me the gates and said, *'Now they're yours.'* Do you understand what that means? Every winter since, I have sat at a desk with rainfall figures and snowmelt projections and decided how much valley gets to stay dry." His voice didn't rise. It lowered, if anything, gathering weight. "In 'ninety-eight I held the gates shut through a February that should have drowned Gallowford. Fourteen hundred people slept because I did the arithmetic correctly. Who does that arithmetic when I'm gone? You?" He looked at Ada. "The bell-keeper? The woman with the boat?"

"The people," Ada said. "All of them. Together."

"Together." Vane tasted the word like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "I've watched *together*, Ms. Eriksen. Together is what happens at a public meeting before everyone goes home and lets the man with the records decide. Order isn't cruelty. Order is the only mercy that scales."

The water groaned beneath them, long and low, and in the silence after it Vane raised the pistol—not aiming it, not yet, just holding it where the lamplight could find it, a fact among facts.

"So here is my last argument," he said. "Not the offer. We're past offers. Just the argument, because you deserve to hear it from someone who believes it." He began, slowly, to walk the edge of the chamber, and his voice took on the cadence of a lecture, of a eulogy, of a man reciting the only prayer he had. "Open these gates tonight and the surge takes the house—that's fine, I've made my peace with the house. But it doesn't stop here. Unmanaged, the source runs wild, and the old sluices downstream are rusted, unmapped, forgotten—except, yes, by one blind old woman and a song. If Marta's sequence fails—if one gate sticks, if one crank seizes, and things built in 1893 are entitled to fail—the flood doesn't stop at the fen. It takes the low fields. It takes the new banks where your returned families are camping. It takes Gallowford's lower street while those lights in the windows are still burning." He stopped walking. He turned. "And when the water settles, someone will stand in the wreckage and ask *who did this*, and the answer will be a name. Yours. Your mother's. The strangers who broke a system they didn't understand, on the word of a song. The Trust drowned five villages in secret and I have hated us for it every day of my life—but we were at least *responsible* for it. We kept the ledgers. We paid the annuities. We stayed. Will you stay, Eriksen? Will you stand in the flooded street and be the answer?"

The chamber was very quiet. Even the water seemed to hold its breath.

Ada felt the argument go into them like cold river water into a boot—finding every gap, filling every hollow. Because it was true, some of it. Maybe much of it. Because Ada had spent the whole journey learning exactly how heavy water was, how little a human hand weighed against it, how easily competence failed and plans dissolved. Because *someone must be blamed* was a sentence with teeth, and Ada could feel them.

They looked at their mother.

Ingrid had not moved. She stood beside the crank housing with her hands loose at her sides, and in the low lamplight her face was unreadable in the way that rock faces are unreadable until you learn to read weathering—and Ada had spent a lifetime learning to read this particular face from a distance, from postcards, from newspaper photographs, and now, at last, from four feet away.

"Ingrid," Vane said softly. "You've seen the figures. You translated the schedules yourself. Tell your child what the numbers say."

Ingrid looked at him for a long moment. Then she spoke, and her voice was quiet and clear and carried the way her voice had carried in lecture halls Ada had only ever seen in photographs.

"Aldous. You've described ownership perfectly. You decide, you answer, you stay. You've built a whole theology out of being the one who holds the pen." She stepped forward, one step, putting herself fractionally between the gun and her child, and Ada's chest tightened at the gesture—at once infuriating and unbearable and, God help them, beloved. "But listen to what you're actually saying. For a hundred and thirty years, the floods came anyway. The dredges raised the fen water. Old Merrow's bells rang underwater. You managed the river the way a thief manages a house he's robbed—carefully, attentively, forever. And every year the ledger grew, and every family that accepted relocation accepted it from *you*, and called it weather, because you taught them to call it weather." She shook her head. "You want someone to blame. Fine. But the choice in front of us isn't between your order and chaos. It's between a crime with a committee and a risk with a community. So let it be weather, Aldous. Real weather. Everyone's together, no one's to own. Let the valley take its chances the way valleys always did before men like you decided chance was a commodity."

"And if the sluices fail?"

"Then we fail with them," Ingrid said. "Out loud. In daylight. With names that mean something because we gave them freely." She turned her head, and her eyes found Ada's, and what passed between them was not forgiveness—not yet, maybe not for years—but it was partnership, hard and clean as a handhold in wet rock. "Ada. Open the gate."

Vane's arm came up.

The pistol rose with it, steady, aimed now—not at Ingrid, Ada saw with a strange lurch, but at the crank housing, at the mechanism itself, at the iron throat of the gate. "Don't," he said, and for the first time all night his voice cracked along some old seam. "I am asking you. As a man who has done the arithmetic for thirty years and never once been thanked—I am *asking* you."

"You're pointing a gun," Ada said.

"Yes." He almost laughed. "That's rather the point of the last century, isn't it?"

And Ada understood, in one cold bright flash, that he would not shoot them. Not because he was kind—though perhaps he was, in his terrible accounting way—but because shooting the crank meant shooting the valley's future, and Vane had spent his whole life refusing to break things he couldn't repair. He was a man who kept ledgers. He wanted, more than he wanted victory, to be able to balance the book.

Ada put both hands on the crank.

It was colder than expected, and heavier—of course heavier, everything real was heavier than expected—and somewhere above them Bryn's distant bell was ringing again, or maybe it was only the water, or maybe it was both, the same sound wearing two bodies.

"Last time," Vane said. His aim hadn't wavered. His eyes had. "Ms. Eriksen. Ada. Whoever you are. Don't make me watch."

"You can look away," Ada said. "Or you can help. Those are the only two options left, and honestly—" they set their feet against the wet stone, feeling the mechanism wake under their palms, feeling the whole buried architecture of 1893 shudder toward consent, "—honestly, you've earned the second one more than anyone."

Then they threw their weight against the crank, and Ingrid's hands closed over theirs, and the great gate groaned against a century of rust and silence—

—and Vane did not fire.

He stood with the gun steady and his face white in the lamplight, watching, and his lips were moving around words that never came out, and Ada thought: *he's counting.* Counting seconds, counting cubic feet, counting the dead of five villages and the sleeping town of Gallowford and the cost of everything, the way he had counted every night of his life at that desk.

The gate gave.

Not all at once—first a seam of black water, thin as a knife, jetting sideways across the chamber floor; then a roar building inside the wall like a train entering a tunnel; then the whole iron leaf swinging inward on screaming hinges as the source came through, ancient and unimpressed, taking back in nine seconds what had been stolen over a hundred and thirty years.

The lamps went out.

In the dark, in the rising roar, Ada heard three sounds: Ingrid shouting the first number of Marta's sequence; the clatter of Vane's pistol hitting stone, abandoned or dropped, impossible to know; and beneath everything, everywhere, the voice of the water—no longer groaning now, no longer asking.

Answered.

And above them, muffled by floors of drowning archive, the bell began to ring the alarm pattern—fast, urgent, wrong somehow, ragged at the edges—as if the hand pulling the rope was running out of time.
