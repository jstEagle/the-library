## 30. The Source Unbound

The water did not rush. That was the first thing Ada understood, standing in the spring chamber with their hand still on the cranked gate's iron wheel, watching the first black seam of the source come through. It *rose*. It came up out of the floor like something patient finally allowed to stand, like a held breath released after a hundred years, and it made a sound no flood should make—a low, chordal hum, almost musical, as though the river remembered every bell that had ever rung over it and was singing them all at once.

"Move," Ingrid said, and her voice had no argument in it.

They climbed. The service stair behind the archive gallery was narrow, stone, built for wardens' boots and lantern-light, and now it was a throat with water rising through it. Ada went first because they were youngest and because their legs knew rough ground the way other people's knew stairs; Ingrid came second, moving with the quick economy of a woman who had spent three weeks learning this house from its underside; Marta came last, one hand on the wall, half-blind and utterly unlost, counting steps under her breath in a rhythm that was not counting at all but song.

*First gate opens to the salmon's leap,* she murmured. *Second to the eel's long sleep—*

"Marta." Ada paused on the landing, water already ankle-deep and cold enough to ache. "The sluices downstream. Bryn has the sequence?"

"Bryn has my voice in her ear and Gallowford's bell in her hands." Marta's clouded eyes found Ada's face with unnerving accuracy. "And the river has its own memory, child. We are only helping it remember faster."

Below them, through the grating of the landing, the first archive room was already drowning.

Ada looked down despite themselves. They could not help it. The room where they had found the Eriksen family Bible—their name in faded ink, their grandmother's grandmother's handwriting marching down the births page like small determined soldiers—was filling with dark water, shelf by shelf, box by box. Ledgers swelled and split their bindings. The 1893 charter, the founding crime itself, the paper Vane had guarded like a heart, lifted off its shelf and turned slowly in the rising water, ink blooming off it in grey clouds like smoke underwater, like the whole century of careful lies dissolving into the thing they had tried to control.

Ada expected to feel triumph. What they felt instead was grief—sudden, physical, a hand closing around the sternum—and beneath the grief, unmistakably, liberation, and the two feelings were not separate at all. They were the same feeling. They were what it meant to let something drown so that something else could live.

"You're mourning it," Ingrid said quietly. Not a question. She had stopped two steps below, water to her calves, her grey hair plastered to her forehead, and she was looking at her child with an expression Ada had spent twenty-seven years wanting and never once received: full, unguarded attention.

"I'm mourning what it took," Ada said. "Not what it kept."

Ingrid nodded slowly. "That's the difference," she said. "That's the whole difference. I spent years not understanding that."

The water reached their knees on the second flight. It carried things now—Ada could feel them bumping against their legs in the dark, soft and terrible: a photograph album, spine-up, turning like a fish; a christening gown gone grey with a century of storage; a wooden horse with one leg, bobbing. Everything the Trust had taken from the drowned villages, catalogued and sealed and owned, was being returned to the water that had taken those villages in the first place, and there was a justice in it so clean it hurt to look at directly.

"The Merrow parish register," Marta said suddenly, from below. Her voice cracked on it. "It'll be in the east gallery. Green binding. My grandmother's name is written in it—her birth, I mean, before anyone knew Old Merrow existed to be erased."

"We can't—" Ada began.

"I know we can't." Marta's hand tightened on the rail. "I'm not asking you to go back. I'm telling the water where to look. That's how it worked in the old days. You told the river your dead, and the river carried them." She lifted her face toward the sound of the flooding galleries below, and sang, thin and clear, four notes of the sluice-song, and Ada would have sworn the water changed pitch in answer.

* * *

Third floor. The water was waist-deep in the corridors now and moving faster, no longer rising but *arriving*, pushing through doorways in brown shoulders, and the house itself had begun to speak—joans and groans of timber, the tick and crack of old joinery taking strain, somewhere below a window giving way with a sound like a gasp. The machinery hummed louder. Ada could feel it through the walls, the great pumps and gates of Headwaters House fighting the source for the first time in a hundred years and losing, valves screaming, gauges somewhere bursting, the elegant wrongness of the place coming apart from its bones outward.

They passed the warden barracks. Empty—Bryn's bell had emptied it, or conscience had, or both. Boots scattered, a card game abandoned mid-hand, a mug of tea still steaming on a table that was already tilting. Ada thought of the young warden who had unlocked the corridor and walked away without a word, and hoped he was high on some hill by now, watching, with the rest of the valley, lights coming on in windows all down the Rivergate.

Ingrid caught Ada's arm at the fourth-floor landing. "Listen."

Ada listened. Under the water-noise, under the groaning house, there was another sound: bells. Faint, downstream, impossibly far and perfectly clear—the Gallowford church bell, ringing not alarm now but the old pattern, the drowned-village pattern, over and over, and threaded through it, if you knew how to hear, the answering chimes of the submerged steeple at Old Merrow, ringing without wind, ringing because the water that covered it was finally moving the way it had always wanted to move.

"They can hear us," Ada said.

"No." Ingrid pushed wet hair from her eyes. "They can hear *themselves*. There's a difference. That's what Vane never understood—that's what none of them understood. You can't own a valley by owning its records. The records were never where the memory lived." She touched the wall, the soaked wallpaper peeling in long strips like skin. "It lived here. In songs. In pencil marks in wardrobes. In grandmothers teaching children tunes while the Trust paid them to forget."

Marta, climbing steadily behind them, said nothing, but her mouth moved silently through the whole verse.

* * *

Fifth floor. Attic stairs. The water was at their thighs and cold as confession, and the house was dying around them in stages now—whole sections of flooring shifting, plaster raining down in white handfuls, and through a shattered window Ada could see the night outside: rain-washed clouds torn open, stars over the ridge, and far below, tiny and numerous, the lights of the valley all awake and burning.

Then, from below—from deep below, from the direction of the study where Vane had made his last offer with a gun in his hand—a single gunshot, flat and enormous.

Ada froze. Ingrid froze. Even the water seemed to pause.

"He wouldn't," Ada said, and heard how young their own voice sounded.

"He might," Marta said gently. "Or he might have shot the lock off a door, or shot at the water, which is what I'd wager. A man like that doesn't end himself. He ends arguments." She resumed climbing. "Grieve when you know. Climb until then."

They climbed. The attic was a low room of beams and dust and one round window, and through it the roof lay shallow and vast, slate slick with rain, ridged like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. Ada got the window open—it fought them, swollen paint, a century of shut seasons—and the night air came in tasting of river and cut grass and freedom, and they hauled Marta through, then Ingrid, then themselves, and stood up under the open sky on the roof of the house that had owned the valley's truth.

Below them, Headwaters House was drinking itself.

Water sheeted from every lower window now, silver in the starlight, pouring out of the rooms where the archives had been, carrying paper. Ada could see it—flakes and pages and whole documents riding the spillways, catching on gutters, tearing free, spiraling down into the flooded gardens where the Trust's roses drowned. A charter page slapped against the round window frame below them, clung there a moment, its ink running, and then let go and went.

"It looks like snow," Marta said softly. "Paper-snow. My grandmother said that when Old Merrow flooded, the water came up through the floors white with the church's papers. She was six. She thought it was snowing indoors." Her blind eyes reflected the stars. "I used to think she was confused. Water doesn't carry paper. But it does. It always did. They just taught us it didn't."

Ingrid sat down on the wet slate, suddenly, like a woman whose legs had finished arguing, and laughed—a short, broken, real laugh. "Twenty years," she said. "Twenty years I translated their charters, catalogued their thefts, told myself I was preserving evidence, keeping the record safe, and the whole time the answer was to give it back to the river. I was the archivist of a crime, Ada. I was the best thing that ever happened to them."

"You were the reason we knew how to break it," Ada said.

Ingrid looked up at her child, water streaming off her chin, and whatever she saw in Ada's face made her reach out and take their hand—gripping hard, an archaeologist's grip, the grip of someone who knows exactly how much force old things can bear.

The house shuddered beneath them. Somewhere below, the great central staircase—the beautiful, absurd staircase Vane had ascended with his courteous offers—collapsed into the flood with a boom that Ada felt through the slate, through their knees, through their teeth. The hum of the machinery stuttered, wound down, and died, and in the sudden quiet the only sounds left were water, and bells, and wind.

And then, out of the noise and ruin, a new sound: an engine, low and rhythmic, and a pole knocking against wood, and a voice—rough, familiar, furious with effort—calling up through the dark from the flooded forecourt:

"ADA ERIKSEN! If you're on that roof, you get down to the edge of it RIGHT NOW, because this ferry is leaving with or without you, and I have a bell-keeper's granddaughter's reputation to think about!"

Bryn Halvard's ferry slid through the drowning gardens, riding the surge, and Ada stood on the roofline of the falling house with their mother's hand in theirs and the whole freed river roaring below, and began, carefully, to climb down toward the light.
