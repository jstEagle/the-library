## 31. Roofline Rescue

Bryn Halvard had poled the Rivergate through spring melt, through ice-jam winters, through the year the weir backed up and drowned her cousin's hayfield. She had never steered through a river that was being born.

The water came down the valley like something remembering its old shape. She heard it before she saw it—a low groan rolling up from Headwaters House, deeper than any bell, the sound of a century of held-back pressure finally let go. Then the ground itself shivered under her boots on the ferry landing at Gallowford, and the river rose in a single smooth swell, not a wave but a lifting, as if the whole valley were a tray and some enormous hand had tilted it.

"Off," she said to the boy who'd been helping her load fuel drums. "Get up to the churchyard. Take the drum with you."

He went. They all went, when she used that voice. It was her grandmother's voice, and her grandmother had been born in Old Merrow, and there was something in it that people obeyed without knowing why.

Bryn looked at her ferry—*Halvard's Pride*, forty years of tarred planking and stubborn oak—and made the calculation she'd been making since dawn, since Marta's message had come downriver with the sluice-song folded inside it like a knife wrapped in cloth. The flood would take everything loose along the banks. The ferry was built to take water over her gunwales and shrug it off. And somewhere upstream, on the roofline of a drowning house, three people were standing in the dark with no way down.

Her grandmother had spent sixty years saying nothing about Old Merrow. Bryn had spent six years taking Trust money to say nothing about strangers. She was done being paid for silence.

She shoved off into the surge.

* * *

The first mile nearly killed her.

The river was no longer a river but a moving country—whole trees cartwheeling past with their roots waving like drowned men's hair, fence rails, a door still on its hinges, the Trust's orange survey buoys bobbing in clusters like startled birds. The current didn't pull so much as *insist*, gathering the boat up and carrying her sideways toward a stand of alders that would strip the hull to ribs. Bryn dropped the pole—it was useless now, touching bottom only in flashes—and hauled on the sweep oar, throwing her whole weight against the swing.

"Come on," she told the boat, the way her grandmother had talked to nets and weather and stubborn livestock. "Come *on*."

The alders came anyway. She took them glancing, a shriek of branches along the starboard rail that tore away the lantern bracket and half the coiled line, and then the current relented, spat her back toward the channel's throat, and carried her upstream against every law of rivers she knew—because this wasn't the river anymore, this was the source's water running downhill to meet the flood coming up from the breached gates, and between the two of them the valley floor had become a churning lake with opinions.

Rain began. Of course it did. The sky had been threatening all week, fat gray clouds stacked over the fens like unwashed wool, and now it came down in earnest, cold needles that turned the world to smears and shadows. Bryn tied the painter around her waist, tied her hood down, and steered by sound: the groan of the house ahead, growing; the hiss of water through drowned orchards; and underneath it all, thin and impossible, a song.

Marta's song. No—the sluice-song, the one every child of the drowned villages had once learned, the one the Trust had never managed to buy or burn because it lived in grandmothers' mouths and children's skipping games. Bryn had known it her whole life without knowing what it was for. *First the miller's daughter opens, second the weaver's gate, third where the salmon rest their shoulders—* She sang it now into the rain, ragged, half-remembered, less for navigation than for courage, and the river seemed to lean an ear.

Headwaters House appeared out of the murk like a whale surfacing.

It sat higher than she expected—the manor had been built on the rise above the emergence, arrogant even in ruin—but the lower floors were already gone, swallowed, and the water was climbing the stone with terrible patience, floor by floor, the way it had climbed the villages a hundred years ago. Lights still burned in the upper gallery, absurd and yellow. And on the roof, black against the last bruised light of the sky, three figures stood at the parapet.

Bryn brought the ferry alongside the garden wall, which was now a quay, and shouted until her throat tore.

* * *

Getting them down was worse than the crossing.

Ada came first, down the ivy-choked corner of the house where the old drainage culvert had gutted out a handhold ladder of brick, and Bryn saw immediately that the archaeologist was hurt—not badly, a gashed arm bound with someone's shirt, but shaking, the deep shake of a body that has done too much on too little. Ingrid Eriksen came after her daughter, slower, one arm locked through Ada's, and Bryn understood with a jolt that this was the famous Dr. Eriksen, the ghost whose absence had hollowed Ada out for years, and that she was real, and gray-faced, and gripping her daughter's hand like a rope over a cliff.

Marta came last. The bell-keeper was ninety if she was a day, half-blind, and had insisted on climbing down herself, refusing Ingrid's shoulder with a snap of words Bryn couldn't hear over the water. When the old woman finally dropped the last few feet into Bryn's arms, she weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks and iron will, and she patted Bryn's cheek with fingers cold as river stones.

"You rang well," Marta said. "Your grandmother would have rung louder."

"She'd have rung longer." Bryn pushed them all down onto the thwart, checked the painter at Ada's waist, and took the sweep oar. "Hold on to each other. If we ship water, you sing the third verse and pray."

"What happens in the third verse?" Ada asked.

"Nobody remembers," Bryn said, and pulled for open water.

Behind them, Headwaters House drank the flood. They could hear it—the deep interior roaring as the source reclaimed its galleries, its archive rooms, its sealed doors, and Bryn thought of all those catalogued lives, the photographs and Bibles and christening gowns shelved like specimens, going under at last into water that had no owner. She did not look long. Some things you should witness and some things you should simply let happen.

A voice stopped her. Thin, furious, unmistakably alive.

"HALVARD!"

Aldous Vane stood at the roof's western corner, soaked through, his elegant coat plastered to him, both hands gripping the parapet as the house shuddered beneath him. Even now—even waist-deep in the consequences of his own charter—he stood like a man giving directions to a coachman.

"The east stair is intact," he called down. "If you come about, I can hold the door against the water for ten minutes. Ten minutes, Halvard. There are documents in my study worth more than your boat, your family, and every village on this river combined."

For a moment, horribly, Bryn considered it. Not the documents. The man. That was the trick of Vane, she realized, the thing that had let the Trust run the valley for a hundred years—he made rescue feel like a transaction you'd already agreed to. He made you calculate.

Beside her, Ada stood up in the pitching boat, one hand on the gunwale, and looked up at the man who had hunted them the length of the river.

"No," Ada said. Not angry. Almost gentle. "You built a house on a spring, Mr. Vane. You knew what springs do."

Vane's face worked. Rain ran off his chin. For one unguarded second he looked not like a tyrant but like an heir—someone handed a kingdom of ledgers and locks by fathers who'd never explained what the locks were *for*—and Bryn felt, unwillingly, a flicker of pity.

"I kept the valley dry," he called. "Sixty years of dry fields. Who will they blame when the water comes?"

"Let it be weather," Ingrid Eriksen said quietly, "not owners."

Whether Vane heard, Bryn never learned. The house groaned, settled, and shed its western chimney in a slide of slate and smoke, and the man stepped back from the parapet—stepped *back*, deliberately, the way a captain stays with a ship—and raised one hand. Not a wave. Something more like a salute, or a surrender, or a man closing a ledger he no longer wished to read.

Then the roofline took him out of sight, and Bryn put the sweep hard over, and the flood carried them away.

* * *

They rode the water down through the drowned gardens, past the boathouses and the boundary oaks, into the wide flooded throat of the valley, and here the song became survival.

Because the flood was not chaos, not entirely. Underneath the violence ran the old channels—the river's original bones, buried under a century of Trust engineering, and the water was finding them the way blood finds its vessels. Where the song marked a millrace, the current ran smooth as glass. Where it marked a ford, the water piled and snarled. Marta, half-blind, drenched, wedged in the bow with her hands on the stem post like a figurehead, called the turns in a voice that cut through the rain like a blade through canvas.

"*Second the weaver's gate—bear left, girl, LEFT—*"

Bryn bore left. The ferry swung through a gap between two submerged barns close enough to slap the walls on either side, and then they were through, into the long straight reach above Gallowford, and the four of them sat gasping in the rain while the valley unspooled below them, transformed.

Lights were coming on.

Not Trust lights—those were dead, the power station drowned with the house—but candle and lamp and oil-lamp lights, blooming window by window down the slopes of Gallowford and the hamlets beyond, dozens of them, hundreds, people wakened by the bell and the roar, standing at their windows watching the river run free for the first time in living memory. Somewhere downstream a dog barked, and another answered, and then the night was full of small ordinary sounds that meant the valley was awake and looking.

"They're not fleeing," Ada said wonderingly. Water streamed off their hood. "They're *watching*."

"People watch births," Marta said. "And funerals. Tonight it's both."

Ingrid Eriksen sat very still, her daughter's hand still in hers, staring down at the lit windows as though memorizing them. "I spent three years translating their charter," she said softly. "Every clause, every schedule of payment. I thought I was preserving evidence." She shook her head. "I was reading a eulogy. This—" she gestured at the flood, the lights, the singing rain— "this is the part that was never written down."

"It'll be written now," Bryn said. "By whoever survives to hold a pencil."

She hadn't meant it bitterly, but she felt Ada flinch beside her, and understood: the cost was still coming. Fields silted. Roads gone. Livelihoods floated away with the fences and the fuel drums. Tomorrow there would be mud and argument and hunger, and the clean bright feeling of tonight would be paid for in installments for years. Bryn had watched the Trust promise order all her life, and order had a price too—it was just collected quietly, from other people, over generations. At least this debt would be owed openly, to faces, in daylight.

That was the whole difference, she thought. That was the entire war.

The Gallowford bridge loomed out of the rain, and Bryn threaded the gap beneath it with an inch to spare on either side, and then the town was around them, lamps swaying, people actually out on the high street now in coats thrown over nightclothes, and a cheer went up—ragged, disbelieving—as the ferry slid past with its cargo of ghosts. Someone recognized Marta and shouted the first line of the sluice-song, and the old woman lifted her chin and answered with the second, and the two voices braided together over the water like something being mended.

Bryn poled them in at the old ferry stairs, where the water stood three steps from the top and lapped gently, almost politely, as though the flood too were tired. Hands reached down to help them up—strangers' hands, neighbors' hands. Ada went first, pulling Marta after, then Ingrid, and Bryn stayed a moment alone in the boat, one boot on the stair, looking back upstream into the dark where a great house was drinking itself to death.

She thought of her grandmother, who had died with Old Merrow's name unspoken in her mouth for sixty years. She thought of the Trust money in her tin box under the stern boards, six years of small betrayals, and decided she would give it to the first family who came asking for help rebuilding. She thought of the marker stone she would carve for the village green, when there was a village green again, and what words it should bear.

*Here stood Old Merrow. The river remembers. Now we do too.*

Loss acknowledged. Regret declined.

She climbed the stairs, and behind her the ferry knocked softly against the stone, patient as ever, ready for whatever the morning asked.

Ada was waiting at the top, wrapped in someone's borrowed blanket, rain-worn and ash-pale. For a moment neither spoke. Then Ada said, "You came upstream. Through that. For us."

"You'd have done it for me," Bryn said.

"I don't know that I would have. A month ago I wouldn't have known anyone well enough to try."

"A month ago," Bryn said, "I was taking money to report you." She held out her hand, palm up, in the lamplight—empty, and meaning it to stay empty. "Terms are simple from here. No more ledgers. Anybody keeps anything secret, they say it out loud within a day. We sink or swim on what's true."

Ada looked at the hand. Took it. Their grip was cold and strong and did not let go quickly.

Down the high street, a woman was calling names through the rain—checking her neighbors, counting her people—and the crowd was answering, one by one, *here, here, here*, and the sound rolled down toward the water like a roll call the valley had been waiting a century to take.

Marta touched Bryn's elbow. "Come," the old woman said. "There's bread in the church hall, and tomorrow there'll be mud to the knees and work to the elbows. But tonight—" she nodded at the lit windows, the free-running dark, the song still threading through the crowd— "tonight we say the names out loud. All of them. Starting with the ones they drowned."

And Bryn Halvard, ferry-woman, sometime informer, granddaughter of Old Merrow, went with her into the light to learn how much a valley could remember when it finally chose to.
