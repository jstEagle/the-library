## 33. What the Water Gave Back

The river gave back its dead slowly, and in no particular order.

Ada learned this in the first week after the flood, walking the new banks at dawn with a canvas satchel and a hand trowel that had once belonged to their mother. The water had rearranged the valley the way a child rearranges a drawer—everything pulled out, shaken, and set down again without regard for where it had lived before. Fields that had been pasture were now gravel shoals. A lane that had run through Gallowford's lower quarter surfaced two miles downstream, its cobbles furred with silt, as if the road had been swimming and had finally come ashore to dry.

And on those banks, everywhere, the objects.

They appeared the way shell middens used to appear to Ada on the Rivergate flats: half-buried, glinting, patient. A pewter spoon handle worn smooth by a century of thumbs. The iron hinge of a door no one had opened since 1893. A doll's porcelain head, one blue eye intact, staring up out of the mud with an expression Ada could not bear to name. The flood had scoured the drowned villages of Old Merrow and the four others—the ones the maps had never admitted to—and carried their contents downriver like a long exhale, depositing them along every bend where the current slowed.

The first morning, Ada had knelt over a scatter of things near the ferry ruin and felt the old reflex rise: catalog, bag, label, remove. Extract. It was what they'd been trained for, what they'd done in the cave, what the Trust had done for a hundred years with everything it touched. Their hands actually moved toward the trowel before something in their chest pulled up short.

These weren't finds. They were returns.

* * *

Word traveled the way word always did in the valley—faster than any official notice, slower than grief. By the second week, people were coming to the banks.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in knots of ten and twenty, picking their way down the cut banks in boots and Sunday coats alike. Old women with walking sticks. Teenagers filming on cracked phones. A man from Gallowford who brought a folding chair and sat all day watching the water, though he never bent to dig. Bryn ferried some of them across where the bridges were gone, refusing payment—"the Trust's not paying me anymore, so I'm rich," she said, and spat cheerfully into the current—and Marta came when she could, half-blind, guided by her granddaughter's arm, standing at the edge of the mud with her face lifted as if listening to something beneath it.

"They're singing," she said once, apropos of nothing, or perhaps of everything. "The river. It's off-key. It's been off-key for a hundred years and now it's finding the tune."

Ada worked the banks the way they'd once worked middens, but differently now. Slower. With questions instead of conclusions. They learned to read the deposits not as strata but as sentences: here, where the current had curled back on itself, a household's worth of small things gathered together—a thimble, a pipe bowl, three buttons, a child's ring carved from antler—likely all from one room, one family, one interrupted evening. There, where the flood had smashed through hard, objects scattered wide and anonymous, stripped of context, orphaned twice.

The hardest part was not the digging. Ada was good at digging; digging had never betrayed them. The hardest part was the moment after, when someone came looking.

* * *

The woman's name was Sef Tonnesdatter, and she arrived on a gray Thursday with a photograph in a waterproof sleeve and hands that shook until she started working.

"My grandmother's house," she said, standing over the section Ada had been opening for two days. "Old Merrow. Kitchen side, facing the water." She said it the way other people recited addresses, but her voice caught on *water*, and Ada understood that she had never seen the house at all—that she knew it only as coordinates in a story her grandmother had told so many times it had worn smooth, like the pewter spoons coming out of the mud.

"Then you know more than I do," Ada said, and handed her a spare trowel.

Sef dug beside them for six hours. She was methodical in a way that made Ada suspect farmers ran in the family, and she talked while she worked—not to Ada exactly, but to the ground, to the grandmother, to the century between them. She told the kitchen into being: the stove against the north wall, the bench by the window where her grandmother had shelled peas, the loose flagstone where—she stopped mid-sentence, went very still, and then began to dig with sudden, focused violence.

The tin came up forty minutes later. Small, rusted through at one corner, but latched. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had somehow held: a wedding ring, a lock of hair gone to copper wire, and a paper packet of seeds—dead, surely, a century dead—but Sef held the packet to the light anyway, turning it, reading a faded pencil label in a language Ada didn't know.

"Marjoram," Sef translated, and laughed, and cried, both at once, sitting in the mud with a dead garden in her hands. "She planted marjoram. In the last spring. Before they came with the notices."

Ada did not touch the tin. That was the rule they'd made for themselves, the discipline harder than any excavation technique they'd ever learned: dig, uncover, identify—and then step back. The object belonged to the living, or to whoever the living sent to claim it. Ada's job was the doorway, not the room.

But when Sef left that evening, carrying the tin against her sternum like a second heart, she stopped at the top of the bank and turned around.

"You're Eriksen," she said. Not a question. "The daughter."

"The child," Ada said. It felt more accurate.

"My grandmother hated your family's name for sixty years." Sef's face did something complicated. "I think she would have liked you anyway. You dig like someone asking, not taking."

After she was gone, Ada stood a long time looking at the section wall, at the clean edges of the day's work, and thought about how many ways there were to be named by a place, and how few of them you got to choose.

* * *

Not everyone who came was welcome, and not everything that surfaced should have.

On the ninth day, a man in a good coat picked his way along the bank below Gallowford with a leather case and an air of polite purpose that Ada recognized the way you recognize a smell from a bad year. He introduced himself as an appraiser, retained—he said delicately—by certain parties interested in documenting the flood's archaeological yield. Certain parties. He did not say the Trust, because there wasn't really a Trust anymore, not the way there had been; Vane's empire had drowned in its own archive, and what remained was a scattering of remnant members, lawyers, and creditors circling the wreckage. But the accent of entitlement survived the institution, the way a crest survives on a lantern rusting in a cave.

"Provenance is everything," the appraiser said, watching a boy pull a brass candlestick from the mud. "These items are technically assets of the dissolved estate. Families will want compensation, of course. Fair market value. We're prepared to be generous."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then they looked at the candlestick, and at the boy holding it, who was maybe fourteen and had the cheekbones of about six different valley families at once.

"What's fair market value for a grandmother?" Ada asked.

The appraiser blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"Because that's what these are. Not assets. Grandmothers. Kitchens. The pea-shelling bench by the window." Ada wiped their hands on their thighs and stood, putting the full unglamorous height of themselves between the man and the bank. "You can tell certain parties that the estate they're referring to was built on theft, and the flood has settled the accounts. Everything here goes back to the descendants. For free. Which is the first time anything in this valley has been free in a hundred years, so I'd hate to ruin the streak."

He left. He would be back, or someone like him; Ada harbored no illusions. Power that had run on consent for a century did not evaporate because one bell rang and one gate opened. But the boy with the candlestick watched the whole exchange, and afterward he came over and asked, shyly, if Ada would teach him how to tell how old a thing was by the dirt around it.

That, too, was restitution. Maybe most of it was.

* * *

Ingrid came to the banks on a bright cold morning three weeks after the flood, and for once she did not bring authority or apology or any of the heavy furniture of their history. She brought a notebook and a thermos and the expression she wore in the field, which was the only expression Ada had ever truly trusted on her mother's face: attention, total and undivided, aimed at the ground.

They worked in parallel for an hour without speaking, twenty feet apart, two figures bent over the same mud, and it was the closest thing to peace Ada had known since before the cave. This, it turned out, was what reconciliation looked like when it stopped trying to be a speech: two people doing the same work, trusting each other's silence.

"There," Ingrid said eventually, without lifting her head. "Two feet left of the stone. See how the silt changes color? That's a floor surface. Something was kept there."

Ada saw it. Of course they saw it—they'd have seen it in their sleep. But hearing their mother read the ground aloud, casually, generously, as if teaching rather than performing, undid something in their chest that all the weeping in room four had never reached.

Together they uncovered a wooden box the size of a bread loaf, its lid swollen shut with a century of water. Inside, packed in wool gone to gray felt: a set of carpenter's chisels, each handle worn to the shape of a particular hand, and a slate with a child's arithmetic still scratched into it—sums half-finished, abandoned mid-column, 1893 or whenever the notice had come and the family had walked away from a half-solved problem and never come back.

Ingrid held the slate a long time.

"I spent my whole career," she said quietly, "taking things out of the ground and putting them in rooms where the descendants couldn't reach them. I told myself it was preservation. Stewardship." Her thumb traced the unfinished sum. "It was just the family trade, Ada. We were charter signatories. Extraction is in the ink of us."

"And now?" Ada asked.

"Now I'm learning to put things back." She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and clear at once. "You taught me that. Don't let it go to your head."

Ada laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of them—and their mother smiled, and the river ran past them, off-key still, but finding the tune.

* * *

By the fourth week, the banks had developed a rhythm, almost a festival quality, though no one would have called it that. People arrived in the mornings with tools and photographs and the names of the dead. They dug in loose crews, strangers recognizing each other's village names like passwords. Someone rigged a wash station from a rain barrel. Someone else started a board at the top of the bank where claims could be posted: *seeking anything marked H., Merrow parish; seeking a fiddle; seeking a blue enamel pot, lid missing.* The blue enamel pot was found on a Tuesday and caused a celebration that lasted past dark, with a fire on the shingle and Marta singing, her voice thin and sure, a song about a girl gathering mussels that everyone under seventy pretended not to know and everyone over seventy sang word for word.

Ada moved among it all with their notebook, doing the thing they had once done alone in a shuttered boathouse for money and silence: recording. But the entries had changed. No accession numbers. No *recovered by*. Just descriptions, locations, and names—names of claimants, names of the original owners when anyone remembered them, the chain of custody running backward through time toward the people it had always belonged to.

One evening Bryn found them at the notebook and read over their shoulder, which was a liberty, but Bryn's liberties had earned a kind of residency.

"You know what you're writing, don't you?" she said.

"A record."

"You're writing the true map. The one they burned. Every object's a pin in it." She tapped the page. "Old Merrow's not underwater anymore. It's right here. Third entry from the top."

Ada looked at the page. At the spoon, the chisels, the marjoram seeds, the blue pot. At the accumulating weight of small returned things, which was somehow heavier and lighter than any treasure.

"It needs the songs too," they said. "Marta's sluice song. The mussel-gathering one. The words are data—place-names, routes, who lived where. It's all one archive."

Bryn grinned. "So write it down before we old ones die and take the tune with us."

"That's the plan," Ada said. And then, because honesty had become a habit they couldn't shake: "It's going to be a book. Eventually. Not the kind my mother writes. Not the kind the Trust would have burned, either, because there's nothing in it they'd want. Just... water, and songs, and who kept them."

Bryn was quiet a moment, which for Bryn was a eulogy-length silence. "Your mother's going to cry when she reads it," she said at last. "Fair warning."

* * *

The last find of the season came up on a morning of high thin cloud, at the bend below the ferry ruin, and it was not a spoon or a slate or a pot.

It was a door.

A whole door, oak, iron-strapped, its paint long gone but its grain deep and sound, lying face-up in the fresh silt as neatly as if it had been laid there for display. And nailed to it at eye height, protected all these decades by its own wood, was a small tin sign, corroded green but legible:

**MERROW PARISH SCHOOL — PLEASE SHUT THE DOOR BEHIND YOU.**

A crowd gathered, the way crowds did now. Nobody claimed it—there was no descendant of the schoolkeeper present, or if there was, they didn't speak. The door lay there in the mud with its instruction facing the sky, and the whole bankful of people stood looking at it, and the river ran past, and somewhere upstream the source pool sat open in its ring of wildflowers, feeding all of this, feeding everything, asking nothing.

Marta arrived late, guided by her granddaughter, and when they read her the sign she nodded slowly, as if confirming something long suspected.

"That's the whole history of this valley in one line," she said. "Please shut the door behind you. And nobody did. So look what came through it."

They raised the door upright that afternoon, all hands, planting it in the shingle at the top of the bank like a standing stone, facing the water. A marker. The first of what Bryn promised would be many—one for every village, at the places the songs named. People hung things on it as they passed: a ribbon, a button, a sprig of something flowering yellow from the new banks. By week's end it wore its offerings the way a shrine wears them, and no one had organized that either. Some inheritances, Ada was learning, assemble themselves, given ground and permission.

They stayed until dusk, sketching the door in the notebook, writing the sign's text letter by letter, dating the entry. Behind them the fire crackled and Marta's granddaughter was learning the mussel song, getting the third verse wrong in exactly the way the third verse had probably always been gotten wrong. Ahead of them the river moved in the dark, audible now even when unseen, running free and loud and slightly off-key, correcting itself note by note.

Ada closed the notebook and found, tucked in the back pocket where it had no business being, a folded scrap of paper in handwriting they knew better than their own.

*Ada — the Trust's remnants are meeting in Gallowford next week. They want a new charter. They want our names on it. Come to room four and tell me what you think inheritance is for. — I.*

Ada read it twice, standing in the firelight with the door at their back and the river saying its long slow sentence in front of them. Then they folded it away, shouldered the satchel, and started up the bank toward the lights of town, toward room four, toward whatever it was they were becoming.
