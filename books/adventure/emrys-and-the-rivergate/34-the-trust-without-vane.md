## 34. The Trust Without Vane

The letter arrived on the third morning of October, carried up the hill to the Eriksen cottage by a young woman in a warden's coat with the insignia freshly unpicked from the collar, leaving a ghost of darker thread in the shape of a key.

Ada knew her before she said her name. The corridor-warden. The one who had unlocked the passage at Headwaters House and walked away without a word, her boots ringing on the stone like a bell that had finally been allowed to strike. In the flood's aftermath she had become, without ever asking to be, a figure: the woman who opened the door. Ada had seen her twice since, always at a distance, always at the edge of the crowds that gathered where the water gave things back.

"I'm Sera Voss," she said, standing in the drizzle with her hands at her sides, the letter dry inside her coat. "I was a warden for eleven years. I'd like to talk to you. Not today, if you'd rather. But soon."

Ada looked at the ghost-key stitched into her collar and thought of all the doors in their life that had been locked by people wearing uniforms, and how this one had been opened by someone wearing the same cloth.

"Tea's on," Ada said. "It's terrible tea. But it's on."

* * *

Voss sat with her coat folded over the back of her chair like a shed skin, and she drank the terrible tea without complaint, and she talked for a long time with the careful, exhausted precision of someone who had rehearsed this in her head for weeks and was determined not to make it sound rehearsed.

The Trust still existed. That was the first thing. Vane had gone down with Headwaters House—refused the roof, refused the boat, refused everything—and with him had gone the charter, the ledgers, the machinery, the whole drowned apparatus of a century of control. But an institution is not a building. An institution is a habit wearing a building, and habits survive floods. There were wardens—dozens of them, scattered through the valley—who had not signed on to drown villages and had not known, until the bell rang, what they were keeping. There were clerks in Gallowford who had processed the relocation payments for years without ever reading past the amounts. There were landholders whose deeds traced back to 1893, who had inherited their fields the way Ada had inherited a family Bible they'd never known existed.

"Some of them want it all back," Voss said. "The old arrangement. The quiet. They're meeting in the mill offices, drawing up papers. They'll say the flood proved the valley needs managing. And they'll be half right, which is the dangerous kind of right." She turned her cup a slow circle on the table. "The rest of us want something else. But we can't build it alone, and we can't build it looking like the old thing with new paint. That's where you come in. Where your mother comes in. Where the bell-keeper comes in."

"The new charter," Ada said.

Voss reached into her coat and laid a folder on the table—not the Trust's heavy cream stock with its embossed crest, but plain paper, the kind schoolchildren used. "A draft. The fourth one. It's not finished. That's rather the point."

Ada opened it and read, and the cottage was very quiet around the reading, quiet enough that they could hear the river a half-mile down, running clear and loud and free, the sound it had made for a century before anyone thought to own it.

The charter was short. That was the second thing Ada noticed, and it moved them more than anything else in it. The old charter had been forty pages of dense, self-protective prose, all of it designed so that no one could ever find the crime inside it. This was three pages. *The water of the Emrys belongs to no one and is the responsibility of everyone who lives along it.* *All records of the Trust, past and present, to be held in public, in Gallowford, open to any person without charge or permission.* *Descendants of the relocated families to hold permanent seats on the governing council.* *No gate, sluice, or weir to be built, closed, or altered without published cause and open hearing.* *The names of the drowned villages to be restored to the maps.*

At the bottom, where the old charter had borne the signatures of eleven men who had dynamited a spring system and called it improvement, there were blank lines. Dozens of them. Waiting.

"There's a seat for a witness," Voss said. "Not an owner. Not a director. Someone who signs not because they hold power but because they've seen what power does when it goes unwitnessed. We thought—" she stopped, started again, more honestly. "I thought of you. I watched you at the second gate. I know what you chose, and what it cost."

Ada closed the folder and did not answer for a long moment. Outside, the drizzle had thickened into proper rain, and the light through the window went the color of pewter, and somewhere down the valley a dog was barking at nothing in particular, and all of it—the rain, the dog, the ordinary gray afternoon—felt like the world continuing, which was the thing Vane had never believed in. He had believed the world was a machine that required an operator. Ada had opened the gates to prove otherwise, and the world, so far, had continued.

"Why me?" Ada asked finally. "You could ask anyone."

"Because everyone else who could sign it is either downstream of the Trust or upstream of it. You're the only person in this valley who's stood at the source." Voss smiled, tired and crooked. "And because if you sign it, people will believe it isn't a trap. They trust what you refused."

* * *

Ada took the folder to Bryn that evening, because there was no decision in their life anymore that felt real until Bryn had looked at it and said something inconvenient.

They sat in the ferry shed at Gallowford landing, where the new service was slowly becoming a fact of the river—two boats now, a third being built, a painted sign that read HALVARD & PASSENGERS in Bryn's blunt carpenter's hand. The shed smelled of tar and wet rope and the particular cold sweetness of autumn water. Bryn read the draft twice, slowly, her lips moving on the second pass, and Ada watched her and thought, not for the first time, that Bryn read the way she poled a boat: with total attention and no wasted motion.

"Well," Bryn said at last. "It's a good document."

"But."

"But it's a document." She tapped the paper. "Paper didn't drown Old Merrow. Paper didn't pay my gran thirty shillings and a train ticket to forget her own street. Paper is what they wrote *after*, every time. The question isn't what it says. The question is who's holding the pen in ten years, and whether the people downstream will still be reading." She looked up. "You're thinking about Vane."

"I'm always thinking about Vane."

"He believed the right things about order. That's what made him dangerous. Not that he lied—he mostly didn't, by the end. He just thought the flood was a price and never asked who paid it." Bryn folded her arms. "So. Are you going to sign?"

"I don't know. That's the truth. Part of me wants to burn it, and part of me wants to frame it, and neither of those is an answer."

Bryn nodded as though this were the most reasonable thing she'd heard all day. "Good. Sign it when the burning and the framing both feel stupid. Not before."

* * *

The hearing was held in Gallowford's church on the last Saturday of October, because it was the largest room in the town and because, as Marta said, the bell should be able to hear what was decided beneath it.

Ada had not seen the church full since the flood. It was full now—not with the polite congregation of Trust patronage, the mill-families in their good coats, but with everyone. Fen farmers with peat still on their boots. Women from the higher settlements whose grandmothers had sung the sluice song. Wardens out of uniform, sitting in the back rows like men and women learning to be civilians. Descendants of Merrow and the four other drowned villages, some of whom had learned their own history only weeks ago, from Bryn's mouth, on her ferry, and who sat now with the particular stillness of people hearing their own names spoken in public for the first time.

Voss stood at the front with the charter on an easel, and she did not present it—she read it aloud, all three pages, plainly, the way you'd read a weather report, and then she said, "It's a draft. It's not done until you've argued with it," and they argued with it for four hours.

Ada sat near the back and listened. They heard a fen farmer demand that the sluice sequence be written down and published, not kept in songs—*no offense to the songs, but songs die with the singers*—and Marta, small and half-blind in the third row, call back, "Then write the song down, lad, and teach it in schools, and it'll outlive your committee." They heard a former clerk stand with shaking hands and confess, in front of the whole town, that she had processed the relocation payments for nine years and had told herself they were pensions, and ask—not for forgiveness, but for a job in the new records office, where she could be the one who made sure no page went missing. They heard a man whose deed went back to 1893 rise and say, "My land was stolen for me. I'll sign it back myself, or my name's on the wrong side of this."

And through all of it, Ada felt the thing they had been circling for weeks finally come close enough to touch: that this was what the Trust had never once allowed. Not agreement—agreement was cheap—but argument. Open, unmanaged, unafraid argument, in a room that smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke, under a bell that had once been the only voice the drowned were permitted.

When the arguing finally wound down, Voss looked out at the room and said, "We'll redraft with what you've said. And then we'll need signatures. All of yours, eventually. But there's one line that needs a particular name." She found Ada in the back. "The witness line. If you'll take it."

The whole room turned. Ada stood, and their heart was going like a fish in a net, and they thought of Vane at the second gate with his gun and his truest case—*someone must be blamed, someone must be in charge, order doesn't maintain itself*—and they thought of Ingrid's answer, *then let it be weather, not owners*, and they thought of Bryn: *sign it when the burning and the framing both feel stupid.*

They didn't feel stupid. Not yet. Not quite.

"Not tonight," Ada said. "I'll sign when it's finished. When it's argued all the way through and there's nothing left in it that anyone's ashamed of. I won't put my name on a promise that still has a corner cut." A murmur went through the church—not displeasure; Ada heard it, relief, almost. "But I'll be here for every redraft. Every argument. Count on that."

Voss nodded slowly. "That," she said, "might be the most Trust-worthy thing anyone's said in this room in a hundred years," and the laugh that went through the church was the first laugh Ada had heard in it, and it sounded like water finding an old channel.

* * *

Walking home in the dark afterward, Ada took the river road, as they always did now, and the Emrys ran beside them loud and silver under a breaking sky, and they thought about names.

Their whole life, they had thought inheritance meant receiving—receiving a name, a deed, a debt, a Bible with your family in it. The Trust had been built on that understanding: that the valley was something the founders had received and could therefore dispose of. But standing in the church, watching a clerk ask for the chance to guard the records she'd once buried things in, watching a landholder offer to sign back stolen ground, watching Marta insist the song be taught—Ada had understood something else. Inheritance was not a possession. It was a *post*. A watch you kept. The valley was not left to them; it was left *with* them, the way a lamp is left with the last person awake.

The letter in their pocket—the plain-paper draft with its blank lines—felt heavier than paper had any right to feel.

At the bend above Gallowford they stopped, as they always stopped, and looked down at the town: the mill with its windows dark now that the Trust's patronage had ended, the new records office with its lights on late—already, the clerk and her volunteers were cataloguing the salvaged archive, the drowned things the water had given back, preparing them for the public shelves. And beyond the town, up the dark valley, the direction of Headwaters House, where the ruins stood open to the sky now, the spring running through the broken floors, wildflowers already coming up through the flooded archive rooms in the warm shallows—white flowers, river flowers, blooming in the wreck of the machine.

Ada stood a long time. Then they took out the draft, and a pencil, and by the light of the rising moon they did not sign it.

They wrote in the margin instead, small and clear, where the redrafters would find it: *Add a line. The charter may be amended by any descendant of the relocated families, at any hearing, forever. No clause in this document may ever be made unarguable.*

Because that was the test, they thought. The corruption didn't live in the men of 1893; it lived in the moment a document became untouchable, a decision unquestionable, a door that no longer opened. Vane had died believing someone must hold the pen. The only way to prove him wrong forever was to make sure no one ever held it alone.

Ada put the draft away and walked on down the river road, and behind them the bell in Gallowford's church rang the hour—nine slow strokes, ordinary, unhurried, the bell of a town that now decided its own alarms—and ahead, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure was waiting by the landing gate, and even at that distance Ada knew the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head, the way she stood as though the ground were a site she was reading.

Her mother had come to hear about the hearing. And from the look on Ingrid Eriksen's face, she had brought news of her own.
