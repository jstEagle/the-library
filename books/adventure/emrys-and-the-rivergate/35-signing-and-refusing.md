## 35. Signing and Refusing

The new charter was written on paper that felt like an apology.

Ada noticed that first—before the language, before the room, before the faces arranged around the long table in what had once been the Trust's map room at Headwaters House. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind meant to outlast the hands that held it. But someone had chosen it deliberately soft-edged, uncrested, free of the interlocked R and T that had stamped everything the old Trust touched: lanterns, ledgers, toll-markers, lives. This document had no crest because no one yet owned what it described.

The room itself still smelled of the flood. Underneath the beeswax and fresh plaster there was a green note, riverish, like stones turned over in shallow water. The windows had been replaced but not yet curtained, and afternoon light came through them unfiltered, laying long pale rectangles across the table where the charter lay open to its final page.

Ada stood at the edge of that light and did not sit.

They had been asked to sit. Warden Oren Castellan—the one who had unlocked the corridor, who had walked away from his post with his keys still swinging on their ring—had pulled out a chair for them personally, with a courtesy so careful it was almost grief. *We would be honored,* he'd said, and Ada had heard in the word *honored* all the weight of a century of dishonor trying to balance itself on a single syllable.

So they stood, and read.

The charter ran to eleven pages. Ada had read all of them twice in the days since Castellan's people had carried copies downriver—to Gallowford, to the fen settlements, to the high-ground farms where relocated families' grandchildren had begun drifting back like birds remembering a route. The document was plain-spoken almost to the point of bluntness. *The waters of the Emrys shall be held in common.* *All records recovered or preserved shall be public within one year.* *Descendants of the relocated villages shall hold no fewer than half the seats of any governing body.* It named the founding crime without euphemism: *In 1893, the Rivergate Trust dynamited the natural spring system of this valley, drowning five villages and falsifying the maps thereof, in order to control the flow of water and thereby the flow of wealth.*

Someone had fought over that sentence. Ada could tell by the ink density, the way certain letters had been pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath. Ingrid had told them about the arguments—three weeks of them, conducted in this same room, between remnant Trust members who wanted the past softened and the descendants' council who refused every softening. Marta had attended one session, been offered tea, declined it, and said only: *Put it plain or don't put it at all. We were drowned in plain sight. Plain is the least you owe us.*

It was plain now.

But it was the last page that had brought Ada here, standing instead of sitting, reading instead of signing. Because the last page contained a table—a place for names—and beside each name a line of small print that Ada had read four times and still felt the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it:

*Signatories hereto accept ownership, stewardship, and authority over the waters herein described, together with all rights, privileges, and obligations appertaining thereto.*

Ownership. Stewardship. Authority.

The words sat together on the page like old friends, comfortable with one another, and it was exactly that comfort that made Ada's skin prickle. A century ago, men with Vane's certainty had signed a different charter using these same three words, and they had believed—genuinely, as Vane had genuinely believed—that ownership was the same thing as care, that authority was just stewardship wearing its good coat. The valley had drowned under words like these. Not despite them. *Through* them.

"Dr. Eriksen."

Castellan's voice, gentle. He stood near the mantel, where the old Trust portrait had been taken down—you could see the paler rectangle on the wallpaper where it had hung—and he waited with the patience of a man who had learned, late and expensively, what impatience cost.

"We don't need your signature," he said. "I want to be clear about that. The charter stands without you. The council has ratified. The sluice-keepers have ratified. What we're asking for—" he paused, choosing, "—is something else. Your name would mean that the people who broke the river and the people who freed it are willing to stand under the same roof and answer for it together."

"And if I'd rather stand outside?" Ada said. Not hostile. Curious, even. They were genuinely curious what he'd say.

Castellan considered. "Then I'd ask you to keep standing there," he said. "Where we can see you. Where you can see us." He almost smiled. "Accountability works better in daylight."

* * *

They found Ingrid in the gallery below, the lower corridor where Ada had first seen a figure moving behind lamplight and let themselves believe—hadn't they, some stubborn part of them?—that it might be a ghost, because a ghost would have been easier than hope.

Ingrid stood at the long table there, surrounded by the rescued archive. The flood had taken the sealed doors and the catalogued cruelty of the collection—the specimen drawers, the numbered boxes—but the water had also, in its indiscriminate honesty, given things back. Downstream, on new banks, objects were surfacing weekly: a christening spoon, a school register, a wedding photograph gone soft as cloth. People came to claim them. Ada had spent two mornings at the Gallowford landing doing nothing but matching objects to faces, watching strangers weep over the belongings of grandparents they'd never met, and it was the hardest and most necessary work they had ever done.

Here in the gallery, Ingrid had laid out what remained of the working documents: the 1893 charter itself, warped by water into a shape like a cupped hand; the relocation ledgers with their annual payments to families who "accepted"; her own translations, done under duress, done brilliantly, done at a price she was still counting.

She looked up when Ada came in. Mother and child regarded each other across the table with the particular wariness of two people who have done fieldwork side by side in a flooding spring chamber and are still learning how to be in a dry, quiet room together.

"You've read it," Ingrid said. Not a question. She knew how Ada read—like digging, whole strata at a time.

"I've read it."

"And?"

Ada picked up a pencil from the table. Turned it over. "There's a word in it I can't sign."

Ingrid's mouth tightened—not anger; recognition. She had raised this child. She knew which words would snag. "Ownership."

"Ownership," Ada agreed. "Stewardship, maybe. Authority, definitely." They set the pencil down. "Every bad thing in this valley started with somebody being handed those words and believing they were a responsibility instead of a permission slip."

"The charter needs signatories," Ingrid said. "If people like us refuse—"

"People like us," Ada said quietly, "are why it needs them."

The silence that followed had texture, like fog. Ingrid looked down at the drowned charter, its cupped-hand curve, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, more careful, the voice she used near fragile finds.

"When I found out," she said, "that our name was on the original—that Eriksens signed the drowning of five villages—I couldn't breathe for a week. You were nine years old. You had a shell collection you kept in egg cartons, labeled in your terrible handwriting, and I remember looking at you and thinking: *this child will inherit my name, and my name is a crime.* So I left. I told myself I was protecting you from it." Her thumb moved along the warped paper's edge. "It took me twenty years and nearly dying in a flooded house to understand that leaving you the silence was worse than leaving you the name. Silence is how crimes stay clean."

"I know," Ada said. "I've read the ledger."

"No." Ingrid shook her head. "You've read *the* ledger. I mean the other one. The one every family keeps without writing it down—who we don't talk about, what we don't say at funerals. That ledger gets inherited too." She looked up. "So. Tell me what you're going to do."

Ada had been turning it over for three days, the way they turned finds over—under good light, against known backgrounds, checking for what the object wanted to hide. And what they kept coming back to was not the charter's last page but a moment on the roofline, water rising floor by floor below them, Vane's house becoming a reef. Bryn's ferry cutting the surge. Marta singing the sluice sequence in a voice like a bell with a crack in it, and everyone steering by her song because the song was true and true things hold.

Nobody on that roof had owned anything. Not the boat, not the water, not each other. And it had worked—it had *held*—because every person there had accepted responsibility without claiming possession. Responsibility was a load you carried. Ownership was a thing you stood on. The difference was the whole history of the valley.

"I'll sign," Ada said. "As a witness."

Ingrid frowned. "The table doesn't have a witness column."

"Then I'll make one." Ada picked up the pencil again. "I'm not taking ownership of the river, Mum. Nobody should. That's the entire lesson—it's the lesson written in five drowned villages and a hundred years of floods. But I'll sign my name to what happened. To what was done in my family's name and in the Trust's name and in the name of order. I'll sign as someone who saw it, who helped end it, and who intends to keep watching. Watching isn't owning. It's better than owning."

Ingrid was silent a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly pressed flat, but real. "You know," she said, "when you were nine, you refused to label your shell collection 'my collection.' You insisted on 'the shells' collection.' Drove me mad."

"It was a good instinct," Ada said.

"It was an insufferable instinct." But her eyes were bright, and she reached across the table and took the pencil from Ada's hand, and turned it over the same way Ada had, as if weighing it. "Witness," she said. "Yes. All right. Yes."

* * *

The signing happened the next morning, in the map room, with the windows still bare and the river audible through them—a sound that had changed since the gates opened, since the source ran unbound. Louder, freer, less metronomic. A river that had spent a century being scheduled now kept its own hours again, and everyone who lived along it was relearning how to listen.

Half the valley seemed to have come. The room was full past capacity: council members from the descendant families, sluice-keepers, fen farmers, Bryn leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed and her grandmother's alarm-pattern whistle on a cord around her neck, Marta seated near the front with her clouded eyes aimed generally toward the light. Someone had brought flowers—wildflowers, ragged and riotous, jammed into a repurposed Trust inkwell—and the smell of them mixed with the green smell of the flood-still-fading, and it was, Ada thought, the best a room had ever smelled.

Castellan presented the charter. He read the founding-crime sentence aloud, plainly, and the room received it the way ground receives rain—some absorbing fast, some slower, all of it going somewhere necessary.

Then the names.

The descendant representatives signed first, in a slow procession, and each signature was its own small ceremony. An old woman from Old Merrow's line signed with a hand that shook and a face that didn't. A young man whose family had farmed fen ground for four generations signed and then stood there a moment, looking at his own name as though checking it for leaks.

Then Castellan turned to the table where Ada and Ingrid sat—sat, this time; Ada had decided that refusing the chair was a performance, and performances belonged to Vane—and slid the charter toward them, open to the last page.

"One matter," Ada said, and stood, and the room stilled. Their voice came out steadier than they'd expected. "Before we sign. There's a word on this page I want to answer."

They laid the charter flat and set the pencil point against the offending line—*ownership, stewardship, and authority*—and then, in the margin beside it, in handwriting that was still terrible after all these years, they wrote:

*Belonging isn't a deed.*

They read it aloud. Some in the room nodded slowly. Marta said, clearly enough for everyone, "That's the first true sentence written in this house in a hundred years," and there was laughter, relieved and rough.

Then Ada signed—but not in the space marked for owners. Below the table of signatories, beneath the printed columns, they added a new line in the same terrible handwriting: *Witnesses.* And beneath it: *Ada Eriksen — witness to the founding crime, the covering of it, and the ending of it. Witness going forward.*

They passed the pencil to their mother.

Ingrid stood. For a moment she looked at the page—at the space where an Eriksen name had appeared once before, a hundred and thirty years ago, above the word *authority*—and Ada watched her mother's jaw set against whatever rose in her.

"I am the great-grandchild of a signatory of the 1893 charter," Ingrid said, to the room, in her lecture voice, the one that could hold a hall of two hundred without effort. "For twenty years I couldn't say that sentence out loud. Today I'm saying it in this room, over this document, because a charter that tells the truth deserves witnesses who do the same." She bent and wrote beneath Ada's line: *Ingrid Eriksen — witness, translator, daughter of the valley, mother of the person who taught me the difference between carrying a thing and standing on it.*

She signed. Straightened. And then, in a smaller voice, just for Ada, just for the margin: "That was generous of you. Putting me second."

"Alphabetical," Ada said. "Obviously."

Ingrid laughed—the full laugh this time, unguarded, and several people in the room turned to look at her as if seeing her for the first time, which, Ada supposed, in a way they were.

* * *

Afterward, the crowd thinned by degrees, the way crowds do—clusters breaking off toward the door, voices spilling into the corridor, the smell of flowers and wet stone giving way to ordinary air. Bryn detached herself from the doorframe and ambled over, hands in pockets, wearing the expression she wore when she was about to say something practical and possibly devastating.

"So you're a witness now," she said.

"Apparently."

"Does witnessing pay?"

"Bryn."

"I'm asking for the ferry fund." But her eyes weren't joking, quite. She glanced at the charter, at the marginal line, and read it aloud softly: "*Belonging isn't a deed.*" She snorted. "My gran could've told them that for free. Cost her a village to learn it, mind." She clapped Ada on the shoulder, brief and hard, the Halvard equivalent of an embrace. "Come down to the landing tomorrow. We've got another crate of things the river gave back. There's a shoe in it that's got Marta crying and laughing at the same time, and I can't make head or tail of it, and you're the one who reads objects."

"I'll come," Ada said. And found that they meant it the way you mean *home*—not as a place you own, but as a direction you keep choosing.

Evening came on gold. Ada walked out alone onto the terrace where the gardens had once drunk stolen water while downstream villages drowned, and looked down at the spring-fed pool at the heart of the grounds—the source, running clear and loud and unscheduled, ringed already in early wildflowers that hadn't grown there in a century. The light lay on it like a hand laid flat, in blessing or in caution, impossible to say.

Behind them, inside the house, someone was reading the new charter aloud to someone who hadn't been able to attend, voice rising and falling through the plain sentences, the plain truths. Ahead of them, the river went down the valley keeping its own hours, carrying silt and seeds and the occasional returned belonging toward the sea.

Ada stayed until the light failed, and then a while longer, listening.

Tomorrow there would be a crate of returned things, and a shoe that made Marta laugh and cry, and work that was never finished—which was, Ada understood now, what work worth doing always was. But tonight there was only the water, and the margin of a page, and a sentence that would outlast every signature around it:

*Belonging isn't a deed.*

Some inheritances you refuse. Some, you become.
