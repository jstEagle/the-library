## 36. Room Four, Revisited

The road into Gallowford smelled of wet ash and new mud, and beneath both—faintly, improbably—of bread.

Ada noticed it before they saw the town: that warm yeast-sweetness drifting down the valley on a wind that had spent the morning crossing flooded fields. After weeks of fen water and dredge-stink and the cold mineral breath of Headwaters House, it seemed almost indecent, that smell. Ordinary. The smell of people going about the small stubborn business of feeding themselves while the world rearranged itself around them.

"Someone's baking," Ingrid said from beside them on the cart bench. She said it the way she might have announced a rare bird sighting—as if ordinary life were now something to be documented, catalogued, treasured in a field notebook.

"They'll be out of flour by Thursday," Ada said. "Half the low stores took water."

Ingrid glanced at them, and Ada felt the look land like a hand on the shoulder—not heavy, just present. "You've been counting," she said. Not accusing. Something closer to wonder.

"Somebody has to."

The cart driver—a Gallowford man named Pell who had ferried them up from the ferry ruin for the price of a story and half their remaining tea—snorted from under his oilskin hood. "Baker's wife already sent her boy up to the mill with a barrow. Trust grain store's open. First time in anyone's memory." He said *Trust* the way you'd say the name of a dog that had bitten you once and was now, cautiously, being given a second chance. "Warden Corrigan signed the order himself. Stood in the doorway while they weighed it out so nobody could say he hadn't."

Ada had met Corrigan only once—the warden who had unlocked the corridor at Headwaters House and walked away, his boots echoing down a hall lined with sealed doors. They'd exchanged no words. There hadn't needed to be any. But his name kept surfacing now, attached to small acts: the grain store, the release of the ferry tolls, the notice nailed to the mill door announcing that the valley's records would be read aloud in the market square, one page each evening, until every family had heard what had been taken from them and what had been kept.

*Read aloud.* Ada turned the phrase over as the cart rattled across the repaired culvert bridge. It was such a modest thing. Not a monument, not an apology carved in stone. Just voices, evening after evening, saying names into the air where everyone could hear them.

Gallowford rose out of the mist the way it always had, Ada supposed—mill race roaring, slate roofs stepped up the hillside, the church tower standing over everything like a chaperone. But the town looked different now, or perhaps Ada looked differently. The bunting strung between the mill and the cooperage was not Trust green but every color its owners had possessed, tied in knots that meant nothing except celebration. Children ran the gutters where snowmelt mixed with river spray, shrieking. A man stood on a ladder rehanging a sign that had been taken down decades ago, according to Pell—*HALVARD & SONS, BOATWRIGHT*—though no Halvard had worked wood in Gallowford since the relocation. Bryn's family name, resurrected in paint.

"Bryn will want her boat back," Ingrid observed.

"Bryn will want her boat back *yesterday*," Ada said. "She's been talking about that hull since the roofline. She knows exactly which plank is rotten. She's been planning repairs in her head the way other people pray."

They left Pell at the market square, where a crowd had gathered around a trestle table stacked with damp, warped, salt-stained books—the first salvaged volumes from the drowned archive, laid out in the open air like patients after surgery. An old woman with spectacles on a chain moved down the row, turning pages with a conservator's gentleness. Ada recognized the gesture because they made it themselves, daily, at the restitution tables by the new banks downstream. Some things the flood had ruined beyond saving. Others it had washed clean of a century's dust and handed back.

"You'll be wanting the inn, then," Pell said, gathering his reins. He didn't ask why. In Gallowford these days, nobody asked why anyone went anywhere; there was too much coming home to do.

* * *

The Gallowford Arms stood where it had always stood, wedged between the chandler's and a building whose ground floor had been a bank and whose upper floors, rumor held, still contained the ledgers of every debt the Trust had ever extended in the valley. Its sign creaked on fresh hinges. Its windows glowed.

Inside, the public room was warm and loud and smelled of woodsmoke, spilled ale, and that same bread. A fire had been lit against the autumn damp, and around it sat perhaps thirty people in various states of drying out, arguing cheerfully about sluice gates.

"—told him, the third gate sticks, it always stuck, my father said so and his father before—"

"—well, Marta sang it different, and I'll take Marta's version over your father's, no offense to your father—"

"—no offense taken, but the third gate *sticks*—"

Ada stopped in the doorway. Ingrid bumped gently into their back.

"Marta's version was right," Ada said, to the room in general.

Every head turned. Ada had been recognized before, in the weeks since the flood—it came with the territory of having been seen cranking open a reservoir gate while a house drowned behind you—but recognition here had a different texture than in the papers, which had gotten hold of the story and dressed it in words like *scandal* and *reckoning*. Here it was simpler. People looked at Ada the way they looked at the weather: something that had happened to all of them, that they were all still learning to live inside.

"The third gate," Ada confirmed. "It stuck. We broke a crank handle on it. If we'd gone in the order your father remembered, we'd have lost twenty minutes we didn't have."

A murmur ran through the room, satisfied, vindicating. Somewhere near the fire someone raised a glass. The old woman with the spectacles looked up from her books and nodded once, gravely, as though filing the information among the records she guarded.

The innkeeper—a broad woman named Sorrel whom Ada had never met but who had clearly been briefed—came through from the kitchen wiping her hands. "Two rooms?" she asked, then caught herself, and something kind and careful crossed her face. "Or one?"

Ada opened their mouth and found they didn't know the answer.

"One," Ingrid said quietly. "If it's free."

"It's not free," Sorrel said. "Nothing's free, that's how we got into this mess. But it's honest-priced." She hesitated, then added, lower, "Room four's empty. Has been since—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Word traveled in Gallowford the way water did: finding every channel, pooling in every low place. Everyone knew Dr. Eriksen had stayed in room four. Everyone knew what she'd been asking about. Everyone knew, now, how it had ended—or how it had refused to end.

"We'll take it," Ada said.

Their voice came out strange. Steady, but strange, as if someone else were speaking through them. Ingrid's hand found their elbow—brief, light, gone again—and they climbed the stairs together through the noise of the public room, past the landing window where evening was coming down blue over the mill race, along a corridor that smelled of beeswax and old plaster.

Room four's door was plain pine, painted the color of weak tea. Ada stood before it longer than a door deserved.

"You don't have to—" Ingrid began.

"I know." Ada turned the handle.

* * *

The room was small and clean and utterly unremarkable: iron bedstead, washstand, a chair with a rush seat, a window overlooking the yard where a cat was conducting surveillance on the woodpile. Someone had put wildflowers in a jar on the sill—yarrow and purple loosestrife, the weeds of the new banks. The wardrobe stood against the wall opposite the bed, dark oak, older than the inn itself probably, its doors scarred where furniture movers had banged it through doorways across a hundred years.

Ada set down their pack. Their hands were shaking. They watched them shake with a kind of clinical interest, the way they'd once watched a trowel tremble over a burial, waiting for steadiness to return or deciding to work anyway.

"I slept here," they said. "After the bell-keeper. That night. I didn't know it was your room until morning, when I found—" They stopped. Started again. This was the trouble with truth: it came out crooked, needing several attempts. "I found the pencil marks. Inside the wardrobe. The map fragment. *Ask the bell-keeper.*"

"I know," Ingrid said. "Marta wrote to me. Or tried to. Her letters took weeks to reach me, and Vane's people read them all." She moved past Ada into the room, and Ada watched her cross it—watched her touch the washstand jug, straighten the flower jar a degree, run one finger along the windowsill as if measuring it for memory. Their mother, cataloguing a room. Some things, apparently, were genetic.

"She told me someone had found the marks," Ingrid said. "She wouldn't say who. She said, *a stranger with your hands.*" Ingrid smiled, small and crooked. "I've thought about that phrase for two months. Wondering whose hands. Hoping, and not daring to hope."

Ada's throat was tight. "You could have written back. Told me. Instead of—" They gestured vaguely, a gesture that encompassed the whole terrible architecture of the last months: the silence, the years of silence before that, the duress, the translation work, the choice to let Ada believe she was dead rather than tell them the truth about the family name in the charter.

"I know," Ingrid said again. No defense in it this time. Just the flat acknowledgment they had worn since the spring chamber, since the second gate, since Vane's gun and the water taking the house floor by floor. She had apologized in the flood surge, shouting over the roar of the source unbound. She had apologized on Bryn's ferry deck, soaked and shivering, steering by Marta's song. She would probably apologize, Ada understood now, for the rest of her life, in small installments, and the apology would never be sufficient and would also never stop being true.

But tonight, in room four, neither of them reached for it.

Instead, Ingrid knelt in front of the wardrobe.

* * *

She opened the left-hand door first, then the right, and the smell of old cedar breathed out into the room. The interior was bare—Sorrel ran a tidy house—but the back panel caught the lamplight at an angle, and there they were: the marks. Faint as breath on glass. A fragmentary line map, sketched in soft pencil: the river's course above Gallowford, the fens, a dotted route through country the official maps called impassable. And beneath it, in Ingrid's small precise capitals: ASK THE BELL-KEEPER.

Ada crouched beside her. Up close, they could see what they hadn't seen that first desperate night—the hesitation marks, the places where the pencil had lifted and returned, the little erasures where she'd second-guessed a bearing. Evidence of a woman working fast, in secret, in a rented room, leaving a message she could only hope would find the right eyes.

"You drew this from memory," Ada said. "Of the culvert survey. You'd memorized the route in case they searched your notes."

"And they did search my notes," Ingrid said. "Twice. They never thought to look in a wardrobe in Gallowford. Vane's imagination had limits. He couldn't conceive of hiding anything in a place so ordinary." She touched the word BELL-KEEPER lightly, reverently, the way she touched artifacts. "He thought secrets lived in vaults."

Ada laughed—a short, startled sound. "He thought history lived in vaults. That was his whole problem."

"He wasn't entirely wrong, though, was he." Ingrid's voice went quiet. "History does live in vaults. In archives. In wardrobes. In songs." She looked sideways at Ada, lamplight catching the gray in her hair that hadn't been there a year ago. "That's rather the point of everything that happened. Whoever keeps the vault keeps the past."

Ada didn't answer right away. Outside, the mill race thundered on, steady as a heartbeat, running free for the first time in a century. Downstairs, the argument about sluice gates had dissolved into singing—someone had started one of the old tunes, one of the ones Marta carried, and others were picking it up uncertainly, learning it the way children learn, by repetition and error and joy.

"In the house," Ada said finally, "when we were cranking the first gate. Your hands next to mine on the handle." They watched their own reflection ghosted in the wardrobe's polished door, overlaid on the pencil map like a second drawing. "That was the first time we'd done fieldwork together since I was nine. Since the Orkney dig. Do you remember? You let me brush the soil off a brooch pin and I was so careful I took an hour."

"You took three hours," Ingrid said. "I timed you. I let you think it was urgent work."

"It *was* urgent work."

"It absolutely was." And then Ingrid did something Ada had no prepared response for: she picked up the stub of pencil that some previous guest had left balanced on the wardrobe's shelf—a carpenter's pencil, flat and black, worn round at the tip—and held it out.

"Your turn," she said.

Ada stared at it. "My turn for what?"

"For the record." Ingrid shifted aside, making room in front of the panel, making room the way she'd made room at the gate crank, without ceremony, without speech, simply moving so that there was space for two. "I left a map of the river as it was imprisoned. Someone should leave a map of it as it is. As it's going to be." She paused. "And I'm not going to write both our names myself. That's not how signatures work."

The pencil was warm from her hand. Ada took it.

For a long moment they just knelt there, side by side, looking at the faint old lines. Then Ada leaned in and began to draw.

They drew the way they'd learned to draw in trenches and middens and drowned churches: slowly, honestly, without embellishment. The river's course as it ran now, unmanaged, finding its own braids through the silted fields. The new pools. The old villages rising onto their higher ground, marked not with dots but with small circles—occupied, inhabited, alive. Where Ingrid's map had shown the fens as a barrier, Ada drew the causeway Marta's song had opened. Where the old map ended at Headwaters House, Ada drew nothing at all—just left the paper blank past the spring, because the spring belonged to itself now and maps were a form of ownership and some things should go unmapped.

"That's good," Ingrid murmured, when the lines were done. "That's very good. You've got—" She stopped, and huffed a laugh through her nose. "You've got my hand. Did you know that? The same pressure. The same little hook on the north-pointing arrows."

"I did know that," Ada said. "I've hated it for years."

"And now?"

Ada considered. The lamp flickered. Downstairs the song found its stride, a dozen voices stumbling together toward the chorus.

"Now I think it's the most useful thing I inherited."

Then they wrote the names. INGRID ERIKSEN, beneath her old capitals, dated. And below it, in their own hand, which was also her hand, which was also nobody's hand but their own: ADA ERIKSEN.

Ada sat back on their heels and looked at the two names together on the wardrobe panel, and something in their chest that had been clenched since the cave, since the photograph, since the midden and the ledger and the long cold miles upstream, finally—finally—let go. Not with drama. With a sound like a knot slipping.

Ingrid was looking at the names too. And then she started to laugh.

It was a small laugh at first, disbelieving, escaping sideways like water finding a crack. Then it grew. Ada turned to stare at her—at their mother, the celebrated Dr. Eriksen, kneeling on the floor of a cheap inn room in a rumpled shirt, laughing helplessly at a piece of furniture—and the absurdity of it hit them like the first warm day after winter, and they laughed too.

"What?" Ada managed. "What's funny?"

"The pencil!" Ingrid gasped. "All of it—the charter, the archive, Vane and his *order*, the whole edifice of a century—and what actually brought it down was a bell, a song, and a *carpenter's pencil*." She wiped her eyes. "He built a fortress against armies and forgot about stationery."

"He forgot about wardrobes."

"He forgot about *people*." Ingrid's laughter softened into something else, something that shone. "He forgot that people write on walls. They've always written on walls. Cave walls, prison walls, wardrobe walls. You can't drown that. Believe me, the Trust tried for a hundred years."

"We are," Ada said, "the least dramatic revolutionaries in history."

"Speak for yourself. I was imprisoned in a manor."

"You were *translating* in a manor."

"I was resisting through footnotes," Ingrid said with dignity, "which is the most archaeologist form of resistance ever devised," and Ada laughed again, really laughed, head tipped back, and realized somewhere in the middle of it that this was the first time—they were both aware of it, neither said it—the first time in the entire span of this story, perhaps in the entire span of their shared life, that the two of them had laughed together. Not politely. Not at a conference dinner. Together, on the floor, at nothing, at everything, at a pencil.

The sound of it filled the small room and went out the window into the blue evening, and downstairs the song swelled as if it had heard.

* * *

Later—much later, after bread and stew and a glass of the innkeeper's cider drunk in the public room while the salvage books were shown around like newborns—they climbed back up to room four. The lamp had burned low. The wildflowers on the sill had closed for the night.

Ingrid sat on the bed's edge, unlacing her boots, and Ada stood at the window watching the mill race silver under the moon. Tomorrow they would go downstream. There were restitution tables waiting, objects to match to descendants, a valley full of grandparents' names being spoken aloud in market squares. There was a new charter being drafted in careful, suspicious, hopeful language, and witnesses to find for it.

But tonight—

"Ada." Ingrid's voice, quiet behind them. "The cave. The one where you found the ledger page. On the coast."

Ada turned. "What about it?"

"When the water's right. When the tide allows." Ingrid looked at them across the lamplit room, and her expression was the one she wore over a promising trench edge: cautious, hungry, unwilling to presume. "I'd like to see it. If you'd show me. Where it started."

Ada looked back at her, and thought of the cave mouth opening in the falling tide like a held breath released; thought of the rusted lantern with the Trust crest; thought of the last page of the ledger, which they had carried all this way and kept, meaning to return it to the sea.

"The tide keeps its own hours," Ada said. "We'll have to ask it nicely."

Ingrid smiled. "In this valley," she said, "that's how everything worth doing gets done."

Outside, far off toward the coast, the sea was already turning over in its sleep, beginning the long slow exhale that would, in three days' time, lay the sand bare and open the cliff's dark door one more time—for whoever the water chose next.
