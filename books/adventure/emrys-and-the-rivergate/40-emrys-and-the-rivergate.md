## 40. Emrys and the Rivergate

The morning the river got its name back, Ada was not there to see it.

They heard about it later, the way they heard about most things now—secondhand, carried upriver on someone's voice, arriving slightly changed like water through gravel. Bryn had poled a party of surveyors past the old weir at dawn, and one of them, a young woman with mud on her boots and no crest on her collar, had said it plainly: *We should call it what it was called.* And no one had argued. That was the miracle of it. No argument. Just a silence in the boat, and then Marta's song, hummed low by the ferry-woman herself, the way her grandmother had hummed it over wash-water and grave-dirt, the way every drowned-village child had once learned it without knowing they were learning a map.

*Emrys runs where Emrys wills,*

*through Merrow's bell and Gallowford's mills,*

*and where the spring comes up to sing,*

*no charter holds, no owner's ring.*

Ada had heard the song so many times now—in Marta's cracked alto at the sluice-gates, in children's voices skipping rope along the new banks—that it had worn a groove in them, the way water wears stone. But hearing that the river itself would carry the name again, on maps printed in Gallowford and signed by a Trust that no longer owned anything but its own apology—that did something to their chest that they still hadn't found a word for. They suspected there wasn't one. Some things you don't catalogue.

Three years now since the flood. Three years since Headwaters House drank itself.

* * *

The path to the source was not a path anymore, which was exactly right.

It was a deer trail, mostly, threading through alder and willow-herb, soft underfoot with three years of leaf-fall and the droppings of animals who had come back as if summoned. Ada walked it slowly, the way Ingrid had taught them—not the way she'd taught her students, brisk and transactional, but the way she'd taught them in the last two years, when she'd finally learned to teach her own child instead of an audience. *Read the ground before you trust it,* she'd say. *The ground is always telling you something. It's people who lie.*

The ground here told a story of healing. The scars from the dredges were gone under moss. The warden patrol-stones had been pulled up and now lined garden beds in Gallowford, a joke Bryn claimed credit for though everyone knew it had been the wardens themselves, the ones who'd heard the bell and hesitated, carrying away the markers of their old obedience one evening like men clearing a table after a funeral.

Ada stopped where the alders thinned and let the sound arrive before the sight, the way they'd learned to. Water. Not the managed, muffled murmur the river had made in the old days—channeled, culverted, apologetic—but a full-throated conversation: riffle and pool, splash and seep, the whole grammar of an unbound stream.

Then the trees opened, and there it was.

The source.

It didn't look like much. It never had, even in the photographs Ingrid had smuggled out, even in the desperate charcoal sketches Ada's mother had made in room four of the Gallowford inn while the Trust watched her translate their founding crime. A pool, perhaps ten paces across, fed by springs that rose through pale sand in slow, silver ropes. The water came up out of the earth the way breath comes up out of sleep—unhurried, certain, needing no permission. Around it, where the manor's terraced gardens had been ripped out root and brick, wildflowers had staged their patient revolution: meadowsweet and purple loosestrife, ragged robin nodding at the margin, and in the shallows water-crowfoot spreading its white stars like a signature across the current.

Where the house had stood, there was almost nothing. That was the part visitors never believed. Three floors of archive, a century of sealed doors and humming machinery, Vane's elegant wrongness built directly over the river's emergence—and the flood had taken all of it down to foundation stones, and the foundations themselves were sinking year by year into the soft ground, going back under, the way everything stolen eventually went back under if you gave the water long enough. Ada could still find the outline if they looked: a rectangle of older grass, a corner of dressed granite furred with lichen. The rest was flowers.

They sat down on a flat stone at the pool's edge—the same stone, they were fairly sure, where Ingrid had sat during those first clandestine visits, taking readings she pretended were for a paper she'd never publish. The stone was warm. The sun was high enough to burn off the valley mist, and the light came down through the alder leaves in moving coins that scattered across the sandy bottom, where the spring-ropes pulsed and swayed like the hair of something sleeping just below.

Ada did what they had come to do, which was nothing.

That was harder than it sounded. For years—for their whole life, really—they had been a person who did things with their hands when the feelings got too large: digging, cataloguing, packing, walking. Idle hands meant open grief, and open grief meant drowning. But somewhere between the fen tower and the second gate, between Vane's gun and Marta's song, Ada had learned that stillness was not surrender. Stillness was how you listened. The river had taught them that, or maybe the grief had, or maybe they were the same lesson wearing different clothes.

So they let the silence hold.

It held. It held the way the pool held the sky—completely, without effort. There were birds, yes, and the water's voice, and far downstream the faint iron knock of Bryn's ferry against its mooring post, but none of it broke the silence; it was all part of the silence, the way notes are part of music. Ada breathed. The spring breathed. Somewhere under the sand, water that had fallen as rain on hills Ada would never see was gathering itself for the long journey down through the valley—past the restored weir, past Old Merrow's steeple standing clear of the water now like a finger pointed at heaven, past Gallowford where the mill-wheels turned again but turned openly, metered publicly, owned by no one and tended by everyone.

Their mother had been dead two years this autumn. It still caught Ada sideways sometimes—a face in a crowd, a particular slant of handwriting—and each time it hurt less like a wound and more like weather, something that moved through and left the air washed clean behind it. Ingrid had died the way she had lived the last decade of her life: deliberately, honestly, with her boots on. She'd spent her final years doing restitution work, matching the flood-returned artifacts to descendants, and she'd been good at it in a way she'd never been good at being a mother until the end, when she'd finally understood that both jobs were the same job: *paying attention to what people have lost, and helping them hold it.*

Her last field notebook was in Ada's pack. Ada took it out now, more from habit than need—they knew every page—and turned to the final entry, written in a hand gone thin but steady:

*The source doesn't want to be found. It wants to be understood. I had it backwards my whole life. You don't arrive at a spring and take its measure. You sit down and let it measure you.*

Below that, in fresher ink, Ada's own hand:

*Measured. Found adequate. —A.E.*

They smiled at it. They still smiled at it, every time. Somewhere Marta was laughing too, wherever old women went when their songs outlived them, because Marta had read that entry over Ada's shoulder once and cackled until she had to sit down on a crate, and said, *Your mother would have hated how much she loved that.*

* * *

Footsteps on the trail. Light ones, deliberate, the gait of someone raised on riverbanks. Ada didn't turn. Only a handful of people knew this place well enough to walk it quietly, and only one of them would come looking today.

Bryn Halvard emerged from the alders with her poles over her shoulder and her gray braid silvered further than last season, squinting against the light like a woman perpetually arguing with the sun. She was sixty-eight and poling stronger than men half her age, and she had refused, with spectacular profanity, every offer to retire.

"Heard you were up here," she said. "Saw your note at the ferry."

"Didn't expect you to climb."

"I climb when I want." She lowered herself onto the stone beside Ada with a grunt that was purely theatrical, and for a while neither of them spoke. The pool did its work on her too; Ada watched her shoulders come down, watched the ferry-woman's constant calculating gaze—the one that had watched too carefully from the very first day at the barter dock—go soft and unfixed.

"Merrow marker stone's finished," Bryn said at last. "Set it yesterday. Granite, from the old churchyard wall. Carver put the names—all forty-one. Took him two years and he cried the whole time, apparently. Good sign, in a carver."

"You'll pole people out for the dedication?"

"Full boat. Standing room." She picked a stem of meadowsweet and twirled it. "My grandmother used to say the village would come back someday. Everyone thought she meant ghosts." She snorted. "She meant grandchildren. She meant us. She just knew it would take the water doing what water does."

Ada nodded. Downstream, the returning villages weren't villages yet—higher ground settled, gardens dug, a chapel roofed at New Merrow, schoolhouse frames going up at the third site whose name Ada had helped recover from a ledger page and a lullaby. But there were lights in windows at night now, where for a hundred years there had been only flood-meadow and denial. People remembered. That had been the whole secret of it, the thing Vane had never understood even as he drowned of it: power ran on consent, and consent ran on memory, and memory, unlike archives, could not be kept in a dry room. It lived in songs. In bells. In grandmothers' grumbles and children's games and the names old people gave the bends of a river that the maps refused to print.

"They're printing the new charts," Bryn said. "Gallowford press. First run next month." She glanced sideways, and there was something careful in it, something almost shy, which on Bryn Halvard was rarer than snow in July. "They've asked who should write the introduction. The historical note. About the name."

Ada looked at the pool. The spring-ropes rose and swayed. A dragonfly stitched the surface tension and lifted away.

"They asked you," Bryn said. "Obviously. I told them you'd say no."

Ada laughed—short, surprised. "Did you."

"Told them you'd say no and then do it anyway, because that's what you do. Refuse the throne, sweep the floor." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and slung her poles. "I'll tell them yes, then. Write it short. You're better at short than you pretend."

She started down the trail, then paused, half-turned, backlit by the alder-shade.

"Marta's tune," she said. "The dedication. We'll sing the last verse at the stone. The one about the spring." Her voice did something complicated on the word *spring*. "You'll stand with us?"

"I'll stand with you," Ada said.

Bryn nodded once, satisfied, and was gone, her footsteps fading into birdsong, and the silence folded closed behind her like water over a stone.

* * *

Ada stayed until the light went long and gold.

They took out the little notebook they carried now—not Ingrid's, their own, the one that was slowly becoming the book people kept asking them to write, the book about water and songs and the people who kept both. They wrote sitting on the warm stone with the pool at their knee, and the words came easily, which after a lifetime of difficult words felt like being forgiven in advance.

*Field notes, final page of the season.*

*The source is unremarkable, which is the first thing to understand about it. No treasure. No monument. A pool, some sand, water rising because that is what water does when no one stops it. Everything we were promised was here—gold, glory, inheritance—was never here. What was here was evidence, and evidence is only treasure if someone has been trying to bury it.*

*Second thing: the burial was the map. Follow what powerful people hide and you will find, at the bottom of it, not their secrets but everyone else's. The Trust kept rooms of other people's letters, other people's shoes, other people's Bibles. They thought ownership was the same as possession. The flood taught them—or taught the ones willing to learn—that some things can only be held by letting them go.*

*Third thing, and I keep this page for whoever finds this notebook after me, the way a ledger page once waited for me under the tide:*

*Some inheritances you refuse. The deed, the name on the charter, the seat at the table where other people's lives get divided up—refuse all of it. Put it down like something hot. Your hands are for other work.*

*But some inheritances you become. A song. A way of reading ground. A stubbornness about who gets remembered. These don't come with papers. They come with practice. You inherit them by doing them, and you pass them on the same way, and no flood and no founder and no polite man with money can take them, because they were never property. They were always just water, finding its way down.*

Ada capped the pen. The pool lay still in the last light, holding the first stars. Down in the sand, the springs went on rising—patient, ancient, unowned—feeding the river that ran now through the valley under its old name, singing Marta's verse to every bank it passed:

*and where the spring comes up to sing,*

*no charter holds, no owner's ring.*

Ada Eriksen sat at the source of the Emrys until dark came down gentle as silt, and let the silence hold, and the silence held, and it was enough—it had always been enough—and tomorrow there would be a dedication, and a song, and a boat full of standing people, and the long bright work of remembering, which is the only inheritance worth the name.
