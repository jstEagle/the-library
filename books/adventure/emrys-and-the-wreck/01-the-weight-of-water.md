## 1. The Weight of Water

The rain began an hour before dawn, and Odile Blackwood counted it the way other people counted money.

Not in inches—nobody out here measured anything in inches—but in weight. Weight on the wings at takeoff. Weight in the fuel she'd have to burn climbing over the ridge at Puerto Cielo if the cloud deck sat low. Weight in the crates of penicillin and quinine lashed into her cargo bay, each one stamped with the ministry's seal and none of them paid for yet, not really, because nothing was ever paid for until the ledger said so, and the ledger lived in a locked drawer in Harrow Voss's office three hundred miles downriver, where it grew fat and patient as a spider.

She stood under the corrugated awning of the fuel shed at San Tomás airstrip and watched the water come down in ropes.

"Bad day to fly," said Mateo, the strip keeper, who said this every morning regardless of weather, the way some men said good morning.

"Every day's a bad day to fly," Odile said. "That's why they pay me."

Nobody paid her much. That was the trouble.

Her father's plane—a 1958 single-engine bush hauler with a tail number he'd painted himself and a compass rose stenciled on the cowling that had faded from red to the color of old blood—sat on the wet apron with its tanks full and its belly heavy. Forty gallons of avgas. She knew the number the way she knew her own pulse, because every gallon was a line item, and every line item rolled uphill into the debt, and the debt was the only inheritance her father had left her that anyone would honor.

He'd left her the plane too. Technically. The plane that wasn't hers, that couldn't be hers, that slept every night under a lien like a dog chained to a stake. He'd left her his hands, though, and those were hers outright: hands that could feather a dying engine onto a sandbar no wider than a canoe, hands that could nurse a carburetor through monsoon damp with baling wire and prayer, hands that currently smelled of avgas and were steady, which was more than could be said for the rest of her.

"You eat?" Mateo asked.

"I'll eat at Caño Frio."

"Caño Frio's four hours."

"Then I'll be hungry for four hours." She pulled her flight jacket off the nail by the shed door. It was her father's jacket too, everything was her father's, and the left cuff was fraying where she worried it between her fingers when she thought nobody was looking. "Medicine doesn't keep. Neither do I."

Mateo shrugged and went back inside, and Odile walked the plane alone in the rain, which was how she preferred to do most things.

Pre-flight was a liturgy. She'd learned it at eleven years old, standing on a milk crate while her father called out the checklist in his gravel voice, and she still heard that voice sometimes—*fuel quantity, check; control surfaces, free and correct*—though the voice had been dead six years now and the debt it left behind was very much alive. She drained the sumps. Water in the fuel sample, just a trace, the first small lie the morning told her. She drained again. Clear. She kicked the tires—not superstition, she told herself, just physics you could feel through your boot—and ran her palm along the leading edge of the left wing where a repair patch from two seasons ago still rode rougher than she liked.

Everything rode rougher than she liked. That was the job.

She climbed in, closed the canopy against the rain drumming on the aluminum overhead—it sounded like applause, or like something trying to get in—and ran up the engine while the whole airframe shivered around her like a living thing waking badly. The radio crackled its morning list of nothing: a fishing boat lost its dinghy near the delta, a missionary station wanted word passed about a measles quarantine, a voice reading prices for rubber and copra as if any of it mattered to the sky.

"San Tomás traffic, Bush Queen Four-Niner-Bravo, departing runway one-seven, remaining in the pattern briefly, then south along the river."

No one answered. No one ever answered. She took the runway anyway.

Takeoff in rain was a negotiation. The strip at San Tomás was laterite clay, red as a wound when dry, and in the wet season it drank water all night and gave it back grudgingly, and the wheels came off the ground heavier than they should have, and for three seconds—the same three seconds every time, she could set a watch by them—Odile did not know whether the plane would fly.

Then it flew. It always flew. And the three seconds folded themselves away somewhere behind her sternum with all the others, and she banked south over the river, and the world below her turned green and drowned and indifferent.

* * *

From the air, the interior made sense. That was the thing nobody understood who hadn't flown it. On the ground the rainforest was chaos, mud and rot and heat and insects, a place designed to unmake you cell by cell. But from eight hundred feet it resolved into pattern: the river braiding and rebraiding like something thinking, the oxbow lakes lying in their scars, the ridgelines running their long spines north to south, the clearings of the settlements opening like small held breaths. Her father had taught her to read it. *The land tells you where it wants the runway,* he used to say. *Your job is just to listen.*

She listened. She also counted.

Fuel burn at cruise: eleven gallons an hour, twelve if she fought the headwind coming up the Caño Frio reach. Legs to the river settlements: five, plus the mail drop at the mission if the strip there wasn't underwater. Reserve: twenty gallons, non-negotiable, the one discipline she never broke even in the years when breaking it might have meant eating. Total available after reserves: enough. Barely. Enough was a word she had built her whole life inside of, the way a snail builds its shell, and she carried it everywhere and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything.

The debt itself she tried not to look at directly, the way you don't look directly at the sun or at certain kinds of grief. Six thousand, four hundred and ten dollars as of the last statement, which Voss had delivered personally, which was a message in itself. Harrow Voss did not deliver statements personally. Harrow Voss sent clerks, sent letters, sent the particular silence of a man who wanted you to come to him. But last month he had stood on the apron at Puerto Cielo in his pale linen suit that never seemed to wrinkle or sweat, holding the paper out to her with two fingers, and he had smiled with all the warmth of a ledger line, and he had said, *After the wet season, Miss Blackwood. Terms are terms. Your father was a man of his word; I trust the daughter is too.*

She had taken the paper without touching his hand. She had thanked him. She hated herself for thanking him, and she had flown home and added the humiliation to the total, though there was no column for it.

Six thousand dollars. Eleven gallons an hour. She did the arithmetic the way other people prayed.

Caño Frio came up on her left forty minutes out, a scatter of tin roofs and drying racks on a bluff above a brown tributary, its airstrip a mowed scar in the green. She keyed the radio. "Caño Frio, Caño Frio, Four-Niner-Bravo, inbound from the north, medicine and mail, estimate overfield in six minutes. How's your strip?"

Static. Then a woman's voice, young, breathless: *"Four-Niner-Bravo, this is Caño Frio—strip is—we've got water on the low end, maybe fifty meters, but the top half is dry, we think, we raked it this morning—"*

"We think" was not a phrase that belonged anywhere near an airstrip. Odile looked at the clouds stacking up over the eastern ridge, gray going bruise-colored, and at the river below already swollen to the roots of its trees, and she made the calculation she always made: the medicine in her hold was for the fever outbreak the mission had radioed about three days ago, two children critical, and the alternative to landing at Caño Frio was backtracking ninety minutes to San Tomás and losing a day, and a day was something the children might not have.

"Fifty meters of water on the low end," she repeated. "Describe it. Standing or moving?"

*"Standing. Kind of. There's a current across it where the creek jumps the ditch—"*

A creek crossing the strip. A current. In the wet season, a current across laterite clay meant the surface underneath was already soup, and soup did not care how good your short-field technique was.

"Tell everyone to stay off the low end," Odile said. "I'm coming in over the top of it. If I go in the water, somebody swim fast."

The girl laughed, high and frightened, and Odile grinned into the static because that was what you did, that was the armor, you made the joke so that fear had nowhere to sit down.

* * *

The approach to Caño Frio ran downhill along the river bend, which meant she came in with the water at her back and the bluff ahead, and the wind, what little there was between storm cells, came off the ridge in gusts that shoved the tail around like a drunk friend. She dropped the flaps two notches. Slowed to sixty-five knots. The trees rose to meet her, close enough on both sides that she could see individual leaves shudder in her propwash, and the strip appeared exactly where the land had promised it would, a brown ribbon with its far end drowned in a sheet of dull silver that reflected the bruised sky.

Fifty meters of water. From the air it looked smaller than it sounded. Everything did.

She flared over the fence line, held the nose up, let the mains kiss down on the dry half with a thump she felt in her teeth, and stood on the brakes, and that was when the strip told her its second lie.

The dry half was not dry. It was merely less wet. The wheels broke through a skin of crust into clay the consistency of cold porridge, and the plane slowed, slowed, and then stopped slowing, and began to hydroplane instead—skating, weightless in the wrong way, steering gone, the drowned end of the strip rushing toward her windshield at a speed that was no longer flying and not yet crashing but some third thing with teeth.

Time went strange. Later she would remember it in fragments: the silver sheet filling the glass. Her hands doing something without her—full aft elevator, a stab of power, instinct older than thought, the stick-and-rudder equivalent of screaming. The propeller biting spray. The left wing dropping as one wheel found the submerged ditch and the other didn't, the world tilting, the treetops swinging across her vision—

—and then the plane slammed broadside into the flooded section with a concussion that rang the airframe like a bell, plowed a bow wave of brown water over the cowling, and stopped.

Stopped. Actually stopped. Engine running. Propeller turning. Rain falling on the canopy with idiotic gentleness, as if nothing whatsoever had happened.

Odile sat with her hands welded to the controls and discovered she was breathing in fast shallow pulls, and that her heart was trying to exit through her throat, and that the compass on the instrument panel—her father's compass, bolted there before she was born, its brass case worn bright by two generations of thumbs—was swinging slowly, lazily, coming to rest pointing the way it had always pointed, as if to say: *you're still here.*

"Four-Niner-Bravo," she said into the radio, and her voice came out level, which astonished her. "I'm down. I'm down at Caño Frio. Somebody bring a rope."

* * *

They brought a rope, and thirty men, and a team of oxen that had to walk an hour from a finca upriver, and it took them until mid-afternoon to drag the plane out of the flood and up onto the high end of the strip, where it sat listing slightly, dripping, its belly caked in red clay like something born rather than built.

By then Odile had already done the things that needed doing. She'd unloaded the medicine and run it up to the mission herself, wading calf-deep, and watched the nurse's face change when she saw the quantity, and been thanked in a way that lodged somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, uncomfortable, warm. She'd checked the engine for ingestion damage—clean, miraculously, the prop tips nicked but true. She'd eaten the plate of rice and beans the young radio operator pressed on her, tasting almost nothing, and answered questions about the outside world with the few coins of gossip she carried, because gossip was part of the freight too, part of what kept the settlements tethered to one another, and she was the only tether some of them had.

And all afternoon, beneath the competence, beneath the jokes and the steady hands and the careful answers, she ran the numbers.

A new propeller: two hundred dollars, minimum, if she could even get one shipped upriver before the rains closed the passes. Landing gear inspection: another hundred, if nothing was bent, and something was bent, she could feel it in the way the plane sat. Days lost: two, maybe three, and days lost were gallons burned at zero progress, and gallons were dollars, and dollars were the debt, and the debt was due after the wet season, and the wet season had only begun.

One landing. One routine landing, the kind she'd made a thousand times, the kind she could make asleep. And it had nearly killed her and would cost her a tenth of what she owed.

That was the arithmetic of her life, laid bare in a flooded strip: she could do everything right, everything, and still drown an inch at a time.

Evening came down purple over the river. The settlement fed her again, and offered her a hammock on the mission porch, and she took it because refusing would have been rude and because her body had quietly stopped taking orders anyway. But sleep didn't come. She lay listening to the rain start again on the tin roof, soft at first, then steady, then a roar, the monsoon settling in for the night shift, and she thought about the ledger in Voss's drawer, growing fatter in the dark. She thought about her father, who had signed that note with the same hand that had taught hers on a milk crate, and she still didn't know, after six years, whether to love him or curse him, so she did both, in the usual proportions.

The compass hung on its mount in the dark cabin across the yard, pointing north through the wall, through the rain, through everything. North, where the river narrowed. North, where the drowned valley of Kestrel Reach lay under its new lake, the one the government had made and didn't talk about. She'd flown around it for two years, the way you walk around a grave.

Tomorrow she'd fly south, light, careful, counting gallons. Tomorrow she'd be competent again. Tomorrow the armor would go back on and fit perfectly, the way it always did, and no one—not Mateo, not the mission nurse, not the oxen drivers—would ever guess how tired she was, or how afraid, or how completely alone she intended to remain, because alone was the one thing in her life that had never cost her anything.

She was almost asleep when the radio operator knocked gently on the post's door and held out a message flimsy, apologetic, rain-spotted.

"Not for me," Odile said.

"For you, señora. Relay from Puerto Cielo." The boy wouldn't meet her eyes. "It's marked urgent. It's from the government."

Odile sat up in the hammock. Outside, the rain kept falling on the drowned strip, on the wounded plane, on the whole soaked breathing dark of the interior, and somewhere north of all that water, Kestrel Reach waited with whatever it was the government suddenly needed her to carry into it.
