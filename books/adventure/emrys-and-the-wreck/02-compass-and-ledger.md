## 2. Compass and Ledger

The trading post at Serrano Bend sat on a bluff above the river like something the water hadn't managed to drown yet, and Odile Blackwood climbed its stairs with her boots leaving wet prints on the boards and her shoulders already aching from the day's loading. Behind her, tied off at the post's rickety dock, her father's plane sat in the last of the light—a 1958 de Havilland Otter with a patched cowling and a tail number he'd painted himself, back when paint was something a man did for love instead of obligation. She didn't look at it. Looking at it always started the arithmetic, and the arithmetic never came out kind.

The post smelled the way it always smelled: kerosene, cured tobacco, river mud tracked in and dried to a pale crust, and underneath it all the sweet-rot of overripe bananas nobody would ever buy. A ceiling fan turned with the enthusiasm of a dying man. The proprietor, old César, was asleep in his chair behind the counter with a newspaper over his face, and Odile stepped around the display of machetes and didn't wake him. She'd learned a long time ago that the best transactions were the ones nobody witnessed.

She was three items into her list—quinine, spark plugs, the good rope, not the rope that frayed—when the door at the back of the store opened, and the temperature of the room changed.

It didn't actually change. But that was how it felt, every time. Harrow Voss had a way of entering a space that made the air itself seem to take his side.

"Miss Blackwood." His voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because everyone else always lowered theirs first. "I hoped it would be you. The Otter on the dock—I'd know that airframe anywhere. Your father flew it like it was part of his hands."

"He did," Odile said, not turning around. She set the quinine on the counter and kept her eyes on César's inventory, the faded chalkboard with its prices two seasons out of date. "He flew everything like that."

"And yet here you are, still flying it, still paying for the privilege." Voss came around the counter's end, and she had to look at him then, because not looking was a courtesy she refused to extend. He was a narrow man, dry as kindling, with a face like a folded map—creases everywhere, all of them leading somewhere you didn't want to go. He wore a linen suit that the humidity had given up on hours ago, and he held his ledger the way a priest holds a breviary. "Do you know what I admire about you, Odile? Your father was a dreamer. You're an accountant. That's a compliment, in my trade."

"It's not your trade I'm worried about being complimented by."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I imagine it isn't."

He laid the ledger on the counter between them, opened it to a page she knew by heart and had never once needed to see. The figures were written in his small, precise hand, each column ruled straight as a runway. Principal. Interest, compounding quarterly at a rate that would have been illegal anywhere the law bothered to reach this far inland. Late fees, itemized with a delicacy that was almost tender. At the bottom, the total, and beneath the total, in the same patient hand, a single line:

*Due in full at the close of the wet season.*

"You've been making your payments," Voss said. "Regular as rain. I want you to know I notice these things. Most of my clients, I have to notice them. You I only have to observe." He turned the ledger slightly, so it faced her, an offering or an accusation. "But observation is not satisfaction. You're carrying the principal like a man bailing a boat with a teacup. Proud work. Slow work. And the wet season, as you know, ends when it ends—not when it's convenient."

"I know when the wet season ends," Odile said. "I fly in it."

"Yes. That's rather the problem, isn't it." He smiled. It was a small smile, economical, the smile of a man who budgeted his expressions the way she budgeted fuel. "Every hour you're in the air is an hour you're not earning on the ground. Every gallon of fuel is a gallon of debt you didn't pay. You're working yourself to death at a loss, Miss Blackwood, and the tragedy of it—the genuine, human tragedy—is that you're very good at it. You could have been mediocre and been free by now. Mediocre people settle. They walk away from obligations. You never walk away from anything. It's your father in you."

"Don't," she said, and the word came out flatter than she intended, and Voss heard the flatness and catalogued it somewhere behind his folded face.

"My apologies." He didn't sound sorry. He closed the ledger with a soft clap, like a trap that had decided, for now, not to spring. "I'm not here to threaten you. Threats are for people who might run. Where would you run? Everything you have in the world is tied to that dock, and half of it is already mine on paper. I'm here to remind you. There's a difference, and the difference is that a threat expires and a reminder doesn't. The note comes due after the wet season. That's four months, give or take what the sky decides. I'd hate to see the season decide for you."

Odile looked at the ledger's cover—green cloth, worn to gray at the corners, the color of the river in the dry months—and felt the familiar thing move through her, the thing she had no better name for than the cold. It started in her sternum and went outward, the way frost goes through a windshield, and it was not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the specific dread of being *owned*. Not the plane—she could lose the plane, she told herself, and survive it, though she didn't believe it when she said it. It was the other ownership, the kind that got into the ledger of a person. Voss didn't just hold her father's signature. He held her father's *name*, and every day she flew to pay it down, she flew inside a debt that had been signed before she was old enough to read.

Her father had signed it in a hospital, that was the thing nobody said out loud. The last winter, the bad lungs, the oxygen bottles stacked by the bed like cargo he couldn't jettison. Voss had come with the loan papers and the sympathy and the soft voice, and her father—proud, drowning, wanting only to leave her the plane and not the ruin—had signed, and Voss had witnessed it with the same pen, and six weeks later they'd buried a man who died believing he'd saved his daughter and had, in fact, sold her at a fair market rate.

"Four months," Odile said. "I'll have it."

"You believe you will." Voss tucked the ledger under his arm. "That's what I like about you. Belief. It's such useful material. Now—I hear you've been taking charter work. Resupply runs. Anything with wheels or pontoons, you'll fly it. Is that right?"

"I fly what pays."

"Then you'll want to keep your ears open. There are people passing through who charter. Government people." He said the word the way a man says *weather*—as a force of nature, impersonal and not to be argued with. "They pay in advance, and they pay well, and they don't ask their pilots to be careful. I mention it only because a woman in your position can't afford to be selective. Selectivity is a luxury, Miss Blackwood. Like everything else, it has a price, and like everything else, the price is going up."

"I'll keep my ears open," she said, which was not a yes, and they both knew it was not a no, and that ambiguity was the entire currency of their relationship.

Voss inclined his head, a small bow, and went out through the front of the store, and the boards of the porch took his footsteps one at a time, unhurried all the way down, and then the room was just a room again—kerosene, bananas, the dying fan—and Odile discovered she'd been holding her breath and let it out slowly, counting, the way she counted everything.

* * *

César woke when she set the last of her purchases on the counter, and they transacted in the wordless shorthand of two people who'd been doing this for years: he squinted at the items, she held up fingers, he named a number, she named a smaller one, they met in the middle where they always met. He threw in a bag of hard candy for the river kids at the downstream settlements, the way he always did, and didn't put it on the receipt, the way he never did. There were people in the world who understood what things cost and what things were worth, and they were rarely the same people, and César was one of the ones who knew the difference.

"Voss was here an hour before you," César said, in Portuguese-flavored river Spanish, folding her change into her palm and closing her fingers over it like he was pressing a coin into a child's hand for luck. "He sat in that chair." He nodded at the corner, the chair with the good view of the dock. "He watches your plane every time. Like a man watching a horse he's already bet on."

"He doesn't bet," Odile said. "He collects."

"Same thing, if you own the track."

Outside, the light had gone the color of old brass. The river below the bluff ran wide and brown and unhurried, carrying its freight of silt and secrets down toward the coast, and the air had the thick, green, pre-monsoon weight of a season gathering itself. She could smell the rain coming—not today, maybe not this week, but soon, the way you could smell a storm three valleys off if you'd grown up with your nose to the wind the way she had. Four months, Voss had said. Give or take what the sky decides.

She crossed the dock to the Otter and did her evening walkaround, the ritual her father had taught her before she was tall enough to see into the cowling without standing on a crate. Struts. Tires. Fuel caps, both of them, because a man upriver had once lost an engine to a wasp nest in a fuel vent and her father had told the story a hundred times until it became scripture. Control surfaces, free and true. Pitot tube, clear. She worked the checklist with her hands and let her mind go where it went every evening, down the same worn track: the numbers. What she'd earned this week. What she owed. The distance between them, measured in flights, measured in seasons, measured in the slow erosion of a woman's life into a line item in Harrow Voss's green book.

At four months' current pace, she'd cover the interest and a sliver of principal. Not the note. Nowhere near the note. And Voss knew it, which was why he'd come in person, which was why he'd mentioned the government charters. He wasn't reminding her of the debt. He was herding her. A debt collector didn't chase a debtor who was already running in the right direction.

She hated him for it, and then she hated the hating, because it was a luxury too, and she couldn't afford luxuries.

The compass was in the map case, where it always was, and she took it out the way she took it out every night, without deciding to, the way a man might touch a wedding ring he no longer wore. It was a brass-cased liquid compass, old style, the kind with a needle that moved like it was thinking. Her father's initials were scratched inside the lid—*E.B.* for Emrys Blackwood, a name she'd inherited the way she'd inherited everything else—and the glass had a scratch across the north point from a fall neither of them had ever explained, though she'd caught him once, drunk on cane spirit, turning the compass in his hands and looking at that scratch like it was a scar he remembered earning.

The needle swung, settled, steadied. North. It always found north. That was the whole promise of the thing, the only promise it had ever made, and it had never once broken it—not in thirty years of rain and river and the particular magnetism of the interior, where the iron in the hills could lie to a cheaper instrument. Her father used to say a compass didn't tell you where to go. It only told you where you were, and that was harder, and more useful.

*Where you are.* She stood on the dock in the brass light with a dead man's compass in her hand and a living man's ledger in her future, and she asked herself the question she asked herself every night, and answered it the way she always answered it: she was here. On the dock. In the debt. In the armor of her own competence, which kept everything out except the thing it was built to keep out, the thing that lived inside with her, patient as the wet season.

She put the compass back in the map case, and her fingers lingered on the lid a half-second longer than the checklist required, and then she made herself let go.

* * *

She was halfway up the post's stairs with a plate of César's wife's rice and dried fish when the government truck came up the switchback road, white with a blue stripe, raising dust that the evening light turned to gold. Two men in it, city shirts, city shoes, the kind of shoes that had never met mud and weren't looking forward to it. They parked crooked and climbed out with the careful, offended movements of men whose maps had promised them better.

Odile stood in the doorway with her plate going cold and watched them cross the yard toward César, watched one of them unfold a paper, watched César shake his head and point—not at the truck, not at the road.

At her.

The taller of the two men turned, found her in the doorway, and started up the stairs with the paper still in his hand and an expression that had already decided it was her problem now. Behind him, the river ran brown and heavy toward the drowned valley in the interior, toward Kestrel Reach, where a survey team was due to make a radio contact in thirty-six hours that Odile Blackwood did not yet know would never come.

"Miss Blackwood?" the man said. "We're told you're the only pilot who flies the Reach this time of year."
