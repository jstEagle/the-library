## 3. The Survey That Wasn't

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Odile Blackwood would remember later because Tuesdays were when the mail plane came and she had learned to dread what it carried. Bills mostly. Once, a lawyer's notice about her father's estate that she'd read four times before understanding there was nothing left to administer but debt. The envelope this time was heavier than bills. It was cream-colored, stiff-backed, and stamped with the seal of the Department of Water and Interior Resources—a stylized drop of water cupped in a hand, which had always struck her as either tender or threatening depending on how long you looked at it.

She stood at the counter of the trading post with the envelope unopened for a full minute while Marta Ferreira counted out fuel chits behind her and the ceiling fan turned its slow, useless circles.

"You going to open it or frame it?" Marta said without looking up.

"It's government."

"Then definitely open it. Government doesn't write to people it's forgotten about." Marta slid the chits across. "Government writes to people it wants something from."

Odile opened it at the counter, which she regretted immediately, because Harrow Voss was three aisles over pretending to examine a rack of fishing line, and she could feel his attention settle on her like a hand on the back of her neck.

The letter was brief. The Department wished to charter her services—her aircraft, the letter specified, *and pilot*—for a resupply run to Survey Team Kestrel-4, currently operating in the upper reach of the Kestrel Valley. Two flights: one now, one in ten days' time. Cargo would be delivered to the post by department truck. Payment upon completion, at rates nearly double what the medicine runs paid.

Nearly double. She read that figure twice. She thought about the ledger she kept in a oilcloth sleeve under the pilot's seat, the columns of numbers that never moved in the right direction, the total at the bottom that Voss recited to her every few months like a man checking the ripeness of fruit.

"They're paying well," Marta said, reading over her shoulder the way river people did, privacy being a luxury of places with doors.

"They're paying too well."

Marta shrugged. "Or they finally looked around and realized nobody else flies the upper reach in wet season. Take it, Odile. Money's money."

But Odile had grown up on her father's stories, and one of them was about the year the government came through buying right-of-way for the dam road, paying cash prices so generous that men who'd haggled over egg prices their whole lives signed papers without reading them. Generosity from the Department was not generosity. It was a down payment.

Still. She took the charter.

* * *

The cargo arrived Thursday morning in two crates stenciled KESTREL-4 and a third, smaller case with no markings at all except a serial number and a padlock. The driver, a young man with the pale, indoor complexion of someone who worked in an office and hated it, handed her a manifest and wouldn't meet her eyes.

"What's in the locked case?" she asked.

He consulted the manifest as if he hadn't memorized it. "Survey instruments."

"Instruments need a padlock?"

"Everything needs a padlock," he said, and got back in his truck.

She weighed everything on the cargo scale twice, distributed it the way she always did—heavy low and forward, the plane balanced like a bird at rest—and flew north out of the post into air that already tasted of rain though the sky was still blue. The wet season was three weeks off by the almanac and maybe two by the feel of things; the mornings were coming up heavier, the river running brown and high, the birds moving downslope in slow reluctant flocks. Her father had called this weather *the gathering*. He'd said it the way other men talked about an approaching storm front, with respect and a little hunger, because gathering weather meant work for pilots who weren't afraid of it.

Odile wasn't afraid of it. She was afraid of plenty of other things—the ledger, mostly, and the sound of Voss clearing his throat—but not the weather. Weather was honest. Weather told you exactly what it intended to do and then did it.

The Kestrel Reach lay forty minutes north, past the last of the settlements, where the valley narrowed and the river slowed and spread into that wide drowned water the maps still showed as forest. Her father had flown her over it once, years ago, before the flooding finished. He'd been quiet the whole way, which for him was unusual, and when she'd asked what was wrong he'd said, *Nothing's wrong. Just don't ever get used to looking at it.* She hadn't understood then. From altitude the flooded valley was beautiful—a mirror laid among green ridges, dead trees standing silver out of the water like the masts of a fleet sunk in place. It was only later, reading the relocation notices posted in the trading post, that she'd understood what the mirror covered. Three villages. A church. Orchards. All of it bought, cleared, and drowned so the dam downstream could hold a reservoir, and the reservoir could hold power for cities she'd never seen.

*Don't ever get used to looking at it.* She looked anyway. She looked every time.

The survey camp was supposed to be on the east shore, on a shelf of ground above the old flood line—a gravel flat where the department had staged equipment since before the dam, back when it was a construction camp. She found it without trouble, a scatter of khaki tents and aluminum sheds visible from six miles out, and she made her approach the way she made all her approaches, slow and wide and reading the wind off the water.

Nobody came out to meet her.

That was the first wrong thing. In the settlements, a plane overhead brought people out of buildings like ants from a disturbed nest—kids first, always, then everyone else. At a survey camp, even a small one, there should have been someone on the gravel, waving her in, marking the strip. There was no one. There was no smoke from the cook tent's chimney either, and no laundry on the line she could see strung between two poles, and no boat at the little dock that jutted into the drowned timber.

She circled once. Twice. On the third pass she keyed the radio.

"Kestrel-4, Kestrel-4, this is supply aircraft out of the post, inbound with your resupply, do you copy?"

Static. The particular static of a frequency nobody is listening to, which has a different texture than the static of a broken set—she couldn't have explained how she knew that, only that she did, the way you know a house is empty by the sound of your own footsteps.

"Kestrel-4, any station at Kestrel camp, come back."

Nothing. She flew the circuit again, lower this time, two hundred feet, close enough to see details: a generator shed with its door swinging gently in the breeze; a stack of core-sample boxes under a tarp that had half blown off; tire tracks in the gravel leading toward the dock and stopping there, as if a vehicle had driven to the water and been swallowed. A table outside the largest tent held what looked like paperwork weighted down with stones, pages lifting and settling, lifting and settling, patient as gulls.

Her scheduled contact window, according to the charter papers, had opened at noon. It was 12:40. The second window, per the flight plan filed with the department, would be in ten days—but the papers also required her to confirm receipt of cargo with the team leader, a name she'd already forgotten, some initials and a rank. She could land. Or she could go back and report no contact, and let the department sort out its own people.

She thought about the ledger. She thought about double rates, payment on completion, and *completion* meaning cargo delivered into hands.

She thought about the tire tracks ending at the water.

Odile Blackwood had survived her father's death, her father's debts, and eleven years of flying alone into country that didn't care whether she landed or didn't, by following one rule above all others: *when something feels wrong, believe it.* Instinct was the only instrument that never needed calibrating. Every pilot who died in the bush had, somewhere in the last hour, felt the wrongness and overridden it—pressed on into weather, stretched the fuel, trusted the strip that looked too short. She had promised herself she would never be that pilot.

She pulled out of the circuit, climbed south, and put the camp behind her.

* * *

The department office occupied the second floor of the customs building in the port town, up a staircase that smelled of mildew and cigarette ash, behind a door with frosted glass and gold lettering. The man waiting for her was named Ovid Renner, Deputy Liaison for Northern Operations, and he had the smooth, unhurried manner of someone who had never once been rained on unexpectedly.

"You didn't land," he said, before she'd finished closing the door.

"No contact at the camp. I called on the scheduled frequency, both bands. No answer, no one on the ground, no sign of activity." She set the charter papers on his desk. "I'm not delivering cargo to an empty camp. It'll sit in my hangar until someone tells me otherwise."

Renner looked at the papers without touching them. "Team Kestrel-4 has been operating in the reach for five months. They're diligent people. They may simply be in the field."

"For a scheduled contact window? With a supply flight they knew was coming?" She kept her voice level. Years of dealing with Voss had taught her that level was more useful than sharp. "Their cook tent chimney was cold. Their boat was gone. Their truck drove to the dock and stopped. Either they're all out sampling somewhere I couldn't see—and in that terrain, with that gear, on foot—they've left, or something's happened."

"Something," Renner repeated, tasting the word. He leaned back in his chair. Outside the frosted glass, a fan turned its slow circles, same as the trading post's, as if the government bought its fans in bulk. "Miss Blackwood. How long have you been flying the interior?"

"Eleven years."

"And in eleven years, how many times has a survey team missed a contact?"

"I wouldn't know. I've never flown for a survey team."

"Because you keep your head down." He said it without malice, almost approvingly, the way a man might admire good joinery. "I've read your file. Reliable. Unremarkable. You fly your routes, you pay your obligations, you ask nothing and report nothing. That's a rare quality, Miss Blackwood. Most pilots are romantics. They want to matter." He smiled faintly. "You seem to understand that mattering is expensive."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a truck changed gears on the waterfront street.

"The team missed their contact," Renner went on. "That happens. Radios fail. Schedules slip. But protocol requires confirmation, and you're the confirmation. So here is what will happen next. You will return to the reach tomorrow morning. If the camp is still empty, you will land, secure your cargo inside the main shed, and conduct a visual check of the immediate area—not a search, a *check*, fifteen minutes on the ground, no more. Then you will return and file a report. Completion payment applies to the second flight regardless. And there will be a hazard supplement."

"How much of a supplement?"

"Enough." He opened a drawer, took out a form, and began filling it in with a fountain pen, unhurried, the nib scratching. "You should know that the department takes the welfare of its personnel seriously. Very seriously. Which is why the check must be done quietly, by someone unremarkable, rather than loudly, by a rescue apparatus that generates questions. Questions are expensive too, Miss Blackwood. More expensive than hazard pay."

She heard it then, underneath the smoothness—the thing her father had heard in the relocation officers' voices all those years ago. Not menace exactly. Something worse: *procedural indifference*, the machinery of the department turning regardless of what was caught in it.

"If they're hurt out there," she said, "every hour matters. You should be scrambling an actual search."

"If they're hurt out there, we will know more from your fifteen minutes than from a week of helicopters." He signed the form and slid it across the desk along with a pen. "Tomorrow, first light. Weather window closes after that, if you've been watching the sky, and I suspect you have."

She hadn't told him about the weather. Nobody at the post had discussed the gathering with anyone from the department. She picked up the pen.

"And if I say no?" she asked, though they both knew the shape of the question already, because there was a ledger in an oilcloth sleeve under her pilot's seat and a man named Voss who recited its totals, and men like Renner always seemed to know exactly which levers a person had.

Renner looked genuinely puzzled, as if she'd asked what would happen if she declined to obey gravity. "Then the contract goes to another pilot, and your reputation for reliability acquires an asterisk. And I'd have to mention to certain interested parties that you declined department work during a period when, I understand, your financial obligations are... maturing." He folded his hands. "But you won't say no. You're not a romantic."

She signed. Her signature came out steady, which surprised her; she'd expected it to shake.

* * *

That evening she sat in the hangar with the crates and the padlocked case and her father's compass open on her knee, doing the thing she did when she couldn't sleep, which was hold the compass and think about him. The brass was worn bright on the hinge side where his thumb had rested for thirty years. The needle swung and settled, swung and settled, pointing home to a magnetic north that had nothing to do with any of this.

She'd checked the manifest against the crates twice. Medicine, radios, batteries, canned food, rope, spare parts for an outboard—all normal survey resupply, all consistent with five more months in the field. Except the quantities were strange. Twice the food a nine-man team needed for five months. Half the medical kit it should have carried. And the locked case, whatever it held, weighed forty pounds and had been loaded onto her plane by a driver who wouldn't look at her.

She thought about the cold chimney. The pages on the table, lifting and settling, waiting for someone to come read them. The tire tracks running into the water like a sentence that stops mid-word.

Don't ever get used to looking at it.

She closed the compass, latched the hangar, and walked back through the dark to her room above the post, and lay awake listening to the first far-off mutter of thunder out of the northwest, counting the seconds between it and the lightning, and telling herself that tomorrow was just a landing, just fifteen minutes, just cargo and a report and double rates.

The thunder said otherwise. Thunder always did.
