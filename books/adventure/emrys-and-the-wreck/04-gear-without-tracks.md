## 4. Gear Without Tracks

The Kestrel Reach did not look like a drowned valley from the air. It looked like a held breath.

Odile came down out of the morning haze at two thousand feet and throttled back to let the country introduce itself, the way her father had taught her before she was old enough to reach the pedals. *Read it slow, little bird. The river tells you everything if you don't interrupt.* Below her the water spread wide and brown-green between walls of rainforest that climbed the valley sides in unbroken green, canopy locking over canopy like interlaced fingers, so dense in places that the trees seemed to be leaning in to whisper about something they'd seen.

She had flown this stretch twice before—once on a mail contract two years ago, once hauling survey crates for a mining outfit that had gone bust before the second run. Both times the Reach had been a corridor of ordinary water with villages strung along its banks like beads: smoke rising from cook fires, dugouts crossing in lazy diagonals, children waving at the sound of her engine the way children everywhere waved at anything that moved.

Today there was no smoke.

She noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth—not all at once but as a wrongness that kept snagging her attention no matter how many times she ran her tongue over it. She was still forty kilometers upstream of the survey's grid coordinates when she began counting the settlements below. There should have been three along this reach alone. She saw the first one's roofs through a gap in the canopy, tin and thatch, the longhouse shapes unmistakable—and no smoke. No dugouts. No laundry strung between poles. No figures on the landing stage where there was always someone on the landing stage, because a landing stage without watchers was a landing stage nobody used.

The second village was worse. It wasn't just quiet; it was *empty* in a way that read even from eight hundred feet. Doors stood open. A canoe lay half-swamped against its mooring post, shipping water, untended for days by the look of the silt line on its hull. And the water itself—

Odile pulled the yoke left and banked gently to get a better angle, and what she saw made her throat tighten around nothing.

The river had come up. Not monsoon-up, not yet—the real rains were weeks out—but up enough that the brown water had swallowed the low ground and was standing in the tree trunks a good meter deep, flat as poured tea, featureless, moving with a sluggish current that carried whole islands of debris. Branches. Palm fronds. A section of fence. Something pale and rectangular that might have been a door or a tabletop, turning slowly in an eddy like a thing considering whether to surface.

Floodwater this far downstream meant floodwater upstream. Which meant the drowned valley the ministry men had described in their clipped, careful briefing—the one they'd called *seasonal inundation, well within operational parameters*—was already doing its drowning ahead of schedule.

*Well within parameters.* She could hear the liaison's voice, smooth as varnish. Everything about that man had been varnished. His shoes, his briefcase, his smile. The way he'd said *you'll want to keep your radio squelch open, Ms. Blackwood, we'll expect position reports every thirty minutes* — as though the missing team were simply late for dinner rather than eleven days past their last contact.

She keyed the radio anyway. Habit more than hope.

"Kestrel One, Kestrel One, this is Cessna Seven-Three-Bravo inbound from the south, do you copy?"

Static answered her, thick and granular, the sound of air being rubbed together. She tried again on the secondary frequency, then the guard channel, then back to primary, working the set the way her father had taught her—patient, methodical, giving each call time to breathe before abandoning it. Radio in the deep interior was always a gamble. Atmospheric skip, dead batteries, a handset left in the bottom of a canoe. There were a hundred innocent reasons for silence.

But there was a difference, she thought, between a radio that didn't answer and a valley that didn't.

She dropped lower. Five hundred feet. The engine note changed as the air thickened off the water, warmer, wetter, carrying the smell of it up through the vents—mud and rot and green growth, the smell of a forest drinking too fast. Her windshield began to collect a fine misting spray kicked up by her own wheels, and she eased back up to six hundred, unwilling to trade visibility for proximity just yet.

That was when she saw the camp.

It sat on a shelf of higher ground where a tributary creek met the main river—a gravel bar and a terrace of firm earth above the flood line, exactly the kind of site a survey crew would pick, because survey crews everywhere picked the same kind of sites, and Odile had hauled enough of their crates to know their habits the way she knew her own reflection. Two large tents. A mess fly stretched between poles. Stacked supply cases under tarps. An antenna mast leaning at a drunken angle against a tree, guy wires slack.

And no people. No movement. No smoke from the galley fire that should have been burning at seven in the morning in a camp of nine souls, because coffee was the one religion every expedition shared.

"Kestrel One, Kestrel One, Seven-Three-Bravo, I have your camp in sight, say your status."

Nothing. The static had a texture now, almost conversational, as though the valley itself were murmuring back at her in a language made entirely of interference.

She circled. Once, twice, tightening the orbit, bringing the camp around her right wingtip like a lasso. At three hundred feet she could see details: the tarps on the supply cases had been tied down properly, corners tucked, someone competent had done that work. But one of the tent doors gaped open, its flap hanging loose, and the other was zipped halfway and then stopped, as though whoever was inside had been interrupted mid-motion. A pair of boots sat outside the mess fly, placed neatly side by side, toes toward the fire ring.

Boots. Placed neatly. In a camp where nobody answered the radio.

Her hands were steady on the yoke. That surprised her a little. Inside, something had begun to move slowly, heavily, like sediment shifting in still water—the same feeling she'd gotten at fourteen, flying with her father, when they'd crested a ridge and found a downed aircraft in a place no aircraft should have been. The body remembers danger before the mind consents to name it.

*No tracks,* she thought, and then made herself actually look, because the thought had arrived fully formed and she distrusted thoughts that arrived fully formed.

The gravel bar was soft after rain. She could see the flood's high-water mark on it, a clean brown line a hand's width below the terrace edge, and below that line the gravel was scoured and smoothed, printed with nothing. Above the line, on the packed earth of the terrace itself, there should have been trails. Nine people living in a camp for weeks wore paths into the ground the way water wore channels—paths from tents to galley, galley to latrine, latrine to creek. She flew the length of the terrace twice, slow, banking until her wing pointed at the ground, and found only the faint suggestion of older traffic near the landing spot, weeks old, weathered soft, half erased by rain.

Fresh boot prints going nowhere. Old ones fading everywhere else. As if the camp had been occupied, and then everyone had simply stepped off the face of the earth, taking their footprints with them.

*Or the water took them.* The thought arrived cold and reasonable and she hated it. The flood had come up. The flood could have caught them. People drowned in the interior every wet season; it was arithmetic, not mystery. But the boots by the fire argued against it, neat and patient, waiting for feet that hadn't come back. And the gear—she swung wide and looked again at the supply stack—survey teams did not abandon functioning radios, functioning boats, functioning food, unless they'd left in a hurry or been made to leave, and either way they'd have taken the boat. Everyone took the boat. A boat was life out here. You could lose everything else and row home.

There was no boat at the camp. There was also no boat wrecked on any of the snags or sandbars she'd passed in the last twenty kilometers, and she'd been looking, some part of her cataloguing driftwood and debris the whole way in without asking permission.

A cold current moved through her that had nothing to do with altitude.

* * *

She climbed back to a thousand feet and made herself do the professional thing first, because the professional thing was armor and she had learned to wear it like a second skin. Fuel check: four hours twenty to dry tanks, better than planned, the tailwind off the coast having been generous. Position report, due to command per the liaison's instructions, every thirty minutes. She keyed the long-range set and raised the relay at Puerto Oscuro, and the voice that came back was young and bored and very far away.

"Seven-Three-Bravo, Puerto Oscuro, go ahead with your report."

"Puerto Oscuro, Bravo. I'm on station over the Kestrel survey camp, coordinates as filed. Camp is—" She paused, hunting for a word that wasn't *wrong*. "Camp appears abandoned. No personnel visible. No response on primary or secondary. Flood conditions throughout the reach, water up approximately one to two meters over seasonal norms. Villages along the lower reach appear evacuated. Request guidance."

A pause on the other end, long enough that she could picture the young man turning to someone off-microphone, hand cupped over the handset. When the voice returned it had shed some of its boredom.

"Bravo, confirm: no personnel at the camp? Check the immediate area. Vehicles, boats, movement."

"Negative on all. I've made four orbits at low altitude. Nobody home."

Another pause. Then: "Bravo, stand by."

She stood by, circling, and while she waited she let herself look at the landscape the way she'd been avoiding looking at it, straight on, without the buffer of procedure. The valley went on northward beyond the camp, the river widening, the drowned forest standing in rows like congregation members at a service for themselves. Mist was beginning to lift off the water in ropes and columns, slow and deliberate, as though the valley were exhaling. Somewhere far upstream, hidden behind a bend, the dam site would be sitting in its staging of concrete and cranes, the great wound the government had opened in the world and called progress. The drowned villages had been moved for it. She remembered the newspaper stories from years ago—*resettlement completed ahead of schedule*, photographs of smiling families in front of identical new houses on the coast—and she remembered her father reading them aloud in a flat voice and saying nothing else, which from him had said everything.

The water below her carried another island of debris past beneath her shadow. This one had a roof on it. Corrugated tin, bent double, riding the current with a length of bamboo framing still attached.

A roof, floating downstream from somewhere upstream. From the direction of the dam.

"Bravo, Puerto Oscuro." The bored voice was back, but it was wearing its boredom like a coat buttoned wrong. "Command advises the survey team may have relocated upstream to the secondary cache. Weather permitting, proceed to visual inspection of the cache site at your discretion. Maintain scheduled reports."

*At your discretion.* She almost laughed. Two minutes ago the camp had been abandoned; now it was a relocation, tidy as paperwork. That was how institutions metabolized bad news—they digested it into language that wouldn't upset anyone's morning. She'd grown up watching her father refuse to do that with words, and she'd built her whole adult life on the opposite principle: say less, mean exactly what you say, owe nothing. It was why debt collectors' letters made her physically ill. Language that lied was a kind of lien.

"Bravo copies," she said, and did not add what she was thinking, which was that the secondary cache was twelve kilometers further upstream, deeper into the flooded country, closer to the dam, and that every instinct she owned was telling her to turn south and burn fuel until the Reach was a bad memory in her wake.

She turned north instead. Because the instruction said to. Because eleven people were missing and she was the only thing in the sky. Because Harrow Voss held the paper on her airplane and her airplane was the only thing standing between her and a life spent walking, and pilots who wanted to keep flying learned early that you did the job first and felt about it later.

Because, said a smaller voice, underneath the others, quieter than the engine: *because those boots were set side by side, toes to the fire, and somebody meant to come back for them.*

* * *

The secondary cache site told her even less, which was to say it told her more.

It occupied a bluff above a sharp bend, high enough to be clear of any flood, and from two hundred feet she could see that it had been struck—not abandoned, *struck*. Tents gone. Crates gone. Even the fire ring had been scattered, stones rolled apart, ash churned into the dirt as though someone had made an effort to erase the fact that anyone had ever cooked a meal there. Only the latrine trench remained, freshly filled and staked with a marker, because some habits outlast everything, and a single object lying in the open near the bluff edge, white against the dark earth.

She spiraled down to look. It was a chart tube, aluminum, the kind cartographers used, lying on its side with its cap off. Empty.

An empty chart tube at a dismantled camp, on a bluff overlooking the exact stretch of river where the survey's maps ended and the blank space on her own charts began—that was not abandonment. That was a message, or the remains of one. Somebody had stood at the edge of this bluff with the survey's most important papers and either thrown them into the water or given them to someone who was leaving, and either way the gesture had the shape of desperation, or the shape of evidence being moved.

The radio crackled against her hip and she flinched before she could stop herself.

"—Bravo, Puerto Oscuro, your thirty-minute report is overdue, acknowledge."

She checked her watch. It wasn't overdue. It was four minutes early. The discrepancy sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Someone on the other end was nervous, and nervous institutions did not get less dangerous; they got faster.

"Bravo, Puerto Oscuro. Report: cache site inspected. Site has been cleared deliberately. No personnel. One empty chart container observed. Recommend—" She stopped. *Recommend what?* That was the question she'd been circling for an hour, the question the whole valley was holding under its brown water. "Recommend command advise whether any other assets are operating in the reach. Over."

The pause this time was longer than the others. Long enough for the static to fill with the sound of her own engine, her own breathing, the small animal sounds of a woman alone in a machine above a drowned world.

"Bravo, Puerto Oscuro." The voice had changed again, and now she could put a name to what she heard in it, because she'd heard it across debt collectors' desks and ministry counters her whole life: the sound of a man reading words someone else had chosen for him. "Proceed to the dam staging area and make visual contact with the project security detachment. They will coordinate the search from the ground. Acknowledge."

Security. Not rescue. Not the rangers, who knew this water, who had boats and divers and local trackers. Security.

She looked down at the filled-in latrine, the scattered fire ring, the empty chart tube pointing at her like a compass needle. Then north, toward the bend, beyond which the dam waited with whatever it was hiding.

"Acknowledge," Odile Blackwood said, to the static, to the valley, to no one, and pushed the nose north into the mist.
