## 5. Ordered Down

The radio crackled to life while Odile was still climbing out of the reach, the Kestrel's drowned arms falling away beneath her like a hand unclenching. She had the yoke trimmed for the long haul south, the medicine crates lashed in the back, and the whole grey afternoon ahead of her—enough to make the lower station before dark, enough to pretend the last two days had been ordinary work.

*Skyward One-Niner, this is Provincial Command, Kestrel Relay. Come in, One-Niner.*

She knew the voice. Deputy Director Halloran, or one of his interchangeable deputies, the kind of man whose authority arrived secondhand through a handset and never had to smell what it smelled like down here. Odile let the crackle fill the cockpit for a three-count before she keyed the mic.

"One-Niner reading you, Command. Signal's thin, I'm crossing the ridge."

"One-Niner, be advised. Your survey resupply flight is amended. You are directed to return to the Kestrel Reach survey site and conduct a ground assessment of team status. Confirm."

The words arrived one at a time, each one landing like a stone dropped into still water. Odile looked at the fuel gauge, then at the sky, then at the fuel gauge again, as if one of them might have changed its mind.

"Say again, Command." Though she'd heard it perfectly. She always heard it perfectly. That was the curse of being the kind of pilot who listened to weather the way other people listened to sermons.

"Ground assessment. The team missed its third scheduled contact. You are the closest asset. Confirm."

*Asset.* She turned the word over. That was what she was to them—not Odile Blackwood, not the woman who'd flown serum through a squall to San Isidro two seasons back, but an asset, a thing with wings and a fuel bill they didn't pay. Her father had warned her about that once, drunk on cheap rum at the kitchen table, drawing circles in spilled sugar: *The government never asks, Odile. It instructs. And instruction is just ownership with better manners.*

She thought about the debt. She thought about Harrow Voss's smile, the way he'd tapped the note with one finger like a man tapping a fish tank. She thought about how a woman who owed eleven thousand marks to a man with ministry connections did not, in any practical sense, own the air beneath her own wings.

"Command, I have weather building over the reach. Cumulus is going vertical on the west wall, and I'm reading the barometer falling like a stone off a dock. I have maybe—" she did the math she didn't want to do, "—forty minutes of margin before that valley closes. And I am *not* equipped for a ground assessment. I'm a supply pilot. I have no search gear, no comms beyond this radio, and no authority."

"One-Niner, your objection is noted. The direction stands."

"Noted," she repeated. The word tasted like copper. "You're noting that I'm telling you the weather will trap me, and your answer is that the direction stands."

"One-Niner, be advised that failure to comply with a directed flight will be documented in your operating record. Further, be advised that the ministry liaison has requested your continued cooperation be reflected in all relevant administrative reviews."

*All relevant administrative reviews.* She almost laughed. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in the capital, her father's debt and her operating record and Harrow Voss's little empire of paper were all tangled together like fishing line, and Halloran's people had just shown her the hook inside the bait. Cooperate, and the reviews went kindly. Refuse, and every debt, every license, every fragile permission she'd built her life on would be *reviewed*—which was to say, taken apart by men in dry rooms.

The storm wall over the western ridge had gone the color of a bruise. Below her, the reach lay flooded and silent, the drowned forest standing in grey water like a congregation that had been told to wait. Somewhere down there was a camp with no smoke, no tracks, no answers to three days of radio calls. Somewhere down there, possibly, were people. Or had been.

That was the thing she couldn't get around, the splinter in the argument she was building for herself. If she turned south, and the team was alive down there—hurt, stranded, running out of the medicine she happened to have in her crates—then she'd chosen her own skin over theirs, and she'd know it every time she looked in a mirror for the rest of her life. And if she turned south and they were already dead, then the weather would try to make her dead too, and Command would file a report saying a contracted pilot disregarded conditions, and the debt men would pick over her plane like gulls.

Both roads ended with someone else holding her life in a folder. The only question was which folder she could stand to be in.

She thought about her father again, but not the drunk version this time. She thought about him at nineteen, flying mail in a plane with a fabric wing, the story he'd told exactly once: how he'd been ordered down once himself, into a river fog, by a man on a radio who never had to land in it. He'd gone. He'd survived. He'd never forgiven himself for going, and he'd never forgiven himself for the year he didn't, when the San Tomas clinic waited on penicillin that sat in a hangar because the weather was *marginal*. There was no clean choice, he'd told her. There was only the choice you could carry.

"One-Niner," she said, and her voice came out flat and steady, the pilot voice, the armor voice. "I'll comply. I want it logged that I objected on weather grounds, that I advised against this approach, and that I am proceeding under direction, not judgment. Log all of it. Word for word."

"Logged, One-Niner. Command appreciates your cooperation."

*Appreciates.* She keyed off and let the static take the word away, because if she'd kept listening she'd have said something into an open channel that Halloran's people would have enjoyed filing.

She rolled into the turn, and the reach opened up beneath her again, all that drowned green spreading to the horizon like a second country laid over the first. She descended through a layer of mist that beaded the windshield and made the world go soft and wrong, and she hated it, hated the compliance sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, hated the men in dry rooms who'd never once felt a stick go heavy in their hands, hated most of all the small, practical voice inside her that said: *you did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said go down, and you can call it duty if it helps you sleep.*

The runway—if the drowned strip could still be called that—appeared out of the murk in pieces. Half of it was under water now, grey and flat as hammered tin. The other half rose and fell in the gusts, and she could see the water breathing over the low end with every gust that came off the west wall.

She flew the approach the way her father had taught her: slow, deliberate, forgiving the plane nothing. The wind shoved her sideways on short final, corrected, shoved again. The wheels touched on the wet gravel with a sound like tearing paper, and the plane skated, bit, skated, and then held, rolling out past a survey marker half-swallowed by the flood.

She shut down and sat for a moment with her hands still on the yoke, listening to the engine tick and the rain begin—fat, scattered drops at first, hitting the fabric like knuckles.

*You're down,* she told herself. *Now find out why.*

She climbed out into a world gone the color of pewter. The camp was a hundred meters off, maybe less—two collapsed tents, a scatter of crates, the black skeleton of a radio mast leaning over the water like a drowned man's arm. No smoke. No movement. The silence had a texture to it, thick and wet, the silence of a place where something had happened and the landscape had agreed to keep it.

Odile checked the fuel, out of habit, though there was nothing to check it against. She pulled the medicine crate free—serum, antibiotics, the things she'd flown in for a team that hadn't answered in three days—and started toward the camp with the rain building on the back of her neck.

Halfway there, she stopped.

The mud at the treeline held tracks. Boot prints, dozens of them, pressed deep and heading not toward the camp but away from it, toward the water's edge—and then nothing. No prints coming back. The water had taken the rest of the story, or someone had.

Behind her, the first true band of the storm came off the ridge with a sound like a freight train clearing its throat, and the rain went from knuckles to fists.

She stood there in the downpour, drenched in ten seconds, looking at the tracks that led into the flood and the camp that led nowhere, and she understood with a cold, settling certainty that she had not been sent down here to find anyone. She had been sent down here to be *somewhere*—to be the name on the flight log, the pilot on the ground, the asset in the valley when whatever had happened here finished happening.

The wind gusted, and somewhere out over the water, in the grey curtain between the rain and the drowned trees, she heard it—faint, brief, unmistakable.

A voice. Human. Calling out.

* * *

Odile turned toward the sound before her mind finished deciding to, the medicine crate swinging at her side. The rain tore the world into ribbons; every direction looked like every other direction, grey water, grey trees, grey sky. The voice came again—closer, or maybe just carried better on the wind—and it wasn't calling a name. It was calling a warning.

"Get off the strip! The low end's going under!"

She spun. At the edge of the flooded runway, thirty meters out, half-hidden by the standing water and the reed beds swallowing the gravel, stood a man. He was waist-deep where the strip dipped, one hand braced on a survey stake, and he was shouting at her plane like it was a child about to touch a stove.

She'd landed on it. She'd landed on it and shut down and walked away, and now the water was doing what the water did, climbing the gravel the way it had climbed it all afternoon, patient as arithmetic.

She ran. The crate banged against her hip, the rain drove sideways, and by the time she reached the plane the water was over her ankles at the tail and rising. She hauled herself into the cockpit, hands slipping on the wet frame, and got the engine to catch on the third try—a cough, a sputter, then the familiar roar that sounded, just then, like the only sane voice in the valley.

But she didn't take off. She couldn't. The storm wall had come down over the valley mouth while she'd walked away from her own aircraft, and the ceiling that had been a thousand feet was now four hundred and falling, the rain so thick it turned the drowned forest into a smear. Even if she got the wheels up, she'd be flying into a wall of weather with a fuel load calculated for a landing she'd already made.

She was trapped. She understood it the way you understand a door locking behind you—not all at once, but completely.

The man was still out there, still braced against the current at the strip's edge, watching her with an expression she couldn't read at this distance. Not panicked. That was the strange part. Everyone she'd ever pulled out of water had been panicked. This man watched the flood climb over the runway like a man watching a clock.

She shut the engine down—no point burning fuel she couldn't replace—and stood up in the cockpit, rain hammering her shoulders.

"Who are you?" she shouted.

The man didn't answer. He was looking past her, at the camp, at the treeline, doing the same slow arithmetic she was—counting the hours of storm, the height of the water, the odds. Then he looked back at her, and even through the rain she could see that he'd arrived at the same total.

"Can you swim?" she shouted, because it was the only question left.

Something moved in his face—almost a smile, gone before it landed.

"Better question," he called back, and his voice was low and even under the storm, the voice of a man who had been alone a long time and had gotten used to it. "Can your plane float?"

The water took the low end of the strip. The plane shifted, settled, and began, very gently, to drift.
