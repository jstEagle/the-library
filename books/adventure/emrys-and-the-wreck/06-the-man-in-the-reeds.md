## 6. The Man in the Reeds

The plane came out of the south with its engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs.

Emrys Vale heard it before he saw it—heard it through eleven days of silence that had taught him every other sound the valley made. The rain on the water had a hundred voices: the fine hiss of drizzle on the flooded reeds, the fat percussion of drops off the drowned kapok leaves, the long low drumming when a squall band walked across the reach and turned the whole surface to static. He knew the croak of herons at dusk and the wet knock of drifting logs finding each other in the dark. He knew, by now, which silences meant danger and which merely meant nothing left alive to make noise.

An engine was neither. An engine was a decision being made somewhere above the clouds, by someone who did not know what he knew.

He sank deeper into the reeds until the water pressed cold against his sternum and only his face broke the surface among the stalks, and he watched the plane descend.

It was a small cargo bird, high-winged, patched at the seams, flying the way no survey pilot flew—low, deliberate, reading the ground the way a man reads a page he doesn't trust. It circled the drowned strip once. Twice. Emrys could see the pilot's silhouette lean forward against the glass, could imagine the arithmetic running behind those eyes: runway gone, water over the markers, fuel gauge falling, weather building in the west where the sky had gone the color of wet slate all morning.

*Turn around,* he thought. *Whatever they sent you for, turn around.*

But the plane didn't turn around. It flared, touched down on the flooded strip with a long hissing skid that threw spray across both flanks, and rolled out through standing water until it slowed, wallowed, and stopped—beached, more or less, in eight inches of floodwater at the downstream end of what had once been Kestrel Reach's only landing strip.

Emrys stayed in the reeds and did the math he'd been doing for eleven days, the same math, always arriving at the same ugly sum.

Eleven days ago there had been nine people on the survey team. Himself included. Nine, plus Chief Ansel Roque and two of Roque's security men who kept apart from the rest and cooked their own food and never wrote anything down. Eleven days ago the dam upriver—the one the ministry swore would hold for another decade, the one his own maps had redlined in ink so final it might as well have been blood—had failed early. Not catastrophically, not all at once; not yet. But enough. Enough to send a wall of brown water down the reach and drown the lower camps and put four meters of river under the airstrip and cut every road and trail out of the valley in a single night.

Enough to give nine people a problem, and then to give seven of them a different kind of solution.

He had run. That was the plain truth of it, the truth he carried in his chest like a swallowed stone. When the water came and the radios went down and Roque stood in the rain saying *we'll move everyone to the upper station, we'll sort it out there*, Emrys had looked at Roque's face and seen the sorting already done. He had taken the evidence case—the maps, the memos, the photographs of the foundation cores, everything he'd spent three months collecting against exactly this day—and he had walked into the flooded forest while the others argued about boats, and he had not come back.

He told himself he'd gone for help. He told himself he'd survive so the truth would survive. Both things were true. Neither thing made him less of a man who had walked away into the reeds while Dr. Ilsa Marchetti was still shouting about the seismographs, while young Okafor was still hauling sandbags nobody needed, while the water rose and Roque's men watched the treeline instead of the river.

So now he lay in the reeds watching a stranger's plane sit stranded in the flood, and he calculated.

If she was ministry, she was a threat. If she was press, she was a weapon he couldn't aim. If she was a supply pilot—and the crates lashed in her cabin said medicine, said mail, said the small honest commerce of the river settlements—then she was something rarer: a way out. A pair of wings, a radio, a person whose disappearance would be noticed by people who weren't paid to notice nothing.

She was also, he reminded himself, a person. And the last time he had let himself think of people as instruments—maps as arguments, colleagues as witnesses, survival as strategy—seven of them had gone into the water.

The plane's door opened. The pilot dropped to the float-step and stood ankle-deep in floodwater, testing the strip with her boot heel, and even at eighty meters Emrys could read her posture: the flat economy of someone checking damage before feeling it. She pulled her cap lower against the drizzle, walked the length of the submerged strip, crouched at the far marker, and stood again with her hands on her hips in a way that said the news was bad and she had expected it to be.

A woman. Alone. No escort, no second aircraft, no security detail.

*They wouldn't send one woman alone if they knew,* he thought. *Which means they don't know. Which means whoever chartered her wants eyes on this valley without wanting questions asked about why.*

Or it meant she was exactly what she looked like—a bush pilot caught by weather, doing her job, counting her fuel. Emrys had spent eleven days learning how little he could trust what things looked like. The dam had looked sound. Roque had looked like a tired professional doing an unpleasant job well. The ministry liaison, Ferro, had looked him in the eye over dinner and said *your concerns are noted, cartographer*, in the warm tone of a man noting them straight into a shredder.

The pilot finished her inspection and went back to the plane, and Emrys watched her work. She chocked the wheels with driftwood. She checked the tie-down points, tested the wind with a wet finger, glanced west at the squall line with the expression of someone negotiating with it rather than fearing it. She moved like the landscape was a counterpart she'd argued with for years and mostly beaten—not a soldier's efficiency, but a working person's, worn smooth by repetition.

And she was going to be stuck here. That was written in the western sky as plainly as anything he'd ever drawn. The monsoon bands were stacking up behind the ridge; by nightfall the reach would be a single gray room with no doors. Whatever her charter was, she wasn't flying out today, and probably not tomorrow, and the strip under her wheels was going to keep rising.

Which meant that sooner or later, the valley would introduce them.

He could stay ghost. That was the first plan, the plan he'd lived by since the flood: stay ghost, stay alive, move downriver at night, reach the coast, reach the auditor, reach anyone with the power to make the evidence matter. Ghosts didn't get captured. Ghosts didn't get shot in the head by men who cooked their own food and never wrote anything down. For eleven days the ghost had served him well—he'd slept in trees, eaten palm heart and stolen from an abandoned fishing rack, watched Roque's patrol boat sweep the reach twice with a searchlight like a slow white blade.

But the ghost had a flaw, and the flaw was sitting right there in the floodwater, chocking her wheels with driftwood: the ghost could not testify. A ghost's maps were just paper. A ghost's story died with him or worse—surfaced without him, discredited, a dead man's paranoia. What the evidence needed was a voice attached to a body attached to a name, standing in front of someone with authority, saying *I drew these contours, I flagged these foundations, I was ordered to proceed.*

And what the ghost needed—this was the part he admitted only grudgingly, the way you admit a leak in a boat you can't beach—was another human voice. Eleven days of silence had begun to do something to him. He'd started talking to himself, first in whispers, then aloud, narrating his movements like a man dictating a report no one would file. Last night he had caught himself answering. The reeds were full of voices that weren't there. He was dissolving into the valley, becoming one more thing the water took.

The pilot opened her cabin door and began unloading crates onto the float-step, methodical, and Emrys saw her pause over one of them—a small box, held longer than the others, set down more carefully. Medicine, maybe. Something for someone waiting downriver. Someone who would wait and not know why the plane never came.

*You could stay ghost,* he told himself. *Let her shelter in the camp wreck, let her find the burned logs herself, let her draw her own conclusions about scorch marks and missing bodies. Let the valley speak for you.*

But the valley spoke in the language of things left behind, and things left behind were exactly what Roque wanted found—or wanted found ambiguous. The camp had been arranged, after the flood, arranged by hands that were not panicking. Emrys had watched from the treeline on the third day as two of Roque's men policed the site: burning the document crate, dragging the boat wreckage to look storm-tossed, taking the team's personal effects away in bags. They had left just enough ruin to tell a story. The story was *act of God, tragic loss, no survivors, nothing to investigate.* It was a good story. It was almost true.

Only Emrys knew the false notes in it, the same way only he knew which contour lines on his own maps had been redrawn by other hands.

The pilot finished her unloading and stood in the shallows looking west again, and the light caught her face for a moment—tired, he thought. Tired in a way that had layers to it, the way his own tiredness had layers. She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, a gesture with no audience intended, and something in it snagged on him. He knew that gesture. It was the gesture of a person who had been holding themselves together by procedure for a long time, and whose procedure was currently underwater.

Two solitudes, he thought. His and hers. Standing eighty meters apart in the same rising water.

He ran the risks one final time, because calculation was the only discipline he had left. Risk: she reported him, and Roque came, and the ghost was caught. Mitigation: she had no radio contact—the mast on the camp wreck was down, and if her own set worked she'd have called in already instead of walking the strip. Risk: she was Roque's, planted, bait. Unlikely—one woman, one old plane, no security, and bait didn't chock its wheels with driftwood like it planned to still be here tomorrow. Risk: she was innocent, and touching her life to his would stain it. This risk he could not mitigate. This risk he simply had to accept, because he had accepted worse ones already, in the rain, with the water rising, and the interest on those was coming due whether he paid or not.

There was one more consideration, and it was not strategic, and he let it tip the scale anyway. If he stayed ghost tonight, she would spend the night alone in the wrecked camp of nine vanished people, finding the scorch marks, finding the half-burned logs, finding whatever Roque's men had missed in their tidying—and she would spend it afraid, in the dark, in a valley that had already eaten seven souls. He had been afraid alone for eleven days. He would not wish it on anyone, least of all a stranger whose only crime was flying into a lie.

Emrys Vale stood up out of the reeds.

Water sheeted off him. He'd forgotten how loud it was, moving—eleven days of stillness had made him forget he had a body, that it displaced the world, that the world answered. The reeds rattled. A heron exploded off the water twenty meters to his left, all wings and outrage, and he saw the pilot spin toward the sound, hand going to her hip where a sidearm wasn't, weight dropping into a stance that said she'd been robbed before or close to it.

He raised both hands, palms out, water streaming from his sleeves. He was aware of what he must look like: a gaunt man in rotted canvas, beard gone to seed, skin river-pale, standing hip-deep in the flooded reeds of a valley where seven people had drowned. He had frightened himself in reflections. He could only imagine the version of him she was seeing.

"Don't shoot," he said, and his voice came out rusted, half-swallowed by disuse. He cleared his throat and tried again, louder, pitching it over the water the way you pitch sound across a lake. "I'm not armed. I'm the one you're looking for. Or—I'm the one somebody should be looking for."

She hadn't moved. Through the drizzle he watched her take inventory of him the way she'd taken inventory of the strip: fast, thorough, unsentimental. Her eyes went to his hands, his waist, the treeline behind him, the water between them. Good, he thought. Check everything. Trust nothing. It was the only sane policy in this valley, and he was about to test it to destruction.

"Who's 'somebody'?" she called back. Her voice was steady. Low, a little rough, the voice of someone used to making herself heard over an engine. "Because nobody sent me looking for anybody. I fly supplies."

"I know." He began wading forward, slowly, deliberately, showing her every step. "Medicine and mail. River settlements. You've been doing the circuit for years—they call your plane the Brown Heron at the trading posts, because of the tail."

That landed. He saw it land—a flicker in her stance, the small recalibration of a person discovering the stranger knows her name. He hated doing it. It was the trick Roque's men would use, familiarity as a hook. But he had perhaps ninety seconds to convert himself from threat to person, and the fastest route ran through proof that he belonged to this world too.

"How do you know my plane?"

"Everyone on the river knows your plane." Eighty meters became sixty. The water shallowed; the reeds thinned; he stepped out onto the sodden margin of the old strip, mud sucking at his boots, hands still up. "I'm Emrys Vale. I was the cartographer on the government survey. The one that's missing."

The word *missing* hung in the drizzle between them. He watched her glance past him, up the reach, toward the place where the camp wreck sat among the drowned trees with its burned crate and its tidy ruin, and he watched her do the arithmetic he'd been dreading: *if he's the survey, and the survey is missing, and he's standing here—*

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. Not cruelly. Just precisely, the way she seemed to do everything.

"Seven of us are," Emrys said. "As far as I know. I ran." The stone in his chest turned over. He made himself say the next part plainly, because he had decided, somewhere in the reeds, that if he was done being a ghost he would start by refusing to lie to the first living soul who'd seen him in eleven days. "I ran when the flood came, and I took the survey's records with me, and I've been hiding in this valley ever since because the people who caused the flood want the records and me at the bottom of it."

Silence, except the rain on the water and the far mutter of thunder in the west. She looked at him for a long moment, and he braced for the scream, or the sprint for the plane, or the flat disbelief of a sensible person hearing a madman's story.

Instead she said, "Caused it. You mean the dam failed."

"I mean the dam was failing," Emrys said, "and they were told, in writing, with maps, for six months. And they proceeded anyway. And when it started to go early, they didn't evacuate the reach. They evacuated the paperwork."

Something shifted in her face then—not belief, not yet, but the recognition of a shape she knew. He'd seen that look before, on river pilots and traders and the settlement nurses he'd ferried maps to in another life: the look of a person who had always suspected the water was rising for reasons that had nothing to do with God, and was now watching the suspicion grow teeth.

"And you've got these records," she said slowly. "On you. In this valley. Where the people who want them buried can't find them."

"In this valley," Emrys agreed. "For now."

Thunder rolled down off the ridge, closer than before, and the first real band of the squall line came walking across the reach behind it, bending the reeds, stippling the flood to white. The pilot glanced at it, then at her half-unloaded plane, then at him—at the wreck of a man standing in the rain offering her the truth like a crate of contraband.

"The weather's closing the valley," she said. "Neither of us is going anywhere."

"No," Emrys said. "We're not."

She exhaled through her nose, a short, hard breath that might have been a laugh in a kinder climate, and jerked her chin toward the drowned trees and the camp wreck beyond them.

"Then you'd better help me get this plane tied down properly," she said, "and then you'd better start talking. And Vale—" her eyes narrowed, and he saw that behind the exhaustion was a mind that missed very little, "—if any part of what you just told me turns out to be a story, I will leave you in this valley myself."

"That," said Emrys Vale, wading toward her through the rising water, "is the most reasonable offer anyone has made me in eleven days."

The rain came down harder, and the valley closed its gray doors behind them both.
