## 7. Stranger's Hands

*Emrys*

He had watched the plane come down the way a drowning man watches a rope thrown into current—with hope and dread in the same breath, because a rope can pull you under as easily as out.

Emrys Vale had been in the reeds for eleven days. Long enough to learn their rhythm, the way the floodwater breathed through them twice a day as the dam's surviving sluices opened and closed. Long enough to know which hummocks held, which mud swallowed a boot to the knee, which of the drowned trees still had hollows dry enough to sleep in. He had eaten river mussels and one unlucky capybara and a handful of palm hearts that had turned his stomach for a day and a half. He had watched the search boats come and go—three of them, white hulls, ministry markings, sweeping in patterns that were too wide, too shallow, too indifferent to ever find anyone who did not wish to be found.

He had decided, in those eleven days, to be a ghost. Ghosts didn't testify. Ghosts didn't get pulled from the water at two in the morning by men in gray boats with no markings at all. Ghosts survived.

Then the plane had come down through the storm bands, and everything he had decided had come apart in the space of a single, terrible minute.

Because he knew that aircraft. Not the airframe—he didn't know airframes the way pilots did—but the *shape* of it, the little bush plane with the patched cowling and the tail number he'd seen on a supply manifest three months ago, back when he'd still been a man with a desk and a title and a government that answered his letters. The pilot who had flown the survey's mail and instruments up the reach, twice a month, regular as a tide. He had never met her. He had watched her land once from the survey camp, a small figure in a canvas jacket walking the strip with her head tilted, listening to the wind the way other people listened to radio.

She was walking the strip now. He could see her through the reeds, moving with a limping, deliberate care, checking her landing gear, her wing, the water lapping at the tires. And the storm was coming down the valley behind her like a wall being pushed over—slow, inevitable, the color of wet slate.

*Stay in the reeds,* he told himself. *Let her fly out. Let her be someone else's problem.*

But the water was already over the strip's midpoint, and the wind had turned, and he had watched enough aircraft in trouble to know what he was looking at: a pilot who had perhaps ten minutes of margin and didn't know it yet. And beneath the calculation, beneath the eleven days of discipline, something older and less convenient stirred—the memory of six people he had mapped rivers with, eaten canned peaches with, argued with over contour lines, and who were now either dead or scattered the way he was scattered, and whose disappearance he had survived by being, precisely, a ghost.

Ghosts didn't warn people about storms. People did.

He stepped out of the reeds.

She spun with a pistol drawn faster than he'd thought possible from that distance, both hands, low stance, weight settled—trained, or at least practiced. He raised his own hands, palms out, and stood very still in the shin-deep water, letting her look at him: the week of beard, the ruined shirt, the boots full of river, the man-shaped ruin of him.

"Survivor," he said. "Survey team. Please don't shoot. I've had a very bad month."

She didn't lower the gun. "Name."

"Emrys. Emrys Vale." The truth, or the first layer of it. He had learned in the last eleven days that the truth was a thing you could hand out in slices, and each slice cost you something different.

"Odile Blackwood." She said it like a fact she was giving him in exchange, a fair trade, and still the pistol didn't waver. "You're the first body I've seen out here, and I don't mean that as a compliment. Where's the rest of your team?"

"Gone." He let the word sit in the rain that had just begun to fall, fine and cold as needle points. "I'll tell you everything. But not standing in the open with that weather coming. Can I lower my hands?"

She considered him for a long three seconds. He watched her weigh it—the gun, the storm, the arithmetic of a lone pilot and a stranger in the flood. Then she jerked her chin toward the plane. "Help me tie her down first. Then you talk."

It was, he would think later, the most sensible thing anyone had said to him in a month.

* * *

*Odile*

She did not trust him. She wanted that on the record, even if the record was only the inside of her own skull.

Nobody survived eleven days—his estimate, and he looked it—in a flooded valley by being lucky. He moved like a man who had rehearsed being found: hands up at the right height, voice pitched to carry but not shout, answers that came fast enough to seem honest and smooth enough to seem practiced. When she'd asked where the camp was, he'd pointed without hesitating. When she'd asked how many were in the team, he'd said *six, plus me* without counting on his fingers. People who were telling the truth from memory hesitated. People telling truth from a script didn't.

But he also had hands like a dockworker's and a way of handling the tie-down ropes that told her he'd secured aircraft before, or boats, or cargo—something heavy, something that killed you if you did it wrong. And when the first real gust came off the valley wall and shoved the plane sideways on its gear, he was already moving to the high wing, throwing his weight against the strut, shouting "Downwind tie first!" over the wind in a voice that expected to be obeyed.

She obeyed. That annoyed her most of all.

The storm bands came in pulses, the way the monsoon always did this early in the season—five minutes of savage wind and horizontal rain, then an eerie, hissing lull, then the next band, each one darker than the last. Between pulses they worked. She had four tie-downs in the plane's kit; the strip's old anchors were underwater, so they used what the land offered: a drowned fence post that still held, the root buttress of a half-fallen tree, a steel survey stake he found by feel in the mud as if he'd planted it himself.

*As if he'd planted it himself.* She turned that over while she wrestled the tail rope around the fence post. Of course he'd know where the survey stakes were. He said he was on the team. The team that had a camp, gear, a radio—and not one living soul visible from the air, and no answer on any channel for six days.

"Your camp," she said, between gusts. "How far?"

"Kilometer and a half, that way." He nodded up-valley, then paused, hands stilling on the rope. "The tents are down. The radio's dead—I checked it the second day. The boats—" A flicker crossed his face, there and gone, like a fish under brown water. "The boats are gone."

"Gone where?"

"The flood took them." He said it evenly, but she had spent years reading weather, and weather had taught her to read the pause before a front hit—the pressure drop, the stillness, the lie the air told right before it turned. He was lying about the boats. Or hiding something adjacent to the boats. She filed it away.

The next band hit before she could press. It came off the ridge like a freight train, driving rain so hard it stung, and the plane groaned against its lines. They threw themselves against the fuselage together, shoulder to shoulder, and she felt the surprising solidity of him, the heat of him through soaked canvas, and hated herself for noticing. It had been a long time since anyone had stood next to her in bad weather. It had been a long time since anyone had stood next to her at all.

"Anchor's holding!" he shouted in her ear.

"It better!" She shouted back. "She's all I've got!"

"What, no family fortune?"

"No!" And then, because the wind had cracked something loose in her, because the fear was high and the adrenaline needed somewhere to go: "Just a debt!"

He didn't laugh. Some part of her had braced for the laugh—the little laugh people gave when you told them the shape of your life and it sounded small to them. Instead he said, close to her ear, "Then we'd better keep her in one piece," and there was nothing small in how he said it. Like the plane mattered. Like she mattered. Which was, of course, exactly the kind of thing a man said when he wanted something.

The band passed. They stood in the hissing aftermath, breathing hard, the plane shuddering under their hands like a living thing.

"Shelter," she said. "Your camp or my plane, and my plane's a coffin in a flood. Show me the camp."

"It's a walk. In this." He looked up at the sky, reading it. She watched him read it, and the reading was expert—too expert for a cartographer? No. Exactly expert for a cartographer. Men who mapped rivers knew weather the way pilots did; it was the same education, just taken from the ground. "There's a rise past the camp, old ground, above the flood line. There was a supply hut on it. Concrete floor, tin roof."

"*Was?*"

"It's ugly. But it's dry under the roof, and the roof's still mostly on." He met her eyes, and for the first time since he'd walked out of the reeds, something in his face wasn't managed. "Miss Blackwood. I know how this looks. Stranger in the flood, no team, no answers. If I were you, I'd have shot me already."

"I considered it."

"I believe that." Almost a smile. It changed his whole face, and she looked away from it, busying her hands with the last tie-down. "I'll answer everything. Tonight. But we get dry first, because wet and cold makes people stupid, and stupid people die in valleys like this one. You know that. You fly them."

She did know that. She checked the knots one final time, ran her palm along the wing's leading edge the way she'd have checked a child's forehead, and turned up-valley. "Walk fast, Vale. And walk where I can see you."

*Always,* he almost said, and didn't, because it would have been too much truth for the hour.

* * *

*Emrys*

The walk to the camp took forty minutes and cost him more than the eleven days before it.

Not the walking—he was past being tired in any way that registered; his body had become a tool he operated and felt nothing about. It was the going back. Every step up-valley was a step toward the thing he had run from, the tents, the radio room, the pegboard where his maps had hung, and the specific, unrepeatable horror of the night the water came. He had planned never to see the camp again. Now he was leading a stranger into it, and the stranger was a pilot with sharp eyes and a sharper instinct for the pause before the front, and everything he had kept buried was going to be lying around in the mud where she could trip over it.

He tried to manage the route. It was the work of an hour to realize he couldn't. The flood had rearranged everything—channels where paths had been, a new sandbar under the old supply trail, a drowned grove that he had to navigate by memory of what *wasn't* there anymore. She noticed. Of course she noticed.

"You knew this ground," she said, wading beside him. Not a question.

"I mapped it." He kept his eyes on the water ahead. "Every contour. Every channel. For two years."

"And now it's all underwater."

"Now it's all underwater." The rain filled the silence after, drumming on the flood, on their shoulders, on the drowned world. She let it go. He was grateful and wary in equal measure; a person who let silences do their work was more dangerous than a person who asked questions, because they were always listening.

The camp came into view through the rain, and he heard her breath change.

He had prepared her with words—*the tents are down*—but words were a poor map of this. The survey camp had been a small, orderly village: six dome tents on platforms, a mess fly, the radio hut, the map room in a repurposed shipping container he'd loved with the pure love he'd never managed to give a person. The flood had taken it all apart with the patient violence of water, which doesn't rage so much as *edit*. The tents were down and half-full of river. The mess fly was a shredded flag on a bent pole. The shipping container sat canted at fifteen degrees, half its door torn open like a rib cage, his maps—his beautiful, doomed maps—fused into a brown pulp that was already becoming mud.

Odile stood in the middle of it, rain streaming off her, and turned in a slow circle. He watched her do what he would have done: read the scene. The gear without tracks. The mess kits still in the wash basin, swelled with water. A boot. One boot, upright on a platform, laced.

"Vale." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she probably used on the radio when things were going wrong. "Where is everybody?"

"I told you. Gone."

"That's not an answer, that's a *direction*. Gone where? Dead? Evacuated? Taken?" She turned on him, and the rain ran off her chin, and her eyes were the color of the storm and just as hard to read and twice as dangerous. "I flew resupply to these people. I know their names off the manifest. Okonkwo. Reyes. Lindqvist. The twins, the—Dara and Damaris Chen. I carried their mail. So when I ask where they are, I am asking as the last person who was supposed to see them alive. Do you understand me?"

He understood her. He understood, with a sick lurch, that he had spent eleven days surviving and had somehow not spent a single minute of them *grieving*, and that her voice had just torn the seal off that box, and that if he opened it here, in the rain, in front of her, he might never get it closed again. Six names. He could say six names. He had drawn each of them onto maps, in pencil, in his own hand.

"Okonkwo went upriver the day before," he said. His voice came out low and rough. "Leave rotation. He might live. Reyes and Lindqvist were in the second boat when the flood took the first one. The Chens were in the first boat." The rain drummed. "I was on the ridge. Surveying. I watched the water come down the valley and I watched the boat—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't know where anyone is. That's the truth. The whole truth I have."

*Not the whole truth,* said the part of him that had kept the drive dry inside a waterproof sleeve against his skin for eleven days. *But the whole truth you have.*

She held his eyes for a long moment. The storm band hit then, the worst one yet, and the world went white with noise, and whatever she'd seen in his face, she'd seen it in the worst possible light—half-blinded, half-deafened, both of them flinching from the sky. When it passed she was looking at the boot.

"Show me the hut," she said finally. "Then you start at the beginning, and you don't skip the parts that make you look bad. Especially those."

* * *

*Odile*

The supply hut was, as promised, ugly and mostly dry.

Half the roof had peeled back like the lid of a sardine tin, and the concrete floor was littered with the flood's leavings—driftwood, a dead bird, silt in windrows—but the corner beneath the surviving roof section was shelter enough. They got a spirit stove going with fuel from her emergency kit, hung her poncho as a windbreak, and sat on their packs with their boots steaming, and she watched the man across the tiny flame and made herself be patient.

He was doing something to his hands. She noticed it because she'd spent a lifetime noticing her own—the way hers wanted a yoke or a wrench or a rope when they were nervous, the way stillness had to be *chosen*. His hands were quiet, folded, deliberate. Too deliberate. A man whose hands were naturally quiet let them be quiet without effort. This man was holding them quiet.

"Start at the beginning," she said.

"The survey started twenty-six months ago." He spoke to the flame at first, then, with visible effort, to her. "Ministry of Water and Power. Purpose: baseline mapping for the Kestrel Reach impoundment. I was the cartographer. Topographic, bathymetric, geologic overlays." He said it all in the level voice of a man reading his own file. "We mapped the valley. We mapped it better than it had ever been mapped."

"And?"

"And the geology was wrong." The flame jumped in a draft. He didn't. "The ministry's site report put the foundation rock as granite. It isn't granite. It's fractured sedimentary—water-saturated, unstable. The dam as designed would sit on it like a house on ice. I flagged it. I flagged it in writing, with maps, with cross-sections. My team lead flagged it. The ministry redlined our reports and issued the approval anyway, and told us our revised maps were—these were the words—'no longer operationally relevant.'"

Odile sat very still. The rain hammered the tin above them, a sound like applause from a huge, hostile crowd. She thought about the drowned village they'd flown over on approach, rooftops just breaking the brown water, and about the word *impoundment*, which was a word for drowning things that made it sound like bookkeeping.

"So the dam—"

"Failed early. Eleven days ago. Ahead of schedule, ahead of every safety test, because the foundation let go the first time the water got high." His voice cracked, just once, and he pressed it flat again. "The valley filled in forty minutes. The survey team was on the water when it came."

She should have felt triumph—*I was right, the crack in the story, here it is*—but what she felt was cold. Because a story that big, a failure that big, had a second half. Failures got investigated. Failures got *admitted*. Unless.

"Vale. If your reports said the dam would fail, and it failed, then you're not a survivor. You're a witness. The most valuable witness in the country." She leaned toward the flame, toward him. "So why have you spent eleven days in the reeds being a ghost instead of walking to the nearest radio station and saying all this out loud?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the next band was building; she could feel it in her back teeth, in the pressure behind the tin roof. He looked into the stove flame and his quiet hands finally moved—one of them going, unconsciously, to his chest. To something under his ruined shirt. Flat. Hard. The size of a notebook. Or a drive.

"Because three days after the flood," he said, "a boat with no markings came up the valley looking for survivors. And it wasn't looking to rescue them."

The wind hit the hut then, the full band, and the tin roof shrieked and a section of it tore away into the dark, and the rain came through in a cold sheet, and by the time they'd got the poncho braced and the flame saved and themselves soaked to the skin anyway, she had her answer without needing to hear it. But he gave it to her anyway, shouting it over the storm, close enough that she felt the words against her ear more than heard them:

"They're still looking, Miss Blackwood. And now they've seen your plane."
