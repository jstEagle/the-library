## 8. Monsoon Wall

She saw it coming the way you saw a wall of water in a narrow canyon—too late to matter, soon enough to be afraid.

The storm had been building all afternoon, stacked gray thunderheads shouldering up over the eastern ridge like a crowd pressing against a door. Now the door had failed. The wall of rain came down the valley in a single moving curtain, a gray-brown smother that erased the ridgelines one by one, and Odile stood in the shallows at the nose of her plane with her hands flat on the cowling and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that she was not going to fly out of Kestrel Reach today.

"Get it tied down," Emrys said behind her. Not shouting. His voice carried the way quiet men's voices do in emergencies, pitched to be heard over panic rather than over noise. "The water's going to come up another meter before this is done. Tie it to the trees, not the stakes."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to tell him she'd landed on strips that flooded every wet season since before he was born, that she knew what water did, that she'd been counting gallons and meters and millimeters of fuel and clearance her whole adult life while he—while whatever he was—did whatever he did. But the rain reached them then, the leading edge of it, fat drops the size of grapes hitting the aluminum skin of the plane like thrown gravel, and argument became a luxury she couldn't afford.

They worked in the first furious minutes of it, when the rain was still individual drops and not yet a solid thing. Emrys ran lines to the strangler figs at the edge of the strip—old growth, roots like the buttresses of a cathedral—and Odile paid them out and cinched them down, her fingers already too swollen with wet to feel the rope burns. The plane was a Fairchild, her father's Fairchild, twenty-two years old and held together by safety wire and stubbornness, and it was the single most valuable thing she owned and the single most valuable thing she owed on, and she lashed it to the trees like a mother tying down a child in a wind.

"Deeper," Emrys called. "Higher ground for the lines. Water'll float her otherwise."

She didn't answer. She moved the lines deeper. The rain thickened from gravel to hammering, and then from hammering to something that wasn't drops at all anymore, just a roaring gray weight, and the world contracted to the length of her own arms.

* * *

They made the camp in the last of the light, or what passed for light under that sky, which was a bruised half-dark like the inside of a closed hand.

The survey camp sat on a shelf of ground above the drowned valley's northern shore—four wall tents, a cook shelter, a radio mast bent double like a straw in a child's fist. The flood had reached the lowest tent and taken it, dragged it down the slope and wrapped it around a tree forty meters away, a pale shroud. The other three stood, but their walls bellied in and out with the wind, and the whole compound had the look of a place abandoned mid-sentence. A tin cup sat on the cook table, upright, full of rain. A pair of boots stood outside one tent door, side by side, laces tucked in. Nobody leaves boots like that unless they're coming back in five minutes.

"Hello the camp!" Odile called anyway, because you called anyway, because the one time you didn't would be the time somebody lay dying inside. Her voice died in the rain. Nothing answered but water.

Emrys moved through the camp like a man walking through his own memory, touching things without picking them up—the bent mast, the cook shelter's ridgepole, the door flap of the middle tent. He stopped at a fire pit near the center of the compound and crouched, and Odile saw his shoulders change, saw something settle into them that hadn't been there before.

"What is it?"

"Come look."

She didn't want to come look. She wanted to find the driest tent, get out of the rain, and stop thinking about the boots by the door. But she came and looked.

The fire pit held the remains of a fire that had been fed with paper. Not kindling—paper. Charred fragments lay in a fan across the wet ground where the rain hadn't quite reached under the overhang of the cook shelter, and the fragments were typed. She could see the ghost of columns, the skeletons of numbers. Somebody had burned documents here, carefully, in a pit, and then the rain had come and saved a corner of the work from them.

"You said the team was evacuated," Odile said. "You said they went downriver before the flood."

"I said what I had to say to get you to stop pointing questions at me like a lamp." Emrys didn't look up. His hands hovered over the charred paper without touching it, the way you'd hover over a wounded bird. "This is the field log. Survey teams keep a daily log—readings, positions, decisions. It's the legal record of the whole operation. Somebody burned it."

"Somebody" being the team, or somebody being whoever came for the team. She didn't ask. She was starting to understand that questions aimed at Emrys Vale went into a kind of fog and came back changed.

The rain hammered. Somewhere up the valley, thunder rolled along the ridgeline like a barrel going downstairs, and under it, fainter, there was another sound—water moving where water shouldn't, the river rearranging its opinions about where it lived.

"We can't stay in the open," she said. "Tents. Pick one."

* * *

The easternmost tent had held. Its floor was a wooden platform raised on posts, and though a corner of the fly had torn and let in a thin bright thread of rain, the interior was dry enough, and—this was what mattered—its aluminum equipment trunks were intact, latched, and heavy.

Odile got out of her soaked flight jacket for the first time in eleven hours and discovered she was shivering so hard her teeth chattered like a telegraph key. Emrys found a camp stove in one of the trunks, and a can of solvent, and—this surprised her—a dry blanket wrapped in waxed canvas, army-issue, smelling of mold and diesel and, faintly, of somebody else's life. He put the blanket around her shoulders without asking, the way you'd throw a tarp over a stalled engine, and she was too cold to refuse it and too proud to thank him, so she did neither, and they left it there between them like a coin on a table.

The stove caught. Its small orange light made the tent a lantern, made the rain on the canvas a sound instead of a threat. Odile held her hands toward it and watched steam rise off her own skin, and for a few minutes neither of them spoke, and the silence wasn't comfortable but it was honest, which was more than she'd gotten from anyone in months.

"There's coffee in that trunk," Emrys said finally. "If you want it. It's theirs."

"It's nobody's now." She dug out the can, found a dented pot, measured water by eye. "Coffee's coffee. It doesn't care whose it was."

He almost smiled. She caught it in profile, there and gone, like a fish turning in dark water. It changed his whole face, and she looked away from it quickly, the way you look away from something you don't have the currency to buy.

The coffee was terrible—scorched, weak, made with rainwater that tasted of canvas. It was the best thing she'd ever drunk. She wrapped both hands around the dented cup and felt the heat travel up her arms and into the place behind her sternum where the cold had set up housekeeping, and she thought, absurdly, of the trading post, of Harrow Voss's soft voice saying *after the wet season, Odile, we'll talk about the wet season*, and she thought: I am trapped in a drowned valley with a stranger, my plane is tied to a tree, and the man who owns my life is a hundred miles away and dry. And the strangest part, the part she filed away to examine never, was that she felt safer here than she had at the trading post.

"Tell me about the logs," she said.

Emrys was sitting across the stove from her, knees up, arms on his knees, a posture she was learning meant *calculating*. The firelight moved on his face. "What do you want to know?"

"Who burned them. Why. What was in them. Whether the team that wore those boots is alive." She counted them off against her fingers. "In whatever order is easiest for you to lie about."

"That's a short list," he said, "with a long answer."

"I've got time." She nodded at the canvas wall, where the rain was making its point over and over. "The valley's closed. Look at it. No plane flies in that, not mine, not anyone's. That storm's sitting on the ridgeline and it isn't moving until tomorrow night, maybe the day after. So whatever you were going to do—walk out, signal someone, disappear into the reeds again—you can't do it. You're stuck with me, and I'm stuck with you, and I'd rather know who I'm stuck with."

The rain filled the pause, drumming, patient.

"You're right that it's closed," Emrys said. "That front came down the valley like it was following a map. It'll sit for a day, maybe two. Nothing flies." He turned his cup slowly in his hands, and she noticed for the first time that his fingers were ink-stained at the cuticles, faint old stains that soap hadn't reached, the marks of a man who drew for a living. A cartographer's hands. The thought arrived from nowhere and stayed.

"Half an answer," she prompted.

"Here's what I can tell you tonight." His voice had gone quiet, level, the voice of a man reading coordinates off a chart. "The survey was a foundation study for the Kestrel Reach dam. I drew the maps. The team found things in the rock that the ministry didn't want found. Two weeks ago the team was ordered to move downriver to the lower station ahead of schedule. The order came through channels that don't usually give orders to field teams. They went. I stayed behind to close out the mapping. That's the truth, Odile, all of it I can give you tonight."

"And the rest?"

"The rest costs something." He looked at her across the stove, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of the river in flood, brown and opaque and moving fast underneath. "It costs you too, not just me. That's why I'm not giving it to you over coffee in a tent. You'll get it when you can't unknow it anyway. Not before."

She should have been furious. Some part of her was—a small hot coal of it, banked down. But a larger part of her was tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix, tired of carrying everything alone, and the stranger's stubbornness had a shape she recognized, because she'd been wearing it herself for years. He was keeping a secret the way she kept her ledger: because the truth of it was the only thing he had that was fully his.

"Fine," she said. "Keep it. But I'm going to read whatever didn't burn."

* * *

They went back out into the rain with the stove lantern, two figures bent double against the wind, and searched the camp while the dark came down around them like water filling a hull.

The fire pit had taken most of it. But whoever burned the log had worked in a hurry at the end—she could read the story in the ash, the careful feeding of pages at first and then the frantic last armfuls—and hurry leaves leavings. In the cook shelter, under a false bottom in a supply crate that Emrys found by weight, they struck the seam: a canvas document case, water-stained, half its contents gone to char where fire had licked it before it was crammed away. The rest was salvage.

They spread the pages on the cook table under the lantern, weighted at the corners with tin cups, and Odile felt the hair rise on her arms as she looked down at them.

Field readings. Borehole logs. Dates in a neat hand—Emrys's hand, she realized; the man drew and wrote like a draftsman, every numeral identical to its brothers. Columns of numbers for water table depth, for core samples, for seepage rates along the dam's foundation abutment. And in the margins, in a different ink, a different hand—angrier, faster—phrases that had survived the water stains:

*seepage triple the model — not seasonal — ask again*

*redlined. proceeding per directive. initial here*

*who signed the variance? no name given*

*V. says drop it. V. says drop it or*

The last entry stopped mid-word. The page was scorched at the corner where the fire had reached it, and the sentence ended in brown char, and Odile sat looking at that small burned absence the way you look at a hole in the floor.

"V.," she said.

"Voss," Emrys said quietly.

The name went through her like a fishhook. She heard it again in the soft voice at the trading post—*after the wet season*—heard the ledger close, heard the particular kindness in his tone that was worse than cruelty because it assumed she had nowhere else to go. Harrow Voss held the paper on her father's debt. Harrow Voss held the paper on her plane. And Harrow Voss's initial was sitting in the margin of a burned survey log in a drowned valley, next to the words *drop it or*.

"That's not possible," she said, and heard how thin her own voice was. "Voss is a financier. He holds notes. He doesn't—"

"Financiers hold more than notes." Emrys was watching her, and she saw him do the arithmetic of her face, saw him arrive at her connection before she said it aloud. "Why? What's Voss to you?"

She should have lied. Every instinct she owned, every year of armor, said lie, say nothing, keep your business your business. But the rain was hammering the canvas like a second heartbeat, and the burned page lay between them with its unfinished sentence, and she was so tired of being the only person who knew anything.

"He holds my father's debt," she said. "The whole operation. The plane, the routes, everything. When my father died, the note came to me. If I miss a payment, Voss takes the plane, and the plane is—" She stopped, because the sentence ended in a place she didn't want to go. *The plane is me.* "Everything," she said instead.

Emrys was silent for a long moment. Outside, the river spoke in the dark, a low continuous conversation of water over drowned things.

"Then you're not neutral in this," he said. "I'm sorry. I know you wanted to be. But nobody who owes Harrow Voss is neutral in anything."

The lantern hissed. Odile looked down at the margin note, at the fast angry hand that had wanted to know who signed the variance and had been told, in effect, to stop asking. She thought about her father, who had signed things too—signed and signed and signed in the last bad year, and she'd never known for what, and Voss had come after the funeral with a leather folder and a voice like warm oil, and she had signed because the alternative was losing the plane and the plane was the only thing left that remembered him.

*Who signed the variance?* she thought. *My father signed things. What did my father sign?*

The thought was too large and too cold to hold, and she set it down the way you set down something heavy in the dark, marking the spot.

"We should sleep in shifts," she said, because the alternative was to keep thinking. "One of us watches the water line. If it comes up the shelf, we move the plane's lines higher, and if it takes the plane—" Her voice did something she hated. "If it takes the plane, I want to see it go. Not wake up to it."

"Understood." Emrys took the first watch without discussion, settling by the tent flap with the lantern turned low, and Odile lay down on the survey team's cot in the survey team's tent under the survey team's blanket, smelling their mold and their diesel, listening to the boots' absent owners in the rain.

Sleep came in pieces. Between the pieces she surfaced to the sound of the storm and saw Emrys at the flap, motionless, watching the dark where the water was, and once she surfaced to hear him speaking—low, half a voice, and she couldn't tell if it was to himself or a prayer or the beginning of the confession he was saving, and she didn't ask. Solitude, she thought, was a country she knew the language of. He was fluent too.

* * *

Near dawn the storm eased from a wall to a roar, which was the storm's version of mercy, and Odile gave up on sleep and crawled out into the gray wet light to check the water.

It had come up four meters overnight. The strip was gone—not flooded, gone, the whole meadow a brown lake with the tops of the grass moving under it like hair. Her plane sat high on its lines, nose down at an angle she didn't like, tail clear, and she stood in the rain looking at it and doing the arithmetic: engine intake at water level, no strip, no wind window for two days at least, and the river still rising. The Fairchild wasn't going anywhere. Neither was she.

Emrys came to stand beside her. He didn't say anything about the plane, which she was grateful for, because anything he said about the plane was going to start a fight.

"The boat," she said instead.

"There's an aluminum boat at the lower end of the camp. Twelve-foot, flat-bottomed. I saw it when—" He stopped himself, and she let him. "If the outboard's still there, and the lower station's still standing, it's a day's run downriver. Maybe two, with the current like this."

"With the current like this, it's a coffin."

"With the current like this," Emrys said, "it's the only door out of the room."

She looked downriver, into the drowned valley, at the tops of trees standing in floodwater all the way to the horizon like the masts of a sunken fleet. Somewhere down there was a lower station, and a missing team, and an unfinished sentence in a burned log, and a man named Voss whose soft voice had just acquired a second meaning, and she understood that the storm hadn't trapped her in the valley.

The valley had been holding the door shut the whole time. The storm had just let her see it.

"Show me the boat," she said.
