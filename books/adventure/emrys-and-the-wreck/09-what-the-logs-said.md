## 9. What the Logs Said

The rain came down on the survey camp's corrugated roof like a verdict being read aloud, syllable by syllable, and Emrys Vale sat with his hands flat on a table that had once been his and listened to it.

Odile was across the room, going through the footlockers with the methodical economy of someone who had spent her life making small inventories—fuel, food, rope, medicine—and he watched her without meaning to. She moved like a woman who trusted her hands more than her luck. There was a smear of mud along one jaw where she'd wiped at her face and missed. The lamp between them guttered each time the wind shouldered the walls, and in its stuttering light she looked younger than she had on the airstrip, and more tired, and more alone, though she would have denied all three.

He should tell her. That was the thought that kept arriving and kept being turned away at the door, like a beggar no one would admit. *Tell her what you are. Tell her why you were standing in the reeds when her wheels touched down.* But telling her meant becoming something other than useful, and useful was the only currency he had left. A man who could patch a hull and read the water and keep his mouth shut was worth keeping alive. A man who said *I drew the maps that drowned this valley* was worth something else entirely.

"Half of these are burned," Odile said, not looking up. "Someone went through them with a stove."

"Someone did." His voice came out level. He'd practiced level for months now, in the reeds, in the hollow trees, in the long nights of listening to the rain and counting the things he would never say. "The field logs. The daily instrument readings. What's left is mostly requisitions and ration tallies."

She glanced up. Her eyes were gray-green, the color of the river after a storm, and they had a way of landing on him that felt less like looking than like weighing. "You know these logs."

"I told you. I'm a survivor of the team."

"You told me you're a survivor." She closed the locker. "You didn't tell me which part of surviving you were."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the monsoon pressed against the camp as if it wanted in, as if it had been sent to collect something. Emrys looked at the table—at the ghost-rings left by mugs, at the scorch mark near the corner where Aldous Reyes had set a pan down too hot and laughed about it—and the past opened under him the way the ground had opened under Kestrel Reach, without warning and without mercy.

He had been twenty-nine years old and certain of everything except himself.

* * *

The ministry's regional office had smelled of floor wax and old ambition. Emrys remembered standing in it with his portfolio under his arm, three years of survey work bound in oilcloth, waiting while Undersecretary Corven read the summary page with the expression of a man reading a menu.

"The foundations," Corven said at last, tapping the page. "You've flagged them."

"I have." Emrys had rehearsed this. He'd rehearsed it on the riverboat up from the coast, on the trail, in the tent at night while the others slept. "The karst under the east abutment is riddled with solution channels. My borings show voids at eleven and fourteen meters. The dam as designed will settle unevenly under first filling. It won't fail catastrophically—not immediately—but it will seep, and the seepage will widen the channels, and within five to eight years you'll have an uncontrolled loss of the reservoir through the foundation itself."

Corven turned a page. Turned another. Somewhere down the hall a telephone rang and rang and stopped.

"You understand," Corven said mildly, "what this means financially."

Emrys had understood many things in those days and misjudged nearly all of them. He understood geology the way priests understand scripture. He understood that the Kestrel Reach dam was the largest public works project in the province's history, that two hundred villages downstream had been promised flood control, that the valley upstream—the villages of Lower Sarn, of Telfer's Landing, of the little church with the tin roof whose congregation sang louder than any he'd ever heard—was already condemned to drowning, purchased, evacuated, emptied. He understood that the money had been borrowed against futures that included the dam working.

What he had not understood was that Corven's question was not a question. It was a door being shown to him.

"There are remediation options," Emrys said. "A grout curtain through the karst. Deepening the cutoff wall. It adds cost—"

"It adds eighteen months and forty percent over budget." Corven closed the portfolio with the soft finality of a coffin lid. "And it tells every creditor we weren't sure. Mr. Vale, your borings are noted. Your concerns are noted. The engineering review board will consider them."

The board considered them for six days. When the maps came back to him, they came back changed.

He had drawn the east abutment in careful hatching, the voids marked in red, a note in his own hand across the margin: *Foundation unstable under design load. Remediation required prior to first filling.* The returned map bore a wide bar of red ink struck through his note—not a correction, not a counter-argument, just a line drawn through the truth the way you draw a line through a name. In the margin above it, in a hand he did not recognize: *Noted. Proceed per schedule.*

Redlined. That was the word the draftsmen used, laughing, for anything the ministry killed. Redline the wetlands. Redline the access road. Redline the village cemetery because graves were cheaper to move than concrete was to move around. Emrys had heard the word a hundred times and never felt its weight until he saw it lying across his own handwriting like a boot print across a garden.

He protested. That much was true and he clung to it in the dark hours, the way a drowning man clings to a plank that is visibly too small. He wrote a formal dissent. He took it to the chief engineer, who read it and said, "Vale, do you know how many men worked on this design? You're asking me to tell them their life's work sits on a sponge." He took it to Corven, who received him graciously and offered him a transfer to the coastal mapping program—a promotion, effectively, out of the reach of anything he might see or say. He took it, finally, to a journalist named Ilsa Brandt, who bought him dinner, listened for three hours, and then published nothing, because her editor's brother-in-law held the construction bond.

After that, the protests thinned. After that, there was only the work, and the work was beautiful, and beauty is the most seductive anesthetic ever devised. He mapped the reservoir basin before the flooding—every contour of the valley that would become the lakebed, every homestead, every orchard, the church with the tin roof rendered in his precise, loving hand. He drew the drowned world in advance. He told himself it was documentation. It was, though he would take years to admit it, a kind of rehearsal.

First filling began in the late dry season, ahead of schedule, because schedules were the only scripture the ministry read. The water rose. The gauges in the gallery behind the dam face began, quietly, to tick upward—seepage, measured in liters per minute, then tens of liters, then hundreds. Emrys saw the readings because he was still the man who made the maps, and the monitoring network was his, and nobody thought it mattered what the mapmaker noticed.

He noticed. He filed a memo. The memo was redlined.

Then the rains came early, and the river came up fast, and on the fourth night of the early rains the east abutment settled nine centimeters in six hours, and the seepage turned from clear to cloudy—which every engineer knows is the sound of a foundation eating itself—and the order went out not to evacuate but to *monitor*, because evacuation meant admitting, and admitting meant bonds called due, and bonds called due meant everything.

The survey team was in the valley that night. Eleven of them, doing a final bathymetric pass of the new reservoir, measuring what had already been drowned. Emrys was among them because he had asked to be—because some part of him wanted to stand on the water he had drawn and feel, at last, the full weight of what his lines had become.

The abutment let go at 3:17 in the morning.

He would never forget the sound, though he had tried. Not a roar—that was the lie the newspapers told afterward, the comfortable lie of explosions. It was a sigh. A vast, geological exhale, as if the mountain beneath the dam had been holding its breath for a hundred thousand years and finally, gratefully, let it out. Then the water came off the reservoir in a wall, and the wall came down the valley, and everything Emrys had ever drawn in ink became, in nineteen minutes, a memory of a shape.

Eleven men and women had gone into the valley to measure a drowned world. Four walked out. Emrys Vale was one of them, because he had been on the high bank taking a bearing when the water took the boat, and he had stood in the rain with a theodolite in his hands and watched the lights of the survey launch go under, and he had done nothing, because there was nothing to do, and *nothing* was the heaviest thing he had ever carried.

The inquiry afterward was brief and clean. Act of God, the finding said. Unprecedented rainfall. Foundation conditions could not reasonably have been anticipated. Emrys read the report and saw his own borings quoted in it—quoted selectively, the voids described as *minor localized features*—and understood that the redline had not been an ending but a beginning. They had not merely ignored his maps. They had digested them. His truth had been processed into their lie, and now his own handwriting testified, in the official record, against everything he knew.

That was when he took the originals. The unredlined maps, the boring logs, the seepage memos, the settlement readings from the last week—all of it, copied in the drafting room over three sleepless nights, sealed in oilcloth and waterproof tubes. He told no one. Telling people got them published-over, or transferred, or drowned. He carried the evidence out of the capital in a surveyor's instrument case, and he carried it into the bush, and he became a rumor, and then a ghost, and then a man standing in flooded reeds watching a supply plane come down through the rain with a stranger at the controls who flew like the terrain was a language she'd been born speaking.

* * *

"—and the fuel drums are intact, which is something." Odile straightened, wiping her hands on her trousers. "Emrys."

He surfaced. The lamp steadied. The rain went on reading its verdict.

"You went somewhere," she said.

"Took stock."

"Mm." She crossed to the table and sat opposite him, close enough that he could see the fine cracks in the skin of her knuckles, the weathering of a woman who lived outdoors and paid for it honestly. "I've been taking stock too. Of you." She said it without apology. "You knew the camp layout in the dark. You knew which locker had the medical kit before I found the stencil. And just now, when I said *fuel drums*, you flinched—not at the word. At the number. You know how many drums this camp was supposed to hold."

"Fourteen," he said quietly.

"Fourteen," she agreed. "So here's my question, and I'm going to ask it once, and then I'm going to drop it, because we're trapped together until this water drops and I'd rather not spend the whole storm circling it." She leaned forward, elbows on the scarred wood. "Who burned the logs?"

The question hung between them. Outside, a gust hit the eastern wall hard enough to make the whole structure groan, and somewhere in the dark a sheet of loose roofing tore free and slapped away into the rain.

"They were burned before I got back here," Emrys said. Which was true. Which was also, he knew even as he said it, a fortress built out of a technicality.

"Before you got *back*." Odile's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about her anger, when it showed—it came down instead of up, settling like silt, clouding everything. "So you were here. Before. With them. The team that's missing."

"Odile—"

"No. You don't get my name in your mouth like we're friends yet." She wasn't cruel. She was precise, and precision hurt worse. "I flew supplies to this survey twice. I met four of them. A big quiet geologist who paid for my coffee at the trading post because I'd forgotten my wallet. A woman named Sefu who talked about her daughter the whole flight up. Now I'm sitting in their kitchen and the kitchen's been searched and the logs are ash, and the only person in fifty miles of floodwater is a man who knows how many fuel drums there were." She held his eyes. "I landed here because Command ordered me to. I stayed because the sky closed. But I am not staying silent because you've decided silence is safer for both of us. So. One time. Who burned them?"

Emrys looked at his hands. They were steady. He had trained them to be steady through worse than this.

"Roque," he said. "Ansel Roque. The security chief. He came through the camp ten days ago with four men and a stove, and he burned the field logs, and he told me—" The sentence snagged on something. He pulled it through. "He told me that the ministry's position was that the survey never found anything worth reporting, and that survivors who agreed with that position would be compensated, and survivors who didn't would be listed as casualties of the flood. There's paperwork either way. Roque is very good at paperwork."

Odile sat back slowly. "You've seen him since the flood."

"He's seen me." Emrys almost smiled, and it came out crooked. "Twice. Both times from a distance I chose. He has dogs, and boats, and a radio net, and the patience of a man being paid by the month. I have a waterproof tube and a bad knee."

"A tube." Her eyes narrowed, and he watched her assemble it—the careful answers, the flinch at the drum count, the way he'd stepped out of the reeds rather than away from the plane. "Of documents."

He said nothing. The rain filled the silence for him, patient and total.

"That's why you're here," she said. "Not hiding from the flood. Hiding from *him*. Carrying whatever's in that tube like it's the last copy of something." She exhaled through her nose, and for a moment she looked less angry than exhausted, and somehow that was worse. "I should have left you in the reeds."

"Yes," Emrys said. "You should have."

"But the sky closed, and here we are." She pushed back from the table and stood, and stood there a moment, looking at the ruined lockers, the scorched table, the window where the black water of the rising flood was already lapping at the sill of a camp that had once held eleven living people and a coffee-drinking geologist with a daughter's photograph in his kit. "Get some sleep if you can. First light, I want to look at the runway again. If the water's dropped even a hand's width, I'm getting us out of here."

"And if it hasn't?"

She paused at the doorway, the lamp throwing her shadow long across the floorboards, out toward the dark water.

"Then we're going to need a boat," Odile Blackwood said, "and I don't see one."

Emrys waited until her breathing slowed in the far bunk, until the rain had covered every other sound. Then he reached into his jacket and rested his palm, briefly, on the oilcloth tube strapped against his ribs—his maps, his truth, his dead weight—and listened to the flood climbing the pilings beneath the camp, inch by patient inch, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.
