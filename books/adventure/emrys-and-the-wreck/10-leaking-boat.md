## 10. Leaking Boat

The radio died the way most things died in Kestrel Reach—slowly at first, then all at once.

Odile had been nursing it since dawn, since the first storm bands came through and turned the sky the color of wet slate. She'd stripped the panel cover with a screwdriver worn smooth by her father's hand, dried what she could reach with a rag gone gray from use, and held the handset against her ear like a shell she hoped might still hold an ocean. Static answered her. Then less than static. Then nothing at all—a silence so complete she could hear the water lapping at the tires of the Cessna on wheels, hear it rising, patient as arithmetic.

"Nothing?" Emrys stood in the doorway of the wrecked survey camp's main hut, rain streaming off his shoulders. He'd found a tarp somewhere and rigged it badly; it shed water down his back instead of off him.

"Dead." She set the handset down with more care than she felt. Care was a habit. You didn't throw things when you were alone with your own equipment for months at a stretch; you learned to set them down gently because there was no one to help you replace them. "Antenna's probably grounded through the mast. Even if I could get up there in this wind, the set itself took water through the vent seams. It's not a repair. It's a funeral."

"So no one knows where we are."

"No one knows anything." She looked out through the broken window at the strip. The runway—if you could still call it that—had been a shelf of packed clay above the flood line two days ago. Now the river had come up over its southern third, brown and muscular, carrying branches and a plastic drum and something pale she chose not to identify. Her plane sat on the high ground still, but the high ground was shrinking by the hour, and the water was coming for it the way debt came for people: inevitably, and with interest.

Emrys came into the hut properly, ducking under the lintel. He moved like a man used to low ceilings and tight spaces, which struck her as odd for a cartographer. She'd have expected someone who spent his life drawing open country to stand taller. But he folded himself onto the bench across from her, elbows on knees, and studied her face with that careful attention of his—the attention of a man reading terrain.

"You're doing the math," he said.

"I'm always doing the math."

"The plane floats for a while if we lash it down. The pontoons aren't fitted, but the fuselage will ride high long enough—"

"It's not rated for it." She cut him off, then heard how sharp she'd sounded and made herself soften. "Sorry. It's just—everyone has an opinion about my plane until they've paid for a wing spar. If she floods, she's done. And she's all I've got that's mine."

He absorbed that without flinching. "Then we keep her dry," he said, "and we find another way out."

* * *

The other way out was lying half-sunk in the reeds forty meters below the camp, and it was leaking.

They'd spotted it that morning from the ridge behind the huts: a flat-bottomed aluminum boat, survey-stenciled along the gunwale, wedged among the drowned grasses with its stern awash. Getting to it meant wading chest-deep through water that moved with a slow lateral current, and Odile did it with her knife between her teeth because she'd seen what the flooded forest did to people who went in empty-handed. The current wasn't strong yet. It would be, when the monsoon wall finished assembling itself over the western ridges and came down the valley like a verdict.

The boat was sound enough underneath—no holed plating, no torn rivet lines—but the transom seam had opened a finger's width where it had taken some impact, and the drain plug was gone. Water sloshed in the bilge, cold and tea-colored, full of drowned leaves.

"She'll float," Emrys said, hauling the bow over a submerged fence post. "She won't float *well*, but she'll float."

"That's what I said about my last relationship."

He glanced at her, surprised into something almost like a smile. It changed his whole face, that flicker—made him look younger, and more tired, both at once. Then it was gone, banked again, and he was all business, tipping the boat to drain what they could before dragging it up onto the remains of the camp's old loading ramp.

Odile crouched at the transom and ran her thumb along the split seam. Aluminum didn't forgive. Wood swelled, canvas stretched, but aluminum just gave up, quietly, and stayed given-up. She knew about things that stayed given-up.

"We need epoxy," she said. "Or JB Weld. Or tar. Or—" She stopped, because Emrys was already moving toward the supply hut with the particular stride of a man who knew exactly which shelf his answer lived on.

The survey camp had been picked over—she'd noticed that yesterday, the drawers hung open, the food stores scattered, the radios smashed with a deliberateness that still bothered her more than she let herself say—but whoever had ransacked the place hadn't bothered with the maintenance locker bolted inside the fuel shed. Emrys had. He came out with a tin of marine epoxy, a roll of self-amalgamating tape, a hacksaw blade, and a coil of copper wire, and laid them out on the ramp like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Boats leak. That's most of what I know about boats." He knelt and began cleaning the seam with a rag and the dregs of a bottle of acetone, working methodically, his hands steady. Rain drummed on the tin roof of the fuel shed overhang, and beneath that rhythm she could hear the river, deeper now, a note lower than it had been at dawn.

She should have been watching the water level. Instead she watched his hands.

There was a competence in them that matched hers, and that unsettled her more than anything else about him. She'd spent six years being the only competent person in every room she entered—flying medicine into settlements where the nurse was also the schoolteacher was also the undertaker, fixing her own engine on gravel bars with a multi-tool and profanity—and she'd built her whole life on the assumption that nobody would ever show up who could do what she did. Solitude as armor. Nobody can let you down if nobody can reach you. And here was this quiet stranger kneeling in the rain, mixing epoxy in a tin lid with the exact ratio of hardener to resin her father had taught her, humming almost inaudibly under his breath, and the armor had a crack in it shaped exactly like him.

"Hardener's going," he said. "Hold the tape end for me?"

She held it. Their fingers touched over the transom, brief and accidental, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them were aware of it, the way you're aware of a change in barometric pressure.

* * *

The work took the afternoon, and the afternoon took the runway.

They worked in shifts of a kind that weren't really shifts—they simply fell into a rhythm, one holding while the other pressed, one passing while the other fastened, trading places without discussion when an arm tired or the rain drove them under the shed. Emrys taped the seam first, stretching the amalgamating tape around the transom in overlapping turns, then bedded the epoxy over it and wired a backing plate of scrap aluminum across the joint, cinching the wire twists with pliers until his knuckles went white.

"Twist it twice more," Odile said. "Wire always works loose in cold water."

He twisted it twice more. Then he paused, wire cutters hovering, and looked at her sideways. "You've patched boats."

"I've patched everything. Boats, engines, roofs, reputations." She took the pliers from him and showed him the trick—setting the twist so the tension locked against itself rather than against the soft metal. "My father believed anything could be fixed if you refused to accept that it couldn't. He was wrong about a lot of things, but he was right about that often enough to keep me believing it."

"What was he wrong about?"

She kept her eyes on the wire. "Money," she said finally. "Mostly money. He could fix anything except his own accounts." She handed the pliers back. "That's not a story for a leaking boat."

"It's exactly a story for a leaking boat," Emrys said quietly. "Leaking boats are where the true ones come out. Nobody lies well while they're bailing."

She laughed despite herself—a short, rusty sound, like an engine catching on the third try. When had she last laughed? She genuinely couldn't remember, and that frightened her a little, in a distant way, the way you're frightened by a room you realize you haven't left in years.

They bailed together after that, scooping the seepage with a cut-down jerry can, and the rhythm of it—scoop, pass, dump, scoop—became a kind of conversation that didn't require words. Somewhere in the middle of it the light began to fail, not because evening was coming but because the storm wall had arrived in earnest, a bruise-colored mass swallowing the western ridgeline, and the temperature dropped five degrees in twenty minutes, and the river rose another handspan.

Odile walked up the ramp to check the plane. She told herself it was a practical inspection. It was also a ritual, and rituals were what you had instead of prayers.

The Cessna sat on ground that was still ground, barely. The southern tie-down point stood in two inches of water. She checked the anchors, doubled the lines, chocked the wheels with the spare tire and a length of driftwood, and stood for a moment with her palm flat against the cold fuselage, feeling the metal like a pulse.

"Hold on," she told it. "I'm not losing you too."

Behind her, Emrys had stopped working and was watching her do it. She felt his gaze the way you feel weather changing. She didn't turn around. Some things you did in private even when you weren't in private, and loving an airplane was one of them.

* * *

By full dark the boat was patched, provisioned, and floating—which is to say, mostly floating, riding with two inches of freeboard and a slow seep at the port chine that they agreed to call a character flaw.

They beached it against the loading ramp and sat under the fuel shed overhang, sharing a pot of the survey team's abandoned instant coffee, which tasted like hot cardboard and was, under the circumstances, the finest thing either of them had ever drunk. The rain came down in ropes. Lightning walked along the far ridge, silent, counting something.

"The water's over the south marker," Odile said. "Another day like today and it takes the mid-strip. Day after that, it's at the plane's wheels."

"And after that?"

"After that, she floats or she doesn't." She wrapped both hands around the tin cup. "If she floats, she'll be battered against the trees before the flood crests. If she doesn't, she's a reef. Either way, flying out isn't happening. Not from here."

Emrys nodded slowly. He'd known. She could see that he'd known since morning, maybe since before she'd landed, and had let her work through it herself rather than telling her. She decided to be grateful for that instead of angry, and found, to her surprise, that gratitude came easier than usual.

"So we go downriver," she said. "In the leaky boat. Toward the lower station."

"Toward the lower station." He turned his cup in his hands. "It's two days' run in good water. In this, three, maybe four. Drowned forest most of the way—you can't see the banks, only the crowns of the trees, and the channels move. You'll need to navigate."

"With what? My compass and your memory?" She meant it lightly. It didn't come out lightly, because saying it aloud reminded her of what he was still not telling her—how a cartographer came to be hiding alone in a flooded valley where his entire team had vanished, why he watched her plane land from inside the reeds like a man deciding whether to exist.

"Yes," he said simply. "Your compass and my memory."

The lightning counted higher. Somewhere out in the drowned dark, something heavy shifted in the current—a log, a section of bank, a house—and the river swallowed it without comment.

Odile looked at the boat, riding its tether against the ramp, taking on its slow dishonest inches of water. Two days ago she had been a woman who flew supply runs and kept her head down and owed a number. Now she was a woman with a dead radio, a drowning airstrip, a stranger whose silences had shapes, and a boat that leaked. Every plan she'd ever survived by—stay small, stay solo, stay owed-to-nobody-but-Voss-and-that-was-business—was dissolving in the rising water, and what remained felt terrifyingly like the beginning of trust.

Not trust yet. Trust earned through work, and they'd only worked one day.

But when Emrys said, "You should sleep. I'll take the first watch on the bilge," and when she found herself actually considering it—actually weighing the possibility of closing her eyes in the presence of another person, which she had not done in longer than she could calculate—she understood that something had already shifted, quietly, the way the river rose. Not dramatically. Just past a line that couldn't be uncrossed.

"All right," she said. "Wake me if the seam opens."

"I'll wake you if it even thinks about opening."

She lay down on the survey cot with her boots on and her father's compass in her pocket, and listened to the rain hammering the tin roof, and to the smaller, steadier sounds of Emrys bailing the boat in the dark—scoop, pass, dump—and slept better than she had any right to.

She woke once, near midnight, to find him standing at the edge of the overhang looking downriver, motionless, his silhouette sharp against a gap in the rain. Following his gaze, she saw nothing—only black water and blacker trees.

But he was standing the way a man stands when he's watching for something specific.

And far downstream, so faint she might have invented it, a light blinked twice, and went dark.
