## 11. Downriver

The rain had settled into a steady gray whisper by the time they pushed off from the drowned runway, and Odile decided almost immediately that she hated the boat.

Not the way she hated the debt, which was a cold, ledgered thing with a face and a name. She hated the boat the way a bird hates a cage—because it was slow, because it sat in the water instead of above it, because every inch of forward progress had to be argued for, oar against current, hull against snag. The survey's aluminum skiff rode low in the stern where Emrys had loaded the fuel cans and the salvaged gear, and it leaked the way old boats leak: not dramatically, but constantly, a thin cold tongue of water finding the seam near the keel and pooling around her boots.

"Bail every ten minutes," Emrys said, settling onto the middle thwart with the second oar. "I'd have patched it better, but the sealant was in the tent that went downstream."

"You mean the tent you were hiding behind when I landed."

"The tent that went downstream," he repeated, without inflection.

Odile shipped her oar and looked at him. He was watching the water ahead, not the water around them, and his hands moved on the oar grips with a competence that didn't match the story he'd given her—that he was a survivor who'd wandered out of the flood zone, that he didn't know where the rest of the team was, that the burned logs in the camp office were a mystery to him too. He rowed like a man who had spent years reading water. He'd patched the hull like a man who'd been taught by someone patient. And when she'd asked his name he'd given it too quickly, *Emrys Vale*, the way people give a name they've been rehearsing.

"Which way?" she asked, because the question she actually wanted to ask wasn't ready yet.

He lifted his chin toward the drowned forest ahead. The flood had swallowed the riverbanks whole; the trees stood to their shoulders in brown water, their crowns making a broken green roof that stretched to the gray limit of sight. Somewhere under that water was a road, a fence line, a village, a valley floor where people had lived. Somewhere under it was the runway she'd landed on yesterday, already three feet deeper.

"The lower station's on the old river channel," he said. "The flood's turned everything into one big lake, but the channel's still the deepest water. If we stay in it, we're in the current's favor. If we stray into the timber—" he nodded at a half-submerged fence post sliding past, its wire still strung and humming faintly in the current, "—we wrap a propeller, or in our case, an oar, and then we're swimming."

"You know this country."

"I mapped it."

The words came out flat and final, a door closed, and Odile let it stay closed. For now. She took up her oar again and they pulled together, the boat sliding between two drowned guava trees, and she checked her father's compass where it sat open on the thwart beside her, the needle trembling toward north with a steadiness nothing else in her life had.

Her father had told her once, when she was small and the compass was already old, that a compass didn't point north. It pointed at all the things that weren't north, and you found your way by what you could eliminate. She'd thought it was a joke. Lately it felt like a philosophy.

* * *

They rowed in shifts through the morning, and the drowned forest did not thin. If anything it thickened, the trees standing closer as the old channel narrowed, their trunks black with waterline stain, their branches dripping. The silence under the canopy was total in a way that made Odile's skin crawl. No birds. No monkeys. The flood had emptied the country of everything that could leave, and everything that couldn't was under the water.

"Tell me about the lower station," she said, mostly to break the quiet.

"Supply depot for the survey. Radio relay. There was a crew of four—cook, mechanic, two radio techs." He rowed through a pause. "They're due to rotate out next week. If they're still there, we can raise the coast on their set. If they're not—"

"Then we take the boat down to the salt line and hope someone's watching the river mouth."

"Someone will be watching the river mouth." He said it quietly, and she caught the weight under it. "Question is who."

Odile bailed the boat, tipping the water over the side in slow, even scoops, and used the work to keep her face still. She'd flown supply runs for the government before. She knew the type of men who got posted to remote stations with private security contracts and no oversight. She knew what *who's watching* meant out here, where a missing survey team was a problem that could be solved with a match and a current.

"You said you were a survivor," she said. "Of the flood. But you didn't sound surprised yesterday, when we found the camp burned. You didn't ask me what happened to them. You asked me what I saw."

Emrys's oar kept its rhythm. One stroke. Two. The boat held its line between the drowned trunks.

"I asked what you saw," he said finally, "because I already know what happened. I just don't know how much of it has reached the water yet."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

She should have pushed. She felt the push sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone—*who are you, what did you do, why were you hiding in the reeds when my wheels touched down*—and she let it sit, because the boat was leaking, the forest was drowned, the storm had closed the valley behind them, and the man rowing her toward the only radio in fifty miles of floodwater was the only thing between her and swimming. Some questions you ask when you can afford the answer. She'd learned that from the debt, too.

Instead she said, "Left fork ahead. Channel bends north."

He glanced at her, and for the first time since the reeds, something that wasn't calculation moved through his face. "You can tell?"

"Water tells you. The main channel runs darker where it's deep, and the current combs the surface smooth. Side sloughs go green and still." She nodded at the fork. "Also, your map memory is good, but my compass is better, and my father's compass is better than both."

"Was," Emrys said. "You said *was* your father's."

The correction landed harder than she expected. Odile pulled her oar in and let the boat drift, the current taking them gently sideways toward a drowned kapok tree hung with gray moss.

"He flew these rivers too," she said. "Before the dam company bought up the valley. Before the survey. He ran medicine and mail and whatever else paid, and he kept his books in the same case as his compass, and when he died the books had a number in them that I've been paying down for four years." She looked up at the moss, the water, the gray sky. "So yes. *Was*. Everything of his is *was* now. Except the compass. And the debt."

Emrys was quiet for a long moment. The boat turned slowly in the current, the drowned trees sliding past.

"My father kept books too," he said. "Ledger clerk, in a company town up the coast. He used to say a ledger never lies, it just waits." He looked at her, and there was something raw under the careful surface of him, something that had been underwater a long time. "I used to think that was wisdom. Now I think it's a threat."

Despite herself—despite the leaking boat and the drowned forest and the man she didn't trust—Odile laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, and Emrys's mouth twitched, and for a moment they were just two tired people in a small boat, and the fear was a thing they were carrying together instead of separately.

"Row," she said. "You're drifting us into the kapok."

* * *

The rain came back at midday, a fine needle rain that hissed on the water and made the drowned forest look like something etched on glass. They rigged the survey tarp as a canopy, and under it they rowed and bailed and ate hardtack and tinned fish, and Odile learned things about Emrys Vale without asking a single question.

She learned that he took his coffee black and bitter and drank it like it was a duty. That he knew the names of trees—not the survey's plot numbers, but the actual names, the ones the old people used, and once, passing a drowned fig strung with rope-thick roots, he said a word in a language she didn't recognize, soft, like an apology. That he flinched—not visibly, but she was a pilot and pilots read micro-movements the way sailors read sky—every time they passed something man-made in the water. A fence post. A roof ridge, just breaking the surface, a drowned house with its chimney standing above the flood like a periscope. A church bell tower, tilted, its bell still hanging, silent.

"You knew them," she said, when the tower had slid past. "The people who lived here."

"Everyone in the survey knew the valley," he said. "We surveyed it. That was the job. You measure a place inch by inch for two years, you—"

"That's not what I asked."

His oar caught a submerged snag and the boat yawed, and he corrected it with a smoothness that told her the catch hadn't been an accident of water but an accident of him, of whatever had moved through him at her question. He brought them back into the channel and rowed in silence for a hundred meters.

"There was a village," he said at last. "Kestrel Landing. Four hundred people. The dam project bought them out—relocation compensation, ministry paperwork, the whole machinery. Most of them went. Some of them didn't want to go, and the ones who didn't want to go were—handled." The word came out of him like a splinter. "The survey wasn't supposed to be about the dam. It was supposed to be about the valley. But the dam was the reason the valley was being surveyed, and the maps we made were the maps the dam was built on, and every number on those maps—" He stopped. Rowed. Stopped. "Every number passed through my hands. I drew the contours. I drew the flood line. I drew the village as a dotted polygon labeled *cleared*."

The rain hissed. The boat leaked. Odile bailed it, ten scoops, twenty, giving him the time the work gave, because she understood about needing something to do with your hands when the truth was too big for them.

"And now the valley's drowned ahead of schedule," she said quietly. "And the survey team that found out why is missing."

Emrys said nothing. But his shoulders, which had been held in that careful cartographer's posture all day, came down half an inch, and Odile understood that she had just been told yes, in the only language he could manage yet.

* * *

Toward evening the drowned forest opened, and they came out onto a wide brown sheet of water where the old channel spread into what had been farmland. The current slackened. The sky to the west was a bruise-colored smear where the storm cell that had trapped them was grinding away south, and Odile found herself doing the thing she always did when the flying was bad—calculating. Distance to the lower station: maybe eight kilometers, by Emrys's dead reckoning. Fuel: irrelevant, no engine. Light: two hours, maybe less. Water in the hull: managed, not solved.

"We should make the station by dark," Emrys said. "If it's still standing."

"And if the crew's still there."

"And if the radio still works."

"And if whoever burned the survey camp isn't sitting in the boathouse waiting for us." She said it lightly, and it didn't come out light, and she saw him register that.

"The men who burned the camp," Emrys said, "are not the men who signed the charter that put you in that plane. But they work for the same people. And the people they work for have a very simple problem, which is that the flood was supposed to be an act of God, and there are documents in this valley that say otherwise." He looked at her across the tarp's shadow. "You flew the resupply run. Your name is on their manifest. You landed in a closed flood zone. As far as they're concerned, you're either a witness or a loose end."

"I'm a supply pilot."

"Today you are. Today is ending."

Odile bailed the boat. Ten scoops. Twenty. The water came back. She thought about her father's compass on the thwart, needle steady, pointing at all the things that weren't north. She thought about the debt ledger in her flight bag, Harrow Voss's neat handwriting, the wet season deadline, the plane that wasn't really hers. She thought about how she had spent four years making herself small enough to slip through the world without being noticed, and how in two days she had crashed into a flood, been ordered down by command, salvaged a boat with a stranger, and was now rowing toward a station that might be an ambush, and every part of it had happened without her permission, and every part of it had happened anyway.

"Emrys." She said his name for the first time since the reeds, and he looked up. "When we get to the station, whatever's there—whatever you're not telling me—you tell me. Before I have to find out the way I found out about the burned logs. Because I've spent four years being lied to by men with ledgers, and I'm not going to spend the fifth one being lied to by a man with an oar."

The rain ticked on the tarp. The boat leaked. Somewhere behind them, the drowned valley held its dead and its documents, and somewhere ahead, the lower station held its dark windows and its silent radio.

"All right," Emrys said.

Just that. But his oar found the water again, and the boat came about, and the compass needle held its line toward the station, toward the dark, toward whatever was waiting there—and for the first time since the runway flooded, Odile felt the current was carrying them somewhere she had chosen too.

They rowed into the last of the light.

Ahead, where the channel bent toward the lower station, a single point of light appeared on the water's edge—steady, white, and wrong. A lantern, hung where no lantern should be, burning at the exact height of a dock post, in a station whose crew should have shown themselves by now.

Emrys's oar stopped mid-stroke.

"That's not how they signal," he said.
