## 12. The Drowned Village

The river widened the way grief does—gradually, then all at once.

Odile noticed it first in the trees. For an hour they had threaded drowned forest where the trunks rose gray and patient out of brown water, their crowns still green, still alive in that stubborn way of things that refuse to admit they've been killed. But now the canopy thinned. The water spread flat and featureless to both horizons, and the current slowed until it barely seemed to move at all, a vast held breath between the hills.

She sat in the stern with her father's compass open on her knee and the survey boat's patched hull whispering beneath her, and she watched Emrys at the oars and understood, without knowing how she knew, that he had gone somewhere she couldn't follow.

He'd been talking for most of the morning—not much, but enough. Enough to keep her from counting the leaks. He'd named the bends before they came to them: *the channel dog-legs left past the dead fig, then you'll want to hug the eastern bank or the silt bar will kiss your keel.* And it was true, every time, true the way only memory is true, and she'd found herself almost comfortable, almost easy, trading small observations back and forth across the gunwale like two people who had done this sort of thing all their lives.

Then the trees stopped, and so did his voice.

"Emrys." She said it quietly, because the silence out here had texture, weight, and she didn't want to tear it carelessly. "Which way from here?"

He pulled one stroke, then let the oars rest. His eyes moved over the water the way a man reads a letter he already knows by heart—the way she read her father's handwriting on the debt note, over and over, looking for some clause she'd missed.

"Straight," he said. "There's no *way* anymore. There's just—" He stopped. Started again. "Keep the compass on two-seven-five. The steeple will come up on the port side."

"The what?"

He didn't answer. He rowed.

* * *

The steeple came up on the port side forty minutes later, and Odile's breath went out of her in a long slow leak, like air from a tire in deep water.

It stood perhaps nine feet above the surface—a white wooden spire, weathered to the color of old bone, tipped slightly east as if leaning to listen. Below it, the church itself was entirely submerged; only the cross-topped peak remained, and around it, scattered across a quarter mile of flat water, the other evidence of a town that had once been here: the ridge of a roofline breaking the surface like a whale's back. A chimney standing alone, brick-red, impossibly domestic. The top six feet of a ceiba tree that had been allowed to stay, its lower trunk fathoms down, its crown still leafing each spring into air that no one breathed.

"Oh," Odile said. It wasn't a word. It was just the sound a person makes when the world rearranges itself.

"Santo Nube," Emrys said. "Population, four hundred and eleven, last census. Two hundred and six households. One school, one clinic, one church, three cantinas, and a football pitch that flooded every wet season anyway, so half the town claimed moving them was no great hardship." His voice was level. Too level. She'd heard pilots use that voice on the radio when the engine was running rough and they didn't want the passengers to know. "The government called it voluntary resettlement."

"And it wasn't."

"It was money," he said. "Money and men with clipboards and a schedule. Some families took the payment and went. Some held out. The ones who held out got letters, and then visits, and then the letters stopped coming in their language." He shipped the oars and let the boat drift, and the current, weak as it was, turned them slowly toward the steeple as if the drowned place wanted a closer look at them. "The dam was supposed to take five more years. The valley was supposed to fill slowly. People would have had time to dig up their dead, at least."

*At least.* The words hung there.

"You're from here," Odile said. Not a question. She'd been assembling it all morning without letting herself look at the whole shape—the way he knew every bend, the carefulness of his answers, the particular quality of his silence now, which wasn't the silence of a man hiding something but of a man carrying something too heavy to set down.

"My mother's family," he said. "The house is under us right now. About there." He nodded at a patch of water indistinguishable from any other patch, brown-green, flat, reflecting clouds. "My mother refused the resettlement payment on principle. My father took it. They fought about it for a year, and then the water came early, and the argument stopped mattering, which is the government's whole method if you think about it. Don't win the argument. Just make it irrelevant."

Odile looked down through the murk as though she might see something—roof tiles, a doorway, the ghost of a kitchen. She saw nothing but tea-colored water and her own reflection, faint, distorted, a woman-shaped smudge hovering over a town.

"What happened to them?" she asked, and then immediately wished she could unask it, because his face did something complicated and brief, a door opening and shutting in the wind.

"They were relocated to the hill settlements above the reach. My mother died three years ago—pneumonia, the clinics up there are a joke, one nurse for six villages. My father—" He picked the oars up again, needing his hands busy; she recognized that, knew it from her own body, the way stillness became unbearable when the thing inside you got too loud. "My father stayed on as a laborer on the dam itself. Built the wall that drowned his own argument. He says it pays better than farming ever did. He says other things too, sometimes, when he drinks."

The boat moved. The steeple slid aft, and Odile kept her eyes on it as long as she could, because turning away felt like a kind of agreement.

* * *

They passed the school an hour later—or rather, passed the place where the school's roof ridge broke the surface, and someone, years ago, had lashed a float buoy to it. Maybe a marker for boats. Maybe a memorial. Maybe just a fisherman's convenience. It bobbed gently in their wake, orange paint faded to the color of a healing wound.

Emrys hadn't spoken since the church. Odile had filled the silence herself at first—pointing out a kingfisher, commenting on the water level, offering the small chatter of a person who can't stand a quiet cockpit—but he gave nothing back, not even the polite grunt, and eventually she let it go and let the silence be what it was. She knew silences like this. She'd lived inside one for four years, since the lawyer read her father's will and the debt note fell out of it like a trapdoor.

But hers had been a silence about money. His was about people. And watching him row—watching the set of his shoulders, the fixed middle distance of his gaze—she began to understand that what he carried wasn't only guilt about maps and redlines and signatures. It was this. Every drowned rooftop between here and the coast was a name to him. Maybe several names. Maybe, she thought, watching his jaw work once, twice, and go still—maybe one name in particular.

"There," he said suddenly, and his voice cracked on the single syllable like ice under a boot.

He was looking at the water off the starboard bow, where a shape rose that wasn't a roofline. It was a tree—but a young one, a mango, planted shallow in what had been a dooryard, and the flood had killed it slowly instead of quickly, and now its bare branches stood above the surface like a skeleton's fingers, and caught in those branches, thirty feet up where the waterline had once been higher, hung the remains of a hammock.

Rotted, sun-bleached, mostly gone. But the shape of it was unmistakable. Someone had strung a hammock between the mango tree and something else—something now underwater—and the flood had risen around them while the hammock waited, and the rope had held, and the cloth had held, long after whoever slept in it had gone.

Emrys shipped the oars. His hands were shaking. He tried to disguise it as adjusting his grip and failed completely.

"Emrys."

"Don't." The word came out raw. He closed his eyes. Opened them. "I'm sorry. Keep rowing. I'll—"

"You're not rowing. I am." She reached over and took the oars from him, and he let her, which told her more than anything he'd said all day. She brought the boat around in a slow arc, giving the mango tree a respectful distance—not close enough to gawk, not far enough to seem afraid—and rowed on while he sat in the bow with his forearms on his knees and his head bowed, and the drowned village slid past them house by invisible house.

"I used to fly mail here," she said, after a while. Quietly. Not demanding anything. Just putting something into the silence so he wouldn't have to carry all of it alone. "Before the evacuation orders. Medicine runs, mostly. There was a midwife in a village up the Serrano branch—Doña Ines—who delivered half the babies in the district and paid me in coffee and gossip. I never learned where they moved her. I tell myself she's fine. I don't actually know."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're doing the thing I do," she said. "The thing where you decide you're the only one who remembers, so you have to remember hard enough for everybody, alone, forever. It doesn't work. Believe me. I've tried it. All it does is make the remembering heavier."

He looked up at her then, and in the flat silver light his eyes were the color of the water itself, and there was something in them that hadn't been there before—not trust exactly, not yet, but the recognition of a fellow carrier. Two mules meeting on the same trail, each recognizing the load on the other's back.

"Her name was Luz," he said. "Luz Ferreira. She taught at the school." He nodded toward the buoy-marked roofline, already astern. "She stayed until the last week. Helped catalog the graves for relocation—every headstone, every name, transcribed by hand so the records couldn't lose anybody. Four hundred and eleven people, and she made sure every one of them was written down somewhere the water couldn't go." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Then she got on the barge with the last of them, and I got on the plane with my maps, and we wrote to each other for two years, and then the letters got shorter, and then they stopped, and I told myself it was the postal service."

"And was it?"

"I don't know." He said it simply, and the simplicity was worse than any answer. "That's the part I can't forgive myself for. Not the maps—I know what happened with the maps, I know whose fault that was, I've had years to build the case against them, it's airtight, I could argue it in my sleep. But Luz. I could have gone back. After the survey started, before everything—I flew over this valley a dozen times and I never once landed. Never asked. Because I was busy, because it hurt, because—" He shook his head. "Because it was easier to let the water have her than to find out whether it had."

Odile rowed. The oars creaked in the locks, a rhythm older than either of them, and the drowned town gave way to open water, and the hills of the lower reach began to gather themselves along the horizon.

"She might be fine," Odile said at last. "People survive being forgotten. It happens every day. It's practically the national industry."

That startled a sound out of him—half laugh, half something else—and he scrubbed a hand over his face and sat up straighter, and she watched him put himself back together piece by piece, the way she'd watched pilots do it after bad landings, checking each rivet.

"When this is over," he said, "if there is an *over*—I'm going to find out. Whatever it costs. That's the promise I'm making right now, out loud, to you, so you can hold me to it."

"Noted," Odile said. "Witnessed and notarized. I charge for that, by the way. Standard rate."

"What's the standard rate?"

"Coffee and gossip. Same as Doña Ines."

This time the laugh was real, brief and rusty, dragged up from somewhere deep, and it changed his whole face, and Odile looked away quickly and pretended to check the compass because some things you shouldn't see too soon, some things need the fog to burn off gradually or they blind you.

* * *

Toward late afternoon the character of the country changed again. The open water narrowed into channels between low, forested hills, and the current picked up, tugging the bow, and Emrys took the oars back without discussion—they'd fallen into that kind of wordless economy, trading labor the way they traded watchfulness—and steered them into the deeper channel where the flow ran smoothest.

And it was there, in the narrows, that Odile saw the second thing.

At first she took it for driftwood. Then for a fence post. Then, as they drew abreast, for what it was: a concrete pylon, square-sectioned, rising eight feet out of the water, and beyond it another, and another, marching away into the hills in a line as straight as anything human beings had ever built. Survey markers. Dam foundation markers—the grid for a wall that was supposed to anchor into these very hills, before the plans changed, before the site moved downstream, before—

Before, she thought, everything.

Except that wasn't quite right either, and she was beginning to suspect it had never been right at all. The markers weren't abandoned. They were maintained—fresh orange paint on the upper faces, recent, vivid against the gray stone. Someone still came out here. Someone still cared where the line ran.

"Emrys," she said. "Those aren't old."

He followed her gaze, and she felt the change come over him like a temperature drop—the loose tired warmth of the afternoon draining out of his shoulders, replaced by something hard and quick and cold.

"No," he said. "They aren't."

"Who paints them?"

"Nobody's supposed to." He was scanning the banks now, both sides, his whole body gone taut as wire. "The foundation survey was officially voided eighteen months ago. Those markers should be graffiti by now. Somebody's keeping the option open." He looked at her, and whatever tenderness had opened between them on the open water was folded away, packed tight, stored somewhere it wouldn't rattle. "Row. Don't stop rowing. And Odile—if you hear an engine, any engine, we kill the lamp, we get under cover, and we don't say a word until we know whose it is."

The light was going gold and long across the water. Somewhere behind them, back among the drowned rooftops and the leaning steeple, the village kept its silence, keeping its four hundred and eleven names beneath the flood. And somewhere ahead—in the narrows, in the hills, in the failing light—something moved that was not water and not wind.

Odile heard it before she saw it: a low, rhythmic thrum, carried flat across the evening calm.

An engine. Coming downriver.
