## 13. Night Signals

The rain had thinned to a whisper by the time true dark came down, and Emrys Vale almost wished it hadn't. Rain was cover. Rain was noise. Rain meant the river could swallow the small sounds of an aluminum boat nosing through drowned forest, and anything that swallowed sound might also swallow them.

He sat in the stern with the tiller against his thigh, letting the current do most of the work, and watched the water for the shapes that mattered: the pale crowns of submerged fence posts, the ghost-lines of old roads running just beneath the surface, the black shoulders of trees that had stood in someone's front yard eleven years ago and now stood in nothing at all. He knew this valley the way a man knows his own handwriting. That was the problem. Every landmark he recognized was another thing he'd drawn, measured, certified with his own careful hand before the water came.

Odile slept sitting up, or pretended to. She'd wedged herself into the bow with her back against a supply crate they'd salvaged from the survey camp, her father's compass open on her knee even now, as if the needle could mean something down here where north itself seemed to have drowned. Her jacket was soaked through at the shoulders. She hadn't complained once, which he'd decided was either courage or armor, and he suspected she'd built the second out of the first so long ago she couldn't tell them apart anymore.

He should wake her. Some instinct older than sense told him he should.

Instead he listened.

* * *

They'd made perhaps nine kilometers since dusk, poling through the flooded timber where the main channel braided into dead-end sloughs, following Emrys's memory of a contour line he'd once inked at two meters' interval. The lower station lay thirty kilometers on—a ranger relay with a radio, a fuel cache, a door that locked from the inside. Thirty kilometers of water that used to be land, in a boat that leaked no matter how patiently Emrys fed sealant into its seams.

The leak was manageable. He'd bailed twice an hour, a rhythm like rowing, and Odile had taken over without being asked when his arms shook. They were learning each other's rhythms the way survivors learn anything—quickly, without ceremony, and with more tenderness than either of them would name aloud.

But the rain had stopped, and the clouds had torn open along the western ridge, and moonlight came through the gap in a single wide blade, laying silver across the floodplain as far as Emrys could see. Beautiful. Exposed. The whole drowned valley turned into a mirror with nowhere to hide.

That was when he saw the light.

It came around a bend of the old river channel two kilometers downstream—or what had been a bend, once; now it was just a suggestion of deeper water threading between treetops—and it swept left to right in a slow, deliberate arc, the way a man sweeps a yard he intends to search rather than merely cross. White light, hard-edged, mounted high. Not a fisherman's lamp. Fishermen didn't search. Fishermen looked *for* things; this light was looking *at* everything, refusing to skip so much as a floating log.

Emrys's mouth went dry. He knew exactly one organization that ran searchlights on the Kestrel Reach, and he knew exactly who signed the requisitions for them, because he'd seen the requisitions himself, filed between hydrological reports in a cabinet he'd never been supposed to open.

"Odile." His voice came out low and flat, and he was grateful for that. "Wake up. Don't move yet."

She came awake the way he'd begun to suspect she did everything—completely, instantly, without wasted motion. One breath and her eyes were open and tracking his face, reading him before he'd said a word. Then she followed his gaze downstream and he watched her go very still, the stillness of a woman who has spent years making herself invisible and knows precisely how much stillness costs.

"That's not weather," she whispered.

"No."

"Searchlight."

"Yes."

"Whose?"

He hesitated one beat too long, and he felt her notice it—the small shift in the air between them, the ledger she was keeping without wanting to. He'd been keeping his own ledger of things unsaid. It was getting heavy. Tonight it might get heavier still.

"The survey's security contractor," he said. "Meridian Protective Services. Or whatever shell name they're flying this month." He kept his eyes on the light. "There's a man named Ansel Roque. Chief of site security. If there's a boat on this river tonight with a searchlight and no registration lights showing, it's his."

"And he's looking for—" She stopped. He heard her arrive at the answer on her own, heard it land in her voice like a stone dropped down a well. "Witnesses."

"He's looking for me," Emrys said. It cost something to say it plainly, and saying it plainly was the only currency he had left. "The team's logs went into the fire the night the valley flooded. Most of them. I took what was left because someone had to, and Roque has spent every hour since making sure no one ever reads them. The other men are gone, Odile. Whatever happened to them, he was there when it happened, or close enough to have let it happen, and I am the only set of hands that carried the evidence out."

The light swept again, closer now. A kilometer, maybe less. Beneath its arc he could make out the faint shape of a hull—long, low, twin engines idling so quietly they'd only hear it when it was too late. A patrol craft, military surplus, the kind Meridian bought through three layers of paperwork so no auditor would ask why a dam project needed gunboats.

Odile moved then—not away from the light but toward him, sliding aft along the crates with the smooth economy of a woman who'd handled boats in bad places before. She killed the little shielded lantern they'd been running dark anyway, though it hardly mattered; the moonlight had already given them away to any eye that knew what to look for. Their hull sat low in the water, gray aluminum, unlit, a sliver of shadow among shadows. It might be enough. It might not.

"Options," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a pilot asking for weather.

"We kill the lamp—we have no lamp burning—and we drift," Emrys said. "No engine, no paddle, no wake. There's a side channel ahead, maybe four hundred meters, where the old orchard road crossed. The water backs up in there behind a strainer of deadfall. It's shallow, weedy, ugly. If we can get into it before his beam reaches us, we become part of the landscape."

"And if we can't?"

He didn't answer, and she didn't need him to.

* * *

They drifted.

Emrys shipped the tiller and lifted the small outboard clear of the water, cradling its weight against his chest so the drop wouldn't knock against the transom. Odile stowed the paddles flat under the gunwale. Then there was nothing to do but sit, two shapes in an empty boat on a silver sea of drowned forest, while the light came for them at the speed of a man walking.

The silence was enormous. Without the engine, Emrys could hear everything: water lapping the hull in small patient tongues, the drip of leaves somewhere off to port, his own pulse ticking in his ears like a second, faster clock. Odile's breathing had slowed deliberately, deep and even, the breathing of a woman managing her own fear the way she managed fuel—down to the last liter, nothing wasted.

"He'll sweep past us," she breathed, barely a movement of lips. "Boats like that run a pattern. Sweep the channel, log contacts, move on. We're debris. Debris doesn't get logged."

"Unless the debris is a fourteen-foot aluminum boat with two people in it."

"Then we'd better be good debris."

The light swung wide across the floodplain, caught the crown of a dead kapok tree, and painted it bone-white for three full seconds before moving on. In that white flash Emrys saw their position laid bare in his mind's eye: forty meters off the main channel, drifting with maybe half a knot of current, angled slightly bow-downstream. Good. Natural. A log would drift like that. A body would drift like that. Let Roque's spotters see exactly that, if they saw anything—driftwood and drowned men, the valley's ordinary dead.

But the patrol boat was turning.

He watched it happen with a coldness spreading through his gut that had nothing to do with the wet. The hull came about in a slow curve, its searchlight sweeping a fan across the water ahead of it, methodical as a scythe. It was working upstream. Toward them. And it was hugging the channel edge—the same channel edge they'd be swept past within minutes.

"Emrys." Odile's hand found his forearm in the dark. Her grip was light and absolutely steady. "The side channel. How far?"

"Three hundred meters. Maybe less. But the entrance is narrow, and if his light catches us crossing open water—"

"Then we don't cross open water." Her voice had changed, dropped into something cool and quick, and he realized he was hearing the voice she used in the cockpit, the one that talked her through flooded strips and dying engines. "There's standing timber between us and the channel, yes? Deadfall, trunks?"

"A whole drowned orchard. Mango, mostly. The rows are still—" He stopped, astonished at himself. "The rows are still there. Underwater. You could almost steer by them."

"Then we pole. Slow. Tree to tree. No wake, no splash, no silhouette against the open channel." She was already reaching for the push-pole they'd cut from a length of bamboo at the camp, fitting it into his hands. "You know these rows. I'll bail and keep us trimmed. We go when his beam is pointed away. Every sweep gives us—I don't know—twenty seconds?"

"Eighteen," Emrys said, and something that was almost a smile touched his mouth. "I counted."

"Of course you did." For one strange moment, in the middle of all that fear, her eyes held his, and the moonlight showed him something in them that wasn't fear at all. "Cartographer."

"Pilot."

"Go."

* * *

They went on the next sweep, when the beam swung away to interrogate a raft of broken reeds, and the eighteen seconds became a rhythm, a terrible slow dance with a machine of light. Pole, glide, hold. Pole, glide, hold. Emrys stood in the bow—standing made them taller, more visible, but it gave the pole leverage, and leverage meant silence, and silence meant living—and walked the boat through the drowned orchard one mango row at a time.

The trees rose around them like the masts of a sunken fleet. Their trunks were black and furred with moss, and the water between them was still enough to hold the moon in broken pieces. Twice the pole found bottom and Emrys felt the unmistakable geometry of a road beneath him—graded fill, firm, engineered—running straight as a ruled line toward hills that no longer existed above the waterline. He'd surveyed that road. He'd signed the drawing. *Access corridor, east bank, station 14+200.* Eleven years later it carried him, fleeing, through the ruins of the thing it had been built to serve.

Behind them the searchlight swept, and swept, and swept.

Halfway into the timber, the patrol boat's engine note changed. Deeper. Slower. Coming up the channel at idle, the way a hunter moves when he thinks he's heard something.

Emrys froze mid-stroke, the bamboo poised above the water, a drop falling from its tip. The drop hit the floodplain and sounded, to his straining ears, like a gunshot.

The light came around.

It swept along the channel wall, caught the white bark of a eucalyptus, slid across twenty meters of open water—and stopped.

Not on them. On the water fifty meters south of them, where something drifted: a tangle of branches, a mat of hyacinth torn loose from some drowned bank, riding the current in a long lazy line. The beam held on it. Held. Emrys could feel Odile beside him not breathing, could feel his own heart slamming against the ribs of the boat as if it wanted out, as if it would swim for shore and leave them.

A voice carried across the water, flat and amplified and terribly calm.

"Contact, mark. Drifting vegetation, negative heat signature. Log it and resume the grid."

Roque. Even distorted by the loudhailer, even flattened by distance and electronics, Emrys knew the voice the way he knew his own signature—because he'd sat across a table from it, because it had redlined his maps in calm blue pencil and said *proceed*, because it had said *the schedule is the schedule* while the rain gauge overflowed and the piezometers climbed and every number Emrys trusted screamed what the numbers eventually said out loud.

*Log it and resume the grid.* As if the valley were a warehouse. As if the missing men were inventory.

The beam lifted, swept on, resumed its patient arithmetic. The engine note climbed. The patrol boat moved off downstream, hunting the channel further along the grid, and Emrys let out a breath that shook despite everything he did to steady it.

Odile's hand found his again in the dark. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the touch didn't say better.

* * *

They backed the boat into the deadfall an hour later, easing it into a pocket of still water walled by fallen trunks and hanging vines, deep enough in the strainer that from the channel they'd be indistinguishable from the wreckage around them. Emrys wedged the bow against a root mass and tied off to a branch with a length of cord, and then, finally, they stopped moving.

The cold came in then, now that the fear had loosened its grip. Odile wrapped herself in the salvage tarp and shivered, and Emrys sat close—not touching, but near enough that his shoulder broke some of the wind coming off the water, and both of them understood the arrangement without discussing it.

"He won't quit," Emrys said quietly. "The grid pattern means he's got orders to clear the reach section by section. By dawn he'll have covered everything from the camp down to the narrows. When he doesn't find us in the open water, he'll start thinking like a surveyor. Narrowing intervals. Checking the cover."

"You think like a surveyor," Odile said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—it was arithmetic.

"Yes."

"Then we leave before he starts thinking like you." She was quiet a moment, and when she spoke again her voice had that stripped-bare quality it got when she stopped performing competence and simply told the truth. "I've spent six years being invisible, Emrys. Small landings, small debts, head down, no names. It worked because nobody with power cared enough to look. But this man is looking. And Voss—" She stopped, jaw tightening. "Voss sold me the rope I've been hanging from, and I'm starting to wonder whose hand is on the other end."

Emrys looked at her—at this woman who'd flown into a drowning valley because she was ordered to and hated herself for obeying, who'd bailed his leaks and steered his drowned orchard and asked for nothing—and the confession he'd been carrying since the camp rose in his throat like floodwater. The maps. The redlines. What the logs said, and what Roque had done when the team read them.

Not yet. Not here, in a strainer with a searchlight sweeping the dark a kilometer away. But soon. She deserved it before the river decided for them.

"He'll be waiting at the lower station," Emrys said instead. "If he's smart, that's where he'll wait. It's the only radio, the only fuel, the only way out of this valley except the gorge."

Odile was silent for a long moment. Out in the darkness, far downstream, the searchlight swept once more across the distant treetops—a white blade turning and turning, patient as the water itself.

"Then we'd better get there first," she said.
