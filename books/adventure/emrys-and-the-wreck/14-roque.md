## 14. Roque

The rain had followed Ansel Roque all the way up from the coast, and he was beginning to think of it less as weather and more as an accomplice.

He stood at the window of the relay station's operations room, watching the water sheet off the corrugated awning below and hammer the mud into submission. Beyond the wire perimeter, the river ran high and brown and fast, carrying with it the debris of everything the flood had taken—branches, roofing tin, a child's chair, half a fence. Things that had belonged to people who were, officially, relocated. Officially compensated. Officially a matter of closed files.

Behind him, the radio operator—Corporal Dessa, a thin young woman with the hollow-eyed patience of someone who had learned to wait out her superiors—adjusted a dial and said, "Liaison's on, sir. Encrypted. He wants voice."

"Of course he does." Roque turned from the window. "Put it on the speaker. I want my hands free."

The speaker crackled, hissed, and resolved into the clipped, nasal voice of Undersecretary Pell, the ministry's man for the Kestrel Reach portfolio—a man Roque had met exactly four times and disliked on every one of those occasions with increasing precision. Pell had the voice of a man who had never been rained on in his life, and Roque resented it on principle.

"Roque. Status."

"Good evening to you too, sir." Roque pulled the field chair out from the map table and sat, unhurried, letting the wet hem of his trousers darken the canvas. "Status is that the storm has closed the upper reach, the river is up two meters and climbing, and I have three boats and a helicopter I can't fly in this. But I assume you didn't burn an encrypted channel to ask about the weather."

"The survey team." Pell's voice had the flat impatience of a man reading from a list he'd already memorized. "The ministry requires an update. The press office is preparing a statement. Families are asking questions."

"The press office," Roque said, "should prepare a statement that says the team is unaccounted for due to catastrophic flooding, that search efforts are ongoing, and that the ministry's thoughts are with the families. That statement will be true in every particular and will remain true indefinitely, because it commits us to nothing."

A pause on the line. When Pell spoke again, his voice had dropped half a register, the official cadence stripped away. "That's not why I called, Ansel."

"No," Roque agreed. "I didn't think it was."

"The cartographer."

Roque had been waiting for that name for six hours. He let it hang in the wet static for a moment, then reached for the tin cup on the map table, found the coffee cold, and drank it anyway. "Vale. Yes. What about him?"

"Confirm it."

"I can do better than confirm it. I can give you a time, a place, and a witness." Roque set the cup down and turned the map table's lamp toward himself, studying the reach spread out beneath the plastic overlay: the drowned valley, the old riverbed threading through it like a vein, the survey camp marked in grease pencil, and—circled twice in red, by his own hand that morning—the flooded strip where a supply pilot had put a bush plane down two days ago and, by every indication, had not yet taken it off again. "Day before the storm front. A pilot out of the southern settlements—Blackwood, runs medicine and freight, works the river settlements on a contract nobody can quite produce the paperwork for—put down on the old strip inside the reach. Emergency charter. The command post sent her in to check on the survey after they missed two contacts."

"And she found him."

"She found someone. She reported a survivor by radio before her set went quiet—male, injured or claiming to be, alone at the camp. Then the storm closed the valley and she went off the net. That was thirty-six hours ago." Roque tapped the red circle. "Now. You can believe, if it helps you sleep, that Vale is dead and the man she found is a ghost. But I pulled Vale's service file when the team went quiet, and I'll tell you what I know about Emrys Vale. Ten years mapping river systems. Quiet. Methodical. The kind of man who checks a bearing three times and writes it down in three places. When the ministry's engineers redlined his foundation reports on the Kestrel dam, he didn't shout. He didn't resign. He filed a formal dissent—properly, through channels—and then he kept mapping." Roque leaned back in the chair, the canvas creaking. "Men like that don't drown by accident in a valley they know better than the water does. Men like that survive, and they keep records."

"You're speculating."

"I'm doing my job, which is to speculate so you don't have to. Here's the speculation, in order. One: the dam failed early because the foundations were unsound and the ministry built it anyway over Vale's written objection. Two: the flood caught the survey team in the valley, and Vale got out with the team's logs, his maps, and whatever else he thought was worth carrying—because a man like Vale doesn't run without evidence. Three: the rest of the team is dead, scattered, or wishes it were, and none of that matters, because the only piece of this that can hurt anyone is the paper in Vale's pack. Four: Vale has made contact with the one person in a hundred kilometers with an airplane." He let that sit. "And five: she's either flying him out right now or she's trapped on the water with him, and either way, every hour of this rain is an hour he spends turning a witness into an ally."

The static breathed. Somewhere on Pell's end, a door closed.

"The pilot," Pell said carefully. "Blackwood. How much does she know?"

"About the dam? Nothing. About Vale? Whatever he's told her, which I'd estimate is a fraction of a fraction. She's a freight pilot with a dead father's debts and a contract she can't afford to lose. She didn't ask for any of this." Roque found himself looking at the red circle again, at the small grease-pencil mark where the strip would be, if the strip were still above water. "Which is a problem, actually, because it makes her unpredictable. A man running from us is predictable—we know what he wants. A woman who stumbled into it is a coin flip. She might turn him in. She might hide him. She might decide she's safer selling him to a newspaper."

"Can she be managed?"

"Everyone can be managed. The question is what it costs." Roque said it lightly, but he was already turning the problem over the way he turned all problems: by inventory. He was not a cruel man, whatever his men whispered when they thought he was out of earshot. Cruelty was inefficient; it created grudges, and grudges created witnesses. What Roque practiced was a colder discipline: the arithmetic of pressure. Every person had a weight that would move them—debt, fear, ambition, love—and the work was finding the weight and applying it precisely, like ballast, until the vessel sat in the water exactly where you wanted it. "Her father flew the reach for twenty years. There's paper on her somewhere—there's always paper. Find it, or find me the authority to find it, and I'll have leverage by morning."

"I can't authorize anything that traces back to—"

"You can authorize nothing," Roque said, "which is why I'm telling you instead of asking you. Listen to me, Undersecretary. I have been paid, in one form or another, to make problems go away for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly one lesson worth the tuition: the cover-up is never what kills you. What kills you is the loose end you were too polite to cut." He stood, took the grease pencil from the map table's tray, and drew a slow line down the river from the red circle, past the ranger relay, past the lower station, all the way to where the reach widened and brackened and finally met the salt. "Vale is a loose end. The pilot is a loose end. The logs are a loose end. And every one of them is currently sitting in a flooded valley with one road out, and the road is water."

"What are you proposing?"

"Containment. Not a search—a cordon. I want eyes on every stretch of navigable water between the reach and the coast. The ranger stations, the fishing camps, the ferry landings, the mission at Two Rivers. I want the pilots' association told there's a temporary flight restriction over the upper valley for rescue operations, and I want it worded so that any plane that ignores it is breaking the law before it's a witness. I want the lower station stripped of anything with a ministry stamp on it—Vale knows that station; he drew half the charts for it—and I want it looking abandoned by tomorrow night." He capped the grease pencil. "And I want a boat crew I can trust at the gorge, because if Vale comes downriver, the rapids are where the river does our work for us. Drowning is very thorough, and it files no reports."

"You're talking about killing a government employee."

"I'm talking about a man lost in a flood," Roque said, and his voice did not change at all, which was the whole trick of it. "The flood has already killed seven people we know of, and it will kill more before the week is out. If Vale dies in it, the statement your press office is drafting tonight will cover him too. Unaccounted for. Search ongoing. Thoughts with the family." He paused. "I'd prefer him alive, for the record. A living man can be negotiated with, and a dead man's papers have a way of turning up in envelopes. But I'd prefer a great many things, Undersecretary, and the rain doesn't consult me on any of them."

Another silence, longer this one. When Pell finally spoke, his voice had gone small and careful, the voice of a man stepping out onto ice and testing it with each word. "If this comes apart—if the pilot talks, if the logs surface—"

"Then we will both be standing in front of people with clipboards, and you will explain why the ministry overrode its own cartographer, and I will explain that I was following the instructions of a man who is now very anxious to have never given them." Roque let the implication do its work. "Which is why it won't come apart. Not because we're clever. Because we're thorough. Cleverness is for the people trying to get out. Thoroughness is for the people making sure the door stays shut."

He heard Pell exhale, a thin whistle through the static. "The cordon. You'll need authority to requisition the ranger boats."

"I'll need authority for nothing, because I'm going to frame every request as a rescue coordination measure, and every ranger captain downstream is going to sign off on it, because no one in this country has ever been reprimanded for searching too hard for flood victims." Roque was already moving, pulling a field notebook from his breast pocket, flipping to a fresh page, writing in his small, square, unhurried hand. "I'll send the disposition list on the next scheduled burst. I want one more thing from you, and then you should go back to your dry office and let me work."

"Name it."

"The pilot's file. Blackwood. Father deceased—there'll be debt paper, there's always debt paper on river pilots, the banks feed on them like leeches. I want the name of whoever holds her note, and I want it tonight." He clicked the pen. "If she's carrying my loose end downriver, I want to know what she's carrying it *for*. Everyone rows toward something, Undersecretary. Find out what she's rowing toward, and we'll know which way to push the current."

"I'll see what I can find."

"See that you find it." Roque closed the notebook. "And Pell—one more thing, off the record, for your own education. The pilot reported a survivor on an open channel. That means every radio operator from here to the coast heard it, which means the story of a survivor in the reach is already loose, and stories like that grow in the telling. By the end of the week, the settlements will have it that a whole man walked out of the flood carrying the government's sins in a satchel. You can't un-ring that. All you can do is make sure that when the story arrives somewhere, it arrives without the man and without the papers." He looked at the window, at the rain still falling with the patience of something that intended to go on forever. "The water is doing most of this work for us. Our job is simply not to get in its way."

The line clicked, hissed, and went dead.

Roque stood for a moment in the operations room's lamplight, listening to the rain. Then he turned to Corporal Dessa, who had the good sense to be looking at her dials and not at him.

"Dessa. Get me the duty roster for the river stations, south of the gorge. And pull everything we have on the lower station—charts, stores, who's posted there, when they last reported."

"Sir." Her fingers moved on the keys. "Are we—this is a rescue coordination, correct?"

Roque looked at her for a moment. She was twenty-three, maybe twenty-four, and she had asked the question in the flat, careful voice of someone who needed the word *rescue* to be said aloud, needed it placed on the record between them like a coin on a dead man's eye.

"It's whatever the situation requires," he said. "The situation requires that no one else drowns in this valley. That's the mission. Write it down however you like."

She wrote it down.

* * *

By midnight the rain had settled into its long, monotonous roar, and the operations room smelled of wet canvas and cold coffee and the ozone bite of the radio. Roque worked through the disposition list by lamplight, station by station, and found the picture it made both simple and satisfying.

Two Rivers mission: a launch, an old man who owed the church money, and a radio. Covered—Father Anselme owed the diocese, and the diocese owed the ministry's rural development fund. A phone call would close that net.

The fishing camps at the narrows: eleven boats, forty-odd men, and a single channel of water between the cliffs. Covered—a fuel shortage was already being arranged through the trading cooperative, and men without fuel stayed home and talked about the weather.

The ranger relay at the bend: a garbled transmitter and a part-time operator. Roque marked it *monitor only*. A broken radio was more useful than a working one; it heard everything and confirmed nothing.

The lower station: stripped by tomorrow night, per his earlier order, and the stripping was already underway—he'd had the confirmation burst at dusk, three words, *station clear moving*, from a crew he'd chosen precisely because they asked no questions and kept no diaries. After that, the lower station would be a mouth with no teeth and a light left burning, and anything that came down the river toward it would be coming toward *him*.

He spread the last sheet of the night's work on the map table: a hand-drawn sketch of the river from the reach to the salt, with the choke points inked in and the timings penciled beside them. At the top of the sheet he wrote, in his small square hand, the only order that mattered, the order that would go out on the morning burst to every station on the list:

**Watch the water. Nothing from the valley reaches the coast. Nothing.**

He read it back twice. It was a good order. It was the kind of order that would be obeyed, because it was vague enough to be deniable and specific enough to be followed, and because every man receiving it would understand, in the marrow-deep way that men understand such things, that *nothing* was the operative word and that the consequences of a *something* would fall on the man who reported it, not the man who issued it.

Roque capped the pen and stood at the window a long time, watching the river.

It was invisible now, past the wire and the awning—just a sound, a vast brown breathing in the dark, moving everything it touched downstream toward the sea. Somewhere up in that black water and blacker rain, a cartographer and a bush pilot were drifting toward him on a leaking boat, whether they knew it or not. He almost pitied them. They thought they were running. They thought the storm was their enemy and the river their road. They hadn't yet understood the essential fact of the reach, the fact Roque had understood since his first week in this country, standing on this same bank in a different flood:

The river only runs one way.

At 0300, the burst receiver chattered and printed its tape. Dessa tore it off, read it, and went very still.

"Sir," she said. "The relay at the bend. It's a fragment—garbled, but the operator flagged it. A voice transmission on the open channel. Female." She held the tape out at arm's length, as if it might bite. "She's asking about the lower station. Whether anyone's still posted there. She says—" Dessa's eyes flicked down the tape and back up. "She says she's coming downriver with a survivor, and she's low on fuel, and she needs to know if the dock is standing."

Roque took the tape. He read it twice, unhurried, while the rain hammered the roof like applause.

"Well," he said, folding the tape into his notebook. "There's our coin flip."

He reached for the lamp, and the room went dark, and outside the river went on running the only way it knew how—toward the sea, toward the salt, toward the net that was already closing around it, station by station, in the dark.

"Get my boat crew up, Dessa," Roque said into the black. "We're going to the gorge."
