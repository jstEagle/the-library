## 15. Bail and Bailing

The boat was named *Perseverance*, which Odile found either very funny or very sad, depending on how much water was in the bilge at any given moment.

They'd found her tied to a drowned fence post where the survey camp's supply shed used to be, an aluminum skiff with a cracked transom brace and a hull that had opinions about staying dry. Emrys had patched the worst of the seams with epoxy from the camp's ruined stores and a strip of canvas off a tent fly, working by flashlight while the rain came down in ropes and the river climbed the runway rung by rung like something patient counting stairs.

Now they were afloat in the dark, adrift in what had been, three days ago, a valley. The tops of trees broke the current around them like the backs of animals. Somewhere under the keel was the road to Kestrel village, and under that were houses, and under those were people's whole lives pressed flat like flowers in a book nobody meant to keep.

Odile bailed.

She'd been bailing for four hours. She would bail for four more. It was not complicated work—a dented aluminum pot, a rhythm, a count—but it was hers, and there was a strange mercy in work that asked nothing of you but that you not stop. Scoop, swing, pour. Scoop, swing, pour. The river gave back everything she threw at it and then some, but she threw anyway, because the alternative was thinking about the fact that they were two small warm bodies in a tin shell on top of forty feet of drowned country, and that somewhere downriver a man named Roque had said *we're going to the gorge* in a voice like a man reading a grocery list.

"You should sleep," Emrys said. He sat in the stern with the tiller lashed, one hand trailing in the black water as if he could read it by touch. "I'll take the pot."

"I'm fine."

"You've been fine for four hours."

"I've been *bailing* for four hours. There's a difference." She swung the pot over the side, watched the water vanish into the same water it had come from. "You steer. Steering's harder than it looks in current like this. You drift ten degrees wrong and you're in a strainer."

"I know what a strainer is."

"Then you know why I'm not handing you the pot." She settled back onto the thwart, knees against the gunwale, and kept her count. "Bail or sleep. Pick one."

He picked bail. Of course he did. That was the thing about him she was coming to understand—Emrys Vale did not argue when arguing cost breath, and he did not waste motion, and when you handed him a job he did it the way water filled a hole: completely, without comment. He took the pot and set a rhythm slightly faster than hers, and she hated him a little for it, and let him.

* * *

Around midnight the rain eased from wall to curtain, and the world acquired edges again. A moon the color of a fingernail came and went behind the cloud scud. They passed a church roof standing alone out of the flood, its bell still hanging, and neither of them said anything about it, and the silence about it was louder than the rain.

"Tell me something," Odile said, mostly to break the silence before it broke her. "Anything. Where you learned to patch a hull like that. You did in twenty minutes what takes my mechanic a day and a lecture."

"Shipyard," Emrys said. "Before the survey. Three years on the coast, refitting river boats. Before that—" He paused, and she heard him deciding, the way she'd heard him decide a dozen times since the reeds, weighing each word on a scale only he could see. "Before that I drew maps."

"Maps."

"Maps."

"That's a job? Drawing maps? I thought satellites did that now."

"Satellites take pictures," he said. "Pictures aren't maps. A map is decisions. What matters, what doesn't, what's safe, what will kill you. Somebody has to make those calls, and somebody has to sign them." His voice did something small and strange on the word *sign*, and Odile, who had spent six years listening to men talk around the things they actually meant, heard it and filed it away.

"So you signed maps for the government, and then your government lost you in a flood, and now you're in a leaky boat with me." She shrugged in the dark, though he probably couldn't see it. "There are worse careers. Mine is flying medicine into places that flood. We're practically colleagues."

"What made you start?"

"Flying?" She laughed, short and without much humor in it. "My father. He flew this same route for twenty years. Mail, medicine, fuel drums, gossip. He used to say the river settlements didn't need a doctor half as bad as they needed somebody who'd show up." The pot went over the side. "When he died, the plane came to me, and so did the note on the plane. Same envelope, basically. Inheritance."

"Voss."

The name landed in the boat between them like a stone. Odile went still, pot halfway to the rail.

"I've mentioned him once," she said carefully. "In passing. Two days ago. You remember everything?"

"I remember everything," Emrys said, and there was no boast in it, just the flat tired truth of a man who wished his memory were worse. "It's the curse of the trade. A cartographer who forgets things makes maps that kill people."

"And Voss?"

"Harrow Voss holds paper all up and down this valley. Planes, boats, trading posts, sawmill equipment. Half the river runs on money he lent and interest he invented." Emrys bailed twice, unhurried. "I knew his name before I ever met you. Everyone on the survey knew it. He was on the ministry liaison list for the dam project. Financing."

Something cold walked up Odile's spine, and it wasn't the rain. "Financing."

"The Reach dam wasn't just a government project. It was a partnership. Public money, private capital." He set the pot down. "I'm telling you this because you deserve to know whose boat you're sitting in, in every sense. Not because I can explain it yet. I can't. Not yet."

"Not yet," she repeated. "That's the second time you've said that."

"It'll be the last time I have to."

"You understand how that sounds. From a stranger. Who knows too much. Who watches the treeline when he thinks I'm asleep."

"I know exactly how it sounds." He turned in the stern, and even in the moonless dark she felt him look at her, the full weight of it. "Every hour you don't put me ashore, you're betting your life that I'm worth the trouble. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to keep bailing until you have enough information to trust me *accurately*. There's a difference. You taught me that tonight—the difference between fine and bailing."

Odile stared at the shape of him and felt something shift in her chest, some plate of armor she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it was armor, grinding a quarter inch out of true.

"Keep bailing," she said finally. "Both senses."

* * *

Two in the morning. The rain quit altogether, which was worse somehow, because then she could hear the river breathing, and the creak of drowned timber, and once, far off, a sound she chose to believe was a bird.

They traded the pot back and forth by unspoken agreement, and somewhere in the third watch the necessity of staying awake became the permission to talk, and the talk stopped being strategy and started being true.

"My father's compass is in my jacket pocket," she heard herself say. "Brass case, cracked crystal. He won it in a card game he swore he lost on purpose. He used it on every flight for twenty years and it never once pointed anywhere useful, and he never once replaced it." She touched the pocket through the fabric. "I check it out of habit. North, south, whatever. It's not about direction. It's about—he held this. When everything else got taken or sold, this stayed mine."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. "My mother was a schoolteacher," he said. "Kestrel village school. Before."

Odile's hands stopped moving.

"Before," she said.

"She taught the third grade for eleven years. She had a bell she'd ring when the class got too loud. After the evacuation she kept the bell in a box. She never rang it again. She said a bell was a promise—you ring it, and children come—and she wouldn't make promises she couldn't keep." He said it evenly, the way you recite coordinates, and Odile understood suddenly that the evenness was load-bearing, that if his voice bent even one degree the whole structure would come down. "She died two winters ago. She never saw the water come up. I used to think that was a mercy. Now I think she would have wanted to see it. To witness it. Somebody should have witnessed it."

The river ran. The boat leaked. Odile bailed, because her hands needed something honest to do while the rest of her rearranged itself around what he'd said and what he hadn't.

"Emrys." She said his name fully for maybe the first time, not as *hey* or *you* or *the man in the reeds*. "Your mother taught in Kestrel. And you took a job mapping the valley they flooded."

"Yes."

"And you flagged things. On the maps. Things they didn't want flagged."

His silence lasted ten seconds, twenty, thirty. She counted them against the drip of the bilge.

"I was on the team," he said at last. "The survey team. All nine of us. I can tell you that much now, because you'd have worked it out by morning anyway, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from the shape of it." He picked up the pot and handed it to her, and their fingers touched on the handle and neither of them moved away fast enough for it to be nothing. "I was on the team. The rest—the rest costs more than I can pay right now. But I'll pay it. Before we reach the lower station, you'll have all of it, every page. That's a promise."

"A bell is a promise," Odile said quietly. "You ring it, and children come."

"Yeah," he said, and his voice did bend then, one degree, and she pretended not to hear it, because some mercies are the kind you grant by looking away.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and green through the mist, the drowned forest resolving out of the dark tree by tree, and with the light came the smell of the flood—mud and diesel and bruised vegetation, sweetness going to rot. Odile's shoulders were two knots tied around bone. Her hands had gone past wrinkled into something prunish and raw. She caught her reflection in the black water and decided not to look again.

But they were alive. The boat was afloat, barely. And the current, she noted with a professional eye, had carried them true all night—down the old channel, past the church, toward the gorge country and the lower station beyond it. Emrys had steered by memory through absolute darkness, calling corrections in a low voice, and she had trusted him without deciding to, which frightened her more than Roque did.

"There," he said, pointing.

Ahead, snagged against a fallen tree, something pale. A tarp, maybe. Driftwood.

Except driftwood didn't have straps.

Odile shipped the pot and took the tiller line from him, easing their head around, and as they closed the distance the pale thing resolved into a jacket—survey orange, reflective tape gone dull—hooked over a strainer log by one sleeve, arms spread wide, empty. And below the waterline, dark against the morning gray, something else spread out slow and patient from the log's downstream side, feathering into the current like ink.

Neither of them spoke. The pot sat in the bilge between them, filling.

Emrys reached out with the boathook, and his hand, she noticed, was shaking—not much, just enough—and Odile realized she was watching a man look at the answer to a question he'd been carrying for days, and that the answer was written in orange nylon and blood, and that whatever he was about to tell her, he was about to tell it standing in front of the proof.

"Emrys," she said softly. "Whoever wore that jacket. Was it one of yours?"

He didn't answer right away. Downstream, faint through the mist, came a sound that froze them both—a motor, low and steady, running against the current.

Coming upriver. Toward them.
