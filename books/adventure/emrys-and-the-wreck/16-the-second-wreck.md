## 16. The Second Wreck

*Odile*

The river changed its mind about them somewhere past the drowned church.

She'd been watching the water the way her father taught her—reading the surface like a page, the way current wrote itself in wrinkles and seams—and for two hours the river had been a single gray sentence, legible enough. Then the sentence broke apart. The water widened, slowed, and began braiding itself around the tops of trees that had never learned to be islands. Dead forest. The flood had come up so fast through this stretch that the trees hadn't even had time to drop their leaves; they stood in six feet of brown water still wearing autumn, still holding their branches out like people waiting to be counted.

"Slow down," Emrys said.

"I'm going slow."

"Slower." He was at the bow with the paddle reversed, using it as a pole, testing depth without being asked to. He'd been doing that all morning—working, quietly, competently, the way he did everything—and Odile had decided sometime around dawn that she hated how much it steadied her. "There's debris ahead. Left of that big deadfall."

She saw it then. A dark shape wedged against a strainer—a massive fallen tree lying across the channel, its root ball torn up on one side, its trunk combing the current like the ribs of something enormous. Water went into the strainer and didn't come out. Everything the river carried ended up there: branches, reed mats, a plastic drum, half a fence gate, and—

Her hands went cold on the tiller before her mind caught up with what her eyes were seeing.

Aluminum. Painted government olive. A boat.

"That's not ours," she said, though of course it wasn't theirs either. Theirs was the survey's second boat, patched twice with Emrys's kit and once with a strip of her emergency sealant, leaking the whole way downriver like a patient thing. This one sat high against the log, tilted, one gunwale crushed inward like a stepped-on tin cup.

"No," Emrys said. "That's not ours."

His voice had gone flat. She'd heard him use that voice exactly once before—when they'd found the burned logs in the survey camp, when he'd read the charred edges of pages he clearly already knew by heart. Flat the way water goes flat over deep places.

"Emrys." She brought the boat around in a slow arc, keeping well upstream of the strainer. A strainer would take their little hull and fold it around them both; every pilot knew that, every river rat, every kid who grew up on moving water. You didn't approach a strainer. You gave it room and you grieved from a distance. "We should mark it and keep moving. That's what you do with a wreck. You don't—"

"There might be someone."

"There's no one." She said it gently, or tried to. "Look at it. That's been there since the flood came through. Days. Maybe longer."

He didn't answer. He was standing now, one hand locked on the bow line, and the rain ran off his jaw and he didn't seem to notice it. And Odile understood, suddenly, with an unpleasant lurch, that he wasn't looking at the boat. He was counting seats. Six men had gone missing from Kestrel Reach. One cartographer had run. That left five, and the boat held four if you packed it cruel, and his eyes were moving across the crushed aluminum doing arithmetic he couldn't stop.

"We'll look," she heard herself say. "From here. We drift past slow and we look, and then we go. Deal?"

"Deal," he said, and she believed him about sixty percent, which was more than she'd trusted anyone in years, so maybe that meant something.

They came alongside at a distance of twenty feet, close enough to see everything and far enough that the strainer's hungry eddy couldn't reach them. The rain thinned for a moment—the storm breathing between bands—and the light went strange and silver, the way it does over drowned country, and Odile saw the wreck of the survey boat laid out clean and terrible in that light.

The stern was stove in where the log had taken it. The motor was gone, ripped off at the transom mount, probably dragged under and snagged somewhere downstream. A coil of rope trailed from the bow line into the water, swaying with the current like a mooring for nothing. The fuel tank floated loose inside the hull, dented, half full. Everything else had been scoured out.

Except the jacket.

It hung on a broken branch of the strainer, snagged at the shoulder, arms dangling. Khaki canvas, government issue, the kind the survey teams wore with reflective tape at the cuffs. Rain-darkened. It turned slowly on its branch, and as it turned Odile saw the name stenciled above the left breast pocket in block letters, half flaked but readable:

**OKONKWO, D.**

Below the pocket, spread across the chest in a stain that the rain hadn't managed to wash out, was blood. Old blood. Brown-black, soaked through the canvas, stiffening the fabric so it didn't move right—it moved wrong, the way cloth moves when it's carrying more than cloth.

"Oh God," Odile said.

Emrys made a sound. It wasn't a word. It was small and involuntary, punched out of him, and it was worse than any word could have been, because it told her everything: he knew the jacket. He knew the man. *Okonkwo, D.* Daniel? David? Some man who had eaten breakfast with Emrys Vale in a camp that no longer existed, who had argued with him over maps or coffee or cards, whose name Emrys could probably write out in full from memory because cartographers remember names the way pilots remember runways.

"He got out," Emrys said. His voice cracked down the middle and he pushed through it. "The boat hit the strainer and he got out. If he was wearing the jacket—if he shed it to swim—"

"Or the river took him wearing it," Odile said, "and hung it up here."

"Yes," Emrys said. "Both of those are true. I know both of those are true." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment, just a moment, and then he was the flat-voiced stranger again, scanning the water below the strainer, reading the downstream eddies the way she read weather. "If anyone came off that boat alive, they'd wash into the slack water behind the root ball. That's where you'd end up. That's where you'd try to get to shore."

"You want to check the root ball."

"I want to know," he said, and the flatness finally broke, and underneath it was something raw and young and drowning. "I've spent nine days not knowing. Nine days listening to the rain and eating snails and telling myself stories. I can't—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I can't carry five names down this river not knowing which ones are still alive. Please."

And there it was. *Please.* From the man who answered questions too carefully, who rationed truth like a man rationing water, who had watched her plane descend from the reeds and taken a full day to admit he'd ever seen a survey team at all. *Please.*

"All right," Odile said. "But we do it my way. Upstream approach, paddle only, engine stays off. If the hull touches that tree we're both in the water, and neither of us is wearing a jacket I'm willing to lose."

Something moved through his face that might have been gratitude or might have been grief wearing gratitude's clothes. He took up the paddle without another word.

* * *

*Emrys*

The root ball was bigger than it looked from distance—a wall of black earth and broken roots taller than a man, furred with river moss, holding a small cathedral of slack water behind it where the current forgot itself. Leaves collected there. Foam collected there. And other things collected there too; Emrys had grown up on rivers enough to know that, and he made himself look anyway, because not looking was the coward's version of the same horror.

Daniel Okonkwo had been the team's hydrologist. Forty-one years old. Two daughters in the capital, photographs taped above his bunk, the younger one missing a front tooth in every picture because the pictures had all been taken the same summer. He'd laughed at Emrys's maps—not meanly, never meanly—had put one thick finger on the contour lines around the dam foundation and said, *Vale, these numbers are going to get somebody fired,* and Emrys had said, *already filed,* and Okonkwo had said, *then why does nobody upstairs look worried?*

Because they redlined you, Emrys had wanted to tell him. Because your numbers became someone else's numbers. Because the map that reaches the minister is not the map that leaves the field.

He'd never said it. There hadn't been time. There was never time, right up until the night the valley filled and there was nothing but time, endless time, time spent swimming and climbing and running while the water ate the world behind him.

The boat drifted along the edge of the slack water. Odile held them there with small corrections of the paddle, precise, unhurried, her face set in the expression he was beginning to recognize as her working face—the one that kept everything at arm's length, the one that had landed a plane on a flooding strip because command told her to and hated herself for obeying and obeyed anyway. He was learning to read her the way she read water. It frightened him how quickly it was happening.

"There," she said.

He followed her chin. At the base of the root ball, half sunk in the silt shelf, lay a hard case—the survey's document case, aluminum, latched, mud-caked. And beside it, caught in the roots, a second piece of khaki: trousers this time, torn, one boot still in the cuff.

No body.

He searched the slack water with his eyes until they burned. Leaves. Foam. The case. The trousers. A water bottle, floating. Nothing else. The river had taken what it took and given back only the things too heavy or too tangled to keep.

"The case," Odile said. "Is that—"

"Field records." His mouth had gone dry. "Hydrology readings. Sample logs. Okonkwo's copies." Not his own copies—his own rode in the waterproof tube strapped inside his shirt, the tube that had cost him everything, the tube Roque's men would kill to recover. But Okonkwo had kept duplicates. Okonkwo had been the careful one, the one who said *paper survives, people don't, make your paper good.* "If it's sealed, some of it might be dry."

"It's forty feet away and the water between us is full of that tree's grip," Odile said. "We're not getting it with the boat."

"I can swim to it."

"You absolutely cannot. Look at the temperature of that water. Look at the current seam ten feet behind you—one slip and you're through the strainer, and I am not diving into a strainer after you. I like you too much, and also I can't fly the boat alone."

The words were out before she seemed to hear them. *I like you too much.* Her ears went pink, visible even in the gray light, and she stared furiously at the root ball as if it had personally insulted her.

Emrys found that, absurdly, impossibly, in the middle of the worst hour of the worst week of his life, he almost smiled. "Noted," he said. "For the record."

"Forget the record." She was already moving, swinging the boat in a wide arc back upstream. "We use the rope. Bow line's thirty feet. I anchor us to that dead sapling on the bank side, you go out on the rope like a tethered diver, you grab the case, you come back. Three minutes. If the rope jerks twice, I haul. Those are the terms."

"Those are good terms," he said.

"They're the only terms. Take off your boots."

* * *

*Odile*

The water took his breath away—that was visible from the boat, the way his whole body seized when he went in, the gasp, the white-knuckle grip on the line. Cold mountain melt under the floodwater, colder than the air by ten degrees, cold enough to stop a heart that wasn't ready. She paid out the rope hand over hand, counting, keeping her eyes on him and on the seam of fast water beyond the slack pool, the one that would eat him if the rope slipped or the knot failed or the river simply decided.

He reached the case in ninety seconds. Fumbled the latch underwater, hauled it up against his chest, and turned back—and that was when the current shifted.

She felt it before she saw it: a change in the pull on the rope, a heaviness, the river taking an interest. Downstream of the strainer the level was dropping as the flood crested and began to fall, and falling water rearranges its debts. The eddy behind the root ball shivered. A limb somewhere in the tangle groaned, low and structural, the sound of weight settling.

"Emrys. Now. Come *now.*"

He came. Swimming hard with the case hugged to his chest, boots long gone, face gray, and the rope sang through her hands as she hauled, and for three seconds—three seconds she would carry for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be—he was at the edge of the seam, the fast water sliding past him close enough to touch, and the only thing between him and the strainer was her grip and a knot she'd tied in the dark two nights ago.

Then his hand found the gunwale. Then her hand found his wrist. Then he was over the side and into the bottom of the boat, streaming, shaking, clutching the aluminum case like a child, and the river went back to pretending it was only a river.

Neither of them spoke for a while. She paddled them out to mid-channel, put a hundred meters between them and the strainer, and let the current take them. The rain resumed, soft at first, then steady. Behind them the wreck of the survey boat hung in its tree, turning slowly, the jacket with its name and its dried blood lifting and falling in the wind like something waving goodbye or asking to be remembered.

"Okonkwo," Emrys said at last. His teeth were chattering. He said the name anyway, carefully, like setting something down. "Daniel Okonkwo. Hydrologist. He had the best laugh on the team and he was afraid of the dark, actual dark, he slept with a lamp on. He kept duplicate records because he didn't trust the ministry's filing system." A breath. "He was right not to."

"Did he make it?" she asked. "Can you tell?"

Emrys looked back at the strainer until it slid behind a bend of dead trees. "The jacket says he bled. The empty boat says the rest. Either he drowned in the flood, or he got out and died on foot, or—" He stopped. "One survivor's kit. One man's clothes. Four-man boat. Where are the other three, Odile? There's no second wreck downstream. I've been watching the banks for nine days. There's nothing."

The question settled over the boat like the rain. She thought about the burned logs in the camp, half-destroyed, deliberately destroyed. She thought about the way he'd said *Roque cut them loose* —no, he hadn't said that yet, he'd said almost nothing yet, he'd given her pieces like a man paying out a rope and she was beginning to understand that the rope led somewhere very deep.

"Three men don't vanish," she said quietly. "Not in a flood zone this size, not with search parties on the water. Boats sink. Men wash up. Something always comes back."

"Unless someone made sure nothing would," Emrys said.

He said it looking at the case in his lap, his thumb resting on the latch, and Odile felt the last comfortable lie of her journey—that this was a story about weather, about bad luck, about a river doing what rivers do—go out of her like heat through a cracked window.

Ahead, around the next bend, the channel narrowed toward the lower station. Somewhere down there was fuel, a radio, a door out of the green. Somewhere down there, possibly, was the answer to where three men had gone.

And somewhere behind them, faint under the rain, so faint she first mistook it for thunder, came the sound of an outboard motor. More than one. Running upstream. Against the current. Looking for something.

Looking, she understood, for them.
