## 17. Confession in the Rain

The rain had stopped being weather and become a place they lived in.

Emrys sat in the stern of the leaking boat with his back against the gunwale and the evidence case between his knees, and he listened to Odile bail. She had been bailing for hours. The tin cup she'd found in the survey camp went into the bilge water and came out, went in and came out, a rhythm as steady as a heart, and somewhere in the middle of the night he realized he was measuring time by it instead of by the rain.

They were tied up under a drowned fig tree, its crown just breaking the surface of the flood like a man treading water who had given up on swimming and decided simply to float. The lower station was two days behind them. Roque's searchlight was a memory that kept replaying against his eyelids every time he closed them. Ahead lay the gorge, the rapids, the salt line—and nothing but his word to stand between Odile Blackwood and the rest of this.

She deserved better than his word rationed out in pieces.

"Odile." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "You should stop bailing for a minute."

"I stop, we sink."

"We won't sink in five minutes. I patched the seam twice today. Five minutes is safe." He almost smiled. "I'm a cartographer. I know exactly how much water a hull takes on per hour. It's the one map I've been keeping current."

She paused, cup halfway to the gunwale, and looked at him across the dark. Even now—especially now—he could not read her face fully. She was a woman built out of unreadable faces, and he understood why. He was beginning to understand a great many things about her that he had no right to understand yet.

"You've been working up to something since the second wreck," she said. "Since I pulled that jacket out of the strainer log and you couldn't look at it. Talk or don't talk, Emrys. But if you're going to talk, do it while my hands are busy. It's easier to hear terrible things when your hands are busy."

He hadn't known that about her. He filed it away among the other things—the way she checked her father's compass without seeming to decide to, the way she counted fuel gallons under her breath like a rosary, the way she had flown into a flooding valley because men with clipboards told her to and hated herself for obeying. He filed it all away, and then he opened the case.

* * *

The rain drummed on the boat's aluminum skin, softening everything, making the world small: two people, a tin cup, a tree standing in forty feet of water where a village used to be.

"The dam at Kestrel Reach wasn't supposed to fail," he began. "That's the first thing you have to understand. Not *wasn't supposed to fail yet*. Wasn't supposed to fail. Full stop. The geological survey I ran in the spring showed shale seams in the foundation abutment that would shear under load within ten years. Maybe fifteen if the winters stayed dry. I flagged it. I red-flagged it. I drew the maps myself—I drew the hazard zones in red ink so thick you could see them from across a room."

"And?"

"And the ministry liaison looked at my maps and said, *redraw them.*"

Odile's hands stilled on the cup. He watched her decide to keep bailing anyway, and loved her a little for it, though he didn't let himself use that word even inside his own head. Not yet. Not when what he was about to say might take even the liking away.

"I refused. So they gave the job to a junior tech who redrew the hazard zones in green. Green means stable. Green means proceed. My maps went into an appendix. His went into the report. The report went to the board, the board signed off, and the concrete started pouring eight weeks later." He turned the case over in his hands. "I should have walked then. That's the version of me I'd like to believe exists—the one who walks. But I had a contract, and a reputation already bleeding from a previous dispute, and I told myself I could do more good from inside. Watching. Documenting. You know how that story ends. From inside is where they keep you."

The rain filled the silence for him. He let it.

"The dam failed fourteen months early. Not because the shale sheared—it did, eventually, that part of my maps was true—but because the eastern spillway gate assembly was sabotaged. Undersized bolts. Mismatched steel. Someone swapped certified components for scrap during construction and pocketed the difference, and the inspection reports were falsified all the way up. When the flood season hit, the gate housing let go, the abutment took the load it was never going to hold, and Kestrel Reach drowned in ninety minutes."

"Ninety minutes," Odile repeated. Her voice was flat in the way voices go flat when feeling would otherwise break them. "There were villages up there."

"There were eleven villages up there." He made himself say it plainly. She deserved plainness; everyone in this whole rotten affair had been given euphemism instead. "Evacuation orders existed. They were issued six hours after the breach instead of before it. Because issuing them before meant admitting the surveys were wrong, and admitting the surveys were wrong meant opening the contracts to audit, and an audit would have found the substituted steel. So they let the water come down on sleeping villages rather than open the books. And then they called it an act of God."

The cup stopped moving entirely now. Odile sat with it dripping in her lap, and in the dark her eyes had gone bright and hard.

"Our team," Emrys continued, "the survey team you were chartered to resupply—we weren't doing hydrology anymore. After the failure, the ministry sent us in to *assess*, officially. Unofficially, our brief was to establish that the geology was unforeseeable. To build the paper wall. But four of us had seen the original data. Marta Reyes, our field engineer. Tomas Ilic, the seismologist. Anwei Chao, who ran the drone surveys. And me. We found things in the wreckage we weren't supposed to find—gate fragments with the wrong alloy stamps, purchase orders that didn't match deliveries, a change-order ledger with signatures from three levels above our liaison. Anwei copied everything. Everything. Flight manifests, metallurgy reports, the green-inked maps next to my red ones. We were going to take it to the federal auditor on the coast."

"What happened?" Odile asked quietly, though he could see in her face that she had already begun to guess. She was a pilot. Pilots learned early how stories about weather ended.

"The night before we were scheduled to move downriver, Chief Ansel Roque called a security briefing. Told us there was militia activity in the valley, that we'd be safer splitting into two boats, that he'd run point and we should follow his marks." Emrys's jaw tightened until it ached. "It was monsoon season. He knew the forecast better than we did—he had the ministry's meteorological feed. He put us on the water directly ahead of the worst cell of the year, in boats he'd personally assigned, on a route he'd personally chosen. The flood surge came down the reach at two in the morning. Tomas and Anwei's boat went into a strainer in the first mile. Marta made it to high ground with half the evidence cache. Roque's boat was simply—gone. He radioed once, said he was swept downstream, and that was the last anyone official heard of Ansel Roque."

"But you don't think he was swept anywhere."

"I think Ansel Roque can read water the way I read contour lines, and I think he chose that route, that night, those boats, the way a man chooses a weapon." Emrys heard his own voice go cold and made no effort to warm it. "Marta and I hid in the reeds for six days. She had a bad gash from the wreck—femoral, ugly. On the fourth day it went septic. There was no medicine. Your supply runs were supposed to bring medicine to the lower station, and the lower station was supposed to relay it upriver, and the relay never happened because someone cancelled it." He met her eyes. "That charter that brought you here? It was the first supply flight authorized since the flood. They wanted the valley empty of witnesses and full of weather before anyone else came looking. You weren't sent to find us, Odile. You were sent so they could say a search had happened."

Her breath left her slowly. "And Marta?"

"Marta died on the fifth day. In my arms, in the reeds, twenty miles from a hospital, holding a hard drive that could have saved eleven villages' worth of justice if it had reached the coast a month earlier." He lifted the case slightly. "This is hers. This is what I ran with. What I've been running with. Every decision I've made since—every careful answer, every half-truth I fed you on the dock, every hour I spent deciding whether to stay a ghost—I was weighing your life against this drive. And I want you to understand that I chose wrong for six days. I let you fly blind into their trap because telling you felt more dangerous than using you."

* * *

The rain eased to something merely torrential. Water dripped through the fig canopy in fat, irregular drops, ticking against the hull like a slow clock.

Odile had not moved. The tin cup rested in her lap, forgotten, and her hands were folded over it as if in prayer, or as if holding something down that wanted to fly free.

"Say the rest," she said. "All of it. Don't curate it for me."

"All of it, then." He took a breath that hurt. "Roque didn't die in the flood. I saw him two days ago on that searchlight boat, which confirms what I already suspected—that he reported us dead, that the ministry has spent this whole time believing the evidence went to the bottom of the reach, and that the moment they learned a cartographer might have survived, they stopped calling it a search and started calling it containment. If they catch me, I don't get arrested. I get disappeared, the same way the team got disappeared. And anyone standing near me gets disappeared as a footnote."

"That's why you stepped out of the reeds when I landed." Her voice was very quiet. "You could have stayed hidden. Let me land, radio nothing, watch them write me off as another lost pilot. Instead you walked into the open."

"Because you were going to be killed either way, and alone you'd never know why." He shook his head. "No. That's not honest. The honest answer is that I'd been alone with a dead woman and a hard drive for six days, and when I saw your plane come down through that weather—flying like the storm was something you were arguing with and winning—I couldn't stand being a ghost one more hour. Selfishness, Odile. That's the rest of it. I told myself it was mercy. It was loneliness wearing mercy's coat."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she set the cup down, deliberately, the way she seemed to do everything—like each motion was paid for in advance—and looked at him with an expression he would carry for the rest of his life whatever happened next.

"Do you know what I inherited when my father died?" she said. "Not money. Not the plane, not really—the plane belongs to Harrow Voss until the note clears. I inherited a debt. A number with my name attached to it. And I have spent three years flying medicine into places nobody else will go, counting fuel like a miser, keeping my head so far down I've forgotten what the sky looks like from above the clouds—all so that some day a man in a linen suit will hand me a piece of paper and tell me I'm free." She laughed, short and bitter, and it cracked something in him to hear it. "And now you sit there and tell me that a government drowned eleven villages to protect a signature, that its security chief murdered a survey team to protect a cover-up, and that the same machinery is pointed at me. And here's the thing, Emrys. Here's the thing I can't make peace with."

"Tell me."

"I'm not surprised." Her voice didn't rise at all. "That's what I can't make peace with. Some part of me has always known the world worked this way—knew it the way you know the river is under the fog. I just thought if I stayed small enough, quiet enough, cheap enough to hire, it would never need anything from me. And it turns out neutrality was just a debt I hadn't been billed for yet."

Emrys leaned forward. The case sat between them like a third passenger.

"So choose," he said. "Not for me. I release you from me—you can take the boat at first light, run the gorge alone, be in the capital by Thursday, and swear you never saw my face. Roque might even believe you. Voss will certainly sell the story for you; he sells everything. You could have your old life back. Small, quiet, head down, alive." He held her gaze. "Or you could help me get this drive to the federal auditor, knowing that from the moment we move, every debt you owe and every debt you didn't know you owed comes due at once. I won't dress it up. One road is survival. The other is truth. I've watched men try to walk both at once and it's how teams end up in strainer logs."

The rain ticked. Somewhere out in the drowned forest, a bird that had no business being awake called once and fell silent.

Odile picked up the compass from around her neck—her father's compass, the brass worn bright at the edges by thirty years of a dead man's thumb—and held it in her palm, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, hunting north the way it always had, indifferent to floods and governments and the debts of the living.

"My father flew these rivers for forty years," she said at last, "and he never once told me what he believed in. I used to think that was wisdom." She closed her fist around the compass. "It was just fear with better manners. I'm done inheriting it."

She looked up, and the hardness in her face had become something else—not softness, nothing so simple, but a kind of settled weight, like ballast shifted forward.

"Tell me exactly where the auditor is," Odile Blackwood said, "and exactly what Roque knows about our route. Because if we're going to run this river, we're going to run it like a flight plan—weather, fuel, alternates, the whole thing. And Emrys—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed her face, "—you're going to teach me what's on that drive. Every page. If I'm going to burn my life down for the truth, I intend to know it better than the men who buried it."

He exhaled a breath he'd been holding for six days, and outside the hull, far upstream, faint beneath the rain, came the sound neither of them wanted to hear: an engine, low and patient, working its way down the gorge.

Two engines. Maybe three.

Odile's head came up. Her hand found the bail cup again, and her voice, when it came, was the voice of a pilot reading weather.

"They found the river," she said. "Bail fast, Emrys. School's starting early."
