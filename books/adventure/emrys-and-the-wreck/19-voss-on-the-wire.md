## 19. Voss on the Wire

The ranger relay sat on a knuckle of high ground where the river bent north, a squat concrete blockhouse with a corrugated roof and an antenna mast guyed with wire that sang in the wind like a badly tuned instrument. Odile had stopped there a dozen times over the years—topping off water, trading coffee for weather reports, letting the rangers' ancient radio set chew through its static. It was the kind of place that existed to be forgotten.

She beached the aluminum boat in the lee of the boat ramp, hull grinding on gravel, and stood in the shallows a moment longer than she needed to, letting the cold water climb her shins. Behind her, downriver, the drowned forest went on and on, black trunks standing in green water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down with all hands. Somewhere back there was the wrecked survey camp, the half-burned logs, the runway that no longer existed beneath six feet of floodwater. Somewhere back there was Emrys, asleep under the tarp they'd rigged in the boat's stern, his face turned toward the sky as if even unconscious he expected to be watched.

She hadn't slept either. Not really. She'd dozed in shifts, one hand always on the bail bucket, and every time she closed her eyes she saw the strainer log, the torn jacket, the dark stain on the bark that the rain kept trying to wash away and couldn't quite manage.

*Keep moving,* she told herself. *Get to the lower station. Get to a phone. Get home.*

Home. The word had a hollow ring to it now, like a drum with a crack in the skin. Home was a fuel-stained cot behind the hangar at Meridian Field and a ledger book with Harrow Voss's name in it. Home was a debt.

She waded ashore and shook out her sleeves.

* * *

The relay station was manned by a single ranger, a heavyset woman named Okonkwo who greeted Odile with the particular lack of surprise reserved for pilots—people who dropped out of the sky at irregular intervals wanting things. She had a kettle going and a shotgun within reach of her chair, and she looked Odile up and down with the frank assessment of someone who spent her days reading rivers and the people who came off them.

"You look like something the river spat back out," Okonkwo said.

"Close enough." Odile held up the battered thermos she'd brought as trade. "Coffee for ten minutes on your set. I need to raise Meridian Field."

Okonkwo took the thermos, weighed it in her palm, and shrugged. "Meridian's been having trouble with their receiver since the storms started. You'll get static and maybe half a message if you're lucky."

"Half is more than I've had in four days."

That earned her something almost like sympathy. Okonkwo jerked her chin toward the radio room—a closet of a space smelling of ozone and old cigarette smoke—and went back to her kettle.

Odile settled into the operator's chair, which had been broken in and re-broken by decades of bored rangers, and keyed the set. The band was a mess of atmospherics; the monsoon system that had trapped them in Kestrel Reach was still sprawling across the interior, throwing lightning somewhere far to the south, and every frequency carried its growl underneath the voices.

She worked through the calling channels methodically. Weather relay: nothing useful. The coastal aviation net: chatter about a closed harbor, a grounded mail run. Then, on the field frequency, buried in hash—

"—ile Blackwood, acknowledge. Repeat, aircraft registration seven-one-one-Kilo, owner of record Odile Blackwood, you are instructed to—"

Her heart did something complicated. Relief first—that was Voss's voice, or had been, before the storm chewed it up. Then a colder feeling sliding in behind the relief, because Voss did not call her by name on open channels. Voss did not use phrases like *owner of record*. Voss spoke to her in person, in his office above the chandlery, with his ledger open and his fountain pen uncapped, because he liked to watch her face when he talked about numbers.

She keyed the transmit switch. "Meridian, this is Blackwood. Say again your last. I'm coming through broken."

Static. A long wash of it, like surf. Then fragments, arriving in pieces, each one worse than the last:

"—lien filed this morning—"

"—Voss Holdings, against aircraft seven-one-one-Kilo—"

"—not authorized to release the aircraft without—"

"—where are you, Odile? Call me. *Call me.*"

The last words came through clean, which somehow made them worse. Clean words meant he'd leaned close to his microphone. Meant he'd wanted to be understood.

She sat with her hand still on the key, listening to the carrier wave hum, and felt the floor of the world tilt a few degrees.

A lien. On the plane.

Not on the debt—the debt had been abstract for years, a number in a book, a weight she carried the way you carry a stone in your pocket until you forget which pocket it's in. But the plane was not abstract. The plane was the *Kestrel*, the old bush taildragger her father had bought new the year she was born, the machine she'd learned to fly before she'd learned to swim, the only thing in the world that had never once lied to her. Her father had signed the note against it, and when he died the note had passed to Voss along with everything else, but there had always been a line, unspoken, that even Harrow Voss didn't cross: the plane flew, and while it flew, Odile paid.

Now the line was crossed. Now the plane belonged, on paper, to the man who owned her paper.

"Bad news?" Okonkwo stood in the doorway with two enamel mugs, steam rising off them.

Odile realized her hands were shaking. She folded them in her lap. "Someone's moved on my property."

"Family?"

"Worse. A businessman."

Okonkwo set both mugs down on the console, looked at Odile for a long moment, and then did the thing that river people do—she asked no questions. "There's a bunk in back if you want it. Storm cell's sitting over the middle reach till morning anyway. Nobody's flying anywhere."

Nobody's flying anywhere. The words landed differently than Okonkwo could have known. Odile picked up one of the mugs and drank, and the coffee was terrible and hot and exactly what she needed.

* * *

Emrys woke when she came back down to the boat, the way he always woke—all at once, silently, his eyes open before the rest of him moved. He'd done it three days running now, and every time it reminded her that whatever else he was, he'd been sleeping outdoors in a flooded valley for weeks, listening for footsteps.

"You were gone a long time," he said. His voice was rough with sleep. In the grey light under the tarp he looked younger and older at once, the exhaustion carved deep around his mouth.

"The relay has a radio." She climbed aboard, careful of the patched seam in the hull, and settled across from him with her knees drawn up. Rain ticked against the tarp like fingers testing a lock. "I got most of a message from Meridian Field."

He waited. That was a thing about him she was learning—he didn't fill silences, didn't rush her. He just made room for whatever she was going to say, and let her decide whether to put anything in it.

"Voss," she said. "The man who holds my father's debt. He's filed a lien on the plane. This morning, apparently. While I was—" She gestured vaguely downriver, at the drowned forest, the burned logs, the whole catastrophe of the last week. "While I was being shot at, roughly."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. "Can he do that?"

"He can do anything the paper says he can do, and he wrote the paper." She heard her own voice come out flat and bitter and didn't try to soften it. "My father borrowed against the aircraft to keep the business alive after the fuel crash. Voss bought the note cheap. Standard practice. And there's a clause—I've read it so many times I could recite it in my sleep—if the debtor defaults or flees jurisdiction, the holder may secure the collateral. Which means if I don't fly back to Meridian and sit in his office like a good little debtor, he takes the *Kestrel*. Legally. With a sheriff's receipt and everything."

"And if you do go back?"

She looked at him. Rain ran off the edge of the tarp between them, a thin silver thread.

"If I go back, I'm grounded. He'll hold the plane 'pending review,' and the review will take exactly as long as it suits him, and meanwhile Roque's people will know exactly where I am." She laughed, short and joyless. "You understand what he's done? He hasn't just taken my escape route. He's built me a funnel. Every road home now runs through his hands."

Emrys turned his mug slowly in his fingers. When he spoke, his voice was low. "I'm sorry. This is because of me. Because you pulled me out of the water instead of leaving me to be a problem Roque solved himself."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, and she saw him flinch—not much, just around the eyes, a man well practiced in absorbing blame. She made herself breathe. "No. Listen. I've been telling myself a story for three days, that I was neutral, that I was just a pilot who got caught in weather, that if I played it right I could hand you over to somebody official and fly home and none of this would ever touch me. That story's dead. Voss killed it this morning with a piece of paper, and honestly?" She stared out at the rain-hammered water. "It was dying anyway. You don't file a lien on a woman's plane because she owes you money. You file it because you want her where you can reach her. Somebody told him to."

Emrys went very still. "Roque can't have reached Meridian that fast."

"Roque doesn't need to. Voss sits on every board and drinks with every ministry man between here and the coast. Half the interior's paper passes through his chandlery." She heard herself say it and felt the shape of it, ugly and obvious. "You said your maps were redlined. You said the signatures went up the chain. Did any of those signatures belong to a supply contractor? Somebody who provisioned the survey?"

Emrys's eyes lifted to hers, and in them she watched the last of his caution die—not all at once, but the way a dam goes, a crack and then a decision. "Voss Provisioning held the survey contract," he said quietly. "Fuel, food, transport. Everything the team ate and burned came through his warehouses. I mapped the delivery schedules myself."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"So," Odile said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, very calm, the calm she found at altitude when the engine coughed and there was nothing below but trees. "The man who owns my debt provisioned the survey. The survey found what your maps said it would find. And the day I turn up missing in the flooded valley with the survey's disgraced cartographer in my boat, he moves to take my airplane." She shook her head slowly. "That's not coincidence. That's a man tidying up. I'm not a debtor anymore, Emrys. I'm loose paper."

* * *

They pushed off at first light, because staying still felt worse than moving, and because Okonkwo, pressing a wrapped parcel of hardtack and dried fish into Odile's hands at the ramp, had said only: "Middle reach is clear till noon. After that, don't be on the water."

The drowned forest swallowed them again. Odile took the stern oars and let Emrys navigate from the bow, his memory of the reach unfolding ahead of them channel by channel—the old cutline where the survey had run their seismic lines, the submerged fence post that marked a homestead now forty feet underwater, the bend where the current gathered itself and pushed hard toward the lower gorge. They spoke little. There was a new understanding between them that neither had named, and naming it felt dangerous, the way saying a word too soon can break a spell.

But Odile's mind would not stop working the problem, turning it over the way she turned a fuel calculation, looking for the error, the margin, the way through.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: she could not go home. Home was a funnel with Voss at the bottom of it. She could not stay here—the middle reach would flood again by nightfall, and Roque's boats were somewhere on this water, hunting a cartographer and, now, presumably, the pilot who'd carried him out. She had maybe a quarter tank of fuel waiting in the plane's tanks—no. The plane was on a runway under six feet of water at the head of the reach, and even if it weren't, flying it anywhere meant landing somewhere Voss's paper could follow.

Every route ended the same way: in someone else's hands.

Except.

She shipped the oars for a moment, letting the boat drift, and looked at Emrys's back, at the waterproof satchel he wore even in the boat, strapped across his chest like a second heart. The evidence. The redlined maps, the correspondence, whatever he'd carried out of the burning camp at the cost of eight lives and his own name. Roque wanted it. Voss, she now understood, needed it buried. Two men, two sets of hands, both reaching for the same thing—and both of them assuming that what they wanted was worth more to her than her freedom.

Voss had priced her freedom this morning. A lien. A signature. He thought he knew what she'd sell it back for.

She'd spent nine years believing him. Nine years of counting gallons, of flying medicine and gossip into settlements that paid in eggs and favors, of telling herself that if she just kept her head down and paid it off, one day the ledger would balance and she'd be free. Free to what? She'd never even let herself finish the sentence. That was the trick of a debt like that—it didn't just take your money. It took the future. It kept you so busy surviving the present that you forgot to want anything.

The rain thinned. Ahead, through the lifting mist, the drowned village emerged from the water the way it always did, roof peaks and a church spire and the topmost branches of an orchard, beautiful and unbearable. Emrys didn't turn around. His shoulders had gone rigid, and Odile let him have his silence.

But she found, watching him, that she had finished the sentence at last. Free to tell the truth. Free to burn the paper. Free to stand in front of men like Voss and Roque and say *no* in a voice that carried, and let the future be whatever it cost.

"Emrys," she said.

He turned. Water dripped from the brim of his hood. Behind him the dead village floated in its green silence, and beyond it the river ran on toward the coast, toward the lower station, toward whatever was waiting there.

"When we get to the lower station," she said, "there'll be a transmitter. And I've decided something."

He waited, making room, the way he did.

"I'm done paying," Odile said, and took up the oars, and rowed them both downriver into the thinning rain.
