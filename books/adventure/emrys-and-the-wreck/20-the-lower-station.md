## 20. The Lower Station

*Emrys*

The river widened as they came down out of the drowned forest, and Emrys felt the change in it the way a man feels a change in a room—the air different, the light different, the water carrying a new smell. Salt, faint and far off, threaded through the green rot of the flood. The lower station sat where the Kestrel emptied toward the coast, a scatter of tin roofs and pilings on the last dry knuckle of land before the mangroves took over everything.

He had drawn that station once. He had drawn every station on the survey's grid, every dock and fuel drum and radio mast, in clean ink on linen paper, and the ministry had stamped his drawings APPROVED and used them to plan the flood. He knew the lower station the way you know a face you've betrayed.

"Fuel drums are gone," Odile said.

She stood in the bow with the leaky boat's paddle, reading the shoreline the way she read weather. Emrys followed her gaze. The fuel cache that should have stood on the dock—four drums, rust-orange, stenciled with the survey's hawk—was not there. The dock itself was there, gray and wet, but the boathouse beside it stood with its doors open and empty, and the radio mast above it leaned at an angle that meant someone had cut the guy-wires.

"Stripped," he said.

"Or never restocked." Odile's voice was flat, careful. "Roque's people could have pulled everything when the survey went dark. Standard procedure. Deny the site."

"Or someone knew we were coming."

She didn't answer that. She didn't have to. They had been killing their lamp at night for two days, drifting in the black water while searchlights swept the channels, and a man did not spend two nights hiding from searchlights without starting to think in terms of *or someone knew*.

The station was a mile off, maybe less, across a stretch of open water that made Emrys's skin crawl. No cover. No drowned trees to slip behind. Just brown water and gray sky and the low drumming of rain that had never quite stopped since the monsoon closed the valley behind them.

"Radio mast's leaning," he said, "but the building under it might still have a working set. The survey kept a relay here. If we can get a message to the ranger post at—"

"I know where the ranger post is." Odile shipped the paddle and looked at him. Rain ran off her chin. "Emrys. Look at the smoke."

He looked. He had been looking at the smoke for ten minutes without letting himself name it—a thin gray thread rising from behind the station's largest roof, too steady to be a cooking fire, too low to be a signal. Someone was there. Someone was warm and dry and patient.

"Could be rangers," he said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"Rangers don't strip the fuel drums and leave the generator running."

The generator. He listened, and beneath the rain he heard it—the small diesel knock of a generator carrying flat across the water. The station was alive. The station was *waiting*.

Odile turned the boat.

"Odile—"

"There's a channel on the far side." She was already paddling, hard, the leaking hull hissing around her strokes. "Old logging cut, comes around behind the station through the mangroves. My father used to run it when the main channel silted up. We can come at the boathouse from the blind side, see what we're walking into before we walk into it."

"And if what we're walking into is Roque?"

"Then I'd rather see him first." She glanced back at him, and in her face was something he had learned to read over a week of bailing water and sharing silence: not fear, exactly. The refusal of fear. "You said it yourself last night. There's no neutral water left in this river. So we choose our ground."

He took up the second paddle. The boat was taking water faster than it should, the patch he'd set on the hull two days ago weeping at its edges, and he bailed with the tin cup while they crossed the open mile with the station's smoke standing in his vision like a held breath.

* * *

*Odile*

The logging cut was exactly as her father had left it, which was to say: half-drowned, overgrown, and passable if you knew the three places where the current tried to eat you. Odile took them through on memory and nerve, the mangroves closing overhead into a dripping green tunnel, the boat's keel kissing submerged roots she could not see but somehow felt coming, the way she always had. Her father's compass sat open in her lap between strokes. The needle held steady on the cut's bearing, and she found herself talking to it in her head the way she had since she was nine years old. *Steady, old man. Get us through this one.*

Emrys bailed. He had stopped asking questions somewhere behind the second bend, and she was grateful, because the answers were ugly. She had run the geometry in her head a dozen times since the smoke: the fuel drums gone, the mast cut, the generator running. That wasn't abandonment. That was preparation. Somebody had turned the lower station into a room with one door, and then stood behind the door.

She thought about Voss's garbled message from the ranger relay—the lien on her plane, the smug static-warped voice of the operator reading it out—and she felt the same cold clarity she'd felt then settle over her now. Voss had sold her route home. Roque was waiting at the only place left to run. The river had narrowed around her like a trap designed by an accountant, and the only thing left to decide was how she went through it: dragged, or on her own feet.

The cut opened onto a black-water lagoon behind the boathouse. Odile backed the paddle and let the boat drift to a stop in the shadow of the mangroves, fifty meters from the station's rear wall, close enough to hear the generator plainly now and something else under it—voices. Two, maybe three. Low. Unhurried. Men who were not worried about anything.

She counted the buildings by memory of her father's stories: the boathouse, big enough for two boats and a workbench; the bunkhouse behind it; the radio shack on stilts between them, its ladder down. The rear of the boathouse had a door and one small window, glass long gone, screened with rusted wire. Through the wire, dim, she could see the shape of the survey's own aluminum boat on its trailer, and beside it, stacked with military neatness, crates.

And leaning against the workbench in the middle distance, arms crossed, watching the front of the station through the open doors as if he were watching a stage—Chief Ansel Roque.

She had never seen him before. She knew him instantly. He was built like the flood itself: broad, patient, wearing the water's gray as a uniform. A rifle lay across a crate beside him, not held, just near, the way a man keeps a tool he expects to use.

"Odile," Emrys breathed. He had seen too. His face had gone the color of the water.

"Three men I can count," she whispered. "Maybe four. One's on the dock—I saw his boots through the floor gaps. Roque's inside. There'll be one more somewhere high, watching the river approach. They're watching the front door of the river, Emrys. They think we come down the main channel or not at all."

"They'd be right to."

"Good." She felt her own mouth do something that was almost a smile and did not feel like one. "Then they're not watching the back."

She laid out the shape of it in whispers, hands moving in the small grammar pilots used in loud cockpits. The boat would drift along the mangrove edge to the boathouse's blind corner. They would go up and over the low rear porch, into the boathouse, and take the radio shack from behind. Emrys would send one message on the survey relay—plain, short, the coordinates of the drowned camp and one sentence about the redlined maps, sent on open frequency so it went everywhere at once. Roque could burn the station; he couldn't burn a signal already loose in the air.

"And then?" Emrys whispered.

"And then we take the aluminum boat on the trailer, because it doesn't leak, and we go downriver through the mangrove delta where a big patrol boat can't follow, and we're at the coast by dark."

"And if it goes wrong?"

She looked at him. Rain dripped through the mangrove canopy between them, and his eyes were steady on hers, and she thought: *a week ago I didn't know your name, and now I can't imagine the river without you in it.*

"Then it goes wrong," she said. "Paddle."

* * *

*Emrys*

The drift to the boathouse took four minutes and lasted a year. Emrys lay low in the bilge with the tin cup in his hand, bailing in silence, watching the rusted rear porch grow from a smudge to a shape to a thing with boards and a rail and a door standing ajar on darkness. The generator knocked. The voices inside murmured. Once, a laugh—short, hard—and he recognized the timbre of it even through the wall, because he had sat across a map table from Ansel Roque for eight months, and Roque laughed exactly that way when he crossed out another man's work.

The porch rail touched the hull with a sound like a knuckle on wood, and Emrys's heart stopped, and nothing happened. No shout. No light. The rain kept up its gray static, and the generator kept its knock, and the two of them went over the rail one at a time, Odile first, moving with a low economy that told him she had done things like this before, or at least imagined them so many times the body had learned them.

Inside the boathouse, the dark smelled of diesel and mildew and, beneath it, the sweet rot of the flood. The aluminum boat sat on its trailer like a promise. The crates beside it were stamped with the ministry's hawk, and one of them stood open: rope, flares, a first-aid kit, rations packed for a patrol that had never left. Roque had prepared to hunt them for days. That was the detail Emrys knew he would carry to the inquiry, if he lived to give one—not the rifle, but the rations. The patience. The paperwork of it.

Odile touched his arm and pointed. The interior door to the radio shack stood at the boathouse's front corner, and through its gap came a slice of lamplight and the murmur of voices and the long shadow of a man pacing.

Emrys had imagined this moment for a week—the moment he stopped running and started testifying—and he had imagined it in a hearing room, dry and formal, with his maps unrolled and his voice steady. He had not imagined it crouched in a flooded boathouse with a tin cup in his hand. But the maps were in the waterproof tube strapped across his back, and his voice, when he used it, would be steady enough. That part he could control. He had learned in the valley that the rest was weather.

Odile moved to the interior door. Emrys followed. Through the gap: the radio shack, cramped, the set's dials glowing a soft green, one man in a gray uniform seated with his back half-turned, headset on, logging something in a ledger with the bored diligence of a man who had been waiting too long. The second man—the laugher—stood by the front windows with a rifle slung, watching the main channel.

Odile looked at Emrys and held up three fingers. Then she pointed at the seated man, at herself. Pointed at the window man, at Emrys. Pointed at the tube on his back, then out the rear door, at the aluminum boat. *You send. We go.*

He nodded. His hands were not shaking. He noted that with distant surprise, the way he had noted, in the valley, that his hands had not shaken when the water came.

Odile went through the door like weather.

What happened in the next six seconds, Emrys would later be able to reconstruct only in pieces. The seated man went down without a sound under Odile's forearm, headset and all, and Odile's hand was already on the transmitter, and Emrys was three steps into the shack with his fingers finding the frequency dial he himself had calibrated a year ago—and then the man at the window turned.

Not because of noise. Because of the generator.

It died. In the sudden silence, every small sound in the boathouse became enormous, and the man at the window heard Emrys's boot on the floorboards, and the man at the window was Ansel Roque, and Emrys understood in one cold flash that the laugher had been the decoy, that Roque had never been watching the front of the river at all. Roque had been waiting in the room with the only radio.

"Evening, cartographer," Roque said, and brought the rifle up.

Emrys threw the workbench crate at him.

It was not a plan. It was eighty pounds of rope and flares moving through the air, and Roque was fast—Roque was terribly fast—but even fast men step back from eighty pounds of anything, and the shot went through the crate and through the boathouse's tin wall with a crack that split the world, and then Odile was there, and the lamp was going over, and the shack went black except for the green dials and the muzzle flash.

He heard himself shouting the coordinates. He didn't remember deciding to. His hands were on the transmitter's key, and his mouth was giving the drowned camp's position to the open channel, to every pilot and ranger and gossiping shortwave set on the coast—*Kestrel Reach, grid seven, survey camp, evidence of sabotage, redlined maps, Roque, Roque, Roque*—and the key clicked under his fingers with a rhythm he knew from a hundred routine position reports, except that this one was a scream.

The second shot took the transmitter. He felt it die under his hand, a spark and a smell of hot copper, and then Odile had his collar and was hauling him backward through the dark, and the boathouse was full of muzzle flash and shouting and the third man from the dock coming through the front doors firing at shadows.

"Boat!" Odile screamed. "Boat, boat, *boat*—"

They got the aluminum boat off the trailer in a chaos of scraping metal, and Roque was in the doorway behind them, and Emrys heard him say, over the gunfire, in a voice of perfect bureaucratic calm: "Ms. Blackwood. Your father's debt is transferable. Remember that."

Then Odile fired back—her father's shotgun from the plane's survival kit, which she had carried every day since the valley and never once mentioned—and the doorway filled with splinters, and Emrys got the outboard started on the third pull with Roque's bullets walking across the water toward them, and the boathouse, struck somewhere in its diesel stores, went up.

The explosion lifted the night. Heat rolled across the lagoon, and the station's tin roofs lit orange from below, and the mangroves threw shadows like hands. Odile ran the boat hard for the delta channels with the fire growing in the mirror of the water behind them, and Emrys crouched in the bow with the evidence tube strapped to his back and the taste of copper and smoke in his mouth, and he understood that the man who had been hunting them had just burned his own station to keep them from it, and that there was no longer any version of the river in which they went back.

"Odile." His voice came out ragged. "The transmitter—did it—"

"Doesn't matter." She was steering by the fire's light, jaw set, eyes forward. "Open channel. If one set on this coast heard ten seconds of you, they heard enough. Marta listens to everything."

Behind them, the lower station collapsed into the lagoon with a sound like the valley's flood given a voice, and ahead of them the mangrove delta opened its black channels into the dark, and somewhere in that dark, Emrys knew, Roque's patrol boat was already running—because a man who burns a station does not stand in the ashes. He moves to the next door.

The first flare went up over the delta a minute later. Red, high, deliberate. Not a distress signal.

A mark. *There they are.*

Odile saw it too. He watched her see it—watched her shoulders drop half an inch and then set again, harder.

"Rapids are past the delta," she said. "The gorge. If we can reach the gorge before his boats reach us, nothing he has can follow us through."

"And if we can't?"

She opened the throttle all the way, and the leaking world fell away behind them, and she said, "Then we'd better learn to fly."
