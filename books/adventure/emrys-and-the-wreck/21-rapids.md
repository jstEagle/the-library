## 21. Rapids

The river told them before the sound did.

Odile felt it first in the hull—a tremor through the aluminum, a shiver like an animal sensing weather—and then in the water itself, which had been brown and sluggish all morning and was suddenly moving with purpose, sliding past the gunwale faster, tilting toward something ahead. The drowned forest thinned on both banks, the dead trunks giving way to black basalt walls that leaned inward, and between them the river narrowed from a mile of floodwater to a throat.

"That's the gorge," Emrys said quietly. He had his map folded open across his knees, the one he'd drawn himself two years ago, ink bleeding where the rain had gotten at it. "Kestrel Narrows. I mapped it before the dam went in. It wasn't supposed to exist anymore."

"Wasn't supposed to?"

"The plan was to drown it. Forty meters of water over the top." He looked up at the walls rising wet and sheer on either side, streaked with old flood lines, and there was something in his face she'd learned to read by now—the look of a man checking his own work against what the world had actually done. "The water's down. Someone opened the spillways."

Odile shipped the oars and listened. Under the ordinary voice of the river there was another sound now, deeper, continuous, like a train running inside the earth. Her father had taken her through gorges when she was small enough to sit on the fuel cans, and she remembered him saying: *the water tells you what it's going to do, if you're willing to hear it.* She was hearing it. It was going to do something terrible.

"How bad?" she asked.

Emrys turned so he could see downstream, one hand resting on the gunwale, fingers spread as though he could feel the current's intentions through the metal. "Bad enough that we should have portaged. There isn't anywhere to portage anymore—the trail's under six years of silt." A pause. "We ride it or we find an eddy and wait it out, and waiting means Roque's boats catch us in a dead channel."

So that was the choice. It was always the same shape of choice lately: the thing that might kill you now, or the thing that would certainly kill you later. Odile found she was already leaning into the current, already choosing, her body deciding before her fear caught up.

"Then we ride it," she said. "Tell me what to expect."

* * *

He talked her through it the way she imagined he must once have briefed the survey team, calm and precise, pointing out features that were half memory and half guesswork because the maps he'd made were locked in a ministry drawer somewhere and the river had spent six years rearranging itself. The first drop was a ledge—two feet, maybe three, nothing, except that below it the water piled into a standing wave they'd have to punch through square. Then a bend to the left around a rib of rock the locals used to call the Old Man's Shoulder, and after the bend—

"After the bend I don't know," Emrys admitted. "That section flooded deepest. Whatever the dam didn't drown, the last six years of high water rebuilt. There'll be wood. There's always wood."

Wood. Strainers, her father called them—trees swept into the current that let water pass and held everything else. She'd seen what a strainer did to a boat once, from the air, from the safe remove of three hundred feet: a cedar trunk lying just proud of the surface, invisible until you were on it, and beneath it a raft wrapped like foil around a bone. Two men had died that day. She'd circled the wreck for twenty minutes before flying on to report it, and she had never forgotten the smallness of the boat against the tree, the way the river simply continued on the other side as if nothing had happened.

They ran the ledge. It was nothing and then it was everything—the bow lifted, hung for a heartbeat in that weightless moment every pilot knows and dreads, and then the wave took them full in the chest of the boat and the world went white. Cold drove down her collar, into her sleeves, found every seam. She heard Emrys grunt, heard the bail bucket go skittering, and then they were through, wallowing in the trough, shipping half a ton of green water that the leak in the stern was already working to make permanent.

"Bail!" she shouted, and hauled on the oars, and the boat answered—slowly, heavily, a wounded thing, but it answered.

The bend came fast. Too fast. The Old Man's Shoulder was bigger than Emrys remembered, or the water was higher, or both; the channel squeezed between the rock and the left-hand wall until it seemed no wider than the plane's wingspan, and the current funneled into it with a sound like tearing cloth. Odile set her line the way she'd set a thousand approaches—pick your point, commit, don't look at the rocks you're not using—and for ten seconds it was almost flying, almost familiar, the same grammar of commitment and correction.

Then the eddy line caught the stern and swung them.

She felt it happen the way you feel a stall begin—that lurch of wrongness before the instruments confirm it. The bow came right when it needed to stay left. She pulled, leaned, pulled again, and the oar bit and the boat hesitated, and in that hesitation the river reached up and took a vote of its own.

The hole was maybe four feet deep as holes went. It was enough. The bow dropped into it, buried, and the water behind them stood up taller than a man, and Emrys was there and then he was not.

No cry. That was the worst part, afterward—the absence of any sound at all. One moment his weight was in the boat, that lean quiet presence she had begun without noticing to orient herself around, and the next moment the river had simply subtracted him, cleanly, the way fog takes a hilltop. The boat kicked free of the hole and spun, and Odile was alone in it, hauling on oars that suddenly weighed nothing because there was no one else pulling the world's other end.

"EMRYS!"

Her own voice sounded foreign, torn out of her. She got the boat straightened—some animal part of her hands still doing their work even while the rest of her screamed—and swept the water behind them. White foam. Black rock. The boil of the hole folding in on itself, closing like a mouth.

Nothing.

* * *

Later she would not remember deciding to turn. She would remember only fragments: the oar snapping back against her wrist; the walls blurring past; the cold climbing her arms as she leaned out over the gunwale until the river breathed against her ribs. She knew what you were supposed to do—never go in after a man in whitewater, never trade one life for two, the rules existed because the rules were written in other people's blood—but her hands did not consult the rules. Her hands had been holding things together since she was nineteen years old and signing papers she hadn't read, and they were not about to start letting go now.

"There!" 

A fleck of orange—the survey jacket, the one he'd worn since the camp, faded to the color of weak tea but still orange enough. It surfaced thirty yards downstream, at the tail of the hole, and went under again, and her heart went under with it.

She rowed like she had never rowed, like the debt collectors and the ministry and Ansel Roque and every ledger ever kept against her name had all dissolved into the single arithmetic of distance. The current helped for once, carrying them both toward the same future at different speeds, and she cut the angle, aiming below him the way you aim below a swimmer, leading the target, and the gap closed—twenty yards, fifteen, ten—

His hand came out of the water.

Just the hand, fingers spread, reaching for nothing, and it was the most terrible thing she had ever seen because it meant he was conscious, meant he was fighting, meant he was drowning in slow motion instead of fast. She swung the bow alongside, overshot, corrected, and threw herself flat along the gunwale with the river trying to peel her off the boat, and she caught his wrist.

He was heavy. Drowning men are heavy—it's the water in the clothes, the water in the lungs, the whole river riding on them—and for a long black moment she held his wrist and the river held him and she understood, with perfect clarity, that she was not strong enough. Her shoulders burned. Her grip began to fail finger by finger, and the boat yawed sideways, taking water, and some cold rational voice in her skull observed that this was how it happened, this exact moment, this was how two people became zero people.

"No," she said out loud, to the voice, to the river, to her father's ghost. "No. Not him. Not this."

She got her other hand on his forearm, then his sleeve, then a fistful of the jacket itself, and she did the thing her body knew from hauling cargo nets and engine cowlings—she stopped lifting and started rolling, throwing her weight backward, using the boat's mass instead of her arms'. The gunwale dipped to within a finger's width of the water. The river poured across her back, down her spine, into her boots, and then Emrys came over the side like a landed fish, all at once, and they crashed together into the bilge in a tangle of limbs and rope and the bail bucket, and the boat, freed of their struggle, wobbled drunkenly upright and ran on.

For a while neither of them moved. They lay in six inches of icy water among the survey gear, and the gorge walls slid past overhead, impossibly tall, impossibly quiet after the roar, and Odile discovered she was shaking so hard her teeth chattered and she couldn't tell if it was the cold or the rest of it.

Emrys coughed. A long, racking, ugly cough, the kind that comes from the bottom of a man, and river water came with it, and then air, blessed air, dragging in through his throat in ragged pulls. His eyes opened. They were unfocused, then focused, and the first thing they found was her face above his, and he stared at it as if memorizing it, as if it were a map of somewhere he'd been trying to reach for a long time.

"You came back for me," he said. His voice was gravel and water.

"You fell out of my boat." Her voice cracked in the middle of it. "That's—there are rules about that."

"You were supposed to run it. You were supposed to—" Another cough. He pushed himself up on one elbow, and she saw with a distant, clinical part of her mind that there was blood on his temple, a shallow gouge where the rocks had written their opinion of him. "Odile. You turned around. In the gorge. Nobody turns around in the gorge."

"I know." She was still shaking. She pressed her palms flat against her thighs to stop it and it didn't work. "I know nobody does. I did anyway."

Something moved through his expression then, something that had been held carefully shut since the reeds, since the camp, since the first wary handshake in the rain—something that looked, in the gray light between the canyon walls, like a door coming open. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. She had spent her whole adult life around men who explained themselves at length, officials and creditors and chiefs, men who filled every silence with words the way the government filled valleys with water, and here was this one, saying nothing, meaning everything, and it undid her more thoroughly than the river had.

She checked him instead of speaking, because checking was something her hands knew how to do. Pupils equal. Pulse fast but steady. Ribs—she pressed along them gently and he hissed but nothing shifted wrong. The head wound was ugly and shallow. He'd live. The knowledge hit her like warmth spreading through frozen hands, painful and sweet at once.

"You scared me," she said, and it came out smaller than she intended, and truer.

"I know." He reached up—one arm trembling with the effort—and touched her wrist, right where her pulse hammered, where she'd gripped him, where the marks of her own fingers were already darkening on his skin. "I felt you let go once. And then I felt you not let go."

* * *

The gorge relented gradually, the way storms do, the walls falling back and the water spreading out and slowing until it was a river again instead of a verdict. They bailed. They always bailed; it had become the rhythm of their days, the tide of their small country of two—bail, row, watch, bail—and now they bailed in silence, side by side, and the silence was different than it had been yesterday. Yesterday's silence had been two strangers conserving words. This silence was full.

The light was going gold and long when Odile finally spoke, because keeping it inside had become harder than saying it.

"When you went under," she said, watching the water slide by instead of watching him, "there was about four seconds where I thought—I thought the river was going to keep you. And I realized I couldn't—" She stopped. Started again. "Everything I've got is mortgaged, Emrys. The plane, the route, the name. Voss owns pieces of me I haven't even found yet. But that four seconds was the first time in six years I've known exactly, completely, what I couldn't afford to lose."

Emrys set down the bail bucket. Water dripped from it, counting seconds. He looked at her with the river-light on his face and the blood drying at his temple, and he said, very quietly:

"I've carried those maps for two years. Every day, everywhere, knowing what they cost and what they'd cost to use. I told myself it was about the villages. About the truth. It was." He paused. "But today, underwater, with the whole river standing on my chest—what I thought about was you on the bank at the camp, telling me to hold the line tighter. That's what I swam for. Not the evidence. You."

Neither of them moved. The boat drifted. Somewhere upstream, faint and fading, the gorge still muttered to itself, and ahead the drowned valley opened its gray-green miles, and between those two facts sat everything they had not yet said aloud, sitting between them like a third passenger, patient, unhurried, certain of its destination.

It was Emrys who broke the stillness—not with words but with a frown, turning his head slowly toward the eastern bank, where the forest came down to the water in a solid dark wall.

"Odile," he said. "Do you smell smoke?"
