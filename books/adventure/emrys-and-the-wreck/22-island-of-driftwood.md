## 22. Island of Driftwood

The river spat them out the way a dog spits out something it decided not to swallow.

Odile felt it first as a change in the water's temper—the rapids behind them still roaring through her ribs, but here the current spread wide and went lazy, braiding itself around gravel bars that surfaced from the flood like the backs of sleeping animals. The boat wallowed. Her arms were shaking so badly she could barely hold the oar, and Emrys was slumped against the thwart, coughing river out of his lungs in wet, ragged pulls, one hand still knotted white-knuckled in the gunwale where she'd hauled him aboard.

"Island," she managed. Her voice sounded like someone else's, scraped raw from shouting his name over the water. "Left side. Gravel."

He nodded without opening his eyes.

She rowed them in on the last of whatever she had left, and the hull ground against shingle with a sound like relief. Odile rolled over the side into knee-deep water—cold enough to make her gasp—and dragged the boat up the bar by its bow line while Emrys lay breathing in the bottom of it like a landed fish. The island was nothing: a tongue of gray gravel and driftwood maybe sixty meters long, ringed with drowned saplings whose tops just broke the surface, their leaves going brown. But it was above the waterline, and after the gorge, above the waterline felt like a country of its own.

When the boat was secure she sat down hard beside it, right there in the wet shingle, and laughed—a short, cracked, unlovely sound that had no humor in it at all.

* * *

Emrys watched her laugh and thought it was the bravest thing he'd seen all day, and he had spent the day watching her pull him out of a river that wanted him dead.

He'd gone under twice in the rapids. He remembered the second time best, or worst: the green churn, the roar pressing on his eardrums like thumbs, a strainer log sliding past overhead with its whole crown of branches combing the current. He'd thought, with strange clarity, *so this is where the survey team went—not drowned by the dam, but by this, by the ordinary violence of water.* And then a hand had closed on his collar, and Odile Blackwood—who weighed perhaps half what he did—had hauled him over the gunwale with a strength that could only have come from fury.

Now she sat in the gravel laughing at nothing, soaked to the bone, hair plastered to her face, and he understood that the laugh was the sound a person made when the alternative was screaming.

"We need a fire," he said. His voice came out ruined. He tried again. "Before dark. Hypothermia kills more people than rivers do."

"I know." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "I've flown supply runs to trapper camps for six years, Emrys. I know what cold does."

"I know you know. I'm saying it out loud so I don't lie down."

That got a real look out of her—one eyebrow, wry even through exhaustion. "Fair," she said, and got up.

The island gave them what they needed, because islands like this always did; the river built them out of everything it stole. Driftwood in every size, some of it bleached silver and light as bone, some waterlogged and useless. Emrys gathered an armload while Odile dug a fire pit below the high-water line and lined it with stones, and when she struck the lighter—her father's, brass, worn smooth—he saw her thumb rest on it a half-second longer than striking a flame required. A habit. A conversation with a dead man. He didn't ask. He had his own dead he talked to silently.

The fire caught on the third try and grew, and they stripped to their underlayers and hung everything else on driftwood stakes like a settlement of small, soggy ghosts. Emrys turned his shirt slowly, watching steam lift off it, and became aware that Odile was looking at him—at the map of bruises the river had printed across his ribs, yellow-green already blooming.

"You should have told me you can't swim," she said.

"I can swim."

"Not in that water. Nobody swims in that water. You went in like a man who'd already decided—" She stopped. Looked away. Fed a stick to the fire harder than the fire needed.

"Decided what?"

"That if you went under, it would save everyone a lot of trouble."

The words came out flatter than he intended, which was how his worst truths always came out. Odile stared at him across the flames, and he watched her decide whether to be angry. He'd seen people make that decision about him before. Usually they chose anger; it was simpler.

Instead she said, "Well. It didn't. So you're stuck with the trouble," and threw him his shirt.

* * *

They ate what the boat's salvage offered: a tin of condensed milk they split with cupfuls of river water, half a packet of crackers gone soft, and the emergency ration bar from the survey kit that tasted like sweetened chalk. Odile chewed mechanically and worked the sun's angle against the ridge of cloud to the west. Two hours of light, maybe three. The storm bands had moved off downstream ahead of them, leaving a sky scrubbed pale and enormous, the first clean sky since the monsoon wall had closed the valley days ago—or years ago; time had gone strange somewhere around the second night of bailing.

While the light held, they worked on the hull.

Emrys turned the boat on its side and found what the rapids had done to it: a long split along the starboard seam, opened and shut again by the impact that had thrown him out, the rivets weeping a thin rust stain down the aluminum. Not fatal. Not trustworthy either. He ran his thumbnail along the crack and thought about Roque's boats somewhere behind them, and the lower station somewhere ahead, and the distance between those two facts measured now in a hull that leaked.

"Epoxy's gone," Odile said, crouched beside him. She'd salvaged the survey's tool roll from the wrecked camp; she laid it out on the shingle with the care of a surgeon. "We used the last of it on the first patch."

"There's pine pitch upstream, in the burned timber. Too far."

"So we improvise." She was quiet a moment, turning things over in the tool roll. Then: "Canvas. We soak canvas in the milk fat, press it into the seam, rivet a strip over it. It'll hold for calm water. It won't hold for anything else."

"It has to hold for calm water. That's all I'm asking it to do."

She glanced at him sideways. "You used to say things like that about the dam, didn't you? In your head. *It only has to hold.*"

Emrys's hands went still on the hull. The fire popped. Somewhere downstream a bird cried once, thin and lonely, and didn't repeat itself.

"Yes," he said. "I used to say exactly that. And then I learned the difference between a thing holding and a thing being held together by everyone agreeing not to look too closely." He picked up the drill. "Give me the canvas."

They worked as the light went amber and then copper, and there was a rhythm to it that surprised him—she held, he drilled; she cut strips, he pressed them home; neither of them had to explain or apologize for the other's silence. He had worked with survey crews for nine years and had never once found this rhythm with any of them. With Odile it arrived without negotiation, the way two instruments find a key.

It was full dark when she sat back on her heels, blew a strand of hair out of her face, and said, "There. Ugly, but it'll float."

"It floated before," Emrys said. "That's not the same as floating well."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." And something in the way she said it made him think she wasn't talking about the boat anymore either.

* * *

Odile woke once in the night to find Emrys sitting up by the coals, feeding them driftwood one piece at a time, keeping the fire alive the way she kept her fuel ledger alive—by refusing to let it drop below a line.

She'd taken the first watch and given him the second, and she hadn't meant to sleep, but sleep had taken her anyway, deep and dreamless, the first such sleep since the valley. Now she lay wrapped in the survey tarp and watched him across the embers: the bruised ribs, the careful way he moved, the face that was beginning to stop being a stranger's face. When had that happened? Somewhere between the reeds and the rapids, she supposed. Somewhere in all that bailing water out of a leaking boat at three in the morning, trading stories because the stories were the only thing keeping their hands moving.

"You're supposed to be asleep," he said, without turning.

"You're supposed to wake me for my watch."

"I was going to."

"Liar."

A ghost of a smile. He fed another stick to the coals. Above them the sky had cleared completely, and the stars came down to the horizon on every side, doubled faintly in the flooded forest where the water lay flat as black glass. It was beautiful the way the valley was beautiful, she thought—which was to say beautifully wrong, a drowned world wearing its drowning like jewelry.

"Emrys." His name in her mouth still felt new. "Back on the boat. You asked me why I fly these runs. Why I don't just... leave. Sell the plane, walk away from the debt."

"I remember."

"I never answered you."

"You answered me. You said the debt isn't money, it's a promise you didn't make." He turned then, firelight moving on his face. "I've been thinking about that."

"Don't hurt yourself."

He almost laughed. Almost. "My father signed things too," he said. "Contracts, affidavits, the final map certification. He believed a signature was sacred. I used to believe that. It's why I redlined the foundations instead of walking off the job—because I thought the signature bound me to tell the truth, not to obey." He looked down at his hands. "Roque's people think a signature binds you to obey. That's the whole difference between us, maybe. What we think our names are for."

Odile pulled the tarp tighter. Her father's compass sat in the tool roll near her head, and she touched it without quite deciding to, the way you touch a doorframe passing through.

"My father's signature is on that note Voss holds," she said. "Forty thousand. He signed it to keep the plane when the bank called in everything else. And then he died, and the note passed to Voss, and I've been paying it ever since like it was mine. Like grief was a loan I had to service." She heard her own voice go dry and flat, the voice she used for numbers. "Six years. Do you know what forty thousand buys in this business? Fuel. Parts. Another season. Always another season. I keep thinking if I'm careful enough, invisible enough, small enough, I'll get free of it. But the finish line moves. It always moves."

"And now?"

"Now Voss has put a lien on my plane, and Roque wants us both at the bottom of the river, and I'm lying on a gravel bar in the middle of a drowned valley having a conversation I'd have paid money to avoid a month ago." She looked at him. "So I don't know what 'now' is. Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow we hit the lower station."

"Then ask me tonight."

The fire settled. Emrys was quiet for a long moment, and she realized he was choosing his words with the same care he gave maps—with the understanding that everything drawn could be redrawn, but only honestly, only once.

"What are we, Odile?" he said finally. "You and me. Because I've been cataloguing it for days and I can't file it anywhere. We're not strangers—we've shared a boat and a war. We're not partners—that implies terms, and we haven't set any. We're not..." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I watch you work and I forget what I'm afraid of. That's not nothing. But I don't know what to call it, and I've learned the hard way what happens to things nobody names."

Odile lay back and looked at the stars, and let the question sit there between them, warm as a coal.

"When I was a kid," she said, "my father told me the compass doesn't point north. Everyone says it does, but it doesn't—it points magnetic north, which wanders. It's been wandering for as long as anyone's measured it. The pole moves. So a compass isn't a thing that tells you where north *is*. It's a thing that agrees with you about where north is *today*, and you correct for the rest." She turned her head to look at him. "I think that's what we are. Not lovers. Not strangers. Two people who agree about where north is today. We correct as we go."

Emrys considered that with the seriousness he brought to everything, and she braced herself for him to take it apart, to map its flaws.

"That's the best definition I've ever heard," he said simply. "Can I use it?"

"It's yours. Everything I have is salvage anyway."

He smiled then—really smiled, the first one she'd seen that reached his eyes—and for a moment the firelight made him look young, unburdened, a man who had not spent months carrying evidence that could hang a ministry. She wanted to keep that version of him. She suspected, with a pilot's pragmatism, that versions of people couldn't be kept, only flown alongside.

"Sleep," she said. "Your watch is over. I'll take the rest."

"You need—"

"*Sleep.* We hit the station at dawn, and I want your eyes working when we get there."

He lay down. Within minutes his breathing slowed and deepened, trust arriving in the body before the mind signed off on it, and Odile sat alone with the fire and the doubled stars and the boat riding easy on the shingle, patched ugly and holding.

*Belonging,* she thought. She'd spent six years believing it was a thing you bought, in installments, and that the note would come due someday and someone would take it back. But here was a man asleep beside her who owed her nothing and whom she owed nothing, and here was a fire she'd built with her own hands on land owned by no one, and here was the strange, unearned fact that she was not afraid.

Not of the dark. Not of the water. Not, for one whole hour, of the ledger.

She fed the fire another stick and watched the east, where the sky would lighten first, where the lower station waited with whatever Roque had left inside it.

The stars began to fade at the edges. Dawn was coming, and it was bringing company.
