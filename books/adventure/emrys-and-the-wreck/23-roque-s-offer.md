## 23. Roque's Offer

Ansel Roque had learned long ago that the river gave nothing away for free.

He stood on the aft deck of the *Cormorant*, the survey's patrol boat, and watched the shingle bar through binoculars as the last light failed behind the western ridges. Two figures moved down there—small at this distance, gray against the gray of the driftwood and the rain-slick stones. One tall and deliberate, the other quick, restless, always in motion even when she was standing still. The pilot. The cartographer. His two loose ends, tied together now by circumstance and by a leaking boat, huddled on a spit of gravel no bigger than a tennis court, building a fire they were foolish enough to believe the dark would hide.

Roque lowered the glasses. Around him the boat settled into its evening routine: the diesel generator's arrhythmic cough, the smell of wet canvas and gun oil, the two men he'd brought upriver playing cards under the canopy with the grim concentration of people who had stopped enjoying the game weeks ago. The rain came down in the fine, patient way it did after dark, less a storm than a condition, something the valley simply was.

He had been patient too. Patience was the whole profession. Security work, real security work—the kind ministries paid for and never named in budgets—was ninety percent waiting and ten percent deciding what other people would be allowed to remember. Roque was very good at both halves. He had spent nineteen days since the flood watching the water carry his mistakes downriver one piece at a time: a fuel drum, a shattered hull section, a jacket that had once belonged to a hydrologist named Ferro who had argued with him about evacuation windows and then drowned arguing with the river instead. He had catalogued each piece without expression. Expression was a luxury for men whose careers survived their errors.

The cartographer surviving had not been in his ledger. That was the flaw—he could admit it now, alone on the deck, because admitting it privately cost nothing. Emrys Vale had been supposed to die with the rest of them, or at least to vanish the way the valley itself had vanished, swallowed into a disaster so total that questions would drown before they could be asked. A dam that failed early. A survey team that found out why. A flood that tidied everything. It should have worked. Rivers were honest that way; they destroyed indiscriminately and kept no minutes.

But Vale had run north instead of south, into the flooded reach instead of away from it, and he had done it carrying a waterproof case that Roque would have given both his hands to open. And now he'd acquired a pilot.

That was the part Roque kept turning over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Odile Blackwood. He'd pulled her file the first night, sitting under a tarp in the lower station while the rain hammered the tin roof, and read it twice. Thin file. Thin life. A bush pilot running medicine and dry goods into river settlements, licensed, insured, unremarkable—except for the debt. Her father had signed a note with Harrow Voss three years before he died, secured against the plane, and Voss had inherited her along with the paper. Nineteen thousand, four hundred and change, accruing. The kind of number that owned a person without ever having to look them in the eye.

Roque understood debt. Not the sentimental kind—the structural kind. Debt was leverage refined to its purest form, better than threats, better than money, because the debtor did your enforcement for you. Every morning Odile Blackwood woke up already working for Harrow Voss, flying her routes, counting her gallons, keeping her head down, and she called it freedom because nobody was making her do it. She policed herself. They all did. That was the genius of the arrangement, and Roque had built half his career on arrangements like it.

Which meant that tonight, when he raised them on the radio, he wouldn't be threatening her at all.

He'd give her exactly what she wanted.

* * *

The radio room smelled of mildew and solder. Roque sat down at the set at 2100 hours, checked his frequency against the notes his signals man had compiled from the survey's own traffic logs—Blackwood's aircraft ran on a civilian band, but she'd have keyed the survey relay trying to raise the ranger post downstream; everyone did eventually—and waited while the operator warmed the tubes.

"Put me on her channel," he said. "Open carrier first. Let them hear us coming."

The operator looked up. "Sir?"

"They'll want to know it's me. It saves time." Roque folded his hands on the desk. "And patch it to the cabin speaker. I want my men to hear how this is done."

The carrier went out—a low, toneless hum rolling across the black water, finding the shingle bar, finding the fire, finding the two people beside it. On the bar, he knew, they would freeze. The pilot would reach for the set she'd salvaged or the one she carried; the cartographer would put his body between her and the treeline out of habit or guilt, Roque didn't care which. Let them stand there in the rain listening to the hum of the thing that had found them. Fear was also a message, and it cost nothing to send.

Then he pressed the key.

"Miss Blackwood. Mr. Vale. This is Ansel Roque, chief of security, Kestrel Reach survey. You'll forgive the intrusion—I know you've had a difficult week."

He let the words sit. Radio discipline said keep transmissions short, but Roque had never much believed in rules that made you sound afraid. He spoke the way he did everything: evenly, unhurried, a man reading from a document he had written himself.

"I'll come to the point, because the rain is coming back and neither of us enjoys shouting over it. I'm aware Mr. Vale left the survey camp with certain materials. Survey records. Maps. Correspondence." A pause, measured to the second. "I'm aware those materials are why you're both sitting on a gravel bar eating cold rice instead of sleeping somewhere dry."

On the bar, he imagined, Vale was saying something low and urgent to the woman. Don't answer. Or maybe: listen. Roque continued regardless. The beauty of an open channel was that silence answered too.

"Here is what I'm authorized to offer. Not threaten—offer. There's a difference, and I'd ask you to notice it, Miss Blackwood, because your whole life has been people threatening you and calling it business."

He heard the click of a transmit key. A woman's voice, tight with static and fury: *"How do you—"*

"Your file," Roque said pleasantly. "It's a small file. Now. The offer."

He laid it out the way he'd rehearsed it on the deck, sentence by sentence, because this was the ten percent, the deciding-what-people-would-be-allowed-to-remember, and it deserved craft.

"One. The note your father signed with Harrow Voss—nineteen thousand, four hundred and some dollars, secured against your aircraft—it gets retired tonight. Not deferred. Retired. Paid in full, by parties who prefer not to be named, through channels Voss won't question because Voss is already on our side of the ledger whether he knows it or not. The lien comes off your tail number. The plane is yours. Free and clear, for the first time in your adult life. You could repaint it any color you liked."

He paused there deliberately, because he'd read the file closely enough to guess what that detail would do to her. A woman who counted gallons of fuel against a dead man's signature. A woman who had never once owned the machine that carried her. Roque didn't need to understand longing to price it.

"Two. You fly home. Tomorrow, next week, whenever the weather gives you a window. No report filed against your license. No inquiry into why a charter pilot landed inside a restricted flood zone. Your name appears nowhere in anything. You go back to your medicine runs and your gossip and your ledgers, and this valley closes over you like water."

"Three. Mr. Vale." Here Roque allowed himself something almost gentle. "Mr. Vale walks too. That's the part I'd ask you to weigh carefully, because I suspect he's told you otherwise—that we'll kill him, that he can't surface again, that his only road runs through whatever's in that case. I'm telling you that isn't true. Hand over the materials, unopened, and Mr. Vale becomes a disgraced cartographer who got lucky in a flood. There will be an internal review. It will find procedural failures. It will assign blame to men who are conveniently dead. And Emrys Vale will be free to map rivers anywhere in the world he likes, under his own name, with nothing hanging over him but his own conscience."

He let the generator fill the pause. Somewhere below decks, one of his men laughed at a card and cut it off quickly.

"That's the offer. The full price of your freedom, named in plain figures: one case of paper, delivered intact, and two lives that go back to being ordinary. I know ordinary doesn't sound like much from where you're sitting. But I've read your file, Miss Blackwood. Ordinary is the thing you've been buying with every gallon of fuel you've ever counted. I'm offering to sell it to you at cost."

He released the key and watched the signal lamp, and for eleven seconds nobody answered, and in those eleven seconds Roque permitted himself the thought that it might actually work—not because the pilot was weak, but because she was tired, and tired people don't want justice, they want their beds back—and then the channel clicked and her voice came through, ragged at the edges but level underneath.

*"You killed them."*

"No," Roque said. "The river killed them. I failed to save them, which is a different failure, and one I'll answer for to whoever asks. What I won't do is let a good team's deaths become a lever for men who want to use corpses as currency. Which is precisely what will happen if those documents leave this valley. Think about who benefits, Miss Blackwood. Not the families. Not the drowned villages. Some auditor in the capital, some newspaper, some politician building a career on other people's grief. The truth doesn't feed anyone down here. It just gets people killed. It nearly got you killed, and you weren't even part of this."

*"I'm part of it now,"* she said, and Roque closed his eyes briefly, because that was the sentence he'd feared, the sentence that meant the offer had landed on ground that couldn't grow it. But he heard the fear under it too, the waver in *now*, and he pressed once more, softly, the way you press a wound to see if it still bleeds.

"You're part of it because a bad week put you here. You're not part of it the way Mr. Vale is part of it. He signed the maps, Miss Blackwood. Ask him what he redlined and what he was told to leave alone. Ask him what his signature is worth. Then ask yourself whether the man asking you to risk your neck for his evidence has told you everything he's carrying." He let that hang. "You have until noon tomorrow. Key this channel. Say yes, and by nightfall you'll own your airplane outright, and neither of you will ever hear my voice again. Say nothing, and I stop making offers."

He released the key. Then, because craft demanded it, he added the last line he'd saved, quiet enough that they'd have to strain through the static to catch it:

"For what it's worth—I hope you say yes. The river's taken enough from everyone. It doesn't need to take you too."

He switched off and sat in the green glow of the dial light, listening to the rain thicken against the canvas overhead, and told himself the offer was genuine. It mostly was. He had no order to kill the pilot; killing pilots drew aviation boards and coroners and the wrong kind of attention, and besides, a dead debtor owed nothing. If she took the deal, the case came back, Vale became manageable, and the ministry's arithmetic stayed balanced. Everyone walked. That was the version he preferred, and he had argued for it on the satellite phone for twenty minutes until the liaison had grunted assent.

But he had also posted Duarte and Kell with the rifle on the bluff above the bar, and he had told them, in the flat tone he used for things that were not quite orders, that if the pair tried to move downriver before noon—if they tried to run the gorge in the dark with a patched hull and a borrowed compass—then accidents happened on rivers, and the river was, after all, the official cause of everything.

The operator cleared his throat. "They didn't answer, sir."

"They answered," Roque said, standing, shrugging into his wet-weather coat. "Silence is an answer. It means the price wasn't high enough—or it means they need the night to convince themselves." He looked at the window, where the rain had thickened into sheets and the shingle bar had vanished entirely, swallowed by the same water that had eaten a village, a dam, and seven careers.

"Noon tomorrow," he said. "And if they're not on this channel by then, wake me. We'll see how their boat handles the gorge."

* * *

Down on the bar, the fire hissed under the rain, and Odile Blackwood stared at the radio as though it might bite, and Emrys Vale held the waterproof case a little tighter against his chest, and neither of them said the word *noon* out loud, because saying it made it real—but both of them were already counting the hours, and both of them knew the other was counting too, and the river between them and the coast had begun, very quietly, to rise.
