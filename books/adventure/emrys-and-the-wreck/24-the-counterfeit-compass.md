## 24. The Counterfeit Compass

The shingle bar had given them one dry night and no answers, and by morning the river had swallowed half of it again.

Odile woke to water lapping at the tarp they'd strung between two driftwood snags, cold seeping through the bottom of her sleeping bag where the gravel had gone soft beneath her. Emrys was already up, crouched over the boat with the patching kit, his back to her, working resin into a seam with the patience of a man who had learned that hurry was just another way of drowning. The rain had thinned to a gray drifting mist that made the drowned forest across the channel look like something remembered rather than seen.

She lay still for a moment and let herself have the small dishonesty of pretending none of it was real — not the burned station behind them, not Roque's voice in the radio speaker offering to buy her freedom like a sack of rice, not the drive wrapped in oilcloth inside Emrys's shirt. Just a woman on a gravel bar, a leaking boat, a man she'd known eleven days. Then the water touched her elbow through the bag's seam and reality resumed its patient work.

She got up, wrung out what she could, and went to help him with the hull.

They worked without talking much. That was another thing she'd learned about him: he didn't fill silence the way most men did, didn't treat quiet as a hole to be patched with words. He let it sit there like furniture. She found she liked it more than she wanted to admit, because it meant when he did speak, the words had weight.

It was while she was holding the gunwale steady for him that the idea came — arrived whole, the way weather sometimes did, all at once out of a clear sky.

"Roque searched the plane," she said.

Emrys looked up. Resin on his fingers, a smear of it along his jaw where he'd pushed his hair back. "What?"

"At the lower station. Before the shooting started. I saw him go through the cockpit after we ran — or I assumed. He knew about the drive being on board." She turned it over in her head as she said it, testing the joints. "He didn't find it because you had it on your body. But here's what matters: he knows how pilots carry things. He'll search you first. He told us so last night — *the cartographer keeps his documents close*. That's how he thinks. Documents close to the man who made them."

"So we don't put them close to me."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "We do better than that. We give him something to find."

* * *

They sat on the overturned hull afterward, sharing the last of the coffee boiled from rainwater, and she laid it out properly, because if this was going to work it had to survive him poking holes in it the way he poked holes in everything — carefully, thoroughly, without malice.

"My father's compass," she said, and took it out of her breast pocket.

It was a heavy old thing, brass-cased, the glass clouded at the edges, the needle still swinging true whenever she checked it against the sun. Her father had carried it thirty years on runs into country that had no names on any official map. It had a hinged lid and a screw-down base plate, and under that base plate there was a shallow void — a quarter inch of empty space where the manufacturer had once fitted a luminous dial insert that her father had removed because he hated anything on an instrument he couldn't read at a glance. She'd found it empty when she was nine, hiding coins in it. She'd found it useful ever since, for spare fuses, for a folded emergency frequency card, for nothing much at all.

"You're going to hide the real drive in a compass," Emrys said. Not a question. Testing.

"I'm going to hide a *fake* drive in the compass." She set the brass case on the hull between them. "A decoy. Something that looks exactly like what Roque wants. And then we let him take it."

"And the real one?"

"Goes in the compass too." She watched his face do the thing it did when a map resolved — the slight narrowing, the recalculation. "No. Listen. The real one goes somewhere he'd never think to look, because he's already decided where it must be. On you. So we make sure that when he searches you, he finds nothing — and when he searches the plane, or the boat, or whatever else he takes, he finds the compass. My father's compass. A pilot's instrument. The kind of thing a woman carries everywhere and never thinks about, because it's just *hers*, just sentimental junk." She tapped the case. "Except it isn't. The base plate comes off. There's room inside for a drive stripped down to its core — no casing, wrapped thin. And Roque will open it himself, because men like Roque can't resist opening other people's private things. He'll find the drive. He'll believe he won."

Emrys was quiet for a long time. The mist drifted. Somewhere across the channel a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

"The problem," he said finally, "is that if he opens it and finds a fake, he'll know within hours. Maybe minutes, once his people try to read it."

"That's why the fake has to be good enough to buy us time, not good enough to survive inspection. We only need hours. We need to be past the weir and into the mangroves before anyone plugs it into anything." She paused. "And honestly? We might need less than that. Because here's the part I keep coming back to."

"Say it."

"He offered to clear my debts." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "All of them. Voss's note, the interest, everything. For the evidence. Which means Roque thinks my price is money. Which means Roque has never once considered that I might want something *else* — that I might want *him* to lose." She looked at the compass, at the worn brass, at the needle trembling in its housing like a small captive heart. "Everyone who's ever held power over me has assumed I could be bought, because my father taught me to count fuel gallons and my father died owing money, and I've spent six years letting them be right. Letting it be true because it was easier than fighting."

Emrys said nothing. He was good at that — at giving her room to arrive at her own sentences.

"So this is me," she said, "not being bought. This is me spending the one thing my father left me that was actually worth something — not the debt, not the plane, not even the flying. The compass. The thing that always pointed home." She laughed, short and without much humor. "He'd hate it. He'd also love it. Both at once. He used to say the best navigation is making the other man follow your heading instead of his own."

* * *

Building the counterfeit took the rest of the morning and most of what remained of their supplies.

The real drive, stripped of its aluminum shell, was a wafer of green circuitry no thicker than three stacked coins. Emrys handled it like a relic — which, she supposed, it was. Eleven dead men's testimony. A dam that had been signed off on paper and failed in fact. His own redlined maps, scanned and annotated in his tight cartographer's hand, the corrections the ministry had ordered struck through and the reasons why, dated, initialed, damning. He wrapped it in a strip of rubber cut from the patch kit, then in foil from a ration pack, then in a final layer of waxed cloth, until it was a flat packet the size of a matchbox.

"It goes in the compass," she said, "but not yet. First the decoy."

The decoy was harder. They had no spare drives, no electronics worth the name. What they had was the survey camp's salvage still lashed under the boat's thwart — and among it, blessedly, a broken handheld radio Emrys had taken from the wrecked second boat, its casing cracked, its guts intact but useless without a battery. He opened it with a knife tip and extracted the largest circuit board, trimmed it with the knife until it matched the real drive's dimensions, and wrapped it in identical foil and cloth.

"Hold them both," he said, and gave her the packets one in each palm, eyes closed, weighing nothing, seeing nothing.

She couldn't tell. Even knowing which hand held which, she couldn't tell. The weights were near enough identical; the wrappings were the same cloth from the same roll.

"Good enough?" she asked.

"For a man searching by feel in a boathouse, in bad light, wanting to believe he's won?" Emrys opened his eyes. "Yes. People see what the shape of their greed tells them to see. Roque wants a drive. We are going to hand him a shape."

But it wasn't enough, and she knew it, and he knew it. A bare packet in a compass case was a packet. It needed provenance. It needed to look *placed* — hidden deliberately, hidden by someone who thought she was clever.

So she built the hiding place the way her father would have.

She unscrewed the base plate of the compass and cleaned out the old void, scraping away years of grit with the knife. She lined it with a scrap of chamois from the tool roll — her father's trick, chamois to stop metal rattling against metal, she could almost hear him saying it, *a quiet instrument is a trusted instrument*. She seated the decoy packet in the void, coiled its lead wire — Emrys had soldered on a stub of wire to suggest connection — around the housing, and screwed the plate back down until it sat flush. From the outside: a compass. Heavy, old, loved. The glass clouded. The needle swinging.

Then she made it honest-looking in the ways that mattered. She oiled the hinge. She polished the case with a rag until the brass caught what little light the day offered. And she took the knife and, with a breath she held longer than she meant to, scratched into the underside of the base plate — invisible unless removed, visible the instant someone did — a single line of coordinates.

Kestrel Reach. The drowned valley. The place where the dam had failed and the team had vanished and everything began.

"If he finds it," Emrys said quietly, watching her work, "he'll think it's a cache marker."

"It's not."

"What is it?"

She screwed the plate home. "It's a signature. Mine. Telling him exactly whose game he's playing." She looked up. "By the time he understands that, we'll be gone."

* * *

There was one more thing, and it was the hardest, and she made herself say it before the light changed and her courage changed with it.

"You carry the real one."

Emrys went very still. "You just spent an hour explaining that he'll search me first."

"Yes. And he'll search you *because* he expects the evidence on you. Which means he'll search you hard, and thorough, and then he'll stop — because he found what he wanted in the compass, and men who've found what they want stop looking. You'll be stripped of every pocket, every seam, every boot. And the drive will be—" she reached over and, before either of them quite registered the movement, tucked the flat wrapped packet into the band of her hair, under the knot at her nape, where the oilcloth sat flat against her skull and her hair fell over it like a curtain. "Here."

Emrys stared at her.

"Pilots hide things in their hair," she said. "Ask any woman who's flown contraband medicine past a customs post. Nobody searches a woman's hair, especially not a man like Roque, who thinks women are cargo that occasionally talks. And if he does—" she shrugged, "—then I lose it, and we're finished anyway, and at least I'll have kept it off you. One of us should stay clean for the handoff. If they take you, you can't give up what you don't have. That's not strategy, Emrys. That's mercy. To you."

"That's not mercy," he said. His voice had gone rough. "That's you deciding your life weighs less than mine."

"It's me deciding *I'm* the one who proposed the bluff, so *I'm* the one who carries its worst risk. That's called command. My father taught me that too, though he mostly taught it by example and bad outcomes." She pulled the knot tighter, tested it with her fingers until the packet disappeared entirely into the dark wet weight of her hair. "Besides. You're the witness. Your testimony is the whole point. If they catch you carrying it, they kill you and drop you in the reach, and eleven men stay dead for nothing. If they catch me carrying it—" she met his eyes, "—they'll bargain. I'm worth more to them alive. Voss's note makes sure of that. Six years of being owned, and today it finally buys me something."

He reached out then, and touched the back of his fingers to her nape, lightly, where the packet hid — not searching, just acknowledging. His hand was warm despite the mist.

"When this is done," he said, "I'm going to spend a very long time learning everything else you hide in there."

"Get us past the weir first."

* * *

They launched in the late afternoon, into a river running high and brown and fast with the week's rain, and the current took the boat the way a hand takes a card it intends to play.

Odile steered from the stern with the broken paddle they'd salvaged, reading the water the way she read weather — by texture, by the wrinkles on its surface, by the places where the brown skin of the river smoothed over something submerged and hungry. Emrys bailed with the tin can and navigated by memory, calling the drowned landmarks as they rose and slid past: the roofline of the schoolhouse, the top third of the church bell tower leaning like a drunk against the fog, the crown of a kapok tree that had been the tallest thing in the valley and was now a hazard to navigation.

The compass rode in her breast pocket, over her heart, where it had ridden for six years. The decoy slept inside it, wearing her father's chamois, wearing her scratch of coordinates like a brand. The truth rode at the base of her skull, flat and silent, warming against her skin until she almost forgot it was there — except she never forgot, not for one stroke of the paddle, because forgetting was how people died out here.

Near dusk the channel narrowed toward the weir — the old spillway the ministry had built to regulate the flooded valley, concrete jaws with a gate rusted half-open, water pouring through the gap in a long white tongue. Beyond it the river widened toward the lowlands, the mangroves, the coast. Beyond it, if Roque kept his word about the meeting, Roque.

Emrys shipped the bail can. "How do you want to play it?"

"We come in slow and visible. No running — running says guilty. We tie up where he tells us to tie up. We act like people taking a deal." She heard her own voice steady itself, pilot-flat, the voice she used on the radio when the engine coughed. "You're sullen. You're a man who lost his nerve and is handing over the goods to save his skin. Don't act it too well — just be tired and scared, which shouldn't be hard."

"And you?"

"I'm a debtor buying her freedom." She touched the compass through the fabric of her shirt, felt the brass, felt the lie inside it sitting snug as a seed. "Which is the easiest role I've ever been offered, because I've been rehearsing it for six years."

The gate loomed. The white water roared. And out of the mist downstream, right on schedule, came the sound of an outboard engine — one, then a second, closing from both banks — and a voice amplified through a loudhailer, smooth as poured concrete:

*"Bring the boat to the near abutment. Keep your hands where we can see them. Chief Roque would like his property back."*

Odile dipped the paddle, turned the bow toward the concrete, and smiled at Emrys with all the warmth she could not afford to feel.

"Places," she said. "Curtain's up."
