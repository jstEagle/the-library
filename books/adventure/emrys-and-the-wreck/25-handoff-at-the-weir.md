## 25. Handoff at the Weir

*Odile*

The weir came out of the mist like a scar someone had tried to stitch shut and given up on. Concrete ribs showed through the brown water where the old floodgates had been dynamited back when the valley was still a valley, and above them the drowned forest stood in ranks, gray-green and patient, as if it had all the time in the world to wait for them to make a mistake.

Odile cut the engine fifty meters out and let the boat drift. Her hands were steady on the tiller. She made a point of noticing that—steady hands, even breathing—the way she used to notice fuel gauges and cloud decks, because if she watched herself closely enough she could almost pretend she was flying a checklist instead of walking into a trap.

"Roque's early," Emrys said.

He was crouched low in the bow, the real drive taped flat against his spine beneath his shirt, wrapped in oilcloth and then in plastic and then in more tape, because Odile had insisted on redundancy the way other people insisted on prayer. The counterfeit sat in her lap: her father's compass, its brass case gutted and reweighted, a slim data chip nested in the space where the needle had lived for thirty years. She had sealed it last night by lantern light while Emrys slept—or pretended to sleep—and she had held it a long moment before snapping the lid shut, feeling like a thief robbing her own father's grave.

"He's not early," she said. "We're exactly on time. That's what worries me."

Fog lay along the water in ropes. Somewhere upstream the river fell through the broken gates with a sound like sustained applause, endless and indifferent. She scanned the near bank: the concrete apron of the old maintenance road, half-drowned, running into the trees. A figure stood at the end of it. Then two. Then, as the fog shifted, four, arranged with the casual geometry of men who wanted to be counted.

Ansel Roque stood slightly forward of the others, hands loose at his sides, wearing a rain jacket too clean for this country. He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An invitation.

"That's a lot of guns for a document exchange," Emrys murmured.

"It's not a lot of guns," Odile said. "It's the exact number he thinks he needs. Which means he's already decided how this ends." She looked at Emrys, really looked, taking in the hollows under his eyes, the tape ghosting the edge of his collar. "Last chance to change the plan."

"There is no plan B that keeps you alive," he said quietly. "This is the plan."

She hated how much she liked hearing that—hated that some small traitorous part of her filed it away as something warm—and put the tiller over.

The boat ground against the apron. Odile stepped out into shin-deep water, the compass case in her left hand, held away from her body, visible. Emrys came behind her, empty-handed, palms open, exactly as they'd rehearsed. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist that beaded on everything and dried on nothing.

"Ms. Blackwood." Roque's voice carried easily across the water, pleasant, almost avuncular. "You've had an eventful week."

"I've had a quiet week," Odile said. "I fly supplies. That's all I do."

"And yet here you are." He smiled without warmth. "May I?"

He extended his hand toward the compass case. She let him take it. His fingers turned it over once, twice, thumbing the brass lid open, and for one suspended second she watched him look down at her father's compass—the compass that had pointed true through every storm her father had ever flown into, until the storm that didn't—and she felt the lie humming inside it like a struck wire.

Roque weighed it in his palm. Looked up.

"This isn't what we agreed to search first."

*Emrys*

Emrys had known, from the moment Odile proposed the bluff three nights ago, that it would come apart at exactly this seam. Not because the counterfeit was bad—it was good, good enough—but because Roque was not a man who gambled on containers. Roque gambled on people. And people, searched in the wrong order, told you everything.

He'd argued for swapping roles. Odile had refused with a logic he couldn't fault: Roque expected the evidence on the man who'd stolen it. Searching Emrys first was the natural move; searching Odile first would have been the suspicious one. So they'd built the whole exchange around the assumption that Emrys would be stripped and shaken down while Odile stood by holding the decoy, and the plan's entire life expectancy was the ninety seconds between Roque opening the compass and Roque turning around.

Ninety seconds. Emrys had done the arithmetic a hundred times. Tape, oilcloth, plastic: forty seconds to find, ten to understand, and then every gun on that apron belonged to Ansel Roque and so did they.

Unless the compass bought more than ten seconds.

He watched Roque turn the case in his hands and saw the moment the chief's thumb found the reweighting—the slight wrongness of the balance, the way a man who handled instruments daily feels a forgery the way a horse feels a loose stone. Roque's eyes came up. They were not angry. They were interested, which was worse.

"Clever," Roque said. "Genuinely. The sentimental object. I might have believed it myself, if I hadn't spent two weeks learning how Ms. Blackwood lies." He tilted his head at her. "You don't. It's your one flaw. You fly straight lines even when a curve would save you."

"The chip's in there," Odile said, and her voice didn't shake, and Emrys loved her for it and feared for her in the same breath. "One copy. Take it and we're done. You'll never see either of us again."

"Mm." Roque passed the compass to the man beside him—a squat, careful-handed lieutenant named Ferris who produced a knife and began, unhurried, to work the brass lid off its hinge. "And Mr. Vale. You'll forgive the discourtesy."

Two of the others moved toward Emrys. He raised his arms, cooperative, and let the first man pat him down—jacket, waistband, boots—while the second took his wrists. Under his shirt, the tape held. Under the tape, the oilcloth. Under the oilcloth, the truth of Kestrel Reach: survey logs, redlined maps, the ministry's own correspondence, everything that proved the dam had been condemned on paper before it was ever built and signed off anyway.

Forty seconds, he thought. Thirty-five.

Then Ferris said, "Sir. There's a compartment behind the needle mount. Weighted."

The world narrowed to the sound of rain on water.

*Odile*

Odile saw it happen the way pilots see weather building—too far off to stop, close enough to count the seconds. Ferris peeled back the false floor of the compass case with a jeweler's patience, and the chip sat there in its nest of cotton, and Roque looked at it, and looked at her, and smiled the smile of a man whose suspicion had just been confirmed as flattery.

"You hid it well," he said. "In the one place I'd have searched last, because you knew I'd want to believe you were sentimental." He turned the chip over between finger and thumb. "But you see the problem, Ms. Blackwood. This is exactly what I asked for. Perfectly, precisely what I asked for. And nothing in my experience of this valley has ever been perfectly what I asked for."

Behind her, she heard the tear of tape.

Emrys moved fast—faster than she'd have credited from a man who'd spent two weeks sleeping in mud—but two armed men with a plan beat one desperate man without one, and they had him face-down on the wet concrete before she'd taken her second breath. The oilcloth packet skidded across the apron, trailing strips of silver tape, and stopped against Roque's boot.

The silence that followed had weight. Rain ticked on the water. Upstream, the broken gates kept up their applause.

Roque crouched, picked up the packet, and weighed it in both hands like a man judging a fish. Then he stood, and looked at Odile, and something in his face closed like a ledger.

"A decoy," he said, "and the real thing. Both delivered to me, on the same morning, by the two people I most wanted standing in one place." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what I would have paid for this? What I did pay? Voss assured me you'd bring it. He assured me you'd have no choice." He tucked the packet inside his jacket. "For once, the man was right about something."

Cold went through Odile that had nothing to do with the rain. *Voss.* Of course. Every mile of this river, every decision since the relay station, had been walked for her by a man counting interest.

"You're going to let us go," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "That was the deal. Evidence for our lives."

"The deal," Roque said gently, "was between me and whoever handed me the evidence. And now that I have it—" he spread his hands, apologetic, terrible "—I find I'm negotiating with no one."

Ferris drew his sidearm with the same unhurried care he'd shown the compass. One of the others started toward the boat.

Emrys moved.

Later, Odile could never reconstruct it properly—only fragments: his shoulder driving into the nearest gunman's knees, the splash of a rifle hitting the water, Roque shouting something clipped and professional, and her own body already moving on the oldest instinct she owned, the one that said *run for the plane, run for the boat, run for anything with wings or a hull.* She hit the water at a sprint, dove, came up gasping beside the boat's hull as a round cracked off the concrete apron where her head had been.

"Odile!" Emrys was in the water too, blood threading pink from his scalp, swimming hard for the mangroves downstream of the apron. "Not the boat—they'll watch the boat—*mangroves!*"

She hesitated one full second—the boat was hers, the boat was freedom, the boat was the only thing on this river she trusted—and then a second round stitched the water a meter from her chin, and she swam.

*Emrys*

The mangroves took them the way a crowd takes a fugitive—closing ranks, hiding them, giving nothing away. Emrys went in over the tangle roots and pulled Odile after him, and behind them the voices on the apron resolved into orders: two men to the boat, two along the shore line, dogs if the dogs could be gotten across the water in time.

They waded chest-deep through the root maze, hands on trunks, breath held at each pause, moving on the simple animal logic of *away*. The fog helped. The rain helped. The drowned forest, which had swallowed a village and a survey team and a dam's worth of promises, swallowed them too, without comment.

After a long time—twenty minutes, an hour; time went strange in the green—Odile stopped, braced against a trunk, and shook the water from her hair. Her face was gray. Her hands, finally, were not steady.

"He has it," she said. "Everything. We gave it to him. We *carried* it to him."

"We gave him what he already knew existed," Emrys said. "It buys him silence for a month, maybe two, until the audit season. It doesn't buy him innocence." He heard how thin that sounded and hated it. "There are copies. There are always copies. I told you—"

"You told me the drive was the insurance." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she steadied it with visible effort, jaw tight. "You told me if we got it to the coast, it was over. Now it's in the pocket of the man who killed your team, and we're standing in a swamp with nothing but—" she laughed, short and bitter, and patted her flight jacket "—a wet radio and my father's empty compass."

Emrys reached out and took her hand. She let him. Her fingers were ice.

"Listen to me," he said. "The plan frayed. Plans do. But we're alive, he doesn't have us, and right now, this minute, Ansel Roque believes the only witnesses to Kestrel Reach are standing in front of him—which means somewhere behind him, unguarded, is everything else. His boats. His records. His chain of command." He squeezed her hand. "We're not finished. We're scattered. There's a difference."

"Is there?" she whispered.

From far back through the mangroves, muffled by water and root and rain, came the sound they had both been dreading and expecting: a dog, baying, and then a second voice answering it, and then a man's shout, much closer than either of them wanted—

"*Tracks! In the mud here!*"
