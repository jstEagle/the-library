## 26. Alone in the Green

The mangroves closed behind her like a door.

Odile ran until the gunfire faded into the green roar of the swamp—her own breath, the slap of water against her shins, the wet slap of leaves across her face. She didn't look back. Looking back was how you tripped, how you died, how you learned the thing you couldn't unlearn. She had learned it once already, in the boathouse smoke, when she'd turned and seen Emrys go down into the reeds and heard Roque's men shouting his name like it was a piece of equipment they'd dropped.

*He's not dead*, she told herself. *They'd have shot him in the open if he were dead. They want him alive.*

They wanted him alive because of what he carried. Which meant that every minute he stayed free was a minute the truth stayed free too, and every minute she stayed free was—

She didn't finish the thought. She climbed instead, hands finding roots and buttresses, hauling herself up a ridge of black mud where the mangroves gave way to taller country: kapok, wild fig, the buttressed cathedral trunks of the old forest that the dam's engineers had never managed to drown. The water fell away below her. The light came down in green columns, thick as syrup. Somewhere behind her a dog barked, high and eager, and her whole body flinched like a struck wire.

Dogs. Of course they had dogs. Roque didn't seem like a man who left anything to chance.

She ran on the ridge until her lungs burned, then walked, then ran again, following the high ground the way her father had taught her to read it—by the trees, by the smell of the air, by the way water pooled in the low places and told you where the land was thinking of going. He'd taught her that in the cockpit, mostly. *The ground is just a map that got tired of lying flat, Odile. Read it like a chart and it'll never surprise you.*

She would have given a year of her life to hear him say it again. She would have given the whole debt.

The tower appeared through the trees the way towers do—suddenly, all at once, a gray lattice rising out of the canopy like the ribcage of something that had lain down there a hundred years ago and never gotten up. An old fire tower, one of the survey-era relics the government had built and then abandoned when the interior stopped being a place they wanted to watch and became a place they wanted to use. She'd seen them from the air her whole flying life. Steel legs, a cab on top, stairs that were probably rotted through or rusted through or both.

It was the highest thing for twenty miles. And twenty miles was how far she'd have to see to have any idea whether Emrys was alive.

She stood at the base with her hands on the first rung, breathing, and let herself feel it—the thing she'd been outrunning since the mangroves. Not fear. Fear was familiar; fear was fuel. This was worse. This was the specific, surgical dread of not knowing. Of having handed her heart to a man she'd known for eleven days and then watched the green close over him.

*He stepped out of the reeds*, she reminded herself. *He walked out of a flood that ate six men. He doesn't die easily.*

But the dogs had been close. The shouting had been close. And she had run, because running was the only thing she could do, and now the not-knowing was the price of it, and she would carry that price in her chest like a stone for as long as it took.

She climbed.

The first twenty rungs were sound. The next ten sang under her weight, a thin metallic complaint, and she froze with her forehead against the cold steel and listened for the dogs. Nothing. Just the swamp breathing, the endless wet rustle, a bird somewhere making a sound like a hinge that needed oil. She climbed the rest of the way fast, before her nerve could change its mind.

The cab was a rusted box with a floor of expanded metal, a survey bench bolted to one wall, and a roof that leaked in three places. Through the glassless windows the world opened up: the drowned valley to the north, flat and silver and terrible, the flood sitting on Kestrel Reach like a lid on a pot. To the south and east, the green. Miles and miles of it, unbroken, indifferent. Somewhere in it, a man was running, or hiding, or lying still with his blood going into the ground.

She made herself scan it methodically, the way she'd scan for a downed aircraft. Sector by sector. Water first—no boat, no wake, no movement on the channels. Then the ridgelines—nothing. Then the fire scars and old clearings, the places a man might cross open ground.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

The green kept its secrets the way it kept everything: completely.

Odile sat down on the survey bench with her elbows on her knees and, for the first time since the boathouse, let herself stop moving. Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of clinical detachment, as if they belonged to another pilot she was obliged to keep alive.

*You're alone*, she thought. *You've been alone your whole life. This is just the loudest version of it.*

But it wasn't true anymore, and that was the problem. For eleven days she had bailed water beside a man, navigated beside him, slept in the same boat with his breathing six inches from hers, and somewhere in all that wet and dark the armor she'd worn since her father died had developed a door, and Emrys had walked through it without ever asking permission, and now the armor had a hole in it and the hole was shaped exactly like a man running through a swamp with dogs behind him.

She had spent years believing that isolation was safety. Her father's debt had taught her that everything you loved could be lien-ed, levied, taken. So she'd loved nothing. Owned nothing she couldn't burn. Flown her runs and kept her head down and told herself that a woman alone in the sky couldn't be hurt the way a woman on the ground could.

Roque had proved her wrong in a single afternoon.

And now she understood, sitting in the rusted cab of a dead fire tower with the whole drowned valley spread out below her like a wound, that she had two choices, and they were the same two choices she'd been circling since the night the storm closed the valley. She could go south, alone, to the coast, to Marta, to a seaplane and a sea and a life where the debt was someone else's problem. She could survive. She was good at surviving; it was the one thing she'd ever mastered.

Or she could stay in the green until she found him.

Not because the evidence needed him—though it did, his testimony was the spine of the whole case, and she knew it. Not because the plan required it—though it did. But because a life where she flew south alone while he drowned in a swamp was not a life she wanted back. She'd had that life. She'd had it for six years, and it had been quiet, and it had been safe, and it had been a kind of death with a paycheck.

"Damn you," she said softly, to the green, to the man in it, to herself. "Damn you for making me care."

The green didn't answer. But far to the southeast, so far she almost missed it, something moved—a flicker at the edge of a clearing, there and gone, too small to be a man, too deliberate to be a bird. Her heart slammed once, hard, and she leaned into the window frame and stared until her eyes watered.

It didn't come again. But she marked it. She marked it the way her father had taught her to mark everything: on the compass rose in her mind, southeast, bearing one-four-zero, at the edge of the old fire scar.

And then she turned to the survey bench, and the rusted junction box bolted beside it, and the stub of antenna wire hanging from the roof, and she let herself think the thought she'd been carrying since the tower first rose out of the trees.

There was a radio up here once. There might still be enough of one left to matter.

* * *

Emrys lay in six inches of black water and counted the dogs by their voices.

Four. Maybe five. They worked the far bank in a loose line, belling and silent, belling and silent, and he timed the silences and used them to move—one length of his body at a time, arm over arm through the flooded roots, no splashing, no sound at all beyond the swamp's own noise, which was generous and constant and the only reason he was still breathing.

They'd nearly had him at the clearing. He'd been thirty feet from the treeline when the first dog had opened up behind him, and he'd done the thing he'd sworn on the drowned village he'd never do again—he'd run. Straight-line, panic-run, the kind of running that got men killed in this country. The shot had gone over his shoulder and taken a strip of bark off a fig tree close enough to feel, and he'd gone left, down into the worst water he could find, because the one thing he knew about Roque's men was that they were soldiers, and soldiers hated swamps.

He'd been right. Mostly. The dogs didn't hate swamps at all.

Now he lay with his cheek in the water and his body wrapped in the roots of a fallen tree and he thought about Odile, and the thinking was the worst part, worse than the dogs, worse than the cold.

He'd seen her go. That was the thing he kept returning to, the splinter in his mind: he'd seen her break for the mangroves, seen her move the way she moved on the river—fast, economical, reading the ground like a chart—and he'd known she'd make it. Odile Blackwood did not die in places she understood. But then the smoke had thickened, and Roque's men had come through the trees in a line, and a dog had swung its head toward the mangroves, toward *her*, and Emrys had made a decision in about a quarter of a second that he'd been paying for ever since.

He'd crashed away to the north. Loud. Deliberate. A branch broken, a boot in open water, a shape moving where a shape shouldn't be.

It had worked. The dog had swung, the men had swung, the shouts had turned from her name to his, and she'd gotten away clean.

And now he lay in the black water with his lungs full of swamp air and he did not know if she knew he'd done it on purpose. He did not know if she thought he'd been taken. He knew her—eleven days, and he knew her the way you know a river you've run once, completely and dangerously—and he knew exactly what she was doing right now. She was blaming herself. She was somewhere high and dry doing arithmetic with her own guilt, deciding that she'd abandoned him, deciding that she owed him a rescue, and Emrys lay in the water and wanted to shout across twenty miles of green: *You don't owe me. You never owed me. That's the whole point of you.*

Because that was the thing he'd figured out about her somewhere in the middle of the night watches, bailing the boat, her hands steady on the tin can, her voice telling him about her father's compass in a tone she probably thought was casual. Odile believed that every relationship was a ledger. That love was a debt like any other, incurred and serviced and eventually called due. Her father had died owing money, and she'd inherited the owing, and she'd never once in her life been on the other side of the ledger—never been the one who was simply, freely, without collateral, given to.

He intended to be the other side of the ledger if it killed him. It might, in fact, kill him. But he intended it.

The dogs swung closer. He pressed his face into the root tangle and breathed through his mouth and counted their voices again—three now, the line had bent—and when they'd passed he moved, one silent length at a time, south. Always south. The salt line was south. Marta's plane was south. And Odile was—

He didn't let himself finish it. He put his hand on the next root and pulled.

* * *

Dusk came to the tower as a slow drowning of the light, the green going gray, the flood going pewter, the first insects rising out of the water in columns you could see from a mile away. Odile had spent the afternoon on the survey bench with the junction box open and its guts in her lap, and she had learned three things.

One: the tower's radio was long dead, its case full of rain and wasp nests, its transformer cracked like an old egg.

Two: the tower's *battery bank* was not. Two heavy glass cells sat in a rusted rack below the floor grating, and when she'd scraped the terminals with her knife they'd shown a dull, stubborn shine, and when she'd touched the voltmeter leads to them—the little meter from the boat's engine kit, the one she'd pocketed at the lower station without letting herself think about why—the needle had jumped. Not much. But it had jumped.

Three: she was not a radio technician. She was a pilot who knew that radios worked and had never once, in six years of flying, needed to know *why*.

She sat in the failing light with the dead radio's guts in her lap and let the despair have her for exactly as long as it took to finish the thought. Then she did what her father had done with every impossible landing, every flooded strip, every creditor at the door: she broke the problem into pieces small enough to carry.

The radio was dead. But a radio was just three things—a way to make a signal, a way to shape it, and a way to throw it into the sky. The battery bank could make the signal. The dead radio's tuning guts might still shape it, if the wasps had left anything. And for throwing it into the sky, there was the antenna wire, forty feet of it, still bolted to the roof, still hanging down the tower's leg like a vine.

And if the radio itself was beyond saving—if the wasps had won—then she didn't need a radio. She needed a *transmitter*, and a transmitter, at its crudest, was just a spark in a circuit, and she had a battery bank, and she had the plane's magneto in her pack—she'd pulled it at the lower station along with everything else that wasn't bolted down, because Odile Blackwood did not leave working parts behind—and a magneto made sparks the way rivers made water.

It was a terrible plan. It was the kind of plan that worked once in a hundred tries and got you killed the other ninety-nine. It was exactly the kind of plan her father would have hated and exactly the kind of plan he would have flown anyway.

She worked through the dark by the light of a stub of candle from the boat's emergency kit, stripping wire, scraping terminals, wiring the magneto to the battery bank to the dead radio's tuning coil with knots and clamps and, at one memorable point, a hairpin. The mosquitoes found her within the hour and made their opinions known. She let them. Pain was just information, her father used to say, and she had a great deal of information to gather before morning.

Because here was the thing she'd realized somewhere around the third hour, with her hands bleeding and the wasp-nest dust in her lungs, and it had come to her not as a plan but as a certainty, the way the important things always did:

Roque was hunting two people through a swamp because two people knew what had happened at Kestrel Reach. But if the whole river knew—if every pilot, every settlement, every boatman from the salt line to the headwaters heard it on an open channel—then Roque couldn't hunt witnesses anymore. There would be too many. The truth would stop being a thing two people carried and become a thing the whole country was standing in, the way they stood in the flood.

She didn't know if the rig would work. She didn't know if anyone was listening. She didn't know if Emrys was alive, and every hour that passed without a sign made the stone in her chest heavier, and she caught herself, more than once, stopping work entirely, hands motionless in the wire, just *listening* to the green as if it might say his name.

It never did. The green kept its own counsel. But she marked the southeast clearing in her mind again, bearing one-four-zero, and she made herself a promise with the same iron her father had used to sign things he shouldn't have: *If you're alive, I'm coming. And if you're not, I'm coming anyway, and I'm going to burn their whole rotten house down with the truth, and that will have to be enough.*

Near midnight, the rig was done. It looked like nothing—like a bird's nest made of wire and desperation, sitting on a dead man's survey bench a hundred feet above the drowned valley. She connected the antenna. She put her hand on the magneto.

And far below her, at the edge of the fire scar to the southeast, in the last gray light before true dark, something moved again. Deliberate. Small. Twice—then a pause—then twice more.

Not an animal. Animals didn't count.

Odile leaned into the window frame, her heart going like a magneto itself, and counted the flashes, and a laugh that was half sob came out of her before she could stop it, because she knew the rhythm, she'd know it anywhere, she'd watched him tap it out on the boat's gunwale through three sleepless nights like a man keeping his sanity in a tin can.

Two and two. *O.* His letter. His signal. The first letter of the only word that mattered.

*I'm alive. I'm here. Come find me.*

She pressed her hand flat against the cold window frame, twenty miles of black green between them, and answered the only way she could—she struck the magneto, once, twice, three times, sending a stutter of raw crackle down the wire and out into the night air, crude as a drumbeat, unmistakable as a flare.

Then she sat back down at the bench, picked up the wire, and went back to work, because the rig wasn't finished, and dawn was coming, and when the sun rose over Kestrel Reach, Odile Blackwood was going to tell the whole river exactly what its government had done.

And then she was going south, bearing one-four-zero, to bring him home.
