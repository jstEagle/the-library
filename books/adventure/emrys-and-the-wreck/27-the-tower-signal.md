## 27. The Tower Signal

The fire tower stood out of the drowned green like the last tooth in an old man's jaw.

Odile saw it first as a smudge against the rain—something too straight for a tree, too stubborn for a stump—and her heart did a thing she didn't authorize, a small leap of recognition, because fire towers meant people, and people meant radios, and radios meant she could stop being a ghost. Then she remembered who was hunting ghosts, and the leap curdled into something colder. A tower was also a place you could be found.

She circled it on foot anyway, giving it the same wary respect she'd give a downed power line. The structure rose maybe eighty feet on four legs of rusted steel, cross-braced with lattice that had gone orange and furred with moss, the cab at the top listing slightly to the east like a drunk leaning into a story. A stairway zigzagged up the outside, half its treads missing, the rest furred with wet lichen that would be ice underfoot even without the ice. Below it, the old keeper's hut had collapsed into itself years ago; ferns grew through the roof like green fingers prying open a coffin.

No smoke. No light. No dogs—yet.

"Emrys," she said quietly, though he was three steps behind her and had been silent long enough that his silence had become a kind of company. "Tell me this isn't a bad idea."

He came up beside her, water sheeting off the brim of the survey cap he'd refused to throw away. He looked worse than she felt, which was saying something: two days in the mangroves, a night in the swamp mud with dogs baying somewhere south of them, and now this gray drizzle that wasn't rain so much as the sky forgetting where to stop. His hands were cut from the reeds and the strainer log and the weir, and he'd stopped noticing them, which worried her more than the cuts.

"It's a terrible idea," he said. "It's also the only one we have."

"That's usually how I know it's mine."

She started climbing.

* * *

The stairs fought her the whole way. Every fourth tread was gone or rotten, and she learned their rhythm by falling into it—the reach, the test, the commit—and somewhere around the fifth flight she stopped thinking about Roque's searchlight sweeping the river below and started thinking about nothing at all, which was its own mercy. Her arms burned. Her lungs burned. The drizzle got into everything, into the seams of her jacket, into the space behind her eyes where exhaustion lived rent-free.

Emrys climbed below her, and she heard him the way you hear weather: patient, persistent, occasionally swearing under his breath when a rung gave way. Once a whole section of railing tore loose in his grip and clattered down through the lattice, and they both froze, hanging there like thieves, listening to the forest absorb the sound.

Nothing answered. No engines. No barking. Just the drip and hush of the drowned valley breathing below them.

At the top, the cab's door hung open on one hinge, and Odile stepped through into a room that smelled like forty years of rust and bird droppings and old cigarettes. Someone had loved this place once. There was still a stool bolted to the floor, a map table scarred with compass points scratched into the wood, a tin cup fused to the sill by decades of rain. The windows were gone—all but one pane, cracked into a spiderweb—and the wind moved through the cab freely, carrying the smell of the flood: silt and diesel and something sweeter underneath, rotting vegetation, the valley digesting what it had swallowed.

And there, in the corner, under a tarp that had once been white—

"There's no God," Odile said, "but there is a radio."

She pulled the tarp off like unwrapping a wound.

It was an old HF set, government surplus from before she was born, the kind with vacuum tubes and a tuning dial the size of a dinner plate. The casing was rusted at every seam. The antenna wire ran out through a broken insulator and dangled down the tower's flank, snapped halfway, whipping in the wind like a cut nerve. The handset cord had been chewed—rodents, probably, or time, which chewed slower but just as thoroughly.

Emrys crouched beside her. "It's dead."

"It's broken," she said. "Dead is a different animal." She was already pulling off her pack, already laying out the contents on the map table with the particular violence of a woman who has decided. "My plane has a radio. My plane has an antenna. My plane is sitting in eight feet of floodwater at the bottom of a gorge I can't get back to before dark, so today my plane is a parts donor."

"You'd gut your own aircraft."

"I'd gut my own *father* if he were standing between me and this." She said it flatly, and then heard it land, and looked away. "You know what I mean."

He knew what she meant. He'd been there at the trading post when Harrow Voss laid the note on the counter like a winning card, her father's signature looping across the bottom in ink gone brown, the debt that had followed her out of childhood and into every cockpit decision she'd ever made. Every gallon of fuel counted. Every load weighed twice. Every risk declined, because the plane wasn't hers—it was collateral wearing her father's paint—and a woman who doesn't own her own wings learns to fly like an apology.

Well. The plane was drowning in Kestrel Reach regardless. Voss had seen to that with his lien, the rat. So the radio could die for a better cause than fuel economy.

"Power first," she muttered, more to herself than to him. She popped the access panel. The tubes were intact—miracle number one. The wiring was corroded but continuous—miracle number two. The power supply was the problem: the tower's battery bank, whatever it had once been, was a tray of green-crusted slugs that wouldn't light a birthday candle. "We need twelve volts, clean-ish, and enough of it to warm these tubes. The set will pull maybe four amps on transmit. Less on receive."

"And you're going to find twelve volts in a burnt-out fire tower."

"I'm going to find twelve volts in my pack," she said, "because I have been flying emergency spares into river settlements for six years, and I have never once landed anywhere without a battery I couldn't justify carrying." She dug past the med kit, past the flare gun Emrys had made her leave loaded, past the foil blanket and the fishing line and the other small superstitions of bush flying, and came up with a sealed gel-cell battery the size of a brick. Twelve volts, seven amp-hours. Enough for maybe ninety minutes of intermittent transmission, if the tubes drew what she hoped and not what she feared.

Emrys stared at it. "You've been carrying that the whole time."

"Since chapter one," she said, and then caught herself using a word that belonged to no one, and covered it by getting to work.

* * *

The antenna was the harder problem, and the more dangerous one.

A broken wire dangling down the tower's leg was worse than useless; it was a resistor dressed as an aerial. She needed height, she needed length, and she needed it insulated from the tower's steel skeleton, which would otherwise drink every watt she fed it and give back static. The original feedline had run to an insulator on the roof ridge above the cab. The insulator was shattered. The wire was gone.

But the tower had been built by men who expected to be isolated up here for weeks, and men like that left things.

She found the maintenance locker welded to the cab's outer catwalk, padlocked shut, and solved the padlock the traditional way—with a pry bar and a prayer—and inside, under a nest of wasps long dead, she found the treasure: a spool of copperweld antenna wire, a bag of ceramic egg insulators, a hand-crank drill, and a coil of braided ground strap. She stood there a moment with the rain running down her face and felt something dangerously close to gratitude toward a dead stranger who had kept his locker tidy.

"Emrys! Up here!" She fed the spool down through the hatch. "I need you on the ground end. We're running a long wire from the roof, down and out over the trees, anchored to something that isn't going anywhere. You see anything solid down there?"

There was a pause while he assessed the world. "There's a dead cedar," he called back. "Big one. Leaning on the ridge north of here. It's not going anywhere either."

"Then we're in business."

They worked for an hour in the drizzle, shouting measurements to each other across eighty feet of rusted air, and it occurred to Odile, somewhere in the middle of it, that this was the most honest conversation they'd ever had. No confessions. No histories. Just numbers and knots and the shared grammar of two people building something together because neither of them could build it alone. He tied a bowline without being asked. She tensioned a guy line without being told. Their hands met twice on the same coil of wire, and both times they let go a half-second later than necessary, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them noticed.

The wire went up, sagged out over the drowned canopy, and took hold on the dead cedar with a double wrap and a tension knot her father had taught her when she was nine, standing on a tailgate, telling her that a knot you can't untie in the dark is a knot that owns you. She tied it so it could be freed with one pull. Some inheritances were worth keeping.

Back in the cab, soaked through, shivering, she connected the last lead, said a word to no god in particular, and threw the switch.

The tubes lit.

Not all of them—one glowed dull orange, one flickered like a dying firefly, and the third did nothing at all—but the dial lamp came up amber and steady, and the set hissed at her, alive, hungry, full of the whole electromagnetic ocean. She worked the tuning knob with two fingers, gentle, coaxing, and the hiss resolved into bands: static, a burst of music from some coastal station playing to an audience of nobody, a voice reading freight schedules in a language she almost recognized, static again.

"We're on the air," she breathed.

Emrys stood close behind her shoulder, and she could feel him not touching her, which was somehow louder than touch. "Roque will hear it too."

"Roque hears everything. That's the point." She turned and looked at him, really looked, water dripping off her chin onto the map table with its forty years of scratched compass roses. "Listen to me. This is the part where you tell me if you want out. Because once I key this mic, there's no version of your life where you're a ghost anymore. They'll know exactly where the evidence went and exactly who carried it. I'm about to make you a name."

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the drizzle thickened toward evening, and the drowned valley below turned the color of pewter, and somewhere far off—too far to be a threat tonight, too near to ignore—a boat engine knocked against the silence like a fist testing a door.

"When I redlined those maps," he said finally, "I thought the worst thing that could happen was that they'd ignore me. That was naive. The second-worst thing was that they'd listen and proceed anyway. That happened. But I spent two months believing the worst thing was being right and powerless." He reached past her and rested his hand flat on the radio's warm rusted case, as if taking an oath on it. "The worst thing is everyone else staying powerless with me. Key it."

* * *

She didn't start with the evidence. She started the way her father had taught her to start every broadcast, every mayday, every call into the dark: with who she was, because a voice without a name is just weather.

"All stations, all stations, all stations. This is Odile Blackwood, pilot, aircraft registration four-seven-Kilo, transmitting on open frequency. I am calling every pilot on this river, every settlement, every relay, every ear. What follows is not distress traffic. It is testimony. If you are receiving, do not acknowledge on this frequency. Listen. Write it down. Pass it on."

Beside her, Emrys held the survey's field logbook—the real one, the one he'd carried out of the flood wrapped in oilcloth against his chest while Roque's men burned the copies—and read aloud in a voice that started controlled and ended anything but.

He read the dates. He read the readings: piezometer pressures at the dam foundation exceeding design limits by March, seepage flows tripling through April, the bedrock beneath the spillway fractured along a fault line his own maps had flagged in red eighteen months before construction began. He read the memo language—"acceptable residual risk," "schedule imperative," "do not resurvey"—and he read the names, all of them, the signatures of the men who had signed off, and then he read the names of the survey team, nine men and two women, and what the flood had done to them after Roque pulled security and cut their extraction, leaving them on the wrong side of a rising valley with a boat that was never rated for that water.

"The government did not fail to predict this disaster," Emrys said into the handset, and Odile watched his knuckles whiten around it. "The government was warned, in writing, with maps, and chose the schedule over the valley. And when the survey found evidence that early failure had been anticipated—that drainage had been deliberately deferred to protect the construction timeline—Chief Ansel Roque abandoned the team in the flood zone to drown, and has spent the weeks since hunting the survivors to bury the record. I am holding that record. Copies exist beyond my control. And as of tonight, so does this broadcast."

Odile took the handset back, because there was one more thing to say, and it had to be hers.

"To the pilots listening—Marta, if you're out there, and I know you're out there, you never miss a frequency—to every man and woman who has ever taken payment from me for a favor I swore I'd repay: I am calling it in. Not money. Attention. Watch the river between the gorge and the salt line. Watch for Roque's boats, watch for his floatplane, watch for any official traffic moving at night without lights. Report sightings on the working channels. Jam him with false ones. Make this river too loud for him to think in."

She paused, and the tube hummed, and the amber lamp threw her shadow huge against the cab wall, a giant with her hand raised over the drowned world.

"They told me to stay invisible," she said. "They told all of us that. Fly low, keep quiet, pay what you owe, ask nothing, know nothing. And I did it for six years, and it bought me nothing except a front-row seat. So here's what I know now, free of charge: invisible is just a word for alone, and alone is exactly what they need us to be. Not tonight."

She keyed off. The carrier light died. The cab went very quiet, filled only with rain and the tick of cooling metal, and for ten full seconds neither of them moved, as if the signal were still traveling through them, looking for somewhere to land.

Then the receiver crackled.

One station. Two. A relay operator sixty miles south, repeating her words back verbatim in a shaking voice, asking for confirmation. A settlement net picking it up, passing it hand to hand up the river like a bucket line fighting a fire. A voice she knew—Marta Reyes, rough as gravel, unmistakable—cutting through the band with a laugh that had knives in it: *"Four-seven-Kilo, you beautiful idiot, we copy all. Say again the names. Say them slow. We're writing."*

Odile sat back on the old bolted stool and put her face in her hands and laughed until it turned into something else, and Emrys put his arm around her shoulders and let her, and outside the rain went on falling on a valley that had finally, after all these years, been given permission to speak.

Below them, far downriver, the engine sound changed—closer now, faster, no longer testing the door but coming through it.

Emrys heard it first. He always heard it first.

"Odile," he said. "We need to be off this tower."
