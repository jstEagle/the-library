## 28. Pilots Rising

Marta Quill had been running the upper rivers since before Odile Blackwood was old enough to hold a yoke, and she had learned long ago that the water told you everything if you were willing to listen. That morning the water was telling her lies.

She stood on the dock at Ferro Bend with a mug of chicory going cold in her hand and watched the river run brown and fast with the monsoon's leavings. The current should have been carrying driftwood in long, orderly lines. Instead it carried boats—three of them in an hour, all of them painted the same government gray, all of them riding low with outboard engines and men who didn't wave. Men who didn't wave on the Kestrel tributaries were either smugglers or officials, and these had too many antennas to be smugglers.

"Third one," said her deckhand, a gap-toothed boy named Turi who read the water better than any sonar. "Heading downriver. Same as the others."

Marta sipped her chicory and thought about Odile Blackwood, who owed her nothing and whom she owed plenty—a carburetor ferried through a storm two seasons back, a bag of antibiotics flown into her brother's settlement when the fever came through, a hundred small kindnesses delivered without invoice or expectation, because that was how pilots on these rivers kept each other alive. The radio at the trading post had been squawking for two days about a rogue broadcast from the old fire tower on Serrano Ridge. A woman's voice, clipped and steady, reading coordinates and names and dates into the open air like a confession being shouted from a mountaintop.

The voice had been Odile's. Marta would have known it anywhere, even chewed up by static—the same flat calm she used when landing on strips that didn't deserve the name.

And now gray boats were crawling down every channel between the tower and the coast, and Marta Quill did not need a weather report to know which way the wind blew.

"Turi," she said, setting down the mug. "Get on the wire. Raise everyone. Callao, Ruiz, the Okonkwo brothers, that fool Denby if he's sober. Tell them it's a favor call."

Turi's eyebrows went up. Favor calls were rare and sacred. A pilot could go ten years without asking one, and when she did, you came.

"For who?"

"Blackwood."

The boy grinned and ran for the shack.

* * *

By noon there were eleven aircraft on the gravel at Ferro Bend, and by evening there were fourteen, because word traveled faster than fuel could be pumped. They came in low over the treeline one after another—bush planes with patched fabric and cargo pods, a floatplane with a cracked windshield taped over, old Denby's ancient taildragger trailing a ribbon of blue smoke that he swore was character. Pilots climbed out stiff-legged and squinting, and they gathered around Marta on the dock where she'd nailed up a chart of the reach, and she told them what she knew.

"The Blackwood girl is in trouble." She tapped the chart. "She's somewhere along this corridor—tower at Serrano Ridge down to the salt line, maybe forty miles of drowned forest and channels. There's a man named Roque hunting her. Security chief for the survey outfit, private contract, but he's got ministry paper behind him, which means he's got boats, radios, and dogs. He wants what she's carrying, and I don't think he means to leave witnesses."

"What's she carrying?" asked Callao, a heavyset man who flew mail and once, memorably, a wedding party of nine.

"The truth, far as I can tell. Something about the dam. Something big enough that the government's flooding the whole valley looking for it." Marta let that sit. She watched their faces—the narrowed eyes, the quick glances downriver. Every one of them had landed in a village the dam project had emptied. Every one of them had hauled evacuees' furniture and grief in the same loads. "I'm not asking anyone to take bullets. I'm asking for noise. Confusion. Roque's people are watching the water and listening to the air. So let's give them more of both than they can sort."

Ruiz, lean and gray, spat contemplatively off the dock. "False sightings."

"False sightings. Chatter on the working frequencies. Floatplanes where there aren't any. If Roque's boats chase shadows from here to the coast, they can't be where Odile actually is." Marta looked around the circle. "She never asked me for anything. Not once in nine years. And I know half of you are flying on favors she's done you, and the other half on favors she'll do you someday, because that's how she is. So."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then old Denby, sixty-eight years old and missing two fingers, raised his tin cup of something stronger than chicory.

"To the girl who landed my engine parts in a hailstorm and wouldn't take payment," he said. "What do we do first?"

Marta smiled, and it was not a gentle smile. "First," she said, "we lie beautifully."

* * *

They began at dusk, because dusk was when the gray boats turned on their searchlights and became easy to find, and because Marta believed in starting a war with style.

Callao took his mail plane up the eastern channel and put on a show. He'd rigged a lantern behind a red lens in his cargo pod, and he flew slow circles over open water while broadcasting, on the frequency every boat in the reach monitored, in a voice pitched just loud enough to seem accidental: *"Contact, contact, moving fast toward the narrows, two boats, armed—I'm getting out, I'm getting out—"*

Then he cut his engine noise and ghosted away into the hills, and three gray boats turned east and burned twenty minutes of fuel chasing a lantern that had already gone home to roost.

Ruiz worked the western side. He'd stuffed his floatplane's pontoon lockers with signal flares bought cheap from a mining outfit going under, and he spent the evening lobbing them from altitudes and angles that made them look like muzzle flashes, then keying his mic with bursts of static and fragments of invented conversation: *"—negative, negative, lost visual—say again, how many paddlers?—"* The boats on the west channel spent the night sweeping reeds for paddlers who existed only in Ruiz's imagination, and Ruiz flew home chuckling so hard his copilot—his nervous teenage daughter, who was getting the education of her life—had to take the controls twice.

Denby, who was not sober but was functional, performed what he called his masterpiece: he taxied his smoking taildragger out onto a sandbar directly in view of a patrol boat's searchlight, staggered around on the bar waving a flashlight in patterns that meant nothing, and then, when the boat approached, took off in a shrieking climb that convinced the men aboard they'd found exactly the kind of desperate fugitive they were hunting. They pursued him up a dead-end slough for an hour. Denby led them past a heron rookery, over a fisherman's net (apologies later, beer included), and finally into fog so thick the patrol boat had to drop anchor and wait for morning. Denby landed at Ferro Bend at midnight, poured himself a celebration, and pronounced the evening "adequate."

By dawn, the picture Roque's network had assembled was a masterpiece of nonsense: fugitives sighted simultaneously in four places thirty miles apart; a seaplane refueling at a lodge that had burned down years ago; armed men paddling a stolen canoe; a woman matching Odile Blackwood's description buying fuel at three different strips within the same hour, which even the most credulous radio operator had to admit was aerodynamically ambitious.

Marta sat in the shack at Ferro Bend with the headset on, monitoring it all, feeding corrections and contradictions into the stream, and she laughed until her ribs hurt—not because it was funny, though it was, but because of what it meant. For years she'd watched Odile Blackwood keep herself small. One plane, one route, no debts of friendship, no entanglements. Flying alone like solitude was a load-bearing wall. And here was the proof, written in fourteen airplanes and a night of beautiful lies, that the woman had been building community the whole time without ever letting herself notice—one delivery, one favor, one storm flight at a time. You couldn't buy this kind of loyalty. You couldn't owe your way into it. You could only earn it, gallon by gallon, year by year, and Odile had earned it without ever sending an invoice.

"She thinks nobody's watching her back," Marta said to Turi, who was asleep sitting up against the fuel drums. "Fool girl. Whole river's watching her back."

* * *

On the third day, the interference stopped being a game.

Roque adapted—that was clear enough. The gray boats stopped chasing flares and started moving in coordinated sweeps, and a floatplane appeared over the reach with military markings freshly painted over older ones, flying grid patterns with a discipline none of Marta's people could match. Worse, someone in Roque's chain had started transmitting on the pilots' own working frequencies, a smooth, patient voice offering rewards for information and consequences for obstruction, naming names. He named Marta's. He named Denby's. He named Callao's, and mentioned, almost gently, that unlicensed charter operations were such a fragile thing, legally speaking.

"He knows it's us," Ruiz said, standing at the chart with his arms crossed. "Of course he knows. Question is whether he can prove it, and whether any of us can afford to find out."

"My license is older than he is," Denby said. "Let him come take it."

"It's not licenses I'm worried about." Ruiz tapped the chart, on the lower reach near the salt line. "It's this. He's pulled everything back to the coast. Boats at the weir, the floatplane on station, and word from my cousin at the ranger relay that there's a seaplane tender anchored off the estuary. He's not searching anymore. He's waiting. He knows she has to come to the water eventually, and he's built a door with only one way through."

Marta studied the chart until her eyes ached. He was right. The game of confusion had bought Odile days, and days mattered—but the coast was a funnel, and Roque held the narrow end of it. Unless.

Unless somebody held the other end.

She looked around the shack—at Turi, awake now and pretending he wasn't listening; at the fuel schedule chalked on the board; at the radio with its worn handset. Then she keyed the transmit switch and called a frequency she'd been saving, the one the coastal pilots used, the ones with seaplanes and salt-stained hulls and no particular love for ministry security men either.

"This is Quill, Ferro Bend, on the open band. For anyone listening who owes the Blackwood girl a kindness—or who just doesn't like men who hunt women with dogs." She took a breath. "There's a seaplane tender off the estuary and a man named Roque building a trap at the salt line. I've got river pilots jamming his boats, but I need eyes on the coast. I need someone willing to sit on that tender's horizon and make his floatplane crew sweat. And I need it soon, because I think she's coming downriver right now, and I think she's coming fast."

Static. Long enough that her stomach started to sink.

Then a voice, female, unhurried, with the flat drawl of the delta strips: *"Quill, this is Ilsa Reyes, Barataria base. Been listening to your circus for two days—it's been the best entertainment this river's seen in years. I've got three floats and a grudge. What do you need?"*

Behind her, one after another, voices joined the channel. A shrimp-boat spotter out of Port Maren. A medevac pilot who'd trained under Odile's father. A man who simply said, *"Blackwood taught me crosswind landings when no one else would talk to me. I'm in."*

Marta closed her eyes for half a second, and let herself feel it: the whole river, waking up.

Then she opened them, spread the chart flat, and started assigning positions. "Reyes, take your floats wide of the estuary, high sun angle, make his lookouts miserable. Maren, I want your spotter circling the tender at maximum legal distance and minimum useful altitude. Everyone else—chatter, chatter, chatter. Fill his ears with ghosts." She paused, then added, quieter, into the mouthpiece, knowing it might carry, hoping it would:

"And Odile—if you're on any frequency, any at all—head for Barataria. Reyes will have a plane warm. Don't try the obvious channel; we'll make the obvious channel a graveyard. Fly ugly, fly low, fly mad. We've got you, girl. First time in your life, let somebody have you."

She released the switch. The shack hummed with radio traffic, fourteen pilots and counting weaving a net of noise across forty miles of drowned forest, and out beyond the salt line, three seaplanes were already climbing into the afternoon light.

Turi looked up from the fuel ledger. "You think she heard?"

Marta picked up her cold chicory, decided against it, and poured it over the dock rail into the brown, running water. "That girl's been hearing this river her whole life," she said. "She'll hear us."

But even as she said it, her eyes went to the chart—to the funnel at the coast, to the tender anchored fat and patient in the estuary, to the thin dotted line of a seaplane route that ended, if Roque had his way, in gunfire on open water—and the laugh had gone out of her entirely. Somewhere down in those green miles, Odile Blackwood was running out of river.
