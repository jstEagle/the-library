## 29. Reunion at the Salt Line

The mangroves ended the way all rivers end—not with a border but with a thinning, the green going gray, the water widening until it forgot which way was up. Emrys came out of the last stand of trees at first light, boots sucking in the mudflats, and stopped when he saw it: a seaplane on floats, tied to a rickety dock that leaned out over the brown water like a man leaning over a rail to spit.

Marta's plane. He knew it from Odile's descriptions—the dented cowling, the tail painted with a faded sunburst, the cargo door that never latched right. Marta herself stood on the dock in a rain slicker two sizes too big, arms crossed, watching him come with the flat appraisal of a woman who had flown supply runs into places where the wrong passenger could cost her everything.

"You're Vale," she said. Not a question.

"I'm Vale."

"Odile said you'd come out of the swamp looking like something the river chewed and spat back." She looked him over. "She undersold it."

He managed something that might have been a laugh. His legs were shaking. Three days in the mangroves since the handoff at the weir went to pieces—three days of mud, mosquitoes, and the memory of Roque's men dragging Odile into the green while he ran the other way because she'd screamed *go* and his body had obeyed before his heart could argue. He'd been navigating by dead reckoning and guilt ever since, counting hours instead of miles, telling himself she was alive because Roque needed her alive, or thought he did, or—

"Where is she?" he asked.

"That's my line." Marta unfolded her arms. "Radio traffic says Roque's floatplane has been circling the delta since yesterday afternoon. Somebody spotted a woman moving along the east channel this morning, alone, fast." A pause. "You want to tell me why a government security chief wants you both bad enough to burn fuel he doesn't have?"

Emrys looked out at the salt line—the place where the river's brown gave way to the sea's steel gray, a seam drawn across the water like a rule under a sentence. Beyond it, open water. Beyond it, the coast road, the capital, the auditor who was waiting for what he carried.

"He doesn't want us," Emrys said. "He wants what I'm carrying. The survey records. The real maps. Proof the dam failed because they built it on ground their own engineers told them wouldn't hold—and proof they knew the valley would flood faster than any evacuation could manage."

"And Odile?"

"Odile is the one who made sure it survived the weir." His voice caught on her name and he let it. "Roque searched me first. She gave him a decoy—her father's compass case, hollowed out, a drive full of nothing inside. It bought me the seconds I needed. Then she told me to run, and I did, and I've hated myself for every hour since."

Marta studied him for a long moment. Behind her, gulls argued over the dock pilings, and the tide breathed in and out against the flats.

"She also said," Marta said finally, "that if a tall, miserable cartographer washed up on my dock, I should keep the engine warm. So." She jerked her chin at the plane. "Get aboard. If she's coming, she's coming now, and I don't like sitting still with Roque's shadow on the water."

* * *

Odile ran because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant seeing again the moment the mangroves closed between them.

Two days. Two days since the handoff, since Roque's men had come out of the treeline with dogs and floodlights, since she'd pressed the compass case into Emrys's hands and watched his face do the arithmetic of what she was about to do. *Take the real one and go,* she'd said. *I'll give them something to chase.* And then she'd stepped out into the light with the decoy held high like a lantern and let them take her.

They hadn't kept her long. That was the thing about being worth more as a lead than as a prisoner—Roque's men had searched her, taken the compass case, found the drive inside, plugged it into a field reader right there in the mud while she knelt with her hands laced behind her head and her heart hammering out a rhythm she'd remember for the rest of her life. Empty files. Garbage data. A folder labeled *FLIGHT LOGS — O. BLACKWOOD* containing nothing but weather reports from settlements she'd actually served, dates and rainfall, honest useless work.

She'd seen Roque's face do the math. Seen the exact moment he understood he'd been played by a bush pilot with a dead father's compass. His hand had started toward her and she'd already moved—shoulder into the nearest man, down the bank, into the black water of the mangrove channel, bullets slapping the surface around her like fat raindrops.

That was two days ago. Since then: swimming channels at slack tide, sleeping in the crook of tree roots with her clothes wrung out and draped over branches, eating nothing but a tin of condensed milk she'd found in an abandoned hunting blind and half a bag of dried fish gone soft with damp. Her left ankle was swollen where she'd turned it on a root the first night. Her hands were cut to ribbons from the mangrove bark. She'd navigated by the same instincts her father had taught her before he taught her anything else—read the water, read the birds, follow the smell of salt.

And now she smelled it. Salt and diesel and fish, and underneath it all, faintly, the sweet exhaust note of an aircraft engine being run up warm.

She came out of the trees onto the mudflat at a dead stagger, and the world narrowed to the dock, the plane, the figure standing at the rail of it.

Not Marta. Taller. Thinner. Mud-stained shirt, hair plastered to his forehead, a bandage around one forearm that hadn't been there at the weir.

Emrys.

He saw her the way people see lightning—there and then everywhere, his whole body turning toward her before his eyes had finished confirming it. He came off the dock at a run, boots throwing mud, and she didn't slow down and neither did he and they collided hard enough to hurt, her fists gripping his shirt, his arms crushing her against him, both of them shaking.

"You're bleeding," he said into her hair, idiotically, because her scalp was split somewhere above her ear and had been for a day and a half.

"You're limping," she said back.

"We're a matched set." His voice broke on it. He pulled back just far enough to look at her face, both hands framing it like he was checking a map against the territory, making sure nothing had been redrawn. "I thought—I heard the dogs turn east, and I thought—"

"I know." She pressed her forehead to his. "I know. Me too."

Behind them, Marta's voice cut across the flats like a whip: "As touching as this is, we've got company!"

* * *

Odile saw it a half-second after Marta pointed—a dark shape low over the eastern channel, coming on fast, the flat silhouette of Roque's floatplane skimming the treetops with its running lights off.

"Dock!" Marta barked. "Now! Both of you!"

They ran. Bullets came before they got there—the floatplane's door gunner opening up at extreme range, rounds stitching the mudflat behind them in long tearing lines, geysers of muck jumping up where each one struck. Odile felt one crack past close enough to part the air near her ear, a sound like a wasp the size of a fist.

Emrys reached the dock first and turned, arm out, and she took his hand and he hauled her up onto the planks and they were both aboard the seaplane before the second burst landed, splinters flying off the dock rails. Marta was already in the pilot's seat, already feeding throttle, shouting over the engine noise.

"Floats are wet, doors shut, hold on to something that loves you!"

The seaplane surged forward across the brown water. Behind them the floatplane banked wide, swinging its gun arc around, and another burst hammered across the stern—one round punching through the fuselage somewhere aft with a sound like a hammer through a drum. Odile threw herself across Emrys and felt him fold around her, shielding her with his body without seeming to decide to, and something in her chest cracked open along a seam she hadn't known was there.

Then the hull touched the swell of the outer channel, lifted, touched again, and lifted for good, and the world tilted up into gray morning light and the sea unrolled beneath them, vast and indifferent and, for the first time in six days, empty of anything hunting them.

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke. The engine droned. Marta flew a wide climbing turn out over the salt line, eyes flicking constantly to the mirrors, and Odile sat wedged in the jump seat beside Emrys with his shoulder against hers, both of them breathing like they'd forgotten how.

"He'll report the decoy," Emrys said finally. His voice was hoarse. "By tonight, Roque will know the drive he took is worthless and that I still have the real one. He can't afford to let us reach the auditor. He'll throw everything he has at the coast road."

"Then we don't take the coast road." Odile leaned her head back against the fuselage frame and closed her eyes. "Marta. How far to the capital strip?"

"Three hours, direct. Less if I cut over the estuary and don't mind the weather building off the shelf." Marta glanced back at them. "You're both wrecked. You know that, yes? You look like survivors of something."

"We are survivors of something," Odile said.

Emrys's hand found hers in the dim cabin—found it, hesitated, then closed around it, careful of the cuts on her palms. She didn't pull away. She turned her hand over instead and laced her fingers through his, mud and blood and all, and felt him exhale a breath he might have been holding since the weir.

"When they took you," he said quietly, "I stood in those trees and I couldn't move. Three days, Odile. Every hour I told myself you were alive because you were too stubborn to die, and every hour I didn't believe it."

"I'm too stubborn to die," she agreed. "It's my best quality. Ask anyone I owe money to."

A sound escaped him that was half laugh, half something rawer, and he brought her hand up and pressed his mouth briefly to her knuckles—an old-fashioned gesture, absurd, undone by the state of her hands—and she felt it land somewhere below her ribs and stay there.

"Marta," she called forward, not letting go. "When we land—when this is done, whatever 'done' ends up meaning—" She stopped. Started again. "Thank you for keeping the engine warm."

"Odile Blackwood," Marta said, eyes on the horizon, mouth curving, "you have owed me favors for nine years. Consider this one called even." A beat. "The next one costs extra."

Ahead, the coast fell away and the sea opened wide, and far off to the south a line of storm cloud was building over the estuary, blue-black and towering, the wet season announcing itself the way it always did—without permission, without apology.

Emrys squeezed her hand. "Whatever happens at the other end of this flight," he said, "the evidence reaches the auditor. And after that—" He paused, and she watched him search for the word the way he'd once searched for contour lines in blank country.

"After that," Odile finished for him, "we find out who we are when nobody's chasing us."

The seaplane climbed into the light, and behind them, small and receding, the drowned valley sank back into its gray water, keeping—for a little longer—its secrets.

But not, she thought, for much longer.
