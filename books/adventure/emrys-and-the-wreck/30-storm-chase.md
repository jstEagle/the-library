## 30. Storm Chase

The squall line stood up off the sea like a wall being built by impatient hands.

Odile saw it from six miles out—a bruised gray rampart stacked over the coastal hills, lightning stitching its seams in slow, silent flashes, the kind of storm that didn't drift so much as *arrive*. Behind them, smaller but gaining, came Roque's floatplane: a hard-nosed amphibian with government markings painted over and the paint not quite thick enough to hide them.

"Tell me you have a plan," Emrys said from the right seat. He had the map case strapped across his chest now, the real evidence riding against his ribs like a second heartbeat. His knuckles were white on the doorframe.

"I have terrain," Odile said. "Terrain is better than a plan."

She rolled the Cessna's wings level and studied the line again, reading it the way she'd learned to read weather before she'd learned to read much else—her father's voice in her ear at eleven years old, *clouds are just water telling you where it's been and where it means to go*. This one meant to go inland, up the river valleys, dragging its tail of rain and turbulence behind it like a net. Anyone flying into it blind would be flying into a blender.

But she wasn't blind. She'd spent nine years threading these hills with medicine crates and mail sacks and other people's gossip, counting gallons against a debt, learning every fold of this coast the way you learn the face of someone you can't stop watching. The storm was a wall. Walls had doors. She knew where every door was.

"Hold on," she said, and pushed the nose down toward the black water.

* * *

Roque was good. She'd give him that. He'd kept them in sight through the mangrove maze below the salt line, swinging wide when she jinked low over the channels, patient as a debt collector—which, in a way, he was. He wanted what they carried, and men like Ansel Roque did not lose things they wanted. They simply raised the price until someone paid it.

The radio crackled. His voice came through flat and unhurried, the same voice that had offered her freedom for a lie.

*"Blackwood. You're out of runway ahead and out of friends everywhere else. Set it down on the water and we talk about your future like reasonable people."*

Emrys reached for the mic. Odile put her hand over his.

"He wants us answering," she said. "Answering is thinking about him. I'm busy."

She was. The squall line's leading edge was coming apart overhead now—first gusts hitting the plane in shoves, rain starting in fat, scattered drops that ticked against the windshield like fingers testing a lock. Below, the coastline unspooled: white sand, then the green rampart of the hills, then—the thing she'd been flying toward since the moment she'd seen the storm building—the mouth of the Serpentine Gorge.

Most pilots knew the gorge as a place to avoid. It ran eight miles through the coastal range, narrow enough in places that her wingspan left less than forty feet of margin on either side, twisting like its name, walled in slick black stone that shed water in a hundred waterfalls whenever the sky broke open. There was no reason on earth to fly it in clear weather, let alone in the teeth of a monsoon squall.

Which was exactly why Roque wouldn't follow her into it.

And exactly why she could.

* * *

The first gust hit them a quarter mile from the entrance and slammed the Cessna sideways like a hand swatting a fly. Odile caught it with rudder and aileron together, boots and hands moving without consulting her, the way her heart beat without consulting her. The river appeared below—the brown flood-swollen ribbon of it—and she dropped toward it, down under the hill crests, into the gorge's throat.

The walls rose around them. Wet stone, ferns clinging in green ledges, water already sheeting down the rock face in silver ropes. The light went from gray to something older, darker, the light of a place the sun visited only at noon and grudgingly.

"This is insane," Emrys said. But his voice had changed—there was wonder in it, buried under the fear, the wonder of a man who made maps looking at terrain no map had ever done justice.

"It's Tuesday," Odile said.

The gorge bent left. She banked with it, wings nearly parallel to the water, close enough that spray off the rapids below freckled the windshield. In the mirror of the river she caught a flicker of movement—Roque's floatplane, still high, still outside, circling the storm's edge like a shark that had lost sight of something beneath the surface.

He hadn't followed. Of course he hadn't followed. No sane pilot would.

*That,* Odile thought, feeding power into the climb as the canyon narrowed, *is the whole trick.*

* * *

The squall caught up with them inside the gorge, because of course it did; storms don't respect geometry. The rain arrived all at once, a solid gray curtain, and the world outside the windshield became a smear of stone and water. Visibility dropped to nothing. Wind screamed down the canyon walls and rebounded off them in eddies that took the little plane and shook it like a dog shaking a rat.

Her father's compass sat in its bracket on the panel, needle swinging wild in the electrical chaos, useless. She didn't need it. She flew the shape of the gorge itself—the feel of it stored somewhere below thought, in the same place her body kept the knowledge of how to breathe.

Left bend, climbing. Right bend, descending into the lee of the wind. The waterfall at Mile Four—she felt for it before she saw it, the cold downdraft spilling off the falls like a hand pushing her down, and she let it push, trading altitude for control, then climbed hard in the rotor on the far side. Her shoulders burned. Sweat ran inside her collar despite the cold.

"You know this by heart," Emrys said. It wasn't a question. He'd gone quiet in the way people go quiet in churches.

"My father flew it twice a month for eleven years." She grunted as an eddy shoved them toward the left wall and hauled them back with both hands. "He used to say the gorge was honest. Everything else on this coast lies—weather reports, government maps, men with ledgers. The gorge just tells you what it is and lets you decide if you're equal to it."

"And were you?"

"Not yet, back then. I was sixteen the first time he let me take the controls in here." A waterfall burst past her wingtip close enough to see individual streams braiding together. "I froze. He took it back, landed us on the pool below, and made me sit there for an hour watching him fly touch-and-goes alone until I stopped being scared."

"Were you?"

"No." She smiled despite everything, despite the rain hammering the plane and the man hunting them and the evidence pressed against Emrys's chest. "I was furious. That's better fuel."

* * *

Mile Six was the narrows. She'd been saving it.

The gorge pinched there to barely more than her wingspan, a slot between walls of wet black basalt, and the river below went white and deafening over a shelf of submerged rock. In the rain, in the dark, in the churn of the storm—it was a corridor the width of a coffin.

She slowed to just above stall speed. Flaps half. Eyes moving between the walls, judging gaps that shifted with every gust.

"Odile." Emrys's voice was very careful. "What's the margin here?"

"Forty feet on a calm day."

"And today?"

"Today the wind decides." She rolled her neck once, felt it crack. "So I stop arguing with it and start listening."

She flew the narrows the way she imagined a tightrope walker crosses between buildings—not fighting the sway but joining it, letting each gust move her and correcting only enough, hands feather-light on a yoke that wanted to tear free. Stone slid past her wingtips so close she could see the mineral glitter in the wet rock. Once—she would remember this always—a fern growing from a crack brushed the strut with a sound like a whisper, like the gorge itself saying *yes, you may pass*.

Then it opened. The walls fell away, the rain thinned to ordinary deluge, and they were through—out the far side into the wide flooded valley beyond, the storm's core now behind them, its trailing edge streaming past overhead like the hem of a departing dress.

Behind them, the gorge swallowed its own secret. If Roque had somehow dared the entrance, he wasn't through yet. And if he circled around—if he climbed over the range to cut them off on the far side—he'd be doing it blind, in the worst air on the coast, through passes he didn't know, in an aircraft heavy with fuel and worse decisions.

Odile leveled off, shook the tension out of her hands one finger at a time, and laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, a sound she didn't recognize as hers.

"We lost him," Emrys said. He sounded like a man who'd been holding his breath for eight miles and had only just noticed.

"For now." She checked the gauges—fuel fine, engine temperature fine, everything fine, the machine forgiving her for what she'd asked of it. "He'll report us missing over the gorge. By tonight every patrol boat on the coast will be watching the river mouths. Which is why we're not going to any river mouths."

"Where are we going, then?"

She looked at the country ahead: drowned forest giving way to higher ground, the long green shoulder of the interior, and beyond it, two days' hard flying south, the capital. Somewhere out there was a federal auditor with a name Emrys had memorized like a prayer. Somewhere behind them was everything they were running from.

But first—

"There's a strip above the timberline, old logging road, nobody's used it since the war," she said. "We land, we wait out the back of this system, and we fly the high route south tomorrow at first light. Above the cloud deck, where Roque's boats can't follow and his floatplane can't hide."

Emrys was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had an odd weight to it. "You flew that gorge like you were born in it. Like the plane was just... more of you."

Odile watched the rain-washed hills roll beneath the nose and thought about all the years she'd treated her flying as a transaction—hours logged, debts serviced, a skill traded for survival. She had never once thought of it as *hers*. It had belonged to the debt. To her father's shadow. To the ledger.

But in the narrows, with forty feet of stone on either side and the storm trying to kill her, there had been no debt, no Voss, no Roque. There had been only her hands and her knowledge and the gorge's terrible honesty. For eight miles she had been the most free she had ever been in her life.

"It's the one thing I own outright," she said quietly. "Nobody can lien it. Nobody can call it due."

* * *

They found the logging strip an hour later, a scar of gravel and alder scrub on a ridge shoulder, sodden but solid. Odile set the Cessna down in a crosswind that would have terrified her a year ago and taxied it under the leaning cover of a collapsed equipment shed, half-hiding the tail among the alders.

While Emrys rigged a tarp over the engine cowling, she stood in the rain at the strip's edge and looked back north, toward the coast they'd left, toward Kestrel Reach hidden somewhere beyond the hills. The storm was still working itself out over the sea, lightning pulsing inside the cloud like something alive turning over in its sleep.

Somewhere under that weather, Ansel Roque was landing, or reporting, or raging. Somewhere in the capital, Harrow Voss was sitting on a ministry board, waiting to hear whether his investment in silence had paid off. Somewhere in the drowned valley, villages that no longer existed waited for someone to say their names aloud.

Emrys came to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching hers. Neither of them spoke for a while. The rain gentled. Down the ridge, a bird started up—some thrush, insistent, singing into the aftermath as though the storm had been a rumor.

"The auditor," Emrys said finally. "Ferrante. He works out of the Ministry of Infrastructure building, fourth floor. I've met him once. He struck me as a man who reads everything." A pause. "Assuming we live to reach him."

"We'll live." She said it simply, and found she believed it—not because the odds had improved, but because she had remembered who she was. "I got us through the gorge. The rest is just distance."

Emrys looked at her then, rain running off his jaw, and there was something in his face she couldn't name yet—something that had been growing since the leaking boat, since the island, since the gunfire at the salt line. She turned away from it before it could become words. Not yet. Tomorrow, above the clouds, with the evidence delivered and the hunt ended—then maybe there would be time for whatever that look was asking.

That night they slept in the plane, seats reclined, doors locked, the tarp flapping softly over the engine. Odile woke once in the small hours to a sky rinsed clean of cloud and more stars than she'd seen in years, and lay listening to Emrys breathe, and thought: *the gorge was honest, and so am I, finally.*

At first light they lifted off the ridge into air like polished glass, climbing through the last rags of mist into blinding sun, and turned south along the spine of the high country.

Two hours into the flight, the radio—silent for days—crackled to life with a voice she knew, tight and urgent, calling her name across every open channel on the band.

It was Marta. And she was saying the word *ambush*.
