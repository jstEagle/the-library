## 31. The Capital's Door

The capital smelled of diesel and cut stone, and Emrys hated it on sight.

He had forgotten how cities pressed against a man—how they took the wide green noise of the world and folded it down into corridors and queues, into the smell of a hundred strangers' sweat and laundry soap. For eleven days he had lived in the open: river, mangrove, salt marsh, the long coastal road where Marta's seaplane had finally set them down at a military airfield forty miles short of the city proper. He had slept in ditches and under bridges with Odile's shoulder warm against his, and none of it had felt as exposed as this—standing on marble steps beneath a colonnade built to make citizens feel small, holding a waterproof satchel that contained the only honest maps in the country.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said beside him.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go quiet and start cataloguing exits." She was looking straight ahead, chin up, wearing the one clean shirt she owned—a man's field shirt, faded blue, sleeves rolled—and somehow she wore it better than any of the clerks and functionaries streaming past them in their pressed uniforms. "There are three doors. We came in one. Relax."

"I'm always relaxed."

"You haven't blinked since we got out of the taxi."

He almost smiled. That was the strange arithmetic of the last two weeks: that he should have found his ease not in solitude, which had been his discipline for years, but in the company of a bush pilot who counted fuel like a miser and swore at engines like old friends. Somewhere between the leaking boat and the shingle bar, between bailing water by lamplight and the confession he'd never meant to give anyone, she had become the fixed point his life swung around. He did not know what to call it yet. He only knew that when she stood next to him, the capital felt marginally less like a trap.

They climbed the steps of the Federal Audit Commission together.

* * *

The building swallowed sound. Their footsteps died on the marble; their voices dropped without being asked. Everything was pale stone and dark wood and brass fittings polished by decades of nervous hands. Portraits of dead directors lined the stairwell, each one painted with the same expression of patient disappointment, as if they could already see everything their successors would fail to do.

Emrys felt the satchel strap cutting into his shoulder and made himself breathe.

Eleven days ago the evidence had been a weight he carried alone—the redlined survey maps, the original hydrology reports with the falsified figures pasted over the true ones, the internal memoranda from the Kestrel Dam Authority, and the recording he'd made on the last night, when Chief Roque's voice had told the team, in the flat tone of a man reading a supply manifest, that extraction was cancelled and that anyone who reached the coast would be dealt with as a deserter. Eleven days ago it had been contraband. Now, walking these halls, it was testimony. The difference was everything, and it changed nothing about how heavy it was.

The auditor's office was on the fourth floor, behind a door of frosted glass lettered FEDERAL OVERSIGHT — SPECIAL INQUIRIES. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took their names, looked at Emrys's travel-worn clothes and Odile's sunburned face, and visibly revised whatever assumptions he'd made downward. But when Emrys said the words *Kestrel Reach survey* and *original documentation*, the clerk's pen stopped moving.

"Wait here," he said, and disappeared for twenty minutes.

When he returned, he brought them through a second door into a room that was nothing like the grandeur of the corridors—a cramped office stacked with file boxes, lit by a single window grimy with city soot. A woman rose from behind a desk buried in paper. She was perhaps fifty-five, silver at her temples, with the squint of someone who had spent thirty years reading fine print by bad light, and she moved with the unhurried economy of a person who had learned that haste in her profession was how documents got lost.

"Auditor Corvane," she said. "Ilsa Corvane. Sit down, both of you. You look like you've been swimming here."

"In a manner of speaking," Odile said.

Corvane studied them for a long moment—not unkindly, but thoroughly, the way Emrys himself once studied terrain before committing a line to paper. Then she held out her hand for the satchel, and something in Emrys's chest clenched hard enough that for a moment he couldn't move.

Odile's hand covered his on the strap.

"It's all right," she said quietly. "That's why we came."

He let it go. Watched Corvane carry it to her desk, break the waterproof seal with a letter opener, and begin, methodically, to lay out the contents in the grey light. The maps first—he saw her recognize them immediately for what they were, because her fingers went still above the first sheet. Emrys had drawn those contours himself, every contour line earned on foot through the drowned valley's rim country. And across the unstable foundation zone, in the ministry's official red grease-pencil, the flat annotation that had signed away a village and eventually nine lives: *REVIEWED — PROCEED AS PLANNED.*

"Where were these supposed to be filed?" Corvane asked, not looking up.

"Central Records, Ministry of Water and Power," Emrys said. "The originals were pulled the week after my final submission. What went back into circulation were copies with amended figures."

"And you kept the originals."

"I kept the originals." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "I told myself I kept them because the data was irreplaceable. Two years of fieldwork. But that wasn't the truth, Auditor. I kept them because I knew, even then, that someday someone would need to see them, and I didn't trust myself to say so out loud until it was too late to matter."

Corvane looked up at him then, and there was no pity in her face, which he found he was grateful for. Pity would have been unbearable. There was only the attention of a woman who had spent her career following paper trails to the people who thought paper was safe.

"Tell me everything," she said. "Start wherever you need to start. I have until evening, and I've read slower things than dam failures."

* * *

It took four hours.

He told it in order, because disorder would sound like invention: the survey commission, the funding routed through the Water and Power Ministry, the mandate to assess the Kestrel valley for a secondary dam that would double the reservoir's capacity. His own surveys—the borings that came back wrong, the clay seams in the valley floor that no amount of engineering optimism could argue into stability. His report. The red pencil. The meeting where the deputy director had explained, with the gentle patience of a man addressing a slow child, that the project had commitments, that the financing was structured, that the hydrological risk assessment had been *recalibrated*, and that Mr. Vale's continued employment depended on his ability to distinguish between science and obstruction.

Then the flood season. The dam holding—but barely, its spillways running at design limits while the ministry publicly announced normal operations. The survey team sent back in, ostensibly to monitor, actually—to Emrys now believed—to gather the documentation that would let the ministry claim the structure was sound. Nine people. Himself among them, because he had gone back for his maps.

And then the night the auxiliary cofferdam failed upstream. Not the main dam—the *cofferdam*, the temporary structure, the cheap one, the one whose maintenance budget had been quietly gutted. It hadn't collapsed catastrophically. It had simply let go in the dark, and the valley had filled four meters in six hours, and the survey camp on the low bench had gone under with everything in it except what men could carry at a run.

Roque's security detail had boats. The survey team had one aluminum boat and a radio that worked for exactly one transmission before the water took the mast. Emrys remembered standing in rising water with the satchel over his head, listening to Roque's launch pull away, hearing—or deciding afterward he'd heard—one of the security men say, *orders are orders, Vale, nothing personal.*

Three of the team made it to high ground with him. The others—

He stopped there. Corvane waited. Odile's hand found his forearm under the desk and stayed.

"The others," he said, "we found downstream. Or part of them did." He would not describe the strainer log. Some things belonged to the rain, and he intended to leave them there.

Corvane wrote for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—there was iron under it now, the sound of a woman who had just watched a case stop being a file and become a reckoning.

"This is sufficient for a formal inquiry. More than sufficient. The falsified hydrology alone constitutes criminal negligence at minimum; the abandonment of the team during the flood event is a separate charge entirely, and the security chief—" she tapped Roque's name in her notes, "—will be referred for prosecution pending corroboration. Which brings us to jurisdiction, and to the unpleasant part."

"There's an unpleasant part," Odile said dryly. "Of course there is."

"The Special Inquiries division has authority to investigate any ministry." Corvane removed her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "What we cannot do is compel cooperation from officials above the rank of director-general without a warrant countersigned by the oversight board. And the oversight board's composition is public record, which means anyone we intend to investigate will know we're coming weeks before we arrive."

She turned to a shelf behind her, ran her finger along a row of bound registers, and pulled one down with the weary familiarity of a woman opening a book she'd hoped never to read closely again.

"Ministry of Water and Power. Board of Directors, current session." Her finger moved down the printed column of names. "Chairman. Vice-chair. Director of Engineering. Director of Finance. Director of—" 

Her finger stopped.

Emrys watched it stop. Watched her read the name twice, the way he himself re-read a coordinate that contradicted the map. And in the silence of that cramped office, with the city humming faintly beyond the dirty window, he understood what was coming a half-second before she said it, because the world had been teaching him this lesson for two years: that rot does not stay politely at the bottom of a structure. That water finds every crack, and so does money.

"Harrow Voss," Corvane said. "Director of Finance. Appointed to the board fourteen months ago."

The name landed in the room like a dropped tool.

Beside him, Odile had gone very still. Not the stillness of surprise—Emrys knew her well enough now to tell the difference. This was the stillness of confirmation. Of a pattern completing itself. He heard her exhale slowly through her nose, the way she did when an engine gave her one bad reading too many and she finally admitted what it meant.

"That's my debt holder," she said. "Harrow Voss holds the note on my father's plane. Has for three years. He's been squeezing me the whole time—small payments, impossible terms, and two weeks ago he took a lien on the aircraft outright." Her laugh had no humor in it. "I thought it was just Voss being Voss. A vulture being a vulture. I flew medicine to dying settlements for that man because I inherited my father's signature and he knew I'd pay anything to keep flying."

"Miss Blackwood." Corvane's voice was careful. "I need you to think very precisely. When did Voss acquire your father's debt?"

"Three years ago. Maybe a little more. My father died and the note transferred—I never even met the original lender. Voss bought it through one of his trading companies."

"Fourteen months ago, Harrow Voss joined the board that approved the Kestrel expansion project." Corvane was writing as she spoke, fast now. "But he was in finance in this region long before that. If he acquired distressed debts from pilots and outfitters working the Kestrel corridor—if he systematically gained leverage over the civilian aviation infrastructure around that valley—"

"He owns the sky over it," Odile said. Her voice had gone quiet and cold, and Emrys recognized the tone: it was the one she'd used on the boat, the night she stopped pretending she could opt out. "Every pilot who flies that reach owes him or fears him or both. He bought up the debts after the evacuation emptied the villages—everyone who left sold cheap, and Voss was there with contracts. I used to wonder why a man like him bothered with bush notes worth pennies. I used to tell myself it was just how vultures are."

"It wasn't pennies," Emrys said slowly, and the shape of it assembled itself in his head the way terrain assembles in fog, one ridge at a time. "It was positioning. If the audit ever came, if anyone ever started asking questions about Kestrel, he'd want control of the transportation. The witnesses fly. The evidence flies. Every route in or out of that valley runs through aircraft whose owners owe him money." He looked at Odile. "He didn't chase you off. He *kept* you. Flying your routes, paying your interest, staying visible and harmless—because a pilot who trusts her own debt holder never looks twice at him."

"And when I crashed into his valley," Odile said, "and picked up his cartographer, he took the plane inside of a day. That wasn't a business decision. That was containment."

Corvane had stopped writing. She sat with her hands flat on the register, looking at the two of them across the cluttered desk, and when she spoke, all the bureaucratic distance had burned away.

"I am going to be direct with you, because you've earned directness. What you've brought me is enough to open an inquiry. It is not enough to survive one. Voss sits on the board that must countersign my warrants. The moment I file, he will know within hours. He has the resources to destroy documents, relocate assets, and—if your account of this security chief is accurate—people who handle physical problems. An inquiry that dies in committee doesn't just fail. It tells everyone involved exactly who talked, and then those people spend the rest of their lives looking over their shoulders." She paused. "Or worse. Not looking over their shoulders, because they've stopped needing to."

"So what do we do?" Odile asked. "We didn't swim down a river and burn our whole lives down to watch this die in a filing cabinet."

"No," Corvane agreed. "You didn't." She drew a clean sheet toward her and began to write with sudden decision. "Here is what happens now. Tonight, I take sworn statements from both of you, and I certify copies of the full documentary record under federal seal—three sets. One goes into my safe. One goes by courier tonight to the Attorney General's office, addressed personally, bypassing the ministry liaison chain entirely. And the third—" she looked up at Emrys, "—stays with you. Because if this goes wrong, if Voss reaches into the machinery and stalls us the way he stalled your maps, the only copy that survives will be the one nobody knows the location of. Can you keep it hidden?"

"I hid it through a monsoon and a manhunt," Emrys said. "Yes."

"Good." She stamped the first page without reading it further—she had read everything already, he realized; four hours of testimony and she had absorbed it whole. "The inquiry will open. Formally, publicly, within the month. Voss will stall, delay, invoke procedure, possibly attempt to have me reassigned. He is good at all of it; he's been doing it for years, which is presumably why they put him on the board. But sworn testimony plus sealed originals plus a federal auditor willing to go on record—" a thin, sharp smile crossed her face, the first one, "—is a different animal than a cartographer shouting in the wilderness. They'll have to answer. Eventually. In the open."

* * *

They left the building at dusk.

The colonnade caught the last light and threw it back gold, and the steps were crowded now with clerks going home, and somewhere below a tram bell rang, and Emrys stood at the top of the stairs feeling lighter and heavier at once—the satchel still on his shoulder, but changed. Inside it now were certified copies under federal seal, and the weight of them was different from the weight of secrets. Secrets corrode. Testimony holds its edge.

Odile stood beside him with her arms folded, watching the traffic, and he could feel her thinking the way you feel weather coming.

"Voss," she said at last. "All this time. Three years of counting gallons. Of landing on flooded strips because a full landing cost less than a missed delivery. Of—" she stopped, jaw tight. "He sat in a leather chair in a boardroom signing off on a dam that drowned a valley, and he also sat across a desk from me making me count coins, and it was the same war the whole time. I just couldn't see the front line."

"He needed you not to see it."

"Well." She unfolded her arms. "He miscalculated. He thought owning my plane meant owning me." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground a smile would build on. "He's about to learn what I do with things people try to own."

Below them, the tram doors opened and released a crowd into the blue evening. Emrys watched them flow and scatter, ordinary people going home to ordinary dinners, unaware that somewhere above them a federal inquiry was assembling itself like a storm front. He thought of the drowned village sliding past under the boat's keel. He thought of the names he would have to say aloud, under oath, in a room with microphones. He thought of Roque still out there somewhere, and of the third copy of the evidence riding against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Corvane said within the month," he said. "Which means Voss knows within the week. Possibly sooner—an inquiry this size leaks before it opens."

"Then we'd better decide what we do with the week," Odile said.

As if the city had been waiting for the words, a boy detached himself from the tram-stop crowd and came up the steps two at a time—a messenger, government-grey uniform, telegram envelope in hand. He glanced at a slip of paper, then at them, and stopped.

"Mr. Vale? Miss Blackwood?" At Odile's nod, he held out the envelope. "From the Commission liaison office. Marked urgent—came in an hour ago, they said deliver personal."

Emrys took it. Broke the seal. Read it once, and felt the gold light on the colonnade go suddenly cold.

He handed it to Odile without a word.

She read it aloud, quietly, just for the two of them:

*BE ADVISED. HARROW VOSS HAS FILED MOTION WITH OVERSIGHT BOARD THIS AFTERNOON CHALLENGING JURISDICTION OF SPECIAL INQUIRIES RE: KESTREL MATTER. HEARING SCHEDULED IN THREE DAYS. VOSS WILL APPEAR IN PERSON TO ARGUE.*

Odile lowered the page. Above them the portraits of dead directors gazed down the stairwell with their patient, disappointed eyes, and Emrys understood that the door they had fought so hard to reach had opened onto another door, and behind that one, in three days' time, Harrow Voss would be waiting—with the full weight of the ministry, the board, and three years of purchased silence arranged behind his chair.

"Three days," Odile said. She folded the telegram along its creases, precise as a woman folding a chart. "Then we'd better get our story ready. Because I'm done being a line item in that man's ledger."
