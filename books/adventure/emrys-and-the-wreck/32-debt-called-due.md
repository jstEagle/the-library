## 32. Debt Called Due

The summons came on paper, which was how Odile knew it was serious. In the capital, everything moved by wire and wireless—flight clearances, fuel allocations, the endless bureaucratic weather of forms and stamps. Paper meant someone wanted a thing held in the hand, weighed, signed. Paper meant a trap with a lid.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, embossed with the crest of the Ministry of Waterways and Power, and it was delivered to her room at the Airmen's Rest not by a courier but by a man in a gray suit who waited in the corridor while she read it, as if her answer might be needed immediately, as if the building itself might need reassuring.

*Mr. Harrow Voss requests the pleasure of your company at his residence, Thursday evening, seven o'clock.*

There was no pleasure in it. There never had been.

Emrys turned the card over twice before handing it back, his thumb tracing the embossed crest like a man reading braille for danger. They had been in the capital nine days now, long enough for the wet season to settle over the city in earnest—a soft, persistent rain that silvered the streets and made the government quarter gleam like something drowned and polished. Long enough for the inquiry to have opened its doors and begun its slow, grinding work. Long enough for Odile to learn what it felt like to walk into a hearing room with forty faces turning toward her at once, and to keep walking anyway.

"He knows," Emrys said quietly. "About the testimony."

"Everyone knows about the testimony. It was broadcast." She set the card on the narrow table between them, next to the teapot and the maps and the evidence case that never left their sight anymore, that slept between them in hotel rooms like a third guest. "He wants something."

"He always wants something."

"Yes." She looked out the window at the rain. "But this time he's asking me to dinner instead of sending men to take my plane. That means he thinks I can be bought."

Emrys said nothing for a moment. When she glanced back at him, he was watching her with that stillness of his—the cartographer's patience, the man who had learned to wait out storms and superiors both. "And can you?"

It wasn't an insult. It was a real question, asked the way he asked all his real questions: without flinching, because he knew flinching was how lies got comfortable.

"I don't know," she said. And it cost her something to say it, but lying to him had become harder than telling the truth, somewhere in the drowned valley, and she wasn't going to unlearn that now. "That's why I'm going."

* * *

Voss's house stood on a hill above the river district, one of those tall shuttered places built by sugar money and defended by hedges. The rain had stopped by evening; the city steamed gently under gaslight, and from the terrace where a servant left her, Odile could see the harbor, the masts, the far smudge of the airfield where her plane sat under canvas with a ministry lien on its tail number.

Harrow Voss came out to meet her himself. That surprised her. In all the years since her father's death, she had met Voss perhaps a dozen times—in the back office of the trading post, once at a funeral, twice across a desk while he counted figures aloud so she could hear exactly how far behind she was—and he had never once come around a desk to greet anyone. He was a smaller man than she remembered, or she had grown larger. His hair had gone from iron to ash. But the hands were the same: soft, precise, the hands of a man who had never hauled anything heavier than a pen and could still make you feel them around your throat.

"Miss Blackwood." He took her hand briefly, dry as ledger paper. "You'll forgive the informality. The ministry has too many ears this month. Even my own."

"You're a board member. Aren't the ears yours?"

A thin smile. "Come. Eat first. Negotiations go better when no one is hungry—it makes people agreeable, and I'd rather you were sharp."

Dinner was served in a room walled floor to ceiling with books, though Odile noticed, as the courses came and went, that none of the spines were cracked. Decorative knowledge. Voss talked easily through the soup and the fish—about the weather, about the scandal of the new airfield paving contract, about her father, whom he named three times with the easy intimacy of a man handling a familiar tool. *Your father was the most careful debtor I ever carried. He hated owing. He paid early when he could. You're very like him.*

She let him talk. She ate little. Under the table, her boot rested against the leg of her chair, and she kept her weight forward, the way she did in turbulence—not braced against the drop, but ready for it.

Only when the plates were cleared and the servant had gone, only when Voss had poured two fingers of brandy into glasses older than both of them, did he lean back and say, pleasantly:

"Now. Let's talk about the note."

* * *

"The note," Odile repeated.

"Your father's note. Fourteen years accruing interest, refinanced twice, secured against the aircraft and—since your recent difficulties—against whatever salvage value the ministry might assign to a pilot's license under suspension review." He said it the way a man reads coordinates off a chart: flatly, precisely, each figure landing where it was aimed. "You've seen the numbers. You know they've passed any point a bush pilot's income could reach in two lifetimes. I want to be honest with you, Miss Blackwood, because honesty is rare this season and I find I miss it."

"Go on."

"The audit is going to be buried." He swirled his brandy, unhurried. "Not stopped—buried. There's a difference. Stopped things leave wounds, questions, martyrs. Buried things leave paperwork. The dam will be condemned on technical grounds—seepage rates, foundation spalling, ordinary engineering failure. No sabotage. No redlined maps. No Chief Roque cutting a survey team loose in a flood. And no testimony from a supply pilot whose logs show she entered a restricted zone without authorization, carrying a fugitive cartographer, and destroyed ministry property during her escape from the lower station."

"I didn't destroy anything. Your security chief burned the station."

"Chief Roque's report says otherwise." Voss smiled again, faintly. "You see how it works? Two stories, and mine has more signatures on it."

Odile set down her glass. Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a strange detachment, the way she noticed instruments holding true in bad air. "You didn't invite me here to threaten me. If you wanted me silenced, there are cheaper ways. You invited me to buy me. So name the price."

For the first time, something moved behind Voss's eyes—something that might have been respect, or appetite. He reached into his jacket and laid a folder on the table between them, square and thick, tied with the flat ribbon the land registry used.

"Here is your father's note," he said. "Original signature. All fourteen years of it. And here—" he drew a second document from the same folder, already drafted, already stamped, waiting only for a second signature, "—is its discharge. Paid in full. Not forgiven—paid. The distinction matters legally, and it will matter to you. Forgiven debts follow a woman around. Paid debts are simply over. The lien on your aircraft dissolves. The tail number clears. The plane is yours, Miss Blackwood—outright, unencumbered, the first thing you will ever have owned free and clear. And your license review closes with a letter of commendation for your assistance in the recovery operation at Kestrel Reach."

The word *recovery* hung in the air like smoke.

"And in exchange," Odile said.

"In exchange, you withdraw your testimony. You recant. You state—under oath, if necessary—that you found Mr. Vale wandering the flood zone, that you sheltered him out of common decency, that you saw no documents, heard no confessions, and that the papers he carries were never shown to you. You go back to flying medicine and mail. You keep your head down, the way you've been keeping it down for fourteen years, the way your father kept it down before you." He folded his hands. "You fly home safe."

* * *

She should have felt tempted. That was the terrible part, the part she would turn over later, alone, in the dark of her rented room: how much of her *wanted* it.

Because he had named it exactly. *Fly home safe.* Fourteen years she had flown with the debt riding shotgun, every gallon of fuel measured against it, every job taken and every job refused, every flooded strip and every storm cell a wager against the day the note came due. She had inherited it at nineteen along with her father's compass and his silence about how he'd gotten into so deep, and she had worn it like a second spine. And now here it was on a table in a book-lined room, offered up whole, discharged, *paid*, with her father's signature on it and her freedom underneath—and all it would cost was her voice.

Her voice. The one thing she had left that was hers.

"Tell me about my father," she said.

Voss raised his eyebrows, genuinely surprised. "What is there to tell?"

"You invoked his signature twice in five minutes. You talk about him like you knew him better than I did. So tell me. How did a careful man—a man who paid early, who hated owing—end up fourteen years in the hole to you?"

Something flickered across the collector's face then, quick as a bird's shadow. He recovered it fast, but she had spent fourteen years reading men across desks, and she caught it: not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man whose arithmetic has just been checked.

"Your father borrowed against the aircraft to cover a bad season," Voss said smoothly. "Then another. It happens."

"It doesn't happen to careful men. You said so yourself." She leaned forward. "Did he borrow it for himself, Mr. Voss? Or did someone come to him—someone with ministry letterhead—and ask him to fly something into Kestrel Reach, years ago, before the dam, before any of this? Something he shouldn't have carried? Someone he couldn't refuse?"

The silence lasted four seconds. She counted them the way she counted seconds between lightning and thunder.

"You've been talking to Vale," Voss said at last, and his voice had lost its pleasantness entirely. "He's been filling your head with—"

"My father's been dead fourteen years. Emrys never met him." She stood. Her chair made no sound on the rug. "You did. And whatever he flew for you, whatever he saw or carried or knew—that's why the note never shrank, isn't it? That's why fourteen years of payments bought nothing. It was never a debt, Mr. Voss. It was a leash. You kept my father leashed, and when he died you put it on me, because a pilot who owes you everything will fly anything and ask nothing."

Voss rose too, slowly, and for a moment the two of them stood across the table with the discharge papers lying between them like a body.

"You're guessing," he said quietly. "Guessing is not testimony. Guessing will not survive cross-examination. Whereas my documents—" he touched the folder, almost tenderly, "—are very durable. So consider carefully, Miss Blackwood. This offer expires when the audit does. Sign, and tomorrow morning you own your plane, your license, your future. Refuse, and you own nothing but a story nobody official will ever confirm—and a debt that will outlive you, the way it outlived him."

Odile looked at the folder. At her father's signature, faded brown at the edge of the page, the letters slanted forward the way his handwriting always leaned into things, as if even his name were trying to move faster than his life allowed.

She thought of him teaching her to hold a compass level, his big cracked hands cupping hers. *The needle doesn't point where you want to go,* he'd said. *It points north anyway. That's the whole trick of it. You don't steer the needle. You steer yourself.*

She thought of the drowned village sliding past beneath the boat, rooftops under green water. Of Emrys's face in the firelight on the shingle bar, saying *I ran because staying meant becoming them*. Of Marta's seaplane rising off the salt line with bullets stitching the water, and every pilot on the river who had jammed Roque's boats with false sightings, who had owed her favors she never knew she'd earned.

Belonging was never something she had to buy. She had learned that in the worst water of her life. And this man, this soft-handed man with his decorative books, was offering to sell it back to her at the price of her own voice.

"No," Odile said.

Voss exhaled through his nose. "Miss Blackwood—"

"You'll get your answer formally, in front of the auditor, where it counts." She picked up her gloves from the chair. "But you should hear it tonight, from me, standing in your house, so you can't spend the next week pretending there's ambiguity. I won't recant. I won't sign. And Mr. Voss—" she paused at the door, and found she was smiling, and that the smile felt like clearing a ridge into open air, "—keep my father's note somewhere safe until Thursday. I'm going to want it."

"Whatever for?"

"So I can burn it myself."

She walked out into the steam-washed night, down the hill toward the harbor lights, and behind her the house windows glowed warm and yellow, and somewhere inside them Harrow Voss was reaching for his telephone.
