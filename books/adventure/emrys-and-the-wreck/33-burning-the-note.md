## 33. Burning the Note

The ministry building smelled of floor wax and old paper, and Odile hated it the moment she crossed the threshold.

She had dressed for the occasion the way she dressed for everything—practically. The same canvas jacket she flew in, brushed clean of river mud. Her hair pulled back. Her father's compass in her jacket pocket, because she had carried it every day since the reach and could not imagine walking into a room full of men in suits without it. Emrys walked beside her in the borrowed shirt Marta had found him, his hands quiet at his sides, his face arranged into the careful blankness he wore like a second skin. The federal auditor's office was on the fourth floor. Voss's office, as it happened, was on the third.

They had not planned to stop. Voss's secretary stopped them for them—a small woman with a telephone pressed to her ear and an expression of professional apology, who said, *Mr. Voss asks that Ms. Blackwood step in for just a moment, he's been so anxious to congratulate her on her safe return,* and before Odile could say that she had no interest in Harrow Voss's congratulations, the door opened and there he was, filling the frame of it, smiling the way a trap smiles.

"Odile." He said her name like a man handling something fragile that belonged to him. "Come in. Bring your friend. We have a great deal to celebrate."

Everything in her said walk away. The auditor was four floors up; Emrys's testimony was scheduled; the machine of the inquiry had begun to grind, slow and rusted but grinding. She did not need this room. She did not need this man.

But she thought of the lien. She thought of her plane sitting on the government apron with Voss's paper clipped to its registration like a hook through the gill. She thought of her father's signature on a note she had never signed, a debt she had inherited the way you inherit a bad knee or a bad river—without consent, without appeal, just the geography of your life rearranged around it.

And she thought: *I want to hear him say it.*

"Five minutes," she said, and stepped inside.

* * *

Voss's office was everything her father's had never been. Her father had worked out of a corrugated shed at the airfield, his ledgers kept in pencil, his debts and credits recorded in a hand that slanted left and pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. Voss's ledgers were leather-bound and gold-tooled, arranged on shelves behind his desk like trophies. His window looked out over the capital's rooftops, the river a brown ribbon in the distance, the whole wet green interior of the country reduced to a haze on the horizon—the place where Odile lived, rendered here as weather.

"Sit," Voss said. "Whiskey? It's early, I know, but the occasion—"

"I don't drink with creditors."

Something flickered behind his eyes—amusement, she thought, or its close cousin. He sat down behind the desk and folded his hands, and Odile was struck again by how ordinary he was. Not a monster. Not a villain from one of her own novels, all scarred knuckles and cold eyes. Just a heavyset man in a good suit with a soft chin and a wedding ring, the kind of man who signed things. Who had signed things. Who had sat on the ministry board that approved the Kestrel Reach dam on foundations his own survey's cartographer had redlined in ink, and who had then, when the valley drowned, arranged the paperwork so that the drowning had no author.

She knew that now. Emrys had told her everything on the shingle bar and again in the auditor's anteroom, and the auditor's investigators had spent two days with the evidence—the redlined maps, the correspondence, the maintenance logs Roque had tried to burn. Voss's name appeared in the margins of enough documents that his only play left was this one. She understood that. She had spent her whole life around men making their last plays.

"You're angry with me," Voss said, in the tone of a man observing the weather. "That's understandable. The lien on your aircraft was a business decision, Odile, not a personal one. Your father understood that about me. He and I did business for nineteen years."

"My father is dead."

"Because of which, the note has passed to you. As I'm sure your counsel explained." He opened a drawer—slowly, deliberately, a man who liked to be watched doing things—and took out a folder. Leather. The same gold tooling as the ones on his shelf. He laid it on the desk between them and rested his hand flat upon it, and Odile felt the room tilt slightly, the way the runway had tilted the day the strip flooded, that moment when the ground you trusted reveals itself as water.

"Here is what I'm offering," Voss said. "And I want you to hear all of it before you decide anything, because I think you're a woman who decides things carefully. Your father was careful too."

"My father drowned with a full fuel load he couldn't pay for because you kept extending him credit he couldn't refuse."

Voss's smile thinned but held. "The offer. Tomorrow, when you appear before the inquiry, you will testify to what you personally witnessed. The crash. The flood. The storm. You will say that a man named Emrys Vale told you he had been a member of the survey team, and that you have no knowledge of any documents, any maps, any correspondence, any—what did the auditor call them—*irregularities*. You were a supply pilot. You flew where you were told. You survived. That is all you know."

"And in exchange?"

Voss lifted his hand from the folder and slid it across the desk. It sat there between them, and Odile did not touch it.

"In exchange," Voss said, "the note is void. Not paid—void. As if it never existed. The lien on your aircraft dissolves. The aircraft is yours outright, free and clear, the first thing anyone in your family has ever owned outright, I believe." He leaned back. "And there is a standing contract, if you want it. Ministry charter work. Regular runs, guaranteed fuel, guaranteed rates. No more counting gallons against a ledger. No more wet-season landings on strips you know will flood because the charter pays double. Your father spent nineteen years trying to buy his way out of the interior, Odile. I'm offering you the exit he never found."

The room was very quiet. The river glittered in the window. Somewhere below, a tram bell rang, and Odile thought absurdly of the bell on the trading post counter, the one Harrow Voss had rung with his knuckle while she counted out payments, always short, always behind, always *next season, Voss, I swear it.*

"You've done the arithmetic," she said.

"I've done the arithmetic."

"Then you know what my father's debt was worth. To the cent. You've known for years. You could have forgiven it any time you liked. You kept it alive because a pilot who owes you is a pilot you can send anywhere. Into the reach, for instance."

Voss said nothing, which was its own kind of signature.

"They didn't charter me because I was available," she said. "They chartered me because you leaned on the ministry, and the ministry leaned on the survey office, and the survey office needed a pilot nobody would miss. A pilot with a debt. A pilot who couldn't say no." Her voice stayed level. She was almost proud of that. "You didn't just own my father's paper, Voss. You used me as your errand girl, and then you put a lien on my plane while I was floating down a river getting shot at by your security chief."

"Roque acted without authorization—"

"Roque acted exactly as authorized. That's what the documents say. I've read them. I've read them so many times I could recite them." She looked at the folder. It was a handsome object. It probably weighed less than the compass in her pocket. "So here's my arithmetic. You're offering me my whole life back. The plane, the debt, the future. Everything I have wanted since I was nineteen years old and signing my name under his."

"Yes," Voss said simply. "That's the offer."

"And all it costs is that the drowned valley stays drowned with nobody's name on it. The villages. The sixty-one people the survey lost. Emrys's maps. All of it stays a weather event. An act of God." She shook her head slowly. "You know what the worst part is, Voss? Six months ago I'd have taken it. Six months ago I'd have signed anything you put in front of me and thanked you for the pen."

* * *

She stood, and Emrys, who had not spoken, who had leaned against the wall by the door the entire time with the stillness of a man who had learned that stillness was armor, straightened.

"Odile," Voss said, and for the first time something real moved in his voice—not warmth, but the flat alarm of a man watching capital walk out of the room. "Think about what you're doing. The inquiry is a committee. Committees are weather. In a year, no one will remember Kestrel Reach. But the note is a document, and documents endure. Your father signed it. His signature binds you. That's how the world works, and no amount of testimony changes it."

Odile stopped at the desk.

"You're right," she said. "That is how the world works. That's how it's *always* worked. My father spent his whole life paying for a signature, and I spent mine paying for his, and you sat behind this desk the whole time collecting the interest." She took the folder. "You know, he kept a compass. Did you know that? Brass case, worn through on one side from his pocket. He'd check it out of habit, even parked, even tied down. I used to ask him why, and he said a man should always know which way out is." She opened the folder. The note lay inside—her father's slanting left-handed script, the embossed pressure of the pen still visible in the paper after all these years, the sum at the bottom that had eaten nineteen years of a man's life and eleven more of his daughter's. "I checked it this morning. Out of habit. And you know what? I already knew which way out was. I just hadn't paid for the nerve yet."

"Odile—" Emrys said softly, a warning or a question.

She reached into her jacket pocket and took out the brass lighter. Her father's. Another inheritance—she had more of them than she'd ever catalogued, and she was only now, standing in this wax-smelling room above the brown river, beginning to understand what inheritance actually meant. Not the debt. Not the compass. The nerve.

"Voss," she said, "I'm going to testify tomorrow. I'm going to tell them everything I saw, everything I was told, everything I read. I'm going to tell them about the lien and the charter and the flooded strip and the man in the reeds. And when they ask me if anyone offered me anything to keep quiet, I'm going to tell them about this room, and this desk, and this folder, and the exact terms of your generous offer—though I expect by then they'll already have your correspondence, so it'll be more of a formality."

"You'd be a fool," Voss said. "You'd be throwing away—"

"I know exactly what I'm throwing away." And she did, that was the thing, that was the whole terrible clarity of it: the guaranteed fuel, the regular runs, the plane free and clear, the exit her father never found, all of it going up, and she had never in her life wanted anything less.

She flicked the lighter.

The folder caught on the second try—the leather was stubborn, the way good leather is—and then the flame found the note inside and the paper went up with a soft rushing sound, and Odile held the burning folder over Voss's inkwell and watched her father's signature curl and blacken and come apart into ash. Nineteen years of her father's life. Eleven of hers. The flooded strip. The counted gallons. The bell on the trading post counter. All of it burning with a smell like a campfire, ordinary as that, small as that, and she felt the strangest thing in her chest, which was not triumph and not relief but something quieter and more permanent, like a load shifting forward on the cargo deck and the plane finally flying level.

"You'll regret this," Voss said. His voice had gone thin. He was on his feet now, one hand half-extended toward the fire as if he might snatch the debt out of the air, save it, salvage the paper if not the power. "You have no idea what you've—"

"I have every idea." She dropped the last burning corner into the inkwell, where it hissed and died. "That's the difference between us, Voss. You think everyone has a price because you've never met anyone who already knew theirs."

Emrys opened the door for her. She walked through it with the lighter in her pocket and the smell of smoke in her jacket and her father's compass against her ribs, and behind her she heard Voss lift the telephone receiver, and she did not turn around, because she already knew who he was calling, and she already knew it was too late.

* * *

The auditor's office received them without ceremony, which Odile found she preferred. A woman with grey hair and reading glasses asked Emrys to confirm the chain of custody on the evidence, and he did, in his careful voice, and then the woman looked at Odile and said, "And you're the pilot," in a tone that was not a question, and Odile said, "I'm the pilot," and that was the whole of her introduction to the machinery of justice.

It was late when they came out. The tram had stopped running; the river had gone dark; the capital's lights lay on the water in long smearing columns. Emrys walked her down toward the airfield district in silence, and it was a good silence, the kind they had learned on the leaking boat, the kind that held weight without asking anything.

"He'll retaliate," Emrys said finally. "Voss. Tonight. Whatever he can still reach."

"I know."

"The hearing is tomorrow at nine. He'll spend tonight trying to have it delayed, sealed, moved, dissolved. He has friends."

"I know that too." She stopped at the corner where their ways divided—his lodging above the chandlery, hers at Marta's hangar—and looked up at the sky, where the wet season's first stars were burning through the cloud in ragged holes. "Emrys. What he offered me was everything. You understand that. The plane. Free. My whole life, the one I've been paying for since I was a kid."

"I know," he said. And then, carefully, the way he said everything: "Why didn't you take it?"

Odile thought about the question. She thought about her father checking the compass out of habit, always knowing which way out was, never once taking it. She thought about the drowned village sliding past the boat, rooftops under green water. She thought about Marta jamming Roque's boats with false sightings, pilots she'd never properly thanked, a community she'd never let herself believe in because believing cost more than owing.

"Because he was right about one thing," she said. "Documents endure. So I'm going to spend the rest of my life making sure the enduring ones are the true ones." She held out her hand, palm up, and in it lay a small twist of ash and charred leather she had fished from the inkwell when Voss wasn't looking—her father's signature, or what was left of it. "Come on. We've got a hearing at nine, and I intend to be early. It's the first appointment I've ever kept that I chose myself."

They walked toward the river together, and behind them, high up in the ministry building, a light came on in a third-floor office and did not go out again all night.
