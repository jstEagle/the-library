## 34. Hearing in the Wet Season

The inquiry chamber smelled of wet wool and old varnish, the way government buildings in the capital always did during the rainy months—water seeping up through the foundations of everything, even here, even where men wore pressed suits and spoke of accountability as though it were a mineral they'd mined themselves.

Emrys Vale sat at the witness table with his hands flat on the scarred wood and tried to remember that he was not on trial. That was the phrase the auditor's counsel had used twice in their preparation sessions: *You are not the accused, Mr. Vale. You are the map.* He had nodded both times. He understood what she meant and understood also that it was not true. In rooms like this one, maps became accused all the time. Maps got redlined. Maps got filed under seal. Maps got their authors quietly retired to the interior with a pension and a warning.

He looked at his hands. They were steady. That surprised him. For eleven days since the salt line—since the seaplane, the gunfire stitching the water, Odile's hands white-knuckled on the yoke while he held the evidence case against his chest like an infant—he had expected to come apart at some predictable seam. He hadn't. He suspected it had less to do with courage than with exhaustion so complete it had burned past fear into something flat and clear, like the light after a storm front passes.

Odile sat two rows behind him. He could feel her there without turning—the way you feel a runway under your wheels even when your eyes are on the instruments. She wore the only clean shirt she owned and her father's compass on a cord around her neck, the decoy having been returned to its case after the weirs and the mangroves and the dogs. She had refused, flatly, to leave it in the hotel safe. *It's been shot at,* she'd said. *It stays with me.* He had not argued. There were things a person carried that no clerk should be allowed to inventory.

The presiding officer was Auditor-General Corvane, a narrow woman with gray-threaded hair pulled back so tightly it seemed to be holding her face in place. She had opened the proceedings at nine sharp by reading the mandate aloud: an inquiry into the Kestrel Reach dam failure, the loss of the government survey team, and "irregularities in the approval chain of the hydrological survey." Eleven words for nine dead people. Emrys counted them while she read, and hated himself for counting, and counted anyway. It was what cartographers did. They measured.

"Mr. Vale," Corvane said, "you were employed by the Ministry of Water and Reclamation as senior cartographer on the Kestrel Reach survey. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"You have provided this inquiry with certain documents."

"I have." The evidence case sat on the counsel table between him and the auditor's lawyer, a steel box with a government seal across the latch that had been applied in front of three witnesses. Inside were the original field maps, his own hand on every contour line; the foundation reports from the bore teams; and, most damning, the review copies—the ones that came back down from the ministry with red ink over his warnings like wounds drawn onto paper.

"Describe for the panel," Corvane said, "what these documents are."

So he did. He talked for forty minutes without notes, because he had carried those maps in his head through flooded reeds and mangrove swamps and a leaking boat on black water, and there was no version of them he didn't know by heart. He described the bore samples from the north abutment—clay seams saturated past tolerance, shear values half of what the design assumed. He described how he'd drafted the instability onto the master map in solid red hatching, standard notation, unambiguous. He described the review cycle: the map going up to the regional office in March, coming back in April with the hatching removed and a marginal note in a ministry director's hand—*reclassify per geotech addendum*—an addendum that did not exist in any file the inquiry had been able to locate.

"The red ink," Corvane said. "Whose hand?"

"We believe Director Sellen's. The inquiry can compare it to his correspondence. I never met him face to face. I knew his pen."

A murmur moved through the gallery. Emrys kept his eyes forward. Behind him, Odile had gone very still, the way she went still in turbulence—not afraid, exactly, but braced, conserving herself.

* * *

They broke at midday, and the rain came down on the capital like a verdict of its own, drumming on the colonnade roof hard enough to make conversation impossible on the steps. Emrys stood under the eave with a cup of terrible institutional coffee and watched Odile do what she always did in unfamiliar places: find the exits, count the vehicles, note which faces appeared twice.

"You did well in there," she said.

"I recited maps."

"You told nine people's story to a room full of men who wanted it to be about paperwork instead. That's not nothing." She blew steam off her own cup. "Sellen's counsel is going to come apart this afternoon. Did you see his face when you said *I knew his pen*?"

"I wasn't watching him."

"No," she said, and something in her voice made him glance over. She was looking at the rain, but he thought she'd been looking at him. "You weren't."

The afternoon session belonged to the ministry's counsel, a soft-bellied man named Pell who had the gift of making cruelty sound like procedure. He took Emrys through the redlined maps page by page, and his method was simple: suggest that every act of documentation had been an act of ambition.

"You flagged the north abutment as unstable," Pell said. "Yet the ministry's independent reviewer rated it marginal-acceptable. Two professionals, same data, different conclusions. Why should the panel prefer yours?"

"Because my conclusion was written before the money moved. His was written after."

Pell smiled thinly. "You're suggesting corruption."

"I'm suggesting sequence. Sequence is my trade. I draw what happened, in the order it happened. What happened first was the red hatching. What happened second was the ink that erased it. If the panel wants to call that corruption, I won't argue."

Corvane's gavel came down before Pell could respond, though whether to protect the witness or the ministry's dignity, Emrys couldn't tell. Procedure ground forward. A clerk read bore logs into the record. A hydrologist testified about rainfall anomalies in the weeks before the failure—rainfall that had been real, but not extraordinary; rainfall the dam should have shrugged off. The numbers did their slow work. You could feel the room's weight shifting, degree by degree, the way a boat settles when water finds a seam.

But Voss's name did not come up. Not once. Emrys noticed it the way he'd notice a missing contour on a chart—a blank where terrain should be. Harrow Voss, debt-holder, lien-taker, board member of the very ministry that had signed off the dam, sat in the second row of the gallery in a gray suit, taking no notes, wearing the expression of a man waiting out weather he considered beneath his attention. And no one said his name.

At the end of the session, Corvane announced that testimony would resume in three days' time, after the panel reviewed the ministry's production of internal correspondence—"production to be verified for completeness," she added, in a tone that suggested she already doubted it.

In the corridor afterward, Odile fell into step beside him. Marta was there too, the rival pilot turned unlikely ally, still smelling faintly of avgas no matter how many times she laundered her good coat. The three of them walked out into the rain-smell of the evening.

"They skipped him," Odile said. No preamble; she'd been thinking it through the whole corridor. "Voss signed the funding authorization. Voss's bank held the construction bonds. Voss sits on the ministry board that received Sellen's review. And today he was furniture."

"He'll surface," Marta said. "Men like that float. It's the rest of us who sink."

Emrys said nothing. He was thinking about completeness—about what a man like Voss would consider incomplete, and what he might do about it while the inquiry waited out its three days.

* * *

The three days passed the way wet-season days pass in the capital: slowly, loudly, with water running in the streets and everyone pretending this was normal. Emrys spent them in a boarding house near the river with Odile in the next room, and they worked like two people bailing a boat—her on the phone lines to every pilot and settlement clerk who owed her or owed Marta or simply owed the truth, him re-drawing the survey maps from memory onto fresh sheets, annotating each with dates and initials, building a parallel record in case the official one developed holes.

It was Odile who found the first hole. She came into his room on the second evening with a printout and dropped it on his drafting table.

"Ministry production list. Everything they've given the inquiry. Look at item forty-one."

He looked. *Internal memorandum, Director Sellen to Board Liaison Office, dated 14 April.* One page. He remembered the original—or remembered its existence, its routing stamp, the way you remember a landmark you only saw once from the air. The original had run four pages. He knew because he'd seen the courier log during the survey; he'd been the one who'd logged the document transfer, back when he was still a man who trusted logs.

"Four pages became one," he said.

"One page that happens to say nothing incriminating." Odile sat on the edge of his cot, elbows on her knees, and for a moment the competence slipped and he saw underneath it: a tired woman in a strange city, far from her river, doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Voss isn't stalling, Emrys. Stalling is passive. This is editing. Someone's deciding what the record gets to contain, and they've got access to the production chain."

"Which means someone inside the inquiry's own support staff."

"Or close enough to touch it." She rubbed her eyes. "I put Marta on it. She knows a records clerk at the annex who owes her a favor from a medevac run two winters ago. If pages are going missing, maybe the clerk can tell us which direction they went."

He looked at her then—at this woman who had crashed into his valley wanting nothing except to pay off a dead man's debt and disappear again, and who had instead chosen, over and over, at every fork in the drowned forest, to stay. He wanted to say something true about it. What came out was:

"When this is over, I'm going to owe you more than I know how to carry."

She gave him the look she gave bad landings—half assessment, half forgiveness. "You don't owe me anything. That's rather the point of me now." Then she stood, and the armor came back up, and she went to make another phone call, and he sat alone with his maps and the sound of rain and the growing certainty that the inquiry was a boat, and it was leaking, and the question was who held the pump.

* * *

On the third morning, everything changed twice.

The first change came at the opening of session, when Corvane rose and announced, in a voice sanded flat with anger, that the panel had conducted its own verification of the ministry's production against the courier archives of the original survey—and found discrepancies. Four documents, she said, existed in the archive index but not in the produced set. She did not name the office responsible. She did not need to. Every eye in the chamber traveled, with the smooth coordinated motion of a flock startled off water, to the row where the ministry's observers sat—and past them, inevitably, to Harrow Voss, whose expression did not change at all, which was itself a kind of confession, delivered in the only language he respected.

Then the witnesses resumed. A procurement officer from the regional office, sweating through his suit, testified that the geotechnical addendum cited in Sellen's marginal note had been commissioned, paid for, and—according to its contracted author—never delivered, because the findings would have been, in the author's words as quoted in a letter, "inconsistent with the project schedule." The chamber absorbed this the way the valley had absorbed the flood: silently, and with the sense that something structural had just given way somewhere below the waterline.

Roque's name surfaced mid-afternoon, and when it did, Emrys felt the room's temperature drop. Chief Ansel Roque, private security contractor, retained by the survey through a services agreement that traced upward through two shell companies to a holding entity whose registered director was—Counsel for the auditor read the name into the record with relish—Harrow Voss.

There it was. The blank contour filled. The chain, drawn at last in unbroken line: Sellen's pen erasing the hazard, the board approving the schedule, Voss's money underneath it all like bedrock, and Roque's security contract laid over the top like a lid. When the team found the sabotage cover-up in the flood's aftermath, it was Roque who had cut them loose in the rising water. Emrys knew because he had run. He had run with the evidence while the others—

He gave his testimony steadily. He had learned, in the swamp with the dogs, in the tower, in the burning boathouse, that steadiness was not the absence of terror but the decision to keep functioning inside it. He named the night. He named the water level. He named the last thing he heard from the camp before the flood took the radio: Roque's voice, calm as a shipping forecast, saying *the schedule is the schedule.*

Voss's counsel objected three times. Corvane overruled him twice and struck the third objection itself, noting for the record that objections were not a substitute for documents—which documents, she reminded the chamber, appeared to be developing a habit of absence.

When session closed, Voss stood, buttoned his gray coat, and left the gallery before the gavel finished falling. He passed within six feet of Emrys in the aisle. He did not look at him. But as he passed, he said, quietly, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather:

"Records burn, Mr. Vale. All kinds."

Then he was gone into the corridor crowd, and Emrys stood in the emptying chamber with Odile's hand finding his elbow, steady as a compass needle, and understood that the inquiry had just crossed from procedure into war.

That evening, a message reached them through Marta's records clerk, scrawled on the back of a filing requisition in a hand shaking harder than a clerk's should shake: the ministry's archive annex had received, that same afternoon, a destruction order—signed, sealed, routine-looking—for the remainder of the Kestrel Reach survey records. Physical originals. Bore cores. Field notebooks. Everything.

And the order designated, as the officer authorized to supervise the disposal:

Chief Ansel Roque.

Emrys read it twice. Outside, the rain kept falling on the capital, patient as water, and somewhere across the city a man who had already cut loose one team was being handed the keys to the evidence vault.

Odile read it over his shoulder. When he looked up, she was already reaching for the phone, and her eyes had gone to that flat, bright place he'd learned to recognize—the place she flew from.

"He's going to fly them out," she said. "And I know every strip he can use."
