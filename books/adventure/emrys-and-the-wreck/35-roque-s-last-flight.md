## 35. Roque's Last Flight

The order came at four in the morning, typed on ministry letterhead and delivered by a courier who wouldn't look him in the eye.

Ansel Roque read it twice under the desk lamp in his office above the river depot, the paper trembling slightly in his grip—not from fear, he told himself, but from the effort of keeping his hands still. *All remaining records pertaining to the Kestrel Reach survey are to be destroyed by incineration no later than 0600. No copies. No exceptions. Confirmation of compliance to be filed with the Office of the Ministry Liaison.*

No signature. Just the liaison's stamp, the same embossed seal that had authorized the survey, the dam, the flood, and every quiet thing that had followed it upriver like a stain following a current.

Roque set the paper down and looked out the window at the water. The wet season was breaking at last; the rain had thinned to a gray drizzle, and the flooded valley downstream was beginning, inch by inch, to give back what it had taken. Somewhere out there, a federal auditor was reading Emrys Vale's maps. Somewhere upriver, a pilot woman named Odile Blackwood was flying sorties for men in gray suits, testifying in rooms she'd never been trained to enter. The machinery he had spent eleven years keeping oiled and silent was coming apart in his hands, bolt by bolt, and the men who built it were now handing him a match and telling him to clean up.

*Destroy the records.*

He almost laughed. That was the whole story of his career in a single verb. Destroy. He had destroyed the survey team's extraction schedule the night the dam let go, stranding nine people in a valley that became a lake in ninety minutes. He had destroyed the radio logs that proved his own office had received Vale's warnings and buried them. He had destroyed a man named Teodorik—quiet, decent, a hydrologist who'd grown a conscience too late—by leaving him at the lower station with a dead radio and a lie about a boat coming back.

And now they wanted him to destroy paper. As if paper were the problem. As if the fire could reach far enough.

Roque poured himself two fingers of the cheap cane spirit he kept in the bottom drawer and drank it standing. Then he did what he had always done, what had made him valuable to men who preferred not to dirty their own hands: he made a calculation.

The calculation went like this. If he burned the records, he became nothing. A functionary with no leverage, no insurance, no shield—just the last man standing in a burning archive, holding the bag when the indictments came down. Vale was talking. The Blackwood woman was talking. The auditor had subpoena power and, more dangerously, ambition. Roque's name was in those files forty times, and every one of his superiors would swear on their mothers' graves that Chief Ansel Roque had acted alone, had exceeded his mandate, had been, in the ministry's careful phrase, *a contractor operating outside the chain of oversight.*

If he burned the records, he was the fall.

But if the records *disappeared*—if they vanished into the wet season, unaccounted for, unrecovered—then every man whose signature was in those files would spend the rest of his life wondering where they were. Wondering what Roque had. Wondering what it would cost to get them back, or to make sure they never surfaced. The files weren't evidence anymore. They were currency. And Ansel Roque had spent his whole life poor.

He looked at the order again. *No copies. No exceptions.*

"No exceptions," he said aloud, to the empty office, and his voice came out flat and cold and almost amused. "Understood."

* * *

The records lived in three places, and Roque spent the last hours of darkness visiting each.

The first was the depot's strongroom, a concrete box behind his own office with a door lock only he and the liaison were supposed to hold keys for. Inside were the original survey files: Vale's redlined foundation maps, the geotechnical reports that had been doctored before submission, the correspondence between the ministry and the dam consortium, and—Roque's own contribution—nine personnel folders for nine men who had gone into Kestrel Reach and not come out, each folder annotated in his hand with the disposition of their effects. He packed them into a waterproof document case, the kind used for river cargo manifests, and set it by the door.

The second place was the floatplane.

She was a converted survey amphibian, twin-engine, old but sound, moored at the depot's private dock under a canvas that had kept the rain off her for two years of quiet readiness. Roque had kept her fueled since the night the dam failed—not because he'd planned to run, not then, but because a man in his position learned to keep an exit warm the way other men kept a dog. He pulled the canvas off in the drizzle, checked the tanks by flashlight, and found her full. Of course she was full. He'd topped her off three days ago, when the first subpoena had landed, telling himself it was routine.

The third place was a steel strongbox bolted beneath the deck plates of the boathouse, and it was the one he almost didn't go for, because going for it meant passing the spot where he'd left Teodorik's effects. But he went. He always went. He pried the plates up with a crowbar, lifted out the box—inside it, the extraction orders he'd countermanded, the radio transcripts, and a single photograph of the survey team taken on their first day, nine sunburned men grinning in front of their tents with the valley green and whole behind them—and he put it in the case with the rest, and he did not let himself feel anything about it, because feeling was a luxury that had never once paid his bills.

By 0540 the depot was empty around him. The night clerk had been given three days' pay and told to visit his mother upriver. The boats were locked. The incinerator behind the depot—a squat iron drum the ministry had shipped in special, with its own fuel supply, its own permit—stood cold.

Roque stood in front of it for a long moment, the document case under his arm, the rain beading on his shoulders.

They had sent him an order to burn the truth at dawn, and they had trusted him to do it, because in eleven years he had never once disobeyed. That was the irony that finally made him smile, there in the gray light with the river sliding past behind him: they had built their whole architecture on his obedience, and the architecture was coming down anyway, and the only thing left worth saving from the collapse was the thing they'd ordered him to destroy.

He walked to the dock. He stowed the case in the floatplane's aft compartment, lashed it, and covered it with a cargo tarp. Then he climbed into the left seat, ran the engines, and taxied out onto the brown floodwater while the depot behind him sat dark and cold, its incinerator empty, its compliance report—filed by courier an hour earlier, forged in his own careful hand—already on its way to the ministry.

*Records destroyed by incineration, 0558. No copies. No exceptions.*

Let them believe it. Belief was the last gift he intended to give them.

The floatplane lifted off the water in a long hiss of spray, climbed through the drizzle, and turned south—not toward the coast, where the auditor waited and the airspace was thick with federal eyes, but upriver. Toward the interior. Toward a border crossing the maps in his case described better than any government chart, a stretch of the highlands where jurisdiction ended and a man with a waterproof case and a full tank could simply *stop existing.*

Roque flew into the rising sun with nine dead men's files at his back and felt, for the first time in eleven years, something that was almost like lightness.

He did not think of it as flight. He thought of it as collection day.

* * *

They found him on the depot's books within three hours, because Roque had made one mistake, and the mistake was fuel.

He'd filed a false compliance report, but he'd also drawn aviation fuel against a ministry chit, and the chit crossed a clerk's desk in the capital who had spent the last two weeks discovering, to his own astonishment, that he had a conscience. By noon the auditor's office knew the records had not been burned. By one o'clock they knew the floatplane's heading, roughly, from a ranger relay that had caught a transponder squawk. And by two, they knew what Roque was carrying, and what it was worth, and they understood—with the particular panic of men who had just realized the fire was in their own house—that if Ansel Roque crossed the highland border with that case, the inquiry would die of starvation in a room full of lawyers.

The problem was aircraft. The auditor had boats, radios, and warrants. What he did not have, on four hours' notice, was a single pilot willing to fly into the interior after a man who had already demonstrated he'd rather kill than be caught.

That was when a young staffer—sweating, earnest, holding a list of licensed operators in the river districts—said the name.

"Blackwood. Odile Blackwood. She knows the reach better than anyone. She's been cooperating with the inquiry. And sir—she has a *reason* to want him stopped. Her debt holder. Voss. Voss is on the ministry board. If Roque gets out with those files, Voss walks, and everything she burned her own life down for—"

"I know who Odile Blackwood is," the auditor said. "Get her on the wire."

* * *

Odile was on the wire in twenty minutes, and Roque would learn later—much later, in a cell, in the flat light of a hearing room—exactly what was said to her. He would learn that she'd been in the air already, running medicine up to a settlement called Two Rivers, when the relay patched a priority call through her headset. He would learn that the auditor's man had been careful, formal, almost apologetic: *We're asking you to volunteer, Ms. Blackwood. No orders. We have no authority to order you. But you're the only pilot in the district who knows that canyon, and Roque has a four-hour head start.*

He would learn that she had been silent for eleven seconds on an open channel, and that the relay operator had checked his equipment twice, thinking the line had dropped.

And then he would learn what she said, because the transcript of that transmission would be read into the record at his hearing, and he would sit there and listen to it in a voice that never wavered:

*"Tell him I'll take the intercept. And tell him this—Roque's flying a loaded amphibian through the Kestrel gorge, and he doesn't know that gorge like I do. Nobody does. My father surveyed half those ridgelines before the ministry drowned them. Tell your auditor he just hired the one pilot in this country who's been flying her whole life to get something back that was taken from her. I'm not volunteering. I'm collecting."*

Roque, in his cell, hearing that transcript read aloud, would close his eyes and see the gorge ahead of him—narrowing, twisting, the walls rising sheer on either side where the old river had cut through the highlands before the dam turned its mouth into a lake—and he would understand, with a cold that had nothing to do with the concrete floor, that the valley he had helped drown had finally found its voice.

But that was later. That afternoon, Roque knew none of it.

That afternoon, he flew north into the highlands with the sun on his right wing and the document case lashed behind him, watching the flooded valley slide away beneath his keel, and he allowed himself, at last, to feel victorious. The drizzle had burned off. The air was glass-smooth above the water. In the case at his back were the signatures of ministers, the doctored maps, the orders he'd carried out and the orders he'd invented, the whole rotten ledger of Kestrel Reach—and it was his, all of it, his passport and his ransom and his revenge, and no one in the world knew where he was going.

He checked his fuel. Four hours and ten minutes to the border crossing, with an hour's reserve. Comfortable. He had planned it comfortably, because Ansel Roque always planned comfortably. That was what eleven years of obedience bought a man: the habit of assuming the world would keep its appointments.

He did not see the single-engine bush plane lift off from Two Rivers settlement forty minutes behind him, climbing hard, a woman at the controls who had counted every gallon of fuel against her father's debt and knew, to the liter, exactly how far a tank could be trusted—and who had spent her whole life learning that the difference between surviving the interior and dying in it was never the plan.

It was the pilot.

Roque turned up the gorge with the afternoon light going long and gold on the water, and behind him, small and patient as a hawk riding a thermal, the intercept began.
