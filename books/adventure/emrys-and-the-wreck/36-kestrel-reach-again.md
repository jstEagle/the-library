## 36. Kestrel Reach Again

**Odile**

The call came at 0400, and Odile was already awake.

She had learned to sleep the way her father's old compass needle settled—quickly, and always pointing back to the same worry. But this morning the worry had a name and a heading: Roque's floatplane, spotted refueling at a mining strip two hundred miles upriver, loaded with crates of records from the survey's field office. Records that would burn, one way or another, unless someone put themselves between Ansel Roque and the open sea.

The federal auditor's man on the wire hadn't asked her. He'd asked Marta, and Marta had said, *You want Odile Blackwood for this. It's her valley.* Which was true in a way that sat strangely in Odile's chest—Kestrel Reach as hers, as if the drowned valley were something she'd inherited along with the debt and the compass and the scarred aluminum of her father's plane.

She stood on the dock in the gray before dawn, watching mist come off the water like breath, and Emrys came down the planks with two thermoses and that careful way of walking he'd never quite lost, as if the ground might be a map with errors in it.

"You don't have to do this," she said.

"I know." He handed her the coffee. "That's why I'm doing it."

She looked at him. Six weeks ago he'd been a shape in the reeds, a stranger's hands on her wing struts, a man who answered questions too carefully. Now his face was the first thing she checked each morning, the way she used to check the fuel gauge—not because she doubted it, but because everything depended on it.

"Roque will run the gorge," she said. "It's the only way to the coast before the weather closes. He knows I know the terrain better than he does, so he'll fly high and fast and pray."

"And you?"

"I'm going to make him pray harder." She drained the coffee and felt it land in her like ballast. "Marta's cordon is staged at the salt line. Four boats, two planes. All we have to do is turn him."

*Turn him,* she thought, taxiing out an hour later into a sky the color of wet slate. As if Roque were livestock. As if six weeks of searchlights and dogs and gunfire could be folded up neatly and delivered to a courtroom. She climbed through the marine layer with the engine pulling steady and honest beneath her, and below, the river unspooled toward Kestrel Reach like a rope paying out over a pulley.

The valley opened around her at midmorning, and it was exactly as she remembered—the water too wide, too still, the drowned forest standing in it like a congregation that had been told to wait. The tops of trees. The roof of a church, its bell tower a black stub breaking the surface. The village that had been evacuated for the dam, the village where Emrys went silent, all of it under forty feet of green water, patient as evidence.

Her father had flown this valley. She realized it now with a strange, late clarity—he'd hauled supplies to the dam crews in the years before the flood, back when the maps were still honest, back when Emrys Vale was drawing contour lines that some ministry clerk would redline with a grease pencil. Her whole life had braided through this place without her knowing it. The debt. The plane. The man in the right-hand seat. All of it converging here, where the water held everything the government wanted forgotten.

"Contact," Emrys said beside her. He'd taken the radio watch without being asked; they'd stopped dividing the work weeks ago. "Eleven o'clock, low. Squawking a survey code—old one, pre-flood. He's trying to look official."

Odile rolled her wings and found him: a dark floatplane skimming the flooded timber, running the reach like a man running a corridor with his coat collar up. Even at this distance she could read the haste in him, the way the plane wallowed slightly in the turns, heavy with whatever was lashed in its cabin. Paper. Years of paper. Redlined maps, requisition orders, the memos where men who wore ties decided that forty feet of water over four villages was an acceptable rounding error.

"He sees us," Emrys said.

"Good." She pushed the throttle forward and let the old engine answer. "I want him to spend fuel looking over his shoulder instead of looking at his gauges."

The chase that followed was not the cinematic thing she'd once imagined chases to be. It was arithmetic. Fuel against distance, cloud against ceiling, the gorge ahead narrowing like a throat. Roque dove for the river when the first squall line reached down for him, and Odile followed, dropping into the canyon where the walls rose sheer and dripping and the water ran white over the drowned road. She knew every bend of it. She had landed on this river in rain, in fog, in the blue half-light before dawn, hauling medicine and gossip and other people's freight while her father's debt rode in the seat behind her like a passenger who never got out.

Roque didn't know the gorge. That was the whole truth of it, and she could see him learning it in real time—a hard bank around a spur that came sooner than he expected, a climb to clear a strainer of flood-piled logs, the floats kissing a wave crest with a slap she could almost hear over her own engine. He was flying scared. Scared men made the mistake of flying *tight*, holding the center of the channel where the current ran fastest and the gusts funneled worst, because their eyes told them narrow meant safe.

"Narrow means options," she murmured, the way her father used to say it, and swung wide into the left side of the canyon where the air was dirty but the room was endless.

Ahead, the gorge spat out into the lower reach. Beyond it, gray on gray, the salt line waited, and somewhere in that haze Marta's boats sat with their engines idling, patient as hooks.

"He's going to run for the coast," Emrys said. "He won't fight you. Not with that load."

"No," Odile agreed. "He'll run. So we don't give him anywhere to run *to*."

She keyed the radio and gave Marta the bearing in three words, the way pilots did, the way a community that had grown up around her without her noticing spoke its own language. Then she dropped behind Roque's tail and stayed there, close enough to see the rivets, far enough to live, herding him down the reach the way a sheepdog moves a flock—never touching, only insisting.

The storm found them all at once, the way monsoon weather did, a wall of rain marching up the valley with the sun still shining behind it. And in the last clean light before the wall hit, Odile saw Roque make his decision: he hauled the floatplane up, climbing for the cloud deck, trading the certainty of the boats below for the gamble of the sky.

He never made the deck. The gust front took him sideways like a hand swatting a fly.

Later, everyone would describe it differently—the pilots' cordon, the auditor's observers, the men in the boats—but Odile saw it plain: the floatplane staggered, dropped a wing, kissed the water with one float at speed, and cartwheeled. Not a crash, exactly. A long, terrible, deliberate-looking disaster, the plane shedding pieces across a quarter mile of flooded forest until what remained of it settled in the shallows near the old church tower, steaming, upside-down, its tail sticking up out of the water like a question mark.

The valley, Odile thought, had finally been given something to say. And it had said it about Ansel Roque.

**Emrys**

Emrys was in the water before the boat finished tying off, which was foolish and he knew it, and he did it anyway.

The cabin was inverted, half-submerged, the door jammed. He got his fingers into the gap where the window had been and pulled until the frame gave, and there—wedged against the ceiling that had become the floor—was Ansel Roque, bleeding from the scalp, conscious, gripping a waterproof case to his chest with both arms like a drowning man clinging to driftwood.

Of course, Emrys thought. Of course he saved the records. Not out of principle. Out of leverage. A man like Roque never let go of the thing that kept other people afraid.

"Vale." Roque's voice came out cracked, waterlogged. "Of course it's you."

"Let go of the case."

Roque laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and blood pinked the water around them. "You think this ends with me? You think a hearing in the capital changes what's under this water?" He lifted his chin toward the drowned treetops, the submerged rooftops, the whole green silence of the reach. "I was the instrument. You want the hands that held the bow, you'll need more than a cartographer and a bush pilot."

"I know," Emrys said quietly. "Chapter and verse. We have the names. We've had them since the night your dogs chased me through the swamp." He reached out. "The case, Ansel. Or I leave you to explain to the auditor why you tried to swim away from a rescue."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—Emrys saw the calculation move behind Roque's eyes the way it had moved in a hundred briefing rooms: weigh the asset, weigh the exit, choose the version of himself that survived. And then, visibly, the arithmetic failed. There was no exit. There was only the water, and the boats closing in, and the woman standing up in the bow of the nearest one with her father's compass case open in her hand, watching him with an expression Emrys had learned to read better than any contour line.

Roque let go.

Emrys hauled him out onto the floating wreckage, and the pilots' boats came alongside, and hands—Marta's hands, and the hands of men and women whose names Odile knew and he was only beginning to—took hold of the security chief of the Kestrel Reach survey and pulled him, alive and cursing, into custody. Someone wrapped a blanket around him. Someone else, with a formality that seemed almost tender, informed him he was detained pending federal audit proceedings. Roque stared past all of them at the water.

"The villages," he said suddenly, as the boat turned for shore. His voice had changed; the institutional coldness had cracked, and underneath it was something older and more tired. "Nobody asks about the villages. Four hundred people moved at gunpoint and nobody—" He stopped himself. Looked at Emrys. And for the first time since Emrys had known him, Ansel Roque said nothing at all.

They brought him ashore at the ranger station as the rain arrived, soft now, almost gentle, the storm spending itself the way storms did after they'd done their work. Odile stood beside Emrys on the dock, soaked to the skin, watching the marshals walk Roque up the planks. Her hand found his without either of them arranging it.

"You went into the water," she said.

"You flew into a gust front."

"We're even, then."

He almost smiled. But his eyes went, as they always went, to the valley—the flooded timber, the church tower, the water lying over everything like a held breath. Somewhere under there were the foundations he'd mapped, the fault lines he'd flagged, the redlines drawn over his warnings by men who signed their names in ink that wouldn't run. Somewhere under there was the village where a schoolteacher named Ilsa Ferro had taught him the names of fish, before the trucks came, before the guns, before the water.

"It's not over," he said. "Roque was right about that much. A hearing isn't a resurrection."

"No," Odile agreed. She turned the compass case over in her hands—the decoy case, the bluff case, the case that had bought them seconds at the weir and cost them everything after, and somehow, impossibly, delivered them here. Inside it, where a forged drive had once sat, she'd placed something new: a photograph Marta had found in the survey office, four hundred villagers lined up on the shore of a lake that didn't exist yet, waiting for the boats that would take them away. "But it's a beginning. And beginnings are the only thing I've ever known how to fly toward."

Overhead, the last of the storm blew itself out, and through the tearing clouds a single bar of sunlight fell across Kestrel Reach, laying a bright road on the water from the drowned church to the open sky.

And then the radio on the dock crackled—the auditor's relay, urgent, patched through from the capital—and a voice Emrys recognized said words that turned the sunlight cold:

*"All units, be advised. Ministry liaison Corvin Voss has resigned his board seat effective this hour and departed the capital by private aircraft. Destination unknown. Repeat: Voss is in the wind."*

Odile's hand tightened around the compass case. Emrys watched her face go through it—the fear, the fury, and then the stillness underneath, the pilot's stillness, the stillness of a woman deciding where she was going before anyone could tell her otherwise.

"My debt papers," she said quietly. "His signature's on every page. If he runs, he runs with the one thing that proves how deep the rot goes." She looked up at the clearing sky, already reading the winds. "Emrys. How fast can you file a flight plan?"
