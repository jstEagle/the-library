## 37. Verdicts

*Emrys*

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and old paper, and Emrys Vale could not stop noticing it. Six months of river water, mildew, mangrove mud, and the copper tang of fear had recalibrated his senses entirely; now the ordinary world arrived as a series of small astonishments. The air conditioning hummed. A clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere down the corridor a woman laughed at something mundane, and the sound passed through him like weather.

He sat at the witness table with his hands flat on the wood, because he had learned in the last year that hands were the first thing to betray a man. Keep them still and the voice would follow.

The auditor's counsel was reading from his own maps.

"—flagged in red, here, on the third revision, dated fourteen months before the failure. Note the annotation: *foundation shear unacceptable under projected load; recommend full geotechnical reassessment before pour.* And the ministry's response, Mr. Vale?"

"Strike the redline," Emrys said. "Initial it. Proceed."

The words came out level. He had rehearsed them in hotel rooms, on Odile's dock, once into the wind over Kestrel Reach itself when he'd thought no one was listening. But saying them aloud in a room with microphones and stenographers and a gallery of strangers was different. Saying them made them real in a way that living through them never had. For eleven months he had carried the evidence like ballast, and now he was setting it down page by page, and instead of lightness he felt only the strange vertigo of a man who has put down something heavy and doesn't yet know how to stand without it.

"And you understood," the counsel continued, "that your objections were being—"

"Buried. Yes." He looked at the panel. Nine members, three of them women, one of them asleep until ten minutes ago. "I understood. I signed off anyway. I want that in the record too. I didn't leak it. I didn't refuse. I redrew the maps clean and I told myself the numbers would hold, and then the numbers didn't hold, and eleven people drowned in a valley I had measured to the centimeter." His voice did not crack, which surprised him. It came out worse than cracking—flat, precise, a cartographer's voice, giving coordinates for his own guilt. "I knew exactly where the water would go. I drew the flood lines myself."

The room held its silence for a moment. In the third row, Odile sat very still, her hands folded, her face doing what it always did with feeling: nothing at all, everything banked behind the eyes. He had learned to read her the way he read contour lines—the faint compression at the mouth, the way her thumb moved against her forefinger when she was holding herself together by friction alone.

The chairman asked if he needed a recess.

"No," Emrys said. "I've been running for a year. I'd like to finish standing still."

*Odile*

They broke for lunch, and Odile escaped to the courthouse steps because four walls had started to feel like a fuselage with the doors jammed.

The capital in the wet season was a city of dripping eaves and umbrellas like a fleet of black beetles. She stood under the colonnade watching rain come off the roof in a beaded curtain and thought about how she had gotten here—not the flight, not the boat, not the fire tower with its dead radio and her voice going out over open channels until every pilot on the river knew her name—but the years before. The years of counting gallons. Of landing on strips that wanted to kill her because refusing wasn't in the ledger. Of Harrow Voss's smile across the trading post counter, patient as rot.

Voss had been indicted three days ago. Eleven counts. She had read the list twice and felt nothing she recognized, and then, walking home past the harbor, had stopped in front of a chandlery window and cried for forty seconds, hard and silent, the way she did everything, and then gone inside and bought rope she didn't need.

That was grief, Marta told her later, over beers at the pilots' hall. That's what it looks like when it's late and the person's already gone. You cry at rope.

Her father had signed that note to keep his plane flying, to keep her fed, and Voss had held it over her for six years like a hook through the gill. Now the note was ash in a brass pan in a federal office, and her name appeared in the hearing transcripts as *a cooperating witness of unimpeachable reliability*, and none of it brought back the strip floods she'd flown through, or the nights she'd slept in the plane because a room cost money, or the particular loneliness of being owned and calling it independence.

She heard footsteps and didn't turn. She knew the weight of them now, the way she knew engines by their idle.

"They're serving terrible coffee in there," Emrys said, coming to stand beside her under the colonnade. "I think it's a tactic."

"Everything in there is a tactic."

"Mm." He watched the rain with her. He had gotten good at silence, at letting it be silence instead of a gap to fill. "You don't have to testify tomorrow, you know. Your part's done. They have what they need."

"I know what they need." She turned the compass case over in her pocket—the decoy case now, hollowed, carrying nothing but a slip of paper with her father's initials. She hadn't been able to leave it home. "I'm testifying anyway. Somebody should say out loud what it costs, the people who aren't in the reports. The villages weren't a line item. They were church bells and dog lots and a woman named Ilsa who taught me to read current off the water." Her jaw tightened. "Roque's people will sit there with their lawyers and call it regrettable resettlement. I want the record to say otherwise."

Emrys said nothing for a while. Then: "Ilsa Reyes. Lower Kestrel. She pulled two of my survey crew out of the water during the evacuation drills, back when we still pretended there'd be drills."

"You knew her."

"I mapped her dock." His voice went quiet. "I map everything. It's the curse of the trade. Every place I've ever wronged, I can draw it from memory."

Odile looked at him—at the man who had stepped out of the reeds with his hands up and his secrets showing, who had bailed water beside her all night and never once lied to her after the night he stopped—and felt the thing she had no better word for than *belonging*. Not the bought kind. The other kind, the kind that turned out to have been free the whole time, which was somehow the cruelest discovery of the year.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when they ask me why I flew into the valley against orders, I'm going to tell them the truth. That I was forced. That men with paper made me do it. And then I'm going to tell them I stayed anyway, after, when nobody was forcing me. Because somebody had to carry the truth out, and I had a plane."

"That'll be in the record forever."

"Good." She pushed off the column. "Records are the only ghosts worth having."

* * *

*Emrys*

The verdicts came down over nine days, and Emrys learned that justice does not arrive like weather but like tide—imperceptible hour to hour, undeniable by week's end.

Deputy Minister Corvane: indicted, then a second time when the first indictment proved too narrow. The dam: condemned, the order signed with a pen that was photographed more carefully than any document in the country's history. The engineering firm: dissolved, its principals barred for life. Ansel Roque: awaiting trial, held without bail, and—this detail Emrys received from Marta with undisguised relish—refusing to speak to anyone except his lawyer, which everyone agreed was the smartest thing Roque had ever done.

And the settlements. That word again, *settlement*, doing its quiet violence. The fund was real—land grants in the hill country north of the reach, rebuilding money, a school with Ilsa Reyes's name over the door because Odile had stood in that hearing room and refused to let the dead be aggregates. Emrys had watched her do it. Watched her take the government's own language apart sentence by sentence, calm as a preflight check, until the chairman asked the stenographer to strike the phrase *economically nonviable communities* and enter instead the names of the villages, all seven, spelled correctly.

But the water was still there. That was the thing no verdict touched. The valley would stay drowned until the demolition crews finished their years of work, and even then the drowned village would be silt and foundation stones, and the church bell—Ilsa's bell, hauled out last month by volunteers, green with a decade of river—would hang in the new school yard and ring for children who had never heard it over water.

On the last day, the panel cleared him. Fully. *No culpable negligence; acted under institutional constraint; cooperated fully.* The chairman said some words about courage that Emrys barely heard, because he was watching Odile in the gallery, and she was watching him, and between them passed the whole unsaid ledger of it: the reeds, the leaking boat, the strainer log, the mangroves, the tower, the salt line with gunfire stitching the water behind them. Everything they'd paid. All of it entered now into a record that would sit in a filing cabinet while they went on living with the memory of it in their hands.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, the rain had stopped and the light came down the way it does after long storms, rinsed and golden, making even the capital look briefly honest.

Marta found them there, still in her flight jacket, smelling of avgas and the long drive down. She jerked her thumb at the sky.

"Heard you're cleared, cartographer. Whole river knows. Old Man Tice flew his mail run low over the hall just to honk the horn." She grinned, then sobered, digging in her jacket. "Almost forgot. This came for you both. Ranger relay forwarded it twice—it's been chasing you up the coast."

It was a telegram, soft at the folds, handled by many hands. Emrys read it first, and felt his chest do something complicated.

*DEMOLITION SCHEDULED KESTREL REACH DAM BEGINNING FIRST WEEK DRY SEASON. WATER TO BE LOWERED GRADUALLY OVER FOLLOWING MONTHS. VALLEY WILL EMERGE. REQUEST PRESENCE OF WITNESSES VALE AND BLACKWOOD AT OPENING CEREMONY STOP THE VALLEY REMEMBERS WHO DREW ITS MAPS.*

Below it, in a different hand, someone had added a line that the telegraph clerk had faithfully transmitted:

*ILSA'S BELL IS GOING HOME.*

Odile read it twice. Then she looked up at him, and the light was in her face, and she said the thing he would keep for the rest of his life:

"Emrys. How fast can you file a flight plan?"
