## 38. The Plane Is Hers

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right somehow. Official things in Odile Blackwood's experience arrived on Tuesdays—tax notices, fuel invoices, the summons to testify—and they always smelled faintly of someone else's ink. This one was different only in what it said.

*Re: Lien instrument 4471-K, aircraft registration Cessna 206, tail number—* her father's old registration, the one she'd never had the money or the heart to change—*the encumbrance recorded against the above aircraft is hereby declared void and of no further effect. Title vests solely in Odile M. Blackwood.*

She read it three times on the dock at Marta's field, standing in the kind of light that made everything look temporary, gold going thin at the edges of the clouds. Then she sat down on the planks with her back against a piling and read it a fourth time, because her hands were shaking and she wanted to be sure the shaking was joy and not something worse.

Voss was in custody pending his own hearing. The ministry board he'd sat on was being audited from the top down. And somewhere in a federal filing cabinet, a piece of paper that had owned her for eleven years now officially meant nothing.

Eleven years. She did the arithmetic the way she did fuel calculations, automatically, without wanting to. She'd been twenty-three when the lawyers handed her the note along with the casket flag, twenty-three and already fluent in the language of gallons per hour and minimum balances. Every load she'd ever flown—medicine to San Rafael, seed rice upriver, the gossip and the mail and once, memorably, a wedding cake packed in wet burlap—every single one had been flown for Harrow Voss as much as for herself. She had been, functionally, a sharecropper of the sky.

Now the letter said otherwise. Now the sky, or at least her small registered slice of it, was hers.

"You're smiling," Marta said, coming down the dock with two cups of something that claimed to be coffee. "I don't think I've seen that before. It's unsettling."

"It's nothing." Odile folded the letter along its creases, then unfolded it, unwilling to crease it at all. "It's just paper."

Marta looked at the letter, then at her, with the flat patience of a woman who had spent thirty years reading weather and could read anything. "That's not nothing. That's the whole sky." She pressed a cup into Odile's hand. "Drink that. It's terrible. You've earned terrible coffee."

Odile drank it. It was terrible. She smiled again anyway, and this time didn't try to stop.

* * *

The plane sat in the grass beside the hangar where it had sat since the hearing adjourned, and Odile walked out to it in the early afternoon with a bucket, a rag, and four cans of paint she'd bought with money that—for the first time—was entirely her own.

The tail had been white for eleven years. Before that, if you knew where to look, if you'd grown up running your fingers along the hangar walls while your father argued fuel prices with men who wore their hats indoors—it had been something else. Her father had painted a compass rose on the tail of every aircraft he'd ever owned. Not a manufacturer's decal, not a registry number done large. A compass rose, eight-pointed, drawn freehand in a single afternoon with a string, a pencil, and a nail for a pivot point. He used to say an airplane without one was just machinery looking for an excuse to get lost. He used to say a lot of things. Most of them had come back to her over the past months, unbidden, in his voice, at moments when she least wanted them and most needed them.

She hadn't repainted the tail after he died. There had been no money for paint, and later there had been no time, and later still there had been no permission—not legally, but truly. A plane under lien felt like a plane on loan from the world, and you didn't redecorate other people's property. So the tail stayed white, scuffed and sun-bleached, and every time she preflighted she touched the aluminum where the rose used to be, the way some people touch doorframes.

Today she spread newspaper on the grass, opened the first can, and stood there for a long moment doing nothing at all.

The blue was close but not exact. She'd mixed it by memory and a photograph Emrys had found in her father's effects—a grainy shot of the old Skywagon on a beach strip, tail turned toward the camera, the rose sharp even through forty years of emulsion. She'd held the photo against paint chips in the hardware store in town like a woman matching grief to a swatch. The clerk had asked if it was for a boat. She'd said yes. It wasn't a lie, exactly.

She started with the outline. String, pencil, nail—the same method, the same pivot point tapped into the skin above the rudder hinge, and when the nail bit and the pencil swung true she heard her father laugh somewhere behind her teeth. *Steady hand, little bird. The compass doesn't care how you feel. It only cares where you are.* She'd hated that saying at fifteen. At thirty-four, standing in the grass with paint drying on her knuckles, she understood it was the kindest thing he'd ever taught her: that direction was not a feeling. That you could be terrified and still be on heading.

The eight points went on slow. North first, always north first—he'd been superstitious about that, or disciplined, which might have been the same thing in him. Then the intercardinals, then the half-winds, each stroke laid off the string like a chord off a fretted neck. The sun moved. A pair of cattle egrets worked the grass around her feet, indifferent to history. Somewhere across the field Marta's old Beechcraft ticked as its engine cooled, and the smell of cut grass and turpentine mixed into something that smelled, impossibly, like childhood.

By late afternoon the rose was finished and she stepped back to look at it, and it was imperfect—the lines wobbled where her hand had wobbled, the west point slightly heavier than the east, the whole thing a degree or two off true north because she'd aligned the string by eye instead of by instrument. Her father would have redone it. She decided she wouldn't. A compass rose that admitted it could err seemed more honest than one that pretended otherwise. She'd spent eleven years pretending otherwise.

Emrys found her there at dusk, sitting on the wheel chock with the empty cans lined up beside her like spent shell casings, watching the paint cure in the last light.

"It's crooked," he said.

"One degree west of north."

"Deliberate?"

"Everything worth keeping is a little crooked." She looked up at him. He had a way of arriving without announcing himself that had stopped startling her weeks ago and become, instead, something like weather she could count on. He was carrying two plates of whatever Marta had made, and he set one down on the wing strut within her reach without comment, which was its own kind of fluency now. They'd developed a whole language of objects placed within reach.

He studied the tail for a while. He was a cartographer; he read surfaces the way she read skies. "Your father's mark?"

"My father's mark." She wiped her hands on the rag, accomplishing nothing—the paint was under her skin now, in the whorls of her fingerprints, and she suspected it would be there for days. Good. Let it be there. "I flew under a white tail for eleven years because I thought I didn't deserve the color. Turns out I just didn't own it."

"And now?"

"Now I own it." She said it plainly, testing the weight of the words, and found they held. "The whole plane. Every rivet. Even the ones I haven't found yet that are going to fail at the worst possible moment."

"That's ownership," Emrys agreed gravely. "Finding the rivets."

They ate sitting on the grass with their backs against the tires, plates balanced on knees, and the plane stood over them with its new tail catching the last of the light, the rose turning slowly from bright to deep as the sun went down. Odile found herself talking about her father—not the debt version of him, not the man whose signature had chained her, but the earlier one, the one who'd let her hold the yoke at six on the straight stretch over the salt flats and who'd told her the wind was a conversation and most pilots never learned to listen. Emrys listened the way he did everything: completely, without hurrying her, asking the occasional question that showed he'd kept every word.

"I used to think the debt was my inheritance," she said, when the plates were empty and the stars were starting. "Like it was the last thing he gave me. Eleven years I carried it like it was."

"And now?"

"Now I think the inheritance was always the compass." She nodded at the tail. "The rest was just paperwork somebody else wrote. Paper burns. I should know—I've burned some."

"You have." His voice was warm with it. "I was there. It was a very good fire."

It had been. She still thought about it sometimes, the way people think about a storm they survived—the sound of Voss's face when the note curled and blackened, the smell of it, the strange lightness in her own chest afterward, as though she'd set down cargo she'd forgotten she was carrying. Burning the papers hadn't erased the debt. Paying it had—in sweat, in risk, in testimony given with her voice steady and her hands not. The burning had just been the receipt.

* * *

She slept badly that night, which surprised her, and woke before dawn knowing why. There was work to do. There was always work to do—but for the first time in eleven years, all of it was hers.

She was at the hangar by first gray light with the cowling open and the inspection checklist spread on the wing, because a plane that had taken gunfire-adjacent abuse, monsoon water, and a forced ditching deserved better than a casual glance before it flew again. She worked through the morning systematically: drained the sumps, checked the oil screen for metal, borescoped the cylinders, pulled and inspected the propeller where Roque's floatplane wake had nearly put her into the canyon wall. The airframe logbook lay open on the workbench, and she'd bought a new one—blank pages, stiff cover—to start fresh alongside the old. The old book she would keep, of course. Every entry, every defect, every hour logged under duress. But the new book would begin with today's date and a single line:

*Annual inspection commenced. Aircraft title clear. Owner: O.M. Blackwood.*

Owner. She wrote it twice, the second time slower, watching her own hand make the letters.

Midmorning, Marta appeared with the terrible coffee and news: the federal auditor's office had released the seized records, and among them, catalogued and photographed, was Emrys's original survey folio—the maps with the redlines, the dam foundation reports, the chain of memos showing who knew what and when. It would all come out at the inquiry. The drowned villages would get their settlement fund. The dam itself was condemned; crews were already moving equipment toward Kestrel Reach to begin drawing the water down.

"Drawing it down," Odile repeated. She tried to imagine it—the valley unbrowning, the drowned village emerging house by house, roof by roof, the church bell tower she'd flown over a hundred times rising out of the water like something surfacing from a long dive. She couldn't quite see it. Some drownings you don't come back from, even when the water leaves. But the village would get its ground back, and the names would get their record set straight, and maybe that was the most the world ever offered anyone: not restoration, but acknowledgment.

"They'll need pilots for the drawdown survey," Marta added, too casually. "Low and slow, photography runs, the works. Dangerous work over soft mud. Pays well."

"I'll think about it."

"You'll say yes."

"I'll think about it," Odile said again, and Marta grinned and left her alone with her engine and her new logbook, which was how Odile preferred to be left.

By late afternoon the inspection was done and the plane was airworthy, and she rolled it out onto the grass and just stood looking at it for a while. The Compass Rose—that was what she'd decided to call it, informally, the way her father had named his planes without ever painting the names on. The tail caught the low sun and threw it back, eight points of faded blue against white, one degree west of north.

She ran up the engine. It caught on the third blade, smooth and familiar, the vibration climbing through her boots like a handshake. She taxied the length of the field and back, checked the brakes, shut it down, and sat in the left seat with her hands resting on the yoke, listening to the metal tick as it cooled.

Through the windshield she watched Emrys come down from the house, walking unhurried through the long grass with his collar up against the evening, carrying nothing, needing to carry nothing. He stopped at the wing and looked at the tail for a long time.

"Well?" she called through the open window.

"It's crooked," he said again, but he was smiling.

"One degree west of north." She patted the seat beside her—the right-hand seat, empty for eleven years except for cargo and the occasional nervous official. "Come here. I want to show you something."

He climbed up onto the wing root and folded himself into the right seat, too tall for it the way he was too tall for everything, and she reached between the seats and pulled out the thing she'd wrapped in a shop towel that morning: her father's compass. The brass one, the one she'd checked out of habit in a trading post a lifetime ago, three chapters of her life back. It had been in her flight bag through everything since—the flood, the rapids, the tower, the salt line. Its glass was scratched. Its needle still settled true.

She held it up between them, level, and they both watched the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, pointing faithfully one degree west of where the mapmakers said north was, and she laughed, because of course. Of course it was. Her father's compass and her father's rose, agreeing with each other and disagreeing with the world, exactly as they'd been built to do.

"He'd have liked you," she said. "He trusted instruments that admitted their error. He said those were the only honest ones."

Emrys looked at the needle, then at her. "And what does yours say right now?"

She checked it properly, the way she'd been taught at six years old over the salt flats. Held it level. Let it settle. Read it.

"Home," she said. "It says home. Though—" she tilted it, teasing, watching him watch her— "it's got a slight easterly bias lately. Toward a certain cartographer. Might need recalibrating."

"Might," Emrys said, and his hand found hers on the yoke, and outside the wind came up the valley from the south, fair and steady, a good flying wind, tomorrow's wind, theirs.

Tomorrow she would fly the first run of the new season—medicine and mail, no secrets, the whole sky hers. Tomorrow, or the day after, there would be photography runs over a valley learning to breathe again. Tonight there was only the cooling engine, the fading rose, and the question hanging in the cabin between them like the smell of fresh paint: what comes next, and who sits in the right-hand seat when they find out.

She was almost ready to answer it.
