## 39. Runways South

The dock at Puerto Cielo smelled of diesel and salt and old wood gone soft with rain, and Emrys Vale stood on it in the blue hour before dawn feeling like a man who had been handed back his own life and didn't yet know where to set it down.

He had slept—actually slept—for the first time in weeks, in a borrowed room above the harbormaster's office, on a mattress that sagged in the middle and a blanket that smelled of someone else's tobacco. He had slept without dreaming of redlined maps, without waking to the sound of water rising over a runway, without reaching for the waterproof satchel that no longer held anything he needed to guard. The evidence was with the auditor now. The testimony was given. Roque was in custody upriver, and the inquiry would grind on for months the way inquiries did, but the running was over.

And still, when he woke, his hands had that floating, unmoored feeling. For two years his days had been shaped by concealment: where to sleep, what to say, which road not to take. Now every direction was open, and open country terrified him more than any flood.

So he came down to the dock before light, because docks were where he thought best. Water gave a man an honest horizon.

She was already there.

He should have known she would be. Odile Blackwood treated dawn the way other people treated appointments—with a punctuality that bordered on devotion. She sat on the edge of the dock with her boots off, feet dangling above the black water, her father's compass open in one hand. The needle swung lazily, undecided, as if even magnetism wanted to sleep in.

"You're up early," he said, coming down the planks.

"I never went to bed." She didn't look up. "I kept thinking I'd hear the audit people knocking. Turns out they don't knock at four in the morning. Who knew."

Emrys lowered himself beside her, leaving a careful hand's width between them—the same careful distance he'd maintained since the reeds, through the storm, through the rapids, through everything. It was a distance he had come to hate.

Below them, the harbor breathed. Fishing boats knocked gently against their moorings. Somewhere across the water a generator coughed awake, and gulls were already arguing about something.

"Your plane," he said. "Marta said they finished the paperwork yesterday. The lien's voided."

"I know." Odile turned the compass over in her fingers. "I signed forty forms. Forty. I counted. You'd think a woman was buying a battleship."

"It *is* yours, though. Outright. First time?"

"First time since Papa died." She said it simply, without ceremony, but Emrys heard the weight underneath—the years of gallons counted against interest, of fuel bought on credit and flights flown in fear of Harrow Voss's ledger. "It's a strange feeling. Like wearing shoes you always rented."

He laughed quietly. "That's a very pilot thing to say."

"That's a very cartographer thing to notice." She finally looked at him then, and in the pre-dawn gray her eyes were the color of deep river water. "What about you? What does a cleared cartographer do with himself? They'll want you consulting on the dam decommissioning. Marta says there's talk of a commission. Official maps. Your name in the credits instead of redlined out."

Emrys watched a fishing boat putter out past the breakwater, its lamp a small yellow star on the dark water. "I don't know," he admitted. "I've spent so long being a ghost that I'm not sure how to be a person again. There's a version of me that goes back to the capital, takes the commission, sits in rooms with men who sign things, and spends ten years making sure it never happens again. That's the responsible version."

"And the other version?"

"The other version doesn't have a plan yet."

Odile nodded slowly, and for a while neither of them spoke. This was the thing about her, Emrys thought—not the flying, not the ferocity, but this: her comfort with silence. She didn't fill quiet spaces with noise the way frightened people did. She let them be.

"My father used to say," she offered eventually, "that a compass doesn't tell you where to go. It only tells you which way you're already facing. The going is up to you."

"He sounds like he'd have liked maps."

"He loved maps. He just never trusted the people who drew borders on them." She closed the compass with a soft click. "Emrys."

"Yes."

"What comes next? For you. Actually."

The question hung there, honest and bare, and Emrys found he couldn't give her the polished answer. Not with her. He'd learned, somewhere between the leaking boat and the shingle bar, that lying to Odile Blackwood was not only wrong but exhausting, like swimming upstream in your own clothes.

"I don't know," he said again. "I keep waiting for someone to tell me I have to run again. Or hide. Or testify. And nobody is. And I don't—" He stopped, started again. "I spent two years holding evidence that could destroy people, and the whole time, the thing that kept me moving wasn't the mission. It was the idea that someday there'd be a place where I could stop. And now it's here, and I'm standing on a dock at five in the morning because I don't know how to stop."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached over and took his hand.

Not dramatically. Not with music swelling or rain falling or any of the machinery of romance. She just took it, the way you'd take hold of a rope thrown to you, firm and certain, her palm rough from yokes and throttle levers.

"You know what I figured out?" she said. "On the island, drying out. I figured out that I'd spent my whole adult life believing belonging was a debt. Something you owed on. Something you paid off, and then maybe, if you were good, if you were useful, if you flew enough medicine into enough mud-hole strips—you earned the right to belong somewhere. To somebody." Her thumb moved once across his knuckles. "And then I burned Voss's papers, and testified, and nothing collapsed. The world didn't end. I just... belonged anyway. Free of charge. It turns out it was never for sale. I just hadn't claimed it."

Emrys's throat was tight. "Odile—"

"Let me finish, because if I stop I'll lose my nerve, and I've crash-landed a plane in a monsoon, so losing my nerve on a dock would be humiliating." She took a breath. "I've got a run tomorrow. South. Medicine to the settlements along the coast road, mail, a crate of vaccines that needs keeping cold. It's three days, maybe four. Good weather window. And my right-hand seat has been empty since Papa died, because I never let anyone sit in it. Not Voss's men, not paying passengers, nobody. It felt like giving away a piece of him."

She turned to face him fully then, and the first thin line of dawn had broken over the eastern hills, laying a pale gold seam across the harbor.

"I'm not offering you a job," she said. "I don't need a co-pilot. I've flown alone for six years and I can fly alone for sixty. I'm asking you—Emrys Vale, disgraced cartographer, terrible bailer, man who got thrown out of a boat in the rapids and made me haul him back in—to sit in that seat. Because I choose you. Not because the flood trapped us together. Not because we survived something. Because you read water better than anyone I've ever met, and you're quiet in the right ways, and when I look at you I feel like I'm facing true north. And that's not a debt either. Nobody owes anybody here. It's just mine to give, and I'm giving it."

The dock swayed gently beneath them. A gull screamed somewhere, absurdly, perfectly timed, and Odile laughed—a wet, shaky laugh—and Emrys realized she was crying a little, and that she wasn't hiding it, and that this too was a gift.

"Say something," she whispered. "Before I die of embarrassment on my own dock."

"I threw myself out of that boat," Emrys said hoarsely. "Technically."

"Emrys."

"I'm choosing you back." His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "I want you to know that. Whatever you're giving, I'm giving it back. Not because you saved my life—though you did, twice, and I've been keeping count. Because for two years the only thing I trusted was paper. Maps, records, evidence. Things that couldn't lie to me. And then there was a woman in a flooded valley who lied to Roque with a straight face and bluffed a security chief with a dead man's compass, and I trusted her with everything I had left, and she never once dropped it." He lifted their joined hands, pressed his lips briefly to her knuckles. "You're the truest thing I've ever charted, Odile Blackwood. Yes. God, yes. The right-hand seat. Any seat. The boat, if it comes to that—I'll even bail."

She laughed again, properly this time, and then he leaned in and kissed her.

It was not a dramatic kiss. There was no storm behind it, no gunfire, no burning station. Just dawn coming up slow over Puerto Cielo, and the smell of salt and diesel, and two people who had been carefully not-doing-this for a thousand miles of drowned forest finally doing it. Her hand came up to his jaw, stubbled and salt-rough, and his slid around to the back of her neck, and the compass sat forgotten between them on the planks, its needle swinging steadily south.

When they broke apart, foreheads touching, Odile exhaled a laugh against his mouth.

"Well," she said. "Took us long enough."

"We had a monsoon. And a manhunt. And a corrupt ministry."

"Excuses." She pulled back, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, brisk again in the way that was hers alone. "Right-hand seat rules. One: you don't touch the yoke unless I say. Two: you navigate, you read weather, and you keep the coffee hot. Three: if we go down, you swim toward me, not away from me. Those are non-negotiable."

"Accepted. Counter-proposal: we get breakfast first. I haven't eaten since the hearing, and I refuse to be proposed to and kissed and hired on an empty stomach."

"Hired?" Her eyebrow arched. "You're not hired, Vale. You're chosen. There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed, standing and pulling her up with him, both of them laughing now, giddy and sleepless and free. "Chosen pays worse, I hear."

"Terribly. Payment is in sunrises and river miles."

They walked up the dock together as the sun cleared the hills and set the whole harbor burning gold, and ahead of them the town was waking—bakeries, engines, children—and behind them the compass lay open on the planks, pointing steadily, faithfully south, toward the coast road, toward the settlements, toward whatever came next.

Toward home, which was no longer a place either of them had to buy.

* * *

By noon the plane sat fueled on the apron, gleaming after Marta's mechanics had worked her over, and Odile walked the preflight with Emrys at her shoulder, teaching him her rituals the way her father had once taught her. Check the tabs. Drain the sumps. Hand on the propeller blade, feeling for the day's temper.

"She's called the *Kestrel*," Odile said, patting the cowling. "Papa named her. After the reach, before it drowned. He always said he'd fly over it someday and see it dry again."

Emrys looked up at her. "The water's being lowered. That's part of the settlement. They start next month."

"I know." She smiled, small and real. "So we'll fly over it someday. Together. And it'll be dry."

They loaded the vaccines in their ice chest, the mail sacks, the crates of medicine labeled in three languages. The right-hand seat waited, empty no longer. Emrys settled into it, ran his hands over the unfamiliar controls, and found that it fit—like a chair in a house you'd lived in long ago and only just remembered.

Odile swung up into the left seat, started the engine, and taxied out into the morning as the tower cleared her south, and the runway stretched out ahead of them, long and golden and entirely, completely theirs.
