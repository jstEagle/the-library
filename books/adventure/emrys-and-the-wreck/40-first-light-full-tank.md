## 40. First Light, Full Tank

The season turned the way seasons do in the interior—not by calendar but by smell. One morning in late October Odile stepped out of her room above the hangar and the air had changed overnight: the wet-season rot gone out of it, replaced by woodsmoke and cold river and something almost like iron. Dry season. The word itself felt like a gift she hadn't earned so much as survived into.

She stood on the catwalk with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the light come up over the strip. The grass was silvered with mist. Her plane sat at the tie-downs where she'd left it the night before, tail number fresh, and—new this year—the tail itself repainted: a compass rose in deep blue and gold, her father's design copied from the brass instrument that now rode in her breast pocket instead of his. She'd done the work herself over three evenings, masking tape and steady hands, and when she'd peeled the last strip away she'd sat down hard on the hangar floor and cried for ten minutes, which was not grief exactly, or not only grief. It was more like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

The debt papers were ash eleven months old now. She still thought about them sometimes—the way they'd curled in the ashtray, Voss's face going gray, the ink lifting off the page in black ghosts before the flame took it. She didn't regret it. That was the strange part. She'd spent six years believing regret would be the price of that match, and instead there was just space. Room in her chest where the ledger used to live.

Below her, Emrys came out of the hangar carrying two mugs and a crate of mail sacks balanced on one hip like he'd been doing it all his life. He looked up, found her on the catwalk, and lifted the free mug.

"Fuel's ordered," he called. "Marta says if we're not wheels-up by seven she's taking our medicine run out from under us."

"Marta can try," Odile said, and went down to meet him.

* * *

Loading the plane had become a kind of liturgy between them, wordless in most of its parts. He passed; she stowed. Medicine crates forward, weight aft, mail sacks strapped where the survey team's instruments used to ride. She knew the numbers without checking the sheet anymore—her own weight-and-balance arithmetic, learned from her father, refined through six years of counting every gallon against Harrow Voss's interest rate. Except the counting had changed too. Now she counted because full tanks meant range, and range meant she could take the long way over the reach if she wanted, and linger, and no one billed her for the minutes.

Emrys climbed into the right-hand seat and settled in with the ease of a man who'd been flying with her for a year. He still did the maps. Some habits survived even disgrace, and thank God for that—his hand-drawn charts of the settlements hung in the hangar now, corrected versions of the ones the ministry had burned, and three of the river schools used them for geography lessons. He'd started signing them. *E. Vale.* Small letters, bottom corner. The first time she'd seen it she'd said nothing, but she'd looked at it a long time.

"Radio check," she said.

"Loud and clear," he said, and then, because he was Emrys, "and the weather's honest today. Cold front pushed the haze out. You'll see the whole valley."

She taxied out with the sun coming up gold behind the hangar and took the strip into the wind, and the plane lifted the way it always did—like something released rather than launched—and the river unspooled beneath her, brown and glittering, running south toward the coast and everything that had happened there.

She flew the direct line first, because medicine didn't wait. Two hours downriver to the confluence settlements, vaccine crates and insulin and a bag of letters that made people cry on the dock. At the second stop an old woman pressed a woven bracelet into Emrys's hand and told him his maps had saved her grandson's school boat from the sandbar at Kilometer Forty, and Emrys went quiet the way he did, and Odile put her hand briefly on the back of his neck, and neither of them said anything about it, and that was its own kind of language now.

Then, with the cargo bay empty and the tanks still better than half full, she swung east.

Kestrel Reach opened beneath them the way it always did—a slow widening of the river into drowned forest, black water standing in the crowns of trees that had been dead five years and would be dead fifty more. But it was different this morning. Different in a way that made her throttle back without deciding to.

There were machines on the water.

Dredges—three of them, big yellow hulks anchored in a line across the lower basin, with barges alongside and men in hard hats moving on the decks. A spill of pale churned water trailed downstream from each one. And where the floodline had marked the trees for years, a new line was being drawn: lower. The water around the nearest dredge sat a good meter below the stain on the trunks, and every day, according to the ranger who'd flown up last week, it sat a little lower still.

"They're really doing it," Emrys said beside her. He'd gone very still, the way he did over the reach, every time, like a man visiting a grave. "They're actually drawing it down."

"The audit order," Odile said. "Slow release through the spillway. Marta says by next dry season the valley floor will start coming back." She banked gently, giving him the view, giving herself the view. "They say the villages might rebuild. The ones that want to."

He didn't answer right away. Below, the water lay flat as hammered tin, reflecting the dead forest perfectly, so that it was hard to tell which trees were real and which were their reflections, and somewhere under all of it—the old road, the church foundation, the orchard, whatever else the dam had taken when it failed early and drowned what the government had promised to protect. Somewhere under it, too, were things less gentle: the wrecked boat against the strainer log, the jacket, the blood, the four men Roque had cut loose when their findings became inconvenient. Two of them had never been found. The inquiry had closed that file in March with a verdict of *death by misadventure*, and Emrys had read the finding twice and then folded the paper into quarters and put it in his breast pocket, and he carried it still, she knew, though he never mentioned it.

"Some of them will come home," he said finally. "To a valley that killed their neighbors." His voice was level, but she heard the seam in it. "I don't know if that's justice or just weather."

"Maybe it's both," Odile said. "Most true things are."

She held the turn a moment longer, watching the dredges work their patient geometry, and then brought the nose back around toward the north and the string of settlements waiting on the upper river. The compass rose on her tail caught the sun as she rolled out, and for half a second the whole plane seemed ringed in gold.

* * *

The upper run was the good kind of tired: nine landings, each one shorter than the last, grass strips and sandbars and once—a new one since the rains ended—a shelf of graded gravel above the rapids where a family had cleared a strip themselves and painted *ODILE* on a board at the threshold, misspelled, which somehow made it better. Emrys laughed until he had to wipe his eyes. She landed there anyway, just to prove the board right, and drank sweet tea on someone's porch while children counted the rivets on her wing.

No secrets. That was the phrase she kept turning over as the day went on, testing it like a coin bitten between the teeth. No secrets in the cargo manifest—medicine, mail, seed stock, a replacement radio battery for the ranger relay. No secrets in the logbook, which any inspector could read front to back. No secrets between the two seats. A year ago she'd have said a life like that was impossible, that everyone in the interior ran on hidden ledgers—debts, favors, names held close, truths traded like contraband. She'd built her whole self on the premise that invisibility was safety. Fly low, stay small, owe nothing, need nothing, and nothing can be taken from you.

And then the survey team vanished, and a stranger stepped out of the reeds, and she'd learned the lesson the long way: invisibility wasn't safety. It was just solitude with better marketing. Everything worth having—the pilots who'd jammed Roque's boats with false sightings, the dockhands who'd hidden Emrys, Marta landing under gunfire at the salt line because Odile had once carried her engine parts in a storm and asked no payment—all of it had come from the opposite direction. From being known. From owing and being owed, freely, in the currency of people who showed up.

"You're smiling," Emrys said, somewhere over the sixth strip. "You only smile like that when you're having thoughts."

"I'm having thoughts," she agreed.

"Dangerous ones?"

"The worst kind. Hopeful ones."

* * *

They overnighted at the relay station, a tin-roofed outpost on a bluff where the ranger, old Ferro, kept a generator, a shortwave, and a reputation for the best fried fish on the river. After supper they walked out to the bluff edge, because the sky had gone clear all the way down to the horizon and the stars over the interior were thick enough to swim in.

Downriver, far off, a single light moved on the black water. A boat, running late. Odile tracked it out of habit before she could stop herself—the old reflex, cataloguing threats—and then made herself let it go. Just a trader, probably. Just somebody getting home.

"Ferro says the ministry liaison resigned," Emrys said. "Quietly. And two of the board members are under indictment. Voss is fighting his in the capital—claims he was a victim of the same fraud he financed." He shook his head slowly. "Men like him always find a way to be the injured party."

"He'll lose," Odile said. "Not because the system works. Because too many people heard the broadcast." She thought of that night in the fire tower, her hands shaking on the broken radio, spilling the whole rotten story onto open channels—every pilot, every settlement, every kitchen with a receiver hearing that the government had redlined the maps and flooded the valley anyway. You couldn't unhear a thing like that. It had spread down the river faster than floodwater, and it had never fully drained.

"And you?" Emrys asked. "How are you, in all this?"

It was the question he asked maybe once a month, always plainly, always expecting a plain answer. She considered lying, out of habit, and then didn't.

"I keep waiting for it to feel temporary," she said. "The clean name. The plane with no lien. You." She looked at him. In the starlight his face was mostly shadow, but she knew it well enough now to see the expression underneath. "A year in, and I still brace sometimes. Like the bill's going to come due. Like belonging is a loan I'll have to pay back with interest."

"And?"

"And I'm starting to believe it isn't." She turned the compass over in her pocket, feeling the worn brass, the needle swinging loose and true inside. Her father had bought it secondhand and trusted it thirty years. She'd inherited it as ballast—as the physical form of a debt—and somewhere along the way it had become what it was always supposed to be. Not a weight. An instrument. Something that pointed.

"Come here," she said, or maybe he said it; by then it hardly mattered whose idea it was. They stood at the edge of the bluff with the river murmuring below and the stars doing their slow wheel overhead, and she leaned into him, and he wrapped his arms around her, and neither of them spoke for a long time, because they'd gotten good at that too—at silence that was shelter instead of distance.

* * *

Wheels-up before dawn on the way home, because Odile had never in her life been able to sleep past first light on a clear morning, and because the tank was full and the sky was empty and some mornings that was reason enough.

She flew the long way. Up the gorge where she'd lost Roque's floatplane in terrain she knew like breath, threading the canyon just to feel the walls rise on either side, close enough to count the ferns. Down the other side into the gold morning haze. Along the coast, low over the salt flats, where the mangroves stood in ranks like a congregation. And then inland again, unhurried, the fuel gauge barely moved, the whole interior spread out beneath her like a country that had finally decided to be hers.

Over Kestrel Reach one last time, she throttled back and let the plane drift on its own momentum, and together they watched the water go down.

The dredges were working already, tiny in the early light, and along the eastern shore a strip of mudflat had surfaced—raw, scarred, unmistakably land. On it, impossibly, green things were already happening: weeds first, then shoots, the valley floor remembering itself. Someone had planted a flag or a marker out there; she couldn't tell what. Maybe a survey stake. Maybe the corner post of a house that hadn't been built yet. Maybe just a stick some kid had found and planted, because kids plant sticks.

"There," Emrys said quietly, pointing. "That flat. That was the village square."

She looked. Water still stood over most of it, thin and bright, but the edges were showing, dark and patient, waiting for the rest.

"It'll come back," she said.

"Not all of it. Not for everyone." He was silent a moment. "But some."

"Some is how everything starts," Odile said, and pushed the throttle up, and the compass rose on her tail flashed gold as the plane climbed into the light.

Below, the river ran south, carrying the season's first dry wind toward the sea, and above, the sky was wide open in every direction, and the tank was full, and there was nowhere she owed anything to anyone, and everywhere she chose to go.
