## 1. The Optimists' Ledger

The supercell came off the flats at four in the afternoon, the way they always did, like a debt arriving early.

Xanthe Dravenmoor watched it build from the passenger seat of a truck that wasn't his, parked on a gravel spur that wasn't his, with a barometer app he'd cracked himself and a radar feed he was technically stealing. The storm stood up over the prairie like something being born wrong—a green-black column with a beardsplit of hail falling out of its left flank, rotating slow and patient, the way big money rotates. He'd been chasing weather for eleven years and he still felt the same thing every time: not fear, not thrill, exactly. More like recognition. Here was a thing that owed nothing to anyone.

"Base is tightening," he said, mostly to himself, and typed the numbers into his slate with two thumbs while the wind started rocking the truck. *Mesocyclone tightening, 14:52 local. Wall cloud developing, inflow from the southeast at 35 knots. Possible tornadogenesis within 40 minutes. Position data available, high confidence.*

That was the product. That was what the buyers wanted—farmers with grain in the ground, rail companies with rolling stock on the line, insurance adjusters who paid a flat rate for a forty-minute head start. Xanthe sold certainty about disaster. It was honest work in the way that selling umbrellas during a flood is honest work: technically, and only if you didn't look too long at the water.

The slate buzzed. Not the buyers' channel. The other one.

He looked at the name—**VELLUM SYNDICATE, ACCOUNTS**—with the little smiling sun icon that their system auto-attached to every message, because someone in their design department had decided that friendliness was the most efficient form of menace. He let it buzz out. Then it buzzed again, and he picked up, because they always called twice, and the second call was never friendly in a different way.

"Xanthe Dravenmoor." The voice was warm, rounded, unhurried. A woman's voice, the kind that read bedtime stories or foreclosure notices, whichever paid better. "How's the weather out there?"

"Profitable," Xanthe said. "Yours?"

"Stable." He could hear her smiling. He could always hear them smiling; it was the Syndicate's signature, the thing people meant when they said *the Vellum people*, with that little pause. "We've been reviewing your account. You've been very consistent. Consistency is a virtue, Xanthe. Most of our clients are erratic. They hide. They make excuses. You just keep sending your little payments, month after month, like the tide."

"I like the tide."

"The tide," she agreed, "always comes back. Which is why we trust it." A pause, filled with the sound of paper, actual paper, being turned. They did that deliberately too; he was sure of it. Everything about the Syndicate was designed to feel older and more permanent than it was. "You're at eighty-one percent of principal, Xanthe. Do you know what that means?"

"It means I'm nineteen percent free."

"It means you're eighty-one percent ours." The smile didn't waver. It never wavered. That was the horror of it, the thing he'd stopped explaining to people who hadn't dealt with them: there was no cruelty in the voice at all. There was nothing in the voice at all except patience, and patience was worse. "But we're not calling to discuss the past. We're calling because we have an opportunity. Someone with your particular skill set."

"I chase storms."

"You chase *things*," she said. "Storms are just the ones that announce themselves."

The wall cloud was lowering now, a gray skirt dragging across the flats, and the inflow wind was pulling past the truck hard enough to sing in the antenna wire. Xanthe watched it with one eye and kept the other on the slate, because that was the arrangement he'd made with his own life: half of him always watching the disaster, half always doing the arithmetic.

"I'm listening," he said, which was a lie, because he'd been listening since she said *opportunity*, the way a starving man listens to a kitchen.

"The Halcyon Expedition," she said. "You've heard of it."

"Mineral survey. Concession-backed. Went upriver four months ago and went quiet."

"Went quiet," she repeated, savoring it. "Eleven people, a hundred and forty thousand in equipment, and a findings report that our— that certain interested parties would very much like to see. The concession permits lapse in nineteen days. When they lapse, everything Halcyon was surveying reverts to the state, and a great many investments become, well. Weather."

"Meaning worthless."

"Meaning *weather*," she said, and he heard that she'd chosen the word for him, tailored it, a small courtesy that was somehow more threatening than a threat. "We need someone to go upriver and find out what happened to them. Recover the expedition. Recover whatever they found. Before the clock runs out."

"And if they're dead?"

"Then recover the report. The data is the expedition, Xanthe. People are just the packaging."

The tornado warning was going out on the public band now, the automated voice flat and calm, and Xanthe pulled the truck onto the road and started driving parallel to the storm, because the buyers paid a premium for tracked data and the storm was moving northeast at twenty-two knots. The light had gone the color of old brass. Hail ticked against the windshield like someone politely asking to be let in.

"Terms," he said.

"Generous. Completion clears a meaningful portion of your balance. We're prepared to say—thirty percent. Perhaps forty, depending on what you bring back."

"Sixty."

The smile in the voice tightened by one degree, the way a rope tightens. "Xanthe. We've been so *patient* with you. Do you know what happens to clients who stop being consistent? I don't want to describe it. I like our conversations. I'd like to keep having them."

"Sixty," he said again, watching the storm, "or find another optimist."

Silence, three seconds, filled with wind and hail and the small wet sounds of the world coming apart eight miles to his north. Then, warmly: "Forty-five, and a fuel advance. Because we believe in you, Xanthe. We've always believed in you. That's what makes us different from a bank. A bank needs you to succeed. We just need you."

The call ended with the little sun icon winking out, and Xanthe sat with it, driving, doing the arithmetic he always did and never finished: forty-five percent off a debt that had been engineered, he was fairly sure, to never fully clear. He'd borrowed eleven thousand from the Vellum Syndicate six years ago to buy a better radar rig and a faster truck. He'd paid back, by his own count, a little over nineteen. The balance had gone up twice. There had been fees he didn't remember agreeing to, and a restructuring he'd signed at two in the morning in a portside office while a cheerful man named Pell had poured him tea and told him about his own mother, and how she'd taught him that every debt was just a story two people agreed to keep telling.

He owed, as far as he could tell, everything. He owed, as far as they were concerned, whatever they said.

The tornado dropped at 15:31, out over empty rangeland, a gray rope writhing down and back up like something testing the ground and finding it wanting. Xanthe filmed it, logged it, sold the track to three buyers within the hour, and made enough to cover two weeks of the interest. Not the principal. Never the principal. He'd understood a long time ago that the interest was the product; the principal was just the hook. He was not a client of the Vellum Syndicate. He was a yield.

* * *

He stopped at dusk in a crossroads town with a name on the water tower and nothing else, and ate fried something at a diner where the radio played weather warnings on a loop and nobody listened. He spread his slate on the table and read what the public record said about Halcyon.

Eleven members. Geologists, a surveyor, a medic, a camp crew. Leader: Ivo Marsh, a name that rang a faint bell—academic type, the kind who gave interviews about *responsible extraction* and was tolerated by the concessionaires for his credentials. The expedition had filed its last position report from somewhere above the Kestrel Escarpment, four months ago, and then nothing. Not a distress call. Not a silence with a shape to it. Just nothing, like a page torn out.

The concession was held by a firm Xanthe didn't recognize, which meant it was held by a firm that didn't want to be recognized, which in this country meant one of three or four names that all shared lawyers. The permits were the interesting part. They had a hard expiry—nineteen days—and hard expiries were rare. Permits got extended, quietly, forever. A hard expiry meant someone *wanted* the clock. Someone wanted the valley to become a problem that had to be solved quickly, by someone deniable, before the paperwork caught up with the ground truth.

Xanthe had been that someone, in smaller ways, more times than he could count. He was good at being deniable. He was good at moving. He'd built his whole life on the principle that a man who never stops is a man who can never be caught, never be pinned, never be *known*. He had no fixed address. He had a truck, a rig, a slate full of buyer contacts, and a debt. He had, when he made himself take honest inventory, which was rare, nothing else. No one would notice if he stopped. That had seemed like freedom once. Lately it had been feeling like the other thing.

The diner door opened and let in a gust of warm rain-smell, and a man came in shaking water off his coat. Ordinary man, travel clothes, the flat competence of someone who drove for a living. He sat two stools down and ordered coffee and didn't look at Xanthe, which was itself a kind of looking.

"Storm got the Kestrel road," the man said to the room in general, to the cook. "Tree down across the cut at mile forty. You going upriver, you want to go around by the ford."

"Nobody goes upriver," the cook said.

"Somebody does," the man said, and now he did look at Xanthe, briefly, with an expression of professional assessment, the way you'd check a bridge before putting weight on it. "Somebody always does. That's how the money moves."

He finished his coffee, left coins, and went out into the rain. Xanthe sat with his cooling plate and understood that he'd just been measured, and passed, and that somewhere a file had a new line in it. That was how the Syndicate worked. Not threats. *Logistics.* Everyone in the country was a line in somebody's file, and the Vellum people just kept better files than God.

His slate buzzed. The smiling sun.

**VELLUM SYNDICATE, ACCOUNTS:** *Fuel advance approved. Barge contact: Sefa Okonkwo, Corvane docks, berth 9. She'll take you upriver. Departure at your convenience; expiry at the state's. We look forward to your consistency, Xanthe.*

He read it twice. *Departure at your convenience; expiry at the state's.* They wrote like that on purpose. He was sure of it. Somewhere in a portside office a cheerful man named Pell had taught a room full of accountants that menace aged best in formal language, like wine.

Xanthe paid for his food, went out into the rain, and stood a while under the diner's awning watching lightning walk the horizon over the escarpment country, far off, blue-white, silent, counting itself. Nineteen days. Eleven people. One river.

He thought about stopping. He actually stood there and thought about it, seriously, for the first time in years: sell the rig, sell the truck, take the loss, run somewhere the Vellum files didn't reach. He ran the numbers the way he ran storm tracks, fast and cold. The numbers said what they always said. There was no *out* that wasn't *through*. The debt was a storm system too; it had rotation; it had inflow; it would follow him across any flat in the world, and it would never, ever drop its funnel anywhere but on him.

So. Through, then. Upriver, into the quiet, to find out what eleven people had found that was worth a hard expiry and a deniable man.

He got in the truck. The compass on the dash—cheap, dented, a salvage-yard thing he'd kept through three rigs—swung and settled pointing north-northeast, toward the escarpment, toward Corvane, toward the water.

"Consistent," he said to it, and started the engine, and drove into the rain.
