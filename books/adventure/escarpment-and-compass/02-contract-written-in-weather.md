## 2. Contract Written in Weather

Corvane smelled of diesel and brine and money that had been laundered through at least three harbors before it reached the air. Xanthe came down off the coastal road at dusk with a storm building behind him like a debt collector he could actually outrun, and for eleven minutes he let himself enjoy that.

The supercell had been his all day — a beautiful, organized monster rolling up from the southeast, stacked like a cathedral someone had built out of spite. He'd tracked it from the flats on instruments older than his truck, calling readings to a broker in Vell who paid by the kilobar and never asked how he got them. Four hundred and sixty credits, wired before the first hail hit the windshield. It wasn't enough to matter. It was exactly enough to keep him moving.

His phone buzzed against the dash. The screen said PELL, VELLUM SYNDICATE, which was funny because Mr. Pell always insisted Xanthe save him under something friendlier. *Pell (friend)*, he'd suggested once, laughing at his own joke while two of his associates stood behind him not laughing.

Xanthe let it ring out. Then it buzzed again. Pell never called twice unless he wanted something, and what Pell wanted was usually delivered with a smile and an invoice.

He answered on the third ring of the second call, because ignoring Pell twice was a message, and messages to men like Pell were read very literally.

"Xanthe Dravenmoor." Pell's voice was warm as bathwater. "You sound windblown."

"I'm working."

"You're always working. That's what I admire about you. Most men in your situation would have slowed down by now. Gotten discouraged." A pause with weight in it. "Your balance is looking tired, my friend. Sixty-one thousand, four hundred, and change. The change grows daily. Interest is such a patient little animal."

"I know what I owe."

"Of course you do. You're good with numbers. That's why I'm calling." Papers rustled on his end, theatrical, unhurried. "An opportunity has crossed my desk. Not mine to offer, mind you — I'm just a well-wisher passing along good news. There's a salvage contract being tendered in Corvane. Halcyon Expedition. Gone quiet upriver, and somebody with permits wants them found before the paperwork turns into a pumpkin. They need a man who can read weather and country both. I thought: Xanthe. He's good with numbers, and he's desperate, and desperation keeps a man honest."

"Pell—"

"Nineteen days," Pell said pleasantly, "until the concession lapses. After that, whatever's up that river belongs to whoever's standing on it, and the standing gets violent. Go sign the contract. Earn real money. Shrink that ugly number we both hate so much." Another pause, softer now, almost tender. "Or don't, and I'll come find you myself, and we'll work out some other arrangement. You know I'd hate that. I consider you a friend."

The line went dead before Xanthe could decide whether to say something stupid.

He sat in the truck lot above the harbor with the storm coming in behind him and Corvane's lights smeared across the black water below, and he did the arithmetic he always did, the one that never changed. Running cost more than staying. Staying cost more than he had. Every road out of his life was toll roads now, and the Vellum Syndicate owned every booth.

So he drove down into the city to sign a contract he already knew he shouldn't.

* * *

The concession office occupied the third floor of a customs house that had been built by an empire that no longer existed and was now run by lawyers who behaved identically. Marble gone gray, brass gone green, corridors smelling of toner and rain-damp wool. Xanthe gave his name at the desk and was made to wait forty minutes under a framed map of the Kestrel valley — the whole drainage, the escarpment drawn in confident hatching, the concession blocks overlaid in colors so cheerful they looked like a children's game. Block Seven was outlined in yellow. Nineteen days left on its clock, though nothing on the map said so. Maps never told you when they were about to stop being true.

"Mr. Dravenmoor."

She was younger than he expected and dressed like the building: precise, faded, functional. Dark hair pinned with what looked like a pencil. She carried a folder thick enough to stun a man with, and she looked at him the way auditors look at receipts — not hostile, just counting.

"Odile Ferrant," she said. "Permits and recovery compliance. You're here about the Halcyon tender."

"I'm here because a friend suggested it."

Something moved behind her eyes — not quite a smile. "We all have friends like that. This way."

Her office was a converted records closet, one window overlooking the harbor cranes, walls of filing cabinets labeled in a system only she understood. She cleared a chair of binders and sat opposite him, opened the folder, and began without preamble.

"The Halcyon Expedition holds mineral survey rights to Block Seven, upper Kestrel drainage, including the escarpment approaches. Eleven members. They went dark thirty-four days ago. Last confirmed contact was a supply request from the forward camp, routine. Since then: nothing. No distress, no radio, no satellite ping. Their beacon transmits on schedule but returns no telemetry, which means either the unit's damaged or someone is powering it manually and sending nothing." She looked up. "Do you know what that means?"

"It means someone wants to be found by the right people and not the wrong ones."

"Or found slowly," she said. "On their own schedule." She slid a page across. "Your contract. Salvage and recovery of expedition personnel and property. You'll be the sole contracted party — no crew listed, no vessel listed yet. We understand you'll arrange those yourself."

"I'll need an advance."

"You'll get twenty percent on signature. The balance on delivery." Her finger tapped a clause halfway down the page. "Read this part carefully. 'Recovery' is defined as the return of all expedition members living or deceased, together with all survey data, samples, correspondence, and equipment belonging to the concession holder."

"All of it," Xanthe said. "Every scrap."

"Everything. If a member of the expedition is alive and unwilling to return, you are still obligated to recover them. If they've destroyed materials, you're obligated to account for the destruction. If they've sold materials—" she turned the page, tapped again, "—you're obligated to identify the buyer."

"That's not salvage," Xanthe said. "That's a warrant with a boat attached."

"That's a contract," Odile Ferrant said, "and you're free to walk out that door. There were four other applicants. Two had gunboats. One had a helicopter. You have a truck and a reputation for finding things in weather that kills other people's contractors, which frankly is the only reason you're still in the room." She folded her hands. "But I'll tell you something the others didn't get told, because the others struck me as men who'd use it badly. The concession expires in nineteen days. At expiry, if Halcyon hasn't filed a continuation — and they can't, they're missing — Block Seven reverts to the state, open claim. Every syndicate, every rival house, every man with a rifle and a lawyer files within the week. What your contract actually is, Mr. Dravenmoor, is a race. Find them, or find what happened to them, before the map changes underneath everyone."

"And if I find them and they don't want to come back?"

"Then you bring them anyway. Or you bring proof they can't be brought. Clause fourteen covers death; clause fifteen covers incapacity. There is no clause covering preference." She said it flatly, without apology, and he understood that the absence of the clause was the most honest thing in the document. Everything else was furniture around that hole.

He should have walked out. He thought about walking out — genuinely, for maybe four seconds, long enough to see the door and price the distance to it. Then he thought about sixty-one thousand four hundred and change, and the change growing daily, patient as mold, and about Pell's voice saying *we'll work out some other arrangement* in that tone that meant kneecaps or worse, delivered like a favor.

"How much is the balance?" he asked.

She named a figure. It was large enough to clear his debt twice over and small enough to prove how much whoever held these permits wanted the job done quietly. Money like that didn't buy rescue. It bought silence with legs.

He signed. His handwriting looked steadier than he felt, which he supposed was the whole trick of handwriting.

* * *

Afterward she didn't dismiss him. She poured coffee from a carafe that had clearly been reheated several times in its life, and handed him a cup, and stood at her window looking down at the harbor while the storm he'd chased all day finally arrived over Corvane and began eating the lights.

"You lied on your intake form," she said.

Xanthe kept the cup level. "Which field?"

"Question nine. 'Personal or financial interest in the Kestrel drainage region.' You marked no." She turned around. "You grew up in the valley settlements below the escarpment. Your mother's family is registered in the parish rolls at Lower Kestrel for three generations. I checked, because I check everything, and because men who mark *no* on question nine are usually the ones with the biggest yes hiding under it."

The rain started against the window, soft at first, then committed. Somewhere below, a crane light swung in the wind.

"So fire me," Xanthe said. "Rescind the contract."

"Why would I? A man with no stake runs when it gets hard. A man with a stake—" she shrugged, "—runs too, but slower, and looks back more often. Either way you signed, and the advance cleared, and clause twenty-two says rescission forfeits it to costs." She sat down again, and for a moment the clerk's mask slipped sideways and he saw the person underneath, tired and sharp and doing math of her own. "I'm not your enemy, Dravenmoor. I want you to understand what you've signed, because in nineteen days a great many people are going to discover what they've signed, metaphorically speaking, and most of them won't have read it."

"What did I sign, then? Say it plain."

She considered him. Outside, thunder rolled up the harbor like furniture being dragged across the sky.

"You signed to be the instrument that makes the paperwork true," she said. "Whatever's up that river — bodies, survivors, a discovery, a disaster — the concession holders need it recovered, contained, and filed before expiry, or their entire position dissolves. And the position isn't just minerals. Concessions like Block Seven are precedent. Whoever's claim stands there stands everywhere. So when I say *recovery may mean more than bodies*, I mean: there may be things upriver that certain parties need to never have existed, and other parties need desperately to exist, and you—" she pointed her pencil at him, almost gently, "—are the courier. Couriers get shot at from both directions. It's in the historical record."

"You're very cheerful about it."

"I'm very accurate about it. Cheer is extra." She gathered his copy of the contract, squared its edges against the desk. "Check-ins every forty-eight hours, radio channel seven, code phrase rotates daily — it's in your packet. Miss two check-ins and security deploys. Their name is Corvenne Solutions, and they are not subtle, and they do not distinguish between you and whatever killed the Halcyon Expedition. Try not to be standing near anything incriminating when they arrive."

"And if I find something that shouldn't be filed?" he asked. "Something that doesn't fit in a box on your shelf?"

For the first time, Odile Ferrant smiled properly, and it was the worst thing he'd seen all day, because it wasn't cruel — it was honest.

"Then you'll have discovered why question nine exists," she said. "Get some sleep, Mr. Dravenmoor. The river leaves at dawn whether you're on it or not. Pilot's name is Sefa — berth eleven, the green-hulled boat. Tell her Odile sent you and watch her face when you do. It'll teach you something the contract doesn't."

He stood, took his packet, and got as far as the door before she spoke again, quieter, almost to herself.

"Three generations in the parish rolls at Lower Kestrel," she said. "My grandmother was from Corvane docks. Nobody's ever offered me a home there. They just offer contracts." She straightened a paperclip that didn't need straightening. "Nineteen days. Don't be late."

* * *

The storm broke fully while he walked back to the truck lot, rain coming down in ropes, lightning stitching the harbor to the hills. Xanthe stood in it longer than he needed to, letting it wash the ink-feeling off his signing hand, and watched the cell track north on instinct alone — pressure dropping in his bad knee, wind swinging quartering out of the southeast. A big one. Maybe the biggest of the season. Any other year he'd have been in the truck already, chasing it for data, for credits, for the clean simple transaction of a storm observed and survived.

Instead he got in the cab, dried his hands on his shirt, and dialed the number scrawled inside the contract packet's cover. A woman answered on the second ring, background noise of water slapping hull and a radio playing something with a lot of accordion.

"Berth eleven."

"Odile sent me," Xanthe said. "Name's Dravenmoor. I need to be upriver tonight."

There was a pause, and then a laugh — short, unamused, knowing. The kind of laugh that had already priced him.

"Everybody Odile sends says it like that's a reference," the woman said. "Be here in an hour. Bring cash for fuel and don't bring anything that floats a lie bigger than this boat, because I will know."

The line went dead. Xanthe stared at the phone, then at the rain hammering the windshield, then at the yellow block on the folded map in his lap — Block Seven, nineteen days, everything he'd sworn he'd left behind waiting upriver like a bill he'd been dodging since he was old enough to dodge.

He started the engine and drove toward the water.
