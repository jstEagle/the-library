## 3. Upriver, Unanswered

The pilot's name was Sefa, and she quoted him a price that told him she already knew he was desperate.

"Two hundred a day, fuel included," she said, standing on the deck of her boat with her arms crossed like the river was hers personally and he was applying for permission. "Three hundred if you want me to go past the old checkpoints. Five hundred if you want me to lie about it after."

"Why would I need you to lie?"

"Because everyone who goes past the checkpoints needs me to lie." She had a face built for skepticism—narrow eyes, a mouth that sat crooked even at rest, like it was permanently mid-assessment. "You're the storm man. The one who sells weather."

"Among other things."

"Among other things," she repeated, tasting it. "Corvane's full of men who do things among other things. Half of them end up in the river. The other half end up owning the river. Which are you?"

"I'll let you know when I find out."

Something flickered across her face—not quite approval, but adjacent to it. She stuck out a hand calloused from rope work. "Sefa Okonkwo. Boat's called the *Mercy*. My grandmother named her. Irony was genetic in our family."

* * *

They left Corvane two hours before dawn, running lights off until they cleared the harbor chain, because Sefa said the port authority charged a departure fee for boats leaving before six and she'd been dodging it for eleven years with the dedication of a woman practicing a religion. Xanthe sat in the bow with his kit between his knees and watched the city's glow shrink behind them until it was just a smudge of orange on the water, like something burning far away.

He'd spent the night doing arithmetic instead of sleeping.

Nineteen days on the concession clock. Call it four days upriver, four back, assuming the river cooperated and nothing went wrong, which on this stretch of the Kestrel meant assuming the water stayed liquid and gravity kept working. That left eleven days to find an expedition that had gone silent somewhere in three hundred kilometers of limestone country, figure out what happened to them, and recover whatever *recovery* meant when Odile Ferrant said it with her careful clerk's mouth.

Eleven days. And every day he didn't answer the Syndicate's calls, the number attached to his debt grew like mold in a wet wall.

His phone buzzed against his chest at first light. He checked it out of habit before his brain could stop him.

**MR. PELL — VELLUM SYNDICATE: Morning, Xanthe! Beautiful day on the flats. Hope you're staying dry. Just a friendly reminder that interest compounds daily, not as punishment but as physics. Call when you have a moment. No rush!**

No rush. Pell signed everything like a greeting card. Xanthe had met him twice, both times in restaurants so nice the menus didn't list prices, and both times Pell had smiled with all forty-odd teeth and explained, patiently, that the Syndicate wasn't a bank or a gang but a *community of shared futures*, and that Xanthe's future was currently shared at a rate of nine percent monthly. The second time, Pell had ordered dessert for the table and mentioned, offhand, that a fisherman downriver had recently renegotiated his terms by losing a hand to a winch. Accident, probably. Rivers were dangerous.

Xanthe turned the phone off. Then, after a moment, turned it back on and put it in airplane mode, because turning it off entirely felt like something a man did right before he stopped existing, and he wasn't ready to test whether the world would notice.

"You're popular," Sefa said, materializing beside him with two cups of something black enough to qualify as industrial solvent. She nodded at the phone. "That's the third time since the bridge."

"Debt collectors."

"Vellum?"

"How'd you know?"

"Because Vellum collectors are the only ones who text like your aunt." She handed him a cup and drank hers standing, facing upstream. "My cousin owed them for two years. They never threatened him once. Just kept being *nice*. He said it was worse than threats. Threats you can fight. Nice is a trap with no edges."

"What happened to your cousin?"

"He moved inland. Grew vegetables. Lives under a different name." She shrugged. "Turns out you can outrun money if you're willing to become nobody. Most people aren't willing. Being somebody is expensive, but being nobody costs everything you've got."

Xanthe looked at the river. "I'm not planning to become nobody."

"No," Sefa agreed. "Nobody plans to. It just happens to people who keep going upriver."

* * *

The first checkpoint appeared around midday, and it was wrong in a way Xanthe couldn't immediately name.

It stood on a gravel point where the river narrowed—a concrete blockhouse, a boom gate that had once stretched across the channel on chains, a flagpole stripped bare. Concession infrastructure. The Halcyon Expedition would have passed through here six weeks ago; the mineral concession required all upstream traffic to log in and out, and the checkpoint logs were how Odile's firm tracked who was where on their paper territory.

The boom gate was down. The blockhouse windows were dark. And there was no one.

"Checkpoint Three," Sefa said, easing the *Mercy* past the gate at a distance that was either caution or contempt. "Used to be four men stationed here. Rotating shifts, company pay, better rations than anyone on the river. Now look at it."

"When did it empty out?"

She was quiet for a moment, reading the water ahead. "I came through in the spring. Still manned then. Came through again three weeks ago—" she lifted her chin at the blockhouse, "—like that. Door open. Stove cold. Logbook still on the desk, last entry dated the same week your expedition went quiet."

"The same week?"

"Give or take." She glanced at him sideways. "You didn't know that."

"I knew the expedition stopped transmitting. Nobody said anything about the checkpoints."

"Nobody says anything about anything upriver. That's the whole business model." She swung the wheel and took them into the deeper channel. "Company pulls its people out fast and quiet when something spooks them. Question is what spooked them. Weather doesn't clear a checkpoint. Bandits don't either—bandits leave wreckage, and there's no wreckage. This looks like..." She searched for the word.

"Evacuation," Xanthe said.

"That's the word."

They passed two more checkpoints before dark. Both abandoned, both orderly, both wrong. At the second one, someone had left a mug on the gatepost, upright, half-full of rainwater, as though the occupant had stepped away for a moment six weeks ago and simply never come back. Xanthe stared at it as they slid past and felt the particular unease of a scene arranged rather than abandoned.

That night they tied up in a cutbank willow stand, and Sefa cooked rice with dried fish and too much chili, and the radio produced nothing but static in every direction. Not dead static—the living kind, thick and layered, like the atmosphere itself had opinions it couldn't finish expressing.

"Solar maximum," Xanthe said, more to himself than to her. "Plus the escarpment. All that limestone plays hell with propagation. Signals bounce around in the rock and come out scrambled."

"You can actually read that stuff? The storms?"

"It's the only thing I ever learned to read properly." He turned the dial through the bands, listening. "Pressure gradients, wind shear, the way birds go quiet before a mesocyclone drops. People think chasing is about driving fast toward danger. It's mostly about sitting very still and noticing what the sky is trying to tell you."

"And what's the sky telling you now?"

He listened. The static had a texture he didn't love—a low, rhythmic pulse underneath the white noise, almost like breathing. Probably ionospheric. Probably nothing.

"The sky's telling me we're a long way from anywhere that can help us," he said.

"Good," Sefa said. "I hate audiences."

* * *

On the third morning they found the cache.

Sefa smelled it first—or didn't smell it, exactly, but noticed the absence of smell, the wrongness of a riverbank with no bird noise on it. She backed the throttle down and let the *Mercy* drift while Xanthe stood up in the bow with binoculars.

The supply cache sat fifty meters up a shingle spit, tucked under an overhang of rock where the escarpment's foothills began shouldering toward the water. It had been a proper one—aluminum cases stacked and tarped, the kind of installation an expedition builds to resupply from without hauling everything. The tarp was burned. Not torn, not weathered: burned, melted onto the cases beneath it in black sheets, and the cases themselves were scorched to a dull rainbow bloom of heat-discolored metal.

But not destroyed. That was the thing that made Xanthe's neck prickle as they grounded the boat and waded ashore. A fire hot enough to melt a tarp onto aluminum should have cooked the contents. Instead the cases, when he pried one open with a multi-tool, held rope coils fused lightly at their edges, canned food swollen but sealed, fuel cans scorched outside and intact within. Burned just enough to mark. Not enough to ruin.

Stamped on every case lid, crisp under the soot: **HALCYON EXPEDITION — CONCESSION 44-K — PROPERTY OF** and then a permit number Odile would recognize on sight.

"This isn't looting," Sefa said. She'd found the fire pit—a ring of stones, wood ash long gone gray, and in the center of it the charred remains of paper. Notebooks, maybe. Maps. Whatever burned there had burned completely, fed and tended, while ten feet away the supplies sat in their half-melted cocoon essentially untouched. "Looters take. Vandals break. This is..." She crouched over the ash, not touching it. "This is a message written in the grammar of property. *We were here. We chose what to burn and what to leave.*"

"Who leaves perfectly good rope and takes the notebooks?"

"Somebody who wanted the supplies to be found." She looked up at him. "And wanted whatever was in the paper to stay lost."

Xanthe walked the perimeter while she checked the boat lines. There were boot prints in the shingle, weeks old, weathered soft—several sets, heading upriver. One set, smaller, heading back downriver alone. He stood a long time looking at that single line of prints going the wrong way, away from the expedition's route, toward Corvane, toward answers, and wondered which member of the Halcyon Expedition had decided the mission was over and walked out without telling the others. Or whether they'd told them. Or whether *telling* was still a thing that happened between those people by the end.

The radio on his hip crackled. He froze.

Static—but shaped static, a carrier wave pushing through the escarpment interference, faint as a voice heard through a wall. He pulled the handset up and thumbed the gain.

"...coordinates are... say again, coordinates..." A man's voice, ragged, clipped by distance and stone. "...do not approach the... I say again, do not..." Then the pulse of that low rhythmic interference rose up and swallowed it whole, and the band dissolved back into white noise.

Sefa had come up beside him. She'd heard it too. Neither of them spoke for a moment.

"Halcyon's frequency?" she asked finally.

"Expedition band, yeah." His mouth was dry. "Six weeks of silence, and now a fragment. Either someone's transmitting from upriver on their equipment..."

"Or someone's using their frequency to make sure anyone listening hears exactly those words." She was watching the treeline above the spit, where the limestone hills went up into shadow. "*Do not approach.* From a man whose employers paid us to find him. You want to talk about messages written in grammar?"

Xanthe looked at the burned cache, the single set of footprints walking away, the wall of green and stone rising northward where the signal had come from. Somewhere up there was Ivo Marsh, or what was left of him, or what wanted to be mistaken for him. Somewhere up there was whatever the Halcyon Expedition had found that made a company evacuate its checkpoints and a syndicate start smiling harder.

He thought about Pell's texts, warm as a card from an aunt. He thought about Odile saying *recovery* like a word with a trapdoor in it.

"We camp here tonight," he said. "Upstream at first light."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, that crooked mouth doing its assessment. "You know what's funny, storm man? Everyone who hires me says some version of *we push on*. Nobody ever says *we turn around*. I've been running this river eleven years and I've never once taken a paying customer home early." She turned back toward the boat. "Starting to wonder what that says about the river. Or about the customers."

That night the static pulsed again, slow and regular, and Xanthe lay in the *Mercy*'s narrow bunk listening to it and telling himself it was the ionosphere.

It sounded like breathing. It sounded like something under the limestone, keeping time, waiting to see how many of them would come up the river before it decided what to do.

At 0300, his phone—still in airplane mode, still dark—buzzed once against his chest anyway.

He stared at it for a long time before he worked up the nerve to look.

One message. No sender. Four words:

**THE GRAVE IS EMPTY.**
