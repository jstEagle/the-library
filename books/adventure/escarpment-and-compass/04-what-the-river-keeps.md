## 4. What the River Keeps

The river taught me everything I know about lying, and the first lesson was this: never lie with your whole face.

I'd been running cargo up and down the Vell for eleven years before Xanthe Dravenmoor ever stepped aboard my boat, and in that time I'd learned to read men the way I read water — by what they disturbed on the surface. Most passengers were easy. Concession men sat too straight and talked too much about themselves. Surveyors asked questions they already knew the answers to. Runners like me kept their eyes moving and their mouths shut.

Dravenmoor was none of these. He stood at my rail for six hours watching the banks go by without once asking how fast we were going or when we'd arrive, which told me he was either a professional or a man running from something specific enough that distance didn't matter, only direction. The storm gear gave him away as a professional. The way he flinched whenever my radio crackled gave him away as the other thing.

So when we found the cache burned black at Mile 114, I did what any sensible pilot does with a passenger she doesn't trust: I lied to him first.

"Lightning strike," I said, before he'd even finished climbing down to it. "Happens every dry season."

He crouched at the edge of the scorched circle where a supply depot had been — Halcyon Expedition stenciled in white on three surviving crates, or the corners of them — and he didn't answer right away. That was my first mistake, giving him time. My second was looking at the ground myself.

Because it wasn't lightning. I knew it wasn't lightning the moment I saw it, the same way you know a drowned man from a swimming one. Lightning burns wild. It licks outward, takes the tallest thing, leaves a starburst of scorch and a smell like struck flint. This fire had been fed. It had been built low and hot and contained, stacked fuel around the crates so the heat went *in*, down into the stores, cooking everything sealed inside until the tins swelled and the grain roasted and the medicine cooked to sludge. The tarps were melted onto the metal in smooth sheets, not torn away. Somebody had wanted the contents ruined but the evidence standing. A lightning strike is an accident of weather. This was an argument.

"Sure," Dravenmoor said finally. He said it the way you say *sure* when you're being polite to a child who's explained something wrong. Then he stood up, dusted his knees, and looked at me with those flat gray eyes, and I understood I'd just made a deposit in a bank I hadn't opened yet. He now knew I lied. What he didn't know was why, or about what, or whether I'd lie to him again tomorrow.

Good, I thought. Let him wonder. Wondering keeps a man honest, or at least keeps him careful, and careful men live longer on this river than honest ones.

We loaded what was worth loading — two intact crates, a signal lamp, a coil of copper wire — and pushed on upriver past the old checkpoint at Kessel Bend, its flagpole bare, its window shuttered, a drift of silt against the door like the building itself had given up and lain down. No challenge call came over the band. Nothing had come over the band all day except static, thick as river fog, the kind of static that isn't empty but crowded, like a room full of people breathing just out of earshot.

* * *

That night we tied off in a cutbank below a stand of ironwoods, and I got out the cards, because there are exactly two ways to learn a man on a boat, and the other one takes weeks.

"Five-card draw," I said. "Stakes are answers."

He raised an eyebrow. We were two days upriver from Corvane, past the last working beacon, and the dark out here has weight to it. You can feel it pressing on the canvas overhead.

"I'm listening," he said.

"You ask me a question, I answer true or I fold. Same for you. Fold costs nothing but the hand." I shuffled. My hands know shuffling better than they know most things. "But if you answer and I catch you lying, you owe me double."

"And how do you catch me?"

"The river catches you," I said. "It always does. Sooner or later everybody tells the truth to the water."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was the first thing his face had done since Corvane that wasn't calculation, and I filed it away.

We played. He lost the first hand and asked me how long I'd run this stretch. True answer: since I was nineteen, first with my uncle, then alone after the flood year took the uncle and half the village docks. I answered it and won the second hand.

Then he won, and he thought about his question for a long time, turning the cards over in his fingers like he could read them by touch.

"Why did you lie about the cache?"

There it was. Not *did you lie* — he'd skipped the cheap question, gone straight past the door to the man behind it. I'll give him this: he doesn't waste motion. Storm chasers can't afford to.

I could have folded. Folding cost nothing. But folding tells a man everything too, and I wasn't ready to tell him everything, so I played it another way.

"Because you were going to say it was lightning," I said, "and I was curious how long you'd let me believe you believed me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only free one you're getting." I dealt. "Your turn comes around when you win it."

He won it two hands later, and this time he didn't hesitate at all, which meant he'd been carrying the question since morning.

"What did you run, before this? For the concessionaires. You recognized that burn pattern too fast for a stranger to it."

The cards made a small sound against each other as I squared them. Out on the water, something big turned over — a fish, a log, the river rearranging its furniture — and settled.

"Gravel," I said. "Explosives. Sometimes people who preferred not to be logged at checkpoints." I laid my discard down. "Three years. Corvane Minerals, then Vellum subcontractors when Corvane sold the routes. I moved whatever fit in the hold and didn't ask what it was for, because asking is how you end up in the hold."

"And the burn pattern?"

"Deliberate," I said. "Fed fire, low stack, covered. It's how you destroy supplies without destroying the proof they existed. Insurance claims, contract disputes — you want the crates identifiable and the contents dead. I've seen it twice before. Once on a rival's depot up the Tarnish fork, once on—" I stopped.

"On?"

"A survey team that found something in the north benches and reported it instead of selling it privately." I met his eyes. "Their report reached Corvane. They didn't."

The wind moved through the ironwoods above us, and for a while neither of us dealt.

"So whoever burned Halcyon's cache," he said slowly, "knew the trade."

"Knew it well enough to teach it." I tapped the deck. "Which means either the expedition burned its own stores—"

"To fake their deaths or cover a retreat. Or—"

"Or somebody followed them upriver and tidied up behind them." I shrugged. "River keeps what it keeps. Question is what you're going to do when we find the rest of it, because we will find the rest of it, and men who burn caches don't usually stop at caches."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he said, "You've been deciding whether to put me off the boat since Corvane."

"Yes."

"And?"

I dealt the hand before answering. Five cards each, face down, futures unknown. That's the whole game, really. Everything else is nerve.

"And you paid half up front," I said, "and you haven't touched the manifest lockbox, and when the collectors called you in Corvane you walked away from the phone instead of toward it, which nobody in debt ever does unless they're planning to be somewhere the phone can't reach." I picked up my cards. "So I figure you're either the most dangerous man on this river or the most honest one wearing a liar's coat, and honestly, Captain Dravenmoor, I've carried both kinds, and the second kind pays better."

"I'm not a captain."

"No," I agreed. "You're cargo that talks."

That got the almost-smile again, fuller this time, gone in a second. We played out the hand. He won it with a bluff so ugly I nearly applauded — bet hard on nothing, stared me down with those dead-gray eyes like the cards didn't matter because he'd already decided the outcome somewhere else entirely. Storm-chaser nerve. You can't teach it and you can't trust it, but it wins pots.

"Question," he said, raking in nothing, since our stakes were answers and he'd already spent his win getting them. "Why'd you quit running for them?"

And there it was, the question under the questions, the one I'd been circling all night like a snag in current. I could have folded. My finger was literally on the cards to push them away.

Instead I heard myself say: "Because they asked me to move a family once. Upriver. Four people, quiet crossing, no log entry. And I told myself it was resettlement, because that's what the paperwork said, and paperwork is very soothing when you read it in the right light." I set the cards down. "Six months later I ran the same route back and saw the settlement they'd been moved *from*. There wasn't one anymore. Just graded earth and a new survey marker. And I understood that the paperwork hadn't been describing a move. It had been describing an erasure, and I'd been the ferry that made it possible."

The static on the radio breathed in and out behind us, patient as tide.

"After that I took honest freight. Fish, fuel, mail. Paid worse. Slept better." I gathered the deck. "Mostly."

"Mostly."

"Well." I shuffled. "Then a storm chaser with syndicate debt and a salvage contract walks onto my dock offering triple rate to go look for the exact kind of expedition that gets erased, and here I am." I fanned the cards at him. "Draw."

He drew. We played another hour, maybe two, and somewhere in there the game stopped being a test and started being a game, which is rarer than you'd think, and worth more. I learned he counted cards without meaning to, that he'd grown up somewhere flat where you could see weather coming for a hundred miles and he'd never gotten over the luxury of it, and that when he talked about storms his voice changed — loosened, like a knot coming undone — and that he hated the syndicate with the specific cold hatred of a man who owes them, which is the purest hatred there is because it's hatred with interest compounding daily.

And he learned things about me too, though fewer than he thinks. That's the second lesson the river teaches: you can tell a man the truth about yourself and still keep the important part back, because the truth is a river and there's always water under the water.

* * *

Near midnight the static broke.

Not cleared — *broke*, like ice cracking, a voice punching through from somewhere close and high and desperate. We both came off our seats. Dravenmoor got to the set first, hands steady on the dial in a way that surprised me, tuning through the wash until the voice resolved:

"—repeat, grid nine-something, do *not* descend without—" A burst of noise ate half the words. "—Marsh. Ivo Marsh. If anyone receives, the vault is—" Static slammed down like a hatch.

Then, fainter, threaded through the interference like a wire through cloth: coordinates. Numbers, read slow and deliberate, the way you read numbers when you know someone might be writing them down and you need them exact. Twice through. Then nothing but the crowd-of-breathers static, and beneath our hull the river going on with its own long sentence.

Dravenmoor wrote the numbers on his forearm in pen, by the lamp, lips moving. When he looked up, his face had changed. Not afraid. Worse than afraid — *addressed*. Like a letter he'd sent years ago had come back with someone else's handwriting on it.

"That's the expedition leader," he said. "Halcyon's lead. Odile's file says missing presumed dead, forty-one days."

"He doesn't sound dead."

"He sounds like a man reading coordinates to strangers he doesn't trust." Dravenmoor flexed his hand, the one holding the pen. "Which raises the question of who he *does* trust, and why he's broadcasting to anyone with a receiver when the people following him have receivers too."

"Maybe," I said, "he's not broadcasting to find help. Maybe he's broadcasting bait." I nodded at the dark beyond the canvas, where the river slid past invisible and enormous. "You hang a lantern over deep water, you find out real quick what's swimming underneath."

He was quiet a moment. Then he said, "You could still put me ashore."

"I could."

"At Kessel Bend. Walk back down. Nobody would blame you — this stopped being a salvage job about four miles ago."

I looked at him sitting there in the lamplight with coordinates inked on his arm and syndicate wolves somewhere behind us and something ahead of us that burned supply caches like a housekeeper burning letters, and I thought about a family on a quiet crossing, and a settlement that wasn't there anymore, and every piece of honest freight I'd hauled trying to sand the shape of that memory off the inside of my skull.

"Kessel Bend's flooded this season anyway," I said. "Bad landing. Whole east bank's soft." I began racking the cards in their box, snap snap snap. "Besides, you're cargo that talks. Cargo rides to delivery."

"And if the delivery kills us?"

"Then I'll want my triple rate refunded," I said, and doused the lamp, and in the dark we listened to the river keep its secrets, both of us pretending we hadn't just decided to go find out what they were.
