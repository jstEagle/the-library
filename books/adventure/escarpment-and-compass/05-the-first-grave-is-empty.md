## 5. The First Grave Is Empty

The forward camp sat in a bend of the river where the current slowed to think, and from the water it looked almost peaceful — three canvas shelters, a survey tripod still standing, a cook tent with its flap tied open like a hand offering something.

Then Sefa killed the outboard and the silence came in, and the camp stopped looking peaceful and started looking wrong.

"Nobody left in a hurry," Sefa said. She kept her voice low, the way people do in churches and crime scenes, and I was starting to understand she didn't much distinguish between the two. "Flap's tied. That's a tied-off camp. You tie off when you're coming back."

"Or when you're leaving something behind you don't want the wind to have."

She looked at me. "That's a cheerful way to put it."

"I'm a cheerful man. Ask my creditors."

We grounded the boat on the mud shelf below the camp and I went up the bank first, which was either bravery or the fact that I weighed less than she did. The grass had grown through the guy-lines of the nearest shelter, green thread stitching canvas to earth. I put my hand on the flap and pushed it open and the smell hit me — rot and river and old smoke, the smell of a place that had been alive and then wasn't.

Inside, the expedition's equipment sat in neat rows.

That was the part that got under my skin. Not chaos. Not the aftermath of violence or panic or flood. *Order.* Bedrolls rolled. Instruments cased and stacked. A theodolite in its foam cradle with the straps still buckled. A row of sample jars along one wall, labeled in a careful engineer's hand — *K-14, K-15, K-16* — the last one half-filled with gray limestone dust and nothing after it, as if the numbering had simply stopped mattering.

"Xanthe." Sefa had gone to the cook tent. "You want to see this."

The food stores were ruined. Not looted — ruined. Sacks of rice and flour split open and left to the rain, tins pried and emptied onto the ground, a crate of dried fruit gone to a black, glistening sludge that had its own weather system by now. Flies rose off it in a sheet when I pulled the tarp back.

"Vermin?" I said.

Sefa crouched, picked a split sack out of the muck, and turned it over. The cut was clean. Knife work, one stroke, deliberate. She did the next one. Same. She looked up at me with the flat expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Somebody destroyed their own food," she said. "All of it. Enough to feed a dozen people for two months, and they cut every sack and walked away from it."

"Maybe they were leaving and didn't want anyone following them to live off it."

"Maybe." She dropped the sack. "Or maybe they weren't going anywhere."

* * *

We spent two hours going through the camp the way I used to read storm cells — systematically, without hope of a simple answer, looking for the structure underneath the surface. Sefa took the survey shelter. I took the mess tent, which had a folding table, a lantern with a cracked globe, and a logbook.

The logbook was Halcyon standard issue, green canvas, the expedition stamp on the cover half worn away. The last entries were in Ivo Marsh's hand — I recognized it from the contract documents, the tight, upright script of a man who'd been educated by someone who believed handwriting was character. The entries started normal. Latitudes. Barometric readings. Notes on the limestone karst formations upriver, the sinkholes, the caves.

Then the entries got shorter. Not less careful — *shorter*, as if the man writing them was rationing himself.

*Day 41. K-22 confirmed. Do not record details in this book.*

*Day 43. T. wants to go down again. I said no. She went anyway. She was right to.*

*Day 45. We have discussed it. All of us. There is no version of this that we can carry out of the valley. Anyone we give it to will use it. Anyone we keep it from will come for it. I have told the team the truth: that we are not an expedition anymore. We are a lock.*

*Day 46. Dug the grave. Better to be ready.*

That was the last entry.

I sat with it for a long time. Outside, Sefa moved around the camp, and the river made its patient noise, and somewhere above us the escarpment stood in its long gray wall with whatever it was holding pressed against its teeth.

*We are a lock.*

I've been paid to find things my whole working life. Storm data, downed aircraft, a senator's runaway daughter, once a prize bull with a taste for trespassing. You learn that everything lost is lost for a reason, and the reason is usually boring — money, weather, bad decisions, love. You learn to stop wondering about the reasons because the wondering doesn't pay.

But nobody digs a grave in advance unless they're expecting to need it. And nobody writes *better to be ready* unless they're expecting the kind of death you can prepare for. And nobody — no sane, salaried, permit-carrying survey crew — destroys their own food supply and then walks into the hills, unless what they found was worth more to them than staying alive comfortably.

Or unless they expected not to stay alive at all.

I copied Marsh's last three entries into my notebook, word for word, and then I sat there looking at my own handwriting next to his and felt the strange vertigo of reading a dead man's mind through a page. Except I didn't know he was dead. That was the thing. The grave was empty.

* * *

We found it behind the camp, up a low rise where the ground turned to gravel and the trees thinned. Sefa spotted it first — a rectangle of disturbed earth, the edges already softening with rain, a field marker at the head made from a survey stake with a strip of red cloth tied to it. The cloth had faded to the color of an old wound.

The grave was dug. Neatly. Human-sized. Lined at the bottom with canvas, the way you'd line it if you cared about the person going into it.

And empty.

"Six feet," Sefa said. She stood at the edge with her hands in her pockets, not stepping closer. "Maybe deeper. They took their time with it."

"Somebody was supposed to go in here."

"Somebody they knew by name." She nodded at the stake. "You don't line a grave with canvas for a stranger."

I crouched at the edge, careful not to touch the earth. The canvas lining was dry. The pit had never held water, never held anything. Rain had been falling on this camp for weeks and the grave was still clean, still waiting, like a bed made for a guest who kept not arriving.

"They dug it on day forty-six," I said. "Whatever Marsh found, he found it before that. He had time to think about what it meant. And what he decided to prepare for was a burial."

"His own?"

"Could be. Could be any of them. Could be whoever came looking." I stood. "You've run cargo for these concession companies. You ever hear of a survey crew that just — stopped?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. The river talked behind us.

"I heard about Halcyon," she said finally. "Everyone on this river heard about Halcyon. Eleven people, good equipment, good permits, and then nothing. No distress call, no bodies, no salvage on the market. You know what that costs? A full expedition kit? Somebody would have fenced it by now if it was floating around. Gear like that doesn't just vanish."

"Unless it didn't vanish. Unless it's sitting in a camp with its straps buckled."

"Right. Which means the people who left it didn't need it anymore." She turned and looked up the valley, toward the escarpment, toward the coordinates that were still a red smudge on the chart in my cabin. "Or couldn't take it. Or wouldn't."

"And the food?"

"Xanthe." She said my name the way she'd been saying it more often lately — not as a label but as an address, a place she was trying to reach. "I burned a cache once. For the Ferrant people, back when I was young and the money was fast. Concession cargo that needed to not exist. You know how you do it?"

"You burn it."

"You burn it *wet*, so it smokes all day and every checkpoint up and down the river sees it and logs it. *Cache fire at mile sixty, total loss.* It's the oldest trick there is. You make the loss public so nobody looks for what was lost." She looked at the ruined cook tent. "This isn't that. This is quiet. This is a crew feeding the ground instead of themselves. That's not a trick. That's a *choice*, and it's the choice of people who decided the food mattered less than whatever they were keeping."

I didn't have an answer for that. So I did what I do, which is turn toward the next piece of data.

* * *

The radio was in the survey shelter, a Halcyon field set bolted to a folding desk, and it should have been dead. The battery bank beside it was flat — Sefa checked, said the word "dead" with the finality of a doctor — and the antenna wire had come down in one corner, draped over the shelter's ridge pole like a strand of hair.

I was cataloguing the sample jars when it crackled.

Not a voice at first. A hiss, a pop, the sound of the atmosphere remembering it had opinions. I froze with a jar labeled *K-19* in my hand and listened, because a dead radio making noise is either a miracle or a warning, and in my experience the two travel together.

Sefa came to the flap. "That's not us."

"I know."

The hiss resolved, briefly, into something rhythmic. Then it fell apart. Then, for maybe four seconds — four seconds I will hear for the rest of my life — it was a voice.

"—do not come up by the river, I'm saying it plainly, do not— " static, chewing the words, " — grid seven-four-one-nine, the *old* markers, not the new ones, they'll have moved the new ones — Tamsin, if you're hearing this, the count is still nine, you understand me, the count is still *nine* —"

Then nothing. The hiss collapsed back into dead air, and the radio sat there on its desk looking like what it was: a machine that had no business speaking.

Sefa had gone very still. "Battery bank's flat," she said, as if saying it again would make it true.

"Capacitor," I said. My mouth was dry. "Storm charge. There's enough atmospheric bleed in a front like the one that came through last night to — I don't know. Maybe. For a few seconds."

"You don't know."

"I don't know." I set the jar down carefully. "Sefa. That was Ivo Marsh."

"You know his voice?"

"I've read his handwriting for two days. It was the same man. Tight, controlled, spelling things out for somebody he expected to be listening." I looked at the radio. "And he gave coordinates."

"Grid seven-four-one-nine." Sefa said it flat, but I saw her hand go to her pocket where she kept her chart pencil, the way some people touch a talisman. "That's not on our permit map."

"No."

"That's *past* it."

"Yes."

We stood there in the shelter with the dead radio and the labeled jars and the whole quiet, ordered camp around us, and I did the math I'd been avoiding since Corvane. Nineteen days when I signed. Whatever had bled away since — the river days, the search days — I had maybe fourteen left before Odile Ferrant's concession lapsed and whatever happened next happened without me. Fourteen days to find a man who had turned himself into a lock, guarding something he thought nobody should carry out of the valley.

And a count. *The count is still nine.* Nine what? Nine people? Halcyon had fielded eleven. Nine still what — alive? Present? *Counted?* The words of a man rationing himself, saying only what a listener needed and no more, because he knew the air between here and the world was full of ears.

"Xanthe." Sefa was looking at me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before. Not the card-player's flatness. Something more careful. "That grave. You said somebody was supposed to go in it."

"Yeah."

"What if it's worse than that?" She nodded back toward the rise, toward the red cloth on its stake. "What if somebody dug it for *themselves*, changed their mind, and went up that mountain instead — and everything since, the food, the silence, the radio — is them making sure nobody else ends up in it?"

Outside, the light was going long and gold, and the escarpment stood against it like a wall built by something with more patience than people. Somewhere up in that gray stone was grid seven-four-one-nine, and a count of nine, and a man who had begged the air not to let anyone come up by the river.

I picked up my notebook. My hand was steady, which surprised me.

"Then we don't go up by the river," I said.

Sefa almost smiled. "And the other thing he said? *They'll have moved the new markers?* Moved by who?"

That was the question I'd been circling all day without landing on it, and now it landed. Somebody had come to this valley after Halcyon went silent. Somebody had set new markers on the approach to whatever Marsh was guarding — markers meant to steer searchers wrong, or steer them *somewhere*. The syndicate hadn't sent collectors yet; Pell's people would have come in loud, and I'd have smelled the gun oil from a mile off. This was quieter. This was somebody who wanted the valley to keep its secret, or wanted to be the only one holding the key.

Either way, we weren't the first ones looking. And the first ones had left the grave empty.

"We find the old markers," I said, "and we find out who moved the new ones. Pack what's worth carrying. We're burning daylight."

Sefa looked at the empty grave one more time as we came down the rise — just a glance, quick, the way you check a mirror — and then she turned her back on it and walked toward the boat, and I followed, and the valley let us go a little further in without saying why.
