## 6. Collectors on the Water

The gunboat came upriver at midday, when the light was flat and the water looked like poured tin.

Xanthe saw it first as a sound—a low diesel thrum that didn't belong to Sefa's engine or to any of the fishing skiffs they'd passed since the burned cache. He was on the bow with the barometer cradled in his lap, taking his third reading of the morning, and the pressure had been doing something strange all day: not falling so much as hesitating, like a man at a door he wasn't sure he wanted to knock on. Storm weather. Or the atmospheric equivalent of what was now rounding the bend two kilometers downstream with a mounted autocannon on its foredeck.

"Sefa," he said, without turning. "Company."

She was already cutting the engine. That was the thing about Sefa—he'd learned it over cards three nights ago, when she'd taken every chip he had with a face like a closed ledger—she read water the way he read sky. She'd heard it before him. She always heard things before him and never said so.

"Vellum?" she asked.

"Who else runs a gunboat this far up?"

"Rival concessionaires. Poachers. The state, if the state still cared." She eased the boat toward the bank, into the shadow of a drowned willow whose roots held the mud together like knuckles. "You want to run?"

He did the math without wanting to. Current speed, their draft, the bend downstream that would buy them maybe four minutes before the gunboat's sightlines opened up. Four minutes against a vessel that could do thirty knots in open channel. The math came back the way math always came back for men like him: insufficient.

"No," he said. "Running tells them we have something."

"We don't have anything."

"Not yet." He watched the gray shape grow around the bend. "But they don't know that, and I'd rather keep them not knowing."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment—the look she gave bad hands before betting on them anyway—and then she killed the engine entirely and let the boat drift into the willow shade, quiet as a held breath.

* * *

Mr. Pell came aboard alone.

That was the performance of it, Xanthe understood immediately. Two other boats hung back in the channel, each carrying enough armed men to make the point without stating it, but Pell crossed the gap between vessels on a springing plank like a man visiting a neighbor, one hand raised in cheerful greeting, his shoes immaculate against the wet deck. He was a broad, pink-faced man in a linen suit that had no business surviving this climate, and he smiled the way weather smiled before hail.

"Master Dravenmoor." His voice carried warmth the way a furnace carried heat—genuinely, mechanically, with no particular regard for what it burned. "What are the odds? Well. Not odds, actually. We've been tracking your transponder since Corvane. But it's nicer to say odds, isn't it? Makes the world feel generous."

"Pell." Xanthe didn't get up from the supply crate where he sat. Standing would be conceding something; he hadn't decided yet what he could afford to concede. "You're a long way from your office."

"I'm a long way from everything I love." Pell settled onto the opposite crate uninvited, arranging himself with the contentment of a man who had never once been unwelcome anywhere, because unwelcome was a word that applied to poorer people. "But you know how it is. Work calls. And you—" he gestured at the boat, the river, the escarpment rising green and enormous to the north, "—you've found work."

"I'm chasing weather."

"You're chasing weather *upriver*, past three abandoned checkpoints, following the exact route a certain expedition took eleven weeks ago before it stopped answering its radio." Pell produced a flask from inside his jacket, unscrewed it, offered it. Xanthe declined. Pell drank anyway, savoring. "Xanthe. May I call you Xanthe? I feel we're past formality. You owe my employers four hundred and twelve thousand, six hundred marks. I have personally reviewed your file. There's a photograph of you in it from eight years ago—you looked thinner, and happier, which is the usual direction people go. My employers sent me to collect that debt. Any method. Any timeline. They were quite flexible, which should frighten you more than a deadline would."

"And instead you're drinking my air and offering me a drink I don't want."

"Instead," Pell said, "I'm going to make you rich."

Behind him, at the rail, Sefa went very still. Xanthe kept his eyes on Pell because looking at Sefa right now would be telling Pell something.

"Here's the situation as I understand it," Pell continued, warm as bathwater. "The Halcyon Expedition found something in these hills. Something valuable enough that its sponsors paid for permits, security, and silence. Something valuable enough that when the expedition went quiet, nobody reported it missing for nine days—which means somebody hoped it would stay missing. Now, my employers—the Vellum Syndicate, fine people, terrible table manners—hold paper on you. Your debt is real, Xanthe. It accrues. In fourteen months it doubles. In three years you'll owe them a life, and they'll take it, and they'll send someone charming to do it, possibly me, and I'll be sad about it, genuinely, I have a capacity for sadness that surprises people."

"Get to it."

"The offer." Pell leaned forward, and for one moment the affability thinned and Xanthe saw the machinery underneath, the gears turning behind the smile, oiled and patient. "Whatever Halcyon found—hand it to me. Today, tomorrow, this week. Before Odile Ferrant's employers learn it exists. Do that, and the ledger burns. All of it. Four hundred twelve thousand marks, gone, forgiven, forgotten. You walk away clean. First time in years, I imagine. Clean feels good, Xanthe. I recommend it."

* * *

The river moved past them, brown and unhurried, carrying its secrets seaward one grain at a time.

Xanthe made himself think slowly, which was hard, because his pulse wanted to think fast. Fast thinking was how he'd signed the syndicate's paper in the first place—four years ago, after the storm season that ate his equipment and his partner both, when a smiling man in an office had said *we believe in you* and meant *we own you*. He knew this shape of conversation. He knew how it ended for people who said yes quickly.

"What exactly do you think they found?" he asked.

"I don't know." Pell spread his hands, delighted by his own honesty. "That's the beauty of it. Could be gold. Could be survey data worth millions. Could be nothing, and you're wasting my time, in which case I've enjoyed the cruise. What I know is that a well-funded expedition went silent in mineral country, and that within a week of their silence, a permit clerk in Corvane sold a salvage contract to a storm chaser with famous debts. Somebody knows something, Xanthe. Several somebodies. And the race is on."

"And if I say I haven't found anything?"

"Then I'll believe you," Pell said, "the way I believe the weather report."

He laughed at his own joke. It was a good laugh, full-bodied, infectious in a clinical sense. One of the men on the far boat laughed too, out of habit or fear.

Xanthe turned the offer over. That was the trap's elegance: he didn't have to have found anything for the offer to work on him. All he had to do was *might find something*. The debt was a hook already set; Pell just needed him to swim. And the truth—the ugly, private truth Xanthe wouldn't say aloud—was that part of him wanted to take it. Four hundred thousand marks of weight, gone. No more calls that began pleasantly and ended with addresses of people he loved being recited back to him. He could hand over whatever Marsh's people had died for, walk away solvent, and tell himself the same lie everyone in this economy told themselves: that survival was neutral. That taking the deal didn't mean choosing a side.

Except.

Except there was a grave dug and left empty at the forward camp. Someone had expected a death out here and prepared for it, and then the death hadn't come, and that wrongness had been sitting in Xanthe's chest for two days like a swallowed stone. Except Ivo Marsh's voice had come through the static speaking coordinates like a prayer. Except Odile Ferrant had looked at him across her desk and said *recovery* in a tone that meant she knew recovery was a euphemism, and had let him sign anyway, and he had let himself sign, and every hour upriver he'd been telling himself the money was the point.

Pell was watching him think. That was the worst part. Pell *enjoyed* watching people think, the way a fisherman enjoys watching the float.

"You're stalling," Pell said fondly.

"I'm considering."

"Consider faster. Because here's my problem, Xanthe, and I'll share it with you as a courtesy: I'm not the only collector on this river. Ferrant's firm has security contractors inbound—I'd guess four days behind me, better equipped, less friendly. The Corvane consortium has hired mercenaries who don't smile at all. And there's a survivor of the expedition somewhere in those hills, and survivors of expeditions that found things are either the richest informants alive or the most dangerous witnesses alive, depending entirely on who reaches them first." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and beamed down with genuine, radiant goodwill. "So. My offer stands for as long as I'm on this river. After that, the market changes, and markets only ever change in one direction."

"And if I find nothing? If Halcyon starved, or drowned, or killed each other over nothing?"

"Then you'll have my sympathy," Pell said, "and your debt." He said it lightly, a small joke between friends, and then he extended his hand.

Xanthe looked at it. A broad, soft, well-manicured hand attached to a man who murdered people with paperwork and called it generosity. Taking it would mean nothing legally and everything symbolically, and they both knew it, which was why Pell offered it, which was why refusing it cost more than it seemed to.

Xanthe took it. One shake, firm, brief. Let Pell read whatever he wanted into it. Every second of ambiguity was a second the gunboats stayed in the channel instead of searching the banks.

"Wonderful," Pell said. "I knew we'd understand each other. Everyone does, eventually. It's my great gift."

He stepped back onto the plank, paused, and turned with the afterthought that wasn't one.

"Oh—one more thing. The pilot." He nodded pleasantly toward Sefa, who met his gaze with the flat expression of a woman counting exits. "Sefa Okonkwo-Vey. Ran cargo for the Meridian concession group, oh, six years back. Good record. Until she didn't. You might ask her sometime what she was carrying, and for whom, and why she stopped." His smile widened by one tooth. "Not a threat. Just context. I believe in informed partnerships."

Then he was crossing back to his boat, waving like a departing relative, and the diesels spun up, and the gunboat swung away into the channel with its escorts folding around it, and the sound faded downstream until the river was just the river again.

* * *

They didn't speak for an hour.

Sefa got them moving, threading upstream through the shallows where the gunboats couldn't follow, and Xanthe sat in the stern with the barometer and watched the needle do its hesitant dance and thought about nothing useful at all. The sun crawled west. The escarpment grew. Somewhere ahead, a dead man's coordinates waited in the limestone, and behind him, four days back, came men who didn't smile.

Finally Sefa cut the throttle at a bend sheltered by rock, dropped anchor, and turned around.

"He knew my name before today," she said.

"He knew your whole file. He probably knows mine better than I do."

"That's not what I mean." She crouched, forearms on knees, close enough that he could see how tired she actually was under the flint. "I ran cargo for Meridian. One shipment. Guns, I told myself they were tools, I told myself a lot of things. The village they went to used them on the next village over. Forty-one people. I stopped after that. Changed my name on the licenses, took honest work, told myself the ledger was closed." Her jaw worked. "And this morning a cheerful man on a gunboat opens his mouth and the whole account is still open. That's what they do, Xanthe. That's the whole business model. Nobody's ledger ever closes. They just stop reading it aloud when it suits them."

"I know how their ledgers work."

"Do you? Because you shook his hand."

Xanthe looked at his own palm like it might explain itself. "Buying time."

"Or selling it. Depends what you find up there." She held his eyes. "So let's say it plain, once, so we both know where the other stands. If whatever Marsh found is worth forgiving four hundred thousand marks—if it's gold, or data, or anything that spends—what do you do?"

The honest answer was *I don't know*, and he almost said it, because honesty had been buying him more traction with Sefa than charm ever had. But there was a truer answer underneath, one he'd been circling since the empty grave, and it surprised him a little to hear it come out of his own mouth.

"I don't know what it's worth," he said. "But I know what it cost. Eleven people went up that river and one grave got dug. Whatever's in those hills, somebody bled to keep it from men like Pell. Handing it to him isn't paying my debt. It's just transferring it—to whoever comes next, to whoever the papers hurt." He flexed his hand. "I shook his hand because a handshake keeps his boats in the channel for another day. That's all it bought. One day. Maybe two."

Sefa studied him for a long time, the way she studied water before committing the hull to it.

"One day," she said finally. "Then we'd better use it."

She turned back to the wheel, and Xanthe went forward to check the barometer again, mostly to have something to do with his hands. The needle had stopped hesitating. It was falling now, steadily, the way it fell before the big ones—the kind of front that shut down rivers and radios and gunboats alike, if you knew how to read it and they didn't.

Weather was coming. Real weather, the honest kind, the kind that didn't smile.

And through the last of the static, thin and broken, the radio breathed a fragment of Ivo Marsh's voice—closer now, or stronger, or both—two words out of a sentence:

*"—don't deliver—"*

Then static, and the escarpment standing against the sky like a wall someone had built to keep the world's promises out.
