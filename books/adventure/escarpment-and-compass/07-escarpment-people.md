## 7. Escarpment People

The storm found them an hour before dark, the way storms do—first as a bruise on the western horizon, then as a sound like the escarpment clearing its throat.

"We can't outrun it," Sefa said. She had the boat hauled up on a shingle bank below cliffs that went up and up until they stopped being geology and started being architecture. "And we can't sit on the water in that. Lightning likes rivers. Ask any dead fisherman."

Xanthe looked at the sky the way he looked at all skies, professionally, hungrily, the way other men looked at ledgers. Supercell, rotating base, hail core already whitening the updraft column. A beautiful thing. A thing that would kill anyone caught in the open. "There's a village," he said. "On the cliff face. I saw smoke this morning."

"You saw smoke."

"Terraces. Stairs cut into the rock. Somebody lives up there."

Sefa squinted at the cliff, then at him, then at the sky, performing some private arithmetic of survival. "Somebody lives up there," she agreed, "and somebody's about to knock on their door in a flood with a syndicate gunboat somewhere behind us. You understand how that reads."

"I understand how it reads."

"Good. Just so we're honest about the kind of guest you are."

They carried what mattered—Xanthe's instruments, the radio, the water, the fragment of Ivo Marsh's voice he'd transcribed onto paper because paper didn't need batteries—and started up the cut stairs as the first rain came down like gravel. The steps were old. That was the first thing he noticed, the thing that lodged in him and stayed: these stairs had been cut before the concession era, before the survey markers, before anything with a serial number had ever looked at this valley. The risers were worn concave by centuries of feet. His boots fit into the hollows like the stairs had been waiting for him specifically, which was nonsense, which was exhaustion talking, and which he felt anyway.

Halfway up, a voice came out of the rain.

"You're not from the river offices."

A woman stood on a landing under an overhang, sixty or so, built like the cliff she lived on, gray braid thick as a hawser. She held a lantern that did nothing against the darkening sky but did everything against the dark. Behind her, half a dozen faces watched from a doorway—old men, a teenager with a sling, a child who looked at Xanthe with the frank appraisal of someone who had not yet learned to pretend.

"No," Xanthe said.

"River offices send men who say yes to everything." She looked at Sefa. "You're not from the river offices either."

"I ran cargo for them once," Sefa said. "I've since developed taste."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth. Not quite a smile. An opening. "I'm Maren. If you're under my roof tonight, you'll work tomorrow. That's the whole law up here."

"We'll work," Xanthe said.

"Then come in before the sky finishes what it's started."

* * *

The village was bigger than it had any right to be. It clung to the cliff in tiers—houses built into overhangs, cisterns fed by channels cut to catch the runoff, a hall at the center whose roof beams were whole tree trunks hauled up here by people who had decided, generations ago, that this was where they would be. Forty people, maybe fifty. Xanthe counted doors out of habit. He always counted doors. It was the first thing the debt had taught him: know your exits.

The storm hit the escarpment like a fist on a drum. The whole cliff hummed. In the hall, the villagers moved through it with the unbothered rhythm of people for whom weather was a neighbor, not an enemy—shuttering vents, checking the cistern channels, a man re-lashing a door with the bored competence of someone doing it for the hundredth time. The child from the landing followed Xanthe at a distance of exactly four steps, close enough to be curious, far enough to be safe.

"You count doors," the child said.

Xanthe stopped. "What?"

"You keep looking at the doors. Papa says river men count doors because they're always planning to leave."

"Papa sounds like he knows river men."

"Everybody here knows river men." The child said it without malice, which made it worse. "They come up, they look at things, they write things down, they leave. You have a thing around your neck."

Xanthe's hand went to it before he could stop himself—the storm locket, the little brass case with his first measured barometric reading inside, from the first storm he'd ever chased, back when chasing was love instead of arithmetic. He'd forgotten he was wearing it. He'd forgotten he was the kind of man who wore it.

"My mother's," he lied, automatically, and then, because the child was looking at him with those unpracticed eyes, "No. Mine. From when I was your age."

"What's in it?"

"A number."

"What's it mean?"

"It meant the sky was about to do something wonderful."

The child considered this with enormous seriousness. "The sky's doing something wonderful right now. You should look."

Xanthe looked. Through the hall's open door, the lightning was walking along the escarpment rim, stroke after stroke, lighting the valley below in white flashes—river, flats, the far hills, all of it appearing and disappearing like something being decided and un-decided. It was, objectively, a magnificent storm. He felt the old pull, the hunger to be out in it with his instruments, measuring, selling, converting wonder into numbers into money into another month of not being collected.

"You really love it," the child said.

"I really love it."

"But you sell it."

Xanthe didn't answer. The child nodded, satisfied, as though the answer had been the silence, and wandered off, and Xanthe stood in the doorway of a stranger's hall feeling seen by someone who hadn't yet learned that seeing people was supposed to cost something.

* * *

Supper was fish, tubers, a dark bread that tasted of something he couldn't name and never stopped tasting. The hall was loud with talk—not the transactional murmur of Corvane, where every conversation was a negotiation wearing a costume, but the plain noise of people who had spent the day working near each other and had things to say about it. Nobody asked Xanthe what he did for a living. Nobody asked Sefa either. They were fed because they were under the roof, and the roof's law was work tomorrow, not confession tonight.

Maren sat down across from him late, when the hall had thinned, and set two cups of something hot and bitter between them.

"You came up from the river," she said. "Following the Halcyon people."

Xanthe kept his face still. "You know about them?"

"Everyone up here knows about them. They came through in spring. Polite men with instruments. They asked about the caves." She drank. "We told them the truth. That the caves are not empty, and not ours to show. They went anyway. Polite men with instruments usually do."

"They found something."

"They found what was always here." Maren set her cup down. "You have coordinates. I can see it in how you hold your map, like it's a wound. Show me."

He shouldn't have. Every professional instinct he had—the instincts that had kept him alive through six years of debt and dodging—said show nothing, promise nothing, be a smooth surface that reflects questions back at the questioner. But he was tired, and the storm was magnificent, and there was something about this hall that made smoothness feel like a kind of poverty.

He spread the map. Put his finger on the coordinates from Marsh's radio fragment.

Maren looked for a long time. Then she did something he didn't expect: she laughed, low and without much humor, and pressed her thumb flat on the spot, covering it entirely.

"That," she said, "is my grandmother's garden."

Xanthe stared at her. "That's—according to every survey, that's unclaimed wilderness. State land. That's why the concession—"

"According to every survey." She lifted her thumb. "Your maps were drawn by people who needed this valley to be empty. An empty valley can be sold. A valley with fifty generations of terraces, of graves, of names— that valley has to be stolen, and stealing is paperwork, and paperwork is what your river offices do best." She turned the map toward herself, studying it not as a stranger but as a woman reading a letter from home. "The escarpment remembers who it belongs to. The caves especially. You go down there thinking you're discovering something, and the valley will let you—right up until you try to take it out. Then it remembers you."

"That's a warning or a threat."

"It's a weather report." She said it flatly, and Xanthe, who had spent his life reading skies, heard the professional calm in it and believed her more than he'd believed anyone in years. "I've watched your kind come up the river for forty years. The ones who come to take, they leave with nothing or they don't leave. The ones who come to ask—" she shrugged—"the valley talks to them. Which are you?"

And there it was. The question he'd been outrunning since Corvane, since the contract, since the first cheerful call from the Syndicate about his outstanding balance. He had a contract in his pack that said *recovery*, and a debt that said *deliver*, and Odile Ferrant's nineteen-day clock ticking down to sixteen now, and every one of those instruments pointed at the same answer: he was a taker. He'd been a taker so long he'd mistaken it for a personality.

"I don't know," he said.

It was, he realized, the first honest thing he'd said to anyone since the river. Maren studied him the way he studied a storm base—not with hope or fear, but with an assessment of what it was going to do.

"Honest," she said at last. "That's rarer than you'd think, up here. It's also worth nothing on its own. Like a barometer. It only tells you what's coming." She stood, collected the cups. "Sleep in the hall. Work starts at first light. And Xanthe—" she'd never asked his name, he realized; someone else had told her, or she'd simply known, the way the valley knew things—"whatever you decide to be when you leave here, decide it before you reach the caves. After is too late."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat by the hall's small fire while the storm walked the rim above them, and he took out the locket, and opened it, and looked at the number his own younger hand had written there. *947 millibars and falling fast.* He'd been thirteen. He'd written it because the sky was about to do something wonderful and he wanted to keep a piece of it.

When had keeping become taking? When had the wonder become inventory? He could trace the line exactly—the year the debt started, the year the Syndicate's cheerful voices entered his life, the year stopping became impossible because stopping meant being still, and being still meant being found, and being found meant the ledger. He had told himself for six years that he was free because he was moving. Sitting by this fire, in this hall, with the storm singing overhead and fifty strangers sleeping around him who had fed him for nothing, he understood for the first time that he had it backwards. The moving was the cage. The debt had built it, but he'd been the one who kept the door shut.

Sefa appeared beside him in the dark, silent as her boat.

"You're not sleeping either," he said.

"I sleep on water. Cliffs make me nervous." She looked at the fire. "The old woman knows about the caves. The kid counted your doors. By morning the whole village will know what you are, and they fed you anyway. Does that not terrify you?"

It did. That was the thing he couldn't say. Being hunted was simple; being *housed* was not. A hunter wanted something from him and the transaction was clear. These people wanted nothing. They'd fed him because the roof's law said so. They'd ask him to work tomorrow because the roof's law said so. There was no invoice. There was no ledger. And Xanthe Dravenmoor, who understood every currency in the valley except the free kind, sat in front of that fire feeling like a man holding a contract written in a language he couldn't read.

"It terrifies me," he admitted.

"Good," Sefa said. "Fear means you've noticed the trap." She lay back, arms behind her head. "Question is whether the trap is theirs or yours."

Above them, the storm passed over, moving east, dragging its lightning with it, and in the sudden quiet Xanthe could hear the escarpment dripping, a thousand small waters finding their way down the cliff, and somewhere beneath all of it—beneath the terraces, beneath the graves, beneath fifty generations of feet worn into stone stairs—the caves, keeping what they kept.

At first light, Maren woke him with a hand on his shoulder and a tool in his hand.

"You said you'd work," she said. "The cistern channel silted in the storm. And after—" her eyes went to his pack, to the map, to the coordinates her grandmother's garden was sitting on top of—"after, you'll want to go up-ridge. There's a path. And there's someone living wild up there who doesn't like visitors, and who was with the Halcyon people when they went down. If you're going to the caves, you'll want to hear what she has to say before you hear what the caves have to say."

Xanthe took the tool. "You know who she is."

"I know who she was." Maren turned toward the light growing at the hall's door. "The valley remembers, Xanthe. The question the next few days will ask you is whether you do."
