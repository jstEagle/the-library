## 8. Nineteen Days, Then Sixteen

The filing room on the fourth floor of the Concession House smelled of toner and old rain, and Odile Ferrant had learned to love it the way a surgeon loves an operating theater: not for its comfort, but because everything in it was exactly where she put it.

She arrived at six-forty, as always, before the junior clerks, before the radiators ticked awake, before the city of Corvane had finished deciding whether the fog was going to lift. She unlocked the drawer with the brass key she kept on a chain under her blouse, and she pulled the Halcyon file, and she read it again.

Not the expedition file. The other one. The one that didn't exist.

It was a thin folder, thinner than any real permit history should be, and that thinness was the whole architecture of the thing. A genuine concession file was a fat, wheezing animal—survey amendments, boundary disputes, fee schedules, the fossilized arguments of dead clerks. The Halcyon file was clean as a lie told slowly. Nineteen days to expiry. That was what the public record said. Nineteen days, then the valley's mineral rights reverted to the state, open season, first claimant wins.

What the public record did not say was that the expiry date had been manufactured eleven months ago, in this room, by her own hand, at the direction of a man named Corvus Aldery who signed his memos with a fountain pen he never let anyone else touch.

Odile set the folder down and looked out the window at the river. Somewhere upriver, past the burned checkpoints and the static, a storm chaser named Xanthe Dravenmoor was spending money he didn't have to find people who were probably dead, on behalf of a deadline that wasn't real.

*You're the instrument,* she thought. *We built you a calendar and pointed you at the ground.*

She had drafted the amendment herself. That was the part she returned to, in the quiet hours, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Not the decision—decisions came from above, decisions were weather—but the drafting. The language. *Pursuant to Schedule 4(c), the concession term shall be deemed to run from the date of original survey approval rather than the date of grant.* One sentence. She had written one sentence that moved the clock back nine years, and she had written it well, which was the problem. Bad drafting got caught. Good drafting got filed, cross-referenced, and forgotten by everyone except the two people alive who knew what it meant.

Her and Aldery. And now, potentially, whoever came up that river with the Halcyon papers in their teeth.

* * *

Aldery summoned her at ten, which meant he wanted something and wanted to watch her understand it.

His office occupied the corner suite with the good light, all pale wood and glass, arranged so that visitors sat below the sightline of the window and therefore appeared to be looking up at him even when they weren't. He was feeding paper into the shredder personally, which he only did when the material mattered. He fed it one sheet at a time, unhurried, like a man basting something.

"Dravenmoor signed," he said. "Ferrant, you handled the contract?"

"I handled the contract."

"And your assessment of him?"

Odile considered the question honestly, because Aldery paid for honesty the way collectors paid for forgeries—as long as it looked right from across the room. "He's competent, indebted, and unattached. We selected him for all three."

"We did." A sheet went into the shredder. "And yet I've had a communication from Mr. Pell's office indicating that Vellum has also taken an interest in the recovery. Which suggests our instrument may develop competing buyers."

"Vellum doesn't buy things," Odile said. "Vellum forgives them. It's cheaper."

Something flickered at the corner of Aldery's mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Be that as it may. If Dravenmoor recovers anything up there, I want it logged through this office first. Nothing reaches the registry before it reaches my desk. Is that understood?"

"It's understood," Odile said, and it was, and that was precisely the trouble. Because she had spent the morning reading the Halcyon correspondence—the real correspondence, the internal kind—and she knew what the expedition had been circling. Marsh's last transmitted report, three months old now, had used the word *precedent*. He'd used it twice. Men like Ivo Marsh didn't use that word about ore samples.

"If I may," she said. "If what's up there is what I think it might be, logging it through this office won't contain it. It'll implicate it."

Aldery stopped shredding. He looked at her with the mild patience of a man watching a dog attempt arithmetic.

"Ferrant. What do you think is up there?"

"Possibly nothing. Possibly bodies and bad geology." She kept her voice level, clerk-flat. "But if the escarpment villages hold pre-colonial title documentation—if anything of that character survived up there—then every permit we've ever issued in that valley is a derivative instrument. Ours, Vellum's, the state's. All of it priced off an asset we never actually owned."

The silence lasted four seconds. Odile counted them; counting was her discipline.

"You've been reading Marsh's reports," Aldery said finally. "Those reports are conjecture from a man under stress in remote terrain. What exists up there, when Dravenmoor brings it down, will be assessed by qualified counsel in this building. That is the process. You are part of the process." He resumed shredding. "Nineteen days, Ferrant. Then the window closes and the rivals file, and none of this matters anymore. See that the docket stays clean."

She left. In the corridor she passed the junior clerks wheeling their carts, and she thought: *the docket stays clean.* As though the map were the territory. As though the machine could keep running if everyone simply agreed to describe it accurately in the wrong words.

* * *

At lunch she did something she had never done before. She pulled Dravenmoor's check-in schedule, and instead of filing it, she copied it.

Small thing. A carbon of a schedule, folded into her handbag between a ration book and a hairpin. But her hands were steady in the way they got steady before large errors, and she recognized the feeling from the one other time in her life she'd committed a crime—age nineteen, a customs form, a sister's passage money, a signature that wasn't hers. She'd never been caught. She'd never forgiven herself either, which she suspected was the relevant data point. Some people stole and slept fine. Odile stole and lay awake doing double-entry bookkeeping on her own soul, and the fact that she did it anyway told her something about the size of what was coming.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't stop running:

If Dravenmoor delivered the vault—or whatever it was—to the Concession House, the permits held, the machine ran, and the escarpment villages stayed what they had always been in every document she'd ever processed: *unencumbered terrain.* A legal term. She had typed it herself, dozens of times. Unencumbered terrain, meaning land without people worth encumbering.

If Dravenmoor destroyed it, the machine also ran. That was the elegant horror of it. Destruction served Aldery just as well as delivery. Either way the past stayed buried and the clock stood.

There was no option on the form for what she privately hoped he'd do, which was the third thing, the inconvenient thing, the thing that would burn her career and possibly her liberty along with everyone else's. She hoped he'd betray them all equally. She found she trusted him precisely to the extent that he owed everyone and loved no one—not yet. Loyalty was the variable that ruined equations. A loyal man could be bought or broken. A man with no stake anywhere would follow the numbers, and the numbers, if the documents said what Marsh feared they said, pointed only one direction: daylight.

So she had copied his check-in schedule. So that when he went dark—when, not if; men like Dravenmoor always went dark eventually—she would know where the darkness started, and could decide, alone, at six-forty in the morning, in a filing room that smelled of toner and old rain, whether to send security after him or send nothing at all.

* * *

Sixteen days to expiry. The number changed on her desk calendar in red grease pencil, and each morning she rubbed out the old figure and wrote the new one, and each morning the ritual felt less like administration and more like a countdown in someone else's war.

On the sixteenth day—the morning the calendar said sixteen—a message came up from the river relay station, routed through three departments and stamped by all of them, arriving on her desk stripped of context as all messages were. Standard text. Check-in overdue: Dravenmoor party, forty-one hours.

Forty-one hours. Inside the tolerance window. Any competent clerk would initial it, file it, wait for the next relay.

Odile sat with the message for a long time. Outside, the fog had lifted the way it sometimes did all at once, the whole city appearing between one breath and the next, and she could see the river, brown and patient, running toward the escarpment country where a man she'd contracted and a deadline she'd forged were converging on something older than both.

She took out the carbon copy of his schedule. She took out a fresh routing slip. She wrote *security assessment, routine* in her small exact hand, knowing full well that nothing about what she was initiating would be routine, and that Aldery's security team traveled with rifles and that her own motives traveled with her, unexamined, in the same bag as the stolen schedule.

Then she paused, pen lifted, and added one line to the routing instructions—unauthorized, deniable, the kind of sentence she used to write for Aldery and was now, for the first time, writing against him:

*Assess condition of party. Report directly to F. Do not engage.*

She initialed it. Filed the duplicate. Rubbed out *sixteen* on the calendar and stood looking at the blank space where the number had been, thinking that somewhere upriver, a man with no stake anywhere was about to become the most dangerous kind of person in the valley: the kind with nothing left to lose but a choice.

The radio on her desk crackled once, faintly, carrying static off the escarpment country—and beneath the static, or perhaps she imagined it, a fragment of voice, gone before she could raise the gain.

She reached for the dial.
