## 9. The Survivor Who Won't Come Down

The coordinates from Ivo Marsh's radio fragment put us at the base of the escarpment's northern face by the third morning after the empty grave, and I spent all three of those mornings wishing they'd pointed somewhere else.

Sefa brought the boat in close to a shingle bank where the river went shallow and braided, and killed the engine with the particular tenderness she reserved for engines she didn't trust. "End of the line for anything that floats," she said. "From here you walk, or you're a fool."

"I've been both," I said. "Usually on the same day."

She almost smiled. Almost. Since the cards game two nights back—since I'd lost three hands on purpose to see if she'd notice, and she'd noticed, and neither of us had said a word about it—there was a truce between us that wasn't quite trust but was at least a shared understanding of arithmetic. Two liars can travel lighter than one.

The hired hands were another matter. Sefa had signed on two rivermen at the last port, brothers named Cade and Rill, big quiet men who carried their gear like it might apologize to them. They'd been paid half up front, which meant they were loyal for exactly as long as it took someone to offer the other half plus interest. I kept them in front of me on the trail and my hand near the flare gun in my pack, which was not a weapon so much as a promise of noise, but noise buys seconds and seconds buy exits.

The escarpment rose ahead of us like a wall somebody had started building out of spite and then abandoned halfway through. Limestone, grey-green with lichen, seamed with shadow where water had been cutting at it since before there were names for water. The forest came up to its feet and stopped, as if the trees themselves had agreed on a boundary.

"Marsh's coordinates," Sefa said, checking the battered receiver against her own dead reckoning. "Should be a cave mouth somewhere along this face. Tamsin Voss was Halcyon's geologist. If anyone knew where the rock opens up, it's her."

"If she's alive."

"Nobody survives four months alone up here," Cade said. It was the first thing he'd volunteered since we hired him.

"Then whoever's been resetting those snares we passed yesterday is a ghost," Sefa said, "and ghosts don't use paracord."

I'd seen the snares too. Freshly set, cleanly made, positioned with an eye for game trails that spoke of long practice. Somebody was eating well up here. Somebody was *staying*.

We made camp at the tree line because I refused to approach unfamiliar ground in failing light, and because the storm building over the high country all afternoon had finally begun to speak—distant thunder rolling down the valley like furniture being dragged across an upper floor. I read the sky while the others ate. The cell was tracking northeast, away from us, all show and no delivery. A liar of a storm. I respected that.

I took first watch because I never sleep well on the first night anywhere, and because the escarpment at dusk did something to the light that made me want to keep looking at it. The last sun caught the upper cliffs and turned them the color of old brass, and in the seams between the rock faces I could see dark openings—dozens of them, maybe hundreds, the mountain breathing through its teeth.

That was when I saw the watcher.

Not a shape exactly. A wrongness in the pattern of shadow near a fallen slab two hundred meters upslope, the kind of stillness that isn't natural stillness. Animals are never perfectly still; they breathe, they twitch, they negotiate with their own bodies. This was the stillness of something that had learned to hold itself like a tool.

I didn't call out. I moved the lantern off my position, drifted ten meters left into deeper dark, and waited.

It took forty minutes. I know because I counted, the way I count everything when the waiting gets bad—a habit from the debt years, when counting was the only power I had over time. Then the stillness detached itself from the rocks and flowed downhill toward our camp, low and fast, and I got my first real look at what four months alone does to a person.

She was thin the way wire is thin—not starved so much as condensed, every unnecessary gram burned away until only function remained. Hair hacked short with a knife. Clothes that had started as expedition gear and ended as camouflage, smeared with ash and mud. She moved through the camp's perimeter without touching a single piece of our gear, which told me she'd already inventoried it from above, and stopped at the cold fire ring, where she crouched and pressed her palm flat against the ashes.

Reading them. Reading how long ago, reading what we'd burned, reading us.

"Tamsin Voss," I said quietly, from the dark behind her.

She moved faster than anything I've ever seen outside of weather. There was a blade in her hand—I never saw where it came from—and then there was pain across my forearm and I was falling backward and she was on me with her knee on my chest and the blade at my throat, and her eyes in the dark were the worst part. Not wild. *Precise.* A fear that had been refined, distilled, reduced to its active ingredient.

"Don't," she said. Her voice was gravel and disuse. "Don't say my name. Don't say any names. You don't get to have the names."

"We're not Syndicate," I managed. The blade dimpled skin. "We're not Pell's people. We're not concession security. We came upriver on a salvage contract and everything we've found says we're the first ones here since your expedition went quiet."

"Contract." She tasted the word like spoiled meat. "You're all contracts. Marsh said—"

She stopped. Her whole body flinched around whatever she'd been about to say, the way a man flinches around a wound.

"Marsh is alive," I said. Not a guess. The radio fragment had been his voice; I'd played it forty times. "We heard him. Coordinates. He was transmitting."

Something moved behind her eyes—hope trying to surface through four months of ice, and her drowning it on purpose, because hope up here was probably the most dangerous animal in the valley. "You heard a recording," she said. "Or you heard nothing and you're lying, because everyone lies, that's the whole—everyone lies, that's the entire architecture of—" She pressed the blade harder. "Why are you here?"

I told her the truth, or the version of truth I could afford: the permits, the expiry clock, the salvage terms, Odile Ferrant's careful language about recovery. I watched her listen the way Sefa listened to engines—with her head tilted, cataloguing every rattle.

"Nineteen days," she repeated, when I finished. "Your employers gave the mountain nineteen days."

"Sixteen now."

"And when the days run out, they come up here with more boats and more guns and more contracts, and they take it anyway." She sat back on my chest, blade lowering a centimeter, not from mercy but from the exhaustion of holding it. "They always take it anyway."

Sefa's voice came out of the dark, level as a spirit gauge. "Voss. It's Sefa Anwar. I ran cargo past this stretch six years ago. Never landed, never touched your ground. But I know who holds title to these waters, and it isn't the people holding the paper."

Tamsin's head turned toward the voice. For a moment the precision cracked and underneath it I saw the geologist, the scientist—the woman who had once looked at rock strata and believed they meant something orderly.

"You," she said to Sefa. "You worked for Corvane Freight. You hauled their drills."

"I hauled their crates. I never asked what was in them. That's not innocence, it's just laziness, and I've had time to tell the difference since."

Cade and Rill had woken by now—they were standing at the edge of the fire ring with their hands visible, which was smart, because Tamsin had clearly already noted where their knives were. The rain the storm had promised finally arrived, a fine cold drizzle that beaded on her hacked hair and ran down her face like the mountain crying through her.

"Get up," she said to me, and rolled off.

I got up, pressing my sleeve to my forearm. Shallow cut. She'd known exactly how shallow. Even her violence was calibrated.

* * *

We talked through the worst of the rain under an overhang she led us to—a limestone lip halfway up the first terrace, dry-floored, defensible, chosen with a professional eye. She built a small fire with a competence that bordered on ceremony, using wood she'd cached nearby, and fed us strips of smoked goat from her snares without comment. Hospitality and hostility from the same hands, portioned equally.

"The others are dead," she said, eventually. It wasn't a question and it wasn't grief, not anymore. It was inventory. "Kessler, Okafor, the twins, Dr. Amari. Dead or gone downriver, and gone downriver means dead too, just slower. Marsh—" She stopped again. That flinch. "Marsh stayed."

"Stayed where?"

Her eyes came up to mine and held there, and I understood that I was looking at the exact point where fear had curdled into something harder. Fear wants to run. What she had instead wanted to *guard*.

"There's a place under the escarpment," she said. "Older than the maps. Older than the words on the maps. The villages below used to know about it—maybe some of them still do—but the concession surveys marked it as empty ground, because empty ground is what you write on a map when what's actually there would cost you everything." She poked the fire. "Halcyon found it. And then Halcyon understood what finding it meant, and that understanding is why I'm the only one who comes down to the river."

"What's in it?"

"No." The word came out flat and final as a closing door. "You don't get the inventory. Knowing what's there is knowing how to price it, and pricing it is selling it, and selling it—" She laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Everyone who learns what it's worth becomes worth something to someone else. That's the disease. I'm not spreading it."

"Pell knows something's here," I said. "He offered to forgive my debts for it sight unseen. The Syndicate has gunboats on the river. Odile Ferrant's firm manufactured the permit expiry to force a recovery before their rivals file claims. Whatever your vault is, there are three powers already racing for it, and none of them need directions from you. They'll find it the way water finds low ground. Slowly, and then all at once."

"Then I'll slow it," she said simply. "I've slowed it for four months. I reset snares. I move camps. I burned our own supply cache so the smoke would read as accident and bring nobody looking. I have slowed it, and I will keep slowing it, and if I die slowing it, the mountain keeps the rest." She looked at each of us in turn. "That's the whole of my remaining life plan. It fits on a postcard."

Sefa snorted softly. "Postcards get intercepted."

"Everything gets intercepted. That's the point."

I crouched by the fire, arm throbbing, rain drumming on the overhang above us, and thought about the empty grave at the forward camp. Someone had dug it expecting a death that hadn't come. I'd assumed dread. Now I wondered if it had been preparation—if someone had stood at that hole and decided that whoever came back changed would not be the person who left, and dug accordingly.

"Tamsin," I said. "Ivo Marsh transmitted coordinates. Live, or close enough. He's asking for something. Maybe he's asking for you."

The firelight moved on her face and I watched the war happen behind her eyes—four months of solitary doctrine against one sentence containing a name she'd mourned. Her hands, resting on her knees, had gone white-knuckled.

"He wouldn't transmit," she whispered. "He swore. We all swore. Silence was the only—"

"Unless he needed help," Sefa said gently. "Unless he sealed himself in with it the way you seal yourself out, and the math changed."

Tamsin stared into the fire for a long time. Rain. Thunder, farther now, the storm dragging itself over the high country and away. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its armor by a single degree, which from her was a surrender.

"I'll guide you," she said. "To the approach. Through the markers—you'll miss them otherwise, everyone misses them, they're carved to be missed. I'll take you as far as the low gallery." She raised a finger, and the finger shook, and she hated it shaking. "Conditions. One: nobody touches the inheritance. Nothing comes out. Not a page, not a stone, not a scrap. You look with your eyes and you leave with your hands empty. Two: if Marsh is alive, he decides. Not your contract. Not your employers. Him. Three—" and here she looked directly at me, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had already survived the loss of everything and therefore could not be threatened with it, "—if any of you break the first condition, I will kill you underground where no one will ever find the body, and I will go back to slowing the water, and I will not lose a night's sleep. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," I said. And the strange thing, the thing I'd examine later, was that I did understand her—understood her the way you understand a storm you've chased for years. She wasn't crazy. She was the only person in this whole rotten ledger who had correctly priced what she'd found, and the price was that it should never be sold.

"Xanthe," Sefa said quietly, warning in it. She knew me. She knew that I'd signed a contract that said *recovery*, and that recovery was a word with a body count built into it, and that I'd lied to Odile Ferrant about having no stake in this valley because having a stake felt too much like being owned.

"I understand her," I said again. "And I'll keep condition one until condition two gives us a better instruction."

Tamsin studied me for a long moment, the way she'd studied the ashes of our fire—reading what I'd burned, reading how long ago, reading me.

"You lie like a man who's practiced," she said at last. "But you just made a promise you didn't hedge. So either you mean it, or you're the best liar on this river." She kicked dirt over the fire. "Either way, we leave at first light. Sleep fast."

She climbed the slope into the dark toward her own camp, moving like something the mountain had grown, and I sat with my bleeding arm and the taste of the bargain in my mouth.

Below us, far down the valley, a light appeared on the water. One light, then a second, riding low and steady against the current.

Gunboats don't run dark unless they want to arrive unannounced.

Pell had found the river.
