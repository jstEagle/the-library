## 10. Pell's Generosity

Tamsin led us north along the escarpment's shoulder for two days before the weather turned, and I spent both of them listening for engines.

She moved like an animal that had unlearned human habits—stopping without warning, tasting the wind, reading the ground the way I read cloud decks. She wouldn't walk in the open if she could avoid it. She wouldn't cross her own old campsites. When Sefa asked her a direct question about the expedition's route, Tamsin answered with a different fact entirely, as though questions were a currency she'd stopped accepting.

"She's not crazy," Sefa said quietly on the second evening, while Tamsin crouched at the tree line watching something we couldn't see. "Or she is, but it's a working kind of crazy. Like mine."

"Yours isn't working."

"That's what makes it mine."

We made camp in a fold of limestone where a spring seeped out of the rock and fed a thread of green through the grey scrub. Tamsin refused the fire. She sat facing downslope all night, and around three in the morning she woke me by pressing one finger hard against my sternum—not a shake, not a strike, just pressure, like a seal testing ice.

"Headlights," she breathed.

There were none. But ten minutes later I heard what she'd heard: the far-off grind of tires on the switchback road that served the concession survey stations, miles east and below us. Then silence. Then, closer, on the river side, the low cough of an outboard being throttled down.

Pell had come upriver. Of course he had. The gunboat had turned back after our last conversation—or appeared to—and I'd let myself believe I'd bought more time than I had. That was the thing about Mr. Pell: he never pressed an advantage immediately. He let you spend your relief first. It was, I think, a genuine kindness in his mind. He wanted you comfortable when you understood him.

* * *

They came up the slope at first light, four of them, and Pell called out from forty meters the way a neighbor calls across a fence.

"Xanthe! Don't shoot anything, please, my people are jumpy and I'd hate for this to become a story."

I had the pistol out anyway, low against my thigh. Sefa was already awake behind me, a machete in one hand and a flare gun in the other, which was the most Sefa arrangement possible. Tamsin was simply gone—melted into the rocks above camp like water into sand. I didn't know whether that was good or very bad.

Pell walked the last twenty meters alone, hands visible, smiling. He was wearing a field jacket too clean for the country and boots that had never been resoled, and he looked rested, cheerful, like a man arriving at a wedding. Behind him his four collectors spread out with professional laziness, rifles slung, faces empty.

"You followed us," I said.

"I followed *the survivor*." He said it gently, as if correcting a small error in my bookkeeping. "You found Tamsin Voss two days ago. You've been moving toward the caves ever since. Xanthe, I want you to know how much I admire that. Genuinely. Most men in your position would have run for the coast."

"Most men in my position have less debt."

"And yet here you are, climbing toward the one asset in this valley worth more than every ledger I carry." He looked past me at the escarpment wall, at the dark seams of the caves, and his smile deepened by a degree. "You're close, aren't you."

"We haven't entered anything."

"No, no. Of course not." He said it the way Odile stamped permits—the form observed, the substance irrelevant. "May I sit? My knees are fifty-four years old."

He sat on a rock without waiting for permission and produced a thermos, poured coffee into the cup, and offered it to me. I declined. He drank it himself with evident pleasure.

"Here's where we are," he said. "The Halcyon Expedition found something under that escarpment. I don't know exactly what—I'm not a documents man, I'm a *recovery* man—but I know its shape. Something old. Something legal. Something that would make certain parties very happy and other parties extinct." He sipped. "My employers want it delivered to them before anyone else knows it exists. Your debt is—let me look—" he pulled a folded paper from his jacket with theatrical care, though we both knew he knew the number to the cent— "one hundred and fourteen thousand, six hundred and twelve marks, plus accrued penalties. Hand over what Halcyon found, and that number becomes zero. Not deferred. Zero. I'll burn the page myself, and I'll enjoy it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the number keeps growing, and eventually someone less pleasant than me comes to discuss it." He spread his hands, rueful, almost apologetic. "I hate that part of the business, Xanthe. Truly. I've asked them to let me handle everything personally precisely so it doesn't have to be unpleasant. And it doesn't have to be! Everyone profits. That's the whole point of what we do. The syndicate gets its asset, you get your freedom, the valley gets—well, the valley gets certainty, which is worth more than gold. Certainty is the only commodity everyone's buying."

Somewhere above us, a stone clicked against stone. Pell didn't look up. His men did, briefly, then returned to their boredom.

* * *

It was mid-morning when the scout came down the slope.

I saw him first—a thin figure in concession-grey moving fast along the ridge to the east, using cover badly, the way a man moves when he's been walking for days and knows he's late. One of Odile's security teams, or a freelance surveyor for the rival claimant, or some third set of vultures I hadn't even counted yet. He was maybe six hundred meters out and closing on a line that would take him directly over our camp, and there was nothing subtle about his trajectory. He was heading for the caves. Everyone was always heading for the caves.

One of Pell's men said something quiet. Pell raised a hand—not alarmed, not urgent, just a conductor pausing the orchestra.

"Ah," he said. "That will be Corvane's interest arriving."

"Pell—"

"He's carrying a camera," Pell said, squinting. "And a permit folio. See the orange spine? That's a Meridian Concessions folio. They filed a competing claim last week; I told you the valley would get crowded." He sighed, and it was the sigh of a man disappointed by traffic, by weather, by the general inconvenience of other people pursuing their livelihoods. "I can't let him photograph those caves, Xanthe. If Meridian files coordinates before my employers take delivery, the whole transaction collapses, and then nobody profits, and I *hate* when nobody profits."

"What are you going to do?"

"What I always do." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and smiled at me with real warmth. "The minimum necessary."

He walked thirty meters down the slope, unhurried, hands still loose at his sides, and spoke to the scout before the man had even registered that anyone was there. I couldn't hear the words. I saw the scout stop, confused, polite—saw him start to reach for the folio, probably to show his papers, because that's what you do when a friendly stranger addresses you on a hillside in a law-abiding country.

Pell shot him twice in the chest.

No warning. No escalation. No anger. The pistol came up between one syllable and the next and came down again, and the scout sat down hard in the scrub with the orange folio still half-out of his jacket, and then he lay back and was finished, and the sound rolled away down the valley and came back once, faintly, off the escarpment wall.

Pell crouched beside the body for a moment. He took the folio, tucked it inside his own jacket, and—this is the detail I can't make smaller than it was—he closed the dead man's eyes. Gently. With two fingers, the way you'd straighten a picture frame. Then he stood and walked back up the slope toward me, and his smile was exactly where he'd left it.

"He had a family in Corvane, probably," Pell said, conversational. "They'll get a settlement. My employers are generous with settlements—it's cheaper than lawsuits and better for morale. Everyone profits, Xanthe. Eventually. Even him, in a way—he won't die in debt, at least." He glanced at me, and his face did something I wasn't prepared for: it softened. "You look pale. Sit down. Have some coffee. I know it looks bad, but I promise you, from where I stand, this is the *kindest* version of events. There are men who would have shot him from six hundred meters and never learned his name. At least now someone closed his eyes."

Sefa made a sound low in her throat. I put a hand back without looking and found her wrist and held it, and felt the machete tremble there, and felt it steady.

"Pell," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Do you hear yourself?"

"Constantly." He laughed. "Occupational habit. Talk to yourself enough and you stay honest."

"You just murdered a man for carrying paperwork."

"I *resolved a conflict* before it became a tragedy." He said it without a flicker, and that was the vertigo, right there—that there was no flicker. No mask slipping, because there was no mask. This was the face. A man who had arranged his soul the way Odile's firm arranged permits: everything in order, everything lawful-looking, nothing underneath. "Xanthe, listen to me. I've watched you work. Chasing storms, selling data, running from the Vellum Syndicate for three years—you're a man who understands systems. You understand that weather doesn't punish anyone. It just *is*. A supercell drops on a church picnic and a funeral alike, and nobody calls the sky evil. What I do is weather. I am the front that moves through, and the wise build accordingly, and the foolish stand in the open holding umbrellas and calling it principle." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and for one moment the warmth in it was completely, horribly sincere. "You're not foolish. That's why I like you. Take the deal. Walk away rich in the only currency that matters, which is *unowned*. Or don't, and discover that I am not the worst weather coming for this valley. Because I promise you—" and here he looked up at the escarpment, at the caves, with something almost like reverence— "whatever Halcyon found down there, there are people who will burn every village on that cliff to keep it buried. I'm offering you the exit before they arrive. That's generosity, Xanthe. That's the last generosity you're going to be offered."

Behind him, his men had begun, casually, professionally, to search the body's pack.

* * *

I bought time the only way I had left: I gave him true things shaped like a bargain.

"The vault's sealed," I said. "Stone mechanism, pre-colonial work. Tamsin's the only one who knows the approach, and she'll die before she opens it for a syndicate man. Kill me, kill her, and your employers get a hole in a cliff and a corpse problem. Give me until tomorrow night. Let me talk her down. She trusts me—barely, but barely is a door."

Pell considered this with his head tilted, and I watched him weigh it, and I understood with cold clarity that he was weighing it honestly—he genuinely believed everyone could profit, that every situation had a version where nobody had to lose, provided the losing was scheduled politely in advance.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "Sunset. I'll be at the river bend below the spring line. Bring her, bring the location, and bring yourself, and we'll all walk out of this valley richer than we deserve." He refolded his debt paper and held it out to me—not handing it over, just showing it, the way a priest shows a relic. "This is the last day it exists, Xanthe. Think of that. One hundred and fourteen thousand marks, gone, like weather clearing."

Then he shook my hand. He had a soft grip, careful, like a man handling something he might later need to sell.

They went back down the slope the way they'd come, stepping around the scout without looking at him, and the outboard started on the river, and the sound faded, and the hillside was ours again except for the body lying in the scrub with its eyes closed and its orange folio gone.

* * *

Sefa and I stood over the dead man for a long time.

"Meridian Concessions," she said finally. "Rival claimant. So there's a third player I didn't even know about."

"There's always a third player. There's a fourth and fifth too, probably, all hiking toward the same hole in the rock." I crouched and went through the pack Pell's men had left—water bottle, ration tins, a cheap compass, and a survey notebook with three days of readings and a sketched map showing the caves' eastern approaches. He'd been closer than any of us. He'd been hours away from finding what Tamsin had spent years guarding.

"We should bury him," Sefa said.

"Tamsin won't allow a fire. And the ground here is limestone—we'd need hours and a pick." I looked at the man's face, slack and ordinary, younger than Pell's estimate of Pell's knees. Someone in Corvane would wait for a settlement letter that explained everything in cheerful language. "We mark him. We tell someone. That's what we can do."

"That's not nothing," Sefa said. But she didn't say it like she believed it.

Above us, Tamsin materialized out of the rocks, wild-eyed, staring down at the body, and I braced for her to bolt—for her to decide we were just another weather front and vanish into the caves alone. Instead she crouched at the edge of camp, keeping distance, and looked at me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before. Not fear. Recognition.

"Now you've seen him do it," she said. "Now you know what optimism looks like up close."

"I know."

"He offered you a clean ledger."

"He offered."

"And you're going to go down into the dark anyway." It wasn't a question. Her voice cracked on *down*, and I realized she wasn't asking about the caves. She was asking about me—about what a man does after he's watched a stranger's eyes get closed by the man who killed him. Whether he climbs toward the thing anyway, knowing every hand reaching for it is dirtier than his, or whether he takes the exit and lives unowned and unforgiven.

I looked at the escarpment wall. Somewhere inside it was a room full of paper that could legitimize or destroy every land claim in the valley—every village, every concession, every smiling man with a thermos. Somewhere inside it, if Tamsin's terror was honest, was Ivo Marsh, alive enough to have opinions about what happened next.

"Tomorrow night," I said. "Sunset. That's our window. After that, Pell stops being generous."

Tamsin stared at me a long moment. Then she turned and started up the slope toward the caves, and over her shoulder she said the thing that made my blood go cold and my feet move:

"Then we'd better go down tonight. Because Marsh left a door that only opens from the inside—and I'm done deciding alone whether the world deserves what's behind it."
