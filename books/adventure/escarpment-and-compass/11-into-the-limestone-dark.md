## 11. Into the Limestone Dark

I've run cargo through water you couldn't see the bottom of, through fog so thick the river was a rumor, through checkpoints where the men with guns smiled like they were doing me a favor. I know what pressure feels like. It's not dramatic. It's patient. It sits on your chest and waits for you to make a mistake.

The limestone under the Kestrel Escarpment pressed down on me exactly like that.

Tamsin went first. Of course she did. She moved through the dark like it owed her money and she'd come to collect, bare feet finding purchase on wet stone where my boots slipped twice in the first hundred meters. Xanthe came behind her with the lamp, then me, then the two hired hands Sefa's money had bought—Dov and Retch, which told you everything about them before they said a word.

"Touch nothing that looks carved," Tamsin said, without turning around. "If it looks carved, it is carved, and if it is carved, it's older than every word anyone has ever said to you about who owns this ground."

"Cheerful," Retch muttered.

"She's been down here alone for how long?" Dov asked me.

"Long enough," I said, and left it there.

* * *

The entrance was a crack in the cliff face that Tamsin had disguised with cut brush, and I only understood how deliberate the disguise was when she pulled it aside and revealed hinges—iron, rusted, but hinges nonetheless. Someone had built a door into the mountain and then made the mountain forget it.

"You knew this was here," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"I helped find it." Tamsin's voice came out flat as slate. "That was the job. Find it, document it, secure it. The Halcyon Expedition, professional inheritors of other people's pasts, hired by people who wanted to own what they found." She ducked through the doorway. "I stayed because I read what we found."

The dark took her words and kept them.

* * *

The first markers appeared within the hour.

Lamps up. That was Xanthe's call, and a good one—we'd burn fuel faster, but we'd keep our minds. The light caught the walls and I stopped walking, because the walls were talking.

Carved figures, rank on rank, going back into the black farther than our lamps could reach. People, mostly. Rows of them, hands joined, some holding objects I couldn't name—a circle, a bird, something like a river shown as a braided rope. Below each figure, lines of script in an alphabet I'd never seen, dense and sure, cut by someone who knew their stone the way I know my boat.

"How old?" Xanthe asked quietly.

Tamsin ran her thumb near—not touching—one of the figures. "Older than the concession registry. Older than the colonial surveys. Older than the oldest map in Corvane's archive, and I've seen the archive lie about its age." She looked back at us, lamp-light carving her face into planes and shadow. "Maren's people didn't build this. Maren's people inherited it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point."

We walked on. The ceiling lowered. My shoulders started to ache from hunching, and I realized I'd been holding my breath in increments, releasing it only when the stone allowed.

* * *

There's a thing that happens to river pilots underground. Maybe it happens to everyone, but I felt it in my hands first: the absence of current. Forty years of reading water, of feeling the pull and push of a living river through a hull, and now nothing. Still air. Still stone. The silence wasn't empty—it was full, the way deep water is full, pressing in from all sides at once.

Dov felt it too. He kept checking his rifle sling, touching it like a rosary.

"How far down does this go?" he asked.

"Down isn't the right direction," Tamsin said. "In."

"In where?"

She didn't answer. Ahead, the passage bent, and when we rounded the bend the lamps found a chamber that made even Retch stop complaining.

Stalactites hung like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and between them, more carvings—but these were different. These were records. Long horizontal bands of script running the length of the wall, interrupted at intervals by larger figures: a woman holding scales, a man planting a tree, a gathering of many figures around a shape I finally recognized, because I'd seen its echo on Maren's door lintel—the escarpment itself, drawn as a sleeping animal.

"They're deeds," Tamsin said. Her voice had changed. Softer. Almost reverent, and hating itself for it. "Boundary records. Successions. Treaties. This whole wall is a legal document older than the concept of paper."

Xanthe stood very still, lamp raised, and I watched his face do the arithmetic. I'd watched him do it on the boat for days—numbers behind his eyes, debt and distance and weather all getting weighed against each other. But this time the numbers weren't adding up to anything he could sell.

"This is what Marsh found," he said.

"This is the anteroom," Tamsin said. "To what Marsh found."

* * *

We rested at the edge of that chamber, and I'll tell you what rest means underground: you sit with your back against ten thousand years of stone and you feel every year of it. I ate dried fish and drank from my canteen and listened to the drip of water somewhere far off, keeping time like a patient creditor.

Retch asked Tamsin if there was anything down here worth taking. Just asking. Just curious.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"The last man who asked me that," she said, "is the reason there's an empty grave at the forward camp."

Nobody asked again.

* * *

The passage after the chamber narrowed until we went through sideways, chests against stone, breathing in slivers. I don't love tight places. Give me open water and a storm front any day—at least out there you can see the thing trying to kill you. Down here the mountain just held us, indifferent as a ledger, and I thought about Odile Ferrant's permits expiring somewhere above us, nineteen days becoming fewer, and how strange it was that a piece of paper could claim to own the skin of the earth while the earth itself kept its own records underneath, in stone, in an alphabet no clerk could file.

Then the passage opened, and dropped.

A stair. Cut into the rock, descending in smooth flights, each step worn concave in the center by feet—thousands of feet, generations of them, long before anyone thought to write a permit. A thin thread of water ran down a channel beside the steps, cold and clean, and Tamsin knelt and drank from it without hesitation, so we did too.

"It's a road," Xanthe said.

"It's the road," Tamsin said. "Everything else up there—" she jerked her chin toward the ceiling, toward the escarpment, toward the whole claimed surface of the valley— "is the detour."

We descended for hours. My knees reported in. Dov's lamp died and we consolidated without discussion, three lights for five people, the math of the dark getting worse. And still the markers came—smaller now, personal, single figures carved in niches like the mountain was keeping attendance. Names, maybe. Or promises. I put my hand near one, not touching, and felt the cold come off the stone like the cold off deep water, and I understood suddenly why Tamsin had gone half-mad guarding this place.

It wasn't the treasure. It was the weight. History down here doesn't sit in the past. It presses. It's all happening at once, all the time, and you either carry it or it carries you under.

* * *

Near the bottom of the stair flight, Tamsin stopped so abruptly I nearly went into her back.

Ahead, the passage ended at a wall—and the wall was wrong. Too regular. Fitted blocks, seamless, with a slot in the center at chest height. Not a seal exactly. A lock waiting for a key, or a door waiting for a reason.

Tamsin stood before it with her hand hovering over the slot, and her shoulders were shaking.

"This is as far as I ever let myself come," she said. "Ivo came closer. Ivo went inside." She turned, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious at being wet. "He sealed it behind him. From the inside. He's been in there for—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. We'd all done the math on the radio fragment, on the coordinates, on the months of silence.

Xanthe stepped forward. He looked at the door the way he looked at a supercell building on the flats—that flat, hungry, calculating stare, except this time I saw something else underneath it. Fear, maybe. Or hope. With him it was always hard to tell the difference.

"Tamsin," he said quietly. "Your condition. No one touches the inheritance."

"No one sells it," she corrected. "There's a difference, and I'm learning the difference matters."

From behind the wall—faint, muffled, impossible—came a sound none of us expected.

Scratching. Deliberate. Rhythmic.

Someone, on the other side, was knocking.
