## 12. The Inheritance Vault

The passage narrowed until Tamsin had to turn sideways, her lantern held high, and the light ran along walls that were not wall at all but writing—rows of carved glyphs, deliberate as ledger lines, descending into the dark like a staircase made of sentences.

"Nobody touch the marks," she said, for the fourth time since we'd entered. Her voice had gone strange down here, stripped of the wildness she wore on the surface, replaced by something almost clerical. Almost reverent. "The old ones counted everything. Who lived where. Who married whom. Who owed what to whom." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "First accountants in the valley."

Sefa's lamp swung behind me. I could hear her breathing, controlled and shallow, the way she breathed through rapids. "How much farther?"

"When the stone stops being rude," Tamsin said.

I didn't understand until another hundred meters, when the chisel-marks changed. The rough-hewn tunnel gave way to dressed stone, fitted so precisely I couldn't have slid a knife between blocks. Someone had built this. Not dug it—*built* it, with intention, with pride, with a geometry that said *we expected this place to outlast us and we were right.*

The air changed too. Cooler. Drier. Moving faintly, though from where I couldn't say—a ventilation shaft somewhere, engineered centuries before anyone in Corvane had thought about airflow as a discipline.

Tamsin stopped at a slab blocking the passage. It stood maybe chest-high, and across its face ran a single line of carving, deeper than the rest, worn smooth in one spot as if generations of hands had rested there.

She put her palm on the worn place and pushed.

The slab moved without a sound. Counterweighted. Maintained. My skin prickled. This door had been oiled within living memory—or within the last few years, anyway—and there was only one person who could have done it.

Beyond the slab, the passage opened.

I've chased storms for eleven years. I've stood under a two-mile-wide mesocyclone with hail cracking off my helmet like rifle fire, and I know what awe does to the body: it empties you. Takes your breath not because the thing is beautiful but because it is *large*, larger than the category you'd built to hold it.

The vault did that.

It was a chamber the size of Maren's whole meeting hall, and every surface was shelf, niche, alcove. Stone cylinders sat in rows, sealed with wax that had gone amber with age. Flat sheets of some material I couldn't name—bark pressed and polished, or hide cured to parchment stiffness—lay stacked in cedar boxes that smelled, impossibly, of the tree they'd come from. Copper plates hung on pegs, engraved edge to edge. And along one entire wall, mounted like trophies or like evidence, stretched a sequence of woven panels: knotted cord, dyed thread, beads of shell and jet, arranged in patterns that repeated with variations the way weather repeats with variations.

"It's an archive," Sefa said quietly. "A whole civilization's filing system."

"Not a civilization," Tamsin said. "Villages. Eleven of them. The escarpment people weren't one nation—they were neighbors who kept records *together*. That's the point. That's the whole point." She walked to the nearest shelf and hovered her hand over a cylinder without touching it. "Deeds. Boundaries described by springs and lightning-struck trees and the flight paths of herons, because rivers move but herons don't change their minds. Genealogies going back past anyone's counting. Treaties between villages. Treaties with people downstream. Treaties—" her voice caught, steadied "—treaties with the first colonial governor. Signed. Witnessed. Recognized."

I looked at her. "Recognized how?"

"The governor's own seal is on the copper." She pointed to a plate near the end of the row, green with patina, stamped with an emblem I knew from the customs house in Corvane—the old crown-and-heron of the founding administration. "They didn't conquer this valley, Xanthe. They *contracted* with it. And then everyone downstream forgot, because forgetting was profitable."

Sefa set her lamp down slowly, like a person setting down something breakable. "You're saying these documents would void every concession in the valley."

"I'm saying worse." Tamsin finally touched a cylinder, lifting it with both hands like an infant. "Concessions are granted by the state. The state's claim comes from colonial grant. Colonial grant is subordinate to prior title when prior title can be proven. This—" she turned the cylinder in the lamplight "—is proof. Not legend. Not oral history a magistrate can wave away. Sealed, witnessed, cross-referenced records, older than the state itself, that say the escarpment villages own the ground every syndicate office is built on."

The silence after that had weight, like the pressure drop before a twister touches down.

"And Halcyon found this," I said. "And reported it."

"Halcyon found this and understood what reporting it meant." Tamsin returned the cylinder to its niche with exaggerated care. "Do you know what happens to a document like that in Corvane? It goes into a registry. Then it goes into a drawer. Then the drawer gets reassigned, and the document gets misfiled, and ten years later nobody can produce it. Or it gets authenticated, and then challenged, and the challenge takes twenty years while the concessions strip the valley bare, and by the time the courts rule, there's nothing left to own but holes." Her eyes came up, fierce and exhausted. "Ivo figured that out first. He always figured things out first. It was insufferable."

"Where is he?"

She didn't answer. She walked instead to the far wall, to a desk I hadn't registered as a desk—it was expedition equipment, actually, a folding table and a camp chair and a battery lamp, all of it jarring against the ancient stone like a gunshot against a hymn. On the table lay notebooks. Dozens. And a radio handset wired to a jury-rigged antenna running up a natural fissure in the rock.

The radio that had been transmitting fragments. The coordinates. Ivo Marsh's voice on the static, half-swallowed, repeating numbers into a valley that wasn't answering.

"He's been down here," Tamsin said, "the whole time."

* * *

We found him in an adjoining chamber, smaller, warmer, clearly lived-in. A bedroll. A stove vented through the same clever fissure. Jars of preserved food, most empty, a few holding the dregs of something dark. And Ivo Marsh himself, sitting up against the wall with a blanket over his legs, watching our lamps approach with an expression I couldn't parse until I got closer and realized it was relief fighting fury, and losing.

"You brought people," he said. His voice was gravel and rust. He'd lost weight the way candles lose wax—all at once, everywhere. "Tamsin. I told you—"

"They were going to find it anyway." She crouched beside him, and for a moment the feral guardian and the expedition leader just looked at each other, two people who'd been carrying the same weight alone in different places. "Better people who understand it than Pell's lot cutting the door open with explosives."

"Pell." His mouth twisted. "He's offered you his generosity yet? He will. He offers everyone. It's how he keeps his hands clean—he never takes anything, he *trades* for it, and the trade is always your future for his present."

"He's already tried," I said. "Twice."

Marsh studied me then, really studied me, the way you study weather. "Xanthe Dravenmoor. The storm broker. Odile Ferrant sent you upriver with a salvage permit and a straight face." He coughed, wet and long, and Tamsin steadied his shoulder until it passed. "Let me guess the terms. Recover the expedition. Secure whatever we found. Delivery before expiry, all very legal, all very deniable."

"That's the shape of it."

"And do you know what 'delivery' means, once the vault reaches Corvane?" He gestured at the chamber around us—at the cylinders, the copper, the woven panels full of knots that were names and promises and boundaries. "It means this becomes leverage. It means the syndicate sits on the true title and sells its silence back to the villages piece by piece, forever. It means the state negotiates a quiet settlement, seals the records, and grants new concessions over the graves of the people who actually own this ground. It means the machine eats the cure along with the disease." He leaned forward, and even diminished, even starving, he had the gravity of a man who'd made a decision and lived inside it long enough for it to become bone. "I stayed down here, Mr. Dravenmoor, because the moment these documents see daylight in the wrong room, they die. I have spent four months keeping them alive in the dark. Four months eating my own supplies and talking to a radio that doesn't answer, because dead men can't be negotiated with, and neither can buried archives."

"So destroy them," Sefa said from the doorway. Her voice was flat, practical. "That's what you're building toward. Burn the vault, void the proof, and the concessions stand—but so does the theft. At least it's honest theft."

"No." Marsh shook his head slowly. "Destruction was the second option. It's the coward's option and I'll admit I considered it. But look—" he reached to the table, took up one of the notebooks, and held it out to me with a hand that trembled from more than weakness. "Read the last entries. I found a third path. It took me long enough, and I'm not sure I can carry it myself anymore, which is why the universe finally sent me a storm chaser with debts and a boat pilot with a conscience she won't admit to."

I took the notebook. The handwriting started strong and degraded page by page, letters slumping like tired soldiers, but the final entry was written slowly, deliberately, each word chosen:

*Day 118. The vault cannot go to Corvane. The vault cannot stay buried. There is a third door: daylight without custody. If the contents are copied and broadcast—every registry, every newsroom, every claimant, all at once—then no one owns the truth and everyone holds it. Legitimacy can't be leveraged if it's already public. The villages' title stands because it was always real. The concessions fall because they were always fiction. You can't ransom what's already free.*

*Someone has to carry it. Someone fast, someone desperate enough to burn their own ledgers doing it.*

*I'm sorry. I know what I'm asking.*

I closed the notebook. My hands weren't steady either.

Broadcast. Not deliver. Not destroy. Copy the genealogies, the treaties, the deeds—copy everything portable—and put it into a thousand hands simultaneously, so that no collector, no clerk, no concessionaire could ever gather it back into a drawer. It was exactly the kind of plan I'd have called insane a month ago. It was exactly the kind of plan that required someone whose whole life had been reading systems—pressure gradients, wind shear, the telltales before a front breaks—and finding the moment to move.

Nineteen days had become fewer. Somewhere behind us, Pell's gunboats were working upriver. Somewhere ahead, Odile's clock was ticking toward expiry, and when it expired, her employers would file claims that would take a generation to unwind—if they could be unwound at all.

"This is a suicide run," Sefa said, reading the notebook over my shoulder. "Corvane under guard, carrying copies of the most dangerous paper in the valley, against the clock, against the syndicate, against the concession house security. You'd need a miracle of timing."

I thought about supercells. About the hour of black sky before the tornado drops, when every sensible person is underground and the only people moving are the ones who've learned that chaos has structure, that the worst weather opens doors that calm weather keeps shut.

"No," I said. "You'd need a storm."

Behind us, deep in the vault, Tamsin had begun pulling cedar boxes from the shelves, laying out the genealogies with a curator's tenderness, choosing what would travel. And above us, far above the limestone and the carved stone, the afternoon light through the escarpment trees had gone the particular bruised yellow I'd known all my life—

—the color the sky turns before it decides to break something open.
