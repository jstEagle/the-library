## 13. Marsh, Alive Enough

The vault had a door, and the door had a voice.

"Stop there." The words came thin through the limestone, scraped down to wire and rust. "State your business or I'll drop the roof on you."

Xanthe froze with his lamp raised. Behind him, Tamsin made a sound he'd never heard from her—not a growl, not a warning, but something small and broken, like a hinge giving way after years of holding.

"Ivo," she said.

Silence. Long enough that Xanthe counted his own heartbeats against the drip of water somewhere in the dark behind them. Then, quieter: "Tamsin Okafor?"

"You sound terrible."

A laugh came through the stone, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing went on so long that Sefa shifted her weight beside Xanthe and murmured, "If that's the man who found a fortune, he's about half a fortune left."

The door was not a door in any sense Xanthe understood. It was a slab of fitted limestone, dressed to match the wall around it so precisely that Tamsin's guidance alone had let them find it—a seam no wider than a knife blade, running floor to ceiling in a chamber of carved markers. She had put her palm flat against it and said, *He's behind here. He has to be. He sealed himself in when we ran,* and her voice had cracked on the last word for the first time since they'd dragged her out of the caves.

Now she pressed both hands to the stone. "Ivo. It's me. Open it."

"No." No hesitation at all. "You brought people."

"One's a pilot. One's—" Tamsin glanced back at Xanthe, and whatever she saw in his face made her pause. "One's a storm chaser who owes money to bad men. He hasn't touched anything."

"He will. Everyone touches everything eventually. That's what people are." A pause, then, closer to the seam, almost conversational: "How did you find me?"

"The markers," Tamsin said. "And the fact that you never came down. I've been guarding the approach for—"

"I know how long. I could hear you sometimes. Singing at the tree line. You always sang off-key when you were frightened."

Xanthe watched something move across Tamsin's face that he had no name for. Grief and relief tangled together, wearing each other's clothes.

"Ivo," she said gently, the way you'd speak to a man standing on a ledge, "you're going to die in there."

"I'm aware. I've done the arithmetic several times. It keeps coming out the same."

"Then open the door and die in the light at least."

Another silence. When Marsh spoke again, his voice had changed—the rust gone out of it, replaced by something harder, a surveyor's precision. "Whoever you are, storm chaser. What's your name?"

"Xanthe Dravenmoor."

"What are you carrying?"

"A compass. A debt ledger I can't pay. Copies of some genealogies your geologist let me photograph before she decided whether to kill me."

"And why are you here? Truthfully. I'll know if you lie. I've spent eleven months listening to rock, and rock doesn't lie, and I've gotten very good at hearing the difference."

Xanthe considered lying anyway. It was, he reflected, more or less his native language. But there was something about talking through a wall to a dying man that stripped the habit bare.

"I took a salvage contract," he said. "Recover the Halcyon Expedition before the concession lapses. Nineteen days when I signed. Sixteen now. If I deliver proof of what you found—or proof you're dead—the mineral rights get sorted by lawyers in Corvane, and every one of them is bought. If I don't deliver, the same lawyers sort it anyway, just without me getting paid, and collectors I genuinely dislike come collect my ledger instead."

"Good," Marsh said. "That's the first honest sentence anyone's spoken near this door in a year. Now here's mine." A scraping sound, weight shifting against stone. "I found the inheritance vault. Deeds. Treaties. Genealogies going back four hundred years before the first colonial charter. The escarpment villages hold lawful title to this valley. Not squatter's rights. Not oral tradition. *Paper.* Sealed, witnessed, older than anything in any registry office in Corvane. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means every concession permit in the region is void," Xanthe said slowly. "Every one. Including the ones Odile Ferrant's firm manufactured the expiry on."

"So you know about that too. Good. Then you understand why I sealed myself in with it." His voice dropped. "Because the moment those documents surface, three things happen. Either they go to the syndicate, who burn them and keep selling the valley. Or they go to the state registries, where lawyers will litigate their authenticity until every witness is dead and the villages die waiting. Or—" and here his voice caught, "—or someone delivers them properly, publicly, and the whole rotten structure comes down, and the people who built it send men with guns to make an example of everyone involved. Starting with the villages. Starting with Maren's people. Starting with whoever carried them."

"That last option sounds right to me," Xanthe said.

"Of course it does. You haven't been shot at yet over paper."

"I've been shot at over weather data. It's surprisingly similar."

The laugh came again, weaker. Then: "Tamsin. Come to the seam. Put your hand where mine is."

She did. Through the stone, faintly, Xanthe heard two palms meet, separated by six inches of limestone that had been standing longer than any nation either of them belonged to.

"I stayed alive to protect these documents," Marsh said. "Eleven months. I ate cave crickets and drank seep water and I did the arithmetic every morning. And now I'm asking you to do the hardest thing I couldn't do myself." A breath. "Burn them."

Tamsin's hand flinched against the stone.

"Burn them?" she whispered.

"Deliver them to no one. Destroy them. Let the valley belong to whoever's strong enough to hold it, as it always has. Because the alternative—" his voice fractured, "—the alternative gets people killed, Tamsin. All of them. Every village. You've seen what Pell does to a scout who looks at him wrong. Now imagine what the syndicate does to a valley that legally owns itself."

Xanthe stood very still in the lamplight. Somewhere above them, muffled through hundreds of feet of escarpment, thunder rolled—the front he'd been watching build all day, the one his bones had been reading since dawn.

"Three options," he said quietly. "Deliver, destroy, or defy."

"Yes."

"And you're asking me to destroy."

"I'm begging you."

* * *

They made camp in the marker chamber because none of them could leave. Tamsin sat with her back against the sealed door, arms wrapped around her knees, and didn't speak for an hour. Sefa dealt cards onto a flat stone and played solitaire with the grim concentration of a woman who needed her hands busy or they'd start doing something else.

Xanthe climbed.

Not far. Just up the passage Tamsin had shown them, to a shelf where the cave opened through a crack to the night sky, and he could see stars and the distant flicker of lightning over the flats he'd chased for ten years. He sat with his ledger in his lap—the physical one, the syndicate's copy, the one Mr. Pell had been so cheerful about—and read the numbers again.

Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six vellmarks. Compounded monthly. The number had been his horizon for three years, the fixed point he steered by. Every storm sold, every data packet couriered, every risk taken—it all bent toward that figure the way iron filings bend toward a magnet. Stop moving, and the number catches you. Keep moving, and you never have to look at anything else.

He'd told himself that was freedom once. He'd stopped believing it somewhere around the burned cache, but the belief hadn't yet been replaced with anything, and a man with a hole where his creed used to be will grab at whatever falls into it.

Below him, faintly, he heard Tamsin start talking to the door again. Low, urgent, in fragments he couldn't quite catch. Pleading. Then angry. Then quiet.

When he climbed back down, she was standing, and her eyes were dry and terrible.

"He wants me to help burn them," she said. "Four hundred years of my ancestors' legal existence, and he wants ash."

"He kept you alive by staying in there," Sefa observed, not looking up from her cards. "Odd way to love someone."

"He's right about the danger," Xanthe said.

"He's right about nothing. He's been alone in the dark so long he's forgotten what daylight does." She rounded on him. "You photographed the genealogies. You have copies. So tell me, Dravenmoor—when you were lying in Maren's village with a hole in your side, and they fed you anyway, and asked nothing—which option did that feel like? Delivery? Destruction?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She saw it land.

"They didn't feed you because you were useful," Tamsin said. "They fed you because that's what the valley does. It remembers who belongs to it. And those papers are the valley remembering out loud, in writing, in a language Corvane's lawyers can't argue with." Her voice shook. "Ivo thinks protecting the documents means burying them. But documents buried aren't protected. They're just dead. Like him, if we leave him in there."

Through the door, faintly: "I can hear you, Tamsin."

"Good," she called back. "Then hear this. I'm not burning them. And I'm not leaving you."

* * *

It was Sefa who broke the deadlock, near dawn, in her flat pilot's way.

"Here's the problem as I see it," she said, shuffling cards in the lamplight while the other two stared at her. "Marsh says deliver means death for the villages. Fine. Marsh says destroy means the valley stays stolen forever. Also fine. Both true. So the question isn't which bad thing to pick." She set the deck down. "The question is whether there's a third thing nobody's thought of, because everyone arguing so far has been thinking like people who own things."

"Meaning what?" Tamsin asked.

"Meaning I've run cargo for concessionaires for nine years, and every one of them has one fear bigger than losing money." She tapped the stone floor. "Losing control of the story. Pell doesn't want the documents because they're valuable. He wants them because whoever holds them decides what happens next. Odile's firm manufactured the expiry for the same reason. Everyone in this game is playing to be the one who moves the pieces." She looked at Xanthe. "But you're a weather man. What do you know that they don't?"

Xanthe stared at her. And then, slowly, he started to smile, because the answer had been sitting in his skull for ten years, wearing work clothes.

"Weather doesn't negotiate," he said. "Weather doesn't hold. Weather doesn't care who holds title. You can't buy a supercell, and you can't threaten it, and when it arrives, every gunboat in the fleet ties up at dock and every radio tower in Corvane goes dark except the ones that matter." He stood. "There's a front coming. Big one. Three days out, maybe four. I've been feeling it in my teeth since yesterday."

Sefa nodded slowly. "And during a blackout—"

"Nobody controls the story. For about six hours, nobody controls anything." He looked at the sealed door, at the faint shape of a dying man on the other side of six inches of stone. "Which means the documents don't have to be delivered to anyone. They don't have to be destroyed. They just have to be *everywhere*. All at once. To every registry, every newsroom, every rival claimant, every village elder with a shortwave set. Simultaneously. Unownable."

Silence in the marker chamber. Even the dripping seemed to pause.

"That's insane," Tamsin said.

"It's a broadcast," Xanthe said. "I've spent a decade timing runs under storm fronts. This is the same skill. Different cargo."

Behind the door, after a long moment, came the sound of laughter—real laughter this time, ragged and disbelieving, dissolving into coughing.

"A broadcast," Marsh wheezed. "You magnificent idiot. Do you know what I've been doing in here? Trying to solve it like a surveyor. Boundaries, claims, jurisdictions. And the answer was weather." The coughing took him again, worse. When it passed, his voice came very quiet. "Open the door, Tamsin. I want to see the man who beat me with a forecast."

* * *

Getting the door open took two hours. The counterweight mechanism was limestone blocks in a channel, designed to be sealed from inside and nearly impossible to breach from outside—but Marsh, on the other side, could shift his end, and between his failing strength and Tamsin's leverage with a pry bar salvaged from the Halcyon camp, the slab ground aside inch by inch.

The smell hit them first. Eleven months of a human life in a sealed room—candle smoke, waste, and beneath it something like old paper and clean stone.

Then the lamplight found him.

Ivo Marsh had been a big man once; the expedition photographs showed a broad-shouldered surveyor with a beard like a ship's figurehead. What remained was a frame draped in skin, propped against a reading table stacked with oilcloth-wrapped bundles. His beard had gone fully grey. His eyes, though—his eyes were still expedition-leader's eyes, sharp and amused and appraising, and they fixed on Xanthe with unnerving directness.

"Dravenmoor," he rasped. "You look like a man who's good in bad conditions."

"It's the only kind I work in."

Marsh gestured at the bundles with a hand that trembled. "There it is. Four hundred years of lawful ownership. Enough paper to hang every concessionaire from the escarpment or make every village rich, depending on whose hands it lands in." He pushed himself upright, swaying, and Tamsin moved to steady him and he let her, which Xanthe suspected cost him something. "So. Last chance for the responsible option. Burn it. Walk away. Live long, dull lives."

"No," Xanthe said.

"No," Tamsin said, at the same instant.

Marsh looked from one to the other, and then at Sefa, who shrugged and said, "I just fly the boat."

Something moved across the ruined face—regret, maybe, or relief, or both wearing each other's clothes, the way grief and relief had on Tamsin's. "I was afraid you'd say that," he said. "And I was hoping you would." He gripped the table. "Listen carefully, then, because I've had eleven months to think and you'll have three days. The documents are real, but authenticity is a war, not a fact. Whoever broadcasts them must also broadcast *provenance*—chain of custody, my logs, Tamsin's testimony, the lot—or Corvane's lawyers will shred it in a year. And whoever broadcasts them becomes the most hunted person in the region. Not eventually. Immediately."

"I've been hunted for three years," Xanthe said. "At least this time I'd deserve it."

Marsh studied him for a long moment. Then he laughed, softly, and reached out and gripped Xanthe's forearm with a strength that surprised them both.

"My father had a saying about men who chase storms," he said. "He said they're either running from something or they've finally found something worth standing in front of." His eyes were bright and feverish and absolutely clear. "Which are you, Dravenmoor?"

Outside, far above, thunder rolled down the escarpment like an answer arriving early—and Xanthe, who had spent his whole life reading the sky, felt the pressure dropping and knew the front was coming faster than he'd forecast.

Two days, maybe less. And somewhere downriver, he'd have bet his ledger on it, Mr. Pell was smiling at the same clouds.
