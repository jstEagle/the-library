## 14. Six Days to Expiry

The clock in Odile Ferrant's office ran four minutes fast, and she had never corrected it.

She'd bought it at a bankruptcy auction eleven years ago—a brass thing with a chipped face, pulled off the wall of a survey firm that had gone under when its permits lapsed. Four minutes fast was a discipline. If you lived your whole day slightly ahead of the truth, you were never late for it. The alternative was what had happened to the survey firm: men in good suits discovering, all at once and together, that the ground beneath their signatures belonged to someone else.

She wound it every morning. She was winding it now, six days before the Halcyon concession expired, when the message light on her desk unit began to pulse amber.

Xanthe Dravenmoor had missed his check-in.

* * *

The contract required contact every forty-eight hours. Voice or coded burst, didn't matter which—only that it came, timestamped, from the field. Dravenmoor had made his first two on schedule, terse as receipts. *Position logged. River rising. Nothing found.* Then nothing for fifty-one hours, and counting.

Odile sat down and read the failure notice twice. Around her, the Corvane floor of Ferrant & Associates was doing its morning imitation of diligence: clerks pulling files, junior associates arguing about filing fees, someone laughing too loudly near the coffee station. The building had been a granary once. You could still smell it in the walls on humid days—old wheat, old money, old patience. Grain waited. That was the whole philosophy of the place. Concessions, claims, permits: all of it was grain in a silo, and Ferrant & Associates owned the silo.

Except the grain in this particular silo was about to spoil.

Nineteen days ago, when she'd put the pen in Dravenmoor's hand, the arithmetic had been clean. The Halcyon Expedition had gone silent upriver; the concession's mineral rights would revert to the state on expiry unless "recovery of personnel and assets" was certified complete. Her client—the client whose name appeared nowhere in her files, because the client was a nesting doll of holding companies three layers deep—needed that certification. Needed it before the rival claimants filed, needed it before the state surveyors came down from the plateau, needed it the way a man with a hole in his boat needs the sea to be theoretical.

So they'd manufactured the deadline. Not illegally—that was the elegant part. The expiry clause had been sitting in the original permit like a landmine under snow, drafted eighty years ago by lawyers who understood that documents outlive intentions. All Odile's firm had done was decline to extend it. Declining was free. Declining left no fingerprints. You simply let the calendar do the work that muscle used to.

And you hired a storm chaser with debts, because a man drowning will sign anything, and because men like Xanthe Dravenmoor asked exactly one question fewer than was safe.

She had liked him, which annoyed her. He'd lied to her in the interview—about having no stake in the valley, about the flatness of his voice when he said it—and she'd signed him anyway, because a liar who lies badly is more reliable than an honest man who's never been tested. He wanted the money. Money narrowed a person until they fit through doors you'd built for them.

Now he'd missed a check-in, and Odile discovered she was not thinking about the certification timeline. She was thinking about the way he'd looked at the permit map on her desk, at the escarpment marked in the cartographer's blank green—the color that means *nothing here worth naming*—and how something behind his eyes had said otherwise.

"Marisol," she called. "Get me the field protocol file."

* * *

Field protocol was a document she'd written herself, two years back, and it existed for exactly this situation: a contractor goes dark inside a live concession window. The options were laid out in ascending order of deniability. One: wait. Two: ping the contractor's bond surety. Three: dispatch security.

Option three was the one her client preferred, and her client had made its preference known in the language clients use, which is the language of concern. *We want to make sure Mr. Dravenmoor is safe,* the message had read, arriving yesterday through three intermediaries. *Send people. Capable ones.*

Capable ones. In Odile's experience, the phrase meant men who could find things and make other things stop existing, and who billed by the week whether they did either. She knew the firm they'd want her to use—Kestrel Ridge Protective Services, which shared a post-office box with one of the rival claimants and half a board member with another. Sending Kestrel Ridge after Dravenmoor wasn't protection. It was a second salvage operation, running parallel to the first, with a shorter moral leash.

She spread the maps across her desk. The river corridor, the burned checkpoint markers, the escarpment rising out of the blank green like a knuckle. Somewhere along that line, a man who owed the Vellum Syndicate more than he'd ever earn honestly was either dead, deserted, or finding something.

Only one of those three outcomes served her client. And here was the thing Odile had been circling for nineteen days without landing on: she no longer entirely wanted her client to be served.

It wasn't conscience, exactly. She distrusted the word. Conscience was what people claimed afterward, retroactive virtue applied like a stamp. What she had was older and less flattering: she was a clerk, and clerks read everything, and reading everything does something to you. She had spent nine years inside the paper skeleton of the valley's ownership. She knew which deeds rested on treaties no one had honored, which genealogies had been pruned, which signatures were forgeries wearing respectable ink. The whole structure was sound the way a termite gallery is sound—you could walk on it right up until you couldn't.

The Halcyon Expedition had gone into the escarpment looking for mineral assays. Odile had read Ivo Marsh's last filed report before the silence, all dry geology and supply requisitions, except for one line near the end that she'd thought about maybe forty times since:

*Local informants describe subsurface structures of considerable antiquity. Recommend we assess legal implications before proceeding further inland.*

Marsh had understood what he was walking toward. And then he'd stopped filing reports.

"Marisol," she said again. "I need a security retainer drafted. Not Kestrel Ridge."

Her assistant appeared in the doorway with the expression of a woman bracing for weather. "The client specified—"

"The client specified capable. I'm choosing capable." Odile pulled a card from the locked drawer—actual paper, actual lock, because some things shouldn't live in systems with logs. A small outfit out of the port district. Ex-naval types, expensive, incurious. "Six personnel. Reconnaissance only. They locate Dravenmoor, they confirm status, they report to me and no one else. Nobody touches him. Nobody touches anything he's carrying."

"And if the client asks why we're not using their people?"

"Then the client can ask me directly, instead of through intermediaries, and I'll explain the liability profile of sending a rival's mercenaries onto our concession." She signed the draft. "Also—I want the original grant documents pulled. The colonial-era ones. Everything from before the permit system."

Marisol blinked. "For the expiry filing?"

"For my reading." Odile met her look evenly. "A clerk should know what's in her own archive."

* * *

That night she stayed late, alone with the founding papers of the valley, and the building's old-granary quiet settled around her like silt.

The documents were beautiful, in the way weapons are beautiful. She read land grants signed by governors who'd never seen the escarpment, boundaries drawn along rivers that had moved since, compensation clauses payable in goods to peoples described in the passive voice—*it being agreed that the inhabitants shall be considered*—considered, weighed, disposed of, all without once appearing as a subject doing anything. Whole communities rendered grammatically invisible. She recognized the trick intimately, because she performed smaller versions of it daily. A well-drafted clause could make a person disappear without anyone ever being charged with making them disappear. That was the profession. That had always been the profession.

Near midnight she found it: a marginal note in a surveyor's hand from 190-odd years ago, in faded iron-gall ink, tucked against the escarpment's eastern face.

*Villages on the cliff report descent caves and stone records kept by their elders. Referred to the Commission. Commission declined to investigate, citing cost.*

Declined to investigate. The state had been told, in writing, more than a century ago, that there were stone records under the escarpment—and had chosen not to know. Because knowing would have cost money, and not knowing was free, and the entire architecture of the valley's ownership rested on that particular freedom.

Odile sat very still. The clock ticked its dishonest four minutes ahead of the world.

If the Halcyon Expedition had found those records—if Marsh had sealed himself in with them rather than let them surface through channels like hers—then whatever Dravenmoor was carrying out of that valley wouldn't just embarrass her client. It would void every grant she'd just read. Every permit. Every deed downstream of every deed. Including, she noted with a strange detachment, the instruments her own firm's reputation was built on administering.

She should have felt fear. Professionally, she did—a cold inventory of exposure, of the client's reach, of what happened to clerks who became inconvenient. But underneath the inventory was something else, quieter and more alarming.

Relief.

Not hope, exactly. Relief—the specific relief of a woman who has spent nine years keeping books for a fire, waiting for someone to finally strike the match so the waiting would end. She had built her career on the assumption that the paper was the reality, that whoever held the best documents held the truth. But she'd always known, in the clerk's private chapel of her mind, that somewhere under the escarpment there might be better documents. Older ones. True ones.

*He'll betray everyone equally,* she'd thought when she signed him. She realized now she'd meant it as a compliment. A man loyal to nothing could still be loyal to a fact.

* * *

Morning. Six days to expiry. The amber light pulsed on her desk unit: still no contact from Dravenmoor. Fifty-nine hours now.

She dispatched the security team herself, at the river dock, in a gray drizzle that made the port look like a pencil drawing of itself. Six men, ex-naval, incurious, exactly as advertised. Their lead, a broad quiet man named Ostrer, took the briefing folder and asked only one question.

"If we find him compromised—working for someone else, say—what then?"

"You observe and report," Odile said. "You don't judge, and you don't act. Judgment is mine."

Ostrer looked at her a moment longer than the question required. Men like him could smell it when the person giving orders was giving orders against themselves. "Understood," he said, and meant: *we'll see.*

She watched the boat pull out into the brown water and stood in the drizzle longer than was sensible. Behind her the city woke up—trams, gulls, the granary-smell of the old buildings—and somewhere upriver a man with a compass was moving through country that remembered who it belonged to, carrying either her salvation or her indictment.

The clock in her office ran four minutes fast. For nine years that had felt like control.

Walking back, Odile admitted the truth of it: she hadn't been living ahead of time at all. She'd been living ahead of the truth, hoping it would never catch up, hiring men like Dravenmoor to keep running from it on her behalf.

Six days. She went upstairs, opened the archive, and began—quietly, methodically, in a clerk's careful hand—to copy the marginal notes.

If he was going to do something inconvenient and honest, someone ought to have the paperwork ready.
