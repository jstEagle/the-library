## 15. Betrayal at the Tree Line

The hand that sold us belonged to a man named Corrick, and I should have seen it in the way he tied his knots.

Sefa hired him and his brother at the last river town—two quiet men with rope-scarred palms who asked no questions about cargo, which on this river is itself a question. Corrick was the older one, the careful one. He checked our lines twice each night. He volunteered for first watch. And every kindness he did us, I later understood, was a line item on someone else's ledger.

But that morning there was only the mountain.

We'd come down out of Maren's village at first light, Tamsin leading, moving through fog so thick it felt like wading. She'd agreed to take us as far as the tree line and not one step past her own terms: nobody touches the inheritance. Marsh's log had changed the arithmetic for all of us—Ivo alive, sealed in with the truth like a man entombed with a bomb—and now we weren't salvaging bodies anymore. We were deciding what to do with a live one.

Tamsin walked ahead of me with a walking staff cut from birch, and she read the ground the way I read sky. A bent sapling meant water below. A certain moss meant limestone near the surface, which meant caves, which meant everything. Twice she stopped without explanation and made us wait while she listened to nothing, and twice whatever she heard passed.

"You're limping," she said, not turning around.

"I'm favoring," I said. "Different thing."

"Same thing, eventually." She waited until I caught up. Her eyes were still wrong—the feral flatness of someone who'd been alone too long with a secret—but there was something underneath it now, something almost like curiosity. "Marsh said you'd come. Did you know that? Last thing he said to me before he went under. *Someone will come, Tamsin. Make them earn it.*"

"He say who?"

"No. Just that they'd smell of money and move like a man being chased." She looked at my hands, then my face. "He was half right about you."

* * *

The tree line came at midday, abrupt as a decision. Below us the forest thinned into scree and low scrub, and above it the escarpment rose in pale shelves, the cave mouths invisible unless you knew—unless Tamsin told you, which was the whole point of her.

We made camp in the last stand of pines, close enough to the rock that wind funneled down and kept the smoke thin. Sefa posted watches. Corrick took first, his brother second, and I took third because I didn't trust anyone else's third.

I slept badly, which turned out to be the only good decision I made all day.

I woke to a sound I couldn't name—a soft metallic click, somewhere downslope—and lay still in the dark doing the math. Wind doesn't click. Rock doesn't click. I rolled off my pad without unfolding my legs, crawled to the edge of camp, and looked down the trail we'd climbed.

A light blinked three times. Paused. Blinked three times again.

Not random. Not lightning. A signal, patient and practiced, answered a moment later by two blinks from further down, near where the forest gave way to scree.

I lay in the pine needles with my heart going like a storm gauge in a hailstorm and thought: *how long*. How long had Corrick been selling our position—one blink per day, maybe, a little morse of betrayal sent down the mountain while he checked our lines twice and volunteered for first watch. How much did Pell pay for patience?

Then I heard the boats.

Not on the river—we were miles above it now—but the sound carried anyway, or I imagined it did: the low diesel idle of gunboats holding position, waiting for a signal to finish. Pell hadn't followed us up the mountain because he'd never needed to. He'd just needed Corrick's thumb on a switch.

I went back into camp and put my hand over Sefa's mouth before she woke enough to scream.

* * *

She understood fast. That's the thing about Sefa—you could see the river running behind her eyes, always calculating current, always looking for the channel that gets you through. When I pointed downslope at the dark and held up three fingers, she nodded once and started waking Tamsin without a single wasted motion.

Corrick and his brother slept ten feet away. We had maybe four minutes before the next watch change, and less than that if whoever was blinking lights decided to stop signaling and start climbing.

"We run for the caves," Tamsin whispered. It wasn't a question.

"They'll follow," Sefa said.

"Not far." Tamsin's grin in the dark was mostly teeth. "The approach narrows. Two abreast, then one, then crawl. Anyone coming after us comes after us blind, in the dark, on ground I've walked for two years." She paused. "Except I have to go back for the markers. The false trail. Give me ten minutes."

"You don't have ten minutes," I said.

"Then I have five."

And that was when Corrick sat up.

He didn't reach for a weapon. That was the worst part—he just sat there in his bedroll with his hands visible, an honest man's posture, and said quietly, "How long have you known?"

"Since the light," I said.

"Ah." He exhaled, and there was something almost like relief in it. "Then you know I can't let you run yet. They're twenty minutes out. If you scatter before they arrive, I get nothing and my brother—" he glanced at the sleeping shape beside him, and for one unguarded second the whole rotten architecture of it showed through: it was never about the money for him, it was about the boy. "My brother has debts of his own. Different kind. Worse people."

"Pell's people are worse people," Sefa said.

"Pell pays," Corrick said simply. "That's more than most."

Tamsin moved. I'll give her this—two years alone on a mountain had made her fast in a way none of us were. She had her staff across Corrick's throat and him back down in the dirt before Sefa finished standing, and then everything happened at once: the brother surging up, Sefa hitting him with a cooking pot because it was the nearest object and she was never sentimental about tools, me grabbing for the packs because the packs had the copies—

The copies. God, the copies. Three nights in Maren's village, hunched over a candle with Marsh's documents spread on a table, copying the genealogies out by hand onto waterproof paper because I trusted no bag, no boat, no single container to hold the only truth in the valley. Originals in the vault where Marsh wanted them. Copies split between my jacket and Sefa's dry bag, because even between the two of us I hadn't dared concentrate them.

Corrick's brother got an arm around Sefa's neck. Tamsin had her staff jammed under Corrick's jaw and was saying, in a voice gone completely calm, "Tell them to hold position or I break the part of you that whistles."

Downslope, the lights stopped blinking.

They'd seen enough. Or heard enough. Twenty minutes became now.

* * *

What I remember of the next hour comes in pieces, the way storms do—fragments with hard edges and gaps between.

Fragment: torchlight blooming in the trees below us, five points of it, then eight, climbing fast and not caring about noise anymore because noise was free now.

Fragment: Sefa going over the edge of the scree with her dry bag strapped tight, sliding on her heels the way river pilots descend mud banks, shouting for us to follow the gully.

Fragment: Tamsin refusing. Standing at the tree line with her staff planted like a flag, screaming at me that the false trail had to be set or they'd find the real entrance in an hour, and when I grabbed her arm she tore free and said, "You have the papers. Papers are the point. I'm the map. Go."

I went. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly what this whole disaster was about. She told me to go and I went, and I told myself it was strategy—she knew the ground, she'd evade, she'd done it for two years—but the truth is simpler and uglier. The papers were the mission. Tamsin was a person. And I had learned, somewhere in thirty-one years of chasing weather and dodging debt, to always choose the portable thing.

Fragment: running. Scree sliding under my boots, torchlight swinging behind me, a voice amplified through a megaphone saying *Xanthe Dravenmoor, we just want to talk, Mr. Pell is a reasonable man,* in the tone of a man reading a menu.

Fragment: the gully narrowing, walls rising, and then hands—Pell's men had split, some following Sefa's slide path, others cutting along the ridge, and one of them was suddenly in front of me with a baton and a headlamp full in my eyes. I did the only thing storm chasers are actually trained to do, which is change direction violently and immediately. I threw myself left into a crack in the limestone that I found by falling into it, dropped six feet into blackness, landed on wet stone, and lay there listening to boots pass overhead while blood ticked off my eyebrow and onto the floor of the mountain.

* * *

I was underground for eleven hours. I won't pretend those hours were orderly.

There was water somewhere—I followed its sound because water going down means air going somewhere, and air going somewhere means a way out, and that rule has saved me in burning buildings and flooded basements and once, memorably, a collapsed storm shelter outside Corvane. The crack I'd fallen into opened into a natural gallery, and the gallery narrowed, and narrowed again, until I was crawling on my belly through a passage that fit me the way a boot fits a foot, with the genealogies pressed flat against my chest inside my jacket and my breath bouncing back at me off stone older than any claim ever filed.

At the worst of it—shoulders wedged, exhaling to shrink myself, thinking with strange clarity *this is how it ends, crushed by the very land everyone's fighting over*—I thought about Maren's kitchen. The bread she'd put in front of me without asking if I wanted any. *The valley remembers who it belongs to,* she'd said. Lying in the dark with a mountain's weight on my spine, I finally understood she hadn't meant it as poetry. The escarpment had been deciding things about all of us this entire time. It had swallowed the Halcyon Expedition's secrets. It had swallowed Tamsin whole and handed her back as a guardian. And now it had me, and it was taking its time deciding whether I was worth spitting out.

I got through. Of course I got through. The passage opened, the water grew louder, and near dawn—if dawn means anything underground—I emerged through a spring outlet into gray light and freezing water, half-drowned, bleeding, and clutching forty pages of handwritten proof that every concession permit in the valley was built on sand.

* * *

Sefa found me two hours later, or I found her—hard to say who was hunting whom. She was upstream of the spring, soaked to the ribs, dry bag gone, one eye swelling shut, sitting on a rock with the expression of a woman conducting a very slow internal execution.

"The bag?" I said.

"Gone. Went into the current at the bottom of the scree." She didn't look at me. "Your half?"

I pulled the jacket bundle out and held it up, dripping.

She closed her good eye. "One copy," she said. "Of the only thing that matters. In a paper jacket on a man who can't outrun a strong walker." She laughed, and it wasn't a nice sound. "You know what the worst part is? I liked Corrick. Checked my lines twice a night. You hire a man to be careful, and it turns out careful is just another word for thorough."

"Tamsin?"

Her silence answered before her words did. "Didn't make the gully. I saw torches converge where she'd stood." She finally looked at me, and her swollen face made the look terrible. "Pell has her. Pell has the map to the vault. And the vault has Marsh, and Marsh has the originals, and the only reason any of it isn't already in Pell's gunboat is that Tamsin spent two years building false trails and might have bought us a day. One day, Xanthe. Maybe."

I sat down on the rock beside her. The river ran past, indifferent, carrying everything toward Corvane eventually—including, in a waterproof bag, nothing at all.

Below us, far downstream, I could hear the gunboats idling. Waiting. Pell was a patient man; he'd proven that with Corrick, with the blinking lights, with twenty years of smiling through other people's ruin. He had the map now. He had the girl. All he lacked was the papers, and he knew exactly who had them, and he knew—because Corrick would have told him, because Corrick was thorough—that I had nowhere left to sell them to.

"Sefa," I said. "How fast can you get us downriver?"

"Faster than he expects," she said. "Slower than I'd like." She stood, wincing, and looked at me with her one working eye. "So what's the play? Because I've watched you stall, and dodge, and split the difference for two weeks, and I'll tell you what I told the river the night I decided to work for you instead of against you: eventually the current picks a direction whether you do or not."

I looked at the papers against my chest. Then downstream, toward the guns. Then back up the mountain, toward the village that had fed me without conditions, where an old woman had said *the valley remembers*, and where I had—for the first time in my adult life—slept without calculating the cost of stopping.

Maren had told me once that the council would need to decide. That the documents weren't mine to spend.

But the council didn't know Tamsin was in Pell's hands. The council didn't know the clock had gone from nineteen days to hours. And I was done letting other people's timelines choose for me.

"There's a radio mast on the concession house roof," I said. "Odile's filing system runs through it. Every registry, every newsroom, every rival claimant in the region pulls from that relay." I stood up, and my knee screamed, and I ignored it. "Pell thinks I'm running with the papers. Let him chase a runner. Meanwhile I'm going to make the papers worthless to steal."

Sefa stared at me. "You're going to broadcast them."

"I'm going to detonate them." I started downhill, toward the river, toward Corvane, toward the storm front I could already feel building on the western horizon like a debt coming due. "Get me a boat, Sefa. And pray the weather holds ugly."
