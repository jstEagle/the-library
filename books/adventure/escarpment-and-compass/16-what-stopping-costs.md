## 16. What Stopping Costs

The fever broke on the third night, and I woke to the smell of bread.

Not baking—just bread, plain and close, the way a room smells when someone has left a loaf near your bed because they decided you'd be hungry before you knew it yourself. I lay still under a wool blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and lanolin and took inventory of my body like a man counting what survived a flood.

Ribs: two cracked, maybe three, on the left side where Pell's scout had put a boot into me at the tree line. Left forearm: stitched, seven of them, neat as survey work. Right thigh: a graze that had bled enough to soak through Sefa's jacket but not enough to matter. Head: a knot behind my ear from the fall down the scree, and a ringing that came and went like weather.

Everything else accounted for. Everything else gone.

I'd lost the vault's location to Pell, Tamsin to his men, and most of my blood to the escarpment. What I'd kept were the copies sewn into the lining of my coat—the genealogies, the treaty fragments, the pages that could unmake every land claim between here and Corvane—and I'd kept them by running, which was the one skill I had never once had to practice.

*You're good at leaving,* I told myself, staring at the smoke-darkened rafters. *It's the only thing you've ever been good at.*

The bread was still warm.

* * *

Maren found me trying to stand an hour later, which is to say she found me on the floor with my hand braced against the bedframe and my leg refusing to file its paperwork.

"You'll tear those stitches," she said from the doorway, not hurrying.

"I need to see the valley road."

"You need to lie down."

"Pell has Tamsin. He doesn't need her to talk—he only needs her to walk him to the caves, and she will, because he'll threaten the village instead of her and she knows it." I got one knee under myself. The room tilted politely and waited for me to notice. "Every hour I'm flat on my back, he's closer to the vault."

Maren crossed the room, took a fistful of blanket, and pulled it back over me with the unhurried authority of a woman who had been moving heavier things than me since before I was born. "Sit."

"Maren—"

"Sit," she said again, and then, softer, in the tone that was somehow worse: "Xanthe. You cannot outrun a man who already knows where you are."

I sat.

She lowered herself onto the stool by the window, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked at me for a long moment with those pale river-stone eyes. Outside, the escarpment caught the late light and held it, cliff face going amber, the village below us arranging itself into evening—goats, lamplight, someone singing badly two houses over.

"You've asked about the road three times," she said. "You haven't once asked how the stitches got put in, or who carried you down from the tree line, or why there's bread by your bed."

"Because I can guess."

"Guess anyway."

"Sefa carried me. Your granddaughter stitched me—she has a surgeon's hands and no bedside manner. And the bread is because feeding strangers is what this place does, even strangers who brought gunboats to your doorstep."

Maren smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile. It was the smile of someone watching a clever animal fail a very simple test. "Half right. Sefa carried you, yes—she cried while she did it, if you want the truth, though she'll deny it. Anja stitched you, and she learned those hands on sheep, so mind the compliment. But the bread—" She leaned forward. "The bread is because Ida saw you limping past her window yesterday and decided you'd be hungry. Nobody assigned her. Nobody paid her. She just decided."

"That's what I said."

"No. You said the village feeds people. Like it's weather. Like it happens *to* you." She stood, slow, and gathered the empty bowl. "This village doesn't do anything to you, Xanthe Dravenmoor. That's rather the point of it."

* * *

They fed me anyway.

That was the part I couldn't get around, the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone all through the next day and the day after. I tried to pay. I had coin—syndicate scrip, concession marks, the currency of everything I'd spent ten years chasing—and I pressed it on Anja and she looked at it the way you'd look at a dead bird someone handed you. I offered it to Ida and she laughed until she had to sit down. I left it under my plate at the common hall and Maren slid it back across the table without breaking her conversation about fence posts.

"It's not charity," she told me finally, on the fourth evening, walking me back up to the sickroom at the speed of continental drift. "Charity is what you give when you've decided someone is beneath you. This is supper. You're hungry. We have supper. The arithmetic ends there."

"And if I never repay it?"

"Then you never repay it." She shrugged. "Do you know what we call a debt like that? Nothing. We don't call it anything. It isn't a thing here."

But it was a thing everywhere else. It was the whole architecture of my life. I lay awake that night doing the math I always did, the ledger I kept behind my eyes the way other men kept prayers: what I owed the Vellum Syndicate, what the interest had grown to while I ran data for storm brokers, what Odile Ferrant's contract promised, what Pell would pay, what anyone would pay, what everything cost. Numbers had been the walls of my world for so long I'd forgotten they were walls. Inside them I could move fast and owe much and touch nothing, because touching things meant they could be taken, and being known meant—

There it was. I made myself look at it, lying in the dark with my ribs aching in time with my pulse.

Being known meant being accountable.

That was why I chased storms. Not the money—though the money was real, and the debt was realer—but because a storm is the one companion that never learns your name. You read it, you run beside it, you sell what you see, and when it's over it rolls away over the horizon and takes everything you showed it. No one gets hurt by knowing you except you. A man who never stops moving never has to be anywhere when the bill comes due. I'd told myself for years that I loved the flats, the supercells, the green-black light under a rotating wall cloud, and some of that was even true. But underneath the love was the arithmetic: *keep moving, stay unknown, stay unowned.* The syndicate had bought my debts; nobody had ever managed to buy me, because I'd made sure there was nothing standing still long enough to buy.

And now I was lying in a village that had fed me four suppers I couldn't price, in a bed I hadn't earned, under a roof whose owner knew my full name, my debts, my crimes of omission, and my current helplessness—and the roof hadn't fallen in. Nobody had collected. Nobody had even *invoiced.*

It should have felt like safety. Mostly it felt like standing on a ledge in high wind.

Because if I stayed—if I let them keep knowing me—then everything I did next would land on them too. Pell wouldn't burn my ledger out of spite. He'd burn their granary. The syndicate didn't punish men like me anymore; I was too small. They punished addresses.

* * *

On the fifth morning I could walk, barely, and I walked the cliff path alone to prove something to myself. The escarpment rose above the village like a held breath, limestone streaked with the orange of old iron, and the wind coming off the flats below carried the smell of rain that hadn't arrived yet—a smell I knew better than my mother's kitchen. Somewhere up there, past the tree line where I'd bled into the scree, past the caves Tamsin had guarded like a dragon with a geology degree, the vault sat in the dark with its treaties and its genealogies and its terrible, patient proof.

Ivo Marsh had sealed himself in with it rather than let it be sold. Tamsin had gone half-feral guarding the approach. And I—I'd run down a mountain with copies in my coat lining, and my first thought, my honest first thought, had been *what's the payout.*

I stood at the path's edge looking down at the village and understood, finally, the shape of the choice. Not deliver versus destroy—that was the choice everyone kept handing me, Odile and Marsh and Pell, each convinced the documents were a thing to be moved. The real choice was smaller and worse. It was whether I was a man who passed through places or a man who belonged to one. Because the documents weren't treasure. They were a *home's deed.* They belonged to the people below me, the ones who'd fed me without arithmetic, and every plan I'd made for them—sell them, leak them, trade them, broadcast them—had been a plan in which the villagers were cargo and I was the courier taking a cut.

Invisibility had always been someone else's design for me. The syndicate liked me invisible; invisible debtors are easy to herd. The concessionaires liked me invisible; invisible contractors leave no witnesses. Even the storms liked me invisible. I had called it freedom my whole life, and it had never once been freedom. It had been erasure, rented out.

A shadow fell beside mine on the path. Sefa. She stood with her hands jammed in her jacket pockets—the same jacket that had soaked up my blood—and looked down at the village with me.

"They voted last night," she said.

"Voted what?"

"Whether to hide the documents, run with them, or stand." She glanced at me. "Maren wouldn't tell me the result. Said it wasn't mine to carry yet." A pause. "Said it wasn't yours either. But she wants you in the hall at noon, and she said—" Sefa's mouth twitched, the ghost of her card-table smile, the one she used when she was about to take my money or tell me the truth, whichever cost more. "She said to tell you they'll be serving supper after. Whether or not you decide to stay for it."

Below us, the village went about its morning. Smoke from cookfires. Goats complaining. Someone repairing the well roof, hammer strokes carrying thin and clear across the valley air. People who had dug a grave for a stranger's death and filled it with bread instead.

My ribs hurt. My leg hurt. Under all of it, in the place where the ledger used to live, something had gone quiet—not empty, quiet, like the center of a storm cell where the pressure drops and the air goes strange and still right before everything changes.

Noon was two hours off. For the first time in ten years, I wasn't counting them.
