## 17. The Village Decides

The rain came off the escarpment at dusk the way it always did, sideways and full of grit, and Maren stood in the doorway of the long hall watching it hammer the slate roofs of Kestrel Hollow until the whole village blurred into one gray shape. Forty-one households. Three wells. One road down to the river, and that road washed to nothing six months of the year. For a hundred years that had been enough to keep the world away.

It would not be enough now.

Behind her, the hall smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the lentil stew old Piotr had made too salty again, the way he did whenever he was frightened, which was often, and which was why he'd been elected to cook for councils instead of sitting on them. Every bench was full. She counted heads out of habit — the same habit she used counting storm clouds, counting debts, counting years. Forty-three adults. The Osei twins were seventeen and technically not allowed to vote, which had never once stopped them from talking.

At the end of the hall, near the fire, Xanthe Dravenmoor sat where they had put him, which was nowhere in particular, which was itself a kind of statement. His shoulder was bandaged. Sefa sat beside him with her boots off and her pistol, uncharacteristically, on the table between her hands, where everyone could see it and no one had to wonder. The documents lay in an oilcloth bundle on the trestle table in front of Maren's own chair. She had not touched them since the girl brought them in from the storm. She had not needed to. She had read enough in the lamplight of the past two days to know what they were, and what she'd read had rearranged something in her chest that had been sitting crooked for sixty years.

"Read it again," said Piotr, from the kitchen doorway, because he always asked for it twice.

Maren did not pick up the bundle. She knew the words.

"A grant of common title," she said. "Dated one hundred and sixty years before the first colonial survey. Sealed with the mark of the escarpment villages — all of them, not just ours. Kestrel Hollow. Greywater. The stone-cutters' settlements above the falls. Signed by a magistrate of the old river authority, witnessed by eleven names, and filed, registered, and confirmed by every subsequent administration that bothered to look, because none of them bothered to read." She let that sit. "The concession permits that Odile Ferrant's firm issued — the ones that expire in six days — were built on the assumption that this land was unclaimed. It was never unclaimed. It was *unnoticed*. There is a difference, and the difference is paper."

"And the paper is real?" That was Dagny Osei, arms crossed, one of the twins, the louder one. "Not forged? Not something the storm-chaser wrote himself?"

"The genealogies are real," Maren said. "I know three of the family names on them. My grandmother's grandmother is in the third register. The hand changes every few generations because different people kept it, the way we keep the rainfall tallies. Nobody forges a document across four hundred years. Nobody *thinks* that way."

"Someone could have," Dagny said. "Someone with money."

"Someone with money would have forged a document that gave *them* the land," Sefa said from the fire. "Not a document that gives it to a village of forty-three people and a bad well."

A ripple of laughter, quickly swallowed. Maren let it pass and then put it down, the way you put down a dog that's gotten excited at the wrong moment.

"Xanthe," she said. "They want to hear it from you. What happens if we do nothing."

He stood up too fast for a wounded man and she watched him catch the edge of the bench and decide, correctly, that no one would respect him if he sat back down. That was something she'd learned about him in the four days he'd been bleeding in her guest room: he would rather fall over than be carried. It was the most legible thing about him. It was also, she suspected, the thing that had nearly gotten him killed twice before he was thirty.

"If you do nothing," he said, "the permits lapse. The concession reverts to the state. The state reissues it — probably to the same firm, possibly to the syndicate that's been chasing me upriver, and either way, within two years there are survey crews on the escarpment, and within five there's a road, and within ten there's nothing left of this village except the name on a mining company's map. The documents in that bundle would be buried under it, or bought, or burned. Whoever holds them then holds them for themselves."

"And if we hide them?" Piotr again. "Bury the bundle. Go on as we are. Wait it out."

"You waited out the last survey," Xanthe said. "Forty years ago. How much of the north terrace did you lose?"

Maren saw the old man's jaw work. Forty years ago she had been a young woman with strong opinions and a dead husband, and the survey had come, and the council had voted to hide the graves and the markers and the boundary stones, and the surveyors had taken the north terrace because there was nothing on the *paper* to say anyone had ever stood there. She had helped carry the boundary stones into the caves herself. She had told herself it was prudence. She had told herself that for forty years, the way you keep telling yourself a thing until the telling wears a groove and the groove feels like a fact.

"We lost the terrace," Piotr said quietly.

"You lost it because you hid," Xanthe said. "I've spent my whole life being invisible. It's a good strategy right up until someone decides to build on top of you." He stopped, and Maren watched him hear his own words, and watched something move behind his face — the thing he'd told her about, at three in the morning, feverish, about the syndicate and the debt and the ledger with his name in it, about how a man can be so thoroughly erased by other people's paperwork that he stops existing to himself. "I'm not telling you what to do. I'm telling you what I know about hiding."

The fire cracked. Rain worked at the roof. Maren let the silence go long enough to hurt.

"Then we come to the vote," she said. "Two motions. First: do we hide the documents, as we hid the stones. Second: do we stand, openly, and use them. Speak in order. Dagny."

"Stand," Dagny said. "Obviously. Bury them and we're burying ourselves with them. My kids aren't going to grow up in a company town."

"Tam."

"Stand." Tam Osei — the quieter twin, which meant only that her sentences were longer. "But not just stand. *File*. If the title is real, it's real in the registries, in the courts, in the newsrooms. A document in a cave is a rumor. A document in the public record is a wall. We don't just show it — we make it impossible to unsee."

"Yusuf."

The miller was slow about it. He was always slow about it; it was why his flour was good. "I want to hide," he said, and there was a murmur of honesty-acknowledgment around the benches, because half of them wanted to hide and it cost something to say it out loud. "I want it the way you want a thing you know is cowardice. But I sat in this hall forty years ago too, and I watched my father vote to bury the stones, and I watched him spend the rest of his life not looking at the north terrace. Hiding didn't keep us safe. It just moved the loss to a place where we couldn't see it happen." He looked at Maren. "Stand. And I'll say this too: whoever carries those papers down to Corvane is going into the teeth of it. The syndicate, the concession men, the mercenaries. We should vote on that separately, and we should vote it *knowing*."

"We will," Maren said. "Sefa?"

"Stand," the pilot said. "I've run cargo for the concessionaires. I've seen what 'unclaimed' means when it's written on their maps. It means people who were never asked." She tapped the pistol once, lightly, and left it there. "I'll say the hard part, since Yusuf started it. If you stand, they come here. Not maybe. They come here. Pell's gunboats are on the river, and Pell doesn't lose gracefully. You're not voting on a document. You're voting on a siege."

"Then let's vote on a siege," Maren said, "with our eyes open. Piotr?"

The old cook had come out of the kitchen and was wiping his hands on his apron, which he did when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed. "I made stew for this council for thirty years," he said. "And for thirty years I've watched us vote small. Vote to wait. Vote to hide. Vote to hope nobody notices us. And it's worked, I'll grant you, the way a mouse's strategy works — until the day the barn gets torn down. These papers say the barn was never theirs." He held up a spoon like a gavel. "Stand. And someone feed the storm-chaser, he's grey as porridge."

Laughter again, real this time, and Maren let it live for a moment before she called the rest of the roll. It went the way she had known it would go since the second day, since she'd read the third register and found her own family's name written in a dead woman's careful hand: *Maren, daughter of Ilsa, of the Hollow, keeper of the rain-tallies.* Eleven generations of keepers. Nobody had ever asked her family's permission to build on their ground, and nobody had ever needed to, because nobody with power had ever believed the ground belonged to anyone worth asking.

The vote was thirty-nine to four. The four who said hide were not scorned; Maren saw to that; two of them were given the job of moving the village's stores and children to the cave approaches if it came to siege, which was hiding's honest work, and they took it with their dignity intact.

Then she called the second vote, and the hall went quiet in a different way.

* * *

"You're asking me," Xanthe said, "to walk into Corvane."

"I'm asking you to carry," Maren said.

They stood in the hall's antechamber while the benches emptied and the council broke into the small loud arguments of people who have just decided to be brave and need to argue about logistics to calm down. Rain still hammered the door. Maren had closed it against the weather and against eavesdropping, though in a village this size there was no real difference between a secret and a rumor with a head start.

"I'm a debtors' mark," Xanthe said. "The syndicate has collectors on the river. The concession firm will have security in Corvane by now — Odile Ferrant will have sent her own people the moment I missed a check-in, if she's half as careful as she looked. Every road into that city has someone on it looking for a man with my face and my debts. And you want to hand me the one bundle of paper in the valley that everyone upriver would burn a village for."

"Yes," Maren said.

He stared at her. She had watched him do a great many calculations in the past four days — storm cells, river currents, the load-bearing of a bad shoulder — and she recognized the look of a man running numbers that kept coming out wrong because he was using the wrong arithmetic.

"Why me? Sefa knows the river. Tam Osei can shoot. Half your council—"

"Half my council is needed *here*," Maren said. "If the village stands, the village has to stand *visible*. Elders on the road, documents copied and witnessed, the neighboring settlements sent word — if we go quiet, we're just another village that got bought. And Sefa stays with the boat, because you'll need a way out of Corvane and she's the only one who can promise you one." She stepped closer. "I'm not asking you because you're expendable. Look at me. I'm asking you because of what you said in there. *I've spent my whole life being invisible.*"

"That's not a qualification."

"It's the only one that matters." She held up her hand before he could argue. "A mercenary carries papers for pay. A hero carries them for glory. Both of them, sooner or later, decide the papers are worth more than the people they're carrying them for — I've watched it happen, twice, in my lifetime, and we buried what was left. I need someone who carries them because he was *asked*. Because someone fed him, and bandaged him, and put a roof over him, and then said: this is ours, will you take it down the mountain for us. That's not a job. That's a trust. And trusts are carried by people who belong to something."

"I don't belong here," Xanthe said. "I've been here four days."

"Eleven generations of my family are in that register," Maren said, "and not one of them was asked whether they belonged here either. Belonging isn't a thing you qualify for, boy. It's a thing that's claimed *on* you. The valley claimed you the moment you bled on its ground. The only question left is whether you claim it back." She picked up the oilcloth bundle from the trestle and held it out. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, or perhaps it wasn't, perhaps that was only the way the room felt. "Tam's copies go to the registries and the newsrooms — she and Dagny are riding for Greywater at first light to have them witnessed and sealed, so that what you carry is the original and what the world gets is everywhere at once. You carry these into Corvane, and you don't sell them, and you don't trade them, and you don't burn them — Marsh wanted them burned and the council voted, and the council outranks a dead man's despair. You put them in front of every clerk, every magistrate, every reporter who will stand still long enough. You make us *unignorable*."

"And if Pell's people take me on the road?"

"Then you eat the documents," Sefa said, from the doorway, where she had been standing long enough to have heard everything, "and I burn the boat, and we start over, and I will be *extremely* unpleasant about it."

Maren didn't smile, but something near it moved in her face. Xanthe looked at the bundle. He looked at it for a long time, and Maren — who had spent sixty years reading weather and longer reading people — watched him arrive at the place he'd been circling since the night he'd told her about the ledger: the understanding that running had never been a choice, only a habit, and that habits could be broken the way a fever breaks, suddenly and with a great deal of sweating.

"The debt ledger," he said. "The syndicate's copy of what I owe. It'll be in Corvane. In their counting house, or with Pell."

"What about it?"

"When I'm done," he said, "I want it burned. Not forgiven. *Burned.* Along with everyone else's."

"That's not in my gift," Maren said. "But if you put eleven generations of my family's name into the public record, I suspect you'll find the whole ledger burns easier than anyone expects. Paper that lives in the dark hates daylight. All of it. Yours included."

He took the bundle.

She watched his hands close on the oilcloth — steady, she noted, steadier than his face — and she watched the moment land in him the way a keystone lands, everything above it settling into a shape that would hold. He was still thin and grey and moving like a man with a bad shoulder, because he was. But the restlessness was gone out of his eyes. In its place was something aimed.

"First light?" he said.

"First light," Maren said. "There's a front coming down off the high escarpment — three days out, maybe four, a bad one, the kind that shuts the river roads and blacks out the city grids. Sefa says your kind of bad." She let herself smile now, small and crooked. "I told the council we'd be mad to send you into Corvane in weather like that. Then I remembered that you're the only man in this valley who *likes* weather like that."

Outside, the rain was easing into that long steady drumming that meant the front behind it was still building. Xanthe stood in the antechamber holding a village's whole legal existence in his hands, and for the first time since he'd staggered up her road four days ago, Maren thought he looked like a man who knew exactly where he was going — not running toward, not fleeing from, but *standing for*.

"One more thing," she said, as he turned to the door. "When you see Odile Ferrant — and you will, she'll find you before you find her — remember that she wrote the expiry date herself. She knows the machine better than anyone. Machines like that have a man inside them somewhere, waiting for a reason."

Xanthe paused with his hand on the latch.

"You want me to recruit the clerk."

"I want you to *offer*," Maren said. "What she does with the offer is her own inheritance to claim." She nodded at the door, at the rain, at the dark road down out of the hills. "Now go. The storm won't wait, and neither will six days."
