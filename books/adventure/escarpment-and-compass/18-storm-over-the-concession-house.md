## 18. Storm Over the Concession House

The front came off the high plains at four in the morning, and it was beautiful.

Xanthe stood on the deck of Sefa's boat with his anemometer humming in one hand and the genealogies sealed in oilcloth against his chest, and he let himself feel it—the old clean joy of a storm arriving exactly when you needed it to. The sky over Corvane's harbor was the color of a bruise going green. Pressure had fallen thirty millibars since midnight and was still falling, the barograph needle carving a cliff face of its own. Wind out of the northwest at forty knots and gusting sixty. Hail core embedded in the leading edge, radar said, though there was no radar anymore except the handheld unit he'd rebuilt from salvage and trusted more than any official feed.

"You're smiling," Sefa said. She had her hands on the wheel and her eyes on the channel markers, which were already disappearing into rain-haze.

"I am."

"That worries me."

"It should worry Pell more." He checked the wind again, then the current tables he'd memorized two nights ago by lamplight in Maren's hall. "Front makes landfall at the harbor around oh-six-forty. Peak gusts between six fifty and seven fifteen. That's our window."

"Our window for what, exactly? Because I've asked three times and gotten weather."

Xanthe looked at her. Rain began to spot the deck, fat and warm, the first outriders of the real thing. "Pell keeps two gunboats on the river approach and a third on the harbor patrol rotation. Odile told us that before she went dark. We can't fight them and we can't outrun them. So we don't try."

Sefa waited. She was good at waiting; it was half her profession.

"We arrive inside the storm," Xanthe said. "Visibility at the height of the squall line will be under two hundred meters. Radar returns will be garbage—clutter, sea echo, hail spikes. Every gunboat captain on that water is going to be fighting his own helm and praying to whatever he prays to. Nobody hunts a smuggler in that. Nobody *can*."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I used to be the thing people couldn't find." He tapped the anemometer. "Six years chasing supercells across the flats. You learn what weather hides and what it reveals. A storm is a wall if you stand outside it. It's a door if you time your entry right."

Sefa snorted, but he saw her shoulders ease a fraction. "And my part?"

"Interference. You take the *Kestrel's Daughter* up the main channel, lights burning, transponder squawking Halcyon's old registry code. Make yourself loud. Pell's people are looking for a quiet boat running dark. Give them a loud one instead, and every eye goes to you while I come in along the breakwater where the harbor master's sensors drown in ground clutter."

"Loud," Sefa repeated. "You want me to sail straight at gunboats with the paperwork that ends the world strapped to someone else's chest."

"Not the originals. Copies. Maren's council insisted—we made three sets. Tamsin has one hidden in the caves. The village has one in the root cellar under the hall. I have the third." He patted the oilcloth bundle. "If I go down, it doesn't matter. If we all go down, it still doesn't matter. That's the point of copies."

"That's the point of *you*, maybe." She adjusted course, and the boat leaned into the building chop. "I get one set of orders: be loud, survive, meet you at the coal dock at eight hundred hours or not at all."

"Or not at all," Xanthe agreed.

"Romantic."

"Storms are romantic. This is just logistics."

* * *

They ran the last twenty miles hard, hull slamming through short steep seas kicked up by the wind against the outgoing tide. Xanthe worked the radar and the barometer and the sky itself, reading the squall line the way he'd once read it across open grassland—watching the shelf cloud build its layered shelf, watching the striations tighten, feeling the temperature drop nine degrees in as many minutes as the cold pool surged out ahead of the core.

At oh-six-thirty the world went green-black.

Hail came first, small and stinging, then larger, rattling off the deck like thrown gravel. Then the rain arrived—not falling so much as arriving sideways, a solid gray wall that erased the harbor, the city, the horizon, everything. Lightning walked across the clouds in long horizontal strokes, and each flash lit Corvane for a quarter second: the concession house on its hill above the waterfront, windows dark, flag whipping itself to threads; the registry towers; the cranes of the mineral docks standing like gallows in the murk.

"Now," Xanthe shouted, and Sefa broke away toward the main channel, running lights blazing, foghorn sounding every fifteen seconds like a ship in genuine distress—which, given what she was sailing toward, she arguably was.

He went the other way, over the side into the skiff, alone.

This was the part he'd done a hundred times in another life: the drop into chaos, instruments dead or lying, nothing left but judgment and nerve. He'd chased storms because they were honest. A tornado didn't smile at you. It didn't offer to forgive your debts. It just was what it was, enormous and indifferent, and there had been years when that indifference felt like mercy.

Now he was using the same skill set to carry a lie about land ownership into the house that sold the land.

The skiff bucked through the harbor mouth. Two hundred meters of visibility, he'd promised, and the storm delivered less—a wet gray blindness broken only by lightning. He navigated by counting: seconds of run between flashes, the bearing of the breakwater lights he'd memorized, the feel of the swell changing character as the harbor's eastern arm began to shelter him. Somewhere out in the channel he heard—or imagined he heard—gunboat engines laboring against the sea, and once, faintly, a loudhailer's distorted bark swallowed whole by thunder.

Hold on, Sefa. Be loud. Be alive.

The coal dock materialized out of the rain all at once, black timber looming, and he brought the skiff alongside with a grace that surprised him, hands remembering things his fear had tried to forget. He tied off, shouldered the oilcloth bundle inside his coat, and climbed the ladder into the teeth of the wind.

Corvane in a severe weather event was a city holding its breath. Shutters down. Streets empty except for water running downhill in ropes. The storm sirens had been sounding for an hour and would sound until the front passed; everyone with sense was underground or indoors. Which meant the concession house would be running on a skeleton crew—and, more importantly, on emergency power.

That was the flaw in their fortress. Odile had drawn it for him, in the last message she'd risk sending: *When the grid fails, we fail over to generators. Generators mean the electronic locks default open. Fire code. It's been flagged for six years and ignored for six years, because nobody who matters ever enters the building during a blackout.*

Nobody who mattered. Xanthe smiled again, grimly, and started uphill.

* * *

The concession house rose out of the rain like something drowned and refusing to admit it. Black stone, tall windows, the Vellum Syndicate's seal above the doors—a stylized vellum scroll, unrolled and blank. He'd always thought that was accidental honesty. A document with nothing written on it yet, waiting for whoever held the pen.

The generators were already thumping somewhere below, a deep irregular heartbeat stuttering with every surge of the storm. He circled to the service entrance on the north side, sheltered from the worst of the wind, and found the electronic lock exactly as promised: dark screen, dead LED, the bolt retracted. The mechanical deadlock behind it, he knew from Odile's sketches, required a physical key—but the service door's secondary latch was a simple interior thumb-turn, and the fire code's grandfather clause meant the outer door itself wasn't steel.

It took him eleven minutes and both hands bleeding to work the frame loose. He kept count of the minutes because keeping count was how he stayed calm, the way he'd once counted seconds between lightning and thunder to guess a cell's distance. Eleven minutes. The storm had been ashore forty. Peak gusts for another twenty-five after that. His window was holding, but it was closing.

Inside: darkness, generator hum, the smell of paper and floor wax and old money. Emergency lighting glowed weak red along the baseboards, enough to navigate by, not enough to be seen by. He moved through corridors lined with framed maps—every land claim in the region, beautifully rendered, beautifully false—and found the records wing by counting doorways the way Odile had taught him in a message disguised as a complaint about filing conventions.

Her office was on the third floor. The transmission room—the syndicate's private wire hub, the machine that pushed permit data, claim filings, and quiet instructions to every registry office in the territory—was one floor above it, and its access ran through her credentials.

Odile Ferrant had handed him the keys to the vault and then gone home to wait and see what kind of man he was. He still wasn't sure himself. But he was about to find out, and so was everyone else.

The stairwell was pitch dark. He climbed by touch, by the red glow bleeding under doors, by the storm shaking the building like a dog shaking a rat. On the third floor he stopped.

Light. Under Odile's door. Not emergency red—lamplight yellow, steady.

Someone was in her office.

Xanthe pressed himself against the wall beside the doorframe, heart hammering, and listened. No voices. Just the faint scratch of a pen, unhurried, patient. And beneath it, almost lost under the thunder, a sound he recognized from a hundred bad nights: the soft creak of a chair accepting weight, shifting, settling. Someone sitting comfortably. Someone waiting.

"Pell keeps his boats on the water," the voice said finally, conversational, pitched to carry through the door. "Which means whoever comes tonight comes through *my* office. Sit-down conversation, Mr. Dravenmoor. Or stand-up. Your preference."

Odile Ferrant's voice. Calm as a barometer reading.

Xanthe stood in the dark with the storm screaming against the glass and the documents against his chest and the transmission room one floor up, and understood that the last door in his plan had just opened from the other side.

He put his hand on the handle.
