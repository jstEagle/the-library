## 19. Climax: Broadcast

The storm arrived on schedule because I had made it my schedule.

That was the trick nobody in Corvane understood about weather—you don't chase it, you negotiate with it. You learn its terms. The front rolling off the western flats had been building for three days, a wall of bruised cloud with a dry slot carved through its middle like a keyhole, and I had spent those three days learning the lock. Pressure falls of four millibars an hour. Wind shear stacked like planks. A squall line that would hit the city at 21:40 and take the grid down with it, because Corvane's power infrastructure was exactly as honest as its land registry—old, patched, and lying about how much it could carry.

21:40. That was my window.

Sefa had the river. Her last transmission from the boat had been one word—*green*—which meant Pell's gunboats were watching the wrong bridges, chasing the decoy barge she'd loaded with crates and a radio that squawked convincingly. Tamsin was somewhere above me on the rooftops with a spotting scope and instructions to say nothing unless everything failed, because Tamsin's version of a contingency plan involved teeth.

And Maren was home, in the village that had decided not to hide. I carried her decision folded inside my jacket, printed on paper older than the country pretending it didn't exist: genealogies, deeds, treaties, the whole lawful spine of the escarpment people, copied in Ivo Marsh's careful hand and mine.

The concession house stood at the head of Registry Square like a tombstone someone had upholstered. Pale stone, brass fittings, a dome that caught lightning beautifully. Inside it lived every claim, every permit, every fiction that turned a valley into collateral. Odile Ferrant worked on the fourth floor, behind a filing system she'd once described to me over bad coffee as "a maze I built so that even I get lost in it." She'd said it proudly. She'd also said, three days ago, in a voice stripped of pride: *Check-in window closes at nine. After that I have to report you, Xanthe. I don't want to report you.*

I hadn't checked in. So she would be reporting me right about now, which meant her security detail would be moving toward the square, which meant the building's internal protocols would be waking up—which meant, if I'd read her correctly, and I had bet my life that I had, the fourth floor would be the one place in Corvane where a man could stand still in a blackout and be briefly, bureaucratically invisible.

I went in through the loading dock at 21:33, wearing the gray coveralls of a grid-maintenance contractor, carrying a toolbox full of storm data and a dead man's patience.

* * *

The building knew something was coming before I did. You could feel it in the way the lights flickered—not failing yet, but flinching. Backup generators hummed somewhere below like animals scenting rain. Two clerks passed me in the corridor without a glance; a man in coveralls with a clipboard is the most invisible creature in any city, more invisible than Pell's collectors, more invisible than I had ever managed to be on my own. Invisibility, I was learning, was never a quality you possessed. It was a design other people imposed on you—and tonight I had stolen the blueprint and redrawn it myself.

The stairwell to four was locked. Of course it was. But Odile had taught me something without meaning to: every lock in this building answered to a permit number, and permit numbers were just weather data with better manners—a set of conditions, a sequence, a pattern you could read if you stopped being afraid of it. I'd spent nineteen days memorizing the concession file numbers she'd let slip, the ones tied to the Halcyon recovery contract. I typed mine into the keypad like a prayer with a deadline.

The lock thought about it. Then it believed me.

Fourth floor. Rows of filing cabinets under green emergency lamps, the registry's heart, all that paper pretending to be permanence. And there, at the far end, lit by a single desk lamp against protocol: Odile Ferrant, standing very still beside the transmission terminal, her access card already plugged in.

"You're early," she said. "Or the storm is late."

"Storms are never late. They're just negotiating."

"Xanthe." She said my name the way you set down something heavy. "My report went out at nine-oh-one. Security is en route. You have maybe six minutes before this floor stops being yours." She didn't move away from the terminal. She also didn't unplug her card. "Tell me what you're going to do, and tell me fast, and don't lie to me. I've spent my whole career reading lies filed in triplicate."

So I told her. The vault. Marsh alive. The genealogies that predated every colonial claim, every concession, every permit her firm had manufactured and backdated and timed to expire like a bomb made of paperwork. The villages' lawful title. The machine she worked for, laid out flat.

She listened without blinking, which from Odile was the equivalent of weeping.

"I suspected," she said finally. "Since the burned cache. Since you lied about having no stake in the valley—I audit liars for a living, Xanthe, it's the only honest part of my job." Her hand rested on the terminal. "If you transmit from here, using my credentials, it isn't salvage anymore. It's testimony. It's me."

"That's why I came to your floor instead of the roof antenna."

"I know." Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died young. "That's the worst part. You used my own filing system against me. Do you know how long I've waited for someone to do that?" She pulled a chair to the terminal. "Sit. Six minutes. The distribution list first—every registry office, every newsroom, every rival claimant who's been salivating over these permits. If it goes to one, they'll bury it. If it goes to everyone, burying it becomes the story."

Lightning whitened the windows. Somewhere below, thunder rolled through the foundations like a verdict being read aloud.

* * *

I sat down at the terminal of the institution that owned my debt and started typing.

The genealogy scans were on the drive against my ribs—Tamsin had catalogued them in the caves by lamplight, muttering names like a litany, and I had learned them the way I learned pressure gradients: not by understanding at first, but by repetition until the pattern became instinct. Dravenmoor lineages weren't in these documents. Nobody named Dravenmoor had ever belonged anywhere enough to be written down. But the escarpment families were—their marriages, their boundaries, their treaties with each other and with governments that had since pretended otherwise. Maren's family name appeared eleven times. Eleven generations of being told their ground was empty.

I built the transmission the way Sefa read water: find the current, stop fighting it. Odile's credentials opened the distribution architecture—a system designed, ironically, to blast permit expiries and claim notices to hundreds of offices simultaneously. The machine that enforced the fiction would now deliver the truth. There was a poetry in that I didn't have time to enjoy.

"Registry annexes, all districts," Odile said, reading over my shoulder, pointing. "Include the provincial archive—they'll deny receiving it, but denial requires acknowledgment. Newsrooms—Corvane Gazette, the coastal wires, the university press. Rival claimants—" she hesitated, then tapped the screen, "—all of them. Especially Halloway Minerals. They'll weaponize it against us within the hour, and I want them to. Let the vultures fight over the corpse of our own permits."

"You're sure?"

"No." She said it cleanly, without drama. "But certainty is just fear with a filing number. Send it."

Outside, the sky tore open. The first true gusts hit the dome and the building groaned like a ship. The lights died—not flickered, *died*—and the emergency generators caught with a stutter, dropping us into dim amber half-dark. The terminal stayed up on battery. My window was now measured in minutes of backup power and seconds of human patience.

I pressed transmit at 21:51.

The progress bar crawled. Four hundred and twelve recipients. The drive spun. Somewhere in the dark of the city, in registry offices and newsrooms and the inboxes of men who thought valleys were inventory, the escarpment people's names began arriving—lawful, witnessed, older than every lie stacked on top of them.

Truth doesn't detonate all at once. It detonates in sequence, room by room, and you can hear it coming.

I heard it coming. Boots on the stairs.

* * *

Pell entered the fourth floor the way he entered everything: smiling, unhurried, as though the world were a restaurant that had held his table.

He wasn't alone. Four collectors fanned out between the filing cabinets, and behind them, wrists bound, marched Tamsin—one eye swelling shut, chin up, glaring at Pell with the pure hatred of someone who had already survived worse than him. My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

"There he is," Pell said warmly. He'd traded his gunboat slicker for a dry coat; he'd come through the storm by the tunnels, of course, the ones under Registry Square that his syndicate had quietly purchased along with half the city's plumbing. "Mr. Dravenmoor. Do you know, when the report came in at nine-oh-one, I actually felt proud of you? Everyone else runs from their debts. You ran *toward* the thing that would settle them. That's character." He spread his hands. "Now. The originals. The copies you just sent are an inconvenience—we'll manage the inconvenience, we always manage inconveniences—but the originals, the certified ones, those are value. Hand them over and walk out of here debt-free. Not forgiven. *Free.* There's a difference, and you of all people appreciate precision."

Behind him, one of the collectors had a hand on Tamsin's shoulder, and she was letting it stay there, which frightened me more than a knife would have. Tamsin never let anything stay.

"Pell," I said, "the transmission's live. Four hundred and twelve recipients. It finished ninety seconds ago."

"I know." His smile didn't waver, which was the horror of him—it never wavered, it simply absorbed facts and metabolized them into optimism. "And in the morning, every one of those recipients will receive a follow-up: forensic analysis demonstrating the documents are forgeries. Very convincing forgeries, produced by a desperate debtor and a feral woman. The story writes itself, Xanthe. It always does. We don't suppress truth. We just make it expensive." He stepped closer, boots quiet on the tile. "Unless the originals surface through proper channels. Certified. Authenticated. Through an officer of the registry whose testimony carries legal weight." His eyes moved past me, and for the first time since the stairwell, his smile adjusted. "Miss Ferrant. Working late."

Odile stood beside the terminal with her access card still in her hand, and I watched her arrive at the moment she'd been walking toward for nineteen days—or maybe her whole career. I watched her choose.

"The transmission originated under my credentials," she said. Her voice didn't shake. Clerks' voices never shake; they've been trained out of it, and some of them grow back. "Authenticated, timestamped, logged. Which means the documents aren't a debtor's forgery, Mr. Pell. They're a registry disclosure. Under statute eleven, a disclosure made by a licensed officer cannot be retracted by the firm—only investigated. And investigations," she added, with the small, terrible pleasure of a woman quoting scripture back to a false priest, "are public record."

Pell looked at her for a long moment. Around us, the collectors shifted, uncertain—men built for threats suddenly standing inside a sentence they couldn't shoot.

"You'd end your career," Pell said gently. "Everything you built."

"Yes," Odile said. "It's astonishing how light it feels."

"And the girl?" One of the collectors tightened his grip on Tamsin. "Loose ends are still loose ends, Miss Ferrant. Statutes don't apply in stairwells."

"They apply here," said a voice from the doorway, and Maren's granddaughter should not have been there—but it wasn't her. It was two of Odile's own security officers, wet from the storm, weapons drawn, and behind them the broad silhouette of Sefa, river-soaked and furious, holding a boat hook like a scepter. "Because we've been listening for four minutes," the officer said, "and Miss Ferrant called us at nine-fifteen. Before you arrived. She asked us to witness everything."

Pell turned. For one unguarded instant, I saw behind the smile: a man doing arithmetic and getting, for the first time in his life, a negative number. Four hundred twelve recipients. A licensed officer's sworn disclosure. His own employees witnessing. The storm knocking out the syndicate's private lines along with everyone else's, so that no order he gave tonight could be quietly unwound by morning.

Optimism, I understood then, looking at his face, had never been his philosophy. It had been his camouflage. And the storm had blown it off.

"Tamsin," I said. "Come here."

The collector holding her glanced at Pell, got nothing—no instruction, no smile, no permission—and in that vacuum, Sefa crossed the room and took the man's wrist and bent it back with the unhurried competence of someone who had unloaded cargo in worse ports than this. Tamsin walked free, spat on the tile near Pell's shoes, and came to stand at my shoulder like a geologist taking a bearing.

Pell straightened his coat. He actually smiled again—smaller now, realer, and somehow worse. "This isn't settlement, Mr. Dravenmoor. This is an installment."

"I know," I said. "Send me an invoice."

He left. Collectors don't run; they withdraw strategically. The door closed behind them, and the thunder filled the space they'd occupied, and Odile sat down slowly in her own chair as if her legs had finally received the memo.

"It's done," she said, to no one, to the terminal, to the green light still glowing. "It's actually done."

Outside, the storm peaked over Corvane, and the city's lights came back on district by district like eyes opening—and every one of those eyes, in every office and newsroom and rival boardroom, was now looking at the same documents, the same names, the same valley that remembered who it belonged to.

Tamsin touched my arm. "Marsh needs to hear it," she said. "He sealed himself in that dark for this. Somebody has to tell him it worked."

I looked at the window, at the storm beginning its long retreat east, and I already knew the next problem, because there is always a next problem: the syndicate's gunboats were regrouping on the river, and the fastest way back to the escarpment ran straight through them.

"Sefa," I said. "How do you feel about a night run?"

She grinned like weather. "Get in the boat."
