## 21. Settling Accounts

Pell came to the escarpment on foot, which told Xanthe everything before the man opened his mouth.

Three weeks after the broadcast, the Vellum Syndicate's gunboats were scrap questions in a magistrate's docket and Mr. Pell's name sat at the center of four separate inquiries, and the man who had once arrived everywhere by river with a smile and a ledger now climbed the switchback trail from the valley floor wearing dust like any pilgrim. No escort. No pistol visible. Hands empty at his sides, palms slightly out, the posture of a man demonstrating he had nothing left to take with.

Xanthe watched him come from the weather vane platform above Maren's hall, where he'd spent the morning re-seating the vane's axle after the last storm bent it. He let Pell climb. It seemed only fair that a man who had made his living letting others do the climbing should feel the gradient himself.

"You look well," Pell said when he arrived, breathing harder than a man his build should. He'd lost weight. The tailored jacket was gone, replaced by a field coat with a patched elbow, and the affability was still there but it ran on something thinner now, like an engine burning its own oil. "The altitude suits you. I always said you were a mountain man pretending to be a river man."

"You said I was an asset pretending to be a man," Xanthe said. "Close enough."

Pell laughed, and the laugh was genuine, and that was the horror of him that had never changed. He sat down on the platform's edge without being invited, looked out at the escarpment villages stitched along the cliff line, the terraces, the smoke, the children running the goat paths with the fearless economy of kids who'd grown up knowing exactly which rocks held.

"Four inquiries," Pell said. "Asset seizures in two provinces. The syndicate has disavowed me in writing — you'd be amazed how fast ink dries when it's running downhill. My accounts are frozen. My boat is impounded." He said it all pleasantly, an itinerary, a weather report. "I've been walking for eleven days."

"Is that a confession or a complaint?"

"It's context." He turned, and for a moment the smile dropped and Xanthe saw the actual man underneath, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the trail. "I came to make you an offer. One last one. The last one I have."

"There's nothing to offer. That's the whole point of what I did. You can't buy what everyone already owns."

"Ah." Pell held up a finger, and for a second he was fully himself again, the collector across the table, the man who'd murdered a scout in the tree line and spoken afterward about efficiency. "But here's what you haven't priced in, Xanthe, because you're an honest man and honest men are terrible accountants. Investigations end. Inquiries close. In two years, maybe three, the syndicate restructures, the names change, the paper gets laundered through three new holding companies, and the men who remember — the men like me, who know where the bodies are filed — become either very valuable or very inconvenient. I'd rather be valuable." He leaned forward. "I know where the syndicate's original debt instruments are archived. Including yours. The physical ledger. The one with your signature, your father's land as collateral, the whole ugly chain. Everything public now started with that paper, and that paper still exists, and the people who hold it will hold a grudge long after the magistrate loses interest."

Xanthe kept his hands still on the vane's mounting bracket. Below them a kid on the goat path waved up at Pell, mistaking him for a visitor, and Pell waved back with real warmth, and the kid ran on.

"You're offering to steal my debt ledger," Xanthe said.

"I'm offering to *destroy* it. There's a difference. Theft is a crime. Destruction is a favor." Pell's voice softened into the register he'd used in the gunboat cabin, the one that made violence sound like hospitality. "All I want in exchange is what you've already got and don't even want: the vault's location, and Tamsin's cooperation with my lawyers. The syndicate's successors will litigate the inheritance claims for a decade. If I can position myself as a consultant — a *reformed* consultant — I survive. You get your paper burned. Everyone profits. That was always my philosophy, you know. I never once lied to you about it."

"You murdered a man and called it logistics."

"And you burned a debt economy and called it daylight." Pell shrugged. "We're both romantics. The difference is I'm asking permission this time."

Xanthe looked at him for a long moment. And the strange thing, the thing he would think about later, was that he wasn't tempted — not by the ledger's destruction, because Maren's council had already anticipated that. The genealogies were copied in three places, the vault's contents were being catalogued under Tamsin's supervision with notarized witnesses, and the debt itself had died publicly, on the record, in front of registries and newsrooms and every rival claimant in the valley. A ledger in an archive was a ghost. It could frighten, but it couldn't collect.

What he felt instead, looking at Pell, was something colder and sadder: recognition. Six months ago he'd have taken this deal. Six months ago he'd have traded anything — the vault, the villages, his own name — to make the paper stop existing, because he'd believed the paper was the thing. That stopping meant paying. That freedom was a transaction.

"You know what I figured out on this cliff?" Xanthe said. "You never once threatened me. Did you notice that? In all our meetings — the gunboat, the tree line, all of it — you never threatened. You only ever *offered*. And the offers were always worse than any threat, because an offer makes the damage your own fault." He stood. "So here's my offer, and it's the only one. Walk back down the way you came. If the syndicate's ghosts come for you, that's between you and them. If you ever set foot on escarpment ground again with an offer in your mouth, Maren's council has a magistrate's writ and a very patient constable. And if you genuinely want to be reformed —" he almost laughed at himself for saying it, and meant it anyway — "the valley takes people. That's what it does. But it takes them as they are, not as collateral. Come back with empty hands and mean it, and we'll talk. Come back with a deal, and don't bother climbing."

Pell looked at him for a long time. The affability drained out of his face by degrees, not replaced by anger but by something more honest: bewilderment. A man who had spent his whole life in a world where everything was an offer, meeting a door that wasn't one.

"You're leaving value on the table," he said finally. "The ledger. Your own debt. You could be *clean*, Xanthe. Actually clean, on paper, forever."

"I was never dirty on paper," Xanthe said. "That was the trap. And I'm not clean now either. I'm just *known*." He picked up the vane's axle wrench and turned it over in his hands, not as a threat, just to have something honest in them. "Turns out that's better."

Pell stood. He straightened his patched coat with the old precision, and for a moment Xanthe thought he might say something true — might ask, even, what it was like, the not-running. But Pell had walked eleven days to make an offer, and a man like that couldn't leave a mountain with empty hands, so instead he said, "You'll regret this when the successors consolidate," and Xanthe said, "Probably. I'll regret it here, though, with people," and that, of all things, was what made Pell's face flicker.

He went down the switchbacks without looking back. Xanthe watched until the trail took him into the valley haze, and then he finished fixing the weather vane, because the wind was coming around to the northwest and the vane needed to be honest about it.

* * *

The vault had become a library.

That was Tamsin's doing. In the weeks since the broadcast she had come down from the caves by degrees — first to the forward camp, then to the cliff village, sleeping at first in the doorway of whatever room she occupied like a dog guarding a threshold, then, slowly, in the rooms themselves. Her hands had stopped shaking except in cold weather. She'd cut her hair with a fishing knife and let Maren's granddaughter redo the job properly, and she had begun, with the methodical ferocity of a woman making up for lost years, to catalogue.

The council had voted to leave the vault where it was — in the limestone, in the dark, on ancestral ground — and to build the catalogue outward from it instead of hauling the inheritance into the light like plunder. So Tamsin worked at a long table in the hall Maren kept for gatherings, under lamplight, with the escarpment's old people sitting across from her in shifts, reading the genealogies aloud in the valley dialect that predated every permit in the region, while she transcribed, cross-referenced, and numbered.

Xanthe came down in the evenings, when the wind work was done. He'd told himself at first that he was helping. He was not, particularly, helping. Tamsin's system had the elegance of a mind that had spent months alone with nothing to organize but survival, and she ran the table the way she'd once run a geological survey — total, unsentimental, exact. What he actually did was sit, and hold documents flat while the lamplight steadied, and listen.

Tonight the old man across the table was Ede, who claimed — and no one disputed — to be the last living person taught the valley's pre-colonial script. He traced a line of it on the oldest deed with one cracked fingernail and translated in a voice like gravel in water: *the ridge line from the split stone to the falling spring, held in common, held in perpetuity, held by those who keep the paths open.* Not owned. Held. The word came up in every document, Tamsin had found. The vault's people had no word for ownership that didn't also mean guardianship. Every deed was a promise with a boundary.

"They didn't write *mine*," Ede said, tapping the page. "They wrote *ours, while we keep it*. Your syndicate clerks read these for a century and saw nothing worth stealing, because there was no *mine* to steal. Only *keep*. You can't foreclose on a keeping." He looked up at Xanthe with bright, ancient amusement. "You burned their ledgers and it turns out the fire was already in the language. You were just the match that got carried indoors."

Tamsin snorted without looking up from her transcription. "He's been waiting three weeks to say that. Let him have it."

Xanthe held the deed flat and felt something settle in his chest that he had no ledger to record. *Ours, while we keep it.* He thought of the compass on the table in his room above Maren's hall, pointing nowhere he needed to go. He thought of Odile Ferrant's testimony, still unfolding in the provincial capital, the paper trail unspooling like a fuse that had finally found its charge. He thought of Pell climbing down the switchbacks with his empty hands and his full mouth.

"Page forty-one," Tamsin said, sliding a document toward him, and her voice had changed — the surveyor's voice, the one that meant she'd found a seam. "Read the witness line."

He read it. The witness line of the oldest genealogy, the one that established the valley's founding families in common keeping, carried three names. The third was rendered in the old script, and Ede translated it slowly, and the name was *Draven*.

The hall went quiet. The lamplight ticked.

"That's not possible," Xanthe said. His own voice sounded far away. "My family came upriver as laborers. Contract workers. My grandfather used to say we'd never owned so much as a fence post."

"Contract workers," Ede said gently, "whose contracts were written by people who had already burned the first set of books." He turned the deed around so the witness line faced Xanthe. "The Draven line was a keeping family. Keepers of the western paths. When the concessions came, the ones who resisted were scattered or renamed, and the ones who signed labor contracts did it because the alternative was worse. Your grandfather didn't say you'd never owned anything because it was true. He said it because by his time it was true, and grief sounds like fact if you repeat it enough."

Tamsin was watching him with the expression she'd worn in the caves, guarding the approach — except now it was turned outward, an offering instead of a barricade. "The inheritance isn't just theirs," she said. "It's a map of everyone who was erased to make the permits look clean. You didn't just broadcast the villages' title, Xanthe. You broadcast the proof that half the valley's *laborers* were descended from its keepers. There are going to be more names. Dozens. Maybe hundreds. People are going to start coming up that trail the way Pell did — except they'll be coming home."

Xanthe looked down at the witness line, at the cracked old script that his hands had held flat while an old man translated his own name out of the dark, and he understood that the vault was not finished with him. That inheritances, real ones, didn't sit in limestone waiting to be catalogued. They walked up switchbacks. They knocked. They arrived on foot, dusty, with empty hands, asking to be let in.

Outside, the wind came around to the northwest, and the new-fixed vane turned to meet it, honest at last about where the weather was coming from — and down on the valley trail, small in the dusk, Xanthe could see figures climbing. Not one man this time. A whole line of them.
