## 22. Escarpment and Compass

The weather vane had been crooked since before Xanthe arrived, and for six weeks he'd told himself it wasn't his problem.

It sat atop Maren's hall like a drunk leaning on a doorframe—a copper heron frozen mid-lift, its tail bent from some storm older than the oldest child in the village. The villagers had stopped seeing it. That was the thing about broken things in places where people stayed: they became furniture, then folklore, then invisible. *The heron leans because the wind asked it to,* one of the grandmothers said, and everyone laughed the way you laugh at a story that's been true so long it's load-bearing.

Xanthe climbed onto the hall's roof on a Tuesday morning with a wrench, a spare bearing he'd traded two batteries for downriver, and no contract authorizing any of it. The escarpment spread out below him in its long red shoulder, terraced and green at the seams, the river a bright thread stitched through the valley floor. Somewhere down there, surveyors were still crawling over the concession lands with their instruments, re-measuring a world that had been measured wrong on purpose for a hundred years.

He worked the old bearing loose while the wind talked to him. It always talked up here. He'd spent eleven years listening to wind professionally—cataloguing its moods, pricing its tantrums, selling its secrets to insurers and grain brokers and anyone else who needed to know what the sky intended. Wind had been his ledger, his currency, his excuse to never unpack a bag.

Now it was just weather. He found he could hear it better that way.

The new bearing seated with a satisfying click. He gave the heron's neck a quarter-turn until it faced true north-northeast, into the prevailing gusts, balanced on its pivot like a living thing deciding. When he let go, it swung once, twice, and settled—pointing at nothing in particular, which is what a good vane does. It doesn't choose a direction. It reports the one the world has chosen.

"About time," Maren called from below. She stood in the hall's doorway with two cups, gray-haired and immovable as the cliff she'd been born under. "That bird's been lying about the wind for nine years."

"It wasn't lying. It was stuck."

"Same thing, in a village." She held up a cup. He came down the ladder and took it. Chicory, bitter and hot, sweetened with the honey Tamsin's bees had started producing now that she'd stopped living wild and started keeping hives again. "Council meets tonight," Maren said, in the tone she used for things that were not requests. "You'll be there."

"I'm not on the council."

"You're on the roof of my hall fixing my vane without being asked. That's how council membership starts everywhere I've ever heard of." She sipped. "Also, the children have been asking when storm lessons start again. Neme broke the rain gauge trying to measure yesterday's squall by hand."

"He caught it?"

"Two fingers short. He wants to know what he did wrong."

Xanthe looked out at the valley. Downriver, past the burned checkpoints and the empty syndicate offices and the registry building in Corvane whose filing system he'd gutted in a blackout six weeks ago, the world was still arguing about what the vault documents meant. Lawyers, tribunals, rival claimants discovering that their grandfather's grandfather's deed rested on paper thinner than the genealogies carved into limestone. The investigations would take years. Some of the concessions were already void; others were fighting like animals in a snare.

Up here, a boy wanted to know why he'd missed the rainfall by two fingers.

"Tell him I'll show him after lessons," Xanthe said. "The error's usually in how you hold your hand against the wind. You brace, you tilt, you cheat yourself."

Maren smiled the way she did when she'd already won an argument hours before the other person knew it had started. "After lessons, then."

* * *

The school met in the hall's lower room, because the village had never built a separate one and saw no reason to start. Eleven children between six and fourteen, sitting on benches their grandparents had planed, learning letters and sums and, three mornings a week, storms.

Xanthe had taught adults before—contract chasers, insurance adjusters, people who wanted weather reduced to a number they could bet against. Teaching children was different work. They didn't want the number. They wanted the sky itself, and they weren't embarrassed to say so.

"Clouds first," he told them, standing at the open window with the whole visible atmosphere as his blackboard. "Everything else comes after. Neme—what do you see?"

Neme, ten years old, missing tooth, permanently sunburned, squinted east with the ferocious concentration of a boy who'd failed once and intended never to fail again. "Tall ones. Gray bottoms."

"Tall ones with gray bottoms. Good. Tall means what?"

"Growing."

"Gray bottom means what?"

"Full." He hesitated. "Heavy."

"Heavy with what?"

"Water." Then, catching himself: "Or hail. If the top goes flat."

"If the top goes flat," Xanthe agreed. "Anvil cloud. That's the sky putting its fist on the table. What do we do when the sky puts its fist on the table?"

"Get the goats in," said a small girl named Iri with total seriousness, and half the room nodded, because the goats were everyone's problem and everyone's joy, and a lost goat could ruin a week faster than any flood.

"Get the goats in," Xanthe confirmed. "And then?"

The answers came tumbling—the cistern covers, the lantern check, moving the drying racks under the hall overhang, warning the ferry pilot if Sefa happened to be moored downstream. He listened to all of it and corrected almost none of it, because they were right. This was the difference between the villages and every city he'd ever chased storms out of: here, weather knowledge wasn't a product. It was a chore list written by the sky, and everyone did chores.

He showed them the pressure in his own body now—the way his old knee ached twelve hours ahead of a front, a trick he'd learned from a horse trader in another life and had never thought worth teaching anyone, because who pays for a man's knee? These children paid attention. That was the whole tuition.

"After the tall clouds," he said, "look at the light. Green light means hail. Yellow-brown means dust coming in ahead of the rain. And if the air goes still—" he held up a hand, palm out, "—still enough that you can hear your own heartbeat, that's not peace. That's the sky holding its breath. Respect it."

Iri raised her hand. "Did you ever get hurt chasing them?"

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when children ask the question adults have been politely not asking for weeks. There was a scar along Xanthe's ribs from Pell's collectors, and a stiffness in his left shoulder from a lightning-adjacent near miss years ago, and other marks he didn't inventory anymore.

"Once or twice," he said. "That's why I teach you to read them instead of chase them. Reading keeps you home. Chasing—" he searched for it, and found the truth without much trouble, which was new, "—chasing was how I kept from staying anywhere."

Neme frowned, unconvinced. "But somebody has to chase them. To learn things."

"Somebody does," Xanthe admitted. "And maybe one of you will, someday, with better instruments than mine and a home to come back to. That's the trick nobody taught me. You can go looking at the whole sky—as long as you've got somewhere to land."

* * *

His room above the hall was small and he had made no effort to make it smaller-feeling, which was to say he'd finally unpacked.

Six weeks, and the bag stood empty in the corner, folded flat, serving no purpose except as proof. His instruments lived on the shelf Maren's nephew had mounted: barometer, anemometer, the rain gauge Neme was forbidden to touch but touched anyway. On the wall, a map of the valley—not a concession map, not a permit map, just the valley, drawn by Tamsin from memory and corrected by a dozen villagers who kept coming by to argue about where the old paths really ran. The corrections were the best part. Every argument added a line. The map grew more true by being contested.

And on the table by the window, the compass.

It had been his father's, and then his, and it had pointed him across eleven years of flats and river country and escarpment country, always toward the next job, the next front, the next place far enough away that nobody knew his name or his debts. He kept it on the table the way you keep a retired tool—not useless, exactly, but off duty.

Some mornings he watched it needle swing when he moved around the room, settling always north, always pointing downriver toward Corvane, toward the registries and tribunals and the concession house with its new, nervous administration. There were days he felt the pull himself. News came upriver weekly now: Odile Ferrant testifying in a hearing room with her files fanned out like a card player laying down a winning hand; Pell's name surfacing in one inquiry after another, the syndicate's smiling enforcer discovering that daylight was a debt even his employers wouldn't cover; Tamsin's cataloguing project attracting actual scholars, actual archivists, people who wept quietly over pre-colonial treaties the way Xanthe once wept over a perfectly formed supercell.

There was a part of him that wanted to be down there for all of it. The old engine. The restlessness that had carried him through Chapter One of this life, chasing a storm across the flats with debt collectors' calls buzzing in his pocket.

But the compass pointed nowhere he needed to go. That was the discovery, and it hadn't come all at once—it had come the way the village's trust had come, in increments, in meals left outside his door during the worst of the healing, in Maren saying *you'll be there* and meaning *you belong here*, in the council voting to stand rather than hide and asking him—not hiring him, asking him—to carry the documents because he was theirs now, and theirs meant something the Vellum Syndicate's ledgers had no column for.

Stillness, it turned out, had a price. Everything did; he'd built a career on that arithmetic. But the price of stillness was being known, and being known meant being accountable, and he'd spent eleven years believing that equation ran only one direction.

It ran both ways. Being known also meant that when the fever took him in week three, someone sat up with him through the night. It meant Neme showing up at his door with a broken rain gauge and absolute faith it would be fixed. It meant that when he walked through the village, people said his name like it was a fact about the place, like the heron on the roof, like the spring below the hall—something that belonged to the landscape and was therefore everyone's to rely on.

Invisibility had been someone else's design for him. The syndicate had liked him unseen, unplaceable, deniable—a man with no address was a man with no claims. Visibility, he'd learned, was the inheritance. Not the limestone vault, not the parchment genealogies. Those proved the villages belonged to the valley. What Xanthe had was smaller and harder-won: proof that he belonged to the villages.

* * *

Evening came gold across the terraces. The council met in the hall's upper room, and Xanthe attended, and discovered that attending meant talking, because Maren had engineered the agenda.

"The registry office sent word downriver," she reported. "They want a village representative at the autumn hearings. Someone who can speak to the documents, the boundaries, the old paths." Around the table, faces turned toward Tamsin, who shook her head—she had the archive, the archive had her—and toward the two elders who spoke the oldest dialect and wanted no part of Corvane's marble rooms.

Then they turned toward Xanthe.

"I'm not from here," he said, which was true, and which he'd said before, and which had stopped working as an argument sometime around week four.

"You're from here now," said Denno, the ferryman, flatly. "You fixed my roof in the storm. You pulled my boy out of the flooded cut. You broadcast the papers that got our land back. Corvane knows your face better than ours."

"That's not a recommendation."

"It's the only qualification that matters," Maren said. "They need someone they can't ignore. You're loud, stubborn, and inconvenient. We've all noticed."

There was laughter around the table, easy and unkind in the way family is allowed to be. Xanthe looked at the map on the wall, at the valley with its contested lines slowly resolving into something truer, and felt the old restlessness stir and find nothing to feed on. The hearings would take days. Maybe weeks. He'd go down, speak, and come back up. Come back. The phrase had weight now, ballast, a direction that ended somewhere instead of just beginning.

"I'll go," he said. "But I'm taking Neme. He needs to see what a registry looks like from the inside, so he knows exactly what he's reading storms for."

"Approved," Maren said instantly, before anyone could object, because she'd probably planned that too.

* * *

Later, alone in his room, he set the compass on the windowsill where the last light caught it.

The needle swung, wavered, settled north—downriver, toward Corvane, toward the hearings he'd leave for in three days. Toward everything still unfinished: the tribunals, the syndicate's slow unraveling, the scholars picking through Tamsin's catalogue, the world remaking itself one voided claim at a time.

He watched it for a long moment. Then he reached out and, gently, turned the compass face-down on the sill.

Not thrown away. Not sold, not traded, not abandoned on some riverbank the way he'd abandoned everything else for eleven years. Just resting. A tool off duty, waiting for the day one of the children needed it—Neme, maybe, or Iri with her serious goat-frown—some kid who'd read the sky all their life and might someday want to read the wider map too. He'd teach them bearings the way Maren's people had taught him belonging: freely, without invoice, as an inheritance.

Below the window, the village settled into evening. Smoke from cookfires. The heron on the roof turning faithfully with the wind, reporting honestly for the first time in nine years. Somewhere, the goats complaining about bedtime.

The compass pointed nowhere he needed to go.

Every direction he needed was right here.
