## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The chart said there was nothing at those coordinates, and the chart had been wrong about a great many things, so Hazel Delacroix had learned long ago to trust the water over the paper.

He stood on the deck of the *Marisol* in the gray half-light before dawn, coffee mug steaming in one hand, and watched the Gulf breathe. The barge he'd been hired to work from sat anchored forty yards off his stern — a rust-streaked flat-top called the *Pilar B*, crewed by two brothers named Fuentes who communicated mostly in shrugs and one word of English apiece ("*sí*" and "no"). The job had come through a broker in Corpus who didn't ask questions, which was the first thing worth noting about it. The second was the pay. The third was that nobody would tell him what he was diving for.

"Charted as empty," the broker had said, sliding the envelope across a table sticky with old beer. "But there's something down there. Sonar contact. Client wants it recovered. That's all you need."

"And the client is?"

"A man who pays on time."

In Hazel's experience, a man who paid on time was either honest or very good at pretending, and both kinds eventually showed you which.

He drained the coffee and started checking his gear. This part of the ritual he loved without apology — the weight of the tank in his hands, the satisfying click of the regulator into its first stage, the small ceremony of laying everything out on the deck boards in an order only he understood. Sixteen years of salvage diving will teach you that your life depends on equipment you can inspect with your fingers in the dark. He'd once done a full pre-dive check during a brownout in a hurricane's outer bands, by feel alone, and come up grinning. His ex-wife had called this "evidence of a disorder." He preferred to think of it as fluency.

The water below was sixty feet down to a bottom of gray mud and shell hash, visibility maybe eight feet on a good day, which today was not. The sonar contact sat in a shallow bowl of the seabed where the current had scoured out a hollow — a shape roughly rectangular, roughly man-made, roughly interesting.

Hazel rolled backward off the dive platform and let the Gulf take him.

* * *

Down was down. That was the whole philosophy of the trade, really. Everything above the surface was negotiation, paperwork, weather, other people's opinions. Below it, the rules were simple and non-negotiable: breathe, equalize, don't rush, and never trust anything that looks like a gift from the sea.

He followed the descent line through water that went from green to brown to a kind of thoughtful darkness. At fifty feet the mud plain opened up around him, and there it was.

Not a ship. Not even close to a ship. It was a container — a shipping-container-sized box, half-buried at an angle, its corrugated sides furred with a season's growth of bryozoans and soft coral. No wreck should have put it here. There was no debris field, no hull plating, no trail of scattered cargo telling the story of how it arrived. It was simply *placed*, dropped or sunk deliberately into this hollow like a letter into a mailbox.

Hazel hung in the water beside it and felt the first cold finger of unease trace down his spine, right through six millimeters of wetsuit.

Salvage men develop a sense for wrecks the way old fishermen develop a sense for weather. A wreck has a grammar. It tells you what happened — the way things broke, the way they settled, the order in which the sea claimed them. This thing had no grammar. It had no story except the one somebody had written for it on purpose.

He swam the length of it slowly, running his glove along the steel. Near the end panel, someone had ground off whatever markings had been there — he could feel the raw, sanded patch under the growth, a scar of deliberate erasure. But corrosion works faster than sandpaper, and in the pitted groove where a stencil had once been, enough metal remained to spell out a fragment:

**—CESSION 44—**

Concession 44. Not a vessel name. Not a registration number. A land-thing. A paper-thing. Concessions were for timber tracts, mineral rights, river dredging permits — documents that lived in government registries, not at the bottom of the Gulf.

Hazel hovered there, breathing slow, watching his own exhaust bubble up toward a surface he suddenly trusted less than usual.

The job, he thought, was to recover "it." Singular. The broker's envelope had contained no manifest, no description, no dimensions. Just coordinates and a wire transfer. Which meant whoever sent him down either didn't know what was down here, or knew exactly and wanted it described as little as possible.

Well. He'd been paid to bring up cargo, and here was cargo. He wasn't going to solve anything at sixty feet with a quarter tank of curiosity and three-quarters of air. He clipped onto the container's lifting points — four proper pad eyes, rated hardware, installed by someone competent, which told its own quiet story — rigged the lift bag, and flooded it.

The container came up out of the mud with a sigh of displaced silt, slow and obedient, and rose through the darkening water toward the light.

* * *

On the surface, the Fuentes brothers helped him haul it onto the barge with chain hoists and the particular gracelessness of men who were strong in every direction except graceful. The older brother, Esteban, whose entire English vocabulary was "*sí*," looked at the container, looked at Hazel, and shrugged the shrug of a man who had hauled stranger things and been paid worse.

It took them most of the morning to cut the seals. They were proper customs seals, lead-and-wire, stamped with the same fragmentary numbering — 44 — and they broke clean under the bolt cutters with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside: crates. Foam-packed, military-neat, each one stenciled with handling codes and a warning label in three languages that amounted to *do not open, do not ask, do not exist*. Most of it was electronics — sonar units, survey instruments, sealed hard cases of equipment that looked expensive and governmental and entirely out of place on a fishing barge off the Texas coast.

And at the back, wedged upright between two crates as if someone had hidden it in a hurry, a single case the size of a briefcase. Gray composite. Sealed. Stamped across the lid in faded yellow paint:

**CONCESSION 44 — DELGADO EXPEDITION — PROPERTY OF THE GRANTOR**

Hazel stood looking at it for a long moment, bolt cutters still in his hand.

"Esteban," he called. "*Sí?*" said Esteban, appearing at his elbow. Hazel pointed at the case. Esteban looked at it, then at Hazel, then performed a shrug of such magnificent ambiguity that it could have meant *take it*, *leave it*, *burn it*, or *I have always liked you and fear nothing*.

Hazel took it.

He told himself it was procedure. Salvage law was murky enough that possession was nine-tenths of everything, and a case stamped with a concession number wasn't naval property on its face — concessions were civil instruments, commercial ones. If the client wanted it, the client could describe it and claim it. Until then, it rode in the wheelhouse of the *Marisol*, under a tarp, next to the spare anchor chain, where nobody would look twice.

He told himself this. He almost believed it. The truth was simpler and less flattering: the case had *Delgado* stenciled on it, and Hazel Delacroix had spent forty-one years being the only Delacroix anybody on the coast had ever heard of, and some superstitious animal part of his brain decided the case already belonged to him the way a stray dog decides a porch belongs to it.

He stowed it. He finished the recovery. By mid-afternoon the barge was riding low with recovered equipment and the *Marisol*'s little diesel was idling, ready to tow her home, and Hazel allowed himself the day's first real breath and the fantasy of a cold beer at the Sandpiper Bar, where the ice was questionable but the company was worse, which made for excellent conversation.

That was when he saw the navy boats.

* * *

There were two of them, sitting out past the channel markers at what their drivers probably considered a discreet distance. Gray hulls, low profiles, patrol boats of the sort the Navy used for security zones and interdiction work. They weren't moving. They weren't fishing, weren't patrolling in any pattern a patrol boat would recognize, weren't doing anything at all except existing at the edge of Hazel's vision like two bad thoughts.

He watched them for ten minutes through binoculars, leaning against the *Marisol*'s wheelhouse with the practiced laziness of a man who was definitely just admiring the view. The boats did not move. One of them turned, very slightly, so that its bow pointed more directly at the barge. Then it turned back, as if embarrassed to have been caught paying attention.

"Huh," said Hazel, to nobody.

He ran through the innocent explanations first, because he was an optimist in the way drowning men are optimists about the surface. Live-fire exercise zone — no, he'd have gotten the notice; he was on the Coast Guard's mailing list twice, once by accident. Drug interdiction — possible, but they'd have run a pattern, and interdiction boats got bored and left. Survey work — no survey vessel sits dead in the water for an hour staring at a salvage barge.

What they were doing was watching. And what you watch, in Hazel's experience, is either something you're protecting or something you're waiting to take.

He thought about the container. About the ground-off markings, the military-grade pad eyes, the customs seals on civilian cargo. About a client who paid on time and asked nothing. About a site charted as empty that held a box the size of a shipping container, sunk with care into a hollow where the current would keep it swept and findable — findable by anyone with the coordinates, which apparently included him and, evidently, the United States Navy.

The job was never about salvage. The thought arrived without drama, the way the worst thoughts do, calm as a tide gauge. Somebody had lost this container, or hidden it, or sunk it to make it disappear, and somebody else — the client, the broker, whoever stood behind them — had hired a salvage man with a reputation for finding things and asking little, because a salvage man recovering cargo is a legal fact, while a navy diver recovering cargo is a federal act with paperwork attached. He was the eraser. He was the second half of the disappearance.

And now he was standing on the evidence.

Hazel lowered the binoculars. The patrol boats sat on the horizon, patient as church.

Here was the arithmetic, and he did it honestly, standing in the wheelhouse with the diesel muttering beneath him. Option one: radio the client, report the navy presence, let the professionals sort it out. Cost: he became a witness, witnesses get managed, and managed men on this coast tended to lose contracts, licenses, and occasionally the habit of sleeping well. Option two: declare the find to the Coast Guard, all of it, the container and the case and the seals. Cost: the same, plus he'd be handing the case to exactly the people whose boats were loitering out there pretending to be scenery. Option three.

Option three was the tarp, the spare anchor chain, and a slow, unhurried tow back to harbor like a man with nothing on his mind but fuel costs and dinner.

"You're going to regret this," he said aloud, to himself, in the tone of a man giving sound advice to an acquaintance known to ignore it.

Then he checked the radar — clear — waited until the patrol boats fell behind a line of shrimp trawlers coming in off the grounds, and brought the *Marisol* about.

* * *

The case rode home under the tarp, warm from the afternoon sun, heavier than its thirty pounds. Hazel felt it the whole way in, the way you feel a cracked tooth — not pain exactly, just a constant awareness that something in your mouth was wrong and would eventually demand attention.

At the dock, Esteban shook his hand with grave formality. "*Sí*," said Esteban, meaning, Hazel chose to believe, *good luck, you beautiful idiot*.

Hazel carried the case up the dock as the light went copper over the harbor, past the bait stand and the net racks and the Sandpiper Bar, where through the open window he could hear Ray Milland arguing with the jukebox about Hank Williams. Normal sounds. A normal evening. He climbed the stairs to his apartment above the chandlery, set the case on the kitchen table under the one working bulb, and stood looking at it the way a man looks at a letter he knows is bad news.

The stamp read DELGADO EXPEDITION. He turned the case over. On the bottom, small and neat, someone had written in grease pencil a string of numbers and letters — a date code, maybe, or a routing mark — and beneath it, half worn away by a thumb that had handled it often:

*Río arriba.*

Upriver.

Hazel got a beer from the icebox, sat down across from the case, and drank for a while in silence, listening to the harbor settle into night around him. Somewhere out there, two gray boats were reporting his course and speed to somebody. Somewhere upriver, whatever a Delgado expedition was, it had left a case behind that the United States Navy wanted found and a client wanted found faster, and both of them had assumed he'd be a tool about it.

He set down the beer. He picked up a pry bar.

"Well," he told the case, fitting the bar under the sealed lid. "Let's see what you know that I don't."
