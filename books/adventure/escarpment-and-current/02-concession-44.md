## 2. Concession 44

The case sat on Hazel's kitchen table like a guest who'd overstayed his welcome and knew it.

He'd made coffee he didn't drink and eaten half a plate of eggs he didn't taste, and through all of it the case just sat there, seawater-stained and patient, its brass latches green with age except where his pry bar had kissed them fresh and bright. Stenciled across the lid in flaking white paint: **CONCESIÓN 44 — DELGADO EXPEDICIÓN — PROP. PRIVADA**.

Private property. Sure. And the Gulf was private property too, if you asked the right lawyers, which apparently somebody had, because navy patrol boats didn't shadow salvage barges for fun.

Hazel wiped his hands on his jeans for the fourth time in ten minutes, a habit his mother had tried to break him of and the sea had only encouraged, and finally picked the thing up. It was heavier than it looked. Documents always were, once you understood what they could do to a man.

"Okay," he told it. "Let's see what you're worth."

The latches gave with a sound like knuckles cracking. Inside, packed in oiled canvas gone stiff as bark: three items.

A leather folio, swollen with damp but intact. A river chart, folded into eighths, the creases soft as cloth from handling. And a radio logbook, spiral-bound, its cover stamped with a call sign — *DE-44 BASE* — and a date range that ended six weeks ago.

He started with the chart, because charts were honest in a way people weren't. This one covered the Río Sombra watershed, two hundred miles of river running from the coastal escarpment up into country so remote the cartographers had basically given up and drawn trees. Someone had marked it up in pencil — fuel caches at kilometer intervals, depth soundings in the margins, a red circle around a bend labeled *PUESTO VIEJO* in handwriting that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. Old trading post. Somebody had wanted to get there badly enough to plan for it.

The folio held permits. That was the first strange thing, because permits meant paperwork meant legitimacy, and legitimate expeditions did not end up on the bottom of the Gulf inside wrecks the navy pretended weren't there. Concession 44 covered timber rights, mineral survey, "incidental archaeological assessment" — that last phrase underlined twice by whoever had read it before Hazel. The signature block bore the name of a firm he'd never heard of: *Brandt & Associates, Legal Counsel*. There were port clearances, environmental waivers, an insurance rider covering "hostile acts (limited)," which was the kind of clause you only bought when you expected the hostility to be more than limited.

And then there was the radio log.

Hazel had kept logs himself for twenty years — engine hours, weather, position, the small liturgy of a working boat — and he knew what a healthy log looked like. Routine entries. Boring ones. This log started boring and stayed boring for forty pages, and boring was exactly what made the last page hit like a hull breach.

*Day 41:* Supply request forwarded. River rising ahead of schedule.

*Day 43:* Comms check scheduled 0600. All stations nominal.

*Day 44:* Base to all stations, be advised we are

That was it. Mid-sentence. No sign-off, no timestamp after, no *end of log*. Just ink trailing off like the pen had been knocked from someone's hand, or like whoever was writing had decided mid-word that some things were better left unwritten.

Hazel set the log down and looked out his kitchen window at the Marisol tied up at her mooring, gray morning light on her patched deck, and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from arithmetic. Six weeks of silence. A wreck that wasn't on any chart. Navy boats spending fuel they didn't have to spend. And now this — a paper trail leading two hundred miles up a river that was about to close for the season.

Somebody had lost an expedition. And somebody else wanted it back before anybody official noticed it was gone.

* * *

He did what he always did when a problem wouldn't fit in his head: he took it to Lupe.

Lupe Ochoa ran the bait-and-tackle-and-everything-else shop at the end of the harbor road, a building that had been condemned twice and outlived both condemnations, and she had forgotten more about the coast than the coast remembered about itself. She was seventy-one, five feet nothing, and could gut a fish, win an argument, and shortchange a tourist, all simultaneously, without breaking eye contact.

She read the permits over her reading glasses while Hazel drank the coffee she'd poured him without asking, because asking would have implied he had a choice.

"Delgado," she said. "Emil Delgado?"

"You know him?"

"Everybody knows Emil. Survey man. Good one, too — the kind who actually walks the ground instead of flying over it and guessing." She tapped the permit. "He came through here in the spring. Bought rope, batteries, water purification tablets. Polite fellow. Asked a lot of questions about the upper river."

"What'd you tell him?"

"That the upper river doesn't answer questions." She handed the papers back. "Monsoon starts early this year. You can smell it. Anybody going up past the escarpment has maybe three weeks before the water takes the rapids away, and I mean *takes them away*, Hazel. Not makes them worse. Erases them."

"Six weeks silent," Hazel said. "So they're either dead or they don't want to talk."

"Or both." Lupe refilled his cup unasked again. "Why are you asking? You found something."

"I found a case."

"In a wreck."

"On a wreck."

"Hazel." She said his name the way she said *storm warning* — flat, factual, final. "You've got that look. The one you had when you salvaged the Cortez engine and didn't tell anybody for a month."

"I told *you* about the Cortez engine."

"After you'd already sold it. That's not telling, that's bragging." She leaned on the counter. "Whatever's in that case, there are people who want it, and people who want nobody to have it, and you are currently the only address both of those kinds of people know about. So the question isn't what's in the case. The question is how much your curiosity costs per pound."

Hazel laughed despite himself. Lupe had that effect — she could price anything, including your own bad decisions, and hand you the estimate free of charge.

"It's not curiosity," he said. "It's due diligence."

"Mm. Due diligence smells exactly like curiosity. Same fish, different market."

* * *

The lawyer called the next evening, which told Hazel two things: the man had been watching him, and he hadn't bothered being subtle about it.

"Mr. Delacroix. Marcus Brandt, Brandt & Associates. I understand you've recovered certain materials from a site in the Gulf."

"I recover a lot of materials from a lot of sites."

"And I'm sure you dispose of most of them responsibly." Brandt's voice was smooth as varnish, the kind of voice that had never once been interrupted by doubt. "I represent the interests behind Concession 44. There was an accident. Equipment was lost. We'd very much like our property returned."

"Property's easy," Hazel said. "Delivery's the problem. Your property seems to be two hundred miles upriver."

A pause, brief and controlled, like a man adjusting his cufflinks. "Then perhaps we should discuss delivery."

They met the following morning at a marina restaurant that existed mostly for people who wanted to be seen eating near boats rather than actually on them. Brandt was younger than his voice — forty, maybe, silver at the temples arranged rather than earned — and he wore a linen jacket in a room full of men in work shirts, which was either confidence or a message, and Hazel suspected both. He ordered coffee he also didn't drink. Lawyers and divers, Hazel thought. Same relationship with caffeine. Something to hold while the real transaction happens underneath.

"The Delgado expedition is overdue," Brandt said. "Communications failure. Weather, most likely. Their families are anxious. Our insurers are anxious. I'll be direct with you, Mr. Delacroix, because I'm told you appreciate directness."

"Who told you that?"

"A mutual acquaintance." Which meant Lupe, or someone leaning on Lupe, and Hazel filed that away with a small cold tick of anger he'd deal with later. "We need supplies run to the expedition's last known position before the monsoon closes the river. Fuel, food, medical, communications equipment. Three weeks' window, maybe less. Your boat is capable. Your reputation says you're capable of more than your boat."

"Flattery's extra."

"Triple your standard rate. Plus a completion bonus that would let you re-hull the Marisol properly instead of patching her like a quilt." Brandt slid a card across the table. "Half up front. Half on confirmation of delivery."

Hazel turned the card over. Blank. Even the card was confident.

Here was the thing, and he was honest enough with himself to admit it: the money wasn't hypothetical. The Marisol was thirty-one years old and leaking at a rate he measured in buckets per day. His account balance had two settings, thin and thinner. Triple rate plus bonus wasn't greed; it was the difference between another winter of patching and an actual yard, an actual haul-out, an actual future. Every man on that waterfront would take this contract. Half of them would say yes faster.

But instinct was sitting at the table too, quiet, the way it always was, and instinct kept looking at the radio log in his memory. *Base to all stations, be advised we are.* Nobody writes a sentence like that unless the rest of it scared them.

"Supplies," Hazel said. "Just supplies."

"Just supplies."

"And if I find the expedition needs more than supplies? If I find people who need pulling out?"

Brandt smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced down to the millimeter. "Then you'll be well compensated for the rescue as well. We're not monsters, Mr. Delacroix. We're a business. Businesses prefer their problems solved quietly, and rescues are quiet. Scandals are loud."

There it was. Not hidden, even — just dressed nicely. *Quietly.* The word sat in the air between them like a hook waiting for a mouth.

Hazel looked at the card. Thought about the Marisol's hull, about Lupe's face when she'd said *the upper river doesn't answer questions*, about six weeks of silence and a sentence that stopped mid-word. Thought about the navy boats burning diesel to watch a barge that wasn't doing anything.

Every alarm he owned was ringing, and every one of them was muffled by the sound of triple rate.

"Half up front," Hazel said. "Cash or wire, my choice. And I keep whatever else I find out there. Salvage rights are mine. Anything beyond your listed supplies belongs to me."

Something flickered across Brandt's face — fast, gone before it finished arriving — and Hazel couldn't tell if it was annoyance or satisfaction, and that bothered him more than the flicker itself.

"Agreed," Brandt said. "You'll have the manifest and advance by Friday. Mr. Delacroix — the window is narrow. Don't be late to it."

"The river's never late," Hazel said, standing. "It just stops waiting."

He walked out into the bright morning feeling the way he felt at thirty meters down with the light failing: committed, alone, and fully aware that the easiest part of the dive was the part where you still had the choice to surface.

Behind him, at the marina window, Brandt picked up his untouched coffee and finally drank it, watching the diver go, and made a small note in a leather book — the same leather, though neither man knew it yet, as the folio sitting in Hazel's kitchen two hundred miles and one whole world away.

Friday, then. Provisioning, parts, fuel caches.

Three weeks before the river closed.

Hazel stood on the dock and looked upriver, toward the escarpment he couldn't see and the current he could already feel, and told himself the number that mattered was the money.

The river, patient as ever, waited to prove him wrong.
