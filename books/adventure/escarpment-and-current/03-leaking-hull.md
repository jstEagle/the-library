## 3. Leaking Hull

The Marisol had a way of telling you things before you were ready to hear them.

Hazel Delacroix had learned to read her the way other men read weather — the shudder in the idling engine that meant the fuel line was thinking about quitting, the particular groan she made in a following sea when her planking remembered she was thirty-one years old and built in a yard that had gone bankrupt twice. She was forty feet of Gulf-built stubbornness with a wheelhouse barely big enough for two people who liked each other, which was fortunate, because Hazel mostly traveled alone.

He stood on the dock at Cordell's Boatyard that morning with a coffee thermos in one hand and a list in the other, watching a yard hand named Denny walk the length of her hull with a mallet, sounding her planks like a doctor tapping ribs.

Denny tapped. The Marisol answered. Most of her answered with the dull, honest thud of sound wood. Then, down near the turn of the bilge, just aft of where the keel swept up toward the stern, Denny's mallet went quiet, and his face did something Hazel didn't like.

"Mr. Delacroix," Denny said, in the careful voice men use when they're about to ruin your week, "you want to come look at this."

Hazel looked. What he saw was a seam that had opened like a bad argument — not wide, not yet, maybe an eighth of an inch of daylight between planks where there should have been nothing but caulking and oakum, running four feet along a butt joint that some long-dead shipwright had cut lazy. Around it, the wood had gone soft in the way wood goes soft when it's been quietly drinking seawater for longer than anybody noticed.

"That's not new," Hazel said.

"No, sir." Denny crouched, ran a thumb along it. "That's been working for a while. She's been pumping it out and you've been calling it normal seepage."

"I call everything under three inches an hour normal seepage."

"Well." Denny stood up and dusted his knees, which was a stalling tactic and both of them knew it. "You're going to want to pull her, re-caulk that whole run, and I'd sister a frame or two while we're in there. It's a five-day job if the lumber yard has white oak, which—" he winced— "they don't. They've got pine. Pine'll do for a season, maybe."

"How long to get oak?"

"Ten days. Maybe twelve, with the holiday."

Hazel did the arithmetic without wanting to. Twelve days put him past the window. Brandt's lawyer money had come with a date attached like a fish comes with a hook: supplies loaded and upriver before the monsoon closed the passes, no exceptions, no extensions, triple pay contingent on arrival. The river season wasn't closing so much as slamming, and every day the Marisol sat on the hard was a day the monsoon ate out of his margin.

"Five days," Hazel said. "Pine. Do it right, do it fast."

Denny looked at him the way yard men look at boat owners who are about to learn something expensive. "I can give you five days. I can't give you a guarantee. You take her upriver into monsoon water on fresh pine butts, you're gambling."

"I gamble professionally," Hazel said. "It's called salvage."

* * *

Provisioning the Marisol was its own small war, and Hazel fought it the way he fought most wars — with lists, haggling, and a low-grade suspicion that everyone was overcharging him, which they were.

By noon he'd filled the deck boxes: rice and beans in fifty-pound sacks stacked like sandbags, diesel filters, a spare impeller, two cases of the cheap canned coffee that tasted like regret but kept a man moving, medical kit restocked, rope, chain, a new anchor light because the old one had drowned in a squall back in June and he'd been navigating by faith since. He bought fuel by the drum from a supplier who charged him a "cash discount" that was somehow higher than the credit price, and paid it without argument, because argument cost time and time was the one commodity the river wasn't selling.

Word travels fast on a working waterfront, faster than weather. By the time he'd finished loading, three separate people had asked him, casually, in the tone people use when they are not being casual at all, where exactly he was headed with all those supplies. He told them all the same thing: charter work, private client, upriver. Which was true, in the sense that a locked case stamped CONCESSION 44 was technically a private client.

It was the fourth person who told him something he hadn't asked about.

She was buying shrimp off the dock — Odile Fontenot, who ran the bait stand and knew more about what moved on that water than the harbormaster, the coast guard, and God combined. She counted change into his hand and then held onto his wrist a half-second longer than commerce required.

"You're not the only one asking after that number," she said.

"What number?"

"Hazel." She gave him the look. The one that said she'd known him since he was eleven years old and stealing oysters. "Forty-four. Somebody came through here ten days ago asking about boats that could make the upper river. Big fella, city shoes, rented truck, talked like a contract. Asked me who ran charters, who could be bought, who couldn't."

"And what'd you tell him?"

"Told him everybody can be bought, question is price." She shrugged. "Then he asked specifically about you."

Hazel felt the morning shift under his feet, the way a deck shifts when a swell comes through that you didn't see. "What'd you tell him about me?"

"That your boat leaks and your prices don't." She smiled, and it wasn't entirely a kind smile. "And that you ask too many questions for a man in your line of work, which is why you're still poor."

"Thanks, Odile."

"Don't mention it. Seriously. Don't." She handed him his shrimp and went back to her scale, and Hazel stood there holding a bag of ice and the growing understanding that whatever Concession 44 was, he was not the first person to go looking, and the first person to go looking had wanted to know whether *he* could be bought.

He put the shrimp in the cooler and tried not to think about it, which worked for almost four minutes.

* * *

The parts problem solved itself the way problems do on that coast — sideways, expensively, and through a man Hazel would rather not owe anything to.

Rudy Achille ran a dive barge out of the east channel and was, depending on the year and the job, either Hazel's closest competitor or his closest friend, and had never once let Hazel forget which was more convenient for Rudy at the moment. He was broad as a door, missing two fingers to a propeller he still swore at by name, and he had a yard full of salvaged marine lumber that he bought cheap from wrecks and sold dear to men in a hurry.

Which was, Hazel admitted, exactly what he was.

Rudy met him at the barge rail with coffee that smelled like it had been brewed during a previous administration. "Delacroix. Heard you need oak."

"Heard wrong. I need white oak, quarter-sawn, and I need it yesterday."

"Everybody needs it yesterday. That's why yesterday costs extra." Rudy led him back along the barge to a stack of planks under a tarp, pulled it off, and there they were — good boards, dense and pale, pulled off a shrimp trawler that had gone down on the Sixteen-Mile Reef in '09 and spent a decade in cold, clean water doing exactly what cold clean water does, which is preserve wood and murder engines.

"Those'll hold," Hazel said, running a hand along one. He knew wood underwater the way he knew women and engines, which is to say well enough to know when he was being lied to. These weren't lying.

"They'll hold better than that. Trawler sat in sixty feet of nothing but sand and snapper. No worms, no rot." Rudy crossed his arms. "Now. Price."

They haggled. It was almost pleasant, the way a knife fight is almost dancing. Rudy opened at a number designed to insult them both, Hazel countered with a number designed to end the friendship, and they settled somewhere in the honest middle with Rudy throwing in a bucket of oakum and a tin of pitch to seal the deal, plus one condition:

"You dive the Meridian wreck next month, you give me first refusal on the prop."

"Fine."

"Good." Rudy kicked the tarp back over the remaining lumber, then paused, and here his face did something complicated. "One thing, though. Since we're friends today."

"Since we're friends today."

"Somebody else came sniffing around these boards. Week, week and a half ago." Rudy picked up his coffee, drank, made a face at it. "Wanted enough lumber to refit a boat for heavy river work. Wanted to know whose boats could make the run above the falls. Paid cash for information, which around here means he got some."

Hazel kept his hands busy checking a plank edge so his face wouldn't have to do anything. "City fellow? Rented truck?"

"Boat, actually. Chartered up from the city marina." Rudy shrugged. "Had a lawyer with him. Both of them real interested in a concession number. Forty-something. Forty-four, maybe."

The dock talk and the lumber talk clicked together like two halves of a hull coming off the ways. Ten days ago. Before Brandt. Before the offer, before the contract, before Hazel had ever heard the words *Concession 44* spoken aloud in a lawyer's office that smelled like leather and lies. Someone had been ahead of him the entire time — asking about boats, about lumber, about men who could be bought — and now that same someone knew Hazel Delacroix had taken the job.

"You know who he was?" Hazel asked.

"Didn't give a name. But the lawyer — I know the firm." Rudy set down his cup. "Same outfit that's been filing paper on the upper river concessions for two years. Brandt's people."

Hazel stood very still on a barge full of dead ships, and let that settle. So it wasn't a competitor. It wasn't a rival salvage man, wasn't a treasure hunter, wasn't any of the ordinary sharks he knew how to swim with. It was Brandt's own firm, running its own inquiry, ten days before Brandt hired him — which meant one of two things, and neither of them was comfortable. Either Brandt had sent a second man upriver behind him, a backup, an insurance policy against the possibility that Hazel Delacroix might develop a conscience somewhere between the dock and the falls.

Or somebody inside that firm was cleaning up loose ends, and Hazel was currently the loosest end on the coast.

"Rudy," Hazel said, "you tell that lawyer anything about me?"

"Told him what I tell everybody." Rudy grinned, gap-toothed and unrepentant. "That you're the best diver on this water and the worst businessman, and if he wants a job done honest he should hire you, and if he wants it done quiet he should hire somebody cheaper."

"Christ, Rudy."

"What? It's true, and it confused the hell out of him." Rudy clapped him on the shoulder with the big hand, the one with all its fingers. "Take your lumber, Delacroix. And hey — whatever's up that river?" His grin faded into something older and more careful. "My granddaddy ran timber up there. Said the river keeps what it takes. Every man who went up with a plan came back with less than he started with, if he came back."

"Comforting."

"Free of charge." Rudy waved him off. "Everything else, you're paying through the nose."

* * *

The Marisol came off the railway on the fourth day, ahead of schedule, smelling of pine pitch and fresh caulking, and Hazel walked her hull with Denny's mallet and heard nothing but honest thud from stem to stern. The new planks sat proud and pale against her weathered flanks like bandages on an old soldier, and he found he didn't mind. A boat showed her repairs. That was honesty. It was everything else in this business that had started lying to him.

He loaded the last of the stores that evening — the drums lashed amidships, the deck boxes dogged shut, Ines-of-the-future's seat unbuilt and unimagined, though the wheelhouse felt crowded already with everything he didn't know. He stood at the rail with a beer he didn't drink and watched the tide turn, and made his list of what he actually knew, which was short:

A sealed case. A concession number. A radio log that stopped mid-sentence six weeks ago. Triple pay from a lawyer whose firm had been asking about him before they ever hired him. Fresh pine — no, fresh oak — on a thirty-one-year-old hull, pointed at a river that was about to close like a fist.

And somewhere upstream, whoever had stopped transmitting mid-word was either waiting for supplies or past waiting for anything.

Odile came down the dock at dusk with a covered dish she claimed was leftover gumbo and they both knew was a send-off meal, because that's what that dock did for its own. She set it in his wheelhouse, looked around at the loaded deck, and shook her head slowly.

"You could say no," she said. "You know that. Nobody'd think less of you. Well. I would, a little, but I'd get over it."

"The pay's triple."

"The pay's always triple right before it's nothing." She patted the doorframe twice, the way you'd pat a horse. "Come back, Hazel. Boats can be replaced. You're harder to salvage than you think."

He watched her go up the dock with the tide running gold behind her, and then he went below, spread the charts across the table, and weighted the corners with coffee cans. The river ran up the page like a vein, narrowing, branching, ending in blue blankness where the surveyors' pens had given up. Somewhere in that blankness was a camp that had gone silent, and somewhere between here and there was a second boat — Brandt's insurance, Brandt's eraser, or something worse — that had a ten-day head start.

Hazel traced the channel with one finger, all the way up to where the ink quit.

"Okay," he told the empty wheelhouse, the loaded deck, the patched hull, the whole listening dark. "Let's go find out who's lying."

The Marisol ticked and settled at her lines, the way old boats do when they've heard worse plans and survived them anyway.

At dawn, he'd cast off.
