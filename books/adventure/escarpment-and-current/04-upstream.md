## 4. Upstream

The Marisol took the river the way an old dog takes a vet's waiting room — with a shudder of protest and then a grudging settling into it.

Hazel had her loaded by first light: diesel drums lashed amidships where they'd ride lowest, rice and salt pork in oilcloth, medical kit, spare propeller, chain, tools, and the sealed case from the wreck riding in a false bottom beneath his bunk like a guilty conscience. Brandt's money had covered all of it and then some. Triple pay, plus a bonus for every day early he arrived at the concession head. Hazel had done the arithmetic three times and each time the number came out wrong — too big for supplies, too big for a man who claimed to be worried about his people. Money that big wasn't worry. Money that big was insurance.

He'd decided not to think about it. He was getting good at not thinking about things. It was practically a trade skill.

The river at dawn was brown silk, wide as a lake in places, folding around sandbars that wouldn't exist next month once the monsoon swelled everything. He ran the channel by memory and by the color of the water — green over deep, tea-brown over bar, white water meaning rock — and made the first fuel cache by nine, a lean-to on a bluff with four drums under corrugated tin and a note nailed to the post in Brandt's lawyerly hand: *PUMP PROVIDED. DO NOT DELAY.*

"Do not delay," Hazel read aloud to the river. "Charming fellow."

The pump worked, which surprised him pleasantly. The second cache, forty miles up, did not work — one drum full of water, one full of something that smelled like turpentine's evil cousin, and two missing entirely along with their padlock. Somebody had been through ahead of him. He stood there a while, listening to the jungle do its endless kitchen-noise business, and topped off what he could and marked the spot on his chart with a small black X. He was collecting those. Little black X's of things gone wrong, marching upriver ahead of him like footprints.

* * *

The first checkpoint found him at midafternoon, where the river pinched between a collapsed bluff and a stand of bamboo so thick it looked solid enough to walk on.

Two pirogues, six men, one very old machine gun mounted on a swivel that had probably been old when Hazel's father was young. Old guns killed you just as dead; they just made you wait longer about it. A heavyset man in a fishing vest with no fishing equipment aboard stood up in the lead boat and cupped his hands.

"Concession traffic! Papers!"

Hazel throttled down and let the Marisol drift the last hundred yards, showing them empty hands and a bored face. "Salvage contract," he called back. "Delgado expedition resupply."

"Fifty dollars."

"For what?"

"River tax." The man smiled with tremendous warmth. "Roads are bad this time of year."

"The road is a river."

"Very bad," the man agreed.

Hazel paid him fifty dollars out of Brandt's cash and watched the bills disappear into the vest with the speed of long practice. The second checkpoint, twelve miles on, cost him eighty. The third wanted fuel — two jerry cans' worth — and Hazel gave them one and lied fluently about how little he carried, and the men looked at the low waterline of his loaded barge and let him keep most of his lie because it was easier than arguing. By dusk he'd paid more in bribes than his father had earned in a season of shrimping, and he sat in the wheelhouse eating cold rice and doing the new arithmetic, which was also coming out wrong.

"Everybody wants a piece of this river," he told the wheelhouse. The wheelhouse agreed.

He anchored in a cut behind a willow island, checked the bilge twice out of habit, and slept with the case under his mattress like a teenager hiding letters from a girl.

* * *

She was standing on his foredeck at first light.

Hazel came up the ladder from the engine room with a wrench in one hand and coffee in the other and stopped so fast the coffee nearly left the mug. A woman stood at the bow rail looking upriver — thin as a fence picket, sunburned to the color of brick dust, wearing a man's shirt, cutoff trousers, and boots that had been resoled with tire rubber and wire. She had a canvas satchel across her chest and she held herself the way people hold themselves when they've been walking for a very long time and have recently stopped.

"Good morning," she said, without turning around.

"How," said Hazel, "did you get on my boat."

"I waded. Your anchor line runs into shallow water on the downstream side. You should move it; that cut silts up overnight." She turned then. Her eyes were bloodshot and steady, and there was mud dried in the creases of her neck like the lines on a map. "You're Delacroix. The salvage man. Brandt's boat."

"And you're a stowaway, which is a thing I'm obliged to throw overboard."

"Ines Okonkwo." She didn't offer a hand. Her hands were busy holding the rail, and he noticed the knuckles were split and wrapped with strips of cloth. "I'm the surveyor. From the expedition."

Hazel set down his coffee slowly. Six weeks of silence, a radio log ending mid-sentence, a navy that wouldn't name its wreck, and now here was the silence itself, standing on his deck asking for a ride. "Prove it."

Something flickered across her face — not offense. Approval, maybe. She dug in the satchel and produced a laminated ID card, soft-cornered with sweat, and a brass survey marker stamped with the concession number, the same number that lived under his mattress. Then she recited, flat and quick, the coordinates of the base camp, the depth soundings of the river reach below it, and the name of the expedition's cook, whom she described as "a saint and a thief, mostly a thief."

Hazel grunted. It could all be rehearsed. But nobody rehearsed the cook detail like that.

"Where are the others?"

"Alive," she said. "That's the short answer."

"What's the long answer?"

"The long answer takes fuel, Mr. Delacroix, and I notice your tanks are half empty and your third cache has been robbed." She said it lightly, but her eyes went to the black X he'd penciled on the chart, still lying open on the wheelhouse shelf, and he saw her file that away with the precision of her profession. "I walked eleven days to intercept this route. Brandt's office leaked the schedule to me the way a rotten barrel leaks everything. If you put me ashore, I'll just follow you upriver on foot, and I'll be slower and hungrier and eventually dead, and you'll have that on your conscience, which I understand from your reputation is a functioning organ."

"My reputation."

"You dive wrecks other people won't. You don't ask questions. You finish contracts." She shrugged. "Reputations are just gossip with a work ethic."

Despite himself — despite the wrench still in his hand and the whole gray ledger of things wrong with this job — Hazel felt the corner of his mouth move. "And what are you? Gossip or work ethic?"

"Both," said Ines Okonkwo. "Mostly gossip. Can I come upstream or not?"

* * *

He let her. He told himself it was because turning her away would cost him more time than carrying her — a day lost running her back downriver, questions at checkpoints, explanations he didn't have. That was even true. But underneath the true reason was another one, quieter: she had walked eleven days alone out of a jungle that ate survey crews, toward a stranger's boat, because the others were alive. People didn't do that for payroll. People did that for reasons, and Hazel had spent three weeks surrounded by reasons he couldn't see, and he wanted one he could look in the face.

She earned her passage inside an hour. She knew the upper reaches from her own surveys — knew where the channel shifted after every flood, knew which bluffs shed rock in rain, knew a fisherman's landing above the second checkpoint where they could buy diesel at half the price of Brandt's caches, from a family who hated the concession with a hatred so old and comfortable it was practically furniture. She spoke three languages of the river and haggled in a fourth. She drank her coffee black and unsweetened and criticized his anchoring technique with the tact of a drill sergeant.

"You tie off too short," she said, watching him set the evening hook in a bend below a clay escarpment. "River's going to come up tonight. First storm bands are already north of us."

"It's been dry for a week."

"Dry here. Wet upcountry." She pointed south, where the sky over the escarpment country had gone the color of a bruise healing. "Monsoon doesn't knock. It just arrives and starts rearranging the furniture."

"You always talk like a weather report?"

"Only when the weather matters." She settled against the rail beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the river said plenty — water sliding past the hull, birds winding down their arguments, the far-off complaint of monkeys who had never once in their lives been satisfied.

Then, quietly, without looking at him: "They're alive, Delacroix. Vane keeps them alive because dead experts can't finish a survey. But alive isn't the same as safe, and every day we spend haggling with fishermen is a day closer to whatever Vane does when the survey's done."

"Vane." Hazel turned it over. "Your expedition chief."

"Our expedition chief." Something crossed her face then, brief and dark, a shutter closing over a window. "Yes."

"And when we get there. What exactly am I walking into?"

Ines looked at him for a long moment. He watched her decide something — watch it happen the way you watch a diver on a platform, the pause at the edge before commitment makes cowardice impossible.

"A camp with more armed men than scientists," she said. "A site that's been sealed since June. And a question I need you to answer honestly before we go one more mile." She turned to face him fully, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, under the river noise, meant for him alone. "When you salvaged that wreck — the one the navy pretends isn't there — what did you find?"

The case seemed to warm against his ribs through the deck planking, through the bunk, through the false bottom, like a coal banked in ash.

"Nothing worth mentioning," said Hazel Delacroix, who was getting worse, not better, at not thinking about things.

Ines Okonkwo looked at him for a long, level moment, and he understood that she had weighed his answer, found it false, and filed it — the way she filed everything — in some precise interior drawer whose contents he very much did not want inventoried.

"All right," she said pleasantly. "Then I'll tell you what I found. And by the time I'm finished, Mr. Delacroix, you're going to want to turn this boat around — and I'm going to need you not to."
