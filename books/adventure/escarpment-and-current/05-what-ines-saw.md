## 5. What Ines Saw

The generator coughed twice and died, and in the sudden quiet Ines Okonkwo heard the river breathing under the trading post floor.

She had been telling Hazel this story for three days now, in pieces, the way you feed a fire you're not sure you want to burn. A paragraph at dusk. A sentence over the cook pot. Tonight, anchored in a backwater with the rain drumming the Marisol's cabin roof like an impatient creditor, she finally told it whole.

"You want it from the beginning," she said.

"I want it from wherever it stopped making sense," Hazel said. He was mending a net that didn't need mending, because his hands wanted something to do while his ears worked. She'd learned that about him. The hands were honest even when the man was being careful.

"Then from the beginning. Which is a spreadsheet."

* * *

The Delgado expedition had been, on paper, a timber survey. Concession 44 covered forty kilometers of river frontage above the last rapids, old-growth stands nobody had logged since the colonial company went bust, and Brandt & Associates — she said the name the way you'd spit out a fishbone — had secured permits to assess it. Marcus Vane ran field operations. Ines was the surveyor: boundaries, elevations, flood lines. Six of them total, plus two local guides who knew the river better than any map ever drawn and were paid, she noted bitterly, less than the satellite phone's monthly service fee.

"The first week was almost pleasant," she admitted. "That should have warned me. Fieldwork is never pleasant. When it is, someone is lying to you about what you're actually doing there."

They made camp on the bluff above the ruins of the old company trading post — a stone shell really, half-swallowed by strangler fig, its warehouse floor tilted toward the river like a drunk leaning on a friend. The colonial firm had built it in the 1890s to buy rubber and hardwood and, if the guides' grandmothers' stories were true, a great many other things that never appeared on manifests. The river had changed course since. What had been dry storage was now, in the wet season, underwater.

Ines found the first anomaly on day nine, running her level line along the warehouse floor.

"There's a staircase down there," she told Vane, pointing at the flooded section where the water went green-black and still. "Under the surface. I can see four steps before they disappear."

Vane looked. Vane smiled. And Ines, who had worked with enough men to know the difference between delight and appetite, felt the temperature of the expedition drop five degrees in a single second.

"It's probably a cistern," he said.

"Colonial trading posts didn't build cisterns below the water table," she said. "And they especially didn't build them with dressed stone steps."

"Then it's a cellar full of drowned rum, and we'll toast your discovery when the survey's done." He clapped her shoulder, friendly as anything, and walked away, and she stood there with her instruments in her hands feeling like a woman who'd just watched someone decide something without saying it aloud.

* * *

She did what surveyors do. She measured.

The water over the submerged floor was four meters deep in the dry season, six in the wet. The staircase descended past where her light reached. The building's foundation stones were quarried granite that matched nothing within two hundred kilometers, which meant someone had hauled it upriver at ruinous expense, which meant whatever was down there had been worth ruinous expense a hundred and thirty years ago.

She also did what careful people do around men like Vane: she kept a second notebook. Not a diary — diaries get read. A working log, duplicate entries, boring language, buried between tide tables. If you wrote *found sealed vault beneath trading post, Vane acting strange*, you got yourself killed. If you wrote *anomaly 7: subsurface structure, granite, access flooded, recommend reassessment*, you were just doing your job.

"Paranoid?" Hazel asked.

"Employed," she said. "There's overlap."

For eleven days nothing happened, or rather everything happened so smoothly it felt like held breath. Then on day twenty, the supply launch came upriver with new orders, and Vane read them alone in his tent for a long time, and came out a different man.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. He didn't shout or pace. He simply began issuing instructions that made no sense. The camp perimeter moved fifty meters back from the bluff — away from the trading post, not toward it, so no one's tent overlooked the water. Radio hours were restricted: one scheduled transmission per week, encrypted, handled personally by Vane. The guides were let go with a bonus that was either generosity or severance, depending on how you read it, and both guides read it correctly and left without arguing, which told Ines more than any document could have.

"And then the guns came upriver," she said. "Two crates, marked as survey equipment. I helped unload them, because I was still, God help me, a team player. Rifles don't weigh like theodolites, Hazel. Everyone in that camp knew what we were carrying. Nobody said a word. We just all became the kind of expedition that has rifles, overnight, by mutual unspoken agreement, the way a family becomes the kind of family that doesn't talk about Uncle Raymond."

Hazel snorted despite himself. "Every family's got an Uncle Raymond."

"Uncle Raymond never armed a paramilitary detail on a timber concession," Ines said. "As far as I know."

* * *

She went into the water on day thirty-one.

Not heroism. Accounting. Her permit deliverables included a structural assessment of all pre-existing construction on the parcel — the trading post qualified — and she had decided, with the particular stubbornness of a woman who has been told twice that something is a cistern, that she would produce the most thorough structural assessment in the history of colonial rubble. If Vane wanted to stop her, he'd have to say out loud why a flooded cellar mattered, and she was betting he wouldn't.

She dove it at dawn, off the books, using her own mask and a borrowed tank she signed out as *silt sampling*. The water was cold below two meters, cold the way deep places are cold, holding winter in their pockets. Visibility was a meter, then half a meter, then her own hands and faith.

The staircase took her down nine steps to a landing. Her light found dressed stone, iron rings set for mooring barges — this had been a waterside entrance once, before the river turned — and a door.

Not a door like a house door. A door like a bank door. Iron-strapped oak, banded, with a lock the size of her head, and above it, chiseled into the lintel, letters filled with a century of silt. She scraped one clean with her thumb.

It was a company crest. Under it, a word in Portuguese, and she knew enough colonial-era commercial Portuguese to translate it standing in freezing black water with her heart going like a bilge pump:

*Arquivo.*

Archive.

"I want to be clear about what I felt," she told Hazel. "Because people assume I felt excitement. Discovery. The romance of it. No. What I felt, floating in the dark in front of a locked vault at the bottom of a drowned warehouse, was the exact feeling of realizing you've stepped on something that can see you. Every instinct I own said: go up, sign out nothing else, file your report, take your pay, forget this. And I ignored every one of them, because I was thirty-four years old and sure that being right would protect me." She shook her head. "Being right doesn't protect anyone. It just makes you a witness. And witnesses are a supply item, same as fuel. You store them or you spend them."

Through the door's rotted lower edge she could see shelving. Rows of it. And on the shelves, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed against the damp by some long-dead clerk who had done his job magnificently, boxes. Hundreds of them. She got her camera — the waterproof survey camera, the one she'd brought against regulations — and shot what she could through the gap: shelf rows, seal stamps, one box close enough to read its label.

The label had a date range. 1888 to 1911. And a category heading that made her blood do something complicated:

*Land Transfers — Forced. Conveyances & Compensation (Nominal).*

"Forced land transfers," she said. "Nominal compensation. Twenty-three years of them, catalogued. That vault isn't a cellar, Hazel. It's the receipts. Every fraudulent purchase, every village moved at gunpoint, every 'treaty' signed by a chief who couldn't read the language it was written in — the company kept records. Companies always keep records. They can't help themselves. Somewhere in that vault is paper proving this entire concession sits on land that was stolen so thoroughly the thieves wrote it down and filed it."

"And Brandt knows."

"Brandt paid for the expedition," Ines said. "You don't fund a timber survey with military logistics unless the trees are a cover story. Vane's new orders came from somewhere, and the timing — the day after I reported the submerged structure — is not a coincidence I'm willing to believe in."

* * *

She surfaced, dried off, and made her mistake: she told Tomas Reyes.

Tomas was the engineer, a soft-spoken man with reading glasses he cleaned obsessively and a habit of fixing things nobody had asked him to fix — the camp's water filter, the cook's radio antenna, once, memorably, the interpersonal dynamics of the survey team, via a batch of rum he distilled in a pressure vessel. He was the only one of them Ines trusted, which she would later learn was both entirely correct and catastrophically insufficient, because trust is not a wall, it's a door, and doors work in both directions.

She showed him the photographs. He looked at them for a long time, cleaning his glasses twice, which for Tomas was the equivalent of screaming.

"Ines," he said finally. "I need you to listen very carefully and not interrupt, because I've been counting things you haven't been counting." He laid it out: the resupply launch had come upriver twice more than the manifest required. The rifles. The fact that Vane had photographed *her* equipment locker while she was sleeping — she hadn't known that; her stomach dropped hearing it. The fact that the weekly radio transmissions had started including a phrase she couldn't hear from her tent but Tomas, sleeping nearer, could: *site secure, subject located.*

"Subject," Ines repeated.

"Vaults aren't subjects," Tomas said. "People are subjects." He put his glasses on and looked at her with terrible kindness. "Whatever Vane was sent here to find, he's found it, and the finding is over, and everything that happens next is about who gets told. You dove that vault off the books. So did I, last night, after you told me — don't look at me like that, I fixed the compressor myself, I know exactly how many tanks went missing. There's a second chamber behind the iron door. Smaller. The lock on it is newer than the rest of the door. Someone added it later, Ines. Someone came back for this place, decades ago, and locked part of it again."

"What's in the inner chamber?"

"I don't know. But I know what's on the outer shelves now versus what was on the manifest of the company's final inventory, which I pulled from the coastal registry before we came upriver, because I am an engineer and engineers check load calculations." His voice went very quiet. "There were eleven hundred and forty catalogued boxes in 1912. I counted four hundred and six through the door gap. Somebody has already emptied most of this vault once. Whatever's left is what they couldn't carry — or what they came back for."

The rain picked up outside the Marisol, and Hazel fed another stick into the little stove, and Ines wrapped her hands around her cup and finished it.

* * *

"So I ran," she said. "Day thirty-eight. Not because I'm brave — running isn't brave. Because I'd done the math. Vane had cut the radios, armed the camp, and started referring to personnel as assets in his logs — I'd seen his clipboard, I was supposed to be measuring flood lines, not reading his clipboard, but the man leaves things on tables like a child leaves toys. An asset that has seen the vault and won't stop asking questions is an asset due for disposal. I had maybe a week. Maybe less."

"Tomas stayed."

"Tomas stayed." Her voice did something small and damaged around the name, and she let it, because hiding it would have been worse. "He said someone had to stay who knew where everything was, in case — and he never finished the sentence, and I've spent six weeks finishing it for him. He gave me his backup memory cards, the ones from the compressor-mounted camera he'd rigged to watch the vault entrance. Said if anything happened to him, the pictures needed to reach someone who wasn't for sale. Then he cleaned his glasses, told me the best route out was the east game trail because Vane's patrols were all west, and shook my hand like we were colleagues ending a shift."

She stared at the stove.

"He chose to stay, Hazel. I've told myself a hundred times he didn't have a choice, that I could have dragged him, and none of it's true. He stayed because he thought he could manage Vane. Engineers. They think everything can be managed." She looked up. "I walked out of that jungle carrying photographs of a locked door and a dead company's filing system, and everyone I left behind is either on Vane's payroll or Vane's hostage, and I genuinely cannot tell you which is worse for them. That's what I saw. That's the whole of it. The expedition was never lost. It was captured. It captured itself, the moment those crates came off the launch, and every one of us stood there and watched it happen and called it a change in operational protocol."

The rain hammered. Somewhere downriver, thunder rolled along the escarpment like furniture being moved upstairs.

Hazel had stopped pretending to mend the net. He sat with his forearms on his knees, looking at her with the flat assessing gaze she'd seen him use on water — the look of a man calculating depth, current, and what the bottom was made of.

"Vane will have posted watchers on the river," he said at last. "Between here and the camp."

"Almost certainly."

"And Brandt wants supplies run up before monsoon closes the window. Which means Brandt wants his people provisioned, which means Brandt believes his people are still in control."

"Or wants you to believe it," Ines said. "Lawyers lie with invoices, Captain. It's cleaner."

Hazel nodded slowly, and she watched him arrive at the same arithmetic she'd arrived at six weeks ago, the one where every route forward passed through the drowning ground. He picked the net back up. His hands started moving again.

"Well," he said. "Good news is, I've been hired to sail straight at the thing everybody's killing each other over, and I'm getting paid triple to do it."

"That's not good news."

"No," Hazel agreed. "But it's the news. Get some sleep, Okonkwo. Tomorrow we start upriver, and I want to know everything you remember about that east game trail — because something tells me we're going to end up swimming it."

Ines lay back in the bunk that smelled of diesel and mildew and, for the first time since she'd walked out of the jungle, slept without dreaming of stairs going down into black water.

She dreamed instead of Tomas cleaning his glasses, over and over, and woke at dawn knowing exactly what it meant.
