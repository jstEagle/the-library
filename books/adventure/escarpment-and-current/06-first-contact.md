## 6. First Contact

The river changed its mind about them a dozen times between the fuel cache and the bend where the map went quiet.

It ran shallow and glassy over sandbars the color of old bone, then deepened without warning into green-black channels where the current pulled at the Marisol's hull like something curious and not entirely friendly. Hazel ran her slow, engine at half-throttle, one eye on the depth sounder and the other on Ines Okonkwo, who stood at the bow with the concession map unrolled across her knees and an expression he was beginning to recognize as her version of calm — which was to say, the stillness of a person counting exits.

"Half a klick past this escarpment," she said, not turning around. "The trading post sat on the high bank. The camp will be on the flat below it. You'll hear it before you see it."

"Hear what?"

"Generators." She folded the map with the crisp economy of someone who had folded that same map a hundred times in worse conditions. "Vane brought diesel generators. Six weeks of silence and they're running refrigeration."

Hazel chewed on that. Refrigeration meant supplies meant resupply runs meant somebody, somewhere, was still feeding this expedition money and expecting something back. It also meant the camp wasn't a camp of desperate castaways eating grubs and boiling river water. It was an operation.

"You knew Vane," he said. "Before all this."

"I hired him." Ines came aft and settled onto the cooler across from him, forearms on her knees. "Or my firm did. Marcus Vane came with references like a cathedral — fifteen years leading surveys on three continents, never lost a man, never lost a permit. He used to make us do radio checks every six hours like clockwork. The man treated protocol like religion." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "So when the radios went dark, I told myself it was equipment failure. For about four days I told myself that."

"And then?"

"And then I found the vault, and he stopped being the man who checked the radios." She looked out at the water sliding past, brown and patient. "You're going to ask me what I saw down there. Everyone does. And I'm going to tell you — but not yet, and not on open water. Because once I tell you, you can't unknow it, and I need you sharp for the next hour first."

Hazel let that sit. He'd known the woman five days, four of them spent arguing about whether she should come along and one of them spent proving she could run a boat better than his deckhand ever had. She'd walked out of sixty miles of jungle alone, which either made her the toughest person he'd ever shared a wheelhouse with or the most dangerous, and he genuinely could not rule out both.

What he knew for certain was that she hadn't flinched when he told her the truth about the case — how he'd found it in a wreck charted as empty, how he'd pocketed it instead of declaring it, how a lawyer named Brandt had paid him triple rate without asking a single question a legitimate client would have asked. She'd just nodded, the way you nod at weather.

"Snipers," he said instead. "You mentioned snipers."

"I mentioned the possibility of snipers."

"You said, quote, 'he'll have men on the escarpment with rifles, because Marcus Vane doesn't leave a door unwatched.'"

"I stand by it." She pointed upstream-left, where the terrain rose in a long red shoulder of rock and clay, capped with green. "There. That's your escarpment. The current runs hard against the point below it — you'll want to hug the far bank until the last possible second."

The Marisol thumped through a standing wave and Hazel adjusted the wheel, feeling the old girl answer the way she always did, a half-beat late but honest about it. Twenty-six years she'd been his. He'd rebuilt her engine twice, re-planked her deck once, and sunk her exactly zero times, a record he intended to protect.

Which was why, when the first shot cracked across the water, his whole body understood it before his brain did.

* * *

The bullet hit the water ten feet off the port bow with a sound like a whip taking a bite out of the river, and the spout it threw stood up pale against the green.

"Ines—" was all he got out, because the second shot came lower and closer, and the third one didn't miss.

It punched through the cabin wall above the wheel, a neat round hole with a splintered mouth, and passed out the other side somewhere near the fuel gauge. Hazel dropped below the line of the wheel itself, hauling the throttle back with one hand while keeping his head in the physical space where bullets were currently not.

"Port bank!" Ines shouted, already flat on the deck. "There's a cut in the mangroves two hundred meters — I surveyed it — take it!"

"Take it? Take it how? That's not a channel, that's a suggestion!"

"It's water deep enough for your draft for maybe forty meters, and after that it's mud, and mud is better than bullets!" A fourth shot sang off the water somewhere behind them. "Move!"

Hazel moved. He swung the Marisol hard to port, engine screaming up through the rev range, and aimed her bow at the green gap in the mangroves that Ines swore existed. For one long second there was nothing there but wall — tangled roots, hanging ferns, water the color of steeped tea — and then the wall opened and the river closed behind them like a curtain.

The shooting stopped. Of course it stopped. Whoever was up on that escarpment had a clean field of fire on the main channel and none at all in here, and they knew it, which meant they'd seen exactly where the Marisol went, which meant the clock was now running whether Hazel liked it or not.

He killed the engine and let momentum carry them in. The bottom came up gentle — he felt it as a soft drag rather than a strike, the hull settling into shallows the way a tired animal lies down — and then the Marisol was grounded, listing slightly to starboard, surrounded by mangrove roots and dripping silence.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere close, a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge complaining.

"Well," Hazel said finally. "That's the first time in twenty-six years anybody's shot at her."

"She took it well."

"She took it like a woman who's been through worse." He climbed out over the bow onto the root-mat, testing his weight. Solid enough. The water here came to mid-shin, warm as bathwater and opaque as coffee. "How far to the camp if we walk?"

Ines rose and stood in the bow, shading her eyes, reading the terrain the way he read water. "Through the swamp, maybe two hours if we're careful. We come out on the flats downstream of the camp. There's a drainage cut — old logging road, flooded — that runs right past their perimeter. We can get close enough to see what we're dealing with."

"And then what?"

"And then," she said, climbing down to join him, "we decide what kind of fools we are."

* * *

They left the Marisol with the engine cover off and the ignition wire disconnected, which wouldn't stop anyone determined but would slow down anyone casual, and Hazel wedged his dive knife under the deck plate with the spare cash and the satellite phone's battery, separated on principle. If the boat was going to be searched, let the search find nothing worth the trouble.

The swamp received them the way swamps do — completely. Within fifty meters the river was gone, the sky was gone, everything reduced to a corridor of trunks and standing water and the particular green light that gets under your skin. Ines moved ahead of him with a confidence that bordered on offensive, stepping on roots she seemed to have memorized, and Hazel followed, carrying the pack, sweating through his shirt inside of ten minutes.

"Sixty miles," he said, mostly to hear a human voice. "You walked sixty miles through this."

"Fifty-eight. I took a wrong turn near the confluence and added two." She held a branch aside for him without looking back. "It's not so bad once you stop fighting it. The jungle isn't trying to kill you. It just doesn't care whether you live."

"That's somehow worse."

"Yes. It is." She paused at a fork he couldn't have distinguished from any other fork in a police lineup. "Left here. And keep your voice down from here on. Sound carries over water."

They walked another hour in a silence broken only by their own footsteps and the occasional conversational gurgle of the swamp draining itself somewhere out of sight. Hazel used the time to be afraid properly — not the white-flash fear of the sniper rounds, which had been too fast for anything but reflex, but the slow kind, the kind that sits down next to you and makes conversation. He was a salvage diver. His entire professional life was built on the assumption that danger came with water around it, that if things went bad he could go down, go deep, and wait. Out here there was no down. There was only through.

And yet. Some stubborn compartment in his chest kept noting details the way it always did — the generator noise getting louder, faint and rhythmic, exactly as Ines had promised; the smell of woodsmoke and diesel cutting through the green rot; and then, threading through it all, voices. Men's voices, unhurried, working.

Alive. The expedition was alive. Whatever else waited for them on those flats, the people Brandt had sent him to supply weren't skeletons in the bush. They were somebody's crew, running somebody's operation, and they had shot at the supply boat without asking questions first.

"They knew it was us," Hazel murmured. "Didn't they. Brandt would've radioed ahead."

"Brandt doesn't radio ahead." Ines stopped at the edge of the tree line and crouched, and he crouched beside her. "Brandt pays Vane. Vane trusts nobody, least of all Brandt. Those men on the escarpment shoot at anything that moves on that channel, because that's what they were told to do." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes in the green shade were very steady. "That's the thing you need to understand about Marcus Vane before we go one step further. He is not hiding from the world. He is building a wall around something, and the wall has orders."

Through the last screen of vegetation, the flats opened up.

The camp sat on the high ground below the escarpment's red shoulder, and it was bigger than Hazel had imagined — not a surveyor's camp of tents and tripods but a compound. Tarped structures in neat rows. A crane. Two aluminum workboats at a makeshift dock. The generators humming behind a fuel bladder the size of his cabin. And men — a dozen at least, visible, probably more not — moving with the unhurried purpose of people doing a job they expected to keep doing.

At the center of it all, half-sunk into the ground beside the ruins of the old trading post, stood a structure that took Hazel a moment to parse, because his eyes kept insisting it shouldn't exist: a steel hatch. Industrial, shipyard-grade, set into a concrete collar like the top of a bunker, with a portable derrick positioned over it and cables running down into the dark.

They were loading crates. Not unloading — loading. Men worked the derrick in pairs, lowering sealed cases into the hatch on a sling, and other men carried more crates from a storage tent on their shoulders, and the whole operation had the rhythm of something that had been happening for a long time and intended to continue.

"What are they putting down there?" Hazel asked quietly.

"Everything they've recovered." Ines's voice had gone flat, the way it went flat when she talked about the vault. "And everything they don't want found. Papers, mostly. Documents. Six weeks of work, sealed and lowered into a hole in the ground." She swallowed. "I told you I'd tell you what I saw. Here it is, and then you're committed, so listen. Under the trading post there's a vault — colonial-era, pre-dating the concession by eighty years. An archive. Land records, shipping manifests, correspondence. The concession Brandt's firm holds — Concession 44 — is built on forged permits, Hazel. The archive proves it. Proves the land was never theirs to log, proves people died keeping it quiet, proves the whole paper empire is a lie with a number stamped on it. That's the prize. Not timber. Not minerals. Paper."

Hazel stared at the hatch, at the crates descending into it one by one, and felt the shape of his own situation rearrange itself around him. He'd thought he was running supplies to stranded scientists. He'd suspected, since the wreck, that the job was dirty. But this —

"The navy boats," he said. "Shadowing my barge. The patrol that charted that wreck as empty. Somebody with reach buried that case in the river and somebody with reach wants it staying buried."

"Now you see it." Ines didn't smile, but something in her face eased slightly, the easing of a person who has finally handed over a weight she'd been carrying alone. "Vane found the vault and understood immediately what it was worth and what it could do to the people who pay him. So he sealed it, armed it, and started moving the contents somewhere permanent. Down is permanent. Water is permanent. He's a diver's problem now, whether he knows it or not."

Below them, the derrick groaned and another crate went down into the dark.

Hazel crouched in the mud at the tree line, a grounded boat behind him, a rifleman somewhere on the ridge above, and a fortune in stolen history sinking into a hole in front of him, and he took inventory of himself the way he took inventory before a dive. Air: sufficient. Options: narrowing. Fear: present, accounted for, and — he noticed with some surprise — changing. It was cooling. Hardening. Turning into something with edges.

Because here was the arithmetic, plain as a tide table: the Marisol was aground in a cut with his name on her papers, his face was known to Brandt's lawyer, and the men on that escarpment had already decided he was worth shooting at. There was no version of this where he paddled back downriver and the whole thing politely forgot him. The window was closing — monsoon was coming, the permits were expiring, and the only direction left was forward.

"No turning back," he said aloud, testing the words.

"No," Ines agreed. "But there's a difference between no way back and no way through. Watch the dock. Tell me what you see."

Hazel watched. Two workboats. A crane with a winch that needed grease — he could hear it squeal on every lift. Eight men on the loading detail, rotating out every twenty minutes or so, which meant shifts, which meant a schedule. And at the perimeter, walking a lazy circuit along the flood edge of the old logging road — the very road they'd need to take to get closer — a single guard with a slung rifle and the posture of a man four hours into a boring shift.

"One guard on the road," Hazel murmured. "Rotating loaders. Winch crew changes on the hour, give or take." He glanced at Ines. "You said there were others. Your colleagues. Alive."

"Alive when I left. Tomas Reyes, our engineer — he'll be in that machine shed, he can't stay away from engines. Priya and Okafor on survey. And the rest." Her jaw tightened. "The ones who took Vane's payroll and decided the archive was theirs to sell. They're alive too. That's part of what makes this hard."

Down at the hatch, the derrick swung its sling out over the opening one more time, and Hazel watched the cable pay out, down and down, into water he couldn't see but could feel in his teeth — black, still, deep, holding its secrets the way the river held its drowned.

Then the guard on the logging road stopped walking. Turned his head. Looked directly at the tree line where they crouched.

And raised one hand — slowly, deliberately — and beckoned.
