## 7. The Trading Post

The rain had been threatening all morning, sitting on the hills like a debt collector who hadn't yet decided to knock. Ines Okonkwo watched it from the treeline above the old trading post and thought, not for the first time, that the concession had been drawn by men who never once got their boots wet.

"You're smiling," Hazel said beside her. He was flat against the ferns the way a man lies when he's learned that lying flat is cheaper than standing up. "I want you to know I noticed that. We're crouched in leech country looking at a building full of armed men, and you're smiling."

"I'm remembering the survey," she said. "Day one. Vane stood right there — see the porch? — and gave a speech about opportunity. He said this post was built in 1911 and abandoned in 1934, and that we'd be the first people to take its measure since." She paused. "He was wrong about the last part. But he was very good at sounding like he wasn't."

"Was he wrong about much else?"

Ines let out a breath through her nose. "You'll see."

* * *

Getting down to the post was the easy part, if you didn't count the slope, which Ines did not, because counting it would have meant admitting her knees had opinions. They came down through a stand of bamboo that rattled like loose change in the wind, and Hazel moved the way she'd begun to expect from him: no wasted motion, no noise, an economy of a man who had spent his life being paid for what he carried back up instead of what he left behind.

The trading post itself sat on a shelf of stone above the river, a long low building of coral-stone and ironwood with a roof that had mostly given up. Vane's camp sprawled below it on the floodplain — canvas, generators, the crates. Always the crates. Men were loading them onto a barge even now, working with the unhurried efficiency of people who believed they had all the time in the world.

"They're moving it out," Hazel murmured.

"Not all of it." Ines pointed past the main structure, to where the hillside folded behind the post. "There's a spring house. Colonial build. Everyone assumes it's a well. It isn't."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I'm a surveyor, and because Marcus stopped letting me near the site three weeks ago, which is generally how you learn where everything important is." She allowed herself the smile again. "Also there's a drainage arch visible at low water that shouldn't exist. Wells don't have drainage arches. Vaults do."

Hazel looked at her for a long moment. Water dripped off the bamboo above them, patient as a metronome. "You've been planning this exit since before I arrived."

"I've been planning this exit since the day I understood what they found." She started downslope. "Come on. We want the spring house before the rain does, or we swim."

* * *

The spring house door was ironwood gone silver with age, and it opened on hinges that had been oiled recently enough to make Ines's stomach tighten — someone from the expedition had come this way, then. Inside was a round chamber, brick-floored, smelling of cold stone and river. A shaft in the center dropped into black water. And along the left wall, half-submerged already, a tunnel mouth gaped where the hillside drank the spring overflow.

"The water's up two feet since Tuesday," Ines said, crouching to test it. Cold climbed her fingers. "Monsoon's early. Or the river's just decided it owns this place again, which, historically, it does."

"How far?"

"Forty meters, maybe fifty. It rises toward the vault gallery. There are air pockets — the old builders vented it through the hill. You'll be swimming in places. You said you could dive."

"I said I dive for a living. Nobody pays me to do it in the dark holding my breath."

"Well." Ines shrugged off her pack and began sealing the documents inside it in the oilcloth she'd carried for six weeks, wrapped tight as a secret. "Consider this the interview."

That earned her a short laugh, surprised out of him, and she found she liked the sound of it more than she wanted to examine. There was something restful about Hazel Delacroix, in the way that a well-built boat is restful — you trusted the hull without needing to admire it.

They went in.

The tunnel took them immediately, the way tunnels do — swallowing light, trading the jungle's green roar for dripping echo. Ines went first because she knew the line: left hand on the wall, twelve paces to the first bend, up over the lip where the ceiling dipped, then the long flooded stretch where the water went from knee to chest to chin. Behind her she heard Hazel breathing in the controlled rhythm of a man conserving himself, and once, when her foot slipped on submerged brick, a hand caught her elbow, sure and unremarked, and let go again.

At the halfway chamber they surfaced into air. It was a small pocket, barely big enough for two heads, and in the dark Ines heard Hazel spit water and say, conversationally, "I've had better Tuesdays."

"It's Wednesday."

"That explains it."

She laughed — actually laughed, a real one, and it echoed strangely off the stone, and she clapped a hand over her mouth too late. The sound died away into dripping. Nothing answered it. Still, her heart hammered for a while after, and not only from the dark.

"My father used to say," she offered quietly, when her pulse had settled, "that the river keeps every secret but charges interest."

"He sounds like a wise man."

"He sounds like a man who lost a farm to a concession company, actually. Same thing." She felt rather than saw Hazel go still beside her. "Different river. Same company, though. That's part of why I'm here."

"Ines—"

"Later." She pushed off from the wall. "It's dry ahead. And what's dry ahead is going to change your mind about several things, so save your questions."

* * *

The gallery opened like the throat of some enormous swallowed thing — a vaulted corridor of dressed stone, colonial work, the kind of masonry that gets built by people who intend to be remembered and resented in equal measure. Their lights found shelves cut into the walls. Crates stenciled with faded crown marks. And everywhere, the soft grey bloom of paper waiting in the dark for sixty years, ninety years, for someone to come and read it.

"This is what Vane found," Ines said. Her voice came out hushed, and she hated that it did, but the room demanded it. "Under the trading post floor. The whole colonial land registry for the upper river. Deeds. Concessions. Correspondence. Court records from the rubber years." She ran her light along a shelf. "Every promise this government ever made to the families along this water, and every promise it broke, filed and stamped and sealed in wax."

Hazel had gone very quiet. He drifted along the shelves like a man in church who didn't know the liturgy but respected the architecture. Then he stopped, and his light settled on a crate lid, and stayed there.

"What am I looking at?"

"Read the stamp."

He read it aloud, slowly, the way people read things they don't believe yet. "'Concession Forty-Four. Renewal granted. Brandt, Vasquez and Hale, agents for the grantee.'" He looked up. "Brandt. My Brandt. The lawyer paying me triple to run supplies to this exact spot."

"Your Brandt," Ines agreed. "Who is also, according to these papers, the same firm that administered the original concession in 1921. Which lapsed. Which was never lawfully renewed, because the renewal required a public hearing, and the public hearing never happened, because—" she pulled a folder from the shelf, careful as lifting a sleeping child, "—because the hearing record says it did. Here. Dated, signed, witnessed. Except the witness signature belongs to a district officer who died eleven months earlier. I checked. Twice. From the satellite phone before Marcus took it away."

Hazel stared at the page. In the lamplight his face did something complicated — the look of a man watching a wreck surface that he'd been diving over for years without knowing it was under him.

"They forged it," he said.

"They forge everything. That's the firm's entire product. Not timber, not minerals — paper. Concession Forty-Four is worth nothing on the ground. The trees are second-growth scrub; the mineral surveys came back empty; I ran them myself." She tapped the folder. "But the archive proves the chain of title is rotten from the root. Every lease, every sale, every debt the river families owe downstream — it all hangs from this one forged link. If this comes out, the whole structure falls. Land claims, compensation, criminal referrals. Generations of it."

"So they buried it."

"They drowned it, technically. Sealed the vault and let the spring do the work. Very tidy. Very them." Ines set the folder down and pulled out another, and this one her hands were less steady on, and she let him see that. "This is the part I need you to understand before we go any further, Hazel. Because you're paid by these people, and I was paid by these people, and there's a difference between us only in the timing."

She opened it. Photographs, brittle and foxed. A list of names in a clerk's copperplate hand. Dates from the rubber years, when the original concession's overseers had worked the river with guns and quotas, and the deaths had been numerous enough to require documentation — not from conscience, but from accounting.

"Twenty-three names," she said. "Killed in the 'clearing operations' of 1922. The firm's own ledger recorded them, because the firm billed the colony for the ammunition." She watched Hazel's jaw tighten. "And here—" she turned to the last pages, newer paper, typed, "—is the modern version. 2019. A community paralegal named Emile Sandoval filed a land claim on behalf of fourteen river families. His claim cited historical records he couldn't possibly have seen, because the vault was still sealed. He'd found fragments. Enough to be dangerous."

Hazel read the date at the bottom of the next page. "Drowned," he said. "Boating accident."

"There was no boat recovered. No body ever examined independently. The file was closed by a magistrate whose retirement consultancy, I discovered, is a firm that shares a post office box with Brandt, Vasquez and Hale." Ines closed the folder. "I helped bury that file, Hazel. Three years ago. I was a junior surveyor doing contract GIS work, and someone handed me a boundary map and asked me to confirm the shoreline, and I confirmed it, and my confirmation went into the packet that closed the case. I didn't know what I was signing. That's true, and it doesn't matter, because the price of working for them is that you never have to know. Ignorance is included in the fee."

The gallery dripped. Somewhere above them, faint through forty meters of stone and water, a generator changed pitch.

"When I found out," she went on, "I told myself I'd finish the contract and leave and never think about it. Then the expedition came up, and the pay was good, and I told myself the same thing again. That's how it works. You keep telling yourself the same sentence until the sentence wears smooth and you can't feel it anymore." She looked at him straight. "So when Vane sealed this place and cut the radios, I decided I was done paying. Whatever it costs. I already bought my complicity once. I'm not buying it twice."

Hazel was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rougher than usual. "The case I pulled off that wreck. The navy one. It had Concession 44 stamped on it. Permits, a map, a radio log that ends mid-sentence." He shook his head slowly. "I thought I'd stolen salvage. Turns out I stole evidence."

"You stole the thread. This is the spool." She rested her hand on the shelf of grey paper. "Everything Vane's loading onto that barge is a fraction of what's here. He can't move it all before monsoon — nobody could. So he's taking the core cache, the most damning boxes, and the plan is that the rest stays drowned forever behind a new seal and a new permit."

"And Brandt sent me upriver to feed the men doing it."

"Brandt sent you upriver because you're the best diver on the coast and because you ask exactly as many questions as your pay grade requires." She almost smiled. "Don't take it personally. It's a compliment, in their language."

Hazel laughed, but it was a thin sound, and he crouched suddenly, forearms on knees, staring at the black water of the tunnel they'd come through. "I've got a boat with a patched hull, a contract I now understand is conspiracy, and a client who'd feed me to the river if it balanced a ledger." He looked up at her. "You know what the worst part is?"

"Tell me."

"For about ten seconds back there, reading that stamp — I was doing the math. What a share of whatever Vane's paying would fix. The Marisol. My debts. My mother's roof." He rubbed his face. "Ten seconds. Then I saw the ammunition bill."

"Everyone does the math," Ines said gently. "The trick isn't refusing to do it. The trick is checking your answer."

He nodded, slow, and stood, and she watched something settle in him — not resolve exactly, resolve was loud; this was quieter, load-bearing, like ballast shifting into place.

"All right," he said. "Show me the core cache. If we're going to steal a hundred years of somebody's sins, I want to know how much it weighs."

She was reaching for the inventory sheet she'd memorized — aisle four, the wax-sealed boxes, the ones Vane's men hadn't reached yet — when she heard it.

Water.

Not the drip of the gallery. Not the spring's patient seep. A rising, rushing sound, deep in the tunnel behind them, and the black pool at the tunnel mouth shivered, and then began, visibly, to climb.

Ines put her hand in it and felt the answer in her bones before her mind caught up. Cold. Fast. High.

"The river," she said. "The river's coming in."

Above them, faint and far, the rain finally made up its mind.
