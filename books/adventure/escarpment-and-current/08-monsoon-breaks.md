## 8. Monsoon Breaks

The rain did not arrive so much as it fell upon them, the way a piano falls upon the street below when someone drops it out of a window.

Hazel heard it first as a sound in the earth — a low, gathering roar upstream, like a freight train that had decided to take a shortcut through the jungle. He had been sitting with his back against the flooded tunnel wall, sharing a tin of condensed milk with Ines by the light of a chemical stick, and he'd stopped mid-sip.

"What's that?" she whispered.

"That," Hazel said, "is the monsoon being early. Which is rude of it. I had plans for Tuesday."

Then the sound arrived properly, and there was no more conversation for a while.

The trading post above them had been built a century ago by men who believed that brick and mortar were a permanent argument against the weather. The river had been winning that argument ever since. The tunnels beneath it — old drainage channels, storage vaults, whatever else colonial ambition had buried down here to keep dry — ran under the compound like the veins of a hand, and Vane's men walked around on the back of that hand all day, loading crates, running generators, never quite knowing what was underneath their boots.

Hazel and Ines knew. They'd spent two days mapping it from the inside, working by chemlight through passages that smelled of bat guano and drowned history, finding the archive room Ines had described — the vault door, the shelving, the crates of paper that somebody a hundred years dead had thought important enough to seal behind iron. And they'd found the thing Ines hadn't mentioned, or hadn't known: a low passage at the archive's far end, half-flooded even in dry season, sloping away toward the river side of the escarpment.

"Where does that go?" Hazel had asked, standing knee-deep in black water, holding his light above the surface.

"The riverbank," Ines said. "There's an old landing below the post. The barges used it before they built the new dock."

"How do you know?"

"Because I surveyed this concession for eight months, Hazel. I know where everything is. That's my whole tragic personality."

Now the whole tragic personality was pressed against him in a tunnel that was filling with water, because the monsoon had come early and come angry, and the drainage channels that had been ankle-deep that morning were thigh-deep by noon and chest-deep by three o'clock, and rising.

* * *

The rain itself they couldn't see — they were forty feet underground — but they could hear it, a vast white noise overhead, and beneath it the deeper sound of the river climbing. Water came into the tunnels from everywhere at once: seeping through the old brickwork in silver threads, pouring down the stairwells in ropes, backing up from the low end where the river side passage connected to the outside world. The tunnels were filling like a bathtub with the tap left on and the drain plugged with a century of silt.

"We have to move," Ines said. She'd said it four times in the last hour, each time more quietly, which told Hazel she was doing the same arithmetic he was.

"I know."

"Hazel. The archive room floods at—" She stopped herself. He watched her decide not to say whatever number she'd been about to say, because the number would only frighten them both.

"It doesn't matter," Hazel said. "We're not here for the archive anymore. We're here for the exit."

The exit was the problem. The main stairwell up into the trading post's cellar was now a waterfall — they'd looked at it twenty minutes ago and watched brown water thundering down the steps like the world's most aggressive banister. Going up meant swimming up a waterfall in the dark, into a compound full of men with guns who were, presumably, right now discovering that their basements had opinions about the weather.

The other way was the low passage. The one toward the river. The one that was already chest-deep and rising faster than anything else, because it was lowest and nearest the river.

"There has to be another way," Ines said.

"There isn't. There's the stairs we can't climb and the hole we can't—"

He stopped. Because that wasn't true either, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person neither wanted to introduce.

The low passage went underwater. Completely. Somewhere along its length — he'd felt it two days ago with his fingertips, a ceiling that dipped until it kissed the surface — the passage dove below grade and stayed under for a stretch before rising again toward the old landing. In dry season it would have been a bad crawl, ten feet maybe, fifteen, something you'd do on a dare with your heart going like a drumline.

In flood season, with the water level climbing every minute, that fifteen feet could be fifty. Could be a hundred. Could be a ceiling that never came back at all.

"You're a diver," Ines said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse. It was hope.

"I'm a salvage diver," Hazel said. "Which means I dive wrecks with a boat above me, a line under me, a tank on my back, and a schedule that says I get to come up. This is none of those things."

"So what you're saying is you're overqualified."

"What I'm saying is I'm going to need you to hold the light."

* * *

They went back to the archive room first, because Hazel wanted the last of their air.

It was a stupid thing, the air. Two scuba bottles they'd brought upriver wedged among the supplies, plus a third he'd salvaged off a drowned workboat in the shallows after the sniper fire — a diver's habit, you never leave a bottle behind, even a stranger's. Three bottles, one regulator apiece, and a spare octopus rig he'd rigged himself out of parts that had no business working together but did, the way old friends sometimes do.

He checked them by feel and chemlight, purged the valves, listened to the hiss. Full enough. Not full. Enough.

"Ines." He made her look at him. Her face in the green chemlight was calm in the way that scared him more than panic would have — the calm of someone who had already run the numbers and gotten a bad answer and decided to live with it. "You don't have to do this part. You can wait here. If the water stops rising, you might be able to wade out the way we came once it drops."

"And if it doesn't drop?"

"Then waiting here is drowning slowly instead of quickly."

She considered that with the seriousness she gave everything, even jokes, especially his jokes. "If I stay here," she said, "I die alone in the dark holding documents. If I go with you, I drown next to a man who makes terrible jokes under pressure. One of these options has company in it."

"That's the worst reasoning I've ever heard."

"It's the only reasoning available. Take it or leave it."

He took it. He handed her a bottle and showed her how the regulator worked — purge button, bite the mouthpiece, breathe out slow when you pull it, don't hold your breath, ever, not for any reason, not even if your lungs are screaming, especially if your lungs are screaming. She repeated it back to him twice without notes, which was more than some deckhands he'd trained had managed.

"Breathe normally," he told her. "Underwater, normal means small. Little sips. You're not drinking it, you're borrowing it."

"And if I panic?"

"You won't."

"How do you know?"

"Because panicking takes breath, and you've never wasted anything in your life. I've seen how you eat crackers."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, which was the point. Fear is a gas; it expands to fill whatever container you give it. A laugh punches a hole in the container.

* * *

The low passage swallowed them at 4:17 by Hazel's watch, though the watch was underwater within a minute and time thereafter became a rumor.

He went first, towing the spare bottle on a strap, feeling along the left wall with his glove because the left wall was the rule — pick a wall, keep it, and the wall will eventually bring you out somewhere, even if where it brings you out is a dead end, in which case at least you'll know exactly where the dead end is. The water was cold in a way the jungle above had no right to produce, and it carried debris: sticks, leaves, something soft that brushed his face and turned out to be a waterlogged rag and not, as his hindbrain insisted for one horrible second, a hand.

Behind him he could hear Ines breathing on the regulator — the little mechanical gasp-and-hiss, gasp-and-hiss, steady as a metronome. Steadier than his own. He focused on her rhythm the way a rower focuses on the stroke of the boat beside him.

The ceiling came down to meet them.

He felt it first as a pressure change, then with his free hand — brick, close, closer, gone, and then the water closed over the top of his head and the world became a throat.

This was the part he never told civilians about diving, the part that didn't make it into the brochures: that the moment the surface closes over you in a place with no visible exit, every animal instinct you own stands up at once and starts screaming the same word. Up. Up. Up. And there is no up. There is only forward, and forward is a guess, and the darkness is total, and the regulator in your mouth is the difference between life and its opposite, and it is a machine, machines fail, everything fails—

Breathe out slow. Keep the wall. Move.

He moved. Fifteen feet. Twenty. The ceiling didn't rise. Thirty, and his shoulder scraped something — a shelf bracket, old iron, rusted to lace — and he felt blood go warm and thin into the cold water and thought, distantly, that the blood would attract nothing down here, that the river's smallest horrors were elsewhere tonight, and that this was somehow comforting.

Forty feet. The ceiling still hadn't risen. His lungs were fine — the regulator was doing its work, the little sips, borrow don't drink — but a different clock had started, the one in his head, the one that counted down not air but nerve. Every diver knows that clock. Air runs out on a gauge. Nerve runs out on a feeling, and the feeling arrives without warning, and it says: *this is the moment you turn around*, and it lies, because turning around in a flooded tunnel is just dying in a different direction.

Ines bumped against his back. He reached behind, found her arm, squeezed twice — the signal he'd given her on the bank of every pool they'd crossed in two days of tunnels: *still here, keep going*. She squeezed back. Still here.

Fifty feet, and the ceiling rose.

Not much. A foot, maybe less. But it rose, and Hazel pulled himself up into the new airspace with a gasp that echoed strangely, and hauled Ines up beside him, and for a long moment they just hung there in the dark, breathing real air, foreheads almost touching, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the breathing wasn't already saying better.

"Status?" she managed finally.

"Alive."

"Anything else?"

"Wet."

* * *

The airspace was a pocket, not a promise. Above them the brick vaulting groaned — actually groaned, a deep structural complaint, like an old man asked to carry furniture — and Hazel understood with cold clarity that this pocket existed because the flood hadn't finished arriving yet. The water below them would keep climbing. The pocket would shrink. The question was whether the passage ahead rose faster than the river climbed, and that question had only one way of answering itself.

They swam on.

Twice more the ceiling dipped and they went under — shorter submersions, ten feet, twenty, each one easier in the body and harder in the mind, because the mind keeps a ledger and the ledger said *you have been underwater in a cave for longer than any sane person should be and the interest is compounding*. Between submersions they found air pockets smaller and smaller, until the last one was barely a cupped hollow under the crown of an arch, big enough for two faces tilted upward, lips to the brick, breathing from a supply of air that tasted of stone and age and, faintly, of the river beyond.

And then the passage opened.

Hazel felt it before he saw it — a change in the current, a lean in the water, the whole mass of it suddenly pulling toward something the way iron filings lean toward a magnet. He pulled himself around a final bend of brick and the darkness ahead went from absolute to merely profound, and there, high above and far ahead, was a gray smudge that moved.

Daylight. Rain-light, storm-light, the color of pewter — but light, honest light, coming down through the mouth of the old landing tunnel where it broke the hillside above the riverbank.

They came out of the water onto a shelf of collapsed masonry at the base of the tunnel mouth, and lay there on our backs — on their backs — while the rain fell into the opening and struck their faces like a blessing with poor aim. Below them, maybe thirty feet down a slope of broken brick and root, the river ran high and fast and brown, carrying entire trees sideways, the monsoon's receipts arriving downstream.

Hazel laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him in a raw whoop that bounced around the tunnel and came back embarrassed.

"We're alive," Ines said, in the tone of someone confirming a suspicious invoice.

"We are alive."

"Your arm is bleeding."

"My arm is *alive* too. Everything's alive. It's a miracle of aliveness down here."

She sat up, wrung out her hair with both hands, and looked at him with an expression he was beginning to catalog — the surveyor's expression, the one that preceded bad news delivered with precision. "Hazel. Look at the river."

He looked. High. Fast. And on the far bank, half-hidden in the rain-haze, a light. Then another. Moving along the water's edge on the compound side, sweeping the flooded landing, the slope, the tunnel mouths.

Vane's men were already out in the storm, searching the banks. Of course they were. The rain that nearly killed them had also, conveniently, washed away every track they'd left, and a man like Vane would know that a flooded tunnel system has exactly two ends, and he owned one of them.

"They'll check this landing within the hour," Ines said.

"Then we're not here within the hour." Hazel got his legs under him, tested the bleeding arm, found it shallow and angry rather than deep and dangerous. Downstream, maybe two hundred meters through the flooded brush, he could see the shape he'd been praying for without admitting he was praying: an old pirogue, half-swamped, tied to a root ball, abandoned or forgotten by whoever had used it last.

The river was running at a speed that would kill a strong swimmer. The rain was falling like a verdict. Somewhere behind them, men with flashlights were working their way toward the one exit Hazel and Ines had just crawled out of.

But the pirogue floated. Barely. And Hazel Delacroix had spent his whole life learning the single most useful lesson the water teaches: *floating is a starting condition.*

"Can you paddle?" he asked Ines.

"I can learn to paddle."

"Atta girl. Get in the boat."
