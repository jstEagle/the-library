## 9. Prisoners of the Camp

They took her boots first.

That was the detail Ines would remember afterward, when everything else had blurred into the general misery of wet canvas and diesel smoke — that Vane's men had stripped them of their boots before anything else, as if boots were the true currency of escape, as if a person without boots was only half a person. They'd marched Hazel and her down from the perimeter fence with rifles at their backs and the rain coming down in gray sheets, and the first thing the camp did was take their boots.

"Sentimental," Hazel muttered beside her, hopping on one foot while a man with a face like a closed fist pulled the laces from his other boot. "I had those resoled in March."

"Save your breath," Ines said.

She was saving hers. She would need it for what was coming, because she knew this camp. She had helped survey it. She had stood on this exact stretch of churned mud six months ago with a theodolite and a clipboard, marking out where the mess tent should go, and Marcus Vane had clapped her on the shoulder and said, *Good work, Okonkwo. This is going to be a fine operation.* And she had believed him, because believing him had been easier than the alternative, and because her salary had cleared on the first of every month like a small dependable sunrise.

Now she stood barefoot in the mud of her own good work, a prisoner of it.

* * *

They put them in the supply tent — not a proper cell, because the camp didn't have one; Vane had never planned for prisoners, only for payroll — and zip-tied Hazel's wrists to a cargo rack while leaving Ines's hands free, which told her something about how Vane's mind worked. The diver was a tool to be kept functional. She was a problem to be managed.

The tent smelled of rope and kerosene and the sweet rot of canvas that had been pitched too long in the wet. Through the fabric wall she could hear the camp going about its evening: generators, voices, the clang of the mess kettle, someone laughing at something. That was the worst part, she thought. The ordinariness. Forty people eating dinner forty feet from where she sat on a crate, and not one of them surprised that there were prisoners. Not one of them asking why.

Hazel tested his bonds once, delicately, the way a man tests a knot he intends to respect until the right moment. "How many?"

"In camp? Thirty-eight when I left. Maybe fewer now. Some took the money and went home."

"And the rest stayed for?"

Ines looked at the tent wall, at the shadows of men moving past it with lanterns. "For the same reason anyone stays anywhere. They were told a story they liked better than the truth."

"You used to be one of them."

"I *am* one of them," she said, and heard how it came out of her, flat and unarguable. "That's the thing you keep missing, Delacroix. I'm not your ally who happens to have a past. I'm one of them who happened to leave. There's a difference, and it matters less than you think it does."

He considered that with the patience of a man used to waiting out weather. Water dripped off the tent ridge and found a rhythm against a fuel drum. Somewhere a radio played, low, a song half-eaten by static.

"I don't keep missing it," he said finally. "I just keep deciding it doesn't change the arithmetic. You know where the vault is. You know which of these people will help and which will shoot. That's worth more than a clean conscience."

"My conscience isn't dirty."

"No," Hazel agreed mildly. "It's just for sale, same as everyone else's. Question is the price."

She wanted to hate him for that. She found she couldn't quite manage it, which frightened her more than the rifles had.

* * *

They came for her after dark.

Not Vane — she'd expected Vane, had rehearsed the conversation in her head all through the march down from the fence, all the things she would say, the evidence she would name. Instead it was Priya Nair, the expedition's geologist, holding a lantern and two enamel mugs of tea, and behind her, hanging back by the tent flap with the studied casualness of a man pretending not to be present, Dr. Achebe, the team's archaeologist, whom Ines had once spent three weeks teaching to read contour lines.

Priya set the mugs down on the crate between them. "Marcus says you can have tea."

"How generous."

"He also says if you cooperate, you'll be on a boat downstream within the week. Paid through the end of your contract. Full severance." Priya's voice was careful, level, the voice of a woman reciting terms she had memorized so she wouldn't have to feel them. "He wants you to sign a nondisclosure. Standard. Everyone signed one."

"Everyone signed one," Ines repeated. "And then everyone watched him weld the vault shut and cut the radios, and everyone decided that was standard too."

"It *was* an adjustment," Priya said, and Ines almost laughed, because *adjustment* — as if the last three months had been a change in the catering schedule. "Ines. Listen to me. You found something old in a hole in the ground. Papers. Paper doesn't feed anyone. Paper doesn't build the road that gets the timber out, that pays the porters, that keeps thirty families employed through the dry season. Marcus has a plan for the site that includes the papers staying buried, and honestly—" Priya glanced toward the tent flap, lowered her voice, "—honestly, some of those documents, if they got out, they wouldn't help anyone. Not even the people you think they'd help. They'd just make lawyers rich."

"There are names in those documents," Ines said quietly. "Villages. Land grants. People who were moved off this river so a trading company could exist, and the company that exists now is the same company with different letterhead. You've seen the concession map, Priya. You surveyed its boundaries. Whose names are on the land inside them?"

Priya's jaw tightened. She was a good scientist, Ines remembered suddenly. Meticulous. Honest in her data and dishonest only in her conclusions, which was the most common form of dishonesty in the world and the hardest to prosecute.

"I'm not here to argue," Priya said. "I'm here because I owed you that conversation instead of letting Marcus's men do it. Drink your tea. Think about the offer." She stood, hesitated, and added, softer: "You walked into the jungle alone, Ines. Nobody does that unless they're running *from* something. Whatever it is you're carrying — it isn't worth what it's costing you."

Then she was gone, and the lantern light swung away across the canvas, and Ines sat with two mugs of tea going cold and the taste of the argument still in her mouth, because the terrible thing, the thing she would never say aloud, was that Priya had argued well. That was how it worked. That was how it had worked on her, eighteen months ago, in a conference room in Houston with Brandt's lawyers sliding a folder across the table: reasonable men making reasonable sounds, each individual step so small, so defensible, until you looked up and you were standing on the far side of the river and couldn't see the bank you'd started from.

Dr. Achebe hadn't spoken the whole time. Now he came forward, slowly, an older man moving like his joints had opinions, and he looked at her for a long moment.

"You taught me contours," he said. "You said, *the line is never the land, Doctor. The line is only where we agreed to stop looking.*"

"I remember."

"I have thought about that sentence for three months," Achebe said. "Every day. Every single day since Marcus sealed the lower chamber." He crouched, painfully, so his eyes were level with hers, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "I signed the paper. I want you to know that I signed it, and I read every word before I signed it, and I signed it anyway, because my wife is sick and the insurance is through the institute and the institute is through Marcus. So do not come to me for courage, Miss Okonkwo. I have none to sell. But I will tell you this for nothing: the third chamber was never flooded by accident. Marcus's own engineers raised the waterline in February. Ask yourself what a man buries deeper when he wants it kept."

He rose, groaning, and shuffled out, and Ines sat very still, her heart going hard against her ribs. The third chamber. She had assumed the flooding was the river doing what rivers do. If it was done deliberately—

Beside her, Hazel exhaled slowly through his nose. "Your former colleagues," he said, "are a real mixed bag."

"They're people," Ines said. "That's worse."

* * *

Tomas Reyes came at midnight.

She heard him before she saw him — the particular careful tread of a man who had spent twenty years around heavy machinery and had learned to move like machinery himself, economical, silent, each footfall placed and committed. He slipped under the tent's back wall where the canvas sagged over a cable drum, and he crouched there in the dark, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

Tomas had been the expedition's engineer. He had rebuilt the generator twice, rigged the dewatering pumps for the vault excavation, taught her to drive the quad bike when the track washed out. He had a wife in Cebu and a daughter who sent him drawings of horses, and he kept the drawings folded in his shirt pocket over his heart, and once, drunk on cheap rum after the trading post survey, he had told Ines that he came back to the river every year because the river was the only boss who never lied to him.

He was lying to himself now. She could see it in his face, in the way he couldn't quite look at her.

"Ines." His voice was barely a breath. "I told them where the fence was weak. On the north side. Where you went out." He said it fast, like pulling a tooth. "They asked me about the sensors, and I told them. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. But Marcus had my contract, and the contract has clauses, and—"

"Tomas."

"—and I need you to know it wasn't personal, it was never personal, I would never—"

"*Tomas.*" She reached out and took his wrist, and he stopped, shaking. "I know. I've always known what you are. You're the man who fixed the pumps when everyone else had gone to bed. That man doesn't owe me an apology. He owes me whatever he came here to give me, so give it, because the lantern rounds come back through in eleven minutes and we both know the schedule."

Something in his shoulders let go. He fumbled at his shirt pocket — not the side with the horse drawings, the other side — and pressed something small and cold into her palm, folding her fingers over it with both of his rough hands, the way you'd close a child's hand around a coin.

A key. Old brass, worn smooth, with a paper tag gone soft with damp. She knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a dream: the pump house key. The auxiliary one, the one Tomas had cut himself years ago and never entered in the equipment log because a man who works around institutions learns which possessions should not officially exist.

"Pump house," he whispered. "Back wall, there's a gap behind the intake pipes — big enough for a slim person, and the grate's held by two bolts I never torqued properly, on purpose, because—" his breath caught, almost a laugh, almost a sob, "—because I told myself it was for maintenance access. I told myself that for three months, Ines. Every night I told myself that."

"What's through the gap?"

"The drainage channel runs under the compound to the river. It comes up behind the fuel store. It's foul and it's tight and the water's chest-deep in the rains, but it goes to the river, and the river—" he squeezed her fingers once, hard, "—the river never lies."

"Hazel can't fit. And his wrists are tied to that rack, and the guard rotation—"

"Second watch is Emeka and the new boy, Duarte. Emeka owes me from the Port Harcourt job. He'll be slow checking the rack. Slow is all anyone ever gives anybody, Ines. Slow is the whole revolution." He was already backing toward the sag in the canvas, already becoming shadow. "Third chamber, what Achebe said — it's true. I helped raise the waterline. I've been telling myself that was engineering too." His voice cracked clean through. "Tell my daughter the horses were the best part of my day. Every day. Tell her that."

And then the canvas settled, and the lantern glow swung up the tent row on schedule, and Ines sat in the dark with a brass key biting into her palm and the sound of the camp breathing around her — generators, rain, forty people sleeping the sleep of the reasonably paid.

Hazel's voice came out of the darkness beside her, very quiet. "Well?"

She opened her hand. Even in the blackness, the key seemed to hold a little light, the way metal does.

"Well," she said, "it turns out loyalty has a market price after all. And tonight somebody paid in the right direction."

* * *

They had until dawn, maybe less. The rain was thickening again, the river rising by the hour, and somewhere upslope Vane was sleeping the sleep of a man who believed his fences held. Ines worked the key's teeth against the zip-tie's ratchet, feeling for the mechanism the way Tomas had shown her once, years ago, laughing, on a broken generator cover: *everything locks, Ines, and everything unlocks, that's the whole secret of the world.*

The tie gave with a click no louder than a knuckle cracking.

Across the tent, Hazel flexed his freed hands and looked at her, and in the dark his grin was quick and crooked and entirely without comfort.

"Pump house," he whispered. "Chest-deep water, a drainage channel, and a guard rotation run by a man who owes a favor. Best odds I've been offered all season."

Above them, thunder rolled down the escarpment, and the river rose.
