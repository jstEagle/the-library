## 10. Vane's Offer

They fed him first.

Hazel hadn't expected that. He'd expected a chair without arms, a man standing behind him, maybe a fist to the kidneys as a formality of introduction. Instead a boy of perhaps sixteen set down a tin plate of rice and river fish, fried crisp, with a wedge of lime, and Marcus Vane sat across from him under the awning of the mess tent and waited politely for him to eat.

"You look like a man who appreciates food," Vane said.

"I appreciate not being shot at," Hazel said. "The food's a bonus."

Vane laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, which was somehow worse than a fake one. He was younger than Hazel had pictured from Ines's descriptions. Late forties, silver coming in at the temples like frost on a fence post, forearms brown and ropey from years of fieldwork. He wore khaki that had actually been worked in, not bought pre-weathered by some catalog. He looked, Hazel thought, like the kind of expedition leader you'd follow into bad country and thank for it afterward. That was the trap of him. The whole architecture of the man was a trap, and it was built to catch exactly the kind of animal Hazel was.

"Your friend Okonkwo won't eat with us," Vane said, pouring coffee from a dented thermos into two enamel cups. "She's been in the holding hut since yesterday. I've told them to leave her be. She's stubborn, not stupid — she'll come around when she's ready." He slid one cup across the table. "Sugar?"

"No."

"Good. A man who takes his coffee straight can be reasoned with." Vane drank, watching him over the rim. "You're wondering why you're not dead."

"The thought crossed my mind. Briefly. Somewhere between the rifle muzzle and the rice."

"Because dead men are expensive," Vane said simply. "You have to explain them. You have to move them. And every so often they turn up downstream wearing handcuffs made of paperwork." He set his cup down. "Whereas a live man with your particular skills is worth more than any crate in my vault."

*There it is,* Hazel thought, and kept chewing.

He ate slowly because eating slowly gave him time to think, and thinking was the only weapon anyone had handed him since the Marisol grounded. Around them the camp went about its business with the unhurried confidence of an operation that believed it had already won. Generators humming. Crates stenciled with the concession number stacked under tarps. Men carrying things toward the trading post ruins with the flat efficiency of ants relocating a colony. Nobody looked at him, which meant everybody had been told not to, which meant he mattered.

The rain had settled into its monsoon rhythm — not the violence of the break but the long patient drumming that could go for days, filling the tunnels, swelling the river, erasing tracks. It drummed on the canvas above them like a landlord demanding rent.

"Let me tell you what I know about you," Vane said. "Hazel Delacroix. Salvage diver, Gulf coast, fifteen years. Owns — owned — a boat called the Marisol, forty-one feet, steel hull, older than sin. Reputation for finding things other people gave up on. Reputation for keeping his mouth shut, which in your trade is worth more than the finding." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "And debts. A yard bill in Port Isla. A sister in Baton Rouge whose medical bills you've been paying off one job at a time. A boat that needs thirty thousand dollars of work before she'll float honest again."

Hazel set down his fork. "You had me researched."

"I had you *read*. There's a difference. Research is what Brandt's people do — folders, photographs, leverage. Reading is what you do when you intend to work with someone instead of over them." Vane leaned back. "I'm offering you work, Mr. Delacroix. Real work. The kind that comes along once in a man's life, if he's lucky, and never comes again if he turns it down."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you and Ms. Okonkwo will be released somewhere inconvenient after the monsoon, and I'll wish you well sincerely, because I bear you no ill will. You came upriver to deliver supplies. You delivered them. That's more than most men manage." He smiled faintly. "But we both know you're not going to say no. Not yet. So let me show you what yes looks like."

* * *

The vault was worse in daylight, or rather, the idea of it was worse, because Vane didn't take him inside. He took him to the lip of it — the flooded stairwell behind the old trading post, where the water stood black and still as poured ink, swallowing the steps three down. A generator ran a pump that wasn't winning. Two of Vane's men stood at the edge with carbines slung, less as guards than as chaperones.

"Colonial archive," Vane said, gesturing at the dark water the way another man might gesture at a view. "Spanish administration, mostly. Concession records going back a hundred and sixty years. Land grants. Titles. Correspondence between governors and ministers about who paid whom for what." He crouched, touched the water's surface, watched the ripples die. "The lower chambers are sealed. Flooded past anything my people can manage. We've tried. Two of my divers went down last month." He said it flatly, without performance. "Neither came back up. The passage narrows, the silt moves, there's a section where the ceiling comes down to about arm's width and the current from the river side pushes through it when the water rises. My men were good divers. They weren't *your* kind of diver."

Hazel looked at the black water and thought about the two men. Thought about how a flooded colonial stairwell kills you: the silt cloud that turns a headlamp's beam to milk, the snagged line, the panic that makes you pull instead of think. He thought about it professionally, the way he always did, mapping the problem before he'd agreed to have the problem.

"What's in the lower chambers?" he asked.

"Money," Vane said. "Not paper money. *Proof.* The original concession instruments. The ones that make everything above us legal — or don't." He straightened. "Brandt's firm holds this concession through a chain of transfers. If the originals in that vault match the chain, we're untouchable forever. If they don't—" He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Then everything we've built here is a house of cards, and the river's rising."

"And you want me to bring them up."

"I want you to bring up *one case*. Sealed. I'll know it when I see it — brass corners, government stamp. Everything else down there stays down there. You don't read it, you don't photograph it, you don't talk about it. You bring me the case, and you walk away with a quarter million dollars and my personal guarantee of safe passage downriver, for you and Okonkwo both." He held up a hand before Hazel could speak. "I know what you're thinking. You're thinking a guarantee from me isn't worth the air it's spoken with. Fair. So here's collateral: the moment you surface with the case, I put you and her in a boat myself, with fuel and papers, and I ride halfway downriver with you. You'll literally be able to watch me keep my word."

It was, Hazel had to admit, a good offer. It was a *beautiful* offer. Quarter million dollars. The Marisol rebuilt hull to wheelhouse, new engine, new tanks, paint the color of morning. His sister's bills gone. The yard debt gone. Another ten years on the water doing the only thing he'd ever been good at, without flinching every time a navy patrol boat slowed to look at him.

He stood at the edge of the black water and let himself want it. Really want it, full strength, no anesthesia. That was the trick of temptation — it only worked if you looked directly at it. Half measures of wanting were just cowardice with better manners.

A quarter million dollars.

Built on what?

Built on a surveyor locked in a hut forty yards away because she'd found something true and refused to pretend it wasn't. Built on an engineer named Tomas Reyes who'd slipped a key to a woman in chains and would pay for it sooner or later, because men like Vane always collected. Built on two drowned divers whose families would be told their husbands died in a boating accident. Built on river families up and down this water whose grandfathers' land grants were sitting in that vault like hostages, and whose grandchildren would spend another century losing arguments to paper.

Built on a radio log that ended mid-sentence.

"You know what's funny," Hazel said. "This is the second time somebody's offered me money to go down into something they wouldn't explain."

"Brandt," Vane said, and his mouth tightened fractionally. "Yes. He mentioned. He has a gift for making men feel hired when they're actually being used."

"He offered triple rate to run supplies. Didn't say supplies of what, didn't say why the navy was shadowing my barge, didn't say why the wreck I salvaged from was charted as empty." Hazel turned to face him fully. "So let's skip the part where you act like this is different. You want the case because it either saves you or damns you, and you don't know which, and you need a diver enough to pay a quarter million to find out. That's not work. That's a coin toss with my lungs."

For a moment something moved behind Vane's eyes — respect, maybe, or its close cousin, recalculation. Then he laughed again, shorter this time.

"God, I miss honest men. Do you know what my days are? Lawyers. Surveyors who tell me what I want to hear. Men who nod." He stepped closer, and dropped his voice, and this was the seduction working properly now, the intimacy of it, the sense of being let inside the tent. "You want the truth? Here's the truth. I came up here six months ago to run a timber survey. Timber. Trees. The most boring crime imaginable. Then Okonkwo's ground-penetrating radar found the vault, and Brandt's lawyers started moving like scalded cats, and I understood that I had walked into the middle of something that started before my grandfather was born and would still be running after my grandson dies. I didn't build this machine, Delacroix. I'm riding it. And the difference between me and you is that I understood early that the only choices left were which seat to sit in."

"That supposed to make me feel better?"

"It's supposed to make you feel *accurate*. Sentiment is a luxury of people who aren't standing over open water." He glanced at the stairwell. "Ines found the vault. Ines also found what happens to people who find things. She chose. She's in the hut. Tomas Reyes chose too — you'll see. Everyone chooses eventually. The only question is whether they choose while they still have options." He clapped Hazel on the shoulder, once, firm, comradely. "Sleep on it. Take the day. Eat. I'll have my dive gear shown to yours — you'll want to inspect it, and you should, because if you do this, you do it with equipment you trust. Tomorrow morning, high slack water, best visibility we'll get this week."

He walked away through the rain, hands in his pockets, unhurried, a man taking a stroll on property he believed he owned.

* * *

They put Hazel in a supply tent with a cot and a lantern and, after an hour, brought him Ines.

She came in guarded by one man, sat on the opposite cot, and studied him for a long moment in the lamplight. Her face was thinner than when she'd boarded the Marisol, and there was a bruise along her jaw he hadn't given her time to notice before. But her eyes were the same — level, unsentimental, taking inventory.

"He made you the offer," she said. Not a question.

"How'd you know?"

"Because you're alive and fed and he's letting us sit in the same room." She pulled the blanket around her shoulders. "That's his sequence. Feed, flatter, show the vault, name a number. He did it to all of us, one at a time, back when the camp was still pretending to be a survey. Most people crack at the number stage. The ones who don't, he shows them the lower chamber plans and lets them realize nobody else alive can do the dive. Pride is cheaper than money. Works faster, too."

"He said two of his divers died down there."

"They did. Good men. Better than he deserved." She rubbed her wrists where the zip ties had been. "Hazel. Listen to me. Whatever you're turning over in your head right now — the boat, your sister, the number — I know. I watched you do the arithmetic on the trip up. You're good at it. You've had practice needing things." She looked at him steadily. "But understand what you'd actually be buying. Not a quarter million. A seat on the machine. And seats on machines like this one don't come with doors."

"I know."

"Do you? Because last night I watched you eat his fish and drink his coffee and laugh at his joke about the checkpoint, and you laughed like a man who hasn't decided yet." No anger in it. Just observation, precise as one of her surveys. "I'm not asking you to be a hero. Heroes are a luxury too. I'm asking you to remember that the vault doesn't just hold his proof. It holds theirs. The river families. The land grants. The names of everyone this concession was built on top of. If you bring up that case and hand it to him, those documents don't stay lost — they get destroyed, and then they never existed, and neither did the people they belonged to."

Hazel sat on the cot's edge, elbows on knees, and looked at the lantern flame. Outside, the rain kept up its patient arithmetic on the canvas.

"My sister's going to ask me someday what I did the year I disappeared upriver," he said finally. "And I'd like to have an answer that isn't a lie I have to maintain."

Ines almost smiled. "That's the closest thing to a moral principle I've heard in this camp in two months."

"Don't get sentimental. I might still take the money."

"You won't." She lay back on the cot. "But you'll tell him yes."

He looked at her sharply. "Which is it?"

"Both." Her eyes closed. "Tell him yes tomorrow morning. Get in the water. Get down to that chamber. And then decide what comes back up with you — because once you're down there, in the dark, with the case in your hands, nobody in this camp can see you. Not him, not his rifles, not his guarantees." Her voice was going soft with exhaustion. "Down there, Hazel, the only law is whatever you carry in your chest. He thinks he's hiring your hands." A pause. "He forgot to check whether your hands answer to you alone."

The guard called time, and she was led out, and Hazel lay in the dark listening to the rain and the generators and the river rising against the old stones, and he understood that she had just handed him the only plan that fit inside a man.

Tomorrow, at high slack water, he would tell Marcus Vane yes.

And whatever came up the stairs out of that black water — it would not be only the case.
