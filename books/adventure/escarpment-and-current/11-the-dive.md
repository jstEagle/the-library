## 11. The Dive

They brought him to the vault at first light, which was a courtesy Vane probably thought of as generosity and Hazel understood as theater. You didn't let a man sleep on a decision like this. You woke him before his instincts had their coffee.

"Breakfast?" Hazel asked the guard who unlocked the storage shed they'd slept in. The man — a wide Samoan named Faleolo who'd been polite in the way of someone following orders he didn't love — shook his head.

"Mr. Vane says you eat after."

"After," Hazel repeated. "That's how I like my meals. Conditional."

Faleolo almost smiled. Almost. It flickered across his face like a fish under a boat, there and gone, and then he was all business again, walking Hazel down the muddy lane between tents toward the old trading post with its stone foundation showing through sixty years of jungle like bones through skin.

Ines was already there. They'd put her at the edge of the work area, two guards between her and the water, close enough that Hazel could see her but not — he noted, filing it away — close enough for them to talk without being overheard. She looked like she hadn't slept either. Her eyes found his and did something complicated: a question, an answer, a warning, all in about half a second. *Don't do anything stupid.* And underneath it, quieter: *Do exactly what you have to.*

Vane stood at the lip of the flooded stairwell, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a garden. He'd shaved. That was the detail that stuck with Hazel. Somewhere in the last twelve hours, Marcus Vane had decided this was a formal occasion, and he'd shaved for it.

"The chamber is thirty meters down," Vane said, without preamble. "The stairwell floods at the second landing. Below that, it's black water — no light penetration, silt on every surface. Your air is on the rack. Forty minutes bottom time, more if you need it, but I'd rather you didn't." He turned. "The final chamber is behind a collapsed partition. Reyes marked it before he—" a pause, smooth as river glass, "—before he became unavailable. Steel door, combination lock, corroded but functional. The combination is 44-19-7."

Concession forty-four. Nineteen twenty-seven, maybe. Hazel filed that too.

"And what am I bringing up?"

"Everything you can carry. There are crates below — pre-war steel, waterproofed. Fill them. We'll haul them up on the line." Vane's voice had the pleasant flatness of a man reading terms aloud. "You do this, you walk out of here with more money than you've seen in your life, and nobody remembers your name. That's the good version, Delacroix. I'd stay in the good version if I were you."

"You've met me," Hazel said. "I live in the bad versions. They've got better company."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not anger. Calculation. He was deciding whether Hazel was worth the trouble of hating yet. Apparently not. He gestured at the gear rack and walked away to shout at men loading crates onto a barge raft, and Hazel was left alone with the water.

* * *

The stairwell went down into dark like a throat.

Hazel had been diving since he was eleven years old, when his father had lowered him off the stern of a shrimp boat with a garden hose and a prayer, and in twenty years of salvage he'd learned one truth above all others: the water doesn't care. Not about your plans, your debts, your little moral crises. The water is honest in a way no jungle, no office, no man with a freshly shaved face could ever manage. It just holds you, or it doesn't.

He checked his rig twice anyway. Old habit. New stakes.

The descent was easy at first — gray-green light, the stairwell walls furred with algae, his bubbles rising past him in silver strings. At the second landing the light gave out entirely, and he clicked on his dive torch, and the beam came back at him full of suspended silt, a snowstorm of the river's own making. Thirty meters. He equalized, watched his depth gauge tick, and tried to think about nothing at all.

Thinking about nothing lasted approximately four minutes, which was how long it took him to reach the collapsed partition.

Reyes's mark was still there — a spray-painted arrow, ghostly under a season of silt, pointing left along a corridor that hadn't been a corridor when the trading post was built. Hazel squeezed through. The steel door was exactly where the surveyor had said it would be, and the lock turned with a grinding reluctance, like an old man being asked to get out of bed, and then the beam of his torch swept across—

Hazel stopped breathing for a moment, which underwater is a bad habit.

It was a room. A whole room, dry until the flood took it, and now drowned in still black water that his torch cut through like a knife through oil. Shelving ran along three walls. Crates sat stacked in careful rows, stenciled with faded lettering in French and Spanish and English. And on a long table in the center, weighted down with brass rods, lay documents — hundreds of them, thousands, fanned and bundled and tied with string, their edges soft with eighty years of patience.

An archive. Not a metaphorical one. An actual, physical, drowning library of everything somebody had once wanted very badly to forget.

He thought of Ines in the tunnel, weeks ago now, telling him in a voice stripped down to wire: *Land grants. Death certificates. Permits signed by men who never existed. It's not paper, Hazel. It's the whole confession, written down and locked in a basement by people who thought the river would keep their secrets.*

The river had kept them. The river had kept them beautifully. And now Marcus Vane wanted them hauled up into the daylight so he could decide which confessions survived.

Hazel got to work.

* * *

The physical labor helped. There's a mercy in work — your hands know what to do even when the rest of you is arguing with itself. He filled the first crate with bundles from the nearest shelf, sealing the lid, clipping it to the lift line. The second crate went faster. His gauge read eighteen minutes of bottom time when he started the third, and somewhere around minute twenty, moving down the far wall of shelving, his torch passed over a flat tin box wedged behind a crate, half-buried in silt, smaller than a bread loaf.

He almost left it. That was the thing he'd remember later — the honest truth of it, the part he'd never tell anyone straight: he almost left it, because taking it wasn't the plan, because the plan was simple, because simple plans kept men alive.

But his hand was already reaching. Hands are honest too.

The tin was sealed with wax and tape. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay a folder — thin, maybe twenty pages — and on its cover, in a clerk's careful colonial hand: *Concession No. 44 — Original Grant & Correspondence.* The original grant. Not a copy. Not a forgery waiting to be discovered. The actual paper some minister or governor had signed in 1927, the root of the whole rotten tree, the document whose existence proved every permit Brandt's firm had filed since was a copy of a copy of a lie.

Hazel crouched in the black water and held it in his torchlight and felt something settle in his chest. Not fear. Fear had been riding along since the sniper fire; fear was crew now, permanent, paid in full. This was different. This was cold and clean and quiet, like the water itself.

Clarity, his father would have called it. The kind you only get at depth, where nobody can reach you and every choice is finally, truly yours.

He could hand it up. Put it in the crate with the rest, let Vane burn or bury or sell it, take the money, rebuild the Marisol, buy back his life at the price of everyone who'd ever lived along this river. That was the good version. Vane had offered it personally.

Or.

Hazel worked fast now, and he worked well, because this — this small theft inside a large one — was the most honest salvage he'd ever done. He folded the folder flat against his chest, under the straps of his buoyancy vest, where it made a thin ridge no one would notice on a man already wearing a rig. Then he took a bundle from the third crate, broke it open, and re-tied it loose — sloppy, hurried-looking, the kind of damage silt and current might explain. If anyone counted, if anyone asked why the original grant wasn't among the recovered papers, there'd be a story ready-made: the water ate it. Rivers do that. Ask any salvage diver.

His gauge read nine minutes. He filled the third crate, clipped it to the line, and gave the tin box's hiding place one last sweep of silt with his fin wash, erasing himself from the room the way the river erased everything eventually.

Then he paused. One more thing. On the table, among the weighted papers, lay a single loose sheet — a land grant, 1931, bearing a family name Hazel had heard four days ago from a woman cooking fish over a fire while her grandchildren stared at him like he was a species of large, damp bird. Okonkwo had told him the archive named names. Here was one, in ink, witnessed twice.

He couldn't take it. Taking two things was greed, and greed got noticed, and besides — the families would need the whole archive someday, every page of it, testified to and catalogued and true. One page stolen was a souvenir. The rest was evidence.

But he memorized it. Name, parcel number, witness signatures, date. He held it in the torchlight and burned it into memory the way you burn a channel marker into your mind before running a shoal at night. Ines would want to know. The river people would want to know. Some debts you pay forward.

Then Hazel Delacroix kicked for the surface, carrying three crates of a dead empire's paperwork on a lift line and one folded lie against his heart, and began the long slow ascent through the black water, thinking — with a calm that surprised him — that his father would have understood. His father, who'd taught him that the first rule of salvage is *know what you're looking for*, and the second rule is *never tell the man paying you what you actually found*.

* * *

Light came back gradually, gray then green then gold, and Hazel broke the surface in the flooded stairwell with his lungs burning and the folder pressed flat as a second ribcage.

Faleolo hauled the crates up hand over hand, powerful and unhurried, and Hazel climbed the stairs into the morning, peeling his mask, blinking at a sky the color of hammered tin. Monsoon weather stacking up over the escarpment to the west, purple-black and patient. The river had risen a foot overnight. He could see it in the mud line on the pilings.

Vane met him at the top. Of course he did. Men like Vane always met you at the top; it saved them the climb.

"Well?"

"Three crates," Hazel said. "Full. There's more down there — another day's work at least, maybe two. The far shelving hasn't been touched." He let himself breathe hard, let his knees bend like a tired man's. "Your steel door's holding better than my back."

Vane studied him. Hazel had been studied by customs officers, insurance investigators, and one memorable ex-wife, and he knew the technique: hold still, look bored, give them nothing but exhaustion. Exhaustion is the most believable emotion in the world.

"The combination worked," Vane said. Not a question.

"Worked fine. Lock's corroded, though. Another season and it'd have seized for good." He shrugged out of his rig, slowly, letting the weight of it drag at him, and Faleolo took it from his shoulders — and for one lurching heartbeat the folder shifted against his chest, a whisper of oilcloth under wet neoprene, and Hazel kept his face empty as a bucket while the big man carried the rig to the rack.

Nothing. Faleolo hung it up. Vane had already turned away, counting crates, and Hazel exhaled through his nose, slow, controlled, a diver's exhale.

Across the compound, Ines stood where they'd parked her, arms crossed, watching the crates come up. When her eyes met his, he gave her nothing — no nod, no flicker, nothing a man with binoculars could read. But he touched his own chest once, briefly, like a man easing a cramp.

Her breath caught. He saw it from ten meters away. She looked down at the mud, and when she looked up again her face was composed and her eyes were shining, and she mouthed one word that the wind mostly stole but that Hazel, who had spent his life reading water, read perfectly:

*Which?*

He didn't answer. Couldn't. Instead he turned to Vane, who was saying something about a second dive tomorrow, about bonuses, about the good version, and Hazel nodded along like a man listening to terms, while behind his ribs the folded paper rode quiet and weightless and heavier than anything he'd ever raised from the sea.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow there'd be a second dive, and Tomas Reyes walking the perimeter with his keys, and Ines working out whatever she was working out with those quick surveyor's eyes, and Vane's boats loading crates for a run downriver before the permits expired and the monsoon closed the window for good.

Tomorrow, everything would move. Hazel could feel it coming the way he felt weather in his knee — pressure building, the river rising, the whole fragile arrangement of lies and loyalties groaning like a hull taking water.

He just hoped, watching Reyes pause at the supply tent and glance — one glance, quickly smothered — at Ines, that they'd all still be standing when it did.

Because across the compound, one of Vane's men had stopped beside the engineer, and said something low, and Tomas Reyes had gone pale as drowned paper.
