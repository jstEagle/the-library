## 12. Tomas's Price

Ines had learned to measure time in the camp by the sounds that were allowed.

The generator coughing awake at five. The cook tent clattering pans like a man clearing his throat before a difficult conversation. Boots on the boardwalk — always boots, never bare feet, because Vane had rules about readiness and the men had learned to love their rules the way dogs love a whistle. And then, twice a day, the radio check: a flat voice from downriver saying *Concession Forty-Four, status*, and Marcus Vane or one of his lieutenants answering *nominal* in a tone so smooth it could have been buttered.

Nominal. Six weeks of nominal. It was, Ines thought, the most dishonest word she'd ever heard spoken aloud, and she had once sat through an entire Brandt & Associates quarterly review.

She was mending a net by the supply shed — they'd given her work to do, the kind of work that kept hands busy and eyes down — when she saw Tomas Reyes cross the compound toward the fuel store with the particular walk of a man carrying something heavier than a jerry can. He didn't look at her. That was the first rule of their entire acquaintance now: he did not look at her, ever, not since the night he'd pressed the small brass key into her palm under cover of handing her a ration tin, and said, in the voice he used for discussing pump maintenance, "The third valve on the water line sticks. You have to know where it is."

She hadn't looked at him either. She'd said, "I'll file a report," which was surveyor for *thank you, I understand, God keep us both*, and he'd nodded and walked away whistling something tuneless, because Tomas Reyes whistled when he was terrified and everyone knew it and no one had ever mentioned it in eleven years of friendship.

The key was sewn into the hem of her shirt now, against her left hip, where it lay like a small hot coal she couldn't put down.

Three days ago, Hazel Delacroix had stood in the cage they were keeping him in — a stockade, really, salvaged chain-link and posts, built for equipment and repurposed for people, which told Ines everything about how Vane classified them — and had looked at her across the firelight with those diver's eyes that gave away nothing except depth, and asked, "Third valve on what?"

"The water line," she'd said. "It runs under the camp. Under the vault access corridor."

Hazel had been quiet for a long moment, chewing on whatever the cook tent called stew. Then: "So there's a way into the archive that isn't the front door."

"There's a way out of the camp that isn't the gate."

"That too." He'd smiled then, briefly, crookedly, the smile of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Your friend Tomas. He knows I'm the only one who can dive the last chamber. He knows Vane needs me breathing until the dive's done. Which means—"

"Which means we move after the dive or not at all," Ines had finished. "Yes. I've done the math too."

"And your math says?"

"My math says every hour we wait, someone else finds out about the key, and every hour after that, someone decides being the one who reported it is worth more than being the one who kept it."

Hazel had looked at her with something that wasn't quite surprise — recognition, maybe, the look of a man meeting his own conclusions wearing someone else's face. "Cheerful," he'd said. "You'd have made a good salvage diver. We're famous for our optimism."

"We're famous for our insurance policies," she'd said, and he'd laughed, quietly, into his stew, and for one strange moment the cage had felt almost like company.

That was three days ago. This morning, the sounds of the camp changed.

* * *

It started with the radios going silent. Not off — silent. The morning check came and went without the word *nominal*, and Ines felt the absence in her chest the way you feel a missing stair in the dark.

Then the men began gathering at the riverbank. Not running — walking, in that deliberate unhurried way that Vane drilled into them, because hurry looked like panic and panic looked like guilt. But they gathered. All of them. Even the cook, even the two who were supposed to be watching the stockade, drawn down to the water like iron filings to a magnet, and Ines understood with a cold, settling certainty that this gathering was the point, that everyone was meant to see.

Vane stood at the bank with his hands in his pockets, looking out at the brown flood-swollen current as if it owed him money. Two of his men flanked a third figure whose hands were zip-tied in front of him, and even before she could make out the face — the broad shoulders, the stance of a man who had spent decades arguing with machinery and mostly winning — Ines knew.

Her hands stopped moving on the net. A fisherman's knot half-tied, hanging stupid between her fingers.

Tomas.

*No,* she thought, uselessly, the way anyone thinks it, the word that has never once in human history changed anything. *No, no, no.*

She forced herself to stand. Forced her legs to carry her down the slope with the others, folding herself into the crowd at its edge, behind the shoulder of a man named Okafor who was large enough to hide behind and incurious enough not to notice. Her heart was going like a bilge pump. She catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything — pulse elevated, vision narrowed, hands cold — because cataloguing was what she did instead of screaming, and she suspected she would be doing a great deal of it in the next few minutes.

They had brought Tomas to the water's edge, where the mud was soft and the current ran fast with the early monsoon, and Vane turned to face the assembled camp with the mild expression of a man about to announce a change to the lunch schedule.

"Some of you," Vane said, "have been wondering why we haven't gone home."

Nobody answered. Nobody breathed, either, as far as Ines could tell.

"I want to be clear that I don't blame you for wondering. This has been a long contract. Longer than any of us signed up for." He let that sit, generous, reasonable, a father explaining bedtime. "But I also want to be clear about what happens when someone decides their curiosity is worth more than their word."

He nodded, and the two guards turned Tomas to face the crowd.

Tomas's face was swollen on one side — they'd worked him over first, then, somewhere private, which was somehow worse than if they'd done it here, in front of everyone, where at least there would have been witnesses to the beginning of it instead of only the end. His eye found the crowd, moved across it, and Ines watched it pass over her without stopping, because Tomas Reyes, even now, even with his hands tied, kept the first rule.

He did not look at her.

And then, deliberately, insultingly, wonderfully, he looked directly at her and winked.

It was such a Tomas thing to do. Eleven years she'd known him — since the Delgado survey, since the day he'd fixed her theodolite with a paperclip and a rubber band and explained, with immense dignity, that all engineering was just applied stubbornness — and in eleven years he had never once met catastrophe with anything but a joke delivered at exactly the wrong volume. When the helicopter lost its tail rotor over the escarpment in '19, he'd spent the descent explaining to the pilot that statistically they were fine. They had not been fine. The helicopter had been written off. Tomas had climbed out, dusted himself off, and billed the charter company for his ruined clipboard.

Now he stood at the edge of the brown water with a split lip and a tied hands and he winked at her, and Ines understood that the wink was a message, and the message was: *Don't you dare carry this. Don't you dare make it heavy. Take the key and go be light.*

She was going to be sick. She filed that too.

"Tomas," Vane said, conversationally, "gave something to someone he shouldn't have. Tomas, do you want to tell them what you gave?"

Tomas said nothing. His jaw set in the way it set when a pump refused to prime.

"No?" Vane sighed, genuinely regretful, a performance so polished it deserved its own funding line. "That's all right. I'll tell them. A key. A small brass key, to a door most of you didn't know existed, to a man none of you were supposed to talk to." He shook his head slowly. "Eleven years, Tomas. You surveyed half this concession with me. I trusted you with the fuel manifests."

"You trusted me with the fuel manifests," Tomas said, and his voice was wrecked but steady, "because I'm the only one who can read my handwriting."

Someone in the crowd made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Vane's eyes flicked up, patient, terrible, and the sound died.

"Here's what happens now," Vane said. "Nothing happens to any of you. Tomas made his choice alone, and he answers for it alone. We finish the job, we get paid, everybody goes home rich enough to forget this river." He turned back to Tomas. "But the river keeps what the river takes. And Tomas is going to find that out a little earlier than the rest of us."

Ines's mind, that treacherous efficient instrument, was already working: *the current, the monsoon swell, the body will go downstream, they won't be able to recover it, that's the design, no grave, no evidence, no closure, just a man subtracted from the world.* She hated her mind for calculating while her chest was caving in. She hated more that the calculation was right, because it meant Vane had planned this, had chosen the spot and the season and the swollen water, and a man who planned an execution around hydrology was a man who had already decided how the next chapter ended and was simply waiting for the river to sign it.

"Tomas," she said.

It came out before she could stop it. One word, cracked down the middle, and his head turned and this time he did look at her, and he smiled with his split lip, that same infuriating gentle smile from the helicopter, and he said, "Ines. Tell them the third valve sticks. Somebody should fix that."

"Ines," Vane said softly, without turning, without needing to turn, because of course he'd known she was there, had counted her into the crowd the way he counted everything. "You'll want to pay attention. This concerns you too."

The guards stepped back. Tomas stood alone at the bank, hands bound, boots in the soft mud, the current sliding past him fast and brown and indifferent.

He looked out at the water — their water, the river they'd argued with together for a decade, the river he'd taught her to read the way she taught him to read contour lines — and Ines saw his shoulders drop, just slightly, the way a man's shoulders drop when he sets down something heavy. Acceptance, maybe. Or relief. With Tomas it had always been impossible to separate the two.

"For the record," he said loudly, to the whole camp, to the river, to whatever record there was, "the survey data is real. Everything I stamped, everything I signed — the geology, the hydrology, all of it, real. Whatever else goes wrong here, that part was honest work." He paused. "Also, Vane, your generator's about to throw a bearing. Left side. You'll want to—"

The shot took the rest of it.

The sound went through the crowd like a shockwave through water, and the river took Tomas Reyes the way it took everything, quickly and without comment, and the brown current folded over the place where he had stood and closed like a page.

* * *

Afterward there was the awful ordinary business of an afternoon. Vane dismissed the camp with a few words about loyalty and schedules. The cook went back to his pans. The generator, true to Tomas's last professional opinion, began making a new and ominous noise from its left side, and nobody went near it, because nobody wanted to be the man who touched the machine the dead engineer had diagnosed.

Ines walked back to the supply shed on legs that belonged to someone else. She picked up the net. She held the fisherman's knot in her fingers and discovered she had no idea how to tie it anymore, her hands full of static, and so she sat down on an overturned crate and did the only useful thing available to her, which was nothing, magnificently, completely nothing, while inside her skull a single sentence repeated itself like a stuck pump:

*He winked. He winked, and then he told me to fix the valve.*

Because that was it. That was the message under the message. Not *don't grieve* — though that too, that insufferable generous man, sparing her even his death's weight. The real message, the engineer's message, the last work order of Tomas Reyes: *the third valve sticks. Use it. Tonight.*

He had known, standing at the water's edge with the gun behind him, that his death was going to be the thing that started the clock. Maybe he'd even planned it — no, not planned, Tomas didn't plan, but he'd *understood*, the way he understood machines, that a camp that watches a man die for a key will spend forty-eight hours being too frightened to search for it, and then twenty-four hours convincing itself the whole thing was handled, and somewhere in that window of fear and forgetting there would be a night with rain on it and a generator about to fail and a guard rotation thin enough to slip through.

He'd bought them the window with his life and made it look like a punchline.

Ines put her face in her hands and let herself shake for exactly sixty seconds. She timed it. She was a surveyor; she measured things; grief got its allotted minute and then it went in the ledger, filed under *costs*, to be paid later, in full, to whoever kept such accounts. When she lifted her head her eyes were dry and her hands were steady and the key against her hip felt less like a coal now and more like a compass needle.

"Hazel," she said that evening, through the chain-link, low, under the noise of the rising wind. He came to the wire the way he always did, unhurried, reading her face the way he read water — for what moved underneath.

"He's dead," Hazel said. Not a question. He'd been at the riverbank; everyone had; that had been the point.

"He's dead, and Vane thinks it settled the account." She wrapped her fingers in the chain-link, close to Hazel's, not touching, just near. "But Tomas left us a window. Fear today, complacency tomorrow, and tomorrow night the storm comes in hard — the barometer's been falling since noon, the river families could tell you that, half this camp's been complaining about their joints. And when the generator throws that bearing—"

"Which it will," Hazel murmured, "on schedule, because dead men apparently keep better maintenance logs than live ones."

"—half the lights go out, and the other half of the guards go to fix them, and the third valve on the water line sticks, Hazel. It sticks, and it opens, and it lets a diver into a flooded tunnel that comes up on the river side of the perimeter fence." She heard her own voice go quiet and fierce, a stranger's voice, a voice with a ledger in it. "Tomorrow night. Not after the dive. Not when it's convenient. Tomorrow night, or we stay here long enough to become people who watched Tomas die and did nothing, and I have been that person once already in my life and I am not going to survive being her twice."

Hazel was silent a moment. Rain began to fall, fat and warm and preliminary, ticking against the tarpaulin overhead like a countdown.

"Tomorrow night," he said finally. "Storm's worst hours. Current'll be murder, visibility zero, and if we miss the tunnel mouth we come up in open water a mile downstream with Vane's boats looking for us." He smiled that crooked diver's smile, the one that meant the arithmetic had come out bad and he was going swimming anyway. "You know, most people's friends just lend them money."

"Tomas didn't lend," Ines said. "He invested."

Out past the fence line, the river rose in the dark, carrying everything it took, and above the camp the first real rain of the monsoon came down like a verdict.
