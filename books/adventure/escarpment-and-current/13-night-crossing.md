## 13. Night Crossing

The key was small and brass and warm from Ines's pocket, and it weighed more than anything Hazel had ever carried.

"Tomas gave you this before—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. They both knew how the sentence ended, out there on the riverbank where Vane had made his lesson of a good man, and the rain filled in the silence like it had been waiting for an opening.

"Before he decided to be decent," Ines said. "Yes." She turned her face up into the downpour, letting it hide whatever her eyes were doing. "We should go."

They were crouched behind a stack of empty fuel drums at the edge of Vane's camp, forty meters from the workboat moored at the timber dock, and between them and the boat lay open ground, one bored sentry under a tarp shelter, and the worst weather the escarpment country had thrown at anyone in eleven years. The monsoon had come early and angry, as if the river itself had opinions about Concession 44.

Hazel checked his watch by feel, counting the seconds against the lightning. The storm had a rhythm. It always did. Big water taught you that — nothing out here was chaos, no matter how it looked from shore. Chaos was just a pattern you hadn't been patient enough to read.

"There," he said, when the thunder rolled long and fat over the ridge. "Every ninety seconds or so, we get about eight seconds of nobody hearing anything."

"You want to cross during the thunder."

"I want to cross during the thunder."

Ines looked at him through the rain, and even soaked to the bone and hunted and grieving, she managed the particular expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Eight seconds for forty meters."

"Fifty if we cut wide of the drums. So we don't do it in one run. We do it in three." He held up fingers. "Thunder one, we move to the winch housing. Thunder two, the piling. Thunder three, the dock. The sentry's got a radio but he won't use it in this — Vane chews people out for false alarms, and every man on this river tonight is half-deaf anyway."

"And if your count is wrong?"

"Then we swim for it, and I remind you I'm the professional."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her, brief and startled, gone as fast as it came. But it was there. Hazel clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a spar. Three days ago they'd been prisoners. Two days ago Tomas Reyes had pressed a brass key into Ines's palm with a handshake that lasted a heartbeat too long. Yesterday they'd watched him die for it. Tonight the arithmetic was simple: the key opened the fuel shed where Vane kept the fast workboat's ignition spare, and every hour they didn't run was an hour Vane's hunters closed the net.

"Thunder one," Hazel said.

They ran.

* * *

The workboat was called the *Buena Fe* — good faith — which Hazel took as either an omen or an insult, depending on how the night went. She was a flat-bottomed cargo skiff, maybe thirty feet, with a canopy over the stern and a hold full of Vane's own diesel drums, which struck Hazel as a poetry too perfect to pass up: they'd be running the gauntlet on the man's own fuel.

The key fit. Of course it fit. Tomas Reyes had been the expedition's engineer; he'd known exactly which lock to arm them with, and the thought of it — the cold, careful generosity of it — hit Hazel harder than the rain had all night. He turned the key, felt the tumblers give, and said nothing, because some things you carry quietly.

Ines cast off the lines while Hazel bled the engine. The motor caught on the third pull, coughed, and settled into a low diesel mutter that the storm swallowed whole. Good. A quiet engine in a screaming sky.

"Headlights?" Ines asked, climbing aboard.

"No lights. We run dark."

"We'll hit something."

"That's why God made rivers shallow on the inside bends." He eased the throttle forward and the *Buena Fe* slid away from the dock, and for one long moment Hazel waited for the shout, the flare, the crack of rifle fire — but the camp stayed blind behind them, drowned in its own noise, and then the current took the bow and the river had them.

He let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the riverbank.

"Don't celebrate," Ines said, reading him the way she read contour maps. "There are two checkpoints between here and the coastal registry. Maybe three, if Vane moved men after Tomas."

"He moved men. He moved everything. That's the point of killing a man in front of the whole camp — everybody works double shifts for a week." Hazel steered by memory and lightning, keeping the boat in the deep channel where the current ran smoothest. "But here's what he doesn't know. He thinks we're desperate. Desperate people run fast and stupid, straight down the middle, and his checkpoints are built for straight-down-the-middle. We're going to run slow and crooked."

"Crooked how?"

"Crooked like a diver." He grinned at her, and hoped the darkness hid how much effort the grin cost. "You'll see."

* * *

The first checkpoint announced itself an hour later as a pair of lanterns hung from a cable strung across the river — low, just above the waterline, the kind of thing you'd only see if you were running high and proud with your running lights on. Beyond it, on the near bank, a lean-to shelter and the orange coal-glow of a cook fire, and shapes moving under tarps. Four men, maybe five. A boat tied alongside, ready to pursue.

Hazel killed the engine a quarter mile upstream and let the *Buena Fe* drift.

"Here's the beautiful part," he whispered, working silently at the deck plates while Ines kept watch astern. "This river's carrying half the hillside right now. All that floodwater is full of logs — whole trees, stripped bark, traveling at six knots and invisible until they're beside you. Every boatman on this stretch knows it. Every checkpoint crew is terrified of it."

"So we're a log."

"We're worse than a log. We're a log with a plan." He'd found what he wanted: a length of polypropylene line coiled under the plates, and one of the diesel drums, still sealed. He lashed the drum to the line, checked the knot twice — knots were prayers, his father used to say, you tied them hoping somebody was listening — and set the drum over the side, paying out line slow so it wouldn't splash. The drum bobbed away downstream, black on black, riding the current dead center of the channel.

Then he steered the *Buena Fe* hard for the far bank, into the slack water along the cutbank, where the willows leaned out over the flood and the current barely breathed.

"What happens when they see the drum?" Ines asked.

"They'll see a floating barrel in flood season, which means one thing: a boat upstream lost its cargo, which means a boat upstream is in trouble, which means their whole job tonight just became rescue and salvage instead of sitting dry under a tarp." He watched the lanterns shrink astern. "And while they're busy being heroes—"

"We slide past in the dead water." Ines shook her head slowly. "You've done this before."

"Once. Off Veracruz, running a customs cordon with a hold full of engines that never met a tariff in their lives." He paused. "I was younger and the pay was better."

"And now?"

"Now I'm running a hold full of truth, which pays nothing and gets you shot." He shrugged. "Career trajectory's a mystery to me too."

The lanterns drifted past on their left, close enough now that Hazel could hear voices — sharp, alarmed, the cry going up as the drum swung into the light. Flashlight beams stabbed the water. An engine started on the bank, rough and urgent. And the *Buena Fe*, engine cold, riding the slack seam along the far bank, slid through the gap where the cable crossed overhead like a fish slipping a net.

Nobody fired. Nobody even looked their way. The luck was spending itself, Hazel knew — luck always spent fastest when you needed it most — but for tonight, the account still had credit.

* * *

They ran through the small hours with the storm easing from fury to sullen persistence, taking turns at the wheel while the other slept badly under the canopy. Around three, Ines appeared beside him with two cups of terrible coffee she'd brewed on a camp stove, and they drank it watching the escarpment loom and fade in the lightning like the shoulder of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"My grandfather worked this river," Hazel said, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the engine. "Timber runs, back when the companies still pretended the trees were infinite. He used to say the river keeps two ledgers. One for what you take, one for what you owe. And it balances them whenever it feels like it, and never when it's convenient."

"Cheerful man."

"Cheerful woman, my grandmother. She said the river doesn't keep ledgers at all, it just drowns whoever's slowest and the rest of us call it justice because the alternative is admitting it's random." He drained the coffee. "I've been trying to decide which of them was right for about thirty years."

Ines was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the canopy. "My mother used to say something like that. About archives, though. She was a librarian in Lagos before we moved. She said every archive is a confession — somebody wrote it down because they couldn't stand for it to be forgotten, and somebody else locked it away because they couldn't stand for it to be remembered." She looked downriver, toward the drowned vault they'd fled, toward the papers in the waterproof case lashed under Hazel's jacket. "Everything Brandt's firm buried in that vault — the forged permits, the deaths they papered over, the families they erased off the land rolls — it's all a confession. We're just the ones carrying it to the priest."

"Federal investigators as priests. There's a joke in there I'm too tired to find."

"Save it. You'll need material for the trial."

The word landed between them — *trial* — and neither of them picked it up. Trials meant witnesses. Witnesses meant surviving. Surviving meant the second checkpoint, and the third, and the rapids below the Narrows where the river dropped through a gap you could spit across, and every mile of it crawling with men paid to make sure a librarian's daughter and a smuggler with a conscience never testified about anything ever again.

Hazel checked the fuel gauge. Half. Vane's own diesel, burning Vane's own money to carry away Vane's own secrets. He allowed himself one small, private, entirely uncharitable smile.

* * *

The second checkpoint was the problem.

They smelled it before they saw it — woodsmoke where there should have been no woodsmoke — and then the lightning showed it to them all at once, cruel and clear: not a lean-to this time but a proper roadblock, a steel barge moored across half the channel with a crane arm and a chain boom lowered to the water, floodlights burning despite the rain, six men minimum, and Vane's lead hunter standing on the deck with binoculars, sweeping the river with the patience of a man who had all night and a payroll to justify.

Rafael Cruz. Hazel had seen him once before, across a campfire, and had spent the intervening days hoping never to see him again. Cruz didn't miss things. Cruz was the reason the first checkpoint had been soft — Vane put his best wolf at the last door.

Hazel eased the throttle back and turned to Ines, and found her already looking at him with the map-face on, the one that meant she was three moves ahead and disliked all of them.

"The Narrows," she said. "It's four miles above the checkpoint. The river drops maybe nine meters through the gap, and the flood has every rapid between here and the coast running white."

"You want to run the Narrows. At night. In a stolen thirty-foot cargo skiff. In a flood."

"I want to be upstream of Rafael Cruz when the sun comes up, and there is exactly one road there." Her voice was steady, but Hazel saw her hands, and her hands were not steady, and he loved her a little for the difference between the two. "Cruz will have boats below the checkpoint waiting for anyone who comes through. Above it, he's got nothing, because nobody in their right mind runs the Narrows in this."

"So we volunteer to be out of our minds."

"We volunteer to be the first ones through the door before it closes." She pointed upstream into the dark. "The flood peaks tomorrow morning. After that, the drop eases every hour. This is the window. You told me yourself — the river forgives nothing, but it also forgets everything. By noon, the Narrows will just be water again."

Hazel looked upstream, where the lightning showed the valley pinching tight between black walls of rock, and heard, beneath the rain, a sound he knew in his bones and his nightmares both: the long, continuous roar of a river being squeezed through stone, loud enough at four miles to feel in the deck plates.

His grandfather's ledger. His grandmother's dice. Somewhere between the two of them was a decision.

"Fuel's half," he said. "Boat is Vane's. Cargo is the truth. Crew is one surveyor who walks out of jungles and one diver who's already drowned once this month." He pushed the throttle forward and the *Buena Fe* swung her bow toward the roar. "In my experience that's either a disaster or a story."

"Which one?"

"Ask me in four miles."

Ahead, the escarpment walls closed in, and the river stood up on end, and the night crossing became something else entirely.
