## 14. Grounded

The storm did not break so much as it came apart, the way a man comes apart — all at once, and then in stages, each worse than the last.

Hazel had run rivers his whole life, but he had never run one like this. The stolen workboat was thirty feet of government surplus with an engine that had been new sometime during the previous administration, and it was carrying them down a channel that had doubled its flow since midnight. The escarpment country funneled every tributary, every gully, every square mile of rain into one brown throat, and they were going down that throat at eleven knots with Vane's boats somewhere behind them.

"Left!" Ines shouted over the engine.

He saw it a half-second too late — the sweep of current peeling off toward the far bank where the water went glassy and wrong. Glassy meant deep. Deep meant fast. He hauled the wheel left and the boat wallowed, shipped a ton of water across the transom, and straightened out just as the smooth water slid past close enough to touch.

"You could mention these things *before* they're abreast of us," Hazel called back.

"I mentioned it when I saw it. You're the one who's slow."

She was lashed to the console by a cargo strap she'd rigged herself, the archive case strapped beside her under a tarp, her arms wrapped around both like a woman holding a child and a grudge. Her face was gray with exhaustion. They had not slept. Neither of them had said so, because saying it would have made it true, and true things had a way of becoming fatal on this river.

The supplies were lashed amidships: fuel drums, food, the medical kit Tomas's key had bought them access to, rope, the tools of staying alive for however long it took to walk or float out of concession territory. It was not much. It was everything.

Behind them, through the rain, Hazel kept checking the rearview — a cracked oval mirror bolted to the console frame — and three times now he'd caught the flicker of running lights where running lights shouldn't be. Vane wasn't giving up. Vane had executed a man on a riverbank twelve hours ago as a lesson, and lessons like that don't get abandoned halfway through.

* * *

The rapids announced themselves the way bad news does — first as a rumor, then as a certainty, then as something you're standing in.

It was the sound that reached him first. Not the river's usual roar, which was everywhere and therefore nowhere, but a different note underneath it, a grinding, churning note like the river chewing stone. Hazel cut the throttle and listened, and what he heard made his stomach drop through the bilge and out the bottom of the hull.

"That's not a riffle," he said.

Ines was already leaning forward, reading the water the way she read maps — topographically, hungrily, looking for the shape beneath the surface. "There's a constriction ahead. Look — the banks pinch in past that point, and the water's piling up against it." She pointed at a line of white haze downstream where the rain seemed to be falling upward. "Escarpment country. The river drops here. Maybe ten feet, maybe twenty, all at once."

"Is there a portage?"

"On a night like this? With boats behind us?" She looked at him, and even soaked and shaking she managed to look offended by the question. "There's a line, Hazel. There's always a line. The question is whether this boat can hold together along it."

He ran the numbers the way he ran everything — quickly, honestly, without flattery. The boat drew two and a half feet. The channel ahead, if there was a channel, would be running maybe four feet over rock, in flood, in the dark, with debris coming down. Every instinct he owned said beach it, wait for light, wait for the water to crest and start to fall.

Every other instinct said Vane's boats were twenty minutes back and closing.

"We run it," he said. "Starboard side, along the seam where the current breaks. Stay in the green water."

"There is no green water. It's all chocolate."

"Then stay in the *less* chocolate water."

He brought the throttle up and drove her at the seam.

For ninety seconds it worked. Ninety seconds of the purest concentration Hazel had ever known — hands on the wheel, eyes reading the boils and V-lines, the boat surging under him like a living thing that wanted very badly to live. He felt the channel before he saw it, the way you feel stairs in the dark, and he turned her into it, and the river took them.

Then the log hit.

It came out of the dark on the port bow — a whole tree, root ball first, riding high on the flood — and there was nowhere to go. He shoved the wheel hard right and the trunk clipped them anyway, a glancing blow that should have been survivable, except the boat was old and the blow opened a seam that had been waiting forty years for an excuse.

Water came in the way water does: politely at first, then all at once.

"We're holed," Hazel said, with the strange calm of a man reporting someone else's disaster. "Forward compartment."

"How long?"

"If we keep moving? Longer than we need. If we stop?" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

They kept moving. The rapids took them the rest of the way — a quarter mile of chaos during which Hazel stopped being a pilot and became a kind of argument between physics and prayer. The boat struck rock twice more, spun once completely around, and shipped so much water that the fuel drums broke loose and began rolling the deck like barrels in a storm-tossed hold. Ines got a boot on one and held it, screaming something he couldn't hear over the roar, her grip on the archive strap never loosening.

And then the river simply ended.

Not gradually. The channel they were in dropped away into a hole — a pour-over, a ledge, the escarpment doing what escarpments do — and the Marisol's stolen sister-boat went over it nose-first with Hazel still holding a wheel that no longer controlled anything.

* * *

He came to on gravel.

That was the first mercy — gravel, not rock, a broad shelf of it below the falls where the river spread out shallow and furious for a hundred yards before gathering itself again. The second mercy was that he could count his own fingers when he held them up. The third was a sound: coughing, ragged and furious, twenty feet downstream.

"Ines!"

"Here." Another cough. "*Here.*"

She was on her hands and knees at the waterline, the archive case still strapped to her back, which should have drowned her and hadn't, because she had apparently decided at some point during the capsize that drowning was not on the schedule. She crawled up the gravel toward him, and when she reached dry stone she sat down abruptly, the way a marionette sits when someone cuts the strings.

"The boat?" she managed.

Hazel looked upstream. The workboat was hung on a midstream boulder, half-submerged, swinging slowly against its own wreckage. The supplies — the drums, the crates, the medical kit, the food, everything Tomas's key had bought them — were either gone downriver or scattered along the shallows like the leavings of a parade.

"Supplies," he said, and started walking.

They salvaged for an hour in the rain. It was grim arithmetic. A five-gallon jug of water, recovered. One crate of ration tins, half-soaked, recovered. A coil of rope. A machete. One waterproof pouch containing a flare pistol and two flares, which Hazel held up and regarded with the expression of a man counting coins at the end of a long night.

"No fuel," Ines reported from the wreck. She'd waded out to the boulder and come back empty-handed. "No medical kit. No radio — the radio's a cube of aluminum now." She sat down on the gravel beside him, dripping, and laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, only disbelief. "We had a boat. We had supplies. We had a plan. Twelve hours ago we had a plan."

"We still have a plan."

"Our plan was *downriver.*"

"It was downriver," Hazel agreed. "Now it's overland. Same direction. Worse transportation." He began coiling the rope with hands that shook and wouldn't stop, so he let them shake and coiled anyway. "You still have the map in your head?"

"I have the whole concession in my head. That was my job." She wiped rain and river out of her eyes. "There's a ranger track that follows the escarpment base. Old survey line. Two days' walk to the ferry crossing, if the ground's passable, and the ground won't be passable."

"Two days."

"Three, in this. And Hazel—" She stopped. He watched her decide whether to say the next thing, and watched her say it anyway, because Ines Okonkwo had never once in his company chosen the comfortable sentence over the true one. "Vane's boats can't run those rapids either. But they don't have to. There's a road on the far bank. He'll put men on the far bank and walk them down parallel to us. We'll hear them across the water tonight."

Hazel looked at the river — wide, brown, roaring, and utterly useless to them now. A wall on one side, hunters on the other, and miles of flooded jungle between them and anyone who might help.

"Rock bottom," he said.

"What?"

"My father used to say it. Rock bottom's not the worst place to be. It's the only place with nothing left to take." He cinched the rope over his shoulder, settled the machete at his hip, and slung the archive case across his chest where he could feel it against his heartbeat. "Everything else is uphill from here."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she stood, tested her legs, found them adequate, and picked up the ration crate.

"All right," she said. "Uphill. But Hazel — if I fall in this mud, you leave me. I mean it. The archive goes on without me."

"Noted," Hazel said. "I'll consider it."

"You'll *consider*—"

"I'm also considering leaving the rations and keeping you. You're the one who knows where we are." He started up the bank, into the trees, and after a moment he heard her follow, muttering something in two languages, neither of them complimentary.

Behind them, faint under the rain, from the far side of the river: engines. Then voices. Then a flashlight beam sweeping the water, patient as a searchlight, patient as a man who knew exactly how much ground there was to cover and exactly how long the night would last.

Hazel didn't look back. He put his hand flat on the archive case, felt the documents inside — the forged permits, the buried deaths, everything the river held — and walked into the dark.
