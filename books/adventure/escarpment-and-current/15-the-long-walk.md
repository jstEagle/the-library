## 15. The Long Walk

The rain had thinned to a fine gray mist by the time they left the river behind, and the concession territory opened around them like a country that had been waiting all along for someone foolish enough to walk it.

Ines went first, because she knew the ground. That was the arrangement they'd fallen into without ever discussing it: Hazel carried the archive in a waterproof duffel slung across his back like a man hauling his own coffin, and Ines picked the line through the bush, reading the terrain the way other people read a newspaper — quickly, cynically, with occasional contempt.

The land here was not kind and not cruel. It was simply indifferent, which was worse. Old logging cuts ran through the secondary growth like scars on a forearm, and the escarpment rose to their east, a long green wall dripping in the mist. Somewhere below them, hidden under the canopy, the flooded tunnels of the trading post were still swallowing the monsoon's runoff, still holding the drowned archive's outer rooms like a throat holding a swallowed coin.

They had the core cache. They had Tomas's key, which was now worthless as metal and priceless as evidence. And they had eleven miles of Brandt-controlled forest between themselves and the coastal registry, with Vane's boats somewhere behind them and Vane's money, presumably, everywhere else.

"Tell me about the ground," Hazel said, when they stopped at the top of a cut to breathe. He said it lightly, but Ines had learned in four days of knowing him that Hazel Delacroix did nothing lightly except joke, and even the jokes were load-bearing.

"Old survey grid," she said. "Brandt's firm cut these lines eight years ago when they were pretending this was a timber concession. The lines are on paper as firebreaks. On the ground they're roads for trucks that never came." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "We follow them until the escarpment forces us east, then we drop into the creek drainage. Two days if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we learn what the word *monsoon* meant to the people who named it."

Hazel grunted, which she'd also learned was his version of laughter, agreement, and despair, depending on pitch. This one had been despair.

They walked. The mist beaded on everything — on the broad leaves that shed it in cold sudden showers down Ines's collar, on the spiderwebs strung between saplings like the forest had been decorated overnight by something with too much time and no taste. Once, a troop of howlers opened up somewhere ahead of them, a sound like the end of the world being rehearsed, and Hazel actually smiled.

"I used to think that was a boat engine," he said. "First year on the Gulf. Heard that sound across the water and nearly called the coast guard."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I was being attacked by a very large outboard."

Ines laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh felt strange in her chest, like a key turning in a lock that hadn't been opened in years. She filed the feeling away and kept walking, because there was a thing she had to say, and every mile she let pass made it heavier.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a collapsed survey hut, its tin roof half-peeled back like the lid of a sardine can. Hazel built a small fire — small enough to hide under a tarp, because he was that kind of careful — and heated water for the instant noodles that constituted, at this point, roughly sixty percent of their diet by weight.

He ate in silence for a while. Then he said, without looking up, "You've been rehearsing something since the second hour of walking. I dive with men who do that. It never ends well."

Ines set down her noodles. The firelight moved on his face, and she thought: *he trusts you*. Not completely — he wasn't a fool — but functionally, structurally, the way you trust a rope you're hanging from. And ropes deserved to know what they were tied to.

"When I was twenty-four," she said, "I worked for Brandt."

She watched it land. Watched him go still in the particular way of a man in water who has just felt something brush his leg.

"The firm," she went on, before he could make it easy for either of them by interrupting. "Okonkwo & Associates, environmental compliance, Lagos office originally, then here. I was a junior surveyor. I was good at it. I was so proud of being good at it." She turned her hands over, looked at the palms as if the story might be written there. "My job was due diligence. Concession applications, land claims, impact assessments. I verified things. That was the whole romance of it, for me — that somewhere in the chain of paper between a corporation and a forest, there was one person whose job was to make sure the paper told the truth. Me."

"And it didn't," Hazel said quietly.

"The Ondo Delta application. 2016." She heard her own voice go flat and procedural, which was how she protected herself; she knew this and let it happen anyway. "There were three villages inside the proposed concession boundary. My field reports documented all three. Occupied. Farmed for generations. Under the law, occupied land couldn't be included without consent, and consent wasn't going to happen, because the company's previous operation upriver had poisoned a creek and called it an accident. I wrote all of it down. Photographs. Interviews. Signatures."

The fire popped. Down the slope, something small moved through the wet leaves and went still again.

"My supervisor thanked me for thorough work," Ines said. "And then the final application came back from the head office, and my villages weren't in it. The boundary had been redrawn. There was a new annex — a historical title search showing the land as *uninhabited at time of grant*, citing a colonial-era survey that turned out to have been done from a boat, in the dry season, when everyone was fishing the floodplain twelve miles away." She looked up. "I asked questions. I was told the annex came from a subcontractor. I asked who. I was invited to consider my career trajectory."

"You signed off anyway."

"I resigned." She said it fast, and hated how much it sounded like a defense. "I resigned and I told myself that was the clean thing. I walked away. I didn't sign the false report — I want that understood, Hazel, I never put my name on it. But I also didn't take it to anyone. Didn't go to the press, the ministry, the villages themselves. I told myself I'd lost the war and saved my integrity, and I took a job with a geological survey two countries away, and I slept fine." Her mouth twisted. "I slept *fine*."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way his hands were careful with gear. "The villages in the delta. What happened to them?"

"They were relocated," Ines said. "That's the official word. Relocated. One of them is now a gravel pit. Another one—" She stopped. Started again. "Another one resisted, and there was an incident involving security contractors, and three men died, and the file that recorded the incident passed through Brandt's document management system, where it was misfiled. Permanently. By a junior associate whose name I found six weeks ago in the vault, in the correspondence, in Marcus Vane's own hand thanking the firm for its discretion."

"And that junior associate—"

"Wasn't me. I was gone by then." She held his eyes. "But it was someone exactly like me. Someone young, and good at their work, and tired, and scared, who decided that one signature was survivable and silence was professional. That's the inheritance, Hazel. That's what gets handed down in firms like that, along with the letterhead. Not the archive. The *burying* of the archive. Each generation trains the next one how to lose things properly."

Hazel got up. Not angrily — that was almost worse. He just stood, and walked to the edge of the tarp, and looked out into the dark where the escarpment would be if there were light to see it, and Ines sat with her cooling noodles and waited for the verdict.

When he finally turned around, his face was unreadable in the firelight, and she realized she'd been wrong about something important: she'd assumed the hard part was confessing. The hard part was this — the space after, where you couldn't control what the other person did with what you'd given them.

"The wreck," he said. "The case with the concession number. The navy boats shadowing my barge. The lawyer offering triple pay." He ticked them off on his fingers, slow. "You knew the name Brandt before I ever said it, didn't you. Back on the Marisol, when I told you who hired me. You flinched. I thought it was the rain."

"Yes."

"And you didn't say anything."

"No."

"Why?" And then, before she could answer, harder: "No. Let me guess. Because you needed the ride. Because I was the only boat running the window. Because telling me might have cost you your way upstream." He shook his head, and there it was — not rage, but the specific hurt of a man doing arithmetic. "You used me the same way Brandt uses people. As a vehicle. Something that moves the problem from one place to another and doesn't ask what's inside."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It probably isn't. Fair's for people who aren't carrying a dead man's key and a bag full of somebody's crimes through a jungle at night." He crouched, fed a stick to the fire, watched it catch. "Tomas. Did you know? When he slipped you the key — did you know what he saw when he looked at you?"

Ines's throat tightened. "I think he saw someone who quit once," she said. "And hoped I'd learned something worth the risk."

"And did you?"

"I'm here, aren't I."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have that isn't a lie." She stood too, now, because sitting felt like pleading. "You want a clean ally, Hazel. I understand. I'd want one too. But nobody in this story is clean — not you, pocketing that case instead of declaring it; not me; not the river families who've been running guns against the concession since before either of us was born. The only people clean in this are dead or paid. What I am is *useful*, and *sorry*, and the only person alive who can read the colonial script in that archive well enough to make it testify. So you can leave me here with my guilt and my compass, and walk eleven miles alone through Brandt's forest with a bag on your back and no idea which documents matter. Or you can keep the rope."

The words hung in the steam of the fire. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered — the monsoon clearing its throat, checking whether anyone was still listening.

Hazel looked at her for a long time. Then he did something she didn't expect: he laughed, short and rough, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"You know what my mother used to say?" he said. "'A salvage man finds what other people sink. Don't act surprised when some of it bites.'" He dropped his hands. "I pocketed that case, Ines. First chance I had, I stole government-adjacent property off a dead man's wreck because I smelled money. So no, I don't get to stand here demanding clean. I get to decide who I trust with my life, and apparently my type is liars with good field skills."

"Apparently."

"Don't push it." He sat back down and nudged her cold noodles toward her. "Eat. We move at first light, and I want you fresh enough to read dead lawyers' handwriting."

Ines sat. For a while neither of them spoke, and the fire burned down to a red eye, and the mist came through the peeled roof in threads. It wasn't forgiveness. She knew that. Forgiveness would take longer than a night and more than a confession, and might never arrive at all. But he'd kept the rope, and tonight that was the whole of the law.

"Hazel," she said, as he banked the fire. "There's one more thing. About the archive. Why I really stayed at the camp those first weeks instead of running."

He paused. "I'm listening."

"The vault wasn't just correspondence and forged permits. The core cache — the part you dove for — it indexes the original land grants. Colonial-era. Which means it doesn't just prove what Brandt did." She pulled her knees up, watching the last coal breathe. "It proves what was stolen *before* Brandt. From the river families. From the villages. Title, all the way down, generation after generation, each theft buried under the last one like sediment." She looked at him across the dying fire. "This isn't one crime, Hazel. It's a geology of them. And tomorrow, when we cross the old survey lines and drop into the creek drainage — we won't be the first people hiding in that forest. The families use those same paths. And if we meet them, they're going to want to know why a stranger is carrying their history in a duffel bag."

Hazel was quiet. Then: "And what do we tell them?"

Ines reached into her shirt and drew out the folded photocopy she'd taken from the vault's outer room weeks ago, before Vane sealed everything — the page she'd shown no one, the page that had started all of this. She smoothed it flat on her knee in the coal-light: a grant deed, 1911, a signature, a river's old name.

"We tell them the truth," she said. "Starting with mine."

Down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, an engine turned over — once, twice, then caught and settled into a low idle, somewhere on the truck roads that weren't supposed to exist.

Hazel's head came up. Ines was already reaching for the map.

"Not first light," he murmured. "Now."
