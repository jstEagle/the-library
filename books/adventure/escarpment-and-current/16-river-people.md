## 16. River People

The woman who found them was mending a net on her porch when Hazel and Ines stumbled out of the treeline, and she did not scream, did not reach for a gun, did not do any of the things Hazel had braced himself for over three days of walking. She simply looked up, took in their state — Hazel's bandaged forearm, Ines's ruined boots, the oilcloth bundle strapped to his back like a second spine — and said, in the tone of a woman commenting on weather:

"You're the ones the money's looking for."

Hazel stopped dead. "What money?"

"Five hundred a name." She went back to her net. "More if you bring papers. My cousin at the ferry bend turned down four hundred Tuesday. He says hello, by the way. He says you owe him a ferry ride."

Ines made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the last of her strength leaving through her mouth. Hazel put a hand under her elbow and steered her toward the porch steps.

"Ma'am," he said, "we've got no money and less sense, but we'd be obliged for a floor to fall asleep on."

The woman bit her thread, tied it off, and studied them both with the unhurried assessment of someone who had spent sixty years reading water and found people considerably easier.

"Floor I can do," she said. "Names come later."

* * *

Her name was Rosa Almeida, and the house belonged to her late husband, which meant it belonged to her, which meant — as she explained over the first of what would become many cups of bitter river coffee — that it had been in her family since before there was a concession number 44, since before there were surveyors or permits or men named Brandt drawing lines on maps in air-conditioned offices a thousand miles from anything that mattered.

"The river gave us this ground," she said. "Then some government in the capital sold it twice. Then Brandt bought both sales and burned one." She shrugged. "We are used to being an afterthought. We are not used to being worth five hundred dollars apiece. It is flattering, almost."

They had come ashore two days earlier, after the rapids took the stolen workboat and most of Vane's supplies with it, and walked south along the high ground with the archive wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to Hazel's pack. The knife wound in his forearm had gone from angry to sullen; Ines had cleaned and stitched it by firelight with hands steadier than his own, and now Rosa's granddaughter — a girl of maybe fourteen called Marisol, which had made Hazel laugh until it hurt — changed the dressing every morning with clinical disapproval.

"You let it go septic," she informed him on the second day. "My grandmother says divers are half fish and half fool. She is generous about the fish part."

"I like her," Hazel told Ines.

"Everyone likes her. That's how she gets away with it."

The settlement itself was less a village than an argument the river hadn't settled yet: eleven houses on stilts along a backwater slough, connected by walkways that flooded every season and were rebuilt every season, because — as Rosa put it — "the land is ours and the water is ours and they take turns." There were no roads in. There was one boat channel, narrow and crooked, that a stranger could not run without grounding, and the families who lived here had grounded strangers on purpose for three generations.

It was, Hazel thought, the first place since the wreck site where he had slept without one ear open.

He lasted two nights before the ear came back.

* * *

It was the third evening when Rosa set down her coffee, looked at the oilcloth bundle leaning against the wall where Hazel always kept it in arm's reach, and said:

"Open it."

Hazel didn't move. "Why?"

"Because my son-in-law says men have died for whatever is in there, and I am too old to be curious politely." She folded her hands. "Also because if it is what I think it is, you should know whose land you have been carrying through the jungle."

He glanced at Ines. She gave him the small nod she'd learned to give him — the one that meant *I've done the arithmetic and we don't have a better option* — and together they unwrapped the archive on Rosa's table.

Water had gotten to some of it during the dive and the rapids; the edges of the older documents had bloomed into soft gray lace. But the core cache — the sealed tin boxes Vane had been willing to kill for — had held. Inside were ledgers, correspondence, and maps. Colonial-era surveys, company records from a trading concern that had operated out of the old post upstream, and beneath all of it, in a folder stamped with a firm name Hazel now knew better than his own birthday, the modern paperwork: the forged concession instruments, the buried incident reports, the payments.

Rosa read slowly, her lips moving, her granddaughter reading over her shoulder and translating the old script into whispers. The room filled with the rest of the family by degrees, the way rooms fill with family — nobody announcing themselves, everybody simply arriving.

After a long while, Rosa touched one page with one finger. A survey map, dated nearly ninety years back, showing parcel boundaries along forty miles of river.

"This bend," she said. "This is us. This slough, these houses, the fishing grounds past the point." Her finger moved. "And this — this is the Ferreira ground, upstream. And this is where the Ochoas were, before." She looked up. "Before what?"

Ines answered, because Hazel couldn't. "Before the company moved them off. There's a ledger entry. Compensation paid —" she checked the page — "eleven pesos per family. To a company agent, not to the families. The receipt is signed by the same man who countersigned the original concession."

The silence went on long enough that Hazel heard the river working under the floorboards, patient as ever.

"My grandfather," Rosa said finally, "always said the paper would come back someday. He said paper is like driftwood. It goes downstream, but it doesn't disappear." She closed the folder with unexpected gentleness, the way you close a coffin or a Bible. "You have been carrying our grandfather's driftwood, diver."

"We were carrying it to federal investigators," Hazel said. "That was the plan."

"And now?"

"Now it's heavier than it was."

Rosa smiled, and it transformed her whole face into something younger and harder. "Good. Heavy things are worth the carrying."

* * *

The trouble with warmth, Hazel had learned over a lifetime on the water, was that it made you want to stay, and staying was how people got caught. On the fourth morning, Rosa's son-in-law Emilio came back from the fishing grounds with news that turned the coffee sour in everyone's cup.

"They were at the Ochoa landing yesterday," he reported. "Two boats, city clothes, a lawyer type doing the talking. Offering money for information. Not just about you two — about anyone who helps you. And offering more than money to anyone willing to sign a statement." He sat down heavily. "Old Ferreira's grandson took two hundred dollars to say he saw nothing. Took it right there on the dock, in front of everyone."

"Took it and then said what?" Hazel asked.

"Said he saw nothing." Emilio's mouth twisted. "Which is true, because he wasn't there. But the paper doesn't care if it's true. The paper just wants his name on it saying the strangers never passed through, so when the story comes out wrong, his name is on the wrong side of it."

"They're building deniability," Ines said quietly. "Buying witnesses along every bend so that when the archive surfaces, there's a fog of paid testimony around it. Standard practice. I've seen it done." She caught Hazel's look. "From the inside. Yes. I know."

Hazel had been turning something over since the second night, and he set it on the table now, next to the coffee pot.

"How many families on this stretch of river?" he asked.

"Counting the Ochoa remnant and the Ferreira line? Thirty-some households," Emilio said. "Why?"

"Because Brandt's buying witnesses one at a time, two hundred dollars at a time, and he can afford to buy every single one of you and still make his margin on the concession." Hazel tapped the folder. "But he can't afford thirty households telling the same story to the same investigators on the same week. Testimony's only cheap when it's scattered. Bunched up, it's expensive."

"You want us to go to the investigators," Rosa said.

"I want you to *already be there* when we get there. Or send your own people, your own statements, your own maps — the ones from the archive copied out fair, with your names on them as the living claimants. Because right now Ines and me, we're two fugitives with a bundle of old paper and a story nobody's paid to believe. But thirty families whose grandfathers' land is named in those ledgers—" He spread his hands. "That's not a story. That's a case."

Emilio looked at Rosa. Rosa looked at the map on the table, at the bend marked with her family's name in faded ink, and Hazel watched sixty years of patience decide it had been patient enough.

"My brother-in-law has a radio," she said. "Shortwave. The concession men think we're too poor for radios." Her smile came back, the hard young one. "Poor, yes. Deaf, no."

* * *

They spent two days copying. Ines worked the documents with a surveyor's precision, reproducing the key pages in careful hand onto whatever paper the settlement could spare, while Marisol — who wrote a prettier hand than anyone, a fact she mentioned often — lettered the headers. Hazel sat with Emilio and drew the river from memory: every checkpoint Vane's men had manned on the way up, every fuel cache, the narrows where the pursuit boat had sunk, the depths and currents he knew the way other men knew their mothers' faces.

"That's worth money, a chart like that," Emilio said, watching him work.

"It's worth more as a gift," Hazel said. "Ask me why sometime when there's time."

"Why?"

"Because everything I've ever sold ended up belonging to somebody worse than me."

Emilio considered that, then nodded once, the way river people nodded — economically, like everything else they did.

On the second night, the radio crackled its coded message downriver to the Ochoa landing and the Ferreira house and a church past the point whose pastor owed Rosa a favor dating back to a flood nobody talked about. By dawn, the plan was moving without Hazel having to steer it, which struck him as either miraculous or terrifying, depending on how much coffee he'd had.

It was Marisol, changing his dressing that final morning, who brought the last piece of news, delivered with the cheerful brutality of the very young.

"Papa says the lawyer man came back to the Ochoa landing. With soldiers this time. Real ones, or dressed like it." She smoothed the bandage. "He asked for you by description. Diver, tall, bad arm. And the lady surveyor." She looked up at him. "Papa says they're coming up the slough tomorrow. Papa says you should be gone tonight."

Hazel flexed his fingers against the table. Beside him, Ines was already repacking the archive with movements gone quick and sure.

"Rosa," he called across the room. "How fast can your brother-in-law's boat run this slough at night?"

Rosa didn't look up from the net she was mending, but her hands paused, just for a moment, and her answer came dry as August.

"Faster than soldiers can march," she said. "Slower than trouble travels. Which means, diver, you'd best leave before trouble finishes its supper."

Outside, the first fat drops of the evening rain began ticking against the tin roof, and somewhere downriver, Hazel knew, the current was already carrying word of them faster than any boat could follow — toward the coast, toward the registry, toward whatever Brandt was preparing to sign.
