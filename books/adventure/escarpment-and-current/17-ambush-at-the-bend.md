## 17. Ambush at the Bend

The ferry crossing announced itself before they saw it — the groan of a cable strung bank to bank, the flat slap of water against a pilings platform gone soft with rot. Hazel crouched at the edge of the cane and studied it the way he'd study any piece of water before committing his body to it.

"Old hand-pull ferry," he said. "Cable's still rigged. Somebody's been keeping it oiled, which means somebody's still using it."

"Or it's a trap," Ines said.

"Everything's a trap. The question is whether it's a trap for us or for somebody else." He wiped sweat and river spray off his face with the back of his wrist. Three days of walking concession territory had worn them down to a kind of moving exhaustion, the kind where your legs kept going on a contract your brain hadn't signed. The archive rode against Ines's back in a waterproof survey case, wrapped in a tarp, tied with the kind of knots Hazel tied when he expected to be underwater when something came loose.

They'd been lucky. He knew better than to say it out loud, but he knew. Luck was like air in a tank — you didn't celebrate it, you just watched the gauge.

They crossed at midmorning, the two of them hauling the ferry hand over hand along the sagging cable, the brown current pushing at the flat-bottomed platform like a dog leaning on a fence. Ines pulled with the steady rhythm of somebody who'd grown up hauling things; Hazel pulled with the rhythm of somebody who'd spent thirty years hauling himself up anchor lines. Halfway across, a kingfisher detonated off the far bank, electric blue, screaming its opinion of them, and Ines actually laughed — a short, cracked, real laugh.

"That's the first time I've heard you do that," Hazel said.

"Don't get used to it. It's a stress response."

"Most of mine are too."

They came down the far bank into a stretch of village — four houses on stilts, a drying rack, a dog that considered them and decided against barking. An old woman on a porch looked at them for a long moment, then looked at the river behind them, then held up two fingers and pointed downstream.

Two boats. Two days ago. Or two hours. The language of the river was efficient that way.

Hazel pressed a folded bill into her hand — not much, but the river families had fed them the night before and refused payment, and pride only stretched so far. The old woman folded the bill away with the speed of long practice and then, unexpectedly, pressed something back: a mango, small and hard as a fist, warm from her lap.

"For the road," Ines translated, though the woman hadn't spoken.

Hazel put the mango in his shirt pocket like a talisman. He'd learned a long time ago that the river gave you small things right before it took large ones. It was a system. It kept its books balanced.

* * *

They heard the outboard an hour later, and Hazel's stomach dropped through the soles of his boots, because there was no outboard that should be on this stretch — the river families ran longtails and paddled when they could, and the concession boats had gone quiet since the storm, conserving fuel. This engine had the tuned, hungry note of a boat that had been maintained by somebody who expected to chase things.

"Off the bank," Hazel hissed, and they went into the cane, but the sound didn't come closer. It circled. It was ahead of them, not behind — and then it cut, and the silence afterward was worse.

"He's herding," Ines said. "He's been herding us since the rapids."

Hazel had to give the man credit. The lead hunter — the one the river families called *el Compadre*, though nobody could say whether it was a name or a joke — didn't chase. He closed. He moved like a man folding a map, creasing the territory down around his quarry, and every crease brought them closer to water Hazel knew and he didn't.

The bend came at midafternoon. The river swung hard against a bluff, and on the bluff's shoulder sat the ferry crossing's upper landing — a second platform, a winch house, and the great iron stanchion where the cable anchored, sunk into a concrete footing at the waterline. Below the landing, the current piled up against the bluff in a long, glassy, murderous eddy line, and beneath that eddy, Hazel knew from thirty years of reading water, the bottom would be scoured to clay and old iron.

The boat sat at the landing. Aluminum hull, concession gray, a .30-caliber mount with the gun absent and the mount rusting, and a man standing in the bow with a rifle held in the easy way of somebody who'd never had to run with one.

*El Compadre* himself stood on the platform. He was smaller than Hazel had imagined — a compact, neat man in a concession polo shirt gone soft with washing, with the patient face of a bank manager and eyes that had nothing banklike in them at all.

"Hazel Delacroix," he said, in English with the accent worn smooth. "The diver. I've been looking forward to this. Mr. Vane speaks of you like weather."

"You can tell him the forecast changed," Hazel said.

The man smiled. "I have a boat. You have legs. The river is high and the monsoon is not finished with us. I would like very much for you to come aboard and we can discuss the case the lady is carrying. Mr. Vane is prepared to be generous. He is, I have found, generous with everyone except the people he needs something from, and you two are exactly that."

"And if we say no?"

"Then I shoot the lady first, because Mr. Vane wants you functional and has no such instructions regarding her, and then you will come aboard anyway, and we will have wasted a very nice afternoon."

Hazel's mind did the thing it did when the water got deep — went quiet, went cold, went to work. Behind *el Compadre*, the second man shifted his weight in the bow, and the boat rocked, and the rocking told Hazel what he needed to know: the boat was tied off on the cable stanchion, a line looped around the iron, and the current was working that line the way the current worked everything, patiently, forever.

And Hazel knew — the way he knew things about water, the way other men knew things about their wives — what a ferry stanchion looked like after sixty years of monsoons. The concrete footing would be honeycombed. The iron would be sound, but the concrete around it would be a rotting tooth in a soft gum. And a boat pulling against that anchor, in high water, at the wrong angle, with a little help —

"You're thinking," *el Compadre* observed. "I can see it. It's in your face, around the eyes. Divers have that look. My advice is don't."

"Good advice," Hazel said. "I've never taken any."

He moved. Not toward the man — toward the water.

It was the one thing nobody ever expected a man to do on purpose. He went off the landing's edge in a flat dive, the brown river closing over him like a door, and the cold of it squeezed his chest the way it always did, the old friend and old enemy. The current took him instantly. He let it. He'd spent his life letting rivers move him where he needed to go, and this one carried him down and back, under the platform, into the shadowed scour channel beneath the stanchion.

He found the footing by feel. His hands walked over concrete gone rough as coral, and then into the honeycomb — pockets of erosion, deep as his forearm, the monsoon's sixty-year signature. The anchor iron rose out of it thick as a man's thigh, and around the iron's base the concrete had cracked into a star, and in the star's cracks the current had planted its fingers and pulled, year after year, the way it pulled everything.

The boat's mooring line ran down past him, taut, humming, tied off above. The geometry was simple and beautiful: the boat pulling downstream against the stanchion, the stanchion held in the bank by rotten concrete, the whole arrangement a lever waiting for a fulcrum.

Hazel had no tools. He had a knife on his belt, a shirt pocket with a mango in it, and thirty years of knowing exactly how much force a structure could refuse before it stopped refusing.

He got his shoulder against the iron, found the crack with his boots, and pushed.

Nothing. Of course nothing. He adjusted, braced against the scour wall, got his whole back into it, and pushed again, and felt the faint, gritty *shift* of a crack widening by a millimeter. It was going to work, and it was going to take everything, and the air in his lungs was already complaining.

Above, muffled by water, a rifle shot. Then another. His chest seized — *Ines* — and the fury of it went straight into his arms. He pushed until his vision sparked, and the crack gave a long, grinding sigh, and a slab of concrete the size of a door sloughed away into the scour channel and was gone.

The iron leaned. The cable above it sang one low, mournful note, a sound Hazel felt in his teeth, and then the whole stanchion — iron, cable, and sixty years of patient erosion — let go.

The river did the rest. It was, Hazel thought in the last clear corner of his mind, the most honest thing he'd ever been part of. No malice in it. Just physics, finally paid in full.

The ferry cable came down across the water like a whip taking up slack, and the moored boat — still tied to the stanchion — was yanked sideways off the landing with a sound like God slamming a truck door. He heard it even through the water, felt it in his ribs, the hull hitting the current broadside, and then the current, which does not negotiate, took the boat under the eddy line and rolled it.

Hazel surfaced thirty yards downstream in the boil behind the bluff, gasping, deafened, alive.

* * *

The fight, when he reached it, was nearly over.

The boat lay on its side in the shallows below the crossing, half-sunk, the current combing through it. One man was gone — swept, or swimming, or smart enough to have gone to ground. The second, the one from the bow, was in the water at the platform's base, dragging himself up the pilings with one hand, rifle lost, and *el Compadre* was on the platform itself, soaked to the collar — he'd been on the landing when the cable came down and had nearly gone with it — and he was not holding a rifle anymore.

He was holding a knife, and he was holding Ines by the hair.

Hazel came out of the water at the platform's ladder, and everything after that happened in the space of about four seconds, which is how long most important things take.

He came up the ladder low and fast, and *el Compadre* turned — the man was quick, Hazel would give him that forever — and the knife came around in a short, professional arc that opened Hazel's forearm from elbow to wrist in a line of cold that turned hot a half-second later. Hazel didn't feel it yet. He knew he would. He'd been cut before; the body always sent the bill late.

He got one hand on the man's wrist and one on his collar, and he did the only thing he knew, the thing the river had been teaching him since he was nine years old: he used the other man's balance against him. *El Compadre* was standing on a platform canted at fifteen degrees with a snapped cable whipping loose beside him, and his footing was a rumor. Hazel pulled him forward and down, and they went onto the planks together, and the knife skittered, and Ines — Ines, who had a knot of hair torn loose and blood on her scalp and the survey case still strapped to her back like she'd been born with it — kicked the knife off the platform into the river with the calm precision of a woman closing a door on a bad year.

Then she put her boot on *el Compadre*'s chest, and she looked down at him, and she said, in Spanish, something long and quiet and absolutely unhurried, and whatever it was, the man went pale under his tan and stopped moving entirely.

"What did you tell him?" Hazel asked, holding his arm.

"I told him what happens to men who work for Brandt when the concession falls," she said. "I told him the registry files already have his name in them. I may have improvised the part about the files." She looked at Hazel's arm and her face changed. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You are bleeding from a wound shaped like a decision you made without consulting me. Sit. Down."

He sat. The planks were warm and the river roared past and his arm had begun, now, to send its bill — a deep, throbbing, entirely sincere pain. Ines cut his sleeve away with the knife she'd fished back from where it had caught on a piling, and cleaned the cut with river water and the last of their antiseptic, and bound it with strips of his own shirt, her hands quick and rough and, underneath the roughness, shaking just slightly.

"You pushed over a ferry stanchion," she said. "Underwater. With your shoulder. Like a crazy person."

"The concrete was already rotten."

"You pushed over a *building*."

"It was a small building."

She tied off the bandage, and then, because she was Ines, she put her hand flat on his chest for exactly two seconds — a check, a calibration, a thing done and put away — and then went to see what the river had left them.

It had left them quite a lot. The sunken boat's cargo had floated free: a fuel drum, a crate of canned food, a first-aid kit better than theirs, a coil of rope, and — Ines hauled it up the bank with a whoop that startled the kingfisher — an outboard motor in a waterproof crate, unmarked, new, and full of fuel.

And, lashed under the gunwale where the current hadn't reached, a canvas map case. Ines opened it, went very still, and turned it toward Hazel.

It was a chart of the river below the crossing. Marked on it, in fresh pencil, were the checkpoints. All of them. Every one Vane's men had run, with times, rotations, and — circled twice, with a note in a neat hand — the location of the registry office at the coast, and the date of the transfer signing.

Three days from now.

"He wasn't just hunting us," Ines said softly. "He was carrying the schedule. Vane sent his own timeline downriver in a boat with two men on it, because he thought this stretch was already closed." She laughed, the cracked laugh again, and this time Hazel joined her, and it hurt his arm, and he didn't care.

*El Compadre* was gone. They found his tracks on the far bank, heading inland, fast — a smart man, a survivor, a man who'd just done the arithmetic on his own name in a registry file. Hazel didn't wish him harm. He wished him a long career of remembering.

They worked until dark. Hazel, one-armed and directing, watched Ines mount the outboard on the old ferry platform's support skiff — a flat-bottomed thing the river families had clearly abandoned but the river had not — and by full dark they had a boat, fuel, food, a chart of every trap between them and the coast, and a deadline.

Hazel sat on the bank with his arm in a sling made of shirt, eating the old woman's mango with his good hand. It was hard as a coconut and sweeter than anything he'd ever tasted.

"Three days," Ines said, looking downriver at the dark.

"Two and a half, if the current's kind."

"The current is never kind."

"No," Hazel agreed. "But it's honest." He looked at the bandage on his arm, at the boat riding at its new line, at the woman who'd kicked a knife into a river without raising her voice. "We'll make it."

Downstream, around the first bend, a light blinked twice and went dark. Somebody had seen the cable fall. Somebody was already telling somebody else. The river was keeping its books, and the news was traveling faster than any boat.

Ines saw it too. "That's a checkpoint signal," she said. "They know."

"Then we'd better be gone before they finish knowing."

They pushed the skiff out into the current, and the river took them the way it took everything — without malice, without mercy, and without waiting.
