## 18. Permits Expire

The coastal registry smelled of mildew and old ambition — the particular odor of a building where important things had been filed badly for a hundred years and everyone had agreed not to notice.

Ines stood at the counter with her hands flat on the scarred wood and her heart doing something she refused to call panic. Beside her, Hazel dripped. Not dramatically — he'd made an effort, wringing out his shirt in the alley behind the bus depot before they came in — but the man shed water the way other people shed dandruff. A slow, persistent leak. There was a dark crescent forming on the tile beneath his boots that a clerk was already eyeing with the weary hostility of someone who had mopped this floor through three administrations.

"You're dripping on government property," the clerk said.

"Am I," Hazel said. It wasn't a question so much as a dare.

Ines stepped in before it became an incident. They had walked out of the concession territory with nothing but the archive wrapped in oilcloth inside a rice sack, two hundred miles of river behind them, one knife wound in Hazel's side going septic-adjacent, and exactly enough money between them for bus fare and bad coffee. She was not going to let him get ejected from the registry over floor tiles.

"Sir," she said, deploying the voice she'd used for eight years in corporate conference rooms, the one that made men assume she was about to agree with them right up until she didn't, "we need to see the concession filings for Concession 44. Timber and mineral extraction, upper Río Serrano basin. Filed originally under Delgado Expeditionary Services, transferred to—"

"I know what it's transferred to," the clerk said, and something in his tone shifted. He looked at Hazel's drip. He looked at Ines's face — which she knew, from long practice, was arranged into pleasant blankness. He looked at the rice sack. "Everyone asks about 44 lately."

"Lately," Ines repeated.

The clerk — his nameplate said R. FONSECA, though half the letters had been picked off by some previous employee with opinions — lowered his voice to the register of a man who has decided, against all institutional training, to be helpful. "Three times this month. Lawyers. Nice suits. The kind of nice you can't afford on a lawyer's salary unless somebody's paying extra." He tapped the counter twice. "You want the public file or the real file?"

Ines felt Hazel go still beside her, the way he went still in the water when something large moved below.

"The real file," she said.

* * *

Fonseca led them back through the stacks, past shelves of ledger books swollen with humidity, past a wall map of the coast from 1961 that showed rivers which no longer existed and omitted one that very much did. He stopped at a cabinet marked PENDING — TRANSFERS and unlocked it with a key from a ring of perhaps forty, selecting it without looking, the way Hazel selected dive weights.

"Here." He pulled a folder thick as a Bible and dropped it on a reading table. "Transfer petition. Concession 44, from Delgado Expeditionary Services — which is defunct, by the way, dissolved six weeks ago, neat as you please — to Meridian Gulf Resources LLC. Signature authority: A. Brandt, counsel. Hearing date is—" he flipped to the last page, "—Thursday. Four days from now."

Ines opened the folder and the world narrowed to paper.

She had spent her career making documents like these. That was the thing nobody understood about surveying, about concessions, about the whole glittering machinery of extraction: it ran on paperwork. Every acre stolen began its life as a form filled out correctly. She read the transfer petition the way she'd once read seismic charts — fast, then slow, then again, hunting the seam.

And there it was. Page nine. *Chain of title verification, appendix C.* A certification, signed and stamped, attesting that the original concession grant of nineteen years ago had been "executed with full compliance under applicable colonial-era land statutes, including provisions governing submerged and riparian holdings."

Submerged and riparian holdings.

"That's it," she said quietly. "That's the whole ballgame."

Hazel leaned over her shoulder. He smelled of river and iodine. "Translate for the diver."

"The original concession was never valid." She kept her voice low, clinical, because if she let it rise it would crack. "The colonial statute it cites — Article 61 — covers submerged archives, state records, anything underwater that belonged to the old administration. It was repealed before the grant was ever signed. Brandt's firm cited a dead law to claim land that includes the vault. And this appendix certifies the citation as compliant." She tapped the signature block. "Once this transfers to Meridian Gulf, the chain of title is laundered. Clean. Every document we're carrying becomes a historical curiosity instead of evidence. They won't even need to kill us after Thursday. We'll just be crazy people with a sack of old paper."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Then: "Thursday. Today's Sunday."

"Today is Sunday."

"So four days."

"Four days, minus however long it takes federal investigators to care about anything." She closed the folder gently, the way you close a coffin. "Which, historically, is longer than four days."

Fonseca had drifted back, ostensibly to reshelve something, actually to listen. Now he cleared his throat. "There's a problem with your theory," he said, "that isn't a problem, if you follow me."

"I follow you," Ines said.

"The hearing requires a certified copy of the appendix to be posted for public comment. Five business days' posting." He smiled thinly. "It was posted eleven days ago. Nobody commented. But the posting record — who viewed it, when, the registry log — that's a public record too." He looked at her with the flat, tired eyes of a man who had watched this machine work for thirty years and had recently begun keeping receipts. "I'm not saying anyone viewed it who shouldn't have. I'm saying the log exists. And logs are funny things. They remember everything and apologize for nothing."

Ines looked at him. "Why are you telling us this?"

Fonseca shrugged, reshelved a book that didn't need reshelving. "My father fished the Serrano. Before the concession, before the checkpoints, before the men with guns told his village the river belonged to a company in a city he couldn't find on a map." He slid the key ring into his pocket. "I file papers. It's not much. But I know which papers matter."

* * *

They took the folder's contents to a café two streets up — the kind of place with plastic chairs, a ceiling fan turning at the speed of continental drift, and coffee that could strip paint. Hazel ordered four cups anyway. Ines spread the photocopied pages across their table and built the case aloud, because saying it out loud was the only way she trusted it.

"Brandt forged the original citation. We have proof — the vault documents show the correspondence, the internal memos, the buried deaths at the trading post. Vane sealed the site and armed his men to protect the physical archive. And now Brandt files a legal transfer to make the forgery moot." She lined the pages up like dominoes. "It's elegant. Honestly, it's beautiful. If I weren't the one being erased by it, I'd admire it."

"You did help build machines like this once," Hazel said. It wasn't cruel. That was somehow worse.

"Yes." She held his gaze. "I did. Which is why I know exactly how it breaks."

"And how's that?"

"Bureaucracy only respects bigger bureaucracy." She pulled out the registry log Fonseca had copied for them — a single sheet showing the viewing history of the public posting. Three entries. Two were lawyers from Meridian Gulf. The third was dated nine days ago, a name she'd had to sit down when she saw it: *M. Vane, authorized site representative.*

"He came here himself," she said. "Nine days ago. While we were walking out of the jungle. He checked the posting, saw the hearing date, and reported back. That's why they chased us so hard downriver — not just the archive. The clock. They needed us gone before anyone could connect the vault documents to this filing." She tapped Vane's entry. "But he signed the registry log, Hazel. In his own hand. That puts Vane physically at the registry nine days ago, connected to a transfer he had no official role in. Site representatives don't attend title hearings. Fixers do."

Hazel turned his cup slowly on its saucer. She'd learned to read him over these weeks — the economy of his movements, the way he thought in currents rather than lines. Now she watched him arrive somewhere.

"So we don't run to the investigators with a sack of paper and a story," he said. "We run to them with a trap already sprung. Something they can verify in hours, not weeks."

"Something that makes ignoring us expensive." She allowed herself the first smile in days. "Vane's signature. The forged citation. And the vault documents proving both. Any one of those is a story. All three together are a scandal, and scandals move faster than permits."

"Where are the federal investigators, exactly? Asking as a man who's spent his life avoiding them."

"The regional office is in Puerto Almendro. Two days by bus." She hesitated. "There's a complication."

"There's always a complication. It's why they pay me."

"They don't pay you anymore. You got blacklisted, your boat's condemned, and you gave away the only valuable thing you ever salvaged."

"See, this is why nobody likes surveyors." But he said it warm, and she felt the warmth land somewhere under her ribs where she hadn't been hit yet, and chose not to examine that.

"The complication," she pressed on, "is that Brandt will learn we're in the registry the moment Fonseca's superiors check the access log. Or sooner. Vane has people here. We saw them at the ferry crossing; we'll see them here. The moment we surface in town, the countdown isn't just Thursday. It's however long it takes them to find us."

* * *

They found a room above a chandlery near the docks — paid cash, gave false names, and Ines felt a small private shame at how easily the lies came now. Hazel cleaned his knife wound at the sink, hissing through his teeth, refusing help with the particular stubbornness of men who would rather bleed than be seen needing. Ines sat on the bed with the archive spread around her like a fallen deck of cards and did what she had done her whole professional life: organized.

By lamplight she sorted the vault documents into three piles. *Proof of forgery.* *Proof of concealment* — the memos about the deaths at the trading post, the payments, the silence bought and sold. And *proof of theft* — the land claims, the river families' names, generations of titles invalidated by a dead statute resurrected like a corpse dressed for church.

Hazel came and sat across from her, shirtless, bandaged, holding his fourth terrible coffee.

"Piles," he said.

"Piles."

"Which pile gets us killed fastest?"

She considered. "All of them, together, carried openly. Any one alone, we might pass for eccentrics."

"So we split them."

"We can't split them. The case only works whole. Forgery plus concealment plus theft — that's the machine entire. Break one gear and the rest keep turning." She looked up at him. "That's the lesson of my whole career, Hazel. You don't reform these things. You don't trim them. You either stop the whole machine or you become a part in it. I was a part in it for eight years. I'm done being a part."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, rain had started again — the monsoon's trailing edge, arriving in ragged pulses against the shutters. Somewhere down the street a dog was losing an argument with a motorbike.

"When we hand it over," he said finally, "it stops being ours. Whatever happens after — hearings, appeals, Brandt's lawyers dragging it out for years — we don't hold it anymore. We're just witnesses. Witnesses are disposable."

"Yes."

"And you're sure?"

She thought about Tomas Reyes, standing at the riverbank with the key warm in her pocket and his blood cooling in the mud. She thought about the river families, and the woman named Rosa who had fed them plantains and shown them the old paths, whose grandmother's name was in the third pile. She thought about the version of herself who had signed compliance certifications in glass towers, telling herself the forms were neutral, that paper didn't drown anyone.

"I've been sure since the trading post," she said. "I just wasn't brave until later. Bravery's mostly scheduling."

Hazel huffed — almost a laugh. "That's the truest thing anyone's said to me all year, and I once heard a priest fall off a pier."

* * *

Monday morning, everything changed shape.

Ines went back to the registry alone — Hazel drew stares like a hull draws barnacles, and besides, one person asking routine questions attracted less attention than two fugitives loitering. Fonseca met her at the counter and, without preamble, slid a fresh photocopy across the wood.

"Amendment filed last night," he said. "Meridian Gulf moved the hearing up. Wednesday. Day after tomorrow." His jaw worked. "And there's more. Look at the signature page."

She looked. The amendment bore a new authorization — not Brandt alone now, but a co-signatory, listed as *site operations consultant,* empowered to certify the physical condition of the concession's assets prior to transfer.

*M. Vane.*

Her stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "He's being put on the record. On purpose."

"On purpose," Fonseca agreed grimly. "You don't co-sign a man onto a title transfer unless you intend for him to own part of it. Or unless—" he met her eyes, "—unless you want him legally bound to the paper. So that whatever happens to the paper happens to him. Some partners insure each other that way. With chains."

Or, she thought, with blackmail. Brandt was welding Vane to the document so that if the archive ever surfaced, neither man could testify against the other without drowning himself. It was the oldest trick in her former profession, and seeing it deployed against her own evidence made her almost dizzy with the elegance of it.

"Witness list," she managed. "For Wednesday. Is there one?"

"Sealed. But hearings of this type require notice to any registered interested parties." Fonseca paused, and then did the thing she would remember for the rest of her life: he tore a page from his own desk calendar, wrote an address on it, and folded it into her hand. "Federal investigators. Regional office. The senior examiner is a woman named Ochoa. She hates Meridian Gulf. Has hated them since a spill case in '09 that got buried. She can't open a file without a predicate — some concrete violation, verified, current. Bring her one and she'll move mountains, and enjoy it."

"And if we bring her nothing concrete?"

"Then you're two wet strangers with conspiracy theories, and by Thursday the paper is clean forever." He straightened his glasses. "Also — and I say this as a neutral officer of the registry — there was a man here an hour ago. Nice suit. Asked whether two travelers had requested concession files. I told him I serve many members of the public." A beat. "You should probably not be members of the public here anymore."

* * *

She found Hazel at the dockside café, watching the harbor with the expression of a man auditing every boat in it for pursuit. She sat, pushed the calendar page across the table, and told him all of it — the amendment, the accelerated hearing, Vane's signature, Ochoa's name.

Hazel read the address twice. Then he laughed, short and disbelieving.

"What?"

"It's the marina district. I know that office. I used to run salvage charters past it every morning — used to wave at the building, half-joking, in case they were taking notes." He shook his head. "Life's got a sense of humor like a riptide."

"Hazel. Focus. Wednesday. We have one day to walk into a federal office, convince a hostile examiner that our sack of paper is worth burning her career on, and trigger action before a signature dries." Ines realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against the table. "And Vane knows we're in town."

"Then we'd better not knock on her door like beggars." He turned the calendar page toward himself, and she watched the diver come up in his face — that cold, clear calculation she'd first seen forty feet underwater, when the world goes quiet and simple. "We knock like sellers. People with something valuable to move, moving it carefully. Ochoa's spent years watching Meridian Gulf buy everyone in this province. What's the one thing she's never seen?"

Ines understood, and the understanding settled over her like cold water. "Someone who won't sell."

"Someone who walks in offering the whole machine — free — and just needs her to catch it before it hits the ground." He drained his coffee and stood, wincing at his side. "Get the piles packed. We go at opening tomorrow. Tonight we rehearse until you can recite the chain of title in your sleep."

"Our sleep."

"Sure. Our sleep." He was already moving toward the door, then paused. "Ines. If this doesn't work — if Ochoa doesn't bite, if Wednesday comes and the ink dries — what's left?"

She looked at the rice sack leaning against her chair leg, holding everything the river had given up and everything people had died to keep buried.

"Then we stand up in that hearing room ourselves," she said, "and say our piece on the record, in front of God and the clerk and whatever gunmen Brandt brought. Because a record is a strange kind of vault, Hazel. Harder to flood than most."

He grinned — tired, crooked, real. "Surveyors," he said. "Always thinking about the paperwork."

"Salvage divers," she returned, gathering the archive into her arms, "always thinking about what's worth saving."

Outside, the rain came down harder, drumming on the tin roofs of the marina district like a countdown — and somewhere across the harbor, in a building with clean windows, a pen was already waiting.
