## 19. Brandt's Table

The building that housed Brandt & Associates stood on stilts above the harbor like a heron pretending it wasn't hunting anything. White clapboard, brass fittings, a receptionist with a voice like chilled wine. Everything about it said: we are respectable, and if you doubt that, the air conditioning will convince you.

Hazel had dressed for the part out of a thrift store in town — a linen shirt that fit him everywhere except the shoulders, trousers with a crease some dead man had ironed into them decades ago, and shoes that pinched in a way he decided to interpret as character-building. He'd shaved twice, which for him was practically ceremonial. Ines had looked him over on the sidewalk and said, "You look like a man who owns one boat."

"I do own one boat."

"Then you look honest. Good. Honest men make the best liars — nobody checks their arithmetic."

She'd said it lightly, but there was nothing light underneath it. She'd spent the morning in the coastal registry office two blocks away, wearing a borrowed blouse and a clipboard she'd earned by volunteering to help an archivist move boxes. Nobody suspects the woman who volunteers to lift things. By now she'd be sitting at a reading table with a view of the filing room door, copying permit numbers onto the backs of tide tables while looking bored enough to die of it.

All Hazel had to do was keep a man named Aldous Brandt talking until four o'clock.

He climbed the stairs at ten minutes past noon, because punctuality was for employees, and gave his name to the receptionist as "Delacroix — regarding a private matter of salvage." He did not say *the* Delacroix. He didn't have to. He watched the word travel through the phone line like current through wire, and within ninety seconds a door opened and a junior associate was ushering him down a hallway lined with framed photographs: dredges, ribbon-cuttings, men shaking hands in front of things they intended to remove.

Brandt's office was at the end, facing the water. Of course it was. The man had arranged his life so that everything he took from the river he could watch while deciding what to take next.

He was smaller than Hazel expected. That was the first surprise. He'd built himself up in Hazel's imagination as something heavyset and slow, a man whose power came from mass. Instead Aldous Brandt was narrow and quick, silver-haired, with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead like a tool he'd stopped needing because other people now read for him. He came around the desk with both hands extended, which is a thing men do when they want you to notice they're not holding anything.

"Mr. Delacroix." His grip was dry and brief. "I'll confess I wondered when you'd come see me."

"Did you."

"You recovered equipment from a wreck site my firm holds certain interests near. You then disappeared upriver for six weeks during monsoon season, which is either very brave or very well paid, and given the state of your vessel when she came back down—" Brandt smiled without warmth, a man doing arithmetic with someone else's face, "—I'd guess both. Sit, please. Coffee? My assistant makes it strong enough to strip paint, which around here is sometimes the point."

"I'll take it black."

"Naturally." Brandt poured two cups himself, waving off the intercom, and Hazel understood that too — the performance of informality, the boss who serves you coffee so you'll forget he signs the orders. He sat across the desk and let the silence do a lap or two. Nerve over firepower. Ines had drilled it into him on the walk over: *You're not selling him the archive. You're selling him the idea that you're stupid enough to sell it, and greedy enough to be bought, and careless enough to talk. Every question he asks is him confirming the permits. Let him confirm them out loud.*

"So," Brandt said, settling back. "You have something of mine."

"That's an interesting way to put it."

"It's the accurate way. A sealed case bearing the concession number of a survey expedition operating under contract to this firm, removed from a wreck in waters adjacent to our licensed corridor. I could have it characterized as theft, Mr. Delacroix. I've had lunch with people who'd enjoy writing that letter."

"And yet here I am, drinking your terrible coffee."

"Here you are." Brandt's eyes flicked — just once, quickly — toward the window, toward the harbor, toward the Marisol sitting at anchor where any man with binoculars could count her rust streaks. "Which tells me you want something. Men who want things can be reasoned with. It's the men who don't want anything that worry me."

*Good,* Hazel thought. *Keep going. Name your price. Say the numbers.*

* * *

The coffee really was terrible. He drank it anyway, slowly, because every minute at this table was a minute Ines spent with her head down among the registry files, and because slowness read as confidence. He set the cup down and leaned back until the chair complained.

"Here's my situation," Hazel said. "Your expedition went quiet. Somebody hired me to run supplies upriver before the window closed. I went. What I found up there isn't timber survey and it isn't mineral cores. There's a vault under the old trading post at Concession 44, flooded, colonial-era, packed with documents somebody has been willing to kill to keep dark. I know, because I watched them try."

Something moved behind Brandt's glasses — not alarm; calculation. A man hearing a number he hadn't expected and adjusting his ledger.

"I also know," Hazel continued, "that the concession itself is paper. And I know paper can be checked. So before you decide how much of what I'm saying is a threat, ask yourself why I came here instead of going to the federal registry office two streets over."

"Why *did* you?"

"Because the registry gives me a hearing. You give me money." He let that sit. "I'm a salvage diver, Mr. Brandt. I've spent thirty years pulling other people's mistakes off the bottom of the Gulf. I'm not an idealist. Idealists drown. But I've also learned that the difference between salvage and theft is mostly paperwork, and right now the paperwork is the only thing standing between your operation and a very long fall. So I'm offering you a trade. The archive — all of it, intact, no copies — in exchange for a number, and clean papers for me and mine. Boat repairs, license reinstated, my name kept out of whatever story gets told about Concession 44."

Brandt steepled his fingers. For a long moment the only sound was the harbor breathing against the pilings below.

"You understand the difficulty," he said at last, gently, almost kindly. "If I pay you, I've confirmed everything. If I refuse you, you walk to the registry. Either way, you've got me. It's a well-built trap, Mr. Delacroix. I mean that as a compliment. But traps have a flaw common to all of them: they assume the animal is alone in the woods."

"There it is," Hazel said.

"There what is?"

"The threat under the courtesy. Go on. Say it plain. Who else is in the woods?"

Brandt rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind him, watching a fuel barge nose its way up the channel. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its warmth entirely, and Hazel heard what was underneath: a clerk's voice. Cold, tidy, administrative.

"A man named Vane retained certain services through intermediaries I do not control and cannot recall. If he believes the archive is compromised — if he believes *you* are compromised — then the number you're asking me for becomes irrelevant, because you won't live long enough to spend it. I tell you this not as a threat but as professional courtesy between men negotiating in good faith. Your problem isn't legal exposure, Mr. Delacroix. It's that you're carrying the only copy of something a violent man wants, and you've just told me where you sleep."

"I sleep on a boat," Hazel said. "Boats move."

"Mm." Brandt turned back. "And the woman. Okonkwo. She surfaced at the registry this morning, I'm told. Very diligent. Copying things."

The floor did not tilt, but it considered it. Hazel kept his face at anchor. *She's already been seen.* Which meant the clock wasn't four o'clock anymore. Which meant the second half of the plan was burning while he sat here drinking paint-stripper coffee.

"She's a surveyor," Hazel said. "Surveyors copy things. It's the whole job."

"Of course." Brandt smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was worse. "Here's my counteroffer, and I'll only make it once. You bring me the archive today — this afternoon, before the close of business — and I will pay you more than you asked for. Not because I'm generous. Because I want this finished cleanly, tonight, with no loose paper anywhere in the world. In exchange, Ms. Okonkwo walks out of the registry building unmolested, and the two of you leave the coast however you like. I'll even write you a reference." He spread his hands. "Or you can keep posturing, and I'll make one phone call, and you can find out how Vane's people say hello."

Hazel looked at him for a moment. Then he laughed — short, genuine, surprised out of him.

"You think I brought it with me."

"Didn't you?"

"Mr. Brandt. I'm a salvage diver. I found a case stamped with your concession number in forty feet of green water and I didn't declare it to anybody, including the navy boats shadowing my barge. You think I'd carry the prize of my life into the office of the man most motivated to take it?" He shook his head. "It's somewhere you'll never find it, and it stays there until I'm on a bus with cleared papers and a bank draft. Those are my terms. Yours were never terms. They were a discount."

For the first time, Brandt looked tired. It lasted maybe two seconds — a flicker, a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy — and then the clerk came back and the face closed up like a hatch.

"Three o'clock," Brandt said. "You come back at three with proof of possession — one document, shown to me and returned to your pocket — and we conclude this like businessmen. If you come back at three-oh-one, or if you come back followed, or if anything happens in the interim that suggests you've been to the registry, the negotiation is concluded by other parties." He walked Hazel to the door himself, hand briefly on his shoulder, a gesture that managed to be both paternal and proprietary. "It's been a pleasure, Mr. Delacroix. Genuinely. Men like you are rare. Most people, when they find something valuable at the bottom, they just... let it stay there."

"Some things float up," Hazel said. "River's funny that way."

* * *

The stairs took him down two at a time and the harbor air hit him like a slap of sense. He made the corner of Fisherman's Row before he let himself breathe properly, and there was Ines, exactly where she'd said she'd be, sitting on the seawall with a paper cone of fried plantains, feeding pieces to a gull she clearly disliked.

"Don't," she said, before he opened his mouth. "Whatever your face is doing, stop it. Walk normal. We're two tourists arguing about fish prices."

"He knows you were in the registry. This morning. He knew before I said a word."

Ines went still, a plantain halfway to the gull. Then she ate it herself, thoughtfully. "Then we have less time than I hoped. Did he confirm?"

"He confirmed everything without confirming anything. He talked about 'licensed corridors' and 'contracted interests.' He offered to buy the archive back at three o'clock — cash, clean papers, the works. He wouldn't have offered a cent if the permits were real, Ines. Real concessions don't get bought back from divers. They get litigated." He paused. "There's more. He mentioned Vane like he's a separate weather system. Like he can't call him off. Which means—"

"Which means when Brandt decides we're a problem, he doesn't send lawyers." Ines crumpled the empty cone and stood. "And when Vane decides we're a problem, he doesn't wait for three o'clock." She was already moving along the seawall, fast, unhurried-looking, the way she did everything. "I got what we need. Fourteen pages. The original concession filing is dated nine days *after* the survey contract it authorizes. The environmental clearance bears a signature from a ministry official who died eight months before the date on the ink. And there's a rider buried in the annex — compensation schedule for 'unplanned loss of local personnel,' pre-signed, blank names. Hazel, they priced the bodies in advance."

They walked. The market noise swallowed their voices whole.

"So we go back at three," Hazel said. "Show him one page. Buy the hour. You get those fourteen pages to the federal investigators' field office before close of business, and by the time Brandt realizes the document I showed him proves nothing except that I can hold paper, it's done."

"Yes." Ines stopped at the mouth of the alley that led to the docks. She looked at him with an expression he'd learned to dread — the one that meant she'd done the math on everyone's odds, including his, and wasn't going to lie about the total. "One thing. He said 'followed.' He was telling you he'd have eyes on you between now and three. Which means I can't walk to the field office as myself, and you can't walk back to his office unwatched, and we need to be in two places at once with one boat and no crew."

"I've been in two places at once before," Hazel said. "Once. It involved a capsized shrimp trawler and I don't recommend the technique."

"Hazel."

"I know." He looked past her, down the row of moored boats, at the Marisol riding at anchor in the afternoon glare — his boat, his whole livelihood, floating on water that suddenly felt like it belonged to other people. Somewhere behind them, up on the heron-legged building, a telephone would be ringing. Somewhere upriver, a man with a rifle would be told to come down to the coast ahead of schedule.

"Three o'clock," Hazel said. "We give him his hour. And we pray the river's slower than the paperwork."

Ines almost smiled. "The river's never slower than the paperwork. That's the whole trouble with rivers."

Behind them, at the top of the stairs to Brandt & Associates, a man in a fishing shirt that had never seen fish stepped out of the shade and began, casually, to follow them down.
