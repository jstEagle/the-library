## 20. Everything the River Holds

**HAZEL**

The office of Brandt & Associates smelled like money trying to pass for restraint — leather that had never seen rain, carpet thick enough to drown in, and behind the desk a window with a view of the harbor that Hazel knew cost more per month than the *Marisol* had cost him outright. He sat in a chair designed by someone who hated spines and did his best to look like a man holding a fortune he didn't want.

Which, as it happened, required almost no acting at all.

"You'll forgive me if I don't shake your hand," Brandt said. He was smaller than his reputation, a neat gray man with the kind of face you forgot while it was still looking at you. "You've been swimming in rivers."

"Salt water, mostly." Hazel set the oilcloth bundle on the desk between them, just far enough away that Brandt had to lean. "But I take your point."

Brandt looked at the bundle the way a starving man looks at bread he's been told is poisoned. "And this is everything? The full cache from the vault?"

"Everything Vane sent me down for." Hazel let that sit. "Minus my fee."

"Your fee being—"

"A clean title on a boat. Cash. And a letter saying Concession 44 was transferred to me before any of this started, so when the questions come, I'm a diver who got lucky, not a diver who got involved."

It was a good lie because it was half true. Every word of it was something a man like Hazel would want, which was what made the bluff work. Ines had drilled him on it for two days in a rented room above a fish fry, pacing the floorboards with a pencil behind her ear until he could recite the whole performance in his sleep. *Don't ask about the permits,* she'd said. *Make him tell you about the permits. Guilty men love to explain paperwork.*

Brandt steepled his fingers. "The transfer documentation would take time. There are filings. Signatures."

"I've got three days before the registry closes the quarter," Hazel said. "After that, I start shopping this to people who don't care whose name is on paper."

Something moved behind Brandt's eyes — not fear exactly. Recalculation. The look of a man doing arithmetic with other people's lives and finding the sums inconvenient.

"You understand," Brandt said mildly, "that Mr. Vane has invested a great deal in the assumption that these documents no longer exist."

"I understand Vane's men shot at my boat."

"And yet here you are, alive, holding his prize." Brandt smiled without warmth. "Either you're very good or very lucky."

"My mother used to say the two run in families."

Down the hall, somewhere past a receptionist's desk, a phone rang and was answered. Hazel kept his breathing even. Somewhere in this building, if Ines's plan held, there was a records room with a copying machine and a woman named Ines Okonkwo who had once worked for this firm and still remembered which door alarms were decorative.

"So," Hazel said. "Do we have a deal, or do I go find a federal building?"

Brandt opened a drawer, unhurried, and took out a single sheet of paper. He turned it around so Hazel could read it upside down: a filing confirmation, dated four days ago. *Transfer of Concession 44 — pending signature.*

"You're late," Brandt said pleasantly. "The concession transfers Friday morning. Which means whatever you're carrying stopped being worth anything Thursday night." He leaned back. "So let's talk about what you're actually selling, Mr. Delacroix. Because it isn't documents. Documents I can forge. What you're selling is the fact that you exist, and that can be arranged either way."

Hazel felt the floor tilt under the chair, the old diver's instinct for a deck going wrong beneath him. *He already filed. He wanted to see what I'd do.* The bluff hadn't collapsed so much as been stepped over.

"Well," Hazel said, buying seconds, "that's disappointing. I had a whole speech prepared."

"I imagine you did." Brandt pressed a button under the desk's edge, casual as a man ringing for coffee. "Vane's people are ten minutes out. I'd suggest you decide quickly whether you're a partner or a problem."

Hazel stood up slowly, hands visible, heart going like a bilge pump. Through the window behind Brandt, out on the gray harbor water, he saw a workboat cutting the ferry lane at a speed ferries weren't allowed to go.

Ten minutes, give or take.

He gave it ninety seconds.

* * *

**INES**

The records room on the fourth floor smelled of toner and dead ambitions, and Ines Okonkwo loved it the way you love a house you grew up in and escaped. She had signed invoices in this room. She had shredded things in this room. She had once, three years ago, helped file an incident report that turned two drowned surveyors into a footnote about equipment loss, and she had told herself then that the system was too big to fight.

She fed pages into the copier now with the steady rhythm of penance.

The registry filings were worse than she'd guessed. Not one forged permit but a lattice of them — environmental assessments signed by consultants who'd never seen the river, compensation agreements for river families whose names had been copied off a census nobody had consented to, and at the bottom of the stack, the thing she'd come for: internal memos linking Brandt's firm to the navy patrol contracts that had shadowed Hazel's barge from the first day. The corruption wasn't a secret somebody kept. It was an org chart.

Her hands shook only once, when she reached the memo dated the week Marcus Vane sealed the vault. *Asset secured. Local staff reassigned per contingency.* Reassigned. She thought of Tomas Reyes slipping her that key with his eyes on the middle distance, and made herself copy that page twice.

Outside the little wire-glass window, the corridor stayed empty. Downstairs, she knew, Hazel was running out of charm. They'd agreed on the signal — the fire alarm, pulled from the stairwell, thirty seconds of chaos in a building where chaos was theoretically impossible.

The copier finished its last stack. She squared the pages, wrapped them in the same oilcloth trick Hazel used, and zipped them into her jacket against her ribs.

That was when the elevator chimed.

Not the alarm. The elevator.

Ines went flat against the shelving, into the gap between a filing cabinet and the wall, and watched two men step out — wet-shouldered, river-mud on their boots, moving with the loose readiness of Vane's hired muscle. One carried a canvas bag she suspected contained restraints. The other carried nothing, which was worse.

They walked past the records room without glancing in. Headed downstairs. Toward Hazel.

Ines counted to ten, then broke every rule of tradecraft she'd ever learned and followed them.

* * *

**HAZEL**

The two men came through Brandt's door at the nine-minute mark, and Hazel was still standing there, which told him everything about how badly the morning had gone.

"Mr. Vane sends his regards," said the one with the bag.

"He should've come himself," Hazel said. "I hear he's short on divers."

The backhand caught him across the ear and put him into the bookshelf. Hazel rode it down, tucked, and came up with the brass whale-shaped bookend he'd clocked on arrival — the one piece of foresight he permitted himself — and swung it into the first man's knee. The crack was deeply satisfying. The second man grabbed his collar, and they went over the desk together, scattering papers, knocking Brandt's immaculate pen set to the carpet.

Hazel had fought maybe six times in his life and won two, and both wins had been underwater where physics was on his side. Here, on dry land, against a bigger man, he lasted about as long as you'd expect. What saved him was the fire alarm.

It shrieked through the building like the *Marisol*'s bilge alarm, and the man on top of him flinched — one heartbeat of looking toward the door — and Hazel drove his thumb into the hollow behind the man's collarbone and rolled free.

Ines was in the doorway with a fire extinguisher.

"Ines—" he started.

She emptied it into the second man's face. The chemical cloud filled the office white and screaming, and through it Hazel heard Brandt shouting — not at them, at the hallway: *"Get him out of here! Get him OUT—"*

"Grab the bundle!" Ines coughed, hauling Hazel up by the arm. "Stairwell!"

"The archive's not in the office," Hazel rasped, blinking acid tears. "It's in a locker at the ferry terminal. You think I'd carry it in here?" He'd learned that much from watching Vane operate: never keep the treasure where the greed is.

They went out through the chemical fog, past Brandt silhouetted at the window yelling into two phones at once, and hit the stairwell at a dead run just as the first elevator opened behind them.

* * *

**INES**

They burst out onto the street into rain — the tail end of the monsoon season throwing its last tantrum, fat drops hammering the awnings — and Ines grabbed Hazel's sleeve and pulled him left, away from the main avenue.

"Ferry terminal's eight blocks," Hazel gasped.

"We're not going straight there. Vane's boat docked at the marina; his men will cover the terminal." She ducked them into the fish market, into the noise and the smell and the crowd of women haggling over shrimp, and only when they were three stalls deep did she let herself breathe. "We go along the seawall. The long way."

"You've done this before," Hazel said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I worked for Brandt for four years. I know which streets his people watch." She met his eyes. Rain dripped off her jaw. "And I know which ones they don't, because I drew the map."

He absorbed that — the confession she'd made on the long walk, surfacing again in daylight — and to his credit, or maybe just his exhaustion, he nodded. "Then lead."

They were crossing the mouth of Cannery Row when she saw them: three figures at the terminal gates ahead, and beyond them, moored at the public pier with its engines idling, a workboat flying no flag at all. Vane had covered the terminal exactly as she'd predicted, which meant the locker with the archive was unreachable, which meant—

Which meant the plan died right there, in the rain, unless the plan got help.

It got help.

It came up the channel from the south in the form of eleven boats — crabbers, skiffs, a shrimp trawler with its nets hanging like funeral drapery — flying river-family colors, and behind them, gray and official and utterly unannounced, two federal vessels with the coast guard auxiliary riding shotgun. Someone had tipped the investigators. Someone with a radio and a grudge and a generation-long memory of what Brandt's firm had taken.

The river families had promised, back at the bend, that they would not forget. Ines had believed them the way you believe weather.

The first loudspeaker announcement rolled across the water, ordering the unflagged workboat to hold position. The workboat did not hold position. It bolted — and ran directly into the path of a crabber who'd spent forty years learning every sandbar in that channel, and there was a grinding crunch that Ines felt in her teeth, and the terminal guards turned to watch their ride die, and that was the opening.

"Now," she said. "Through the market. Out the loading dock. We come at the lockers from the water side."

"You want to swim for it?"

"I want to wade for it. Chest-deep, behind the pilings, where nobody looks." She was already moving. "You're a salvage diver, Hazel. Try to contain your terror."

* * *

**HAZEL**

The water behind the terminal pilings was brown and warm and full of things Hazel preferred not to think about, and it was, without competition, the happiest he'd been in a week. Chest-deep, moving hand over hand along the barnacled concrete, with the rain drumming the surface and the shouts of the raid echoing off the warehouses — this was his country. This was the one place the world made sense.

"There," Ines said, pointing with her chin. Locker 118, water-side access, padlocked with a cheap brass lock that surrendered to a rock in four seconds.

The oilcloth bundle inside was dry, intact, and heavier than sin. Hazel slung it across his back under his jacket and felt the shape of it against his spine — the drowned colonial archive, the deeds and ledgers and buried deaths of sixty years, riding against his kidneys like a passenger.

Above them, on the pier, boots thundered. A voice — Vane's voice, raw and cracked, nothing like the controlled man from the camp — shouted something about lawyers, about rights, about *do you know who I am*, and the answer came back flat and federal and final.

Then another voice cut through, closer, and Hazel's blood went cold, because it was Brandt's, and it was coming down the pier stairs toward the water line, and it was saying: *"The diver. Vane says the diver took the core cache. Find the diver."*

Hazel sank to his chin. Ines slid beneath the pier beside him, her mouth at his ear.

"Brandt's selling Vane," she breathed. "Listen."

They listened. Through the pilings, in fragments, the story assembled itself: Brandt telling whoever would listen that Vane had gone rogue, that the expedition's violence was Vane's initiative alone, that the firm's records would show — and here his voice took on the smooth cadence of a man reading prepared text — *a pattern of unauthorized conduct by an independent contractor*. Six weeks of jungle, a dead engineer, a burned camp, and Brandt was folding it all into Vane like a bad hand of cards.

"He built him," Ines whispered. "Funded him, armed him, pointed him at the vault. And now he's unwinding him in real time."

"That's what they do," Hazel said. "Corruption doesn't have loyalty. It has inventory." He watched the gray federal shapes moving along the pier above, watched Brandt get intercepted mid-staircase by two agents who did not seem interested in his prepared text, and felt something in his chest that wasn't quite satisfaction and wasn't quite grief. "Come on. While everyone's watching everyone else."

They waded north along the seawall, two soaked ghosts under a dying storm, the archive dry against Hazel's back. Behind them the raid swallowed the offices whole — he could picture it, the toner-smelling records room, the memos, the lattice of forged paper coming apart thread by thread. The river families' boats held the channel. Nobody was getting off that pier with anything worth taking.

At the seawall ladder, Ines stopped and looked back once at the smoke-gray skyline of the district where she'd sold the best years of her conscience.

"It's not over," she said quietly. "Brandt will trade. He'll give them Vane and half the ledger and keep his skin. That's what men like him do — they survive at half weight."

"Then we make sure the other half floats," Hazel said. "Tomorrow. Federal building, nine o'clock. Your testimony and my bundle."

"And after that?"

Hazel looked out at the harbor, at the current running strong with the last of the monsoon, carrying everything it held toward the open sea. His boat was a grounded wreck in the shallows upriver. His trade was probably already dead — firms like Brandt's talked to each other. He owned a duffel bag, a knife with someone else's blood on it, and a locker key.

"After that," he said, hauling himself up the ladder, "I hear there's a legitimate survey crew hiring."
