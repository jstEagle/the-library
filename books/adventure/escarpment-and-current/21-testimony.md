## 21. Testimony

The federal courthouse in Port Cazador had been built by men who believed that justice should feel like a waiting room. Everything in it was beige — the walls, the carpet, the water fountain, the faces of the clerks who stamped your paperwork without looking up, as though the fate of an entire river valley were just another form filed in triplicate.

Ines Okonkwo had spent three weeks in that building, and she had learned two things about it. First, the coffee was terrible in a way that felt deliberate, as if someone high up had decided that witnesses should never be too comfortable. Second, the air conditioning ran at a temperature calibrated to remind you that you were no longer in the jungle, where heat had at least the decency to be honest about what it wanted from you.

She sat now on a bench outside Hearing Room 4C, her testimony finished for the day, her hands still faintly unsteady around a paper cup of the terrible coffee. Through the frosted glass she could see the silhouettes of the lawyers moving like fish behind a net — Brandt's people on one side, immaculate and slow-moving; the federal investigators on the other, quicker, hungrier, with the particular patience of people who had finally been handed something true.

Three weeks of depositions. Three weeks of saying the same sentences in slightly different orders while men in good suits tried to find the seam in them. *When did you first enter the vault beneath the trading post? Who sealed it? What did Marcus Vane say on the night of June ninth?* She had answered until the words lost their meaning, until "vault" sounded like a noise a bird might make, until she dreamed in affidavit.

And still they kept coming back to one question, asked gently, in different voices, by different people, as though kindness might shake loose what pressure could not:

*Why did you go back?*

Because I helped bury it the first time, she thought, and drank the terrible coffee. Because guilt is a current, and I have been swimming against it so long my arms are made of it.

The doors of 4C opened and Hazel Delacroix came out, moving with the careful gait of a man whose ribs were still knitting. The knife wound from the ferry crossing had healed clean, but he favored his left side when he forgot himself, and he forgot himself often. He spotted her, lifted two fingers off his cup in salute, and lowered himself onto the bench beside her with a grunt that he pretended was not a grunt.

"How'd it go?" she asked.

"They asked me how much Vane offered me." He turned the cup in his hands. "I told them the truth. Enough to buy back the Marisol twice over and retire someplace where it doesn't rain sideways."

"What did you say when they asked why you refused?"

Hazel looked at the ceiling, at the water stains blooming there like continents nobody had bothered to chart. "I said the price list didn't include Tomas Reyes."

Ines nodded. That was the answer she'd given too, more or less. It was strange, she thought, how a dead man's name could weigh less than a living man's silence, and still be worth more.

* * *

The verdict, when it came, came not with thunder but with a fax.

Ines heard about it in the courthouse cafeteria, from a junior investigator named Okafor who had taken a liking to her and brought her news the way other people brought flowers. He slid into the seat across from her, waving a sheet of paper like a man fanning a small fire he'd started on purpose.

"Judge voided the concession," he said. "All of it. Concession 44 is null, the transfer filing's dead, and Brandt's firm is under criminal referral. Fraud, conspiracy, and — get this — desecration of protected heritage sites. Somebody upstairs discovered the archive counts as colonial-era patrimony." He grinned. "You're officially a historic treasure, Ms. Okonkwo. Congratulations."

"I've been called worse by better-dressed people."

"And the river families' claims?" she asked, because this was the question she'd carried upriver in her chest alongside the documents, the one that mattered more than her own skin.

Okafor's grin softened into something more careful. "Moving forward. The land commission accepted the petitions last night. It'll take years — these things always take years — but the archive names every parcel. Every theft. There's paper for all of it now." He tapped the fax. "Your drowned vault just became the most important piece of evidence on this coast."

Ines sat with that for a moment. Somewhere above the escarpment, past the last checkpoint and the flooded tunnels and the black chamber where Hazel had nearly died holding his breath, the river was doing what rivers do — rising, falling, keeping its own records. And now, for the first time in a hundred years, its records agreed with the ones on land.

"Thank you, Okafor."

"Don't thank me. Thank whoever tipped the raid team about the registry office." He stood, gathering his papers. "River families, wasn't it? The Ferreiras?"

"The Ferreiras," she confirmed. Old Mateo Ferreira, who had fed them plantains and suspicion in equal measure, and whose granddaughter had paddled two days through monsoon swell to carry word to the investigators because, as she put it, *the river keeps accounts, and it was time somebody settled them.*

* * *

They held the disposition hearing for the recovered property on a Thursday, and that was where Hazel Delacroix managed to insult a federal magistrate, decline a small fortune, and break Ines's heart a little, all inside four minutes.

The Marisol was the issue. The navy had impounded her at the start of everything — the wreck job, the case stamped with the concession number, the whole tangled beginning — and the salvage assessment had come back brutal. Hull compromised below the waterline. Corrosion in the stringers. Engine mounts shot. The surveyor's report used the phrase *beyond economical repair*, which Ines suspected was bureaucratic language for *this boat has given everything she had and then some, let her rest.*

The magistrate, a dry woman named Calloway who reminded Ines of a heron — all patience and sudden precision — read the findings aloud and then looked over her glasses at Hazel.

"The vessel is condemned to destruction or disposal, Mr. Delacroix. However, given your cooperation in this matter, the court is prepared to authorize compensation. The recovered materials' value, prorated for your role—"

"No, ma'am."

A ripple went through the room. Lawyers looked up from their laptops like cattle startled by weather.

Calloway blinked once, slowly. "Excuse me?"

"The money." Hazel stood, which took him a moment, hand braced on the rail. His voice was even, but Ines had spent enough weeks watching him read water to see the current underneath. "I don't want it. The salvage fee from the original job — the wreck nobody would name — I want that returned too. Put it in the fund for the land claims, if there is one. If there isn't, make one."

"Mr. Delacroix, you are entitled—"

"I know what I'm entitled to." He almost smiled. Almost. "That's sort of the whole problem, Your Honor. Everything I was ever entitled to, I got by pulling things out of the water for people who didn't want them found. I'd like to try being entitled to nothing for a while. See how it sits."

There was a silence in which Ines could hear the air conditioning thinking.

"You understand," Calloway said, "that the firms in this district will hear about this. Refusing payment reads as a message. Some of them will blacklist you rather than wonder which side you're on."

"Yes, ma'am. I counted on it."

"You're ending your livelihood in this room."

Hazel glanced back at Ines — just a glance, half a second, the kind of look exchanged between people who had hauled each other out of floodwater and therefore required no translation — and then faced forward again.

"My livelihood was a boat that's already gone," he said. "What I've got left fits in a duffel bag. Turns out most of it was never mine to sell anyway."

Calloway studied him for a long moment, heron-still. Then she wrote something on her pad, and Ines would spend years wondering what it was.

"So noted," the magistrate said. "Compensation declined. The vessel Marisol is remanded for disposal." A pause, and then, in a tone that had thawed perhaps two degrees: "For what it's worth, Mr. Delacroix, the record will reflect that you are the only party in these proceedings who asked for less than he was owed. In my experience, that is either madness or integrity, and the two are frequently indistinguishable until the appeal."

* * *

Afterward, they walked out through the courthouse steps into the late-afternoon light, and Port Cazador did what Port Cazador always did: smelled of diesel, salt, and fried dough, in roughly that order of honesty.

Hazel moved slowly down the stairs, favoring his side, and Ines matched his pace without comment. They had gotten good at that — matching pace, sharing silence, the whole vocabulary of two people who had survived the same river.

"Blacklisted," she said. "By every firm on the coast. You realize you've achieved the exact opposite of what divers usually want."

"Story of my year." He squinted out at the harbor, where the water lay flat and glittering and indifferent. "You know what's funny? I spent twenty years trying to be the best salvage man they'd hire. Now I'm the one they won't touch, and I sleep better than I ever did."

"That's not funny, Hazel. That's sad."

"It's both. Most true things are." He shrugged, winced, regretted the shrug. "Besides, I've got prospects. A certain surveyor mentioned something once, mid-monsoon, about legitimate work. Though I notice she's been suspiciously quiet about it since."

Ines looked at him. He was watching the harbor with elaborate casualness, the way a man watches anything when he actually cares very much about the answer to a different question.

"I was waiting," she said, "until the testimony was done. Until it was real. I've watched enough things fall apart at the last signature to trust a promise before the ink." She dug into her bag and produced a folded sheet — a crew roster, a coastal survey contract, legitimate permits, the kind of paper that had cost her three weeks of phone calls and favors to assemble. "Deckhand position. Terrible pay. Excellent company. The boat's called the *Second Chance*, which I fought against, but the funding agency loved it."

"*Second Chance*," Hazel repeated. He took the paper and read it twice, slowly, the way he read charts. "Whoever named that boat has never been on one."

"She has. That's why she named it that." Ines allowed herself a smile. "Well? Do you want it or not?"

Hazel folded the paper with great care and put it in his shirt pocket, over his heart, which was either sentimentality or convenience, and knowing Hazel it was both.

"I want to say something first," he said. "About the reward. About all of it."

"Hazel—"

"One minute. I've been practicing." He turned to face her fully, and for a moment the harbor noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. "I pocketed that case on the first day because I figured whatever was in it, I could sell it to somebody. That was the whole shape of my life. Find it, lift it, sell it, move on. Then I met you, and you'd already sold yourself once — to Brandt, to that firm — and you spent everything after that trying to buy it back. And I thought, there's a woman paying interest on a debt she already cleared." He shook his head. "I'm not saying it well."

"You're saying it fine."

"Tomas died so we could carry those papers out. The Ferreiras bled for that valley for generations before we ever showed up. All I did was hold my breath in a dark room." He looked out at the water again. "The river doesn't owe me anything. But I figure I owe it a working life. So — deckhand. Terrible pay. Excellent company."

Ines found, to her surprise, that her eyes had gone hot, and she blamed the courthouse air conditioning, which dried everyone out and was clearly a plot.

"There's one condition," she said, when she trusted her voice.

"Name it."

"We stop at the mouth of the river on the way north. Properly. Not running, not hiding. Just — standing there. I never got to say goodbye to any of it."

Hazel nodded once, gravely, as though accepting terms of surrender. "I'll bring the terrible courthouse coffee. We'll pour some out. River people respect an offering."

"That's not a real tradition."

"It is now. I just founded it."

* * *

That evening, Ines sat alone in her rented room above a chandlery near the docks, writing in the notebook she'd kept since the jungle — the same notebook, water-warped, salt-stained, its cover held together with tape and stubbornness. She wrote down the day's events the way she recorded survey data: plainly, precisely, without decoration.

*Concession 44: voided. Brandt: referred for prosecution. Vane: in custody, awaiting extradition. Land claims: filed and moving. Marisol: condemned. Hazel: unemployed by choice, employed by me as of Monday.*

She paused, pen hovering.

Then, underneath, smaller:

*Tomas Reyes, engineer, who chose the truth when it cost everything. The river keeps accounts. Today, some of them were settled. Not all. Never all. But some.*

Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, reflected in water that had carried all of it — the archive, the bodies, the boats, the rain — out toward the sea and back again, patient as paper. Somewhere north, past the escarpment, the river families were reading the names of their stolen land for the first time in a century, and somewhere south, the vault beneath the trading post was flooding quietly in the dark, keeping its last secrets the way it always had.

Ines closed the notebook.

Monday, she thought. We sail Monday. And for the first time in longer than she could measure, the thought ahead of her was not a threat or a deadline or a debt.

It was just a horizon, waiting to be surveyed.
