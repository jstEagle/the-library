## 22. Escarpment and Current

The monsoon's last afternoon came in gold.

Hazel Delacroix stood on the escarpment above the river mouth with his hands in his pockets and nothing else to his name, watching the water do what water does — carry everything away, eventually, if you give it enough time. The storm had broken apart overnight into ragged white clouds that trailed eastward like a fleet in retreat, and beneath them the river ran high and brown and honest, hauling the season's wreckage out to sea: branches, silt, a drowned canoe, somebody's roof. All of it going out on the current. None of it coming back.

He'd been standing there an hour. Maybe two. He'd stopped keeping track of time back in the flooded tunnels under the trading post, when time had become something that happened to other people, and he'd never quite picked the habit back up.

"You're going to ruin your eyes," Ines said behind him, "staring at the sun like that."

"It's not the sun I'm staring at."

"No," she agreed, coming up beside him. "It's the water. It's always the water with you."

She settled onto the flat rock at the escarpment's edge the way she did everything — precisely, without wasted motion, a woman who had spent months mapping terrain that wanted to kill her and had developed strong opinions about footing. She'd brought two cups of coffee in dented tin mugs, which she'd acquired from the river families downstream, along with most of her current wardrobe and, Hazel suspected, a permanent claim on their hospitality. She handed him one without asking if he wanted it. She'd learned that about him early. He always wanted it.

They drank in silence for a while. The coffee was terrible — chicory-heavy, boiled too long, sweetened with condensed milk by someone who believed sweetness could fix anything — and it was the best thing Hazel had tasted in weeks.

Below them, where the river widened and lost its argument with the sea, a survey crew's barge sat at anchor. White hull. Clean lines. A crane on the aft deck that actually worked. *Delgado-Rivera Hydrographic Survey*, the letters said, freshly painted over whatever the barge had been before its life of legitimate science. Ines's new employers. Her old ones were mostly in federal custody or federal protection, depending on how cooperative they'd been, and Marcus Vane was in neither category, because Vane had gone into the flood country during the escape and the flood country had kept him. The river collected its debts. It always had.

"They sail at dawn tomorrow," Ines said. "Full complement. Sonar techs, two cartographers, a hydrologist who talks like a man being paid by the adjective." She sipped her coffee. "And they need a dive lead who knows this stretch of water better than the charts do. Because the charts, as we have established, lie."

"Charts are just rumors with coordinates," Hazel said.

"Someone should write that down."

"Somebody probably did. It's probably in the archive."

That got a laugh out of her — short, surprised, the kind of laugh she still seemed to be relearning how to make. Six weeks ago she'd walked out of the jungle alone with a stolen concession map sewn into her jacket lining and a story nobody believed, and somewhere between then and now she'd testified for eleven hours in a federal hearing room, named every name she knew, and watched the whole rotten structure come down in a rain of injunctions. The concession was voided. The permits were ash. Brandt's firm was being dismantled with the slow, thorough appetite of prosecutors who'd been handed a gift-wrapped paper trail, and the river families' land claims — the ones the archive proved out, deed by forged deed, grave by unmarked grave — were moving forward through channels that suddenly, miraculously, seemed to want to hear them.

Ines had done all that. Hazel had mostly gotten wet.

"I talked to Okafor this morning," she said. "The crew chief. I told him about you."

Hazel looked at her over the rim of the mug. "What exactly did you tell him?"

"That you're the best salvage diver on the coast. That you found a sealed case in a wreck charted as empty and spent six weeks finding out why it was empty. That you dove a black flooded chamber under a colonial trading post and came up with the only document that mattered, and Vane never knew until it was too late." She paused. "I also told him about your boat."

"Ah." Hazel turned back to the river. "So the interview's over, then."

"The Marisol is a barge-shaped tragedy, Hazel. She's condemned. They're going to break her up for scrap, and honestly, having seen her hull from the inside, I think scrap is flattering her."

"She got us downriver through a checkpoint gauntlet in the worst storm in a decade."

"She got us *into* rapids and then quit, which is what I said she'd do, which is why I was holding the archive and not the supplies when she went down." Ines shrugged. "She was a good boat for a bad job. That's not an insult. Most boats are bad boats for bad jobs."

Hazel couldn't argue. He'd bought the Marisol at thirty-one with the first real money he'd ever earned, and he'd loved her the way you love anything that keeps you afloat — gratefully, uncritically, right up until the moment she doesn't. She'd hauled his livelihood for nine years. Then she'd grounded herself in the shallows under sniper fire to save his life, taken a bullet below the waterline doing it, limped upriver on a patched hull, and finally died in the rapids with her cargo hold full of Vane's men's supplies, which Hazel considered a fitting end for a working boat. She'd gone out fighting somebody else's war and taking their gear down with her.

The navy had impounded the wreck, then condemned her, then — after the investigation went public and everyone involved discovered the word *liability* — quietly offered Hazel a settlement check so small it felt less like compensation than like a door closing. He hadn't cashed it yet. It sat in his jacket pocket, folded in quarters, and he took it out sometimes and looked at it the way you look at a scar.

The firms had handled him differently. No settlement there, no letter, nothing official at all — just a silence that spread outward from Brandt's collapse like oil on water. Three salvage contracts canceled in the same week. A marina manager who suddenly needed his slip for someone else. An insurance man who'd known Hazel ten years looking at him across a desk and saying, *nothing personal, Hazel, but nobody wants to be next*, as if Hazel were a fire that had burned down one building and might burn down another. Blacklisted without a list. Unemployable by reputation, which was the only kind of blacklist that stuck, because you couldn't appeal it and you couldn't prove it and everybody involved could go on pretending it didn't exist.

For two weeks it had worked. He'd woken up in a rented room above a bait shop with no boat, no trade, no prospects, and a folded check worth less than his old winch, and he'd lain there listening to the gulls and thought: *this is what it costs.* And then he'd thought: *and I'd pay it again.* Both thoughts were true at once, and he'd made his peace with living between them.

Then Ines had shown up at the bait shop with terrible coffee and a job offer, and the peace had gotten easier to keep.

* * *

They walked the escarpment ridge as the light went long, following the old path the river families used, past the shrine somebody's grandmother maintained at the high point — a shelf of stone holding candles, a rusted bell, and a wooden plaque carved in three languages, one of which Hazel recognized from the archive documents as the language of the people who'd lived here before the concession, before the trading post, before any of it. The plaque said something he couldn't read, but the grandmother had told him once, through Ines's translation, and he'd thought about it every day since.

*The river remembers what the paper forgets.*

Down at the landing below, the survey barge rode at anchor with her running lights just coming on, and the evening catch was coming in around her — small boats, outboard engines, families that had fought Concession 44 for three generations and won. One of the boats raised a hand as it passed. Hazel raised his back. Two months ago these people had hidden two fugitives in a fish smokehouse while Vane's hunters searched the bank twenty yards away, and nobody had said a word, and afterward the matriarch had fed them fish stew and told Hazel, in the tone of a woman stating physics rather than opinion, that the river sent whom the river sent.

He'd spent his whole career believing the water owed him nothing. It had taken these people to teach him the other half of it: that it gave more than it took, if you knew how to receive.

"So," Ines said. They'd stopped at the ridge's end, where the escarpment dropped away and the whole mouth of the river lay open below them, brown current meeting green sea in a line you could see from here, the last of the monsoon riding out on top of it. "Okafor's offer. Are you going to make me stand here awkwardly, or are you going to answer?"

Hazel looked at the water a while longer.

He thought about the vault. About the black cold of the final chamber, forty feet down inside a drowned colonial basement, his lamp beam finding shelf after shelf of tin document boxes furred with silt, and Vane's voice crackling in his earpiece saying *bring it all up, Delacroix, all of it,* and Hazel's own hands moving through the murk with a calm he hadn't known he possessed — lifting the core cache into the lift bag, and then, in the last ten seconds before ascent, palming a single folder and sliding it into his suit against his chest, gambling everything on a man like Vane never counting what he'd already decided he owned. The folder had held the death certificates. Eleven names. Laborers who'd died digging the concession's first roads, recorded as drowned, as fevered, as departed, filed and forgotten by a firm that needed them forgotten. Those eleven names had undone everything. Not the sonar surveys, not the satellite imagery, not the lawyers' careful arguments. Eleven dead men's names, carried up out of the dark against a fortune, in the chest pocket of a man who'd been paid to leave them there.

He thought about Vane's offer, too — the share, the number, the specific sum that would have rebuilt the Marisol twice over and bought him a house on stilts and retired his mother's debts. He'd stood in Vane's tent dripping river water and looked at the figure written on the paper and felt the full seductive weight of it, the way you feel a current trying to take your fins, and he'd understood that this was the moment the whole thing turned on. Not the diving. Not the gunfire. This: a tired man, a number, a pen.

He'd said no. Not nobly. He'd said it in a voice that shook, and he'd walked out of the tent expecting a bullet in his back, and instead he'd gotten Tomas Reyes's key an hour later and a night crossing through the worst weather of the year, and Tomas had paid for that key with his life on the riverbank, and Hazel had carried that too, ever since, the way you carry a debt to a man who can't collect.

"What survives," he said aloud, mostly to himself.

"What?"

"Something my father used to say. Salvage diver, forty years. Everything comes up eventually or it doesn't, and either way the water keeps it or gives it back, and none of it's yours. What survives isn't what you salvaged." He turned the mug in his hands. "It's what you refused to sell. He meant cargo. I think he maybe meant more than cargo."

Ines was quiet for a moment. "You refused to sell," she said. "Tomas refused. The river families refused for a hundred years. That's the whole ledger, isn't it? Everything else is just what the current carries off."

Below them the last storm clouds reached the horizon and came apart into the sea, and the water underneath went suddenly, briefly clear at the edges, the way it did after big weather — a day, maybe two, of visibility before the silt returned. Hazel had spent his life waiting for those windows. You didn't dive in the murk; you dove in the hours the river allowed, and you learned to move fast and trust your hands.

His hands, he noticed, had finally stopped shaking. Somewhere around week three they'd started again, whenever he closed his eyes and saw the chamber, and now, standing in the gold light with terrible coffee and no boat and no money and a condemned wreck for a headstone, they were steady.

"Tell Okafor yes," he said.

Ines smiled — the full one, the rare one, the one that rearranged her whole face. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. But I've got conditions."

"Of course you do."

"One: nobody calls me a hydrographic technician. I'm a dive lead or I'm nothing. Two:" he fished the folded check out of his jacket and held it up, "somebody tells me who I donate this to, because I've been carrying it around like a hot coal and the river families won't take it and I'm done arguing with the matriarch, she wins every time."

"The legal fund," Ines said immediately. "The land claims. It'll cover filing fees for four of the family parcels."

"Done. And three." He looked out at the barge, at the clean white hull and the working crane, at the legitimate future sitting at anchor in the last of the light. "First job, we resurvey the Marisol's wreck site. Properly. Mark it. Put her on the charts."

Ines studied him. "She's in twelve feet of water in a rapids run."

"She's in *my* twelve feet of water in a rapids run, and charts are just rumors with coordinates, and I'd like this rumor to be true." He drained the coffee. "A boat should die someplace documented. Otherwise it's like she never happened."

Ines pulled a battered notebook from her jacket — the same notebook, Hazel realized, that had survived jungle and flood and prison camp and testimony, its cover soft as cloth now, its pages holding the shape of everything she'd refused to let be erased — and wrote it down. *Dive lead: H. Delacroix. First objective: mark wreck site, Marisol.* She read it back to him like a vow, which, he supposed, is what it was.

"Dawn tomorrow," she said. "Okafor wants to meet you at the dock at five-thirty. Fair warning: he'll shake your hand and then he'll talk for forty minutes about sediment displacement, and you're going to have to pretend to enjoy it."

"I've survived worse conversations."

"You've survived a flooded tunnel, a sniper, a knife wound, and Marcus Vane's payroll meeting. Sediment displacement should be restful."

They started down the path toward the landing, side by side, the escarpment dropping away behind them and the river running wide and easy ahead, carrying the season out to sea. Halfway down, Hazel stopped once and looked back — at the shrine, at the ridge, at the whole gold-lit height of the place where he'd stood with nothing and found it was enough — and then he didn't stop again.

At the dock, the matriarch's granddaughter was waiting with a parcel wrapped in banana leaf, pressed on him before he could refuse: fish stew for the morning, because the river sends whom the river sends, and the sent should eat well before they sail.

Hazel took it in both hands.

Out past the bar, the current ran clean and strong toward open water, and for the first time in as long as he could remember, Hazel Delacroix wasn't watching the water to see what it would take.

He was watching to see where it would take him.
