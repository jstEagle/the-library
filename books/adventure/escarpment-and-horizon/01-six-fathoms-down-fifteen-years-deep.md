## 1. Six Fathoms Down, Fifteen Years Deep

The tide table said slack water at 11:40, and Petra Eastwick trusted tide tables the way other people trusted scripture: completely, and with a private understanding that they would eventually be the death of them.

They checked their gauges a third time anyway. Air, full. Reserve, full. Knife, lamp, slate, the little brass depth gauge that had belonged to nobody in particular and had come to Petra in a crate of secondhand gear from a man in Corrow Bay who hadn't asked any questions. That was the whole art of salvage diving, as far as Petra had ever practiced it: acquire things from people who didn't ask questions, and become, in turn, a person who didn't answer them.

Fifteen years of that. Fifteen years of harbors where nobody knew the name Eastwick meant anything at all, because in most harbors it didn't. Fifteen years measured not in birthdays — Petra had let those go soft and uncounted somewhere around the ninth year — but in miles. Miles down the coast. Miles of hull scraped and cargo recovered and quietly sold. Miles between Petra and a town called Grayharbor that Petra had left at nineteen with one duffel bag and no intention, ever, of looking back.

And yet here they were, anchored four miles off the escarpment coast, close enough that on a clear night — which this was, damn it, moonless and clear — you could see the lights of Grayharbor itself strung along the shore like a necklace someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up.

"Sentimental," Petra said aloud, to the boat, because the boat was the only company that never judged. The *Kestrel's Debt* was forty years old, patched in six places, and smelled permanently of diesel and salt and the particular staleness of a person living alone aboard for too long. "You're sentimental, is what you are."

The boat did not argue. It only rocked, patient, while Petra shrugged into the harness.

Below them, sixty feet down in the dark, lay the wreck the charts called simply *Navy Site 9*, though every diver within fifty miles called it the Flagged Lady, because she'd gone down flying colors nobody could quite identify, and because there was something about her — even the fishermen agreed, and fishermen agree about nothing — something about her that made you want to take your hat off.

She was also, according to three separate notices posted in three separate harbor offices, closed. Restricted. Off-limits to all salvage operations by order of the Coastal Authority.

Petra had read all three notices. Petra had then done what Petra always did with notices, which was fold them into neat squares and use them to shim the galley table so it stopped rocking.

*Just a survey,* Petra told themselves, the way they told themselves every time. *Look, don't lift. In and out. Nobody has to know.* It was the same litany they'd been reciting for fifteen years, and like most litanies it worked precisely as well as the faith behind it, which was to say: barely, and only in daylight.

They went over the side at 11:31.

* * *

The water off the escarpment at night was a different country than the same water by day. By day it was green-gold and busy, kelp forests swaying, fish going about their small urgent business. At night it was ink with opinions. The lamp made a cone maybe eight feet wide, and everything beyond it was a rumor.

Petra descended hand over hand down the anchor line, counting fathoms the way their father had taught them before Petra decided to stop being anyone's child. One. Two. Equalize early, equalize often. Three. The cold came up through the suit like a hand closing around the ribs, gentle but insistent, reminding Petra whose territory this was.

Four fathoms. The bottom rose to meet them, pale sand scattered with shell hash, and there she was.

The Flagged Lady lay on her starboard side, half-buried, masts snapped and pointing seaward like fingers accusing the horizon. She was bigger than the charts admitted — charts usually were wrong in the direction of convenience, and whoever drew this one had wanted very much for her to be small. Her hull ran a good hundred and twenty feet if it ran an inch, copper-sheathed along the waterline in patches, and the sheathing was wrong. Old pattern. Older than anything the Coastal Authority should have been parking off a fishing coast and calling Site 9.

Petra hung in the water above her and felt the familiar thing happen, the thing they hated: the wonder. It started low in the chest, a warmth that had nothing to do with the suit heater, and it spread, and it whispered things like *look at her* and *who were you* and *what are you carrying.*

Wonder was dangerous. Wonder was how divers died, staying down one breath too long because the wreck was beautiful. Petra had watched it happen once, off Sallow Point, to a cheerful man named Brannic who'd loved wrecks the way some people love unsuitable lovers, right up until the sea took offense. So Petra did what they always did with wonder: they folded it up small, put it in the box with everything else, and got to work.

*Survey only. Look, don't lift.*

They swam the length of the hull first, running the lamp along the planking. No new damage since the last visit. A school of pollack poured through the broken rail like smoke through a keyhole. Near the stern, a nameplate should have been — there was a scar in the wood where one had been pried away, long ago, deliberately, by hands with tools. Somebody had erased this ship before the navy ever got around to restricting her.

"Huh," Petra said into the regulator, which produced a sound like a startled duck.

That was when the light flickered.

Not the lamp — Petra knew the lamp's failures intimately, and this wasn't one. This was deeper, stranger. For half a heartbeat, everything went dark. Not dim — *dark*, absolute, as though the sea itself had blinked, as though the water around Petra had briefly forgotten how to carry light at all. The pressure in Petra's ears changed. Not increased — *shifted*, the way air shifts in a room when a door opens somewhere far away.

Then it passed. The lamp burned steady. The pollack regrouped and resumed their smoke impression. The wreck lay exactly where she'd lain for — how long? Petra didn't know, and that was another wrongness. Wrecks this size got found. Got dived, got stripped, got written up in the trade papers. A hundred-and-twenty-foot ship sitting intact within sight of a working harbor, ignored for decades? Ships didn't stay secret like that. Secrets stayed secret like that.

Petra checked the depth gauge. Six fathoms. They checked it again, because the number refused to sit still in their mind. Six fathoms, and yet the descent had felt longer. Deeper. As though they'd gone down through more water than existed here, as though the site had a little extra darkness tucked into it, the way old houses have extra rooms.

"Fifteen years deep," Petra muttered. It was an old joke of theirs, self-directed, the kind of joke that keeps a person company. Everything they found, they found fifteen years deep, because that was how far down they'd buried themselves, and the sea, being thorough, kept handing their own burial back to them.

They worked the stern section for forty minutes. Survey only. Slate sketches, measurements, photographs with the little waterproof camera that cost more than the boat. The stern windows were intact — glass, after all this time, glass — and through them Petra could see a cabin interior, preserved, orderly, chairs still upright against the bulkhead as though the passengers had just stepped out and meant to come back.

Nobody steps out and comes back. Petra knew that better than most.

At the fifty-minute mark, with reserve margin shrinking comfortably but not luxuriously, Petra began the ascent. And halfway up the anchor line, at three fathoms, the dark came again.

Longer, this time. Two heartbeats, three. The water went black as a shut eye, and the cold turned inside out — not colder but *emptier*, a hollowness in the ears and chest, and beneath it, faint as a bell rung in another room, a sound. Not machinery. Not whales. Something rhythmic and deliberate, like a hammer on wood, or a knuckle on a door.

Then the sea remembered itself, and Petra was hanging on an anchor line under their own boat with the lights of Grayharbor glittering overhead through forty feet of ordinary, honest water.

Their hands were shaking. They noted this with clinical interest, the way they noted everything about themselves, and climbed the ladder, and stripped the gear, and sat in the cockpit wrapped in a blanket drinking tea that was mostly whiskey, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning came up gray and apologetic. Petra made breakfast — eggs, bread fried in the pan, tea without whiskey this time, discipline being the load-bearing wall of their entire personality — and laid the night's photographs out on the chart table in a fan.

The stern scar where the nameplate had been. The copper sheathing, wrong-patterned, older than old. The cabin windows, intact, orderly, expectant. And in the corner of the third photograph, caught at the edge of the lamp cone where Petra hadn't noticed it at the time: stone. Worked stone. A squared edge of masonry rising out of the escarpment's base, half-swallowed by sand, directly below the cliff line.

There was no masonry on the charts. There was nothing on the charts. The escarpment coast was drawn with the cartographic equivalent of a shrug.

Petra stood over the photographs for a long time, tea going cold, and became aware that the box inside them — the one where they kept the wonder, and the grief, and the name Eastwick, and a childhood spent in a boathouse in Grayharbor learning knots from a grandfather with flour on his sleeves — had rattled. Just slightly. Just once. Like something inside it had turned over in its sleep.

"Nothing down there but a dead ship and a dead family," Petra said to the kettle, which had heard worse.

But they spent the rest of the morning re-reading the tide tables, and they caught themselves doing it, and they made themselves stop, and then they did it again anyway. Because tonight — the tables said, in their flat indifferent arithmetic — tonight there would be an extreme low. A freak one. The kind of low that happened, according to the almanac's dry footnote, "not more than once in a generation, and then not predictably."

A low tide like that wouldn't just bare the sandbars. It would pull the water back off the escarpment's base like a sheet pulled off furniture in an empty house.

Petra looked at the photograph again. The worked stone. The squared edge.

Somewhere behind their sternum, the box rattled again, and this time it sounded less like something sleeping and more like something knocking.

"Sixty years," Petra told the photographs, in the tone of a person negotiating with a hostage-taker who happens to be themselves. "Whatever you are, you've waited sixty years. You can wait until I've finished my eggs."

The photographs, being photographs, said nothing. But the tide tables lay open on the chart table, and the numbers in them were patient, and patient things were always the worst kind of trouble.

Outside, the escarpment stood black against the brightening east, and at its base, under six fathoms of cold green water, something waited with the particular stillness of a door about to open.
