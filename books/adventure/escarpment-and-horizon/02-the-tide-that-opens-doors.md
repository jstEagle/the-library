## 2. The Tide That Opens Doors

Petra Eastwick had a rule about tides: never trust one that behaves out of character.

The sea was like people that way. Most of them kept to their habits, and the ones who didn't were either about to give you something wonderful or take something you couldn't afford to lose. Fifteen years of salvage diving had taught Petra to tell the difference exactly never. You found out afterward, usually while underwater.

So when the tide began going out on the third morning and simply—kept going—Petra stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* with a mug of coffee going cold in one hand and felt the particular itch between the shoulder blades that meant the world was rearranging itself.

"Odile said it'd be a big one," muttered Sef, the deckhand, who was not technically a deckhand but a nineteen-year-old runaway from upcoast whom Petra had fed once and been unable to shake since. He had the unshakable cheer of someone who had not yet learned what the water could do. "Spring tide plus the storm swell offshore. She said she'd seen it this low maybe twice in forty years."

"Odile says a lot of things," Petra said. "Half of them are true."

The other half were truer than you wanted.

The escarpment coast fell away below them in gray terraces, limestone shelves stepping down into water so clear it looked like a lie. And as the tide drained out—drained and drained, past every mark Petra had ever noted in the margin of the chart book—the escarpment gave something back.

At first it was just a dark line at the waterline, the kind of shadow that might be weed or might be nothing. Then the shadow grew a shape. Then the shape grew an edge. And by the time the tide bottomed out, a full hour lower than any table predicted, there it was: a mouth.

A cave mouth, tall as a barn door, cut into the base of the cliff where no chart showed anything but solid rock. The sea had pulled back from it like a hand withdrawing from a drawer, and inside, the stone glistened wet and black, breathing out cold air that smelled of salt and old iron and something else—something mineral and deep, like the inside of a bell.

Petra set down the coffee.

"You're going in," Sef said. It wasn't a question. He'd already started rigging the dive ladder, because he'd learned that much about Petra if nothing else: the itch always won.

"I'm going to look," Petra corrected. "Looking is free."

"Nothing's free," Sef said cheerfully, handing over the mask. "You taught me that too."

* * *

The cave floor was walkable for the first twenty meters, ankle-deep in retreating seawater, the ceiling rising overhead into darkness that Petra's lamp couldn't finish. The acoustics were wrong in there—every drip echoed twice, as though the rock were repeating itself for its own amusement. Petra moved slowly, one hand trailing the wall, feeling the chisel-marks in the stone.

Chisel-marks.

That stopped everything. Waves didn't chisel. Water carved in curves, in scallops, in the soft grammar of erosion. These marks were straight. Deliberate. Someone had widened this passage once, long ago, with tools and intent—and then the sea had swallowed the work so completely that no chart, no harbor record, no fisherman's story Petra had ever bought drinks for mentioned it existed.

The passage turned, narrowed, opened again into a chamber the size of a chapel. The ceiling here was high and dry, furred with pale mineral bloom, and the floor was a shelf of smooth stone still slick with the departing tide. In the center of the chamber, sitting precisely where a thing would sit if someone had put it there on purpose—

A strongbox.

Iron-banded, oak beneath the iron, crusted with a century of salt but sound. Sound in the way of things that had been built well by people who expected them to wait. And on the lid, under the crust, catching the light of Petra's lamp:

A crest.

Two keys crossed over a wave. Beneath them, a motto in old lettering that Petra's eyes read before Petra's mind could stop them: *DEEP KEEPS WHAT DEEP IS GIVEN.*

Eastwick.

The name arrived in Petra's chest like a hook set hard. Not the word itself—Petra hadn't spoken the surname aloud in fifteen years, signed documents with initials only, let dockmasters assume whatever they assumed—but the crest itself, the actual stamped iron of it, the specific arrangement of keys and wave that had been burned into a child's memory on the door of a boathouse that no longer existed.

Petra stood in the dripping dark of a cave that should not exist and looked at the family crest and felt—not joy. Anyone watching would have expected joy. Treasure hunters dreamed of this moment their whole lives: the box, the crest, the glint. There were men in the harbor right now who would have wept.

What Petra felt was closer to the sensation of hearing your own name called from a dark room in a house you thought you'd moved out of. The past didn't knock. It had never once knocked. It waited until the tide went out far enough, and then it simply opened the door and stood there, patient as stone, holding out its hand.

"No," Petra said aloud, to the box, to the cave, to whoever had engineered this moment across the decades. The word came back doubled off the walls, as though the rock disagreed.

Petra crouched anyway. Of course Petra crouched. Curiosity was the family disease; exile had only ever been a treatment, never a cure.

The lock was a mechanism of three dials set into an iron plate, each ringed with numbers worn nearly smooth. No keyhole. A combination, then—a word made of numbers, waiting for someone who knew how to speak it. Petra touched the dials and felt the faint, horrible intimacy of it, like touching a sleeping person's wrist.

There was a fourth dial, smaller, offset. Petra leaned close. Around its edge, in letters fine enough that they'd clearly been meant to be found only by someone kneeling exactly here, exactly this way:

*SIX TIDES.*

Six tides. Petra sat back on the wet stone and laughed once, short and without any humor in it at all, because the number meant nothing and therefore could mean anything, and things that meant anything were how families got hold of you. Grandfather Eastwick had loved riddles the way other men loved drink—daily, ruinously, and with great tenderness. Every birthday card had contained one. Every one had been a small test, a small door. *Answer this and you may come in.*

Petra had stopped answering them at fourteen.

And now here was the largest one of all, grown to the size of a chapel, wearing the family crest like a face.

* * *

Back on the deck of the *Kestrel's Debt*, the light was turning gold and the tide had begun its slow climb home. Within two hours the cave mouth would be gone again, drowned and deniable, as though the coast had shown its cards and then swept them off the table.

Sef had the kettle on. He always had the kettle on; it was his entire philosophy of crisis management. He poured two mugs and handed one over and asked nothing, which was another thing Petra had taught him without meaning to, and which he performed with the exaggerated delicacy of a young man handling live ordnance.

"So," he said, after a full minute of heroic silence. "Was it treasure?"

"It was a lock."

"A locked treasure."

"It was a locked question." Petra wrapped both hands around the mug. The warmth helped. Warmth usually helped; that was why Petra kept the boat cold. "Someone put that box in that cave on purpose. Dug the approach, sealed the chamber, set the tide to reveal it. That's years of work. That's a fortune spent on rock. Nobody does that for cargo."

"For what, then?"

For a message. For a trap. For a door with a name on it. Petra drank the tea and did not say any of these things, and watched Sef watch Petra not saying them, and understood that the boy was cataloguing every silence for later use, the way he did.

Out along the escarpment, the water was coming back. It moved differently today—Petra noticed it the way you notice a stranger walking with a familiar gait. Swells arriving out of rhythm, a long low pulse from the southeast that the barometer swore wasn't weather. Somewhere beyond the horizon, something large was displacing water. Engines, maybe. Or intention.

And there—far off, at the edge of seeing—a smudge of smoke where no village stood. Two smudges. Three.

Sef followed the look. "Fleet?"

"Too early for the salvage fleet. They're still provisioning." Probably. Odile would know. Odile always knew, and charged accordingly, and Petra had been carefully not asking her questions for fifteen years on the principle that questions were how harbors got their hooks into you.

But the tide tables were pinned above the chart table now, and Petra had caught themselves, twice, doing arithmetic against them. Six tides. Whatever the phrase meant, the box had said it like a deadline, and deadlines were the one language the sea and Petra spoke fluently.

"Fifteen years," Petra said quietly, mostly to the tea.

"What?"

"Nothing. Rig for night mooring. I want lines doubled—we're staying over the site."

Sef blinked. "We're diving it again? You said looking is free."

"It was free." Petra drained the mug and set it down with more care than the gesture required. "Then I touched the lock. Now we're paying interest."

The first evening tide came in and took the cave mouth without ceremony, the black water folding over it like a page being turned. By full dark there was nothing to see but the escarpment and the stars and, far to the southeast, the smudges of smoke burning steady and deliberate, like men who knew exactly how long they had.

Petra stood the midnight watch alone, which was normal, and did not sleep, which was also normal, and found at some hour past two that they had drawn the crest on the back of a chart margin without deciding to—two keys, one wave, the motto underneath in a child's careful hand, reproduced from memory after fifteen years as though the hand had never stopped practicing.

Deep keeps what deep is given.

Fine, Petra told the crest, the cave, the grandfather-shaped silence behind all of it. Keep it. Keep all of it. I came out here to be no one, and I've gotten very good at it, and whatever you're selling, the tide can carry your offer back out with it.

The chart margin stared up from the table. Below the motto, in the space where the tide tables were printed, Petra's own handwriting had added a single line, sometime in the last hour, in ink:

*Tide one ends 0640.*

Five remaining after that. The box had said six. The sea, patient as stone, had already begun counting.
