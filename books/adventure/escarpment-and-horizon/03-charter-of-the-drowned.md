## 3. Charter of the Drowned

The strongbox sat on Petra's chart table like a guest who had arrived without an invitation and intended to stay.

Salt water wept from its seams onto the wood, pooling in the grain. The crest stamped into the lid caught the lamplight—the escarpment over three waves, with a horizon line struck clean through them all—and Petra looked at it the way you look at a scar you'd almost forgotten you had. There, but not yours anymore. Not for fifteen years.

They should have left it in the cave.

That was the sensible thing. Take nothing, mark the coordinates, come back at slack water with a clear head and no navy patrol schedule burning a hole in their pocket. But the tide had opened that cave mouth like a door held by a polite stranger, and the box had been sitting there on a stone shelf, dry as a sermon, as if it had been waiting specifically for them. As if it knew their name.

Petra dried their hands on their shirt, which accomplished nothing, and picked up the pry bar.

"Right," they said aloud, because the cabin was empty and empty cabins make liars of everyone. "Let's see what you want."

The lock was old brass, green with age, but the mechanism turned under gentle pressure—oiled, maintained, *serviced*. Someone had been down there within living memory. That thought settled cold in Petra's stomach even as the lid came up with a sigh of displaced air, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay papers.

Not coin. Not plate. Not the gold a rival fleet would gut a man for.

Papers.

Petra unwrapped them with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. Three documents. The top one was heavy stock, watermarked, sealed with wax that had survived fifty years underwater better than most marriages survive fifty years above it. The crest again. And beneath it, in copperplate handwriting that Petra recognized before they could stop themselves from recognizing it—

*To my heir, whosoever of my blood shall stand claim upon this wreck within six tides of its revealing.*

Grandfather Aldous's hand. Petra would know it anywhere. They'd spent a childhood being taught letters by it, tracing the loops of capital E's while rain hammered the boathouse roof, being told that a person's signature was a promise made visible, so don't you ever sign anything you wouldn't say out loud.

Petra sat down. The deck chair creaked. Outside, the harbor muttered against the hull, indifferent.

The charter ran four pages. It was, of all things, a salvage charter—a proper one, drawn up under the old coastal law that predated the navy's jurisdiction claims by two generations, witnessed by names Petra half-remembered from gravestones. It named the wreck: the *Meridian's Grace*, Eastwick flagship, lost with all hands off the escarpment in the year of the great storm. It described her position with a precision that made Petra's skin prickle, because the coordinates were not where any chart said she should be. They were deeper. Under the escarpment's root, where the charts showed bare rock.

*Which is exactly where I found her,* Petra thought. *Exactly where the blackout started.*

And then the fourth page, and the clause.

*Should no heir of the Eastwick line present this charter and stand formal claim upon the wreck within six tides of the cave's opening, the claim lapses entire, and the wreck, its cargo, and its charter pass to the Crown's admiralty, there to be disposed of as the Admiralty sees fit.*

Six tides.

Petra read it three times. The words didn't change. The trap didn't change. Six tides from the low water that had opened the cave—which meant the clock had already been running since yesterday morning, while Petra slept badly in this very berth, dreaming about nothing in particular. Five tides remained. Five chances to dive a wreck that sat deeper than any diver should go, in waters the navy patrolled on a schedule Petra had spent three nights memorizing, and to do it all while carrying a piece of paper that might as well have been painted on their back.

*Admiralty sees fit.* Grandfather had written those words knowing—*knowing*—what the Admiralty saw fit to do with Eastwick property. Knowing what had happened to the fleet. Knowing what had happened to the family.

"He engineered this," Petra said to the empty cabin. "The old bastard engineered this."

Because he had. There was no other reading. The cave didn't open on ordinary tides; the old-timers' legend said it opened once in a generation, when the sea drew breath. The strongbox hadn't been lost—it had been *placed*, on a dry shelf, behind a door that only opened for someone patient enough or desperate enough to be there when the water fell away. And the charter named an heir without naming a name, which meant it would wait. Fifty years, sixty, however long it took, for an Eastwick to come poking around a forbidden wreck at night like a thief in their own inheritance.

Petra laughed. It came out wrong, half a cough.

"I'm not an Eastwick," they told the box. "I haven't been since I was nineteen."

The box said nothing. Boxes are smug that way.

* * *

The flashback came the way flashbacks do—not invited, but summoned, the way a smell summons a kitchen.

Petra was nine years old, sitting on the boathouse dock with their feet in the water, and Grandfather Aldous was mending a net beside them with fingers that moved like they were arguing with the rope and winning.

"You measure everything in miles," the old man said. It wasn't a complaint. It was an observation, delivered the way he delivered weather reports.

"You said distance was honest," Petra said. "You said miles don't lie."

"Miles don't lie." He pulled a knot tight. "But they don't tell the whole truth either. A mile is just a year walking slowly, little fish. You count your life in miles, you'll always be leaving somewhere. You count it in years—" he tapped Petra's knee with a rope-hard finger, "—you might notice you've arrived somewhere instead."

"Where did you arrive?"

He smiled at the net. "Here. Eventually. After a lot of miles I regret." Then he'd done the thing he did sometimes, the thing that had frightened Petra even at nine: he'd looked out past the escarpment, past the horizon line, at nothing anyone else could see, and his face had gone old and far away. "One day the sea will ask our family a question. When it does, don't run from it. Running was my mistake. I answered it too late, and I paid in the only coin I had left."

"What question?"

"The one with teeth." He'd flicked water at Petra then, breaking the spell, grinning his gap-toothed grin. "Help me with this net, or I'll tell you the story of how I met your grandmother, and it takes four hours and I exaggerate."

Petra had helped with the net. Children always help with nets. And they'd forgotten the words, mostly, filed them away in the drawer of childhood things that don't mean anything until they mean everything.

Sitting in the cabin twenty-six years later with a dead man's charter open on the table, Petra remembered every word.

*Don't run from it. Running was my mistake.*

"Well," Petra said quietly. "I ran. Fifteen years of running. So congratulations, old man. Your trap worked on the second generation too."

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, laid out cold on the chart table beside the charter, because arithmetic was the only comfort Petra trusted:

Five tides remaining. The cave and the wreck required slack water at the escarpment, which occurred twice daily, roughly. But the wreck's true depth—the depth the charts lied about—meant a working dive of nearly forty minutes bottom time if Petra pushed every limit they'd sworn never to push. And the navy.

Petra spread the patrol notes they'd kept in their head for three nights. The gunboat *Vigilant* ran the escarpment station on a fixed rotation—out of the naval anchorage at dawn, up-coast sweep, return past the site at midday, evening run at dusk. Between the midday pass and the dusk run there was a window. Two hours, maybe less, depending on how bored the watch officer was and whether the fog cooperated.

Two hours. Once per day. For five days.

It could be done. On paper, it could be done. One dive per window, five dives, each one deeper and longer than the last, each one a fresh opportunity for the navy to notice a boat loitering off a closed site, for a rival fleet to follow them out, for the sea itself to simply take them, because the sea took people off the escarpment the way orchards took fruit—routinely, seasonally, without malice.

And that assumed the navy didn't already know. Petra kept circling back to that. A wreck doesn't get flagged on charts and closed by patrol order unless somebody upstairs cares. Somebody had cared enough, decades ago, to erase the *Meridian's Grace* from the public record—or so the absence of any record implied. Somebody had cared enough to keep a gunboat running a routine sweep past bare rock for years. You don't guard nothing that hard unless nothing is something.

The charter, if presented, would be a bomb. Legally, the old coastal law still stood—Odile Vance at the harbor office would know, Odile knew every statute the way other people knew hymns—but legality was a thin shield against men with guns and orders. Presenting the charter meant becoming visible. Meant becoming *the Eastwick heir*, the name on a document, a person with a history the whole harbor could dig through. Everything Petra had built fifteen years of careful anonymity to avoid.

The alternative was simpler. Do nothing. Let the six tides lapse. Let the Admiralty dispose of the wreck as it saw fit, whatever that meant, and let the cave close over its secret for another generation. Stay invisible. Stay safe. Stay alone, which was the part of the bargain nobody wrote down but everybody signed.

Petra looked at the crest on the lid. Escarpment, waves, horizon struck through.

"You could have just left me a boat," they told it. "Or money. Money is a perfectly good inheritance. Nobody has to dive for money."

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so Petra did what sleepless sailors do: put the kettle on and talked to the dead.

"You planned this." The cabin was small enough that talking to a strongbox felt almost social. "You knew the navy would come sniffing eventually, so you hid the proof somewhere only an Eastwick could reach. You set a deadline so none of us could sit on it forever, because you knew us—you knew we'd sit on it. You knew I'd sit on it." Petra wrapped both hands around the hot mug. "And you gave me six tides, which is not a gift, grandfather, it's a *noose with a schedule*."

The lamp flickered. Coincidence. Drafty cabin.

But here was the thing that kept Petra turning the charter over, literally, reading the witness signatures again: the condition wasn't *recover the treasure*. It wasn't *salvage the cargo*. It was *stand claim*. Stand there, on the record, with your name attached, and say *this is mine, and mine to answer for*. Grandfather hadn't left them wealth. He'd left them a decision. The one decision the family had failed to make, made impossible to defer.

Petra thought about the near-blackout on the descent, three nights ago. The way the water had seemed to press in, the way their torch had found rock where charts promised open bottom. The wreck was deeper than it should be. Stranger. Almost as if it had been *moved*—or as if the charts had been redrawn to hide where it truly lay.

A navy that falsifies charts. A family erased from harbor rolls—that much Petra remembered from childhood whispers, aunties speaking low about how the Eastwicks used to be *somebody* here, before. A charter hidden behind a tidal door with a six-tide fuse.

This wasn't an inheritance. It was a case file. And Petra was holding the only copy.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere across the harbor, a dog complained about the moon.

"Five tides," Petra said finally, and heard their own voice steady in a way that surprised them. Not brave, exactly. Committed. There's a difference, and divers know it: bravery is a feeling, commitment is a decision you make before the feeling shows up. "Fine. Five tides. But I'm doing this my way, old man. Quiet. Careful. Nobody learns whose name is on that paper until I decide they learn it."

The strongbox gleamed, insufferably serene.

Petra blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the water, and did the last piece of arithmetic, the one that mattered: first usable window tomorrow, between the *Vigilant*'s midday pass and dusk. Which meant provisioning tonight, checking lines and air, sleeping—if the sea permitted—in short angry shifts.

And which meant, come morning, walking down the harbor pontoon past the harbor office, where Odile Vance would be watching the water the way she always watched it, missing nothing, and Petra would have to look that sharp old woman in the eye and lie, or half-lie, or find some third thing that wasn't quite either.

Petra was still rehearsing the lie when the knock came.

Three raps on the hull, deliberate, unhurried. Not the harbor master's knock—too soft for Odile, who knocked like she was collecting debts. Not a neighbor's; neighbors shouted across water, they didn't creep up in the dark.

Petra went still, one hand finding the pry bar without deciding to.

Through the porthole, gray in the pre-dawn murk, rode a navy launch. And standing in its bow, uniform pressed despite the hour, was a lieutenant with a face like a locked drawer, raising one hand in a greeting that was not a greeting.

"Miss Eastwick," the officer called across the water, and the name landed on the cabin like a dropped anchor. "Might I come aboard? We should discuss your... family's property."

Petra looked at the charter on the table. Looked at the porthole. Five tides, and the navy already knew the name.

So much for quiet.
