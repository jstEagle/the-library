## 4. The Fleet Wakes

Odile Vance had been harbor-master of Greywater Reach for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned to read the water the way other people read letters. A calm morning could lie. A rough one could be honest. And a fleet that began provisioning at four in the morning was telling you exactly what it intended to do, no matter what its captain said over brandy.

She stood at her office window with a chipped mug of chicory coffee and watched the *Meridian Star* take on fresh water at the far quay. Behind it, two more hulls were swinging in on the morning tide — the *Cormorant* and something newer, grey-painted, with a crane arm like a heron's neck. Three ships. Cranes. Compressors being hauled aboard in slings. That wasn't fishing. That wasn't even honest salvage.

That was an expedition.

"Well," she said to the empty office, "somebody's been talking."

The somebody, she suspected, wore a navy uniform and had drunk too much of the wrong man's rum at the Anchor & Chain three nights back. Rum loosened more than tongues; it loosened ledgers, manifests, patrol schedules. Odile hadn't heard the conversation herself, but she'd seen the aftermath — young Denny Pell, who swept the tavern floor, sweeping with his ears instead of his broom, and coming away pale as a gutted fish.

She set down the mug, pulled on her coat, and went out into the grey light to do what Vance women had always done in Greywater Reach: stick her nose where it belonged.

* * *

The harbor woke around her in its usual order. The gulls first, arguing about nothing. Then the bait boats, coughing out past the breakwater with their lamps still lit. Then Marta Kellerman's bakery, exhaling the smell of hot bread across the cobbles until every dog in town sat down in protest.

Odile walked the long quay slowly, nodding to people, saying nothing of consequence, and listening to everything.

"Morning, Odile." Old Bram Hodge, mending nets that were mostly knots. "You see what's come in?"

"I see it."

"Three of them." He jerked his chin toward the *Meridian Star*. "That's the Voss fleet. Rennick Voss himself is supposed to be aboard the big one. You know what he does to a coast when he's done with it? Strips it like a chicken carcass. My cousin worked the Sable grounds the year Voss came through. Said you could walk from here to the lighthouse on what was left."

"I've heard the story, Bram."

"It gets worse every year," Bram said comfortably, which was how all his stories worked. "By now there's bones."

Odile left him to his arithmetic of ruin and kept walking. At the chandlery, Fen Osgood was already loading crates onto a handcart — rope, lamp oil, tinned meat in quantities that made her eyebrows climb.

"Fen. Big breakfast for someone."

"Order came down last night," Fen said, lowering his voice without being asked. "Fleet account. Paid cash." He said *cash* the way other people said *counterfeit*. "Nobody pays cash for tinned tongue, Odile. Not unless they're going somewhere they can't send bills."

"Where are they going, then?"

"That's the thing." He glanced up and down the quay, though at this hour the only witnesses were gulls and one suspiciously attentive cat. "Escarpment coast. North stretch. The fellow who placed the order wouldn't say why, but he bought out my whole stock of dive candles. Every one. And dive candles, Odile—" he tapped the side of his nose— "are for caves."

Odile thanked him, bought a loaf of Marta's bread she didn't need, and stood a while at the rail looking north along the coast, where the escarpment ran its long grey spine out toward the horizon, wearing its fog like a collar.

Dive candles. For caves.

There was only one cave on that stretch of coast that anyone had ever bothered to make a story about, and Odile had grown up on that story the way she'd grown up on salt air — without noticing, until it was part of her lungs.

Heir's Wreck, the children called it. Six tides, the story went, and the sea would open a door for the one who had the right to walk through it. Claim it or lose it forever. She'd played at it as a girl on the shingle below the cliffs, chanting the rhyme with the other kids, digging for treasure that never came up. It was a game. It was always a game.

Except that Elias Eastwick had never once laughed at it.

She remembered that now, standing at the rail with the bread going cold under her arm. Old Elias, who'd been her father's friend and hers, who'd taught her to splice line when her hands were too small to close around the rope. Other men told the cave story as a joke. Elias told it quietly, once, sitting on a bollard with little Petra asleep against his shoulder, and he'd looked at Odile — just a girl herself then — and said, *"It's not a story, Odie. It's instructions."*

She'd thought he meant it as a joke. She'd been eleven. Jokes were easier.

* * *

By mid-morning the fleet's provisioning had become the whole harbor's business, which is to say everyone knew and no one would say. Odile held court in her office above the quay, doing paperwork badly and listening well.

Marta came up with rolls nobody had ordered. "They took my second-best cabin at the inn. The Voss man. Paid for a week."

"A week," Odile repeated.

"And he asked after tide tables. Low-water tables specifically. For the escarpment reach." Marta set down the basket. "Odile. Nobody asks after low water on the escarpment. There's nothing there but rock and bad memories."

"There's a wreck there," Odile said.

"There's a *story* about a wreck there."

"Mm." Odile turned her chair toward the window. Below, on the quay, a navy launch was tying up — white hull, two ratings, and Lieutenant Corran stepping ashore with his cap squared and his face arranged into official blankness. He crossed to the *Meridian Star*, spoke briefly with a man in shore clothes who had the look of a fleet agent, handed over a folded paper, and touched his cap brim in a way that managed to be both courteous and damning.

A paper passed between the navy and the Voss fleet, first thing in the morning, before either had so much as bought a fish.

"Well," Odile said again, to the window glass. "Somebody has *definitely* been talking."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best, which was nothing visible at all.

She visited the net-menders and mentioned, idly, that the Voss fleet paid its crews half-shares and docked them for broken gear. She dropped by the Pilots' Guild and observed that cranes like the ones going onto the *Cormorant* had a way of dragging anchor chains through good lobster ground, and wouldn't that be a shame, with the season opening soon. She bought a round at the Anchor & Chain — strictly for intelligence purposes — and remarked within earshot of six fishermen that a harbor that let outside fleets strip its own coast deserved whatever empty water it got afterward.

None of it was a speech. None of it was a plan. It was yeast. You put it in, you kept it warm, and by tomorrow the whole loaf would have risen on its own.

It was nearly dark when Denny Pell found her, breathless, broom still in hand.

"Missus Vance. Missus Vance, I have to tell you something and I don't want to get nobody in trouble."

"Denny, in this harbor, trouble finds people whether they're introduced or not. Sit down."

He sat on the edge of a crate like it might bite him. "Three nights back. At the Chain. There was a navy officer — I didn't catch his name, young fella, gold braid — and he got to drinking with Mr. Quill, you know Mr. Quill, does the fleet agents' books —"

"I know him."

"Well, they got friendly, and the officer says — I heard it plain, missus, I was right behind them with the broom — he says, 'If your masters want the site, they'd best move quick. Command's drawing up closure orders. After that, nobody touches it, and the charts get amended.' And Mr. Quill laughs and says, 'Amended to say what?' And the officer says—" Denny swallowed— "'Amended to say there's nothing there.'"

The office was quiet except for the clock and the gulls settling on the roof.

"Nothing there," Odile repeated.

"Yes'm. And then Mr. Quill says, 'And the Eastwick clause?' — that's what he said, missus, the *Eastwick clause*, I wrote it on my hand so I wouldn't forget — and the officer goes quiet a long time, and then he says, 'The clause requires an heir. Find me an heir.' And they both laughed, missus. Like it was funny."

Odile looked at the boy for a long moment. Fifteen years ago, a whole family had gone off the harbor rolls as if they'd been written in water. Fifteen years, and the name still moved through this town underground, like a current under ice.

"You did right, Denny. You tell anyone else?"

"No'm. Well. God, and now you."

"Now me," she agreed. "And it stops there. Go home."

He went. Odile stood at the window a long time, watching the fleet's riding lights come up one by one across the dark water, three small fires burning over a secret.

An heir. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because Odile Vance, who read water and paperwork and men's faces, had also read something else these past weeks: a lone diver working the escarpment at night, running dark, diving deep enough to frighten the fish. A diver whose boat was old Eastwick build, patched but recognizable, if you knew what to look for. A diver who never gave a name, never took a drink, never stayed — and who, last Tuesday, had come into her office for a mooring permit and stood with their back to the wall the way people do when they're used to doors being kicked in.

She hadn't known. Not for certain. But she'd signed the permit with a steady hand and charged them the resident rate, and if that wasn't a bet, she didn't know what was.

"Elias, you old fox," she murmured. "You didn't leave them a fortune. You left them a *door*."

And now the navy wanted it bricked shut, and the Voss fleet wanted it stripped, and somewhere out on that black water was the only person alive who could open it — probably wishing, with their whole stubborn heart, that the door had stayed buried.

Odile took a clean sheet of paper. She was not a woman for speeches, but she was a woman for lists, and lists were how harbors survived.

*Tide tables,* she wrote. *Low water, escarpment reach, next six days.* Underneath: *Who owes the Eastwicks.* Underneath that, after some thought: *Everyone.*

She was blotting the third line when the knock came — two raps, brisk, official. Through the frosted glass: a silhouette in a peaked cap.

"Harbor-master?" A voice she recognized from the quay that morning. Corran. "Navy business. Might I have a word?"

Odile folded her list into her coat pocket, smoothed her face into the bland hospitality of a woman with nothing on her mind but mooring fees, and opened the door.

"Lieutenant," she said warmly. "Come in. Can I offer you some chicory? Fair warning — it's terrible."
