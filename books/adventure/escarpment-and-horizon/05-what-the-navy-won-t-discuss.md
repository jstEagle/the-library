## 5. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The knock came at dawn, which told Petra everything before the door was even open. Nobody knocked on a salvage boat's cabin door at dawn out of friendliness. Friends hammered. Friends shouted your name across the water and made you spill your coffee. A polite knock at first light was the sound of someone who had rehearsed this conversation on the way over.

Petra opened the door holding a mug of tea they had no intention of offering.

The man on the deck was navy, but he was wearing it the way some people wear borrowed coats—correctly, and yet somehow not quite his. Lieutenant's insignia, pressed uniform despite the salt spray, a face that looked like it had been assembled from reasonable decisions. He was maybe thirty-five, with the kind of pale complexion that suggested he'd spent most of his career indoors reading things other people weren't supposed to read.

"Lieutenant Corran," he said. "Royal Coastal Service." He pronounced the branch name carefully, as if Petra might otherwise confuse it with something less official.

"Petra Eastwick." They said their own name flatly, watching for the flicker. It came—a small contraction around the eyes, there and gone. So the name meant something to him. Good. That saved time.

"You've been diving the escarpment shelf."

"I dive a lot of places."

"Not a lot of places have a naval exclusion buoy on them."

"Not a lot of buoys have been dragged forty meters off station by weather," Petra said, and sipped their tea. "Terrible thing, weather."

Corran looked past them into the cabin—the charts pinned up, the gear hanging from hooks, the general organized chaos of a working diver's boat—and then back at Petra with an expression that said he'd already catalogued all of it and found it wanting. "May I come in?"

"It's my boat."

"That wasn't a no."

"It also wasn't a yes. That's how boats work. Everything's a negotiation with the water." Petra stepped back anyway, because refusing entry would be interesting to him, and the last thing you wanted to be to a man like this was interesting. You wanted to be boring. Boring men got left alone; interesting men got followed.

He stepped inside, ducked under the low beam, and stood in the middle of the cabin with his hands clasped behind his back like a man visiting a museum of poverty. His eyes went to the chart of the escarpment coast—Petra had thought about taking it down, decided against it, because taking it down would be interesting too—and lingered there exactly one second longer than casual.

"You work alone," he said.

"I work better alone."

"No tender. No spotter. No surface interval log that anyone else has seen." He turned. "That's not brave, Ms. Eastwick. That's just a way of making sure nobody notices when you don't come back."

"There's a comforting thought. I'll embroider it on a pillow."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, a man who had been trained out of smiling and hadn't fully taken. "I'm going to be direct with you."

"Please. Indirection makes me nervous, and when I'm nervous I talk too much, and nobody wants that."

* * *

Here is what Petra had learned about institutions in fifteen years of staying invisible: institutions never sent their best people to do the quiet ugly work. The best people were too valuable, or too honest, or both. What they sent was someone competent enough not to botch it and expendable enough to absorb the consequences. Watching Corran stand in their cabin with his careful posture and his rehearsed opening lines, Petra found themselves doing something dangerous—wondering which kind he was.

"So," Corran said. "The wreck on the escarpment shelf."

"What wreck?"

"The one you've been diving for eleven days."

"Have I? My logbook says—" 

"Ms. Eastwick." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was worse than a threat. "We've been watching you since the third night. Your lights. Your bubbles. There's a patrol cutter that does a loop every ninety minutes, and you've been timing your descents to its blind arc, which is either very clever or very insulting, depending on how much credit you want me to give you."

"All the credit. Take all the credit. I worked hard on it."

"The site is closed. It's been closed for—" he paused, and Petra watched him do arithmetic he didn't enjoy, "—a long time. Whatever's down there belongs to the Crown, and the Crown has determined that whatever's down there isn't worth the risk of recovering. That determination was made by people whose job it is to make determinations, and it isn't going to be revisited because one freelance salvager got curious."

"Curious," Petra repeated. "Is that what we're calling it."

"What would you call it?"

"Hungry." They set the mug down. "Look, Lieutenant. I'll save us both the speech. You're here to make me an offer, because if you were here to arrest me, there'd be two of you and one of me would already be face-down on the deck. So make it. I've got bottom time to plan."

For a moment something flickered behind his eyes—surprise, maybe, or relief. Rehearsed conversations went easier when the other party skipped ahead.

* * *

The offer, when it came, was dressed in reasonable clothing.

A payment. Not enormous—enormous would have been an insult to both of them, a number that said *we know what you know and we're scared of it*. This number was modest, almost humble. Three months of good salvage work, maybe four. Enough to re-rig the boat, replace the compressor that had been wheezing since spring, fix the slow leak in the hull that Petra had been pretending not to notice.

Enough to be tempting. That was the craft in it.

"And in exchange," Corran said, "you move on. South, ideally. There's good work south. Warm water, friendly harbors, wrecks nobody cares about. And the escarpment becomes a place you never went."

"And if I don't take it?"

"Then the exclusion zone gets enforced. Fines. Confiscation. Possibly charges—I'd have to check what the current regulations support, and frankly the current regulations are vague, because nobody expected anyone to actually—" he stopped himself.

"To actually find something worth enforcing regulations about," Petra finished.

Corran said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

Petra walked to the little porthole above the chart table and looked out at the harbor coming awake. Fishing boats heading out in their loose ragged line. Gulls conducting their eternal shakedown operations. Somewhere out past the point, under sixty feet of cold green water, a strongbox sat in a sea cave with their family crest stamped on its lid, and a charter inside it with their name written in ink that had dried before Petra was born.

Six tides. They had five left now. They'd counted the morning's tide out twice, the way you count money you can't believe you have.

"Can I ask you something, Lieutenant?" Petra said. "Off the record. As off the record as anything gets on a boat with no witnesses."

"I'd prefer you didn't."

"Do you know what's down there?"

His jaw tightened. Just slightly. "I know what my briefing file says."

"And what does it say?"

"It says the site contains hazardous material of historical origin and that recovery operations were suspended pending environmental review." He recited it the way a child recites a prayer learned by rote—accurately, and without one gram of belief. "It has been pending environmental review for thirty-one years."

"Long review."

"Bureaucracy is thorough."

"Thirty-one years thorough."

"Ms. Eastwick." He turned to face them fully, and for a moment the borrowed-coat quality dropped away and Petra saw the actual man underneath—young-ish, tired, carrying something heavy in a place where heaviness wasn't authorized. "I'm going to tell you something I will deny saying. The review isn't pending. Reviews end. This one was *ended*, by hand, by people who signed their names to ending it, and the file I was given has pages missing from the middle. I noticed because the page numbers skip. Someone in my position is not supposed to notice page numbers." He straightened his cuffs. "So when I say take the payout, I am not threatening you. I am telling you, as one person who reads documents to another: whatever you think is down there, the people who closed this site thought it was worth erasing paper over. Erasing paper is the most expensive thing a government ever does. They don't do it for hazardous material."

The cabin was very quiet. Water ticked against the hull. Somewhere above, a gull screamed its opinion about fish prices.

"And if I take the money," Petra said slowly, "and go south, and dive friendly wrecks—what happens to the cave?"

"What cave?"

"The cave that opens at extreme low tide. The one your patrols somehow haven't logged in thirty-one years, even though half the old-timers in the harbor can sing you a song about it."

Corran's expression did something complicated. "There's no cave in any file I've seen."

"No," Petra agreed. "There wouldn't be."

* * *

They stood there for a while, the two of them, in the strange truce of people who have accidentally told each other the truth.

Petra thought about the number. Four months of work. A new compressor. The hull leak fixed by someone whose trade it was, instead of patched at midnight by someone whose trade was holding their breath. A season south, warm water, anonymous harbors where nobody knew the name Eastwick meant anything at all. That was the seduction of it, and Petra was honest enough with themselves to feel the full weight of the seduction: not the money, really. The *vanishing*. Fifteen years of practice at being no one, and here was the institution itself, offering certification. Official anonymity. A stamp on the passport of nowhere.

You could build a whole life out of that. Petra knew, because they had. A life measured in miles, each port a clean slate, each departure a small erasure. It was a fine life. It was a life with no one in it, which was the price, and for fifteen years the price had seemed fair.

"Say I take it," Petra heard themselves say. "Say I sign whatever you brought and sail south tomorrow. What happens to the next diver who gets curious? Because there's always a next diver. The sea doesn't keep secrets forever, Lieutenant. It just charges interest."

"That's not my problem to solve."

"No. But it's going to become yours. That's what I hear in your voice. You've already done the math on what happens to you if this goes wrong, and you've noticed that 'following orders' only insures you so far." Petra picked up the tea, found it cold, drank it anyway. "I had an uncle who used to say that. About insurance. 'Only insures you so far.' He was talking about rigging, though. He was usually talking about rigging."

"I don't have an uncle," Corran said, and it took Petra a moment to understand it wasn't literal—that it was about the briefing files again, the missing pages, the family tree with branches sawed off. Everyone in this story was an orphan of paperwork.

He produced an envelope from inside his jacket and set it on the chart table. Plain, unsealed, fat with possibility. "The terms are inside. You have until Thursday. After that, enforcement begins, and I stop being able to be reasonable." He moved toward the door, then paused under the low beam, one hand on the frame. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much, professionally speaking—when I pulled your file, it was thin. Very thin. Fifteen years of movement and almost nothing in it. No debts, no incidents, no next of kin listed." He glanced back. "People with nothing in their files are usually hiding one of two things. Something they did, or something that was done to them. I've met a lot of the first kind. They take the money every time."

"And the second kind?"

"The second kind," Corran said, "usually asks me one question before I leave. I've never once been able to answer it." He ducked out onto the deck, and the morning light hit him, and he became just a uniform again, walking carefully along a stranger's deck toward his launch.

Petra called after him, before they could decide not to.

"Who signed the order closing the site?"

Corran stopped. Stood very still at the rail, his back turned, gulls wheeling around the mast above him like punctuation.

"That's the question," he said quietly. "Every time." And then he was down the ladder and into his launch, and the wake of him spread out across the harbor and flattened into nothing.

* * *

Petra picked up the envelope. Turned it over. Weighed it in one hand—it was mostly paper, but paper with weight, the specific gravity of other people's fear.

Four months of work. A dry bunk. A fixed hull. A life with the past amputated cleanly, no scar, no ache, no six tides ticking down. All they had to do was nothing. Nothing was the thing Petra did best. Fifteen years of professional-grade nothing.

They carried it to the chart table and set it down next to the escarpment chart, and stood looking at the two of them together—the offer and the map, the easy road and the drowned one.

The tide tables hung on the wall beside the chart, penciled full of Petra's own annotations. Five tides left. Tomorrow's low would be lower still—the spring tide building, the sea pulling back from the escarpment like a held breath. If there was ever a morning the cave would give up its secrets, it was tomorrow morning.

And underneath the tide table, where Petra had pinned it days ago and mostly managed not to look at it, was the rubbing they'd taken of the strongbox lid. The Eastwick crest. The anchor wrapped in kelp, the motto worn nearly smooth: *WE KEEP WHAT THE SEA KEEPS.*

Grandfather's handwriting lived in Petra's memory the way certain smells live—involuntary, total. They could see him writing that motto. Could see the exact grip of his pen, the way he'd tap the nib twice before a capital letter. A man who wrote mottos believed in futures. A man who sealed a charter in a box and sank it beneath a legend believed his descendants would come looking.

Or hoped. Hoped was the word that hurt.

Petra looked at the envelope a long time. Then they did the thing they'd known they were going to do since Corran's boots first touched the deck, the thing the sensible part of them had been arguing against the entire conversation and losing.

They didn't burn it. Burning was dramatic, and drama attracted attention. Instead they folded it once, slid it into the waterproof document tube beneath the chart table—the same tube where the strongbox's first photograph lay wrapped in oilcloth—and left it there. Evidence. Leverage. Or maybe just a door left open, in case the drowning started and they needed to buy their way back to the surface.

"You can still take it," Petra said aloud to the empty cabin, and the cabin, being a cabin, said nothing back.

Outside, the morning was tipping over into proper daylight, and the harbor was loud with ordinary life, and somewhere in the coastal archive three streets up from the chandlery, behind a door with a brass handle and a card that said LIMITED HOURS, there were records. Old ones. Harbor rolls, shipping registries, newspaper morgues, the sedimentary layers of a town that had been writing things down since before the navy learned to stop writing them.

If the legend of the cave existed anywhere in ink, it existed there. And if Grandfather had built that legend deliberately—if the song the old-timers sang was a message in a bottle, floated forward through thirty years of silence—then somewhere in those shelves was the reason why.

Petra shrugged into a coat that smelled of diesel and cold mornings, pocketed the notebook they kept for things they didn't want to remember accurately, and climbed up into the light.

Five tides. One archive. And a lieutenant's parting words circling back on them like a wake that wouldn't flatten: *the second kind usually asks one question.*

Who signed the order.

Petra intended to find out—even if the answer, like everything else in this story, was waiting underwater.
