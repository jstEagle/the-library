## 6. Legends Charge Admission

The coastal archive of Greywater Harbor occupied the ground floor of what had once been a customs house, and it retained the customs house's genius for making visitors feel suspected of something. The windows were narrow. The radiators clanked like a hull taking on water. And behind the front desk sat a woman of perhaps seventy with reading glasses on a chain and the expression of someone who had spent forty years catching people in lies about shipping manifests.

Petra had been in the harbor three days since the cave. Three days of not sleeping, of running the strongbox's brass fittings under their thumb until the crest was worn smooth as a river stone, of telling themselves that the sensible thing—the *Petra Eastwick* thing—was to take the box out past the shelf and drop it where no chart would ever find it.

Instead they were standing in an archive, dripping harbor fog on a linoleum floor, about to ask a stranger for help.

"You're leaking," the woman said, by way of greeting.

"Sorry?"

"Fog. On my floor." She nodded at the small dark constellation Petra had tracked across the entryway. "Either you dry off or you mop. House rules are posted."

There were no rules posted. Petra checked anyway, mostly to buy time, and found only a yellowed notice about the proper handling of microfilm. "I'm looking for records on the escarpment coast," they said. "Wrecks. Specifically—" They hesitated over the name the way you hesitate over a stair in the dark. "Heir's Wreck."

The woman took her glasses off entirely, which Petra would later understand was the archive equivalent of a fire alarm.

"Well," she said. "Sit down before you ask me anything else. You've got the look of someone who's about to hear bad news standing up, and I don't run that kind of establishment."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You said *Heir's Wreck* in a voice like a man naming his own funeral. Sit."

Her name, according to the little brass plate on the desk, was MRS. A. QUILL, ARCHIVIST. According to Mrs. Quill herself, five minutes later, she was "the only person in this harbor who reads the old ledgers for pleasure, which tells you everything about this harbor." She installed Petra at a scarred oak table beneath a lamp with a green glass shade and disappeared into the stacks with the purposeful gait of a woman who knew exactly which drawer held the skeleton.

Petra used the time to look around. The archive smelled of paper and cold iron, and the shelves rose all the way to a ceiling crossed with iron beams—customs house bones, still holding up the weight of other people's cargo. There was a map of the escarpment coast on one wall, older than any chart Petra owned, and they found themselves standing in front of it before they'd decided to move. The escarpment ran along the top edge like a signature: a long limestone scrawl with the sea gnawing at its feet. Someone had inked tiny ships along the coastline, each with a date. Most of the dates were clustered in one twenty-year span.

The span their grandfather's fleet had sailed.

"Here we are." Mrs. Quill returned with a ledger the size of a tombstone and set it down with a thump that made the lamp shiver. "Harbor annals, 1889 through 1931. Kept by a succession of harbor-masters who mostly recorded fees and complaints, but every so often one of them had a poetical turn." She opened it with both hands. "And here's your wreck."

*Heir's Wreck*, the entry began, in a hand that looped and flourished like kelp in current. Petra read it twice, then a third time aloud, because some things don't feel real until they've been given air.

*"Concerning the vessel lost upon the escarpment reef, called by the common folk Heir's Wreck: it is said she carries the lawful inheritance of the Eastwick line, and that the sea herself holds the door open six tides from the finding thereof. Let the heir come within six tides of the opening, or let the inheritance pass to the deep forever. So it is written in the charter, and so the old divers swear it, though I record it as legend and charge no admission."*

"The last part's the harbor-master being funny," Mrs. Quill said. "He wrote that every year. *Recorded as legend, admission free.* He thought he was very clever."

"It's not a legend." Petra's voice came out flat and strange. "It's a clause. It's written exactly like a clause in a salvage charter. Six tides. The same words."

Mrs. Quill looked at them over her glasses for a long moment. "You know," she said, "in forty years, most people who come asking about wrecks want treasure maps. Coin counts. Where the gold is and how fast can I get to it." She turned the page with a care that suggested the paper was older than both of them combined. "You're the first one who ever read a legend and heard *contract language*. That's either very sad or very interesting, and I suspect in your case it's both."

* * *

They worked through the morning. Mrs. Quill produced a second ledger, then a folder of loose papers tied with string gone soft as yarn, and gradually the shape of the thing assembled itself in front of Petra like a wreck rising out of green water.

The legend of Heir's Wreck was old—older than the annals—but it changed character in 1904. Before that, the entries spoke vaguely of a cursed ship, a drowned heir, the usual salt-water folklore. After 1904, the language sharpened. *Six tides.* *The charter.* *Let the heir claim or forfeit.* And in the margin of the 1904 entry, in different ink, someone had added a line so small Petra had to bend close enough to fog the page:

*E.E. says the sea keeps better faith than men do.*

E.E. Ezra Eastwick. Their grandfather.

Petra sat back. The radiator clanked. Somewhere above them, gulls argued over the customs house roofline.

"He engineered it," they said slowly. "The legend. It wasn't folklore that grew up around the wreck. He *wrote* it. Or he rewrote it—he took whatever story was already there and he built the clause into it, tide by tide, so it would spread. Harbor-masters repeat legends. Sailors repeat legends. Legends get told in taverns for a hundred years." They touched the margin note. "But contracts get lost. Burned. Seized. He hid the real terms inside the story because a story can't be confiscated."

Mrs. Quill had gone very still, in the way of people watching someone else realize something enormous. "Go on," she said quietly.

"If the navy came for the charter—and they did, didn't they, they came for everything—if it was just paper, it could be destroyed. But if the same terms lived in the mouths of every diver on this coast, in the annals, in the tavern songs..." Petra laughed, and it came out wrong, half grief. "You can't sink a legend. He made the harbor itself the backup copy."

"That," said Mrs. Quill, "is the most unflattering and most accurate thing anyone has ever said about how archives work. We're all just backup copies with worse lighting." She pulled the string-tied folder toward her. "Now. Do you want to see what else E.E. left lying about?"

Inside the folder were letters—or rather, drafts of letters, unsent, folded and refolded until the creases had gone furry. Petra recognized the handwriting from the strongbox papers, from the charter: Ezra Eastwick's hand, steady and slanted, growing shakier toward the bottom of the stack. The earliest was dated fifteen years ago. Two years before the family vanished from the rolls. Three years before Petra had walked away and never looked back.

*To whoever finds this and has the wit to look*, the first draft began. *I have watched this coast swallow our name and call it weather. I have watched men in navy coats measure our masts for salvage and our children for silence. Very well. If they will erase us from the rolls, I will write us into the tide instead. Let them burn every document. The story will remain. And a story, properly built, is a door that waits.*

Petra read it twice. Then a third time, lips moving.

*A door that waits.*

All those years they'd thought of the family exile as a wound that simply happened—a shipwreck with no cause, a hole in the water everyone agreed not to dive. They had built their whole life on the assumption that the Eastwicks had been unlucky, or foolish, or both, and that distance was the only sane response. Miles, not years. Never look back at a drowning.

But this wasn't a man writing his own elegy. This was a man laying a trail. The legend, the clause, the strongbox behind a cave mouth that only opened on a tide so low it came once in a generation—none of it was a monument. It was an *invitation*. Addressed to no one, which meant addressed to anyone, which meant addressed to the one descendant stubborn enough and stupid enough to go diving forbidden wrecks at night.

"He wanted us to come home," Petra said. Their voice cracked on *home* like ice under a boot. "Fifteen years I thought—I built my whole life on staying away from this coast, and the whole time he was—" They stopped. Started again. "He couldn't say it. Whatever the navy held over the family, it was too heavy to say outright. So he said it in contract language. In a legend. Because that was the only envelope the censors wouldn't open."

Mrs. Quill refilled Petra's tea without being asked, which in her establishment was apparently the highest available honor. "My husband was a salvage man," she said, settling back into her chair. "Forty years on the water. He used to say that men who live by the sea learn to say the important things sideways. Directly is too dangerous. You tell the truth to the water, and the water tells it back when you're ready to hear it." She tapped the draft letter. "Your grandfather was fluent."

The later drafts grew shorter. The handwriting loosened. The last one was barely four lines, dated eleven months before the fleet went down:

*If none come by the sixth tide, then let it be the sea's, and no harm done. I would rather the deep keep our name than the men who erased it. But oh, I hope. I hope someone opens the door while I'm still above water to see their face.*

Petra put the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

He hadn't seen their face. None of them had. The fleet sank; the family scattered; the door stayed shut through however many thousand tides while Petra measured their life in miles and told themselves the sealing of the past was their own idea.

Grief arrived, right on schedule—but it arrived changed, the way salvage changes when you bring it up: same object, different weight. It wasn't a stone anymore. It was a hand, extended across fifteen years and six fathoms, palm open.

*Come home. Whenever you're ready. The door will wait.*

"Well," Petra managed, after a while. "Legends charge admission, apparently. And I've been paying it my whole life without knowing there was a door on the other side."

Mrs. Quill snorted into her tea. "That's the first thing you've said today that sounds like an Eastwick. Your grandfather had exactly that kind of joke. Terrible timing, perfect aim."

* * *

Petra stayed until the light through the narrow windows went amber and the radiators gave up their shift. They copied the clause word for word into their logbook, and the dates, and the marginalia, and Mrs. Quill watched them do it with the satisfaction of a woman adding a volume to her collection.

"One more thing," she said as Petra stood to leave. She slid a final sheet across the desk—not old paper, but fresh, typed. "Tide tables. Next week's. The low water on Thursday is the lowest of the season. Lower than the one that opened your cave, if I'm reading the gauge history right—which I am, because reading gauges is the closest thing I have to a vice."

Petra stared at it. "How did you know about the cave?"

"I didn't." Mrs. Quill smiled, and for a moment the suspicion fell away and something kinder looked out. "I know the moon, the coast, and the fact that you flinched when I said *Thursday*. Archives, dear. Everything arrives here eventually." She gathered her glasses. "Whatever you're going to do, do it before the fleet does. Three provisioning chits came through the harbor office yesterday stamped with the Meridian Salvage Company. That's gunboats dressed as barges, if you ask me, and nobody asked me, which is how I know I'm right."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor in rags, and the escarpment stood clear against the evening for the first time in days—limestone and shadow, and somewhere in its feet, a door waiting on the tide.

Six tides from the finding. Petra counted backward against the tables in their pocket and felt the arithmetic close around them like a fist.

Five remaining. Four, if the fleet moved fast.

They went down to the boat at a walk and broke into a run halfway along the pier.
