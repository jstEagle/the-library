## 7. First Tide: The Descent

The tide table said the window opened at 4:17 in the morning and closed at 7:02. Two hours and forty-five minutes to claim a wreck my family had lost before I was born, or lose it forever.

I'd slept maybe three hours, and not well. Every time I closed my eyes I saw the crest on that strongbox — the escarpment and the horizon line beneath it, the little anchor worked into the H of *Eastwick* like the letter had grown a tooth. My grandfather's mark. The mark that had been stamped on crates in our boathouse when I was small enough to hide inside them, back when the name still meant something other than a warning.

I made coffee on the *Kestrel's* single burner and drank it standing at the rail, watching the water off the point go from black to a deep bruised purple. The navy patrol boat had passed at two, running dark except for its mandated lights, and it hadn't come back. Corran's man had told me the site would be "monitored." Monitored, apparently, by nobody, which suited me fine. Whatever the navy wanted buried out there, they seemed content to let the tide do their digging for them — right up until the tide started doing mine instead.

"You're up early," said a voice from the darkness astern, and I nearly went over the rail.

It was Odile Vance, standing in her skiff with a thermos and an expression that suggested she'd been up considerably earlier than me.

"Harbor-master," I said, when my heart had climbed back down out of my throat. "Do you make a habit of drifting up behind divers in the dark?"

"Only the interesting ones." She bumped her fender against my hull and held up the thermos. "Got a refill?"

I did. We traded coffee for silence, which was, I was learning, how Odile preferred to conduct most business. She looked out at the point where the cave mouth waited under the last of the ebb, and she didn't ask what I was doing. She already knew. Half the harbor knew; the other half knew by breakfast.

"Six tides," she said at last. "That's the old story. You get six."

"Five now, after this one."

"Mm." She turned her cup slowly. "My grandmother used to say the Eastwicks counted tides the way other families counted birthdays. That every claim, every salvage, every loss got measured out in them." She set the cup down. "Your grandfather once told my father that a tide was the sea keeping honest books. Everything comes back. It's just a question of when."

"That sounds like something a man says right before he drowns."

"It sounds like something a man says who plans to come back anyway." She pushed off from my hull with one boot. "Good luck, Eastwick. I'll be watching the water. Not you. The water."

I watched her skiff fade into the gray, and I thought about what she'd said, and I decided not to think about it anymore, because thinking about it made my hands unsteady, and unsteady hands were a liability below.

* * *

The descent started easy enough. The cave mouth opened onto a chimney of rock that dropped through the escarpment itself — not a natural formation, I realized almost immediately, but cut. Chiseled. Someone had carved a shaft into the cliff a century ago, squared the corners, and left tool marks I could still feel under my gloves, crisp as the day they were made. The Eastwicks hadn't found this place. They'd built it.

That should have been my first warning that nothing down here was going to be simple.

At thirty feet the light died. At fifty, my lamp found the first timbers — black oak, tarred, still holding their shape after all these years in the cold still water of the cave pool. And at sixty, I came out of the chimney into a cavern so large my light simply gave up trying to reach its far side, and I hung there in the black, breathing recycled air, listening to my own heartbeat, feeling very small and very far from anything human.

Then I raised the lamp, and the *Merrow's Pride* rose around me.

She lay on her beam ends across the cavern floor, masts snapped, hull split along the rocks that had caught her — but intact. Whole. The cold fresh water seeping through the limestone had preserved her the way peat preserves bodies: everything slowed, everything kept. Her rails still stood. Her deck cargo was lashed down where the crew had secured it, coils of rope still bright with pitch, barrels stacked in their beds as if the loading gang had just stepped ashore for lunch. A ship's bell hung in its bracket, green with age but whole, and when my exhaust bubbles touched it, it gave the faintest sound — less a ring than a memory of one.

I had dived wrecks on four coasts. I had seen ships torn open, scattered, eaten down to their skeletons. I had never seen a ship *waiting*. That was the only word for it. The *Pride* wasn't dead down here. She was waiting for someone to come back and finish whatever she'd been doing when she stopped.

I swam her length slowly, touching nothing at first, just looking. Through a broken stern gallery I could see into the great cabin, and my lamp caught a chair still upright against a desk, a lantern hanging in its gimbals, a coat draped over the chair back — wool, dark blue, brass buttons gone to bloom. Somebody had been sitting at that desk when the end came, and had gotten up, and had not come back.

I know it's foolish to feel watched by furniture. I felt watched by furniture.

The hold was where the past stopped being gentle.

The cargo was listed in my grandfather's charter — I'd read it a dozen times by lamplight, the way you reread a letter from someone you can't forgive. Timber, salt fish, iron stock, general merchandise. Honest freight for a working fleet. But the *Pride's* hold didn't match her manifest, because half the hold wasn't cargo at all. Along the starboard side, behind a false bulkhead whose seam I found by following a draft of colder water, were strongboxes. Dozens of them. Iron-banded, lead-sealed, each one stamped with the crest I'd pulled from the cave mouth three days ago, each one numbered in a hand I recognized — because I'd seen that same slanted, impatient script on the flyleaf of the only book my mother had ever taken with her when she left.

They weren't treasure boxes. Treasure gets carried on the deck, guarded, insured. These had been hidden. Hidden by people who expected the hiding to matter.

And then I found the scuttle valves.

Every old wreck has holes, and every diver learns to read them. Rocks tear ragged. Collisions punch inward. Corrosion eats thin places into lace. What I found on the *Pride's* port side was none of those. It was a seacock — a proper bronze valve, designed to be opened by hand from inside the hull — and it was open, and the wheel had been jammed open with a marlinspike driven through the spokes and bent double. Below it, scratched into the bronze housing in letters small enough to miss and deep enough to last:

**LET THE SEA KEEP THE BOOKS**

I put my glove against the scratch and stayed there a long moment, bubbles ticking up past my faceplate, while the cold worked its way through the suit and into the understanding underneath.

Ships sink. That's the sea's oldest trade. But a ship doesn't sink herself. Someone aboard the *Merrow's Pride*, on her final voyage, had opened her veins deliberately and nailed the wound wide, and had left a message for whoever came looking — a message written in the certain knowledge that the sea keeps everything, forever, and gives it back only to those patient enough to wait for the tide.

My grandfather hadn't lost this wreck. He'd sunk it. Or he'd been aboard when someone else did. Either way, the charter in my cabin, the clause about six tides, the cave cut into the living rock of the escarpment — none of it was sentiment. It was a dead-drop. A vault. A message in a bottle the size of a ship, addressed to whoever in the family was stubborn enough to come home and read it.

Fifteen years I'd spent making sure no one could find me. Turns out my grandfather had spent his last years making sure something could.

I checked my gauge. Fifty minutes down, air at two-thirds. Enough to look, not enough to take. The strongboxes would need lifting gear, rigging, probably two more dives just to plan the work properly. I marked the false bulkhead with a wax pencil on my slate, took rubbings of two of the box numbers, and made myself turn for the chimney even though every part of me wanted to stay and pry and dig and know.

On the way back through the great cabin, my lamp swept the desk one more time, and this time it caught what I'd missed: a ledger, lying open under a sheet of glass that someone had wedged over it — glass, underwater, deliberately placed to protect the page. The ink had faded to ghost-gray but the headings were legible. *Cargo received. Cargo sealed. Witnessed.* And beside the last entry, a signature I knew from the charter, followed by a second signature I didn't recognize at all, followed by a third word, larger than the rest, pressed hard enough into the paper that the nib had nearly torn through:

**REMEMBER.**

I remembered, suddenly and completely, being seven years old, sitting on my grandfather's knee in the boathouse while he sharpened a plane iron, asking him why we lived so far from the harbor when everyone knew we were a harbor family. He'd gone quiet in that way he had, the whetstone stopping mid-stroke, and he'd said: *Because the harbor forgot us first, little fish. One day it'll remember. Be ready.*

I'd thought he was talking nonsense. Old men talk nonsense. I'd built fifteen years of distance on the assumption that the past was a country you could emigrate from.

It isn't. It waits. It cuts doors into cliffs and seals boxes in lead and jams valves open with marlinspikes, and it counts the tides.

* * *

I surfaced into gray morning light and the smell of rain coming, and for a full minute I just hung on the *Kestrel's* ladder breathing ordinary air and letting my hands stop shaking.

They mostly stopped. Then I looked up, and they started again.

There was a boat out on the water. Not the navy patrol — I knew her silhouette too well by now, and this wasn't her. This was a long, low salvage tender flying no colors at all, riding high and empty, sitting off the point at exactly the distance a boat sits when it wants to see without being seen. She had no reason to be there. The fishing grounds were miles north, the shipping lane east, and no honest working boat idles off a dead coast at dawn for the view.

As I watched, she turned — unhurried, deliberate — and stood away toward the harbor, and I caught the flash of binoculars from her wheelhouse catching the early light. Catching *me*.

I hauled myself aboard, stripped my gear, and sat down hard on the deck plates with my back against the mast, and I made myself think it through like a professional instead of a granddaughter.

Someone knew I'd dived today. Someone had come to watch the first claim dive specifically — which meant someone knew about the tide schedule, the charter terms, the six-tide clock. The navy knew, but the navy had a patrol boat, and this wasn't her. Which left the other fleet. The one Odile had warned me was provisioning, the one that had been buying dive gear and compressed gas all week, the one whose owners had grown rich and comfortable in exactly the years since my family's name had been scraped out of the harbor rolls.

Comfortable men don't like it when drowned things start coming back.

And here was the thought that turned my awe cold in my chest, the one I couldn't put down: if the *Pride* had been scuttled on purpose — if my grandfather had hidden records worth killing a fleet over — then the people who'd wanted those records drowned in 1890-something might not be entirely gone either. Institutions outlive their officers. Navies especially. Somewhere up the chain, somebody's grandfather had signed the order to open that seacock, or the order to look away while it was opened, and institutions keep their grandfathers' secrets the way the sea keeps its dead.

I'd gone down expecting a wreck. I'd come up holding evidence. Evidence of what, exactly, I wouldn't know until I opened those boxes — but I knew this much: the scratch on the valve housing hadn't been a farewell. *Let the sea keep the books.* That wasn't grief. That was a plan. My grandfather had turned the ocean into a filing cabinet and dared the future to come collect.

I got the *Kestrel* underway, running easy toward the harbor, and I was halfway there when I noticed the mooring line felt wrong in my hands — shorter than I'd left it, the splice frayed, a bright clean cut in the hemp that no rock and no wear could account for.

Somebody had been aboard my boat while I was down.

I stood in my own wheelhouse, dripping, holding a piece of cut rope, and understood that the descent had only been the beginning. Whatever was in those strongboxes, someone was already willing to cut lines over it. Next time it might be air hoses. Next time it might be me.

Five tides left. And the whole harbor, it seemed, was counting.
