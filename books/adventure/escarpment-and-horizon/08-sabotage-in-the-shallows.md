## 8. Sabotage in the Shallows

Odile Vance had run the harbor office at Greywater Reach for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about trouble: it always came at dawn, it never announced itself politely, and it always smelled faintly of diesel and bad decisions.

This morning's trouble smelled of cut hemp.

She found the mooring line first — or rather, she found where the mooring line *should* have been. The *Kestrel's Rest*, Petra Eastwick's boat, was swinging loose in the shallows off the north quay, grinding her keel against a shelf of old ballast stone with a sound like a patient tooth being pulled. The line itself lay coiled on the dock planks, neat as a Sunday napkin, its end frayed in a way that no honest wear produces. Honest wear splinters. This had been sawed.

Odile stood over it for a long moment, coffee going cold in her hand, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the fleet's provisioning barges started arriving three days ago. Gunboats to the south. Salvage cranes to the east. And now this.

"Morning, Odile," called Bram Tuller from the bait shack, cheerfully oblivious. "Rough tide last night, eh?"

"Very," said Odile. "Bram. Did you see anyone on the north quay after dark?"

"Me? I was asleep by eight. Well — asleep by nine. Well." He considered. "I was here."

"Was anyone else?"

"Old Maren walked her dog around eleven. Dog stopped at that boat of Eastwick's and barked at it for a while. Dogs do that, though. That boat has an opinion about dogs, I think."

Odile thanked him and looked out at the *Kestrel's Rest* again. The diver herself was nowhere in sight, which meant Petra was either asleep below decks or already down at the wreck, and either way, Odile reflected, the woman had the survival instincts of a barnacle — attached to something dangerous and pretending not to notice.

Well. Barnacles were also the toughest things in the sea. There was that.

* * *

The air lines were worse than the mooring.

Petra surfaced an hour later, hauling herself up the ladder with the economy of motion Odile had come to recognize as the woman's entire personality, and went straight to check her gear before she even wrung out her hair. Odile watched from the quay as Petra uncoiled the reserve line, ran her thumb along it, and stopped.

From thirty feet away, Odile could see the stillness come over her. It wasn't surprise. It was the stillness of someone confirming something they'd already feared, the way you touch a bruise to make sure it's yours.

Petra looked up then, and their eyes met across the water, and Odile made a decision she would later describe to herself as *practical* rather than anything warmer.

"Eastwick!" she called. "Office. Now. Bring your lines."

"I'm busy."

"You're *fouled*. Bring your lines."

To Odile's mild astonishment, Petra brought them.

* * *

The harbor office smelled of tar paper, old tea, and the particular institutional dust that accumulates around paperwork nobody wants to file. Odile spread the air line across her desk under the good lamp while Petra stood in the doorway like a cat considering whether the room was a trap.

It probably was, Odile thought. But it was a trap with tea in it.

"Look here." She turned the hose so the lamplight caught it. A fine slit, three inches long, worked into the rubber just below the coupling — invisible unless you flexed it, and fatal at forty fathoms if you didn't. "That's not chafe. Chafe is honest. That's a blade."

"I know what a blade looks like."

"I imagine you do." Odile poured two cups of tea and pushed one across. Petra ignored it with the discipline of long practice. "Question is whose."

"The fleet's crews arrived Tuesday."

"The fleet's crews arrived Tuesday," Odile agreed, "and half of them have been drinking at the Salt Cellar every night telling everyone who'll listen that there's nothing at the escarpment worth diving, which is what people say when there is something at the escarpment worth diving." She sat back. "But I'll tell you what interests me more, Eastwick. Whoever did this knew your berth. Knew your gear locker. Knew which line was primary and which was reserve — because they only fouled the reserve. Sloppy enough to leave the mooring cut obvious, careful enough to leave the slit in the hose nearly perfect. That's not a drunk deckhand. That's somebody who's watched you work."

Something moved behind Petra's eyes — quick, guarded, gone. "Maybe I've got an admirer."

"At your age? Flattering but unlikely." Odile sipped her tea. "Here's what's going to happen. You're going to report this to me, officially, as damage from unknown causes. Not sabotage. Damage. Storm debris, if you like. We had weather Tuesday."

"We didn't have weather Tuesday."

"No one will check. Because if you file 'sabotage,' it goes in the ledger, and if it goes in the ledger, the navy reads the ledger, and if the navy reads the ledger, Lieutenant Corran learns that his little problem at the escarpment is rattled enough to complain." She set down her cup. "And if the fleet learns you're rattled, they'll stop being subtle, and subtle is currently the only thing keeping you alive."

Petra was quiet for a moment. Then: "Why are you doing this?"

"Doing what?"

"This." A small gesture that took in the desk, the tea, the whole office, possibly the harbor. "You don't know me. Nobody here knows me. I've spent fifteen years making sure of it."

Odile looked at her for a long moment — this salvage diver with salt-cracked hands and a face like a locked drawer, standing in her office holding a sabotaged air line and asking, genuinely asking, why anyone would help.

"Your grandmother," Odile said finally, "once talked me out of selling my father's boat when I was twenty-three and stupid and everything felt like drowning. Took her four hours and most of a bottle of plum wine. I never properly thanked her." She shrugged. "You have her chin. It seemed owed."

"My grandmother died before I was born."

"Yes, well. Debts don't care about scheduling." Odile stood and began gathering the fouled line into a coil. "I'll log this as storm damage. I'll also put word out through the harbor that the *Kestrel's Rest* is under my office's watch, which means any fool who touches her answers to every licensed diver between here and Cape Morrow. That's forty-one people, Eastwick, and I'd stack them against any fleet afloat."

"Why?" Petra asked again, quieter. Stubborn as a limpet, this one.

"Because," Odile said, "somebody in this harbor owes your family four hours and a bottle of plum wine, and I've decided it's me. Now sit down and drink your tea before it goes cold. We're not done."

* * *

They were not done, because Odile had spent the previous evening doing something she was quite good at and rarely advertised: listening.

The Salt Cellar kept its taproom open till midnight, and Odile had gone down ostensibly to collect a disputed mooring fee from a fisherman named Doss, and had stayed for two hours nursing a single cider and letting the fleet crews talk. Fleet crews always talked. They were paid too little and worked too hard and were far from home, and far-from-home men talk to fill the silence.

Most of it was the usual grumbling — wages, the cook, the cook again. But around ten o'clock, a lean man with a foreman's bearing had come in, and the room's noise had changed shape. Not quieter. *Carefuler.* He'd sat with the crane crew, spoken to no one outside it, and left without finishing his drink.

Nobody leaves a Salt Cellar cider unfinished. That was practically sacrilege.

"His name's Quill," Odile told Petra now, sketching on the back of an old tide chart. "Foreman of the *Marrow's Pride*'s dive crew. Been with the fleet eleven years, which makes him senior, which means he doesn't take night work himself — he assigns it. And guess who drew quay watch Tuesday night."

"You can't know that."

"I can know anything I like; I own the harbor rolls." She tapped the chart. "What I *can't* prove is that he gave the order. What I can prove is that yesterday afternoon, Quill took a launch up the coast alone and met nobody, which is what guilty men call 'checking the fishing' and what innocent men call nothing, because innocent men stay at their work."

Petra studied the chart. Up close, Odile could see the exhaustion underneath the composure — the kind that comes from sleeping badly in a boat that rocks, alone, watching the water for things that aren't fish. "So we go to the fleet master. Report him."

"And say what? 'One of your men may have committed a crime against a diver who officially isn't diving anywhere'?" Odile shook her head. "No. We do this smaller. Quill meets someone up the coast, which means Quill reports to someone above him, which means Quill is a finger, not a hand. You don't break fingers, Eastwick. You follow them to the wrist."

"And then?"

"Then we decide whether to snap it." Odile smiled, and was aware it wasn't a kind smile. "Nineteen years behind this desk, girl. You learn patience or you learn bitterness. I chose patience, mostly. But I keep a little bitterness in reserve. For occasions."

* * *

The occasion arrived faster than expected, as occasions do.

By late afternoon, Odile had confirmed two things. First, that Quill's launch had been seen anchored off Gannet Point — within sight of the escarpment, close enough to count divers' bubbles with a glass. Second, and considerably more interesting, that the *Kestrel's Rest* had taken on fresh provisions that morning from a supplier who normally served only navy contracts.

Which meant someone inside the navy was feeding Petra. Or feeding on her. With Corran, Odile honestly couldn't yet tell the difference, and she'd met enough lieutenants to know the species came in both varieties.

She was weighing this on the office steps when Bram Tuller came puffing up the quay at a pace his knees clearly resented.

"Odile! Odile, you'll want — there's a launch coming in, flying the fleet's colors, and they're asking at the fuel dock after the Eastwick boat. Asking *specifically*. Man aboard says he's got a delivery."

"For whom?"

"That's the thing." Bram caught his breath. "He wouldn't say. Just keeps saying 'the owner.' And Odile—" he lowered his voice with the delicacy of a man announcing a death, "—it's the lean fellow. The foreman. Quill."

Odile looked out across the water. The evening light was going gold and long, the fleet's masts stood dark against the horizon like a row of needles, and somewhere out past the escarpment, six tides were draining away one by one while a stubborn diver pretended not to be counting them.

"Well," she said, taking off her apron with great ceremony. "How convenient. I *do* want."

* * *

Quill was waiting at the fuel dock, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed innocence and found it ill-fitting. Up close he was older than his bearing suggested, with rope-scarred knuckles and eyes that flicked constantly toward the *Kestrel's Rest* swinging at her berth — re-secured that morning, Odile noted, with new line and a knot of her own devising that would take a knife an hour and a confession longer to undo.

"Evening, madam. Looking for the owner of that vessel. Got a parcel."

"Owner's not available."

"She's right there on the boat."

"She's unavailable," Odile repeated pleasantly, positioning herself squarely between Quill and the gangway. "I'm the harbor-master. Anything for the boat comes through my office. Harbor regulation nine. Very dull reading, but strictly enforced."

Quill's jaw worked. "It's personal business."

"Then it can wait for the owner's convenience. Which, given her schedule, might be some weeks." Odile let the silence stretch until it creaked. "Or — and I offer this purely as neighborly advice — you can tell me who sent you, and I'll pass the message along tonight, and everyone sleeps soundly."

For a moment she thought he'd bolt. Instead something in him sagged, very slightly, like rigging losing tension. He glanced once more at the boat, then held out a small oilcloth packet.

"Combination plate," he said quietly. "Brass. For a strongbox." His voice dropped further. "Tell her — tell her Tam said the third number's wrong on the one she's got. Tell her the lock was changed in the spring of the year the fleet went down. Tell her—" he hesitated, and for a heartbeat the foreman's mask slipped entirely, and underneath it Odile saw something raw and old and grieving, "—tell her her grandfather trusted the wrong cousin, and I've been paying for it ever since."

He turned and walked back to his launch before she could ask a single question.

Odile stood on the dock a long while, the packet heavy in her palm, the gold light dying on the water. *Tam.* The name meant nothing to her — and everything, suddenly, because of the way Quill had said it. Like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

She climbed the steps to the office, wrote *storm damage* in the ledger in her neatest hand, and then crossed the quay to the *Kestrel's Rest*, where a lamp was burning low in the cabin window.

"Eastwick," she called softly, rapping on the hull. "Wake up. You've got mail — and I think you'd better sit down for the name that came with it."

Above the escarpment, unseen, the fourth tide was beginning to turn.
