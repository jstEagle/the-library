## 9. Second Tide: The Manifest

The tide table said the water would be right at 4:17 in the morning, and for once the sea kept its appointment.

Petra had spent the hours between tides the way they spent most hours between anything: awake, working, and pretending that was a preference. The *Kestrel's Rest* sat on her mooring like a tired dog that had learned not to bark, and Petra moved around her deck by lantern light, checking lines, checking gauges, checking the three separate hiding places where the charter now lived—two of them decoys, because if the last week had taught them anything, it was that other people were very interested in what an Eastwick carried.

*Two tides down,* they thought, pulling on the wetsuit in the cold cabin. *Four to go.*

The arithmetic of it still felt absurd. Six tides. Their grandfather had written a legal instrument that turned the ocean's own breathing into a countdown clock, and somewhere under the escarpment, a wreck full of answers was ticking along with it. Petra had grown up measuring life in miles—the distance from one harbor to the next, from one name to the next alias—and now every measurement was tidal. It was disorienting, how quickly a person could reorganize their whole soul around a deadline.

They paused at the companionway, hand on the frame, and let themselves have the thought they'd been avoiding since the first dive.

*The ship was scuttled.*

Not sank. Not lost. *Opened*, deliberately, from the inside, and then left to settle onto the escarpment shelf like a body arranged to look natural. Petra had seen the seacocks. You didn't knock those open by accident, not all of them, not in a storm, not in a way that left the hull otherwise intact enough to preserve a captain's table set for dinner.

Someone had murdered a ship. And ships, unlike people, kept records of everything.

"Right," Petra said aloud, to the empty cabin, because saying things aloud made them real and real things could be dealt with. "Let's go meet the paperwork."

* * *

The descent was easier this time. Petra knew the route now—the crack in the escarpment wall, the corridor of stone that opened into the cave, the cave that opened, impossibly, into the wreck's forward hold through a gash torn in the hull. The legend said the cave had been there when the Eastwicks ran these waters; the geology said the escarpment was older than any family that ever claimed it. Both could be true. Families were good at making themselves seem permanent.

Forty feet down, the water turned from gray to green to a darkness that the dive lamp carved into shapes. The *Meridian's Pride*—for that was the wreck's name, and learning it had felt like learning a stranger's middle name—lay on her starboard side at fifty-one feet, which was deep enough to matter and shallow enough to lie about. Charts said forty. Charts, Petra was learning, had been edited as carefully as harbor rolls.

The forward hold was a cathedral of silt and shadow. Cargo cradles stood empty except for the ones that had held the heavy things, the dense things, the things worth bracing a ship for. Petra had catalogued them on the first dive without understanding: crates too small for trade goods, too well-built for ammunition, each one stenciled with a number and a word that time had eaten down to its bones.

Tonight they went for the manifest.

It should have been in the purser's compartment, aft of the hold, behind a bulkhead door that had swollen shut sometime during the second Eisenhower administration. Petra worked at it with a pry bar, feeling the old iron give grudgingly, the way old iron does—not defeated so much as persuaded. Inside, the compartment was dry. Not damp. *Dry.* Sealed so well that sixty years of ocean had knocked politely at the door and never gotten in.

The lamp found paper.

Petra had recovered documents before. Paper underwater is usually pulp, a brown ghost of itself, dissolving if you look at it too hard. But this was wrapped in oilcloth, then waxed canvas, then lead sheeting, and when Petra lifted the bundle free of its case, it weighed like something alive. Heavy. Deliberate. The kind of packaging you use when the contents have to survive exactly this.

They tucked it into the recovery bag, checked the clip twice, and hung in the water a moment longer, looking back into the purser's dark.

"Whatever you are," Petra murmured, and the words came out as a stream of silver bubbles, "you'd better be worth four more tides."

* * *

Surfacing at dawn is a particular kind of loneliness. The world is arriving whether you want it or not—gulls arguing, the first ferry coughing out of the harbor, the sky going pink over the escarpment like a bruise healing in reverse. Petra climbed the ladder into the *Kestrel's Rest*'s cockpit, stripped the rig, and sat dripping with the lead-wrapped bundle on the chart table in front of them, not touching it.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing they'd been circling since the cave, since the crest, since the charter with their own name inked in a dead man's careful hand:

Some doors, once opened, don't close again. And some doors were locked for a reason, and the reason might be kindness.

Their grandfather had sealed this. Had wrapped it in lead and sunk it in his own ship and built a legend around it like a fence with a gate only his blood could unlock. That wasn't treasure-keeping. That was *quarantine*. Grandfather Eastwick—whoever he had been beyond the photograph and the signature and the furious, hopeful clause—had looked at whatever was in this bundle and decided the world was safer without it, and his descendants safer still.

Petra put the kettle on. This was important. There were decisions in life that required coffee, and decisions that required tea, and decisions that required standing in your own galley at five in the morning holding a kettle like a talisman while your inheritance dripped seawater onto your chart table. This was all three.

Then they unwrapped it.

* * *

The manifest was written in a clerk's beautiful copperplate, the kind of handwriting that belonged to a man who took pride in his columns the way other men took pride in their sons. *Cargo Manifest, S.S. Meridian's Pride, Final Voyage.* Petra read down the list expecting the usual: timber, machine parts, salt cod, the mundane freight of a coastal fleet.

And then the last page.

**Special Consignment — Naval Registry 7-C. Sealed records, per Admiralty directive. Contents: Harbor Roll Amendments (1931–1949), Fleet Movement Logs, Salvage Charter Correspondence, [illegible] Testimony, Depositions re: Scuttling Order [redacted]. To be delivered under escort to [redacted]. NOT FOR COMMERCIAL MANIFEST. DO NOT LIST PUBLICLY.**

Petra read it three times. The kettle screamed and they ignored it, which for Petra was roughly equivalent to ignoring a house fire.

*Depositions re: scuttling order.*

Not cargo. Not gold, not salvage, not anything a fleet could fight over or a navy could quietly pocket. *Records.* Testimony. Names. The paper trail of something somebody in an office very far from this coast had decided should die at the bottom of the escarpment, aboard a ship full of people who presumably hadn't been told they were carrying their own epitaph.

Petra sat down slowly on the bench, the manifest flat under their palms, and did the thing divers learn never to do: they stopped moving entirely and let the thoughts come.

The Eastwick fleet hadn't been carrying treasure. They'd been carrying *evidence*. Someone had trusted their grandfather—or ordered him, or begged him—with the truth about something, and the navy had found out, and the solution had been a storm that wasn't a storm, a sinking that wasn't an accident, a family erased from the harbor rolls so thoroughly that Petra had grown up thinking *Eastwick* was just a shape their mother's mouth made when she was sad.

And the charter. God, the charter. The six-tide clause, the cave, the crest—all of it suddenly reframed. Their grandfather hadn't hidden the records and hoped no one would come. He'd built a *lock with a key shaped like his bloodline*, and waited, and died waiting, betting that someday someone with his jaw and his stubbornness would go diving where the charts said not to.

He hadn't left Petra an inheritance.

He'd left them a *job*.

"No," Petra said to the galley, to the kettle, to the pink morning coming through the porthole. "No, absolutely not. I'm a salvager. I sell things. I don't—" They gestured at the manifest, at the idea of it, at the entire concept of being the kind of person who carried truths too large for one diver. "I measure in miles. Miles have exits."

But their hands, traitorous, Eastwick hands, were already smoothing the copperplate flat, already memorizing the registry number, already doing the diver's math: *if the depositions exist, where are the originals? Who else signed? What happened to the escort?*

The kettle screamed again. Petra got up, poured tea they wouldn't drink, and caught their reflection in the galley mirror—a face they'd spent fifteen years keeping off of documents, framed now in a porthole that showed the escarpment, the harbor, the whole watching coastline.

Somewhere out there, a navy lieutenant was composing offers. Somewhere out there, a rival fleet was loading air compressors. And somewhere in between, apparently, was a middle-aged salvage diver holding a dead man's mail.

*Four tides,* Petra thought. *I have four tides to figure out what I'm willing to drown for.*

* * *

They spent the middle of the day doing what Petra did best: turning fear into procedure. The manifest went into a waterproof tube, which went into a spare tank, which went into the bilge cavity beneath the water heater, which was the third hiding place and the only one Petra actually believed in. They photographed every page with the battered camera they used for wreck surveys, and hid the film roll inside the hollow handle of the boat hook, because paranoia, like ballast, works best distributed low and wide.

By afternoon the light had gone hard and white, the kind of coastal glare that makes everything look exposed, and Petra sat in the cockpit with the tide tables and a pencil, drawing the next forty-eight hours as a series of boxes and arrows like a battle plan drawn by someone losing a war.

That was when the boat came by.

It was a harbor skiff, one of the little workboats the locals used for pots and lines, and it slowed as it passed the *Kestrel's Rest*—not suspiciously, not aggressively, just the slow pass of someone taking a look. The man at the tiller raised two fingers off the wheel, the universal maritime greeting that means *I see you and mean no harm yet*, and Petra raised two fingers back.

But as the skiff drew level, the man called across the water, casual as weather:

"Tide'll be big tomorrow. Bigger than the tables say." A pause. "Old-timers' knowledge. Escarpment pulls extra when the moon's fat."

Petra frowned. "Thanks."

"Mm." The man's eyes flicked once—just once—to the *Kestrel's Rest*'s deck, to the dive gear drying on the rail, and then away. "Harbor remembers things," he said, and put the tiller over, and chugged off toward the fish market as though he'd said nothing at all.

Petra sat with that for a long moment. *The harbor remembers things.* Either it was a warning, or it was an offer, and after fifteen years of keeping their name out of every mouth on this coast, Petra genuinely could not tell which frightened them more.

They looked down at the tide tables, at the box-and-arrow war plan, and added a new line in pencil: *Ask Odile who that was.*

* * *

Evening came down over the anchorage like a lid. Petra cooked actual food—eggs and the good bread, the meal of a person trying to convince their body it had a future—and ate in the cockpit watching the lights of the rival fleet come on one by one across the bay. Three ships now. Maybe four; the farthest light might have been a buoy with ambitions. They sat there like patient animals, and Petra knew, the way you know weather in your knees, that they weren't waiting for the tides.

They were waiting for Petra to find something worth taking.

Which meant the manifest couldn't stay aboard. Which meant the copies needed to travel. Which meant trusting someone, which was a skill Petra had let rust for fifteen years and was now being asked to use under combat conditions.

They were washing the pan when the radio crackled—the VHF, channel 16, the hailing frequency everyone monitors and nobody admits to listening to. Static, then a voice, carefully neutral, reading coordinates.

Coordinates Petra knew by heart. The mouth of the cave.

"—advisory to all vessels. Restricted zone established per naval order. Entry prohibited. Salvage operations in the vicinity are subject to inspection and seizure. Repeat—"

Petra dried their hands very slowly.

Seizure. Inspection. The words were polite. The words were always polite; that was how you knew. Somewhere ashore, Lieutenant Corran was converting patience into paperwork, and the paperwork would arrive with the morning tide, and it would be legal, and it would be final, unless the thing in the bilge cavity beneath the water heater became something more than a dead man's secret first.

Petra picked up the handset, then put it down. Picked up the tide tables instead, and studied the fat moon rising over the escarpment, huge and orange and shameless.

*Big tide tomorrow,* the old-timer had said. *Bigger than the tables say.*

Third tide. The captain's cabin. The logbook—if it was still there, if the navy's divers hadn't beaten them to it, if the fleet's guns stayed quiet another eighteen hours.

"Okay, Grandfather," Petra said to the moon, to the manifest sleeping in its bilge, to the whole drowned conspiracy of it. "You wanted an heir. Let's see if you left me anything besides the name."

Out past the point, running lights moved against the dusk—a single boat, fast, running dark except for the minimum the law allowed, cutting a line straight toward the anchorage. Petra watched it come, and something cold and old stirred in their chest, some memory from before the miles began.

They knew that wake pattern. They'd seen it as a child, from a dock, waving at it.

The boat didn't slow. It passed the *Kestrel's Rest* at half a cable, close enough to count rivets, and for one second the running lights caught the face of the man at the wheel—

A face Petra had last seen in a funeral photograph, back when they were small enough to be lifted up to see it.

The boat ran on into the dark, and Petra stood in the cockpit with the dish towel still in their hands, hearing their own heartbeat over the sound of the water.

"That's impossible," they said.

The sea, which had heard that sentence before and would hear it again, said nothing.
