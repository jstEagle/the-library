## 10. The Face in the Fleet

The trouble with pretending you have no past, Petra had learned, is that the past never agrees to the arrangement.

They were three days into the fleet's arrival—three days of watching masts multiply along the horizon like a bad rash—and they'd developed a system for it. Dive at slack water when the gunboats' search patterns thinned. Surface behind the sea stack where the escarpment's shadow pooled even at noon. Keep the *Kestrel's* engine cold and let the current do the talking.

It was working. It was also exhausting, and Petra was beginning to understand that exhaustion made people careless, because on the fourth morning they rowed out to check a marker buoy they'd planted near the cave mouth and found someone else already there.

A diver, treading water beside the buoy, mask pushed up on his forehead. Grey-stubbled, sun-cured to the color of old rope, wearing a fleet wet suit with the name *MARROW* stenciled across the shoulder in peeling letters.

Petra's first thought was tactical: he's seen the buoy, he'll report it, the site is blown.

Their second thought arrived a half-second later and stopped their heart like a fist through the ribs.

Because the man turned toward them, squinting against the morning glare, and Petra knew that squint. Knew it from a boathouse roof twenty years gone, from a man teaching a child to read tide tables by lantern light, tapping the paper with a finger missing its top joint. A finger that was missing its top joint right now, raised against the sun.

"Uncle Tam," Petra said. The name came out of them sideways, uninvited, like water finding a crack.

The man went very still. Treading water, he went still—which took a particular kind of discipline—and for a long moment the only sound was the buoy knocking against its chain and the gulls arguing over something dead further up the coast.

"You've got the wrong man," he said finally. His voice was gravel that had been rained on for years. "Name's Corrick. Marrow's crew. Salvage certification out of Grayhaven."

"Your name is Tam Eastwick," Petra said. "You taught me to tie a bowline before I could write my own name. You burned your hand on the kettle the winter I was seven and told me scars were just stories your skin remembered." They heard their own voice shaking and hated it. "You've been dead fifteen years."

Something moved behind the man's eyes—the flicker of a lamp behind a shuttered window—and then the shutters came down again, and what looked back at Petra was a stranger wearing a familiar face.

"Wrong man," he repeated, and rolled off the buoy, and swam for the fleet with long, economical strokes that Petra would have known anywhere, because they'd copied those strokes as a child, paddling in the shallows, trying to be him when they grew up.

* * *

Petra did not go back to the cave that day. They sat in the *Kestrel* with the hatch open and the morning coming in cold, and they let the memory arrive the way it wanted to, all of it, unsorted.

Tam Eastwick. Younger brother to Petra's mother, the family's wild card, the one who could hold his breath four minutes and laugh about it after. The one who'd been aboard the *Meridian's Pride*—the last ship of the Eastwick fleet—when it went down off the escarpment with nine souls aboard. They'd pulled one body from the water. It hadn't been Tam's. But the harbor had needed an ending, and a drowned man with no boat and no trace after three weeks of searching was an ending good enough for a grief-stricken town, and so Tam Eastwick had been declared lost, mourned, carved onto the memorial stone in the churchyard, and forgotten.

Presumed dead. Presumed by everyone, apparently, including the boy or girl or whatever Petra had been back then, small enough to still believe that the adults around them knew things.

And now here he was, breathing fine, diving for the fleet that had come to strip the wreck their family had died around.

Petra laughed once, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. Fifteen years they'd kept themselves sealed—no harbor rolls, no family name spoken aloud, no letters, no visits, a life measured in miles put between themselves and everything Eastwick. And the whole time, one of the ghosts had been swimming around two hundred miles south, working under another name, presumably counting his miles too.

The question that wouldn't leave them alone, sitting there in the cold hatch-light, was simpler and worse than *how*. The question was: *did he know I was alive?*

Because if he did—if he'd known, all these years, while Petra ate alone in port towns and slept in the *Kestrel*'s narrow berth and told no one anything—then the exile Petra had chosen had been, in part, a lie someone else had permitted. And Petra found they couldn't decide which possibility hurt more: that Tam had searched for them and failed, or that he hadn't searched at all.

There was only one way to find out. And neutral water, as any diver knew, belonged to whoever got there first.

* * *

The fleet anchored a mile east of the escarpment, close enough that at night Petra could hear their generators through the hull, a low industrial hum like the coast itself had started snoring. Their tenders ran regular circuits between the ships and a supply sloop out of Grayhaven, and twice a day—one mid-morning, one late afternoon—a single tender crossed the open water between the fleet and the harbor, carrying messages, officers, and once, memorably, a piano, which struck Petra as either morale management or a threat.

Petra chose the mid-morning run, and chose the fog that came with it, a thin grey soup rolling in off the deep water that would give them ten minutes of cover and maybe eight of nerve.

They took the skiff, not the *Kestrel*—smaller, quieter, deniable—and they waited at the edge of the shipping lane where the tender would pass, riding the swell with the engine off, feeling like every kind of fool. This was not salvage. This was not tactics. This was a forty-year-old diver about to ambush their dead uncle in the fog because their chest hurt.

*You've got the wrong man,* the stranger had said. Fine. Then Petra would ask the wrong man why he tied his bowlines left-handed.

The tender came through the fog at half speed, running lights doubled in the murk, and Petra let it pass and then followed at a distance, matching its wake, until it slowed at a point well clear of both fleet and shore—the crews called it the parley patch, Odile had told her once, though Odile had never told Petra much of anything, and Petra had learned it instead from a drunk deckhand who'd mistaken them for a buyer. Neutral water. Where men from rival fleets met to trade, argue, and occasionally throw each other overboard.

The tender stopped. A single figure climbed down into a dinghy and began to row, unhurried, toward the middle of the patch.

Toward Petra.

For one absurd moment Petra thought: *he knew. He's known since the buoy.* Then they saw the figure's silhouette—broader, younger, wrong build entirely—and understood it was coincidence, some fleet man rowing out to meet a contact, and that they were about to be discovered loitering in the parley patch like the world's worst smuggler.

Except the dinghy came alongside, and the man in it looked down into the skiff, and said, in a voice roughened by salt and years:

"I wondered how long you'd sit out here."

Tam. Not the fleet man at all—he'd watched Petra follow the tender, had slipped over the side somewhere in the fog, and had rowed out to meet them on his own terms. Of course he had. He'd always been three moves ahead; it used to be the thing Petra loved most about him.

"You cut your hair," Petra said, because it was the only sentence in their head that wasn't screaming.

"You got old," Tam said. "We're even."

* * *

They rafted the boats together, hulls knocking gently, two people floating on a seam of fog with the whole armed weight of the fleet a mile away and the whole armed weight of the navy somewhere beyond that. Tam sat with his oars shipped and his hands flat on his knees, and up close Petra could see the years on him properly now—the weathering, the scar tissue on his forearms that hadn't been there before, the way his left ear ended in a ragged crescent instead of a lobe.

"That was the cold," he said, catching them looking. "Water gets you eventually. Takes payment in pieces."

"Fifteen years," Petra said. "Fifteen years, Tam. There's a stone in the churchyard with your name on it. My mother—" They stopped. Swallowed. "She grieved you. She grieved you for years, and then she grieved the whole family, and then she died, and I stood at her grave alone because I'd already left by then, because staying felt like drowning, and the entire time you were *alive*, doing salvage runs out of Grayhaven under a name that isn't yours."

"Yes," Tam said.

Not an excuse. Not an explanation. Just *yes*, laid on the water between them like a card played face-up, and somehow that was worse than any lie would have been.

"Why?" Petra's voice cracked on it. "Why let us think—why let *me* think—"

"Because being dead was the safest thing I ever was." He said it quietly, without drama, the way you'd report a depth reading. "Petra, listen to me. You've been inside that wreck. You know it's not treasure down there. You know what the navy's afraid of. Well, I've known for fifteen years, because I was in the water when the *Pride* went down, and I saw what happened before it happened, and I swam away from my own funeral because the alternative was joining the rest of them."

"The scuttling."

"The scuttling," he agreed. "And everyone connected to it. Your grandfather figured it out first—that's why the charter exists, that's why the legend does, he built himself a door in case anyone ever came home. But doors open from both sides, girl." His eyes, pale as harbor ice, held hers. "The men who sank that fleet are mostly dead now, but the institution isn't. The habit isn't. There are people in that fleet—good divers, some of them, friends of mine, God help me—who were paid to make sure no Eastwick ever claims that wreck. Paid well, and warned better. And there are others who aren't in it for money at all. They're in it because if that manifest surfaces, a lot of respectable names go into the water with the ships."

"So you took their money." Petra heard the bitterness come out of them and didn't try to catch it. "You took fleet money and you've spent fifteen years making sure the door stays shut. That I stay gone. That's what this is, isn't it? You weren't surprised to see me at the buoy. You've been watching me since I arrived."

"I've been watching you since before you arrived," Tam said. "I flagged your boat registration in Grayhaven eighteen months ago. I've known every move you've made on this coast, because I made sure I would." He leaned forward, and the fog beaded on his stubble, and for a moment—just a moment—Petra saw the man from the boathouse roof underneath, the one who'd tapped tide tables with a shortened finger. "Everything I've done since the *Pride* went down, I've done to keep one Eastwick alive. You. You're the last one. Do you understand what that means? You're not just the heir, Petra. You're the evidence. As long as you stay anonymous, you're a rumor. The moment you claim that charter—legally, publicly, in front of witnesses—you become a fact. And facts get handled."

"Handled," Petra repeated. "That's a word men use about oil spills."

"It's a word men use about Eastwicks," Tam said. "Ask me how I know."

The fog was thinning. Petra could feel it going, the light strengthening from grey to silver, and somewhere out in it the tender was waiting, and somewhere beyond that the fleet's generators hummed their low patient hum.

"Come with me," Petra said suddenly. "Leave the fleet. Come to the harbor. Odile Vance is there—she remembers you, she keeps your old chart weights on her desk like relics. We can do this together. The claim, the records, all of it. You don't have to be dead anymore."

Something crossed Tam's face then that Petra would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It wasn't temptation. It was grief—the specific grief of a man being offered exactly what he wants and knowing the price tag attached.

"No," he said. Gentle. Final. "But I'll tell you something for free, because you're family, and that's the one debt I've never managed to drown." He reached across and gripped the skiff's gunwale, steadying the two boats, steadying perhaps himself. "The strongbox. Your grandfather's box, in the cave. It takes two keys and a combination. The keys are findable—I won't say where, and don't ask. But the combination is six numbers, and six numbers is a date, and it's not the date you think it is. Everyone assumes it's the day the fleet sank. It isn't. It's older than that. Ask yourself what your grandfather loved more than his ships, and count backward from there."

Petra stared at him. "You know the combination."

"I'm the only one left who does," Tam said. "Besides you, maybe, if you think hard enough about a summer when you were small." He shipped his oars. "One more thing, and then we're strangers again, and it has to hold, Petra, because if the fleet sees us talking, neither of us sees another tide."

He leaned close, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, just breath and salt between them.

"Claim the wreck, and you die with it. That's not a threat from me—it's a promise from men whose names I'm not free to say. I've buried three divers who got curious about that compartment, and I dug the holes myself so nobody else would look too closely at what was in them." His jaw worked. "Stay anonymous. Take Corran's money if he offers it again. Let the wreck keep its secrets, and live. Please. You're the last of us, and I am so tired of burying my family."

Then he pushed off, and rowed into the thinning fog, and became a stranger's silhouette, and then nothing at all.

* * *

Petra sat in the skiff for a long time after the sound of the oars faded, rocking on the swell, doing arithmetic they didn't want to do.

Three divers. Three holes. Uncle Tam with a shovel.

And yet: *the combination is six numbers, and it's not the date you think it is.*

Betrayal and lifeline, wearing the same face, holding the same hands. Petra turned it over and over as the fog lifted and the escarpment emerged ahead of them, black and patient, the cave mouth hidden somewhere beneath the water line, waiting out its tides.

*A summer when you were small. What your grandfather loved more than his ships.*

Petra rowed for the *Kestrel* with their mind racing backward through years they'd sealed shut, through lantern light and tide tables and a voice saying *count backward from there*, and by the time they reached the mooring they had a suspicion forming—not a certainty, but a shape, like a wreck resolving out of green water.

Six numbers. A date older than the sinking.

Petra hauled the skiff aboard, checked the fog-burned horizon for gunboats, and went below to dig through the one box of family things they'd never had the heart to burn.

If they were right about the date, they'd need to be back in the cave before the third tide.

And if they were wrong—they'd be diving blind into a rigged compartment with a fleet overhead and a dead uncle's warning ringing in their ears.

Petra opened the box.
