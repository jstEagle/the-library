## 11. Third Tide: The Sealed Compartment

The third tide came in gray and businesslike, the way tides do when they've stopped being a metaphor and started being a schedule.

Petra sat on the deck of the *Kestrel's Mercy* with a mug of tea going cold in one hand and the cargo manifest spread out under a weight of two wrenches and a coffee tin, and read it for the fourth time. Not treasure. Records. Crates stenciled HARBOR AUTHORITY — DO NOT INVENTORY, which was the kind of stencil that made inventors nervous and salvagers rich, or dead, depending mostly on luck.

"Records," Petra said aloud, to the gull perched on the rail who had been freeloading breakfast crumbs for three days. "Somebody scuttled a whole fleet over paperwork."

The gull said nothing, which was more courtesy than most people had shown.

The plan for today had been simple, in the way that all plans are simple right up until they aren't: go down at slack water, breach the captain's cabin, get out before the current turned. The cabin was aft, below the main deck, behind a door that the first survey dive had shown to be swollen shut with sixty years of salt and pressure. Breaching it meant a crowbar, a saw, and patience — and patience underwater is a different animal than patience on land. On land you can walk away from your impatience. Down there it follows you, breathing through your regulator.

Petra checked the tanks twice. Checked the knife, the light, the backup light, the line reel, and the little brass compass that had belonged to a grandfather Petra had spent fifteen years pretending not to remember. The compass didn't point north anymore; it pointed wherever it pleased, which felt appropriate. It was coming along anyway.

"You're not obliged," Petra told the reflection in the porthole glass — the reflection being the only conversation partner who never asked questions. "You could take Corran's money. You could be nobody again by Thursday."

The reflection looked tired and unconvinced, which was fair. Nobody believes their own lies in a porthole.

* * *

The descent was clean this time. No blackout, no gray closing in at the edges — just the slow blue dimming, the wreck rising up to meet the light like something surfacing in reverse. The *Meridian's Pride*, the Eastwick flagship, lay on her starboard side along the escarpment shelf, masts pointing down into the deep like fingers pointing at whatever came next.

Petra had been thinking about that image too much lately. Fingers pointing down. At what?

The cabin door was exactly where the survey said it would be, and exactly as hostile. Petra wedged the crowbar into the seam and leaned, and the wood groaned — a long, low sound that traveled through the hull and out into the water, and Petra froze, because down here every sound has an audience even if the audience is only fish. Nothing came. Just the tick of settling silt and the patient percussion of the sea doing its eternal bookkeeping.

Second lean. Third. On the fourth, the door gave all at once, and a cloud of sediment bloomed up like ink, and Petra hung in the water outside the doorway while the cabin erased itself and then slowly, slowly reassembled — a desk first, then a chair, then the pale suggestion of shelves, then everything else arriving out of the murk in order of density.

It was small. That was the first thing. Captains' cabins in stories are grand, all brass and charts and windows onto adventure. This one was barely bigger than the galley on the *Mercy*, and it was tidy in a way that hurt. A chair pushed in. A cot made up — actually made up, blankets squared, after sixty years underwater, because wool holds its discipline longer than people do. A shelf of books fused into a single brick. And on the desk, centered, weighted down with a brass bar, a logbook.

Not floating. Not scattered. *Placed.*

Petra's heart did something complicated against the ribs, there in the cold and the dark, six fathoms past sensible.

Because things don't stay placed on desks underwater. Things fall, drift, scatter, get eaten. Unless someone put them somewhere they'd stay. Unless someone wanted them found — but found by someone specific, someone patient, someone who'd come looking. The manifest had been in the hold, buried in cargo like any honest document. This was different. This was a letter left on a table with the lamp lit.

Petra took the logbook. Leather-bound, brass corners gone green, the cover stamped with the Eastwick crest — the escarpment and the horizon line, the same mark that had stared up out of the strongbox like an accusation. Under the crest, in faded gilt: *E. EASTWICK, MASTER.*

Grandfather Ezra. The man whose face lived in Petra's memory as a smell — pipe smoke and tar and the particular salt of a coat worn every day for thirty years — rather than as a picture. The man who had taught Petra to tie a bowline before Petra could write their own name, and who had then, along with everyone else named Eastwick, evaporated from the coast so completely that asking about them in any harbor tavern got you a look usually reserved for people who mention politics at funerals.

Petra clipped the logbook inside the jacket, checked the air — plenty, thanks to a tide that was behaving — and took one last look around the cabin. The books on the shelf were readable through the fusion: navigation, mostly, and a slim volume of poetry that made Petra's throat tighten for reasons it declined to explain. On the wall above the cot, a small framed photograph had survived better than it should have, its glass holding back the sea the way glass does when it decides to make a point. Two figures on a dock. A woman laughing, caught mid-turn. A younger man holding a child on his shoulders, the child's hand reaching for something out of frame.

Petra knew the child's hand. Had spent years not knowing it, which is a full-time occupation.

Time to go. The tide wouldn't wait, and neither would whoever had fouled the mooring lines two nights ago. Petra kicked for the doorway, paused, and looked back once at the made-up cot, the squared blankets, the lamp-less lamp hook waiting for a light that would never come home.

"I'm reading it up top," Petra told the cabin. "You'll forgive me. My hands are useless down here."

The cabin, being a cabin, said nothing. But the whole way up the anchor line, Petra had the distinct sensation of being escorted.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Mercy* rocked gently at anchor, the afternoon sun doing its best, the tea now fully cold and therefore legally dead. Petra dried off, wrapped the logbook in a towel like a rescued bird, and carried it below to the chart table, where the single lantern threw a circle of warmth just big enough for one person and one secret.

The leather cover opened with a crackle. The early pages were ordinary — a master's log, neat and dry, entries about weather and cargo and a cook named Bram who was apparently at war with the concept of rice. Petra found themselves smiling despite everything. Grandfather Ezra had written his cook's grievances with the gravity of naval dispatches. *Bram maintains rice requires stirring. Rice disagrees.* Fifteen years of silence, and the first thing the man said across it was a joke about rice.

Then, four-fifths of the way through, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. The dates stopped being regular. And the voice underneath the words shifted from a master reporting to a man confessing.

*We took aboard crates at Kessel Point under navy escort. Manifest says instruments. The crates are wrong. Too heavy for instruments, too many seals for anything honest. First Mate Odile's mother — no. Wait.*

Petra stopped. Read it again. *First Mate O. Vance.* Odile. Harbor-master Odile Vance, who had covered for Petra at the office, who had slipped warnings under the *Mercy*'s door with the discretion of a woman passing notes in church — her mother had sailed with the Eastwicks. Had been *first mate*. Petra set the book down, walked a lap around the tiny cabin, and came back, because some facts need to be circled before they can be believed.

He kept reading. Ezra kept writing.

*Opened one crate. Not instruments. Records. Ledgers, correspondence, orders — the navy's own paper, the kind that proves what was done at the escarpment stations during the war years. Names. Dates. Signatures. The sort of cargo that doesn't sink quietly and doesn't float either. The escort officer smiled too much and asked too few questions, and I have been a salvage master thirty years and I know what a man looks like when he is delivering a coffin and calling it freight.*

*We are not carrying treasure. We are carrying evidence. And I believe we are meant to arrive nowhere.*

Petra's hands had gone still on the page. Outside, a wave slapped the hull, indifferent. Inside, fifteen years of carefully maintained ignorance began, quietly, to catch fire.

The next entry was dated three days later.

*Navy launch shadowed us since Kessel Point. Captain Halloran received a signal tonight he would not show me. He showed me his face instead, and a man's face will tell you what a signal won't. We are to alter course to the escarpment route. There is no reason to take the escarpment route. There is every reason someone wants us there.*

*I have done a cowardly thing and a brave thing in the same hour, which I am told is how most hours go at sea. I have written to my son — Petra's father — and told him nothing, because telling him would paint him to the same target. Instead I have drawn up a charter. If the worst comes, the wreck and everything in it passes to my heirs, conditional on claim within six tides of its opening. Six tides. Tide law is old law, older than the Admiralty, and the harbor courts still honor it because the harbor remembers what the capital forgets. Let the navy erase an Eastwick from the rolls. They cannot erase a charter written into the bedrock of tide law.*

*If my grandchildren are reading this — and one of you stubborn creatures will be, because stubbornness is the Eastwick inheritance and I apologize for nothing — then understand: we did not run. We were driven. And what they sank us to bury, I have arranged to keep. The records are in the sealed compartment below the waterline, portside, forward of the engine room. The combination is with Tam.*

*Tam knows. God forgive me, Tam knows, and Tam was always the soft one, the one they could bend. If you find this and Tam is not beside you, do not assume him dead. Assume him ashamed. Shame keeps men alive far longer than loyalty does.*

Petra read that last line four times. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, the gull complained about something.

Uncle Tam. Presumed drowned with the fleet, mourned in absentia by a family that no longer existed to mourn, name scrubbed from the harbor rolls along with all the rest. *Assume him ashamed.* Petra hadn't seen Tam in the flesh yet — but two nights ago, on neutral water, a diver from the rival fleet had surfaced near the *Mercy* without authorization, looked at Petra through a mask for three seconds too long, and descended without a word. Petra had filed it under hostility. It might have been recognition. It might have been grief wearing a stranger's gear.

And there it was, the thing Petra had been circling for days without landing: the family hadn't vanished. They'd been *erased*. Scrubbed from the rolls, driven from the coast, sunk with their own ship and blamed for the sinking — all because Ezra Eastwick had loaded the wrong honesty onto an honest ship. The exile Petra had built a life inside, the miles measured out year after year to keep the past at hull's length — none of it had ever been a choice. It had been a sentence, served by descendants who didn't even know the crime.

Inheritance, Petra thought, wasn't a legacy. It was an indictment. The crest on the strongbox wasn't a coat of arms. It was a warrant.

Petra laughed — one short, ugly syllable that bounced around the cabin and came back embarrassed. Corran had offered a payout to stay invisible. The navy had spent sixty years making sure the Eastwicks stayed invisible. And Petra had *thanked* them for it, in a way, had treated anonymity as a personal achievement, a discipline, a craft. All that time swimming away from a name, and the whole time the name had been chained to the seabed, holding down the one truth the Admiralty couldn't afford to have surface.

They weren't offering Petra freedom. They were offering to renew a sentence.

The last entry in the logbook was short. The handwriting had steadied again, the way it does when a man has finished deciding.

*Weather fair. Crew in good spirits. Bram has conceded that rice benefits from stirring, though he claims this was always his position. Whatever comes tomorrow, let the record show the Meridian's Pride sails well and her people are good. History will be written by whoever survives to hold the pen. I intend to inconvenience the pen.*

*— E. Eastwick, Master*

Petra closed the book and sat in the lantern light for a long time, listening to the water work the hull.

Then, because grief and fury need somewhere to put their feet, Petra got up and made fresh tea, properly this time, and drank it standing at the porthole watching the escarpment go gold and then purple in the evening. Three tides down. Three remaining. The sealed compartment waited portside, forward of the engine room, holding whatever the navy had sunk a fleet to bury — and between Petra and it stood a rival fleet provisioning in the harbor, a lieutenant whose orders were starting to smell to him like something rotten, and an uncle who might be alive, might be ashore, might be the reason the mooring lines had been cut.

The knock came at dusk. Three raps, unhurried, on the *Mercy*'s hull ladder — the knock of someone who expected to be let in.

Petra opened the hatch onto the last of the light and the silhouette of a broad-shouldered man in fleet oilskins, hood back, face older than memory but built on the same bones as Petra's own.

"Hello, little minnow," Uncle Tam said. "Your grandfather always wrote too much. Can I come aboard before we both say something we mean?"
