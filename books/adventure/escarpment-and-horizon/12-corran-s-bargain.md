## 12. Corran's Bargain

Lieutenant Aldous Corran had been in the navy long enough to know that the most dangerous documents were never the ones marked *Top Secret*. Those, at least, came with a warning label, like a jar of pickled eels. The dangerous ones arrived in plain buff folders with no markings at all, delivered by a courier who didn't salute and didn't stay for tea.

The folder on his desk this morning was one of those.

He'd read it three times before his second cup of coffee went cold, which for Corran constituted a crisis of almost religious proportions. He was a man who believed that coffee, like justice, should be administered hot and without delay.

"Sir?" Ensign Pell hovered at the door, a young man whose ears turned pink whenever he was about to deliver bad news. They were pink now. "The harbor-master's office called again."

"What did they want?"

"To know if we're planning to close the escarpment site. Officially. She asked whether she should post notices."

Corran looked at the folder. The folder did not answer him. Folders rarely did, though he sometimes suspected this one was thinking about it.

"Tell her nothing yet," he said. "Tell her I'm reviewing the situation."

"You've been reviewing it for two days, sir."

"Yes, Pell. That is generally how reviewing works. It involves review."

Pell's ears went from pink to something approaching crimson, and he withdrew. Corran envied him. There was a time—eleven years ago, fresh from the academy, still convinced the service was a machine for making the world more honest—when Corran himself would have asked the impertinent question. Now he was the man being asked, and he found he disliked the position intensely.

He opened the folder a fourth time.

*Directive 9-Charcoal.* No issuing office. No signature, only a stamp: an anchor wrapped in a serpent, which was not any heraldry Corran recognized from his twenty-two years of service, and he had spent an embarrassing portion of those years studying heraldry because he found it restful. The directive ordered three things:

One: seize all vessels operating within the exclusion zone, beginning with the salvage boat *Kestrel*, registered to one P. Eastwick.

Two: declare the escarpment site closed under the Wrecks Act, retroactive to the original sinking date—which was legally nonsensical, since you could not retroactively close a site that had been open for forty years, but the directive did not appear interested in legality so much as in the general shape of it.

Three: ensure no documentation pertaining to the vessel designated *EW-7* reached civilian hands. The phrase used was *"ensure,"* unqualified, unrepeated, and heavy as a depth charge.

Corran set the page down and rubbed his eyes.

Here was the thing that kept snagging in his mind like a fishhook in a sweater: the date. *EW-7* had gone down forty-one years ago. Corran was thirty-eight. Whatever Directive 9-Charcoal was protecting, it predated his birth, his commissioning, his entire understanding of what the navy was for. And yet the paper in front of him carried the quiet authority of something that had been maintained, updated, passed hand to hand like a family recipe—for four decades.

You did not keep a lie warm for forty years unless it mattered to someone very powerful, or someone very frightened. Often both.

* * *

At noon he walked down to the harbor wall, ostensibly to inspect the patrol launch, actually because his office had begun to feel like a room inside the folder itself. The morning was bright and cold, gulls wheeling over the fish market with the entitled air of gulls everywhere, and Odile Vance was on her office steps arguing with a man about mooring fees.

"—and I don't care what your cousin says, Tulliver, your cousin has said many things and been right twice, both times by accident—"

"Odile." Corran touched his cap. "Harbor-master."

"Lieutenant." She gave him a look he couldn't parse, which unsettled him; he was accustomed to parsing Odile Vance the way one parses weather—quickly, accurately, and with mild dread. "Come to inspect my harbor? It's full of boats. Very inspective."

"I wanted to ask you something off the record."

"Nothing's off the record in my harbor, Lieutenant. Everything here floats into everything else eventually. But go on."

"The diver. Eastwick." He watched her face carefully. "What do you know about them?"

"That they pay their slip fees on time, which puts them in the top ten percent of my clientele. That their boat is better maintained than half the fishing fleet. And that they dive alone at night, which is either courage or stupidity, and in my experience the two are cousins." She folded her arms. "Why?"

"No reason."

"Aldous Corran." His first name, deployed like a boarding pike. "I have known every officer stationed at this depot for thirty years, and I will tell you what I told the last one who came sniffing after an Eastwick: the harbor remembers things the navy prefers forgotten. If you're about to do something you'll need forgiving for, do it quickly, because forgiveness around here has a short shelf life."

"And if I'm trying to prevent something?"

Her expression shifted—a flicker, there and gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "Then you'll be the first," she said, and went back inside.

Corran stood on the steps a while longer, watching the water. Somewhere out past the escarpment, a woman or a man or simply *Eastwick* was diving on a wreck that forty years of paperwork insisted did not exist. And somewhere above them both, someone with a serpent-wrapped anchor for a signature was waiting for Corran to do his job.

His job. He turned the phrase over. Lately it felt less like a coat that fit and more like a coat he'd stopped questioning the fit of.

* * *

The order came through at dusk, by signal lamp from the offshore cutter, spelled out in flashes against the darkening water: *EXECUTE DIRECTIVE 9-CHARCOAL PHASE ONE AT FIRST LIGHT. CONFIRM.*

Pell stood beside him on the signal deck, reading the flashes upside down with the ease of youth. "Phase one, sir? What's phase two?"

"Pell, when a document doesn't tell you what phase two is, the correct response is not to ask."

"Sorry, sir. Only—it seems odd, doesn't it? Seizing a salvage boat over a wreck nobody's filed a claim on. There's no legal basis I can find. I checked the Wrecks Act. Twice."

"I know you checked it twice. You left both copies on my desk with the relevant sections highlighted. In yellow. And pink."

"I thought color-coding might help, sir."

It had helped. That was the trouble. Pell's highlighting had made it impossible to pretend otherwise: there was no section of any act that authorized what the directive demanded. There was only the directive, and the serpent, and the silence of everyone above him.

"Confirm receipt," Corran said at last.

The lamp flashed. The cutter flashed back. Out on the black water, somewhere between the light and the escarpment, the *Kestrel* rode at anchor, and its owner was presumably asleep, or diving, or doing whatever it was people did when they had spent fifteen years making themselves into nobody.

Corran went below and did something he hadn't done since his academy days. He opened his personal locker—the locked compartment, the one Pell thought held spare charts—and took out a slim notebook. On the first page, in his own careful hand, dated eleven years ago, was a single line:

*Day one. Serve honestly. Ask questions.*

Below it, nothing. Eleven years of blank pages, because asking questions had turned out to be incompatible with advancement, and he had chosen advancement, quietly, the way most people choose most things—without ever quite admitting a choice had been made.

He uncapped his pen.

*Day four-thousand-and-something,* he wrote. *Ordered to seize a boat belonging to a family my own service erased. Ordered to protect a secret older than I am. Asked myself: whom does the navy serve? Realized I was afraid of the answer. Realized further that being afraid of the answer is itself an answer.*

He stared at the words. They looked small on the page, and enormous in his chest.

* * *

First light came gray and windless, the sea like poured pewter. Corran took the launch out himself rather than send Pell, telling himself it was standard practice for a seizure operation, knowing perfectly well it was because he wanted to see Eastwick's face when he did what he'd come to do. He needed to see whether the diver looked guilty. Guilt would make this easier. Guilt was the ballast that kept these operations steady.

The *Kestrel* sat low and tidy at her mooring, lines coiled with a precision that spoke of either discipline or loneliness. A figure was already on deck, checking tanks—Petra Eastwick, compact and salt-cured, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had long ago stopped performing for anyone.

Corran brought the launch alongside. "Permission to come aboard."

Eastwick looked up. Sea-colored eyes, unreadable, taking in the launch, the two armed sailors behind him, the paper in his hand. "That depends, Lieutenant. Are you coming aboard to talk, or to take my boat?"

"The latter, I'm afraid." He held up the order. "By authority of the Wrecks Act and standing naval directive, this vessel is seized, and the escarpment site is declared closed. Effective immediately."

"Closed." Eastwick said the word slowly, tasting it. "A wreck that's been sitting there for forty years. Closed now. Funny timing."

"The timing isn't mine."

"No," Eastwick agreed. "I imagine it isn't." And then, with a calm that Corran found far more disturbing than anger would have been: "Can I ask you a question, Lieutenant? One diver to a man with a gunboat."

"You may ask."

"Why does the navy care? Not the fleet—fleets are just greed with engines. The *navy*. Somebody signed papers to put you here. Somebody upstairs decided a rusted hulk on the escarpment was worth a lieutenant, two sailors, and a legal fiction." Eastwick stepped closer, boots quiet on the deck. "What are you protecting?"

And there it was—the question Corran had been circling for three days, asked plainly by a stranger on a salt-stained deck, while the sailors behind him shifted uncomfortably and the pewter sea waited.

"I follow orders," Corran said, and heard how it sounded. Hollow. Secondhand. A coat borrowed from a much larger man.

"So did whoever sank it," Eastwick said quietly.

The words landed harder than they should have. Corran looked at the order in his hand—at the seal, the stamp, the word *ensure* sitting in its sentence like a stone in a shoe—and felt something shift in him, some structural member giving way with a sound only he could hear.

"Eastwick," he said. "I'm required to inform you that you may contest this seizure at the district admiralty office. The nearest one is in Port Calloway. Two days' sail."

"I know where it is."

"The clerk there keeps irregular hours. Often the office is closed entirely. Sometimes for weeks." He met those sea-colored eyes. "I'm told the paperwork moves faster if the claimant appears in person. With evidence. Original documents, ideally. Sealed ones carry particular weight with the admiralty."

For a moment neither of them moved. Behind him, one of the sailors coughed. The gulls wheeled. Somewhere out past the point, thunder muttered—not rain clouds, Corran realized, but a squall line building over the open water, fat and purple-black, dragging the whole horizon down.

Eastwick's expression changed by a millimeter—enough. Understanding passing between them like a rope across dark water.

"That's very procedural of you, Lieutenant," Eastwick said.

"I believe in procedure," Corran said. "It's the only religion I was ever any good at." He turned to his sailors. "Put a seal on the cabin. Log the seizure. We're done here."

As the launch pulled away, he watched the *Kestrel* shrink behind them—watched, too, the squall line crawling toward the escarpment, and understood, with the clarity that arrives precisely too late to be comfortable, exactly what he had just done. He had handed a condemned diver a map to the gallows and called it directions to the courthouse.

Back at the depot, Pell was waiting with the morning signals. "Sir, message from the cutter. Fleet's been sighted—six hulls, guns run out, making for the escarpment. And, sir—" Pell hesitated, ears going pink. "There's a second copy of Directive 9-Charcoal in the signal file. Older edition. Dated thirty years back. Same serpent stamp."

Corran took the flimsy. Same wording. Same *ensure*. Thirty years of the same order, issued again and again to officers whose names he didn't know, each of whom had presumably stood where he stood, holding the same paper, feeling the same crack run through their keel—and each of whom had, apparently, confirmed receipt.

He looked out the window toward the escarpment, where the storm was gathering and six gunboats were converging on one small boat and one stubborn diver, and he picked up his pen.

*Day four-thousand-and-something, continued,* he wrote. *I am not the first officer to notice. But perhaps I can be the last one who does nothing about it.*

Outside, the first thunder rolled in off the sea, and the tide tables on his wall showed the fourth tide of six beginning its turn.
