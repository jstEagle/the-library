## 13. Fourth Tide: The Storm Gate

The barometer on the *Kestrel's* bulkhead had been falling all morning like a man sliding down a banister he'd sworn he wasn't going to slide down. By noon it had dropped so far that old Maren's voice in Petra's head—*when the glass runs, the diver hides*—was practically shouting.

Petra ignored it. There were things you ignored and things you didn't, and a grandfather's logbook describing a sealed compartment aboard Heir's Wreck fell firmly into the second category. Especially when the salvage fleet was due to anchor over the site by tomorrow's dawn tide, guns showing, lawyers showing, everything showing except mercy.

The squall came in off the escarpment at two in the afternoon, early and rude, the way weather does when it knows you're in a hurry. It didn't look like much from inside the harbor—a gray smudge eating the horizon—but Petra had spent fifteen years reading water the way other people read faces, and this smudge had teeth.

"Marginal," Petra said aloud to the cabin, to the charts, to nobody. "That's the word. Marginal."

The word did not help. Marginal conditions meant the navy's patrol boats would be hunkered down behind the point, which was good. It also meant forty-knot gusts on the surface, visibility underwater dropping to arm's length, and a descent line that would thrash like something dying. It also meant the fleet might delay. Might. Odile's last message, passed through the fishmonger's boy in the usual cipher of wrapped herring, had been blunt as a cleaver: *They're not waiting for weather. They're waiting for you to quit.*

Petra checked the air lines twice. Checked the reserve bottles three times. Checked the knife at the belt, the slate, the spare light, the line reel, and then—out of habit rather than sense—the small brass compass that had belonged to a grandmother whose face Petra could no longer quite assemble from memory. The needle swung and settled, pointing home, or what had passed for home before home became a word Petra avoided.

"Fourth tide," Petra told the compass. "He gets four tides. I get four tides. Fine. We're both cutting it close."

* * *

The run out of the harbor was the worst fifteen minutes of the season. The *Kestrel* took the swell on the quarter and complained about it in the language of groaning timbers, and twice Petra had to choose between a heading that kept the boat comfortable and one that kept it upright, and chose upright both times, because comfort was a luxury for people with more tides left than they needed.

The escarpment rose out of the murk like the back of something sleeping. Rain came sideways. At the mooring buoy—Petra's own buoy now, set with Odile's quiet blessing and a length of chain borrowed from a man who owed the Eastwicks more than he knew—the sea was a churning gray argument.

Petra anchored anyway.

There is a moment, known to every working diver, when the body votes no and the mind overrules it. Petra had learned long ago to let the vote happen, to honor it, and then to put on the gear regardless, because the body was a coward with excellent instincts and the mind was the only part of you that could count tides. The suit went on stiff with cold. The hood swallowed the world down to a porthole. The first breath off the regulator tasted of rubber and resolve.

At the surface, before the descent, Petra paused—one full minute, hanging in the green chaos, watching the wreck's shadow far below shift and shudder as the storm's energy drove down through the water column. Fifteen fathoms down, the Eastwick fleet's last ship lay on her side against the escarpment's foot, and somewhere inside her, according to a dead man's careful handwriting, was a compartment sealed with wax and stamp and intention, rigged against anyone who came without the right to come.

*Rigged,* the logbook had said. Petra had turned that word over for two days. Rigged could mean a wax seal that crumbled if you breathed on it wrong. Rigged could mean a collapse trap, the kind of thing a desperate man builds when he expects the navy to come digging. Grandfather Eastwick had expected exactly that. Had built accordingly. Had died—or vanished, the two words had stopped being different—before he could ever disarm his own work.

"You built a door," Petra said into the regulator, bubbles tearing away southward, "and then you locked it against everybody including me. Thanks, Grandfather. Really. Warm man."

And went down.

* * *

The descent line was a living thing in the surge, whipping figure-eights, trying to take Petra's fingers with it into the dark. Visibility, which had been a generous six fathoms on Tuesday, was down to perhaps four feet—a brown-green soup shot through with drifting curtains of sand. Petra descended by feel and by count, hand over hand, watching the depth gauge the way a nervous passenger watches the road.

At ten fathoms the cold changed character. At twelve, the surge eased, the storm's fists unable to reach this deep, and the water went still and strange, the way it always did down here, as though the wreck sat in a pocket where time had been poured thick. Petra's light found the hull—black oak furred with weed, the great scar along her flank where she'd struck the escarpment, or been struck, depending on which truth you believed, and Petra now believed the second one with a fury that made the regulator feel tight.

Through the breach. Into the corridor. The interior of Heir's Wreck was a held breath. Lanterns still hung in their brackets, their glass clouded but whole. A boot stood upright in the silt, patient as furniture. On the second dive Petra had found the mess table set for eleven, plates furred white, cups waiting, and had surfaced that night unable to eat, seeing eleven places set for men who had gone into the water and never come out—and one of them, possibly, an uncle who hadn't drowned after all, who had climbed into a navy boat and lived a life under another name. That thought was a splinter Petra kept deciding not to dig at yet. Later. After the fourth tide. After the compartment.

The logbook's directions had been precise in the way of a man who knew precision would matter: *Past the galley, through the coal hatch, third frame aft of the mast step. Look low. Look for the mark.*

Petra swam the corridor with the light cupped close, past the galley—its stove still bolted down, its shelves holding jars whose contents had become geology—and found the coal hatch wedged half-open by some ancient violence. Through it, into a space that had been the forward hold, where the silt lay deep and undisturbed except for the single track of Petra's own previous survey.

Third frame aft of the mast step. Petra counted frames with a gloved hand, touching each rib of the old ship like a rosary. One. Two. Three.

Look low.

The light dropped. And there it was.

Not hidden, exactly. Disguised. What looked like collapsed ballast stone—a heap of rock any salvager would swim past without slowing—was arranged, Petra saw now, with a mason's care. The stones were fitted. The gaps were mortared with something that had cured harder than the surrounding rot. And set into the face of the heap, low, exactly where a kneeling man's eye would fall, was a disc of brass the size of a dinner plate, green with age, wiped clean in a circle by Petra's own thumb before the mind caught up with the hand.

The crest. The stag and the escarpment and the three stars. The Eastwick mark, staring back at its last heir across sixty years of cold water.

Petra's heart was doing something unprofessional. The gauge said the breathing gas was fine; the rest of the equipment disagreed, rattling with pulse. *Steady.* The old discipline, the miles-not-years discipline, the keep-moving-keep-stranger discipline. But underneath it, for the first time in fifteen years, something warmer was moving too—not courage exactly, more like recognition. This was family work. Stubborn, secretive, over-engineered family work. Nobody else on this coast would have built a door out of lies and mortar and then stamped it with the family name where only family would kneel.

Grandfather hadn't locked Petra out. He'd locked everyone else out, and left the key shaped like a name.

* * *

The problem was the trap.

Petra circled the ballast-falsework slowly, light close, cataloguing. The logbook had warned: *The seal is honest. The stones are not. Come with the charter or don't come at all.* Which was poetry, and also, Petra now understood, engineering documentation. The fitted stones weren't just a facade. They were load-bearing lies. The compartment behind them—a void maybe two meters square, framed in iron—was held shut against the pressure of the deep by the very stones that hid it. Remove them in the wrong order, and the sea would come through that opening like a fist through paper, bringing half the hold's ceiling down with it. Grandfather had built it to survive divers. To kill careless ones. To make sure that whatever was inside stayed inside until someone came who deserved it—or at least someone who read instructions.

Petra knelt in the silt and studied the brass disc. Around its rim, worn nearly smooth but legible to a fingertip, ran letters. Not words. Numbers. A sequence. And beneath the sequence, in letters small enough that they must have been cut with a jeweler's tool:

*IN ORDER OF THE TIDES.*

Six numbers. Six tides. The clause from the charter, written in brass: the claim window, the deadline, the inheritance terms—all of it reproduced here, on the door itself, for the heir who arrived with the knowledge and the right.

But the sequence was incomplete. Five numbers, clear as bell-strokes. The sixth—the final number, the one that would release the last stone and open the way—had been effaced. Scratched over deliberately, deeply, by a tool wielded in anger or grief or simple desperation, sometime in the years after the seal was set.

Someone had been here. Someone had knelt where Petra knelt, had read these numbers, and had destroyed the last one before leaving—so that no one after could open what they themselves could not, or would not.

Petra hung there in the black water, breathing recycled breath, and felt the shape of that absence like a tooth socket. Uncle Tam. It had to be. The man who survived, who took the fleet's coin, who had spent years making sure no Eastwick returned—he had come here once, early, alone, had gotten this far, had knelt at his father's door with five-sixths of a key, and had gouged out the rest rather than watch anyone else succeed where he had chosen to fail.

Or couldn't bring himself to try. Petra remembered the man from childhood only in fragments: big hands, a laugh like a hatch slamming, the smell of tar. A man who taught a child to tie knots. A man who had then, apparently, spent decades tying a different kind.

*Come with the charter or don't come at all.* But the charter was in the strongbox, and the strongbox was in the captain's cabin, and the combination—Odile's research suggested—was split between the charter itself and something Tam carried. The old man had designed a lock that required his sons to cooperate even in ruin. Even across death. Even across betrayal.

"He wanted us to need each other," Petra said, and the words came out as a stream of silver beads rising toward a surface forty feet up and a storm above that. "The stubborn, sentimental, impossible old—"

The light flickered.

Petra froze. Checked the lamp. Checked the gauge. The lamp steadied—cold, probably, the storm chill reaching even here—but the flicker had landed on something Petra's eye had skipped twice already: a thin wire, hair-fine, running from the base of the falsework up into the darkness of the breached ceiling, disappearing among the broken frames overhead. Not corrosion. Not weed. Wire, drawn taut, anchored, deliberate.

A trigger. Not for the stones—for the ceiling. If the falsework moved without the proper sequence, the wire would pull, and whatever grandfather had braced or propped or counterweighted up there would stop being braced, and several tons of ship would come down on the heir and the inheritance together, and the navy would get its erasure after all, tidy and free of charge.

Petra backed away one slow kick at a time, hands raised slightly, absurdly, as if the wreck could see surrender. The wire hung in the light's edge, quivering faintly with the distant pulse of the storm, patient as everything else down here.

* * *

Surfacing was like being born angry. The squall had matured from threat to fact; the *Kestrel* was pitching hard enough that catching the ladder took three tries and cost a glove. Petra got the gear off by touch and memory, got below, got the hatch dogged, and stood dripping in the lamplight while the boat argued with the sea around her.

Five numbers. That was what the fourth tide had bought: five numbers and a destroyed sixth and a wire that promised death for impatience. Tomorrow the fleet would arrive. Tomorrow the fifth tide would come and go whether Petra dove it or not, and the sixth—the last tide, the deadline written into law and legend and brass—would arrive the day after, with gunboats overhead and every looter on the coast watching.

Unless the sixth number existed somewhere besides that scratched-out brass.

Petra spread the logbook pages on the chart table, weighted with a mug and a wrench, and read the final entries again, hunting. Grandfather wrote like an engineer and grieved like a carpenter—in joints and fittings, never directly. But near the end, in a hand grown shaky, one line:

*Tam has the last of it. Pray God he is kinder to my heir than he was to me.*

Petra sat down slowly on the locker, the boat rolling beneath, rain hammering the deck overhead like applause from an enemy.

Tam had the number. Tam, who sailed with the fleet coming to bury the site. Tam, who had warned *claim the wreck and die with it*—and Petra understood now that the warning hadn't been a threat. It had been a man reporting, accurately, on the machinery his own father had built.

Somewhere out in that storm, on one of those gun-gray ships, an uncle was carrying the last digit of his family's name in his head, and had been carrying it for thirty years like a stone he couldn't swallow and wouldn't spit out.

Petra looked at the barometer, still falling. Looked at the chart, where the fleet's approach lane crossed the site like a blade.

"Fifth tide," Petra said to the empty cabin. "I'm going to need a bigger boat. Or a smaller family."

Outside, lightning walked the horizon toward the escarpment, and the sea between here and the fleet began, very quietly, to fill with ships.
