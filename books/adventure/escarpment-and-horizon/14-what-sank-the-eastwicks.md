## 14. What Sank the Eastwicks

The compartment was exactly where the logbook said it would be, which was the first thing that frightened Petra Eastwick that morning.

Everything else—the squall still muttering overhead, the visibility like swimming through cold tea, the collapse trap rigged somewhere in the bulkhead seam—those were fears with familiar shapes. But a dead man's directions being *right*, sixty years after he'd written them, in a hand cramped from writing by lamplight on a rolling deck? That was a different order of dread. That meant her grandfather had known, precisely and calmly, that someone would come looking. That it would take this long. That whoever came would need to be told exactly where to put their hands.

*Third frame aft of the galley bulkhead,* the logbook had said. *You'll know it because the others are rust and this one isn't.*

He'd been right about that too. Petra ran her gloved fingers along the frames in the dark, counting, and felt the difference before she saw it: five ribs of flaking iron, and then one that came up smooth as a church rail. Someone had coated it—tallow and pitch, maybe, or something better—and someone had maintained it, because tallow didn't last six decades. Which meant Tam. Which meant her uncle had been down here, keeping this one small thing oiled, all those years he'd spent making sure no Eastwick ever came home.

She didn't have room in her chest for that feeling yet. She set it aside the way she set aside tools, handle out, ready to pick up later.

* * *

Getting the panel open took forty minutes and most of her patience.

The trap was elegant, she'd give it that. Not a charge—a scuttler's trick, a counterweighted section of hull plating that would shear loose if you pulled the panel instead of lifting it, bringing half the compartment down and burying whatever was inside under a ton of escarpment rock. Her grandfather hadn't wanted to kill anyone. He'd just wanted to make sure that anyone who came for his records came *carefully*. Came the way family would.

*Pull nothing,* the logbook had warned. *Lift everything. The Eastwicks were never thieves. We only ever carried what was given to us.*

"Given," Petra muttered into her regulator, working the lifting bar into the seam. "Sure. Given at gunpoint, probably."

The panel came free with a groan of protesting sealant, and the water inside the compartment—sealed water, still water, water that hadn't moved since before Petra was born—swirled out around her in a slow gray bloom. She got her light in through the gap.

Three things sat in the dark.

A lead-lined document case, naval pattern, stamped with an anchor-and-crown insignia that made her stomach drop through her boots and keep going. A smaller oilskin packet, tied with waxed cord, unmarked except for a single letter burned into the leather: **E**. And between them, absurdly, a child's tin soldier, one arm raised forever in a salute, propped upright against the case as if standing guard.

Petra looked at the tin soldier for a long time.

She remembered it. That was the terrible part. She remembered sitting on her grandfather's knee in the boathouse, age six or so, marching that soldier along the armrest while he told her about the fleet—*the pride of the coast, little fish, eleven ships and every one of them named after somebody's mother.* She'd asked him which ship was named after her mother, and he'd laughed until he wheezed, and then he'd gone quiet in a way she hadn't understood then but understood now, here, sixty fathoms down, holding the guard duty he'd assigned to a toy.

He'd known she'd come. He'd left her something to recognize.

"You old schemer," she said, and her voice came out wrecked even through the regulator, and she had to spend a minute just breathing, watching her bubbles climb toward a surface that suddenly seemed very far away and very thin.

Then she got to work.

* * *

The oilskin packet came out first. It was light, buoyant almost, and she stowed it in the mesh bag at her hip with the tenderness she usually reserved for live ordnance. The document case was another matter—it weighed like sin and guilt combined, lead-lined against salt water and probably against prying instruments too, and getting it through the breached panel without snagging her lines took every trick fifteen years of salvage had taught her. She talked to it the whole way. She'd learned long ago that heavy objects behaved better if you insulted them personally.

"Oh, you're a big one, aren't you. Real impressive. Sixty years of sitting on your arse in the dark and now you want to be difficult—"

The case cleared the frame. Somewhere above her, thunder rolled down through the water like furniture being dragged across the ceiling of the world, and Petra checked her gauge and did not like the number she saw, and decided not to see it.

One more thing. The logbook had been specific: *Read nothing down there. Water and paper and grief don't mix, and neither do secrets and panic. Bring it up whole or don't bring it up.*

But there was a fourth item she hadn't expected—a flat brass plate screwed to the inside of the panel itself, and her light caught lettering on it, engraved, deliberate. She shouldn't have stopped. She stopped anyway.

It read:

**TO WHOEVER THE TIDES BRING —**
**IF YOU ARE AN EASTWICK, WELCOME HOME.**
**IF YOU ARE NOT, KNOW THAT WHAT IS WRITTEN BELOW**
**WAS TRUE WHEN WE SANK AND WILL BE TRUE WHEN YOU READ IT.**
**THE NAVY DID NOT LOSE THIS FLEET.**
**THE NAVY AIMED IT.**
**— A. EASTWICK, MASTER, ESCARPMENT CHARTER**

Petra hung there in the gray-green dark, sixty fathoms under a squall, and felt fury arrive in her body before her mind could catch up to it. It came in through the soles of her feet, up her spine, hot behind the eyes—the specific heat of a person learning that the story they were told about their own life was a forgery. Fifteen years of exile. Fifteen years believing the Eastwicks had failed, had scattered, had somehow deserved the erasure that followed them like a smell. Every harbor master who'd checked her papers twice. Every employer who'd gone quiet when her surname surfaced. Every mile she'd put between herself and this coast, measuring her life in distance because distance was the only unit that never lied to her.

They hadn't failed. They'd been *deleted*.

Her grandfather had watched eleven ships go down with names on them, had swum away from the only life he had, had spent his remaining years building a legend like a message in a bottle—six tides, an heir, a claim—because a charter clause was the one weapon the navy couldn't confiscate. Law was law, even for admirals. You could sink a fleet in an afternoon. You could strike a family from the harbor rolls with a signature. But a salvage charter, properly witnessed and properly claimed, was iron. He'd wrapped the truth in a fairy tale because fairy tales were the only cargo the navy never thought to search.

And everyone in the harbor had laughed about it for sixty years. *The cave legend. The heir's wreck. Old man Eastwick's ghost story.* Odile had said it herself, that first night, with affectionate scorn: *legends are how coasts lie to themselves.*

No. Legends were how coasts told themselves the truth in a voice the censors wouldn't recognize.

Petra's breath was coming too fast. She knew the signs; she'd pulled two divers back from the edge of panic in her career and both times the trick had been the same—give them a task, any task, the smaller the better. So she gave herself one. Check the tie-downs. Check the bag. Check the gauge.

The gauge was worse than the last time she'd looked, which was the point of gauges, and she began the ascent.

* * *

Breaking the surface was like being born into bad weather. Rain needled the swells, the sky was the color of dishwater, and *Kestrel*—her boat, her whole floating biography—was wallowing at her mooring with the anchor light swinging like a metronome counting down something. Petra hauled herself up the ladder with the document case slung across her back, and the weight of it on deck made the whole boat sit differently, stern-heavy, as if the past had physically boarded.

She got her mask off. She got her hood back. She stood in the rain shaking—not from cold, though the cold wasn't helping—and looked at the case dripping on her deck.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the case, to the storm, to her grandfather wherever he was. "Let's see what you died carrying."

Below, in the relative dry of the cabin, she cut the waxed cord on the oilskin packet first. Inside: paper. Dozens of sheets, folded tight, protected by wax and grease and sixty years of a dead man's stubbornness. Harbor rolls. She recognized the format immediately—she'd forged enough variations of harbor documentation in her exile years to know it like scripture. These were the escarpment rolls, going back generations, and the Eastwick name ran through them like a keel through a hull. Licenses. Berth rights. Salvage charters. A family woven into the harbor's official memory, page after page—

And then the pages changed. Newer paper. Different ink. A navy stamp where the harbor seal should be, and a single line repeated down the margin in a clerk's careful hand:

*Struck per Admiralty Order 44-C. No appeal. No record retention.*

Page after page of it. Eleven ships' worth of crew, their families, their berths, their licenses—all of it struck, all of it erased, an entire community deleted from the harbor's books with the bureaucratic tidiness of a pencil line. Petra found her grandmother's name. Found her mother's, aged nine, listed as a dependent berth-holder. Found her own name, added years later in her grandfather's hand and then struck through in someone else's, the ink of the cancellation laid over the ink of the hope.

"No appeal," she read aloud. "No record retention." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "You didn't even let them keep the *paper*."

The document case was heavier business. Lead-lined, sealed with a naval compression clasp—the kind used for evidence, for court exhibits, for things meant to survive scrutiny. Her grandfather hadn't stolen it. She understood that with sudden certainty. He'd *rescued* it. When the fleet went down, when the order came to lose the records along with the ships, someone in the salvage operation—maybe her grandfather himself, master of the charter, with legal right to claim wreckage—had pulled the evidence out of the water before the navy could finish drowning it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: the Admiralty's own paperwork. Orders, signed and countersigned. A requisition for scuttling charges, logged as "training expenditure." A map of the escarpment approaches with the Eastwick fleet's route marked in red and a notation in a staff officer's hand: *Interception window favorable. Weather will be cited.* And beneath it all, the crown jewel, the thing the navy had killed eleven ships and one family to bury: a manifest discrepancy report showing that the Eastwick fleet had been carrying, quite legally and quite visibly, duplicate records of naval coastal operations—records that, if they ever reached a tribunal, would hang a dozen careers from the yardarm.

They hadn't sunk a treasure fleet. They'd sunk a filing cabinet. And then they'd spent sixty years making sure nobody remembered there had ever been a cabinet.

Petra sat back on the cabin bench, rain hammering the deck above, and let the two feelings arrive together the way the logbook had promised they would: fury and vindication, twins, holding hands. Vindication because her grandfather had been right, had been *righteous*, had built his ridiculous six-tide legend as a load-bearing lie over a foundation of pure fact. Fury because being right had cost him everything, and cost her mother a childhood, and cost Petra a family she'd spent fifteen years pretending she'd never had.

"They erased you," she said to the empty cabin. "And I helped. Every mile I sailed away, I helped."

The boat knocked gently against its mooring, patient as a dog waiting for a walk.

* * *

She was still sitting there, documents spread out and drying under the lamp, when she heard the engines.

Not one engine. Several, big ones, the deep diesel thrum that meant displacement hulls and gun mounts and men who filed paperwork about weather. Through the rain-fogged port she saw them coming around the escarpment headland in formation, running lights doubled, searchlights already sweeping the water in long white blades.

The fleet. The rival fleet, tipped and provisioned and hungry, arriving a tide early—or arriving exactly on schedule, because someone had leaked the tide tables along with everything else. Behind them, farther out, the grayer shapes of navy patrol boats holding station like chaperones at a dance neither side wanted to admit was a fight.

Petra looked at the documents drying on her bench. Then at the searchlights raking the swells. Then at the documents again.

Sixty years ago, her grandfather had watched those same lights come over that same headland, and he'd had a fleet, and it hadn't been enough. She had a thirty-foot salvage boat, a bag of wet paper, and roughly four hours until the next low tide.

But she also had something he'd never gotten: the truth, intact, in her hands. And somewhere behind those searchlights, if Odile Vance had done half of what Petra suspected she'd been doing all week, there were harbor divers who might be willing to stand between a stolen family and the people who'd stolen it.

Petra began folding the documents back into their oilcloth, fast and precise, hands steadier than they had any right to be.

"Hold together," she told the papers, the boat, herself. "One more tide. Grandfather only needs us to hold together one more tide."

Out on the black water, the first searchlight swung toward *Kestrel* and stopped.
