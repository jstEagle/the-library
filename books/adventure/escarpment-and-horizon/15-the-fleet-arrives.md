## 15. The Fleet Arrives

Odile Vance had run the harbor office at Gullspit Reach for nineteen years, and in that time she had developed a personal taxonomy of trouble. There was small trouble, which arrived by rowboat and could be settled with tea and a fine. There was medium trouble, which arrived by letter and required a second pot of tea. And then there was the kind of trouble that arrived with three gunboats and a salvage fleet riding low in the water like a drunk uncle at a christening.

That kind of trouble anchored off the escarpment at half past four on a gray morning, and Odile watched it from her office window with a mug of coffee she had stopped drinking because her hands needed to be free for what came next.

"Brannigan," she said without turning around.

Her deputy looked up from the tide tables he'd been pretending to read for an hour. "Aye?"

"Count them for me. I want to be wrong in a precise way."

Brannigan leaned past her shoulder. Three navy gunboats sat in a wedge formation a half-mile offshore, searchlights sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs—the way a man sweeps a room before he decides whether to burn it down. Behind them, six salvage vessels flew the colors of the Meridian Combine, their cranes already swinging out over their rails like mantises stretching after a long nap.

"Nine hulls," Brannigan said. "Ten if you count that tug hanging back like it's ashamed of itself."

"I count ten." Odile set down the mug. "And I count one diver down there under all of it who has until the sixth tide to finish something, and no idea yet that she's got company."

"You think she knows?"

"I think Petra Eastwick has spent fifteen years perfecting the art of not knowing things," Odile said. "It's practically a trade skill. But even Petra can't not-know nine hulls."

* * *

The harbor woke the way harbors do—gradually, then all at once. By five o'clock the fishermen were standing on their decks looking south instead of checking their nets. By half past, word had traveled up the escarpment road and come back down wearing embellishments: the navy was here to seize the wreck; the navy was here to bomb the wreck; the navy was here because there was gold; the navy was here because there was something worse than gold.

Odile stood on the office steps and let the crowd gather, because crowds were weather, and you didn't argue with weather—you read it.

"The Combine's been tipped," said Marta Quill, who ran the chandlery and had never once in her life entered a room quietly. "Somebody leaked. Somebody with a navy desk and loose lips."

"Somebody always leaks," Odile said. "Question is what we do about it now that they have."

"What *can* we do?" That was young Fen Okafor, third-generation netmender, arms crossed, chin up, twenty-two years old and certain of everything in the way only twenty-two-year-olds can be. "They've got guns. We've got—" He gestured at the harbor, at the boats, at the general situation. "Fish."

"We've got more than fish," Odile said. "We've got a wreck sitting inside our charter waters, we've got a claim clause older than any of those gunboats, and we've got—" she paused, weighing how much to say, and decided the morning had gone past the point where secrets helped anyone but the people with guns, "—an heir working that site right now. An Eastwick."

The name went through the crowd like a dropped glass. Old women crossed themselves. Old men took off their hats, which in Gullspit Reach was either reverence or the beginning of an argument.

"The Eastwicks are dead," someone said.

"The Eastwicks were *erased*," Odile corrected, and let the word sit there. She'd spent two nights with the documents Petra had copied to her—she would not say smuggled, though smuggling was the accurate verb—and she knew now what she'd only suspected for thirty years: that the family hadn't abandoned the coast, the coast had been made uninhabitable for them, deliberately, by men with ledgers and gunboats very much like the ones currently squatting on their horizon.

"So here's my thinking," she went on, before the crowd could start arguing with itself. "Petra needs one more tide. One. The sixth tide turns tomorrow evening, and everything legal, everything binding, everything that makes that wreck hers instead of theirs, happens underwater where none of us can follow. Our job is not to dive. Our job is not to fight. Our job is to make sure that when Petra comes up for air, the only thing waiting is daylight."

"And how," asked Marta Quill, "exactly do we stop nine hulls with daylight?"

Odile smiled the smile she used on tax inspectors and difficult tides. "We don't stop them. We *inconvenience* them. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

* * *

By seven, the plan had a shape, and the shape was, in Odile's private assessment, completely mad, which meant it was probably the only thing that would work.

She stood at the harbor mouth with a brass megaphone she hadn't used since the flood year, addressing perhaps forty boats and the assorted swimmers, gulls, and children who counted as her audience.

"They want the site clear," she called. "They'll post a closure notice, they'll declare it a hazard zone, they'll use words like 'salvage rights' and 'national security' until your ears fall off. But here's what they can't do. They can't sink forty fishing boats in charter waters in front of witnesses. They can't arrest a whole harbor. So we're going to give them a whole harbor."

She laid it out simply. The divers would go first—not to the wreck, never to the wreck, just into the water between the fleet and the site, floating, visible, unarmed, in a line. The fishermen would anchor in a loose crescent behind them, engines cold, lines in the water as if this were any ordinary morning of doing absolutely nothing productively. The children would be sent home immediately, over loud objection, because Odile had limits.

"If they want that wreck," she finished, "they'll have to come through us to get it. Not with weapons—with paperwork. And I promise you, gentlemen and ladies, nobody in the history of the sea has ever won a fight against forty boats full of paperwork."

Fen Okafor raised his hand. "What if they just... push through anyway?"

"Then we float," Odile said. "We are extremely good at floating. It's the one thing every person in this harbor can do better than a gunboat."

* * *

The picket formed at noon.

Odile watched it assemble from the deck of her own boat—a patched old ketch named *Second Opinion*, which she'd bought specifically so that whenever anyone asked her advice she could truthfully say she'd consulted one. Forty-one vessels, when she'd promised forty. The extra was Tam Ashgrave's trawler, sliding in at the last minute with its engine running rough and its captain looking like a man attending his own trial.

Tam. Odile had known him as Tam Eastwick once, before the drowning that wasn't a drowning, before the other name and the Combine money. He'd come to her office two nights ago, gray-faced, and told her enough—that he'd survived the scuttling, that he'd taken the fleet's coin, that he'd spent years keeping Eastwicks away from that wreck out of shame so old it had fossilized into habit. She hadn't forgiven him yet. She suspected forgiveness was going to be less a decision than a tide, something that came in slowly whether you willed it or not.

But he was here. In the line. On the right side of the water, finally, and whatever else was true about Tam Ashgrave, today that counted.

"He's shaking," murmured Brannigan beside her.

"He's human," Odile said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Out past the crescent of fishing boats, the gunboats had noticed. A launch put out from the lead vessel, white ensign snapping, and a lieutenant Odile didn't recognize stood in the bow with a loudhailer of his own, considerably newer than hers.

"This is a closed salvage operation," the lieutenant announced. "All civilian vessels must withdraw beyond the exclusion buoy within one hour."

"Which buoy?" Odile called back pleasantly. "I see several buoys. Some of them are mine. Some of them are crab pots. You'll want to be specific."

The lieutenant's jaw did something complicated. "Madam, I am authorized to—"

"You're authorized to write letters," Odile said. "I've read them. They're very dull. Now, here's my counteroffer, and I'll only make it once. You see these forty-one boats? Every one of them is registered, chartered, and insured in these waters, and every single captain has sworn an affidavit this morning stating they observed unusual naval activity near a heritage wreck site. Heritage, Lieutenant. Chartered under the old coastal acts, which predate your service, your admiral's service, and quite possibly your nation's current opinion of itself. If one hair on one diver's head gets wet that shouldn't, those affidavits go to three newspapers, two magistrates, and my cousin Agnes, who has been waiting thirty years for something to be publicly furious about and will be *magnificent*."

Silence, except for the water slapping hulls and somewhere a gull laughing at all of them, because gulls understood power better than navies did.

"You'd obstruct a lawful operation," the lieutenant tried.

"I'm obstructing nothing. We're fishing." Odile gestured grandly at the forty-one motionless boats. "Badly. It's a traditional method. Very slow. Takes all day. Possibly several days."

* * *

The afternoon wore on the way sieges do when both sides have discovered they're embarrassed.

The fleet didn't advance. The picket didn't retreat. Divers bobbed in the water between the lines like corks with opinions, and Odile rotated them in shifts because even righteous floating gets cold. Marta Quill's chandlery sent out sandwiches by skiff, which turned the picket line into something dangerously close to a picnic, and Odile allowed it, because morale was ammunition too, and cheaper.

Twice, gunboats shifted position, and twice the entire crescent of fishing boats drifted—drifted, mind, purely coincidentally, engines cold, hands visibly nowhere near throttles—into their new path, forcing them to sheer off. The second time, the lead gunboat's captain could be seen through his own bridge glass having what appeared to be a religious experience.

At four o'clock, Brannigan appeared at Odile's elbow with his tide tables actually open for once.

"Sixth tide turns at 6:40," he said quietly. "If she's coming up, it'll be soon."

Odile looked south, toward the escarpment, toward the water above the wreck where the searchlights waited for dark like patient animals. Somewhere under that gray surface was a woman who had spent fifteen years measuring her life in miles, who had wanted nothing from anybody, who had been handed a family, a legacy, and a firing squad all in the same week—and who was, right now, at the bottom of everything, finishing it.

"She doesn't know we're here," Brannigan said. "Does she?"

"No."

"Shouldn't somebody tell her?"

Odile considered this. "When she surfaces," she said, "she's going to look up expecting guns, or worse, empty water and a long swim home alone. Instead she's going to see forty-one boats and the whole stupid harbor floating between her and trouble." She felt something move in her chest that she decided not to examine until later. "Some gifts you don't announce. You just make sure they're there when the head comes up."

* * *

At 6:15, the light went gold, the way it does before it goes honest, and the water off the escarpment changed.

Odile saw it first as a disturbance—a boil of white, far out, right where the charts said the wreck lay. Then a shape breaking surface. Then a hand, gripping something, and a head, and a gasp that carried across a quarter mile of evening water because the wind had gone still, the whole coast holding its breath along with everyone else.

Petra Eastwick surfaced into a harbor full of her neighbors.

For a moment, nothing moved. The girl in the water stared at the boats—at the nets, at the faces, at old Mrs. Calloway waving a sandwich, at Fen Okafor standing proudly on his skiff like a lighthouse with opinions—and Odile watched, across all that distance, the exact moment the arithmetic failed. Fifteen years of miles, meeting forty-one boats' worth of reasons to stay.

Then the lead gunboat's searchlight snapped on, early, eager, and swept toward the swimmer, and every boat in the picket moved at once—not fast, not armed, just *present*, sliding between the light and the woman in the water like a wall built out of stubbornness and bad fishing.

Odile raised her megaphone one last time.

"Harbor law!" she bellowed, and her voice cracked across the water like a whip made of thirty years of tide tables. "Any vessel interfering with a charter claim in progress, witnessed by this assembly, forfeits salvage rights and answers to the coastal bench! You want her, Lieutenant? Come and take her from all of us!"

The searchlight wavered. Held. Wavered again.

And in the gold-and-gray water between the lines, treading air and tide and history, Petra Eastwick lifted the strongbox above her head—small, salt-streaked, impossibly heavy—and the bell of the wreck below rang once through the hull of the sea, a sound every soul in that harbor felt in their teeth.

The sixth tide had turned. The claim was made.

Odile lowered the megaphone, and found Brannigan grinning at her like an idiot.

"Well," he said. "Now what?"

Odile watched the gunboats, watching the fleet, watching the lieutenant on his launch reach very slowly for his radio—and watched, at the edge of the picket line, Tam Ashgrave's trawler turn toward the swimmer, moving to bring her home.

"Now," Odile said, "we find out what the navy does when it loses."
