## 16. Fifth Tide: Tam's Confession

*Petra*

The fifth tide came in gray and mean, the kind of water that made even harbor-born divers mutter to their saints. Petra sat on the *Kestrel's* stern with a thermos of coffee gone bitter, watching the fleet's lights strung across the anchorage like a carnival that had heard about a funeral and decided to cater it.

Tam had sent word an hour before slack water. A boy on a skiff, no note, just a message memorized and recited with the flat delivery of someone paid by the syllable: *"Neutral water. The old beacon rock. He comes alone or not at all."*

Alone. Petra almost laughed. They hadn't been alone since the tide tables started mattering more than sleep. Odile's picket of harbor divers floated somewhere out past the point, Corran's gunboats idled off the escarpment like patient dogs, and somewhere below all of it lay a wreck full of truths that weighed more than lead.

But they went alone anyway. Some conversations you couldn't have with witnesses.

The beacon rock rose out of the shallows a half mile from the fleet's anchor line—bare, barnacled, crowned with the rusted stump of a navigation light nobody had lit in thirty years. Petra tied off the skiff and climbed the wet stone, boots finding holds their body remembered even though their mind insisted they'd never been here before.

That was the thing about this coast. It kept its own records.

Tam was already there, sitting on the rock's seaward side with his back to the wind, an old man shaped like driftwood and dressed in fleet canvas with the name patches cut off. He didn't turn around.

"You got my message," he said.

"I got your message." Petra sat down an arm's length away, close enough to talk, far enough to move. Old habits. "You look terrible, Uncle Tam."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter still worked in his chest. "Drowning does that to a man. Even when he doesn't finish the job."

The tide turned beneath them. Petra felt it in the rock, a long slow exhale, the sea letting go of the escarpment for another hour.

"You were at the scuttling," Petra said. Not a question. "You were fourteen."

"Fifteen." Tam picked at a barnacle with his thumbnail, worrying it loose. "I was in the engine room of the *Merrow's Daughter*. Your grandfather put me there because I was small enough to fit in the bilge spaces and too young to be useful anywhere else. When the charges went—" His hand opened, empty. "I went through a seam in the hull like water through a sieve. Cold enough to stop a heart. I should have stopped."

"But it didn't."

"No. It didn't. And I've spent forty years wishing I could tell you that was luck." He finally looked at Petra then, and his eyes were the same gray as the water, the same gray as every Eastwick who'd ever stood on this coast. "It wasn't luck, Petra. I swam for the fleet's lights. I saw them waiting. They knew where we'd surface, because they're the ones who sank us."

* * *

*Tam*

The girl—no, not a girl anymore, a diver with their grandfather's jaw and their mother's stubbornness set into a face weathered by miles—looked at him without flinching, and Tam thought: *there it is. There's the Eastwick in them. That look that says the truth is going to come out of me one way or another, so I may as well hand it over whole.*

He'd rehearsed this confession for decades. In bunks, in bars, in the bottomless hour before dawn. Every version ended with him forgiven, which told him something about how honest the rehearsals were.

"The fleet found me half-dead on their deck," he said. "And a man named Aldous Crane—you'll find his name in the records you pulled, if you haven't already—sat down beside me with a blanket and a bottle and made me an offer. Not a threat. An offer. That's what I couldn't forgive myself for, later. They never had to threaten me. They just asked what my life was worth, and I named a figure, and they paid it."

"What figure?"

"Enough to disappear. Enough to start over under a new name in a new port, with new papers and a story about a fire that took my family." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "The fire was real enough. Just not the kind that burns."

Petra said nothing. The silence had weight, the way deep water has weight, and Tam let it press on him. He'd earned the pressing.

"And after?" Petra asked finally. "After the money?"

"After the money I discovered that disappearing is a trade, like any other. You get good at it. And the fleet kept me good, because a man who can vanish is useful, and a man who helped vanish an entire family is *very* useful, because he's got no place else to go." Tam turned the loose barnacle over in his palm. "They gave me work. Watching. Whenever anyone came asking about the escarpment—the navy's people, treasure hunters, one historian from the university twenty years back—I made sure they lost interest. Quietly. Sometimes I bought them a drink and told them the wreck was stripped bare. Sometimes I loosened a mooring line. Once—" he looked at the water "—once I did worse. But never to family. Never to an Eastwick. Because for thirty years, no Eastwick ever came. You all stayed gone. I made sure of that too, near the end. Letters returned. Wires unanswered. I told myself I was protecting you."

"Were you?"

"No." The word came out of him like blood from a wound, relieved to be out. "I was protecting myself. If none of you ever came home, then home stayed drowned, and I never had to stand on this coast and admit what I traded for a warm bunk and a cold heart. Shame isn't malice, Petra. But it does the same damage. It sinks things just the same."

* * *

*Petra*

The tide was halfway through its turn, and Petra could feel the clock of it in their teeth. One tide left after this. One chance to place the charter in the strongbox and trigger the claim before the clause expired and the wreck went to whoever held the deepest guns.

But they sat still. Because underneath the urgency, something older was happening—something that felt like a diver's light sweeping across a compartment sealed for forty years.

"You watched the harbor," Petra said. "All these years. You must have known about the legend. The six tides. The heir."

"I wrote part of it." Tam's mouth twisted. "Your grandfather dictated it to me when I was twelve, sitting on this exact rock, making a game of it. 'Someday, lad, some Eastwick will hear that story and wonder why it fits them so well.' He built the clause into the charter and built the charter into the legend, so that even if the navy burned every paper in the province, the law of it would live in the mouths of fishermen. Clever old man. Stubborn as ballast." He paused. "I spread the legend too. After. Kept it alive in the taverns. Told myself it was penance. Really it was just the only piece of him I let myself keep."

"Why give me the warning at the mooring? Why now? What changed?"

Tam was quiet for a long moment. A gull screamed somewhere over the point, indifferent to everything.

"You surfaced," he said simply. "Third tide. I was on the fleet's rail with binoculars, doing my job, watching for you—and you came up out of that water alive, and you looked at the fleet, and you had your grandfather's face. And I realized I'd spent forty years guarding a grave when what I was actually guarding was a door. And doors, Petra—" his voice cracked, and he let it "—doors are meant to open."

* * *

*Tam*

Now, Tam thought. Now or never, and never had been winning for forty years.

He reached inside his jacket and brought out a brass plate the size of a playing card, green with age, drilled with five rotating rings of numbers. He'd pried it off the strongbox's inner mechanism during the only dive he'd ever made back to the wreck—two years after the sinking, before the navy's patrols thickened, before his nerve failed him. He'd gotten the plate off and then he'd hung in the black water outside the captain's cabin, shaking, unable to go in, unable to face whatever his grandfather had left behind. He'd surfaced with nothing but the plate and his cowardice, and he'd carried both ever since.

"Your grandfather changed the combination the week before the sinking," Tam said. "I know because I watched him do it. He used six numbers. Five of them are on the rings everyone's tried—the fleet's been guessing at this box since before you were born, and they've sheared two drill bits on it. But the sixth number isn't on the box. It's on this plate. It's the index setting. Without it, the right numbers in the wrong order just make expensive noise."

He pressed the plate into Petra's glove. Their fingers closed around it, and Tam watched them read the engraving on the back—the old Eastwick motto, the one on the crest, worn nearly smooth: *WHAT THE SEA KEEPS, THE SEA CAN GIVE.*

"The index number," Petra said. "Where is it?"

"It's a date," Tam said. "The day the charter was signed. Your grandfather told me once, when I was small enough to believe he was telling me a secret instead of planting a seed. He said, 'Remember the ninth of the harvest month, lad. The day our name got written into the coast.' Ninth month. Ninth day." He met their eyes. "Nine-nine. Set the index to nine-nine, and the box will know its own."

* * *

*Petra*

Petra turned the brass plate over in their hands. Forty years underwater in a man's pocket instead of in the sea where it belonged. Heavy as a sin. Light as a key.

"You could have come back," Petra said. "Any time. You could have walked into the harbor office and said your name."

"And done what? The fleet owns half the judges on this coast. I'd have been dead inside a week, and the box would still be shut, and you'd never have known there was a sixth number at all." Tam shook his head. "No. My job wasn't to come home. My job was to stay alive long enough to hand the key to whoever finally kicked the door down. I just didn't know it would take this long, or hurt this much, or that the person kicking would have to hold their breath the whole time."

The tide was moving now in earnest, the channel between the beacon rock and the point beginning to run. Petra stood, knees stiff, the plate safe inside their suit.

"Uncle Tam." The word came out rusty. They hadn't said it aloud since they were nine years old. "What you did—taking their money, keeping us gone—it doesn't wash out. I'm not going to pretend it does."

"I wouldn't ask you to."

"But you came back. You're sitting on a rock in the cold handing me the last piece, with the fleet's guns in sight of you, knowing what they'll do if they find out." Petra looked at him—at the driftwood shape of him, at the gray eyes, at the wreck of a man who had spent four decades being a lock when he might have been a key sooner. "That counts. Out here, that counts for salvage. We don't throw away what the sea gives back."

Tam's face did something complicated, something that had been frozen for forty years and was trying to remember how to move. He reached up—one hand, trembling—and gripped Petra's forearm the way divers grip, wrist to wrist, the old signal: *I have you.*

"Sixth tide's at dawn," he said hoarsely. "The fleet plans to blanket the site with searchlights and divers at first light—they'll beat you to the box unless you go early, in the dark, on the last of this ebb. Corran's patrol turns north at midnight; I've seen the rotation board. You'll have maybe ninety minutes of window." He squeezed once, hard. "And Petra. The collapse trap in the captain's cabin. Your grandfather rigged it himself—I watched him build it. It's not meant to kill. It's meant to seal. If it goes, the cabin shuts like a fist. Get what you need and get out before you touch anything twice."

"I will."

"And when the bell rings—" His voice failed entirely this time. He cleared his throat, tried again. "When the bell rings, listen for whether it sounds angry or glad. Your grandfather swore the old bells could tell the difference."

Petra hopped down to the skiff, untied the line, and pushed off into the running tide. Halfway to the *Kestrel*, they looked back. Tam was still on the rock, small against the gray sky, and he raised one hand—not waving, exactly. More like hailing. Like a man signaling a ship that had been overdue for forty years and had finally, impossibly, come up over the horizon.

Petra raised their own hand in answer, then bent to the oars.

Ninety minutes. One index number. Six tides down to the last one.

Behind them, faint on the wind, they could have sworn they heard the fleet's searchlights humming on—all along the anchorage, one after another, waking early.

Someone had talked.
