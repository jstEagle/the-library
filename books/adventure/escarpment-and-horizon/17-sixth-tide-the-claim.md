## 17. Sixth Tide: The Claim

The searchlights came on at dusk, all six of them, strung along the fleet's gunwales like a row of hostile moons. They turned the harbor into something out of a fever dream—every wave silvered, every shadow burned away, and Petra's little boat sitting in the middle of it all like a moth pinned to a collector's card.

"They want an audience," Odile said, standing at the rail with her arms crossed. She'd come out on the picket line's last skiff, along with eleven harbor divers who had no business being there and every intention of staying anyway. "They want to watch you fail in front of the whole coast."

"They want to watch me drown," Petra said, checking the regulator for the fourth time. It was fine. It had been fine the first three times.

"Well. They're going to be disappointed on at least one count."

Petra looked at her. Odile Vance had known the family. Odile had spent three days lying to navy clerks with a straight face, had personally thrown a fleet agent off her dock, and had organized a human wall of divers that had held the gunboats at the harbor line for six hours. And still, when Petra looked at her, the old instinct said: *don't tell her anything. Don't need her. Don't give anyone a door.*

The instinct was quieter than it used to be. That was the strange part. Fifteen years of building a life like a sealed hull, and it had taken one week, one wreck, and one impossible charter to start prying the seams open.

"The strongbox combination," Petra said. "Tam's numbers."

"Memorized." Odile tapped her temple. "Right alongside the recipe for pickled herring and the names of every man who ever shorted me on rope. I don't forget things, Eastwick. It's my one flaw."

Petra almost smiled. Almost. The rest of the smile was somewhere down at forty fathoms, waiting with everything else.

The tide tables said the sixth tide would turn at 11:47. The charter said the claim had to be *placed* before the turn—placed, witnessed, and the bell rung. The navy's lawyers had spent the afternoon arguing that a bell underwater didn't count as ringing, and the harbor's lawyers—two retired fishermen who worked for beer—had argued back that the charter was written by an Eastwick, and Eastwicks had never once in recorded history meant anything convenient.

The charter was in a waterproof case strapped to Petra's chest. Grandfather's handwriting on the claim page. *To my heir, whoever you turn out to be, and however long it takes you to find your way home.* Petra had read it so many times the words had worn grooves in the inside of their skull.

"Petra." Odile's voice dropped. "You don't have to do this alone. I've got four divers willing to run support lines."

"I know."

"You're going to do it alone anyway."

"I know."

Odile exhaled through her nose, the way she did when she was deciding not to argue. "Your grandfather was the same way. Drove the whole harbor mad. Then he'd come home with something none of us could've gotten any other way, and we'd forgive him for a season." She gripped Petra's shoulder, brief and hard. "Come back. That's the only instruction. Everything else is negotiable."

* * *

The water took Petra at 10:52.

Under the searchlights, the descent was nothing like the night dives of the weeks before. The light didn't stop at the surface—it speared down in long silver columns, and the wreck of the *Meridian's Pride* rose up through them like a cathedral being unveiled. Petra had been inside her a dozen times now and had mapped every deck, but under the lights she looked new. She looked *witnessed*. Every rivet and rail stood in plain view, as if the fleet's own arrogance had become the floodlight for its exposure.

*Good,* Petra thought, and meant it with a savagery that surprised them. *Look at her. All of you, look at her. She's been down here sixty years telling the truth and none of you bothered to listen.*

The rigged compartment was aft, below the captain's cabin, behind the bulkhead the logbook had described. Tam's confession had given them the last three numbers of the strongbox combination, but the compartment itself was another matter. Grandfather had sealed it the way a man seals something he expects to be stolen from: generously, with traps.

Petra had spent two dives studying it. The collapse trap was elegant and vicious—a counterweighted section of bulkhead keyed to the strongbox's locking mechanism. Open the box wrong, and the section came down, bringing half the aft hold with it. Open it right, and the counterweight would hold—for a while. Grandfather had built in a margin. He'd also built in a margin on his own patience, apparently, because the whole design said *I trust you to be quick* in iron and salt.

The strongbox sat in its niche like a heart in a chest.

Petra checked the gauge. Forty-one minutes of air. The ascent would take twelve if everything went well. It had been a long time since anything went well, but Petra had learned this week that hope was a thing you could carry in your teeth if you had to.

The first four numbers, Petra knew by heart—had known them since the cave, since the crest on the box had matched the crest burned into their childhood like a brand. The last three were Tam's. *He gave them to me with his hands shaking,* Petra thought, working the dial in the green-black dark. *He survived the scuttling, sold himself to the fleet, spent twenty years making sure no Eastwick ever came back—and in the end he gave me the key with his hands shaking.*

*Forgiveness as salvage,* Odile had called it. Petra was still deciding whether to keep the phrase. It was a good phrase. It was also the kind of phrase a person said about other people, never about themselves, and Petra suspected that before this was over, they'd have to say it about themselves too.

The dial clicked through the fifth number. The sixth. The seventh—

—and stopped, half a mark short.

Petra's heart did something acrobatic and unauthorized. They breathed. In through the regulator, out through the regulator, the way they'd taught a hundred panicking divers to do. *The dial sticks. Grandfather's mechanisms stick. Salt gets in everything. Turn it gently, like you'd turn a stubborn bolt on your own boat, like you'd turn—*

*—like you'd turn anything that belonged to family and hadn't been asked nicely in sixty years.*

Petra turned it gently.

The lock gave with a sound like a held breath released, and somewhere above, deep in the wreck's bones, the counterweight caught with a heavy, satisfying *chunk*. The bulkhead held. The aft hold held. The wreck held.

Petra laughed into their regulator—a small, ridiculous, bubbling sound—and if any fish judged them for it, that was between the fish and their conscience.

* * *

The charter came out of the waterproof case stiff as a plank. Sixty-year-old paper doesn't like water, even water it was born to, and Petra handled it the way you handle anything that's been waiting longer than you've been alive.

There was a slot in the strongbox's inner lid, cut clean and deliberate, sized exactly for a folded charter. Of course it was. Grandfather had thought of everything except, apparently, the emotional wellbeing of his descendants, which was very on brand.

Petra slid the charter in.

For a moment, nothing. The water hung still. The searchlights' columns swayed overhead, slow as kelp. Petra's own breath sounded enormous in their ears, in and out, in and out, the only clock that mattered.

Then the strongbox answered.

It was a small sound first—a pin dropping somewhere inside, a mechanism waking up after a lifetime of sleep. Then a chain of them, ticking through the wreck like a rumor moving through a harbor crowd, and Petra realized the claim clause wasn't just in the box. It was *in the ship*. The charter had been the keystone of a mechanism threaded through the whole vessel, and now that it was seated, the *Meridian's Pride* was doing what she'd been built to do: announcing herself.

And the bell.

The bell was in the forward house. Petra had passed it a dozen times, had noted it in the survey the way you note anything—a shape, a fact, a feature. What Petra had not known, because Tam hadn't known and the logbook hadn't said, was that the bell's cradle was wired into the same mechanism. That when the claim was seated, a weight would release, a lever would fall, and the bell would swing—

The first toll rolled through the water like weather.

Petra felt it in the chest, in the teeth, in the fillings. It was not a loud sound, exactly. It was a *deep* one—the kind of sound that doesn't so much travel through water as become water for a moment, and then become you.

The second toll. The third.

*Claim witnessed,* Petra thought, and their eyes stung, which underwater is a complicated experience. *You old pirate. You built a church down here and called it a wreck, and you made sure that whoever came home would have to ring the bell so the whole coast would know.*

*You didn't want me to sneak back. You wanted me to arrive.*

* * *

The collapse came early.

Later—much later, sitting wrapped in blankets on Odile's deck with a mug of tea they'd never drink—Petra would reconstruct it. The counterweight had held, but the mechanism's release had shifted load it was never meant to carry after sixty years of rust. The trap hadn't been triggered wrong. It had simply gotten old, the way everything did, the way everyone did, and it had come down anyway.

The first warning was a groan through the hull, low and structural, the sound a ship makes when she changes her mind about something.

Petra was already moving.

The aft passage they'd come in through was folding—the bulkhead section dropping in a slow, terrible, deliberate way, like a door closing in a house where someone had died. Beyond it, the corridor to the forward stair was still open, but the deck was buckling in waves, and the searchlight columns overhead were filling with sediment, the silver water going to milk.

Petra swam.

Not the controlled, measured, air-disciplined swim of the past weeks. This was the flat-out, lungs-burning, fin-tearing swim of a person who has just decided, with their whole body at once, that they are not done. The corridor narrowed. A shelf of plating came down behind them with a boom they felt rather than heard. Their shoulder caught a stanchion and spun them, and for one white instant the wreck was all around—walls and dark and the bell still tolling somewhere above, patient, indifferent, ringing out the claim while the claimant fought for the exit.

*Come on,* Petra told the wreck, and it wasn't a plea. It was the way you'd talk to a stubborn bolt, a fouled line, a family. *You waited sixty years for an heir. You don't get to keep one.*

The stair. The light—real light now, searchlight silver pouring down the stairwell like a blessing. Petra kicked for it, and the last thing the aft hold did before it settled was exhale a great cloud of silt that chased them up the stairs like a hand letting go.

* * *

Petra broke the surface at 11:44, three minutes before the tide turned, into a harbor gone entirely mad.

The searchlights had found them instantly, six white beams converging, and the noise—Petra had never heard a harbor make this noise. It wasn't cheering, exactly. It was cheering with sixty years of grievance in it, boat horns and whistles and Odile's voice cutting through all of it like a blade through butter: "*Eastwick! Report!*"

"Charter's placed!" Petra shouted back, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they found they didn't care. "Claim is seated! The bell—"

The bell rang again.

Above the water, this time. Clear across the flat calm of the turning tide, the sound came up through the wreck and out into the open air, and it carried the way sound carries over water at night—miles, it seemed, all the way to the escarpment and back. The harbor went quiet to listen. Every diver on the picket line, every crewman on the fleet's decks, every soul leaning over a rail, all of them hearing the same thing: the *Meridian's Pride*, rung for the first time since she went down, announcing that she had an heir.

Then the harbor made the other noise, the one without any grievance in it at all.

Odile hauled Petra over the gunwale by the collar, which was not gentle, and wrapped them in a blanket, which was, and shoved tea into their hands, which was neither. "You're bleeding," she said.

"My shoulder met a stanchion. The stanchion won."

"You're bleeding and you're grinning."

"I'm allowed one or the other. I'm spending both."

Odile laughed—actually laughed, a short, rough, delighted sound—and then the fleet's searchlights swung away from Petra's boat, all together, like a congregation turning to face the altar. Because the bell was still ringing. The tide had turned, the mechanism had done its work, and the *Meridian's Pride* was tolling out over the water with the patient insistence of a thing that had been waiting six decades to be heard.

And down the harbor line, Petra saw the fleet's boats beginning to move—not toward the wreck, but away from it, falling back, their searchlights dimming one by one as if someone aboard had finally read the charter's terms and understood what they meant.

On the nearest gunboat's deck, a figure stood apart from the crew, looking at the wreck. Even at this distance, in the last of the light, Petra knew the set of those shoulders.

Tam.

He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. Something older than a wave. Something like a salute, or an apology, or a man finally setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

Then he turned and walked below decks, and Petra understood, with a certainty that settled into the bones like warmth, that whatever came next—the navy, the lawyers, the fleet's masters with their guns—the hard part was over.

The bell rang once more, long and low, as the tide turned home.
