## 18. Gunboats and Testimony

Lieutenant Aldous Corran had been awake for thirty-one hours, and the coffee on the *Vigilant*'s bridge had gone the particular gray of coffee that has given up on itself. He drank it anyway. It seemed fitting. Everything else in his professional life had also given up on itself sometime around midnight, when a bell had rung underwater and every carefully worded report he'd ever filed had begun quietly catching fire.

He stood at the rail and looked down at the escarpment site, where six gunboats—six, the Admiralty had sent *six*, as though Petra Eastwick were an invading armada rather than one person in a patched drysuit—sat at anchor in a rough crescent, their searchlights still sweeping the water out of what Corran could only describe as institutional embarrassment. The claim had been witnessed. That was the problem. The claim had been witnessed by half the harbor, from the cliffs, from fishing skiffs that had no business being out in that chop, from Odile Vance's harbor launch flying—Corran had checked twice—the harbor ensign at the top of the mast like a woman planting a flag on conquered territory.

Which, he supposed, she had.

"Sir?" Ensign Brightwell appeared at his elbow, holding a clipboard the way a man holds a live eel. "Signal from the flagship, sir. Commodore Hesk requests your assessment of the situation."

"My assessment."

"Yes, sir."

Corran considered this. His assessment was that the navy had spent forty years suppressing evidence that it had scuttled the Eastwick salvage fleet and erased eleven people from the harbor rolls, and that last night a diver had surfaced carrying documents proving it, and that those documents had already been copied, notarized, and distributed to a harbor-master who kept duplicates in three locations because—her words, relayed to him by a very nervous junior signalman—"I've met navies before, dear."

"I'll draft my assessment," Corran said, "shortly."

"Aye, sir." Brightwell did not move.

"Was there something else?"

"The commodore also asks, sir, whether the claim is legally valid. He used the phrase 'on what grounds,' sir. Twice."

"On the grounds," Corran said, "that a salvage charter issued under Crown patent in the year 1889 contains an inheritance clause that our own legal office verified as binding four days ago, which is why they tried to buy the heir off instead of arresting them, which is why we are all standing here in the dark watching searchlights chase a bell." He paused. "You may quote me on the first part only."

Brightwell wrote it down anyway, all of it, because Brightwell was twenty-three and had not yet learned which parts of a lieutenant's honesty were survivable.

* * *

The records sat in the *Vigilant*'s chart room, because Corran had brought copies aboard himself at two in the morning, after Odile Vance had handed them across the gap between her launch and his boarding ladder with the expression of a woman passing a casserole dish across a fence.

"You'll want these," she'd said. "Not for the fleet. For you."

"Harbor-master, if you're attempting to compromise a naval officer—"

"Aldous." She'd used his Christian name, which no one had done since his mother, and which she had somehow earned the right to do over the course of one week and several cups of terrible tea in her office. "Your orders reference a closure authority signed nineteen years ago by a captain whose name isn't in any registry I can find. Your orders tell you to erase a wreck that your own hydrographic charts list as a hazard to navigation, which means somebody wanted it unlisted and listed at the same time, and nobody who ever served on this coast believed both of those things at once. You've been reading those orders for a month. You know exactly what they are." She'd pressed the oilcloth packet into his hands. "So here's what they actually closed. Read it. Then decide who you work for."

He had read it. All of it. In the chart room, alone, with the lamp turned low as though the paper might be shy.

It was worse than he'd expected and more precise than he'd feared. The Eastwick fleet hadn't been lost to weather. It had been boarded at night by a naval party, its crews put ashore or put under, its hulls opened to the sea with the methodical efficiency of men following a checklist. The reason wasn't treasure. The reason was paper: cargo manifests, requisition ledgers, correspondence documenting a supply operation that certain officials had run through the escarpment coast during the border troubles, off the books, profiting off the books, and killing to keep off the books. Eleven names. A family struck from the rolls so thoroughly that when Corran had pulled the harbor archives a week ago, the pages had been razored out with a blade—and someone had been careless, because the stubs remained, and stubs, he now understood, were testimony too.

There was a logbook excerpt, in a hand Corran recognized from the charter documents: Elias Eastwick, grandfather of the current heir. *They think sinking us sinks the truth. But paper floats longer than men. Let the sea keep it. Let the law come looking someday. Let there be a door home.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink, a note added decades later: *If you are reading this, the door worked. — P.E.*

Corran sat with that for a long time. Then he did the thing he suspected he'd been doing, in slow motion, since the day he'd first boarded Petra Eastwick's boat with an offer he hadn't believed in: he stopped pretending.

* * *

Morning came up gray and clean over the escarpment, and Commodore Hesk came up the *Vigilant*'s accommodation ladder at oh-eight-hundred precisely, because Hesk was a man who believed punctuality could outrun consequences.

He was wrong about many things, but he dressed beautifully.

"Lieutenant," he said, accepting coffee he would not drink. "Report."

"Sir. At approximately 2340 last night, the claim clause of the Eastwick salvage charter was executed by the legal heir, Petra Eastwick, in the presence of no fewer than forty witnesses including the harbor-master, three licensed pilots, and a journalist from the *Coastal Ledger* whom I believe was invited specifically to make this conversation difficult."

"I'm aware of the theater, Lieutenant. I'm asking about the site. Is it secured?"

"No, sir."

Hesk's eyebrows performed a small maneuver. "Explain."

"The site is legally private property as of last night, sir. Under the charter's terms, the wreck, the cave, and the surrounding seabed within the marked bounds belong to the Eastwick estate. Any naval presence beyond navigational courtesy constitutes trespass, and the harbor association has already retained counsel. Two firms. They're competing for the privilege."

"That charter is a piece of folklore."

"It's a piece of Crown patent, sir. Our own legal office confirmed it. In writing." Corran let that sit. "In fact, sir, I have documentation regarding the site that I believe requires your attention. It concerns the original loss of the Eastwick fleet."

Something moved behind Hesk's eyes—not guilt; Corran doubted the machinery for it had ever been installed—but calculation, quick and cold, the look of a man pricing a problem. "Whatever you've recovered from that wreck is naval property. Salvage from a closed site reverts to the Crown."

"With respect, sir, the site stopped being closed at 2340 last night. And the documentation in question didn't come from the wreck." Corran set the oilcloth packet on the chart table between them. "It came from the harbor archives, cross-referenced against our own records, and it has already been copied and lodged with three independent parties, including the *Coastal Ledger*. Destroying it now would be an admission. Ignoring it will be one too, but slower."

Hesk looked at the packet the way other men looked at ordnance. "You understand what you're handling, Lieutenant. Careers have ended over less. Yours, notably."

"Yes, sir." Corran found, to his mild surprise, that he was smiling. "I've been doing the arithmetic. Nineteen years ago, someone signed orders erasing a family. Every officer since who executed those orders told themselves the same thing I told myself a month ago: that it was above my rank, beyond my reach, someone else's inheritance. And the debt compounded. Sir, I've read the manifest. There are eleven names on it. One of them belonged to a boy of nine who survived the scuttling and spent his whole life making sure no Eastwick ever came home, because he couldn't bear to look at what coming home would cost. That's what our silence built. That's the interest." He slid the packet forward. "I'm paying down the principal. Today. With my name on the transaction."

For a long moment the only sound was the halyard clinking against the mast, and below it, faintly, the harbor waking up: gulls, diesel engines, someone on a nearby deck swearing cheerfully at a fouled winch.

"You'll be court-martialed," Hesk said at last. It was almost gentle, in the way a closing door is gentle.

"Probably, sir."

"You'll lose everything. Pension, standing, the lot."

"I've done the sums, sir. I get to keep the part I actually use."

Hesk studied him for a long moment, and then—this was the thing Corran would remember afterward, the detail he'd turn over at two in the morning for years—Hesk picked up the packet, weighed it in one hand, and said, quietly, "My predecessor's predecessor signed the closure order. I found it in the safe when I took command. I never asked what it closed." He set it down again. "Perhaps I didn't want to be the officer who asked."

"No, sir," Corran agreed. "But somebody has to be eventually. Might as well be someone whose career is short enough to fit inside the scandal."

* * *

By noon, the signals had gone out.

Corran drafted them himself, sitting at the chart table with Brightwell hovering at a respectful distance, radiating the anxious energy of a young man watching his commanding officer walk into a propeller. The order to stand down the picket went first: all vessels to withdraw to standard anchorage, the site to be treated as private property pending civil adjudication. The second signal was longer. It went up the chain, past Hesk, past Hesk's superiors, to the Admiralty board itself, and it attached everything: the manifests, the ledger excerpts, the razored archive stubs photographed and catalogued, the logbook pages, and a covering statement of four paragraphs that Corran wrote in one pass and did not revise, because revising, he had learned, was where courage went to die.

*I, Lieutenant Aldous Corran, hereby submit evidence that the loss of the Eastwick salvage fleet in [year] was effected by a naval party under false colors, pursuant to orders whose provenance I have been unable to establish through legitimate channels, and that subsequent site closures were designed to suppress, rather than secure, the wreck's contents. I executed those closures in good faith until such time as good faith required otherwise. I request inquiry. I accept whatever follows.*

"There," he said, and pressed the transmit key, and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

"Sir," Brightwell said carefully, "the harbor launch is approaching. Harbor-master Vance. She's requesting to come alongside."

"Of course she is. Grant permission. And Ensign—put the good kettle on. Not the bridge one. The one in the wardroom that doesn't taste like regret."

Odile Vance came up the ladder like weather, coat collar up, gray hair escaping its pins in every direction, carrying a basket that smelled, improbably, of cinnamon. "Aldous Corran," she announced, "you beautiful, doomed fool."

"Harbor-master."

"Don't 'harbor-master' me, I watched your signal traffic. Four paragraphs, no revisions—I have friends in the cable office, don't look surprised." She thrust the basket at him. "Buns. From Marta's. The whole harbor knows by now, and the whole harbor has opinions, and the prevailing opinion is that you're either the bravest man on this coast or the stupidest, and Marta split the difference and gave you the ones with extra icing."

"That's... genuinely the kindest intelligence assessment I've ever received."

"You'll want to hear the rest." She helped herself to coffee without asking, made a face at it, drank it anyway. "The inquiry, when it comes—and it'll come fast, they'll want to bury this in a quiet room before the papers finish printing—you won't face it alone. The harbor association is submitting as interested party. Half the old families on this coast lost people to that fleet or traded with it. There are granddaughters out there, Aldous, who've been told their great-uncles drowned in weather, and they are *furious*, and fury with genealogical records behind it is a force even the Admiralty respects." She set down the cup. "Petra sent something, too."

Corran went very still. "Eastwick did?"

"A note. Passed through me, because apparently the two of you communicate exclusively through intermediaries and baked goods, like rival espionage services with a shared baker." She produced an envelope, salt-stained at one corner, and held it out. "Read it later if you like. But I'll tell you the gist, because I'm nosy and I've earned it. It says: thank you. And it says: you could have stood on the ladder that night and taken my boat apart, and instead you stood there looking seasick while I lied to you badly, and you knew I was lying, and you let it go. It says that's the moment they decided you might be worth saving too."

Corran took the envelope. His hands, he noticed distantly, were not entirely steady. Thirty-one hours without sleep, he told himself. Nothing to do with anything else.

"They also say," Odile added, rising, gathering her basket remnants with the efficiency of a woman with twelve more errands, "that when the inquiry comes, they'll testify. On the record. Name spelled correctly. They spent fifteen years buying anonymity at retail, dear, and they've decided to sell the whole stock at once." She paused at the top of the ladder, and her voice softened into something almost gentle. "You know what happens to you now. Inquiry, tribunal, probably stripped rank. You could refuse to confirm the statement. Recant. They'd let you, if you groveled properly."

"I know what happens."

"And you're staying anyway."

"I'm staying anyway."

She nodded once, satisfied, and went down the ladder into the bright cold morning, and Corran stood at the rail and watched her launch cut back toward the harbor, where the masts stood thick as winter reeds and somewhere among them a boathouse with fresh paint on its trim was waiting for its family to come home.

Behind him, Brightwell cleared his throat. "Sir? Signal from the flagship. The commodore requests—well, technically the Admiralty requests—that you place yourself ashore and available for inquiry at your earliest convenience."

"My earliest convenience," Corran repeated.

"Yes, sir. Also, sir, there's a small boat coming out from the harbor. One person. Diving gear aboard. They're asking permission to transit the anchorage, and, ah." Brightwell consulted the message form with visible trepidation. "They've signed it 'P. Eastwick, owner of the seabed you're anchored on.' Requesting, sir. Politely. With a smiley face drawn next to the signature."

Corran laughed—a real one, surprising him, rusty as an unused winch. "Grant transit. Full courtesy. And Ensign? When the tribunal asks who assisted me in drafting the stand-down order, I intend to name you."

Brightwell went pale, then, slowly, pink with something that was unmistakably pride. "Aye, sir," he said. "Thank you, sir."

Overhead the gulls wheeled, and out at the escarpment the tide was turning, and the bell on the wreck of the Eastwick fleet—claimed, witnessed, and legally speaking none of the navy's business anymore—waited in the green light below for the heir who was even now rowing out to visit it, in broad daylight, under their own name, hiding from absolutely no one.

Corran packed his sea chest that afternoon. It didn't take long. An officer's life fits in a trunk, it turns out; it's the conscience that takes up the room, and for the first time in nineteen years, his traveled light.

The summons would reach him by morning. What waited ashore—the inquiry, the old families with their furious genealogies, a harbor preparing to drag its drowned dead back into the light—was a bigger fight than any he'd fought in uniform.

He found he was looking forward to it.
