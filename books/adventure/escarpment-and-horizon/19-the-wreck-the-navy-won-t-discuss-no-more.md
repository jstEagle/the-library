## 19. The Wreck the Navy Won't Discuss No More

The story broke on a Tuesday, which Petra would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the harbor fish market smelled worst and nobody was in a mood to be lied to.

It came out the way these things always did—not with a bang but with a clerk's error. A junior archivist in the naval district office, copying files for a routine records transfer, attached the wrong folder to an interdepartmental memo. The wrong folder contained the Eastwick file. All of it. The scuttling orders, dated forty-one years past. The harbor roll amendment that had struck every Eastwick from the books as if they'd never baited a hook here. The internal correspondence, dry as ballast stone, discussing how best to manage "the residual legend" so it wouldn't "attain evidentiary weight."

*Residual legend.* Petra read those words four times, standing in Odile's office with the printout trembling slightly in their hands, before the humor of it landed.

"They wrote us up like weather," Petra said. "'The Eastwick phenomenon.' Like we were fog."

"Fog gets an apology from the harbormaster when it delays shipping," Odile said, not looking up from her own copy. She had her reading glasses pushed down to the end of her nose and her lips moving faintly, the way she did when she was memorizing something for use against someone later. "You got deleted. I'd take the fog treatment."

Petra set the pages down. Their hands were steady now—diver's hands, hands that had learned that shaking things sank them—but something behind their ribs was not steady at all. Forty-one years. A whole family erased by two paragraphs and a signature. And now here it all was, photocopied onto cheap paper that smelled like toner, sitting on a desk that smelled like tar and old coffee.

"How far has it gone?"

Odile finally looked up. There was something in her face Petra hadn't seen there before—not satisfaction exactly, though she'd earned some, having copied those records three separate ways the moment Corran handed them over. It was more like the look she gave a tide gauge when the water was doing something no one could stop.

"Gone? Child, it went *everywhere*. The district office tried to recall the memo." She snorted. "You can't recall a memo. That's like trying to recall the tide. By noon yesterday three papers had it, the maritime board had it, and old Mrs. Tulliver had it, and Mrs. Tulliver is worth ten papers. She stood up at the council meeting last night—ninety-one years old, had to be carried up the steps—and read the scuttling order out loud. The whole order. In her church voice."

"What happened?"

"The mayor resigned. The naval liaison walked out. And then—" Odile paused, and Petra braced, because Odile only paused when the news was going to land sideways. "Then they started singing."

"Singing what?"

"*Lowlands Away.* The mourning verse. The one your grandfather's crews used to sing when they lost a boat." Odile took off her glasses and folded them with great care. "Half the council chamber joined in. People who weren't born when your family left. They knew the words anyway. Legends keep, Petra. Turns out that was the navy's whole problem—they couldn't sink this one deep enough."

* * *

For three days the escarpment coast behaved like a place Petra didn't recognize.

Boats came out to the site—not gunboats, not salvage trawlers with their cranes folded like mantis arms, but fishing skiffs and pleasure sloops and one enormous, improbable paddle-steamer that the river tour company dragged out of retirement for the occasion. They anchored at a respectful distance, and people stood at the rails and looked down at the water over Heir's Wreck, and some of them dropped flowers, which drifted on the current until they looked like a garden trying to grow upside down.

Petra watched it from the deck of their own boat, coffee going cold in hand, feeling like a lighthouse keeper whose light had suddenly become famous.

"You're staring," Tam said.

Tam had been aboard for two days now, sleeping in the spare bunk he'd once slept in as a boy, back when the boat belonged to the family and the family still existed on paper. He moved around the deck like a man relearning a house after decades away—reaching for the rail where it used to be, finding it where it now was, flinching a little each time. He was growing a beard, or possibly just failing to shave; with Tam it was hard to say where grief ended and grooming began.

"I'm watching," Petra corrected. "Staring implies I don't know what I'm looking at."

"And do you?"

Petra considered the flotilla. A woman was holding a child up over the rail so the child could see the water. An old man in a coat too heavy for the season was standing perfectly still, hat in his hands, the way you stand at a grave.

"No," Petra admitted. "I know what they're looking *at*. I don't know what they're looking *for*."

"Same thing everybody looks for at wrecks," Tam said. "An ending. Wrecks are the only stories that stay put long enough to finish." He cleared his throat. "Your grandfather used to say that. Among other things."

Among other things. Tam had been talking like that for two days—opening doors into the past and then standing aside awkwardly, as if inviting Petra through and apologizing for the mess inside. Petra had decided to let him. Forgiveness, they were learning, wasn't a thing you finished; it was a thing you maintained, like a hull, checking it daily for leaks.

A launch puttered out from the harbor, flying the maritime board pennant, and Petra sighed and put down the coffee.

"That'll be the offer," they said.

* * *

The offer arrived in the person of a board solicitor named Mr. Quennel, who wore a suit too warm for the deck and held his briefcase the way divers hold a catch bag: valuable, wet-averse, and likely to attract attention.

He laid it out on Petra's chart table with the reverence of a man dealing cards he hoped you wouldn't notice were marked.

"Full salvage rights," he said. "Title to the wreck, the cargo, the charter claim—all confirmed, all yours, uncontested. The board will fund recovery operations. There are collectors who would pay extraordinary sums for Eastwick provenance, particularly now. You could name your figure."

"And the other part?" Petra asked, because there was always another part, and Quennel's eyes had flicked twice toward the cabin door as if hoping it would open and swallow him.

Quennel winced. "There is a discreet provision. Given the... sensitivity... of certain recovered materials, the board would view favorably an arrangement whereby the heir maintained a low public profile. No interviews. No testimony at the inquiry. The records speak for themselves, after all. Your name needn't be attached to any of it. You'd have the wreck, the money, and—" he gestured delicately at the flotilla outside, at the flowers, at the child being held up to look— "*quiet*. Which I understand," and here his voice softened into something almost human, "is something you've had practice wanting."

Petra looked at the paper for a long time.

It was a good offer. That was the terrible thing about it. It was everything they'd spent fifteen years building toward without ever admitting they were building: money enough to vanish properly, anonymity guaranteed by contract, the past converted cleanly into assets and filed away. No more harbor watching. No more names. Just miles, and water, and nobody knowing who was under the surface.

They thought about the near-blackout on the first descent, fifteen years deep, alone in the dark and fine with it—or telling themselves they were fine with it, which was a different thing entirely.

They thought about Odile covering for them at the harbor office, lying smoothly to a saboteur's face for a person she barely knew. About half the council chamber singing *Lowlands Away* for a family they'd never met. About a ninety-one-year-old woman carried up stairs to read a dead man's name back into the world.

"Mr. Quennel," Petra said. "Do you dive?"

The solicitor blinked. "I—no. Why?"

"Because if you did, you'd know that the first rule is you never hold your breath. You breathe through it. Even when it's easier not to. Especially then." Petra slid the paper back across the table. "I'll take the salvage rights. The title, the charter, all of it. But strike the quiet clause. My name goes on everything—the inquiry, the memorial, the lot. Signed, witnessed, published in the harbor gazette if they'll print it."

Quennel stared. "You understand what you're declining. The payout alone—"

"I'm not declining the payout. I'm declining the erasure. Turns out my family already did that once, on the navy's behalf, and I hear it doesn't suit us." Petra stood, and offered a hand, and watched the solicitor wrestle briefly with the question of whether heirs shook hands. They did. This one did. "Tell the board the heir claims the wreck openly, or not at all."

At the rail, pausing to gather his dignity and his briefcase, Quennel turned back. "For what it's worth—and it isn't much, professionally speaking—I think the board expected this. Half of them hoped for it. A cover-up needs a secret to survive, Ms. Eastwick. You've just taken theirs away."

"Good," Petra said. "Secrets sink. Stories float. I should know—I've been both."

* * *

The reclaiming ceremony happened on the spring tide, because Odile organized it and Odile believed ceremonies should be scheduled around practicalities. "No point consecrating a memorial you can't reach at half-tide," she told the council, and the council, still shell-shocked from Mrs. Tulliver, agreed to anything.

They met at the cave mouth at dead low water. The escarpment stood exposed in the afternoon light, its ledges dripping, the cave mouth open like a held breath finally let go. Half the harbor seemed to be there—fishermen in their good coats, the paddle-steamer crowd, Mrs. Tulliver enthroned on a folding chair that two young divers had carried down the rocks while she directed them like a harbormaster docking a barge.

Petra stood at the edge of the crowd with Tam, who had shaved after all, and who kept adjusting a collar that didn't need adjusting.

"There's a lot of people," Tam murmured.

"There's a lot of people," Petra agreed.

"You could still leave. Boat's right there. Nobody would—"

"Tam." Petra looked at him. "Fifteen years I left. It turns out leaving is easy. It's the easiest thing in the world—you just keep swimming. What's hard is turning around." They nodded toward the crowd, where Odile was marshaling the choir with the brisk tyranny of a woman who had waited her whole life for an official reason to sing. "Besides. If I leave now, Mrs. Tulliver will come after me, and she's ninety-one, and I'd never forgive myself if she pulled something."

The ceremony itself was short. The council chair read the restoration order—every Eastwick struck from the rolls reinstated, posthumously and otherwise, "with full honors and arrears of apology"—and there was a sound from the crowd that wasn't quite laughter and wasn't quite weeping and was somehow both at once. Then Odile raised her hand, and the choir began *Lowlands Away*, and the harbor sang the mourning verse for the drowned Eastwicks, and then—because Odile had rehearsed them ruthlessly and would tolerate no shortcuts—the second verse, the one almost nobody remembered, the homecoming verse.

*Come home, come home, the tide's turned fair...*

Petra felt it land somewhere below the ribs, deeper than any dive. Fifteen years measuring life in miles, in fathoms, in the distance between themselves and everything—and here was a harbor singing the distance closed.

Afterward, they came to Petra one at a time, as harbor people do, never in a crowd. Fishermen who wanted to shake the heir's hand. Children who wanted to know if the wreck really had a bell that rang by itself (it had rung once, at the claiming, and Petra had privately resolved never to investigate why). A woman who pressed a bundle of dried flowers into their hands and said her grandmother had been crew on an Eastwick boat and had never once spoken ill of the family, not even when it was dangerous.

And then, near the end, a man in civilian clothes, gray at the temples, standing with his cap in his hands like the old man on the steamer. Corran. Formerly Lieutenant Corran, though the resignation had been submitted the same week as the testimony, and the board had accepted it with the particular coldness institutions reserve for consciences.

"Heard you took the rights but refused the quiet clause," Corran said.

"Heard you took the truth and paid for it with your career," Petra said. "Seems we're both bad at bargains."

Corran almost smiled. It was clear the muscle was out of practice. "I wanted to see the cave. The reports called it 'the anomaly.' Fourteen years I worked that coast and nobody would tell me what the anomaly was." He looked up at the dark mouth in the rock, water pooling in its throat as the tide began its slow return. "It's a door."

"It's a door," Petra agreed. "My grandfather built it to be found. Took our family three generations and a navy cover-up to get around to opening it, but he was patient. Divers usually are."

Corran nodded slowly, then held out his hand. "If the inquiry needs a witness who knows how the orders moved through the chain—who signed what, who buried what—I'll testify. Officially. On the record. Under my own name."

Petra took the hand. "That's the whole point of the exercise, Lieutenant. Names on the record. Mine went on first."

* * *

Evening came down soft over the escarpment, and the crowd drained away up the cliff path carrying lanterns, and Petra stayed.

They climbed to the cave mouth while the tide was still low enough to walk in, and stood in the passage where the strongbox had waited forty-one years for someone with the right name and the poor sense to come get it. The charter itself was back in the box now, sealed under the claim clause, legally speaking the most important piece of paper on the coast—but Petra had copies, and Odile had copies, and Mrs. Tulliver, Petra suspected, had committed it to memory as a weapon for future council meetings.

Inside the cave, the air smelled of salt and stone and, faintly, of the wreck below—the deep preserved smell of a ship holding its breath. Somewhere beneath their feet lay the captain's cabin, the logbook's resting place, the sealed compartment with its rigged collapse now defused and documented. The whole drowned fleet, waiting to become a memorial instead of a secret.

Petra put a palm flat against the wet rock.

"All right, Grandfather," they said quietly. "You got your door opened. Hope you're satisfied. It cost me six tides, one uncle, one career's worth of navy trouble, and every mile of distance I ever put between me and this harbor." A pause. The water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock. "Worth it. Don't tell anyone I said that."

From above, faintly, came voices—Tam calling down that Odile had commandeered the boathouse kitchen and was cooking for twenty whether anyone showed up or not, and that Mrs. Tulliver had brought a bottle of something confiscated from her late husband's stores and was telling everyone it was medicinal.

Petra laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, loud enough to echo off the cave walls and go rolling down through the water toward the wreck, like a message sent ahead.

They climbed out of the cave into the last of the light, and the harbor spread below them, lamps coming on one by one along the quay, each lamp a window, each window somebody who knew their name now and would use it, cheerfully, constantly, forever.

Fifteen years ago, that thought would have sent them running for the horizon.

Tonight, Petra stood still and counted the lights instead. Twenty-three windows. Twenty-three neighbors. And tomorrow there would be a wreck to map, and a boathouse to restore, and a table to set for everyone who'd sung.

The tide was turning. Petra turned with it, and walked home.
