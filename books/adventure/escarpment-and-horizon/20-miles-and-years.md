## 20. Miles and Years

The *Kestrel's Wake* had a hole in her hull the size of a dinner plate and a list to port that made her look like a drunk leaning on a lamppost. Petra Eastwick stood on the dock at first light, coffee in hand, and regarded the damage with something that was almost affection.

"You did this," they told the boat. "You know that."

The boat said nothing, being a boat, but the harbor answered for her. Gulls wheeled and complained. Somewhere down the quay, Odile Vance was already bellowing at a fisherman about mooring fees, her voice carrying across the water like a foghorn with opinions. And on the far side of the slip, an old man in a canvas apron was watching Petra with the frank, unhurried curiosity of someone who had decided weeks ago that he was going to introduce himself eventually.

Petra measured the distance between them out of habit. Forty feet of dock planking. They'd spent fifteen years measuring everything in miles — miles to the next port, miles of open water between themselves and anyone who might ask a question, miles of cold dark between a diver and the surface. Distance as armor. You could survive almost anything if you kept it far enough away.

Forty feet, though. That was harder to survive.

"Morning," the old man called. "You're the one who sank the navy's story, then."

"I'm the one who fixed my boat badly," Petra said. "The other thing happened along the way."

He laughed — a big, unguarded laugh that scattered the gulls — and came closer, extending a hand rough as barnacle stone. "Merrick Halloway. I run the chandlery two doors up. Sold your grandfather rope for thirty years, if you can believe that. He always paid late and argued about it."

Petra took the hand. It felt strange, shaking hands with a piece of their own history that hadn't drowned. "I believe the paying late."

"He'd have liked you." Merrick nodded at the *Wake*'s wounded flank. "That patch job's an insult to epoxy. Come by the shop this afternoon, I'll set you up proper. Family discount."

"I'm not sure I qualify for—"

"You're an Eastwick standing on an Eastwick dock in an Eastwick harbor," Merrick said, already walking away. "You qualify. Don't argue. Your grandfather argued, and look where it got him."

*Where it got him* was a memorial plaque now, bolted to the escarpment above the cave mouth, and a name restored to the harbor rolls after thirty years of erasure. Where it got him was complicated. But Merrick said it lightly, the way people here were learning to say it, and Petra found they didn't flinch.

Progress, maybe. Or just numbness wearing a friendlier coat. Time would tell which.

* * *

The afternoon went to the hull.

Petra worked alone by preference — fifteen years of habit didn't dissolve just because a harbor full of strangers had recently decided to stand between you and a fleet of gunboats — but solitude, they were discovering, came in flavors now. There was the old kind, the kind you built like a bulkhead, and there was the new kind: the simple, chosen quiet of sanding fiberglass while the sun moved across the water. The second kind had doors in it. People wandered through.

A girl of perhaps ten sat on the piling for most of an hour, watching them work with the solemn intensity of a customs inspector.

"You're the diver," she announced finally.

"I'm a diver."

"My da says you went down past where the charts go red."

"The charts are wrong about a lot of things."

She considered this. "My da says the wreck's got a ghost."

"Your da says a lot of things."

"He says the ghost rings the bell when the tide turns." Her eyes went wide and defiant, daring Petra to laugh. "Did you hear it?"

Petra set down the sander. They had heard it — that impossible bronze note rolling up through forty fathoms of green water as the claim clause triggered, heard it through their bones more than their ears, and every rational explanation they'd constructed since (pressure shift, hull resonance, the collapse trap letting go) felt thinner than the memory of the sound itself.

"I heard something," Petra said. "Whether it was a ghost or just the sea clearing its throat, I couldn't tell you."

The girl nodded slowly, satisfied, as though this confirmed everything. "I'm Nell," she said. "Nell Corran. My uncle used to be in the navy. He doesn't anymore. He fixes roofs now and he's much nicer."

Petra's hands went still on the gunwale. *Corran.* The lieutenant who had boarded the *Wake* with veiled threats and a payout offer; the lieutenant who had ended his career by submitting scuttling evidence up a chain of command that would rather have sunk it. Retired, apparently. Fixing roofs.

"Tell him," Petra said carefully, "that the diver remembers what he did."

Nell frowned. "Which thing?"

"All of it."

She shrugged with the magnificent indifference of childhood and ran off down the quay, and Petra went back to sanding, smiling before they noticed they were doing it.

* * *

Three days later, the mapping began.

It had been Odile's idea, floated over bad stew at the harbor office: the site was a memorial now, legally protected, but protection meant nothing if nobody knew what was down there. The harbor needed charts. Real ones. And there was exactly one person alive who knew the wreck's interior like the lines of their own palm.

"It'll take weeks," Petra had said.

"Weeks is good," Odile had said. "Weeks means you stay."

So Petra stayed, and every morning at slack water they went down with slate and measuring line, and every evening they spread the day's work across the boathouse table — the Eastwick boathouse, restored now, its windows reglazed, its floor swept of thirty years of salt-rot — and drew the ship that had killed and saved their family in careful ink.

It was strange work. Tender work. Each compartment they charted was a room someone had lived in: the crew mess with its benches still bolted down, the engine room where the sabotage charge had torn through the bulkhead, the captain's cabin where their grandfather's logbook had waited three decades in the dark like a letter that refused to stop being written. Petra mapped all of it without flinching, mostly, and when they reached the sealed records compartment — empty now, its contents copied and sent into the world like sparks from a fire — they mapped that too, and labeled it in small letters: *here the truth was kept.*

Odile came by most evenings to check the charts and drink tea that neither of them liked very much.

"You've got the cargo hold wrong," she said one night, tapping the drawing. "There's a false wall on the starboard side. Your grandfather showed me once, when we were young and stupid. Hidden space behind the timber racking."

Petra looked at her. "You knew him well?"

"Well enough that he asked me to keep an eye on the harbor if anything ever happened to him." She turned her cup in her hands. "I did a poor job of it for thirty years. Watching isn't the same as doing. So now I do."

"That's why you rallied the divers. The picket."

"That's why I did half the fool things I've done since you rowed into my office looking like a drowned cat with a legal grievance." She smiled, and it softened the iron of her face. "He'd be proud of you, Petra. I want that said plainly, because dead men get remembered in summaries and you deserve to hear the whole sentence."

Petra looked down at the chart, at the neat lines of a ship they'd once known only as a job site, a payday, a forbidden depth. *Proud* was a word that fit badly in their mouth after so many years of exile. But it fit better every day.

"False wall," they said at last, voice not entirely steady. "I'll chart it tomorrow."

* * *

The neighbors arrived the way tide does — gradually, then all at once.

First it was Merrick with his family-discount epoxy and his endless inventory of stories about a rope-buying, argument-having, late-paying grandfather Petra had never really known. Then it was the widow Brannock from the net shed, who left smoked fish on the *Wake*'s deck with no note, because notes were for people who wanted thanks and she wanted nothing except, she said, to feed someone who'd earned it. Then the ferryman Doss, who taught Petra the harbor's unofficial rules (never tie up at berth four, never trust the Tuesday ice, always wave to the lighthouse keeper even though he'd been dead eleven years because his widow still kept the light and watched). Then Nell Corran, who appeared every few days with questions of escalating strangeness and a notebook she claimed was for school.

By the end of the month, Petra realized with genuine alarm that they knew everyone's names.

This had never happened before. In fifteen years of ports and anchorages, Petra had cultivated anonymity like a crop — first names only, histories vague, departures early. A life measured in miles required it. You couldn't leave cleanly if people knew you.

But here was the arithmetic of the harbor, unavoidable as tide tables: Merrick, who argued about rope prices the way Petra's grandfather supposedly had. Brannock, whose fish tasted like a memory Petra didn't have. Doss, who waved at ghosts. Nell, who believed in wrecks and uncles and the essential decency of both. Odile, who had kept a promise for thirty years out of loyalty to a man who couldn't thank her.

Petra sat on the *Wake*'s deck one evening, hull patched and riding true, and tried to calculate the distance home. There wasn't one. That was the discovery. Home wasn't a coordinate anymore; it was a radius, and the radius was full of names.

They'd spent years measuring escape routes. Nobody had ever taught them how to measure staying.

* * *

On a grey, soft morning with rain coming in off the escarpment like a rumor, Petra rowed out alone to the site — not to dive, just to float above it, engine off, the buoy marking the cave mouth bobbing a few yards away. Below them, forty fathoms down, the *Heir's Wreck* rested in her new dignity: charted, protected, ringed by the harbor's mooring buoys, visited now by divers who came to pay respects rather than strip cargo. The bell hung silent in the morning calm. The ghost, if there was one, was off duty.

Petra trailed a hand in the water and thought about the ledger of it all. A fleet lost. A family erased. A lie thirty years deep, hauled up into the light by one diver with a strongbox and a deadline. And underneath all of it, stranger than any of it: a grandfather who had engineered a legend as a door, and a granddaughter who had finally opened it, and a harbor that had walked through after them.

The rain thickened. Petra pulled up the anchor and turned the *Wake* toward shore, toward the boathouse where fresh charts waited to be drawn and a chandlery where an old man would argue cheerfully about rope, toward a table where someone would soon be feeding them whether they liked it or not.

Halfway back, they passed the ferry making its morning run, and Doss raised a hand from the wheelhouse, and Petra raised one back, easy as breathing.

Then, ahead, at the dock: a figure Petra hadn't seen since the trial testimony — broad-shouldered, roof-builder's forearms, a duffel bag at his feet and an expression caught somewhere between apology and hope. Tam. Uncle Tam, survivor, traitor, forgiven, who had taken a room above the chandlery last month and mostly kept to himself, and who was now standing at the foot of the *Wake*'s berth as though he'd been working up the nerve for days.

Petra throttled down and let the boat drift in.

"Morning," Tam called, too loudly. "I, ah. Merrick says you need help re-caulking the deck seams. And I'm — I was good at that, once. Before." He stopped, swallowed. "I don't suppose you'd want the help."

The rain came down softer. The harbor smelled of salt and woodsmoke and fish, and somewhere behind it all, faint and impossible, Petra thought they heard a bell.

"Aye," Petra said, and threw him the line.
