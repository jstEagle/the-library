## 21. The Heir's Table

### Odile

The boathouse had been Odile Vance's problem for three weeks, and she had solved it the way she solved everything: by delegating shamelessly and taking all the credit.

The Eastwick boathouse had stood empty since before she'd run her first tide as harbor-master—thirty years of gulls nesting in the rigging loft and salt rot eating the beams from the inside out. Now it smelled of fresh-cut pine and lamp oil, and forty-odd people crowded between the restored slipways where a long table had been built from the old *Meridian's* keel timber. Salvage oak, someone called it. Odile thought it was just good wood that had finally stopped drowning.

She stood at the head of it with a mug of cider and surveyed her work like an admiral reviewing a fleet.

"Vance," said Brannock the net-mender, sidling up with the expression of a man about to complain on principle, "you know I don't hold with ceremonies."

"You're wearing your good coat."

"I'm wearing my only coat."

"And you combed your beard."

"Wind did that."

"The wind," Odile said, "blew from the north today. Your part is on the left."

Brannock grumbled into his cider, which was how he said thank you in this harbor, and Odile let him.

She had spent most of her life watching people leave this coast—the young ones especially, slipping out on the morning boats with their duffels and their big eyes—and she had told herself it was simply the way of harbors. Water runs downhill; so do the ambitious. But watching the room fill tonight, watching faces she'd known since they were knee-high to a cleat, she understood something she'd never bothered to put into words: a harbor isn't the water. It's the table. And somebody had to build one long enough to seat everybody.

Petra Eastwick had built one without meaning to. All they'd done was tell the truth, once, loudly enough. The rest was carpentry.

The doors opened. Conversation dipped, rose again, dipped lower. Petra came in with salt still in their hair and a look on their face like a person walking into weather they'd been warned about but hadn't quite believed.

Odie raised her mug. "Sit down, heir. You're letting the cold in."

"I could stand by the door," Petra offered. "Guarding it."

"You could not. Sit."

And to Odile's considerable satisfaction—they sat.

### Tam

Tam Ashgrave—that was the name he wore now, though the name under it had been Eastwick for fifty years before he buried it—took the chair farthest from the lamp, out of habit older than the war he hadn't fought in.

He had expected this evening to be unbearable. He had rehearsed unbearable. He'd prepared three exits and a headache.

Instead, Maren Quill's youngest had climbed onto the bench beside him and demanded he explain how a man survives a ship going down under him, and when Tam said, "Badly, mostly, and then swimming," the boy laughed like it was the finest joke ever told in this county, and half the table laughed too, and Tam found that the unbearable thing had quietly become something else. Something with weight, but bearable weight. Like ballast. Ballast keeps you upright. That was the whole trick of it, and it had taken him four decades and one confession to learn what every child on this coast knew by seven.

He caught Odile watching him from the head of the table with that look she'd perfected—the one that said *I know exactly what you are and I've decided it's acceptable*, which was, Tam reflected, more mercy than he'd ever gotten from anyone with authority.

His hands were steady around the mug. That was new. For thirty years his hands had shaken whenever the water went flat and gray, the way it had gone flat and gray that morning the fleet went down. Tonight they were still. He kept looking at them, half expecting them to remember how to tremble.

Across the table, Petra was being cornered by the ferry-women, who wanted to know if the wreck's bell really rang on its own or whether there was a mechanism. Petra was explaining, patiently, that bronze doesn't need a mechanism, it needs a tide and a reason, and the ferry-women were nodding as though this were perfectly sound engineering.

*They came back,* Tam thought. *Both of us. One of us honest now.* He drank his cider and decided that counted as the family reunion nobody had planned and everybody, apparently, needed.

### Corran

Retired Lieutenant Corran had attended exactly two social functions in his naval career, both funerals, and he had approached tonight's gathering with the same posture: back straight, hands available, exit mapped.

He should not have come. His counsel had advised against it in terms usually reserved for defendants planning to attend their own trials. But the tribunal had cleared him—*commendation pending, career concluded*, which was the navy's way of saying *thank you, now disappear*—and Corran had discovered in the weeks since that disappearing was harder than it looked. He'd tried it. He'd gotten as far as the ferry landing twice.

So here he sat, in civilian clothes that fit him like an apology, between a fish-packer who kept offering him pickled herring and an old woman who remembered his grandmother and would not stop telling him so.

"You have her chin," the woman said for the fourth time. "That stubborn chin."

"Ma'am, I've never—"

"Oh, I know, I know. Navy took the records. Navy took everything." She patted his arm with a hand like a knotted rope. "But chins don't lie, dear."

Corran looked down the length of the keel-timber table—at the divers and net-menders and ferry-women, at Odile holding court, at the old man in the corner whose face Corran had studied in declassified crew photographs until he could have drawn it from memory, alive after all, sitting in lamplight eating bread—and felt something loosen in his chest that he suspected had been clenched since his first day in uniform.

For twenty-two years he had believed the service was a structure, and structures held because everyone bore weight in order. Then he'd read a logbook in a drowned captain's cabin and learned the structure had been bearing its weight on one family's back for sixty years, and calling it order.

He had submitted the evidence anyway. Not because it was safe—it had ended him—but because a lieutenant named Corran, age nine, standing on these same docks, had once asked his grandmother why the Eastwick name wasn't on any of the harbor rolls, and been told *some names get sanded off, dear, best not to ask*. He'd spent his whole career not asking. The chin, apparently, had other plans.

The fish-packer refilled his plate without asking. "You'll want the herring. Trust me."

Corran ate the herring. It was, he had to admit, excellent.

### Maren Quill

Maren Quill had fished these waters for thirty-one years and had opinions about everything, most of them loud. Tonight she kept them mostly to herself, because she was watching.

She watched Odile, who had organized this dinner the way she organized everything, with lists and threats and love disguised as logistics. She watched old Tam, who'd come back from the dead twice now—once out of the water, once out of his own silence—and who flinched only a little when the door banged. She watched the retired navy man eat herring like a man being forgiven in installments.

And she watched Petra Eastwick, who had saved every soul in this harbor without asking any of them first, and who now sat among them like a diver running out of air—polite, correct, counting the minutes to the surface.

Maren knew that look. She'd worn it herself the winter her husband died, when the whole harbor had turned up with casseroles and condolences and she'd smiled at every one of them and wept alone in the net shed, because grief shared is grief doubled if you've never practiced sharing anything.

Somebody had to break the seal on that woman. And Maren Quill had never in her life waited for somebody.

"So," she said, loud enough to turn heads, "are you going to tell it or are we going to sit here pretending we didn't row our boats out in a storm to watch you nearly drown?"

"Maren—" Odile began.

"No, no. We've all been polite for three weeks." Maren planted her elbows. "Politeness is for strangers. Tell the story, Eastwick. The real one. Start with the night dive, the one where you nearly blacked out at six fathoms like an amateur."

A ripple of laughter went around the table—not unkind, but expectant. Forty faces turned toward Petra, and Maren saw the exact moment the diver understood there was no current strong enough to swim against this.

### Petra

Petra had measured their whole life in miles. Distance from the harbor: forty. Distance from the family name: immeasurable, maintained daily. Distance from the past: total, until a low tide opened a hole in the escarpment and the past climbed out holding a strongbox.

Now they sat at a table made of their grandfather's ship, surrounded by people who knew their name, their business, their near-drownings, and their preferred way of cooking mussels, and Petra discovered that distance was a habit, and habits could drown.

"Start with the blackout," Maren insisted.

"It wasn't dramatic," Petra said. "I misjudged the depth. The charts were wrong. I surfaced, I corrected my tables, I went back down. That's diving."

"That's diving," Brannock agreed solemnly, "if you survive it."

"Fine." Petra set down the mug. The room quieted in a way rooms rarely did in this harbor, where quiet was a resource more precious than fresh water. "The night dive. Six fathoms down, fifteen years deep—that's what I called it afterward, in the log. Because the wreck sits at six fathoms, but everything it carried sat deeper. In me, I mean. Fifteen years since I'd said the name Eastwick out loud."

They told it. Haltingly at first, then finding the current. The navy-flagged wreck that shouldn't have been there. The descent where the dark pressed in like a held breath. The low tide that fell farther than any tide in living memory, opening a cave mouth in the escarpment like the coast itself exhaling. The strongbox, green with sixty years of sea, stamped with a crest they'd spent half a life scrubbing out of themselves.

"When I saw the crest," Petra said, "I didn't feel joy. I want to be honest about that. I felt a trap closing. My grandfather had written me into a salvage charter like a clause in a hull contract—claim the wreck within six tides or forfeit it forever. And I thought, *he's doing it to me from beyond the grave, he's making me come home.*"

"And was he?" asked the ferry-woman with the sharp eyes.

Petra considered. "Yes. But not the way I thought. I thought it was a cage. It was a door. There's a difference, and it took me five tides and a great deal of nearly dying to see it."

They told about the manifest, and the room went still at the right places. They told about the sealed compartment, and the collapse trap rigged with a wrecked diver's patience, and Brannock swore softly into his beard. They told about the records—what the navy had scuttled along with the fleet, what names had been sanded off which rolls—and Corran, to his lasting credit, did not look away.

They even told about Tam. Carefully. Kindly. "My uncle survived the sinking," Petra said, "and spent thirty years guarding the wreck against us, because he thought keeping the Eastwicks away was the only way to keep them alive. He was wrong, but he was wrong out of love, and I've seen worse reasons for worse things done by better people." Across the table, Tam raised his mug a half-inch, which from Tam was a eulogy.

"And the bell?" the youngest Quill demanded. "Did it ring by itself?"

Petra smiled—the rare one, the one that reached all the way up. "Bronze needs a tide and a reason. The tide turned. I'd placed the charter in the strongbox. The claim clause triggered, the old signal rig fired, and the bell rang over the whole harbor while I was swimming out of a collapsing compartment with my ears bleeding." A pause. "It rang by itself the way a heart beats by itself. Something had to start it. After that, it just kept going."

Silence, then—not empty silence but full silence, the kind a full room makes when everyone is thinking the same thing and none of them need to say it.

Then Maren Quill stood, banged her mug on the keel timber, and said, "To the heir," and forty voices said it back, and Petra Eastwick sat very still amid the noise, learning the shape of a word they'd refused for fifteen years.

*Home,* thought Petra. *Not a place you escape to. A place you surface into.*

Later, when the cider was gone and the lamps burned low, Odile appeared at their elbow with a folded paper. "Came with the afternoon boat. No return address."

Petra opened it. Read it twice.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

Petra looked toward the windows, where beyond the glass the escarpment stood black against the stars, and somewhere below the black, at the turn of the next low tide, a cave mouth waited with its door open.

"A letter," Petra said slowly, "from the naval archive. Someone's been requesting the Eastwick files. Every file. For months." They turned the paper over, as if the answer might be written on the back. "And whoever it is signed the last request with a name I haven't heard since I was six years old."

Odile's eyebrows went up. "Whose?"

Petra folded the letter and tucked it away, and for the first time in fifteen years, the name came easily, spoken aloud in a warm room full of people who would help:

"My mother's."
