## 22. Escarpment and Horizon

The tide tables said the water would fall away from the escarpment at four minutes past six in the morning, and for the first time in Petra Eastwick's life, they were up before the tide out of eagerness rather than dread.

They stood at the cave mouth with a thermos of Odile's terrible coffee—she called it *harbor strength*, which was a polite way of saying it could strip varnish—and watched the sea pull back from the rock like a curtain being drawn. The cave mouth emerged in stages: first a dark seam, then a gape, then the full arch of it, dripping and barnacled, breathing its cold mineral breath into the morning air.

A year ago, Petra had stood on this same shelf of rock with their heart hammering and their hands shaking, staring into that black water and seeing nothing but a trap snapping shut around them. Six tides, the charter had said. Claim or forfeit.

Now the cave just looked like a door. Their door.

"You're going to dawdle all morning," said a voice behind them, "or are we actually diving?"

Tam leaned against the boathouse wall, arms crossed, wearing a wetsuit that fit him badly because he refused to admit he'd put on weight since the scuttling. His hair had gone almost entirely gray now, but his eyes were the same ones Petra had known when they were small enough to ride on his shoulders down the harbor road—the eyes of a man who had once taught a child to hold their breath by making a game of it.

"We're diving," Petra said. "I'm savoring."

"Savoring." Tam snorted. "Your grandfather used to say the same thing. Stood on this exact rock for twenty minutes one morning while the tide came back in. Your grandmother nearly left without him."

"She should have," Petra said, grinning. "It would have served him right."

"It would have," Tam agreed solemnly, "and then she'd have turned the boat around at the harbor mouth because she couldn't stand the thought of him swimming home. That was the whole trouble with the Eastwicks. We act like we're made of stone and we're actually made of—" He waved a hand vaguely at the horizon, at the harbor, at everything. "That."

Petra let the word sit there, unspoken. They had spent fifteen years refusing to name what they were made of. Miles, mostly. Distances deliberately kept. The far side of every map.

But that was before.

* * *

The descent was easy in a way descents had never been easy before. No fleet overhead. No navy patrol boats idling beyond the point with their guns trained on nothing in particular. Just the two of them, dropping through green-gold light into the wreck that the whole harbor now called by its proper name: the *Meridian's Pride*, last ship of the Eastwick line, scuttled by men who had feared what she carried more than they feared drowning her.

Petra swam the main deck slowly, trailing fingers along railings gone soft with decades of salt. The hull had been opened carefully since the claim—Odile had insisted on proper survey work, no blasting—and light fell through the new cut in long dusty shafts, illuminating the cargo hold where the records had lain sealed for so long. Copies of those records hung in the harbor office now, framed, next to the restored rolls of the families the navy had erased. The originals were in the provincial archive, under seal, pending the inquiry that Corran's testimony had set in motion.

Corran himself had retired to a fishing cottage two coves north. He sent Petra a card every solstice. It always said the same thing: *Still glad I chose wrong.* Petra had stopped trying to figure out if he meant it as a joke.

They drifted through the captain's cabin, where grandfather's logbook lay open under glass now—a museum piece, though the harbor treated it less like an exhibit than like scripture. Tam hovered at the doorway of it, not entering. He never entered. Some rooms you weren't ready to walk back into, even underwater, even after everything was forgiven. Petra didn't push. Forgiveness, they had learned, wasn't a door you kicked open. It was a tide. It came in on its own schedule.

Instead they swam together along the starboard corridor, past the crew quarters where bunks still held their blankets like sleeping men had only just left them, and came at last to the place Petra had been circling all morning without admitting it: the strongbox.

It sat open now, empty, its lid propped back. The Eastwick crest gleamed dully on its face—the escarpment and the horizon, carved by some long-dead smith who had probably thought he was making something decorative and had instead made a prophecy. Petra ran a thumb over the metal.

Inside, once, had been a charter naming an heir. Inside, once, had been a trap and a door and a grandfather's desperate hope, all folded into wax seals and legal language.

Now it held nothing, and somehow that felt like the truest inheritance of all. An empty box. Room.

"Your grandmother's ring is still in the purser's drawer," Tam said quietly beside them, his voice strange through the regulator. "Third drawer down. I checked last month. Couldn't bring myself to take it."

Petra looked at him.

"I'll get it," Petra said.

"I know you will." He paused. "That's the difference between us, girl. I spent my whole life thinking coming back meant admitting I failed. You figured out it just means coming back."

He turned away then, swimming toward the bow, giving Petra the privacy of the moment—which was, Petra reflected, exactly the kind of gift Uncle Tam had always been best at giving, even when he'd been pretending to be someone else entirely.

Petra took the ring. Third drawer down, just as he'd said, wrapped in oilcloth against the years. A thin gold band with a chip of blue stone that might have been glass, might have been sapphire; grandmother had never told anyone which, and had enjoyed the mystery too much to ever resolve it. Petra closed their fist around it and felt it press into their palm like a small warm heartbeat.

Then they kicked for the surface, and the light took them.

* * *

They broke water facing east.

It was habit now, deliberate habit, the way Odile had taught them to end every dive: come up facing open water, get your bearings, know your way home. But this morning the instruction landed differently. This morning the horizon was doing what it always did—stretching out, endless, indifferent—and Petra found they could look at it without counting miles.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, coming home. You expected the big feelings. The dramatic ones. The weeping at graves and the shouting matches and the grand reconciliations. What you got instead was this: coffee that tasted like tar, an uncle who complained about his wetsuit, a harbor full of people who knew your name and used it casually, without ceremony, the way you'd use the name of a chair or a street. Belonging, it turned out, was mostly furniture. Mostly ordinary. Mostly showing up.

Odile was waiting on the rock shelf when they climbed out, wrapped in a coat against the morning chill, looking like a woman who had personally arranged the sunrise and found it adequate.

"Well?" she said. "Did you visit properly?"

"We visited."

"And?"

"And I got it." Petra opened their hand. The ring caught the early light, blue flashing.

Odile went very still, in the way of harbor people receiving news bigger than their faces wanted to show. Then she reached out and touched the ring with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"Your grandmother wore that the day she signed the harbor petition," she said. "I was eleven. She let me hold it while she talked to the councilmen. Told me, *you keep this safe, little fish, and someday I'll tell you what it opens.*" She laughed, short and wet. "Never did tell me. Woman kept her secrets tighter than her hatches."

"She's telling you now," Petra said. "Sort of. Through me. It's a family tradition—information arriving forty years late via inconvenient relatives."

"That tracks," Odile said dryly. "Breakfast? Marta's got the good bread today, and Tam will complain about his suit whether or not we feed him, so we may as well feed him."

From the water, Tam's voice floated up: "I heard that."

"You were meant to!"

Petra stood a moment longer, ring in hand, and looked back at the cave mouth. The tide was turning; already the water was creeping up the rock, reaching for the arch, preparing to close the door for another day. Tomorrow it would open again. And the day after. Low tide twice daily, faithful as anything, for as long as the escarpment stood and the moon kept pulling.

For fifteen years Petra had measured life in miles—the distance from here to anywhere else, the length of the escape route, the depth of the water over the things they never wanted to see again. It had been a useful unit of measurement. It had kept them alive.

But units of measurement, like tides, changed.

Now they counted differently. Counted in years, maybe—or in smaller things. In mornings. In loaves of Marta's bread. In the number of people who'd notice if their mooring line went slack. In tides until the inquiry report came out, in days until the memorial bell rang again, in hours until dinner at the boathouse where the whole improbable, stubborn, forgiving population of the harbor would squeeze itself around one table and argue about whose turn it was to wash up.

In seconds, sometimes. Like now. Standing on wet rock with a dead woman's ring in their living hand, watching the sea close a door they no longer needed to keep shut.

"Eastwick!" Odile called. "Bread waits for no one!"

Petra smiled, pocketed the ring, and turned their back on the horizon—not running from it, not anymore, just choosing, freely and for good, the other direction.

Home was behind them, up the harbor road, smelling of woodsmoke and baking. Home was a table with too few chairs and too many people determined to squeeze onto them anyway. Home was a name on a charter, spoken out loud, finally theirs.

The tide came in behind them, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, and walked them home.
