## 1. The Debt on the Dock

The tax collector arrived on the morning tide, which was fitting, because everything that ever went wrong in my life came in with the water.

I was below decks when he stepped onto my boat, going through the engine log with a pencil stub and a cup of Marisol's coffee, which is to say I was having the last good hour of my old life and didn't know it. The *Kestrel* rocked gently against her moorings at Sallow's Landing, and the rain hadn't started yet, and for about forty minutes the world was a reasonable place.

Then Odessa's boots hit the deck planking overhead—three hard strikes, our signal for *official trouble, come up looking respectable*—and I put down the pencil and looked at my father's handwriting in the log, dated eleven years back, and thought: *there it is.*

You can always tell. Trouble has a sound like a held breath.

I climbed up into the grey light. The man standing on my dock planks was a tax collector the way a pike is a fish—technically true, and missing the whole character of the thing. Grey coat, grey ledger, grey little mustache groomed to the width of a coin. He had a clerk behind him carrying an iron document case, and the clerk had the particular misery of a man who'd been made to stand in river weather holding government property.

"Captain Vane." He didn't offer a hand. Tax collectors never do; hands are for people who might want something from you. "Aurel Finch, Provincial Revenue Office."

"Captain Vane isn't here," I said. "She's a fictional person invented by whoever's name is on those papers."

Finch consulted his ledger anyway, which told me everything about how our conversation would go. "Felix Vane, master of the river steamer *Kestrel*, registry number 4-4-1-9. Daughter of Tomas Vane, deceased nine years this autumn. Executor of his estate."

"Estate," I repeated. "That's a generous word for what he left me."

"He left you the boat."

"He left me the boat," I agreed. That much was true. He'd also left me the debts, but nobody seizes a dock over debts; they seize things that float.

"Then you'll want to see this." He opened the ledger to a page marked with a ribbon so red it looked embarrassed to be there. "Outstanding obligations against the estate of Tomas Vane, accruing interest since date of death. Harbor fees, dredging levies, three years of lapsed survey taxes on the upper berth he leased at Corben's Reach—" he read the figure aloud, and I'll spare you the number, because numbers like that aren't information, they're weather. They're the kind of thing that comes down on you regardless of what you do.

"I've paid on that debt every season," I said. "Eleven years. Ask anyone at the Revenue office in Corben."

"I'm sure you have." He said it the way you'd say *I'm sure the tide comes in*, meaning: yes, and it doesn't matter, because the arithmetic was designed before either of us was born. "Payments have been received. Principal has not moved. Interest, Captain, is a patient animal."

Behind him, the clerk shifted the iron case from one hand to the other. I knew what was in it. Everyone on the river knows what's in the iron case when it comes aboard your boat. Your papers. Your registry, your certificate of inspection, your pilot's bond—the whole paper skeleton that lets a hull be a ship instead of scrap. Without them, the *Kestrel* wasn't mine. She wasn't anything. She was thirty tons of riveted irony sitting in the water waiting for someone official to call her condemned.

"You're taking her papers," I said.

"I'm serving notice of seizure," Finch corrected, with the small pleasure of a man who enjoys precision more than mercy. "You have ten days to satisfy the obligation or arrange terms. After that, the papers transfer to the Provincial office and the vessel becomes property of the Crown's revenue fleet."

"The revenue fleet," I said. "They'll give her a number instead of a name."

"They usually do."

I looked past him, out at the river. It was doing its morning work, brown and unhurried, carrying somebody's logs downstream and somebody else's hopes upstream. Eleven years I'd kept the *Kestrel* one payment ahead of ruin, running cargo nobody important wanted hauled, keeping my head below the political waterline, staying so invisible I sometimes startled myself in mirrors. And the whole time, the debt had been down there in the dark under my keel, growing teeth.

My father. Dead nine years and still finding ways to spend my money.

"Ten days," I said.

"Ten days." Finch snapped the ledger shut. Then—and this is the part I want on the record—he paused. Looked at me with something that wasn't quite sympathy. Sympathy would have been worse. This was more like a man checking his watch before a scheduled event. "Though I should say, Captain, that you may have a visitor before then. Someone with authority to discuss alternatives."

"What kind of alternatives?"

"The kind," said Aurel Finch, "that are not discussed by tax collectors."

He left the notice pinned to my wheelhouse door with a brass tack, square in the middle, where no one could miss it. Then he and his miserable clerk went off down the dock to ruin somebody else's morning, and I stood there in the grey light reading the seizure order twice, because the first time through I'd been reading it as a woman with options, and I wanted to read it again as a woman without them.

Odessa came up beside me. She didn't say anything. Odessa never says anything when saying nothing will do; she just stands there being six feet of quiet competence until you feel obliged to explain yourself.

"Papers," I said.

"I heard." She turned her coffee cup in her hands. "How much?"

"More than the boat's worth. Maybe more than the boat's worth twice."

"Huh," said Odessa, which from her was a eulogy.

* * *

Halloran came at dusk, which told me he'd wanted to watch me sit with the notice all day. Government men love a deadline the way priests love a dying man—it focuses the soul.

I knew who he was before he introduced himself, because he was the only man on that dock wearing city clothes that fit, and because the two men flanking him were wearing their coats loose over holsters in the way that passes for subtlety in the provinces. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with a face built for being trusted—open, weathered, the kind of face that makes you think of fathers and harbor pilots and other categories of person who have historically ruined me.

"Captain Vane. Deputy Director Halloran, Office of Territorial Survey." He offered his hand, and I took it, because refusing would have been a data point too. His grip was warm and dry and exactly calibrated. Everything about Halloran was exactly calibrated. "May I come aboard?"

"It's not my boat anymore," I said. "Ask the Revenue office."

"By Friday it will be. Today it's yours." He stepped aboard anyway, and I let him, because the alternative was making a scene on my own dock in front of his guns, and I have survived this long by knowing which fights are real fights.

We stood in my wheelhouse, which suddenly looked shabby, the way your house looks when company arrives who owns the company that built it. He ran a finger along the chart table, examined the dust, and smiled like the dust pleased him.

"Do you know what's upriver, Captain?"

"Rain," I said. "Cliffs. The Escarpment. A lot of water coming down fast and nowhere polite to put it."

"A government survey team," he said. "Nine people. Cartographers, geologists, a hydrologist. They went up into the high valleys above the escarpment headwaters seven weeks ago to chart the tributary systems. They were due back twelve days ago." He let that sit. "They're overdue."

"That's a shame."

"It is. And it's why I'm here." He turned to face me fully, and I'll grant him this: he didn't perform reluctance. Whatever he was about to ask, he'd already decided I was going to do it, and the performance was only courtesy. "I need a boat and a captain to take a rescue party up the Monsoon Coast route, through the gorge, to the last known coordinates. The season's turning early this year. Every day matters."

"So hire one. There are bigger boats than mine. Captains who haven't spent a decade minding their own business."

"There are," Halloran agreed pleasantly. "But I don't need a big boat. I need this boat. Draft of four feet, shallow-water screws, an engineer who can keep a dead engine alive on prayer and baling wire—I've read your service record, Captain, or rather, I've read the version of it that exists." Something flickered there, quick as a fish turning under the surface. "And I need a captain who knows the gorge sandbars the way your father did. Who learned them from him."

The rain started then, soft against the wheelhouse glass, and I became very aware of the brass-tacked notice behind me, and of the fact that Halloran had not once looked at it, because he didn't need to. He'd written it, or signed it, or at minimum had bought the man who did.

"And in exchange?" I asked, though we both knew.

"In exchange, the estate's obligations are satisfied in full. Papers returned, lien dissolved, your father's name cleared of arrears." He paused, delicately. "And certain other records remain where they are. In the dark. Unexamined."

There it was. Not the debt—the *other* thing. The thing I'd buried deeper than any sandbar, the reason a woman with my hands and my father's charts spends eleven years hauling fertilizer and fence posts instead of making a name. Halloran hadn't said it aloud. He didn't have to. Men like him don't threaten; they *inventory*. They let you hear your own secrets rattling around in their pockets.

"You said rescue party," I said. "How many men?"

"Four. Led by my surveyor, Brandt. You'll find him efficient."

"Efficient," I said. "On the river we call that cold."

"On the river," said Halloran, "you call a great many things by gentler names. That's part of why I trust you." He took a folded contract from inside his coat and laid it flat on my chart table, squared its corners to the edge, and set his pen on top of it. All very neat. All very final. "Seven weeks is a long time, Captain. If any of those nine people are alive up there, they're alive because someone reaches them soon. I'd like that someone to be you."

And here's the thing I keep turning over since, the splinter under the skin of it: I believed him. Or some of him. There was something in his face when he said *alive* that wasn't theater. Halloran wanted those people found. What he wanted *done* with them once found—that was the part he'd left out of the contract, the way you leave out the fine print about interest, the way my father left out the fine print about everything.

I picked up the pen. It was a good pen, heavy, government issue, the kind that signs away rivers.

I thought about Odessa below, and the twins, and Marisol burning dinner on purpose because she says it keeps the crew humble. I thought about ten days, and the iron case, and the *Kestrel* with a number instead of a name. I thought about my father's sandbars, which I have never once shared with another living soul, and which Halloran apparently knew about anyway, which should have frightened me more than it did, right then, with the pen already moving.

Cornered animals sign things. That's what corners are for.

"If anyone on my crew refuses this run," I said, "they walk off paid in full, and that comes out of your office, not mine."

"Agreed."

"If Brandt touches my wheel, I put him ashore wherever we happen to be, contract or no contract."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Halloran's face. "Agreed."

"And if I find out this was never about a rescue—"

"Captain." He said it gently, almost kindly, gathering his coat, pausing at the wheelhouse door with the rain silvering down behind him. "If you find that out, what could you possibly do about it?"

He went down the dock into the wet, and his men folded around him like water closing over a stone, and I stood alone with the contract drying on my chart table and the seizure notice tacked to my own door, and outside the river kept on with its brown, patient work, rising by inches, the way it does before a monsoon.

Below, I could hear Odessa stoking the stove, and Pip laughing at something Rook said, and Marisol swearing at a pot. My whole world, in three sounds.

In ten days' time, none of it would be mine to protect unless I got those papers back.

I signed.
