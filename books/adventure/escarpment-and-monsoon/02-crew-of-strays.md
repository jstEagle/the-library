## 2. Crew of Strays

You can tell a lot about a person by how they answer a knock at an unreasonable hour. Odessa answered hers with a wrench in one hand and a look that said she'd been awake anyway, because sleep was for people whose engines didn't talk to them at night.

"Felix," she said. "It's four in the morning."

"It's five somewhere upriver."

"Upriver's not my problem." But she stepped back to let me in, which was the whole answer, wasn't it. Odessa Kwan had a way of saying no with her mouth and yes with her feet. I'd learned to read her feet.

Her workshop smelled of coal oil and scorched copper, and every horizontal surface held some engine in various states of confession. She cleared a stool by sweeping a carburetor onto a rag and sat me down like a doctor about to deliver bad news.

"You're up before dawn looking like a woman who signed something," she said. "So either you got married or you got bought."

"Close to the second."

I told her most of it. The tax man on the dock with his little ledger and his little smile. My father's debts, resurrected like something drowned that wouldn't stay down. Halloran in his government grey, offering to make it all evaporate if I'd just ferry a rescue party up the Monsoon Coast and into the throat of the escarpment country. A survey team gone missing. Families wanting answers. All very humane, all very official, stamped twice and smelling faintly of lampblack and lies.

"Rescue," Odessa repeated, in the tone she used for parts that were advertised as new.

"That's what the paper says."

"And you believe the paper."

"I believe the paper was written by the same kind of man who wrote my father's debts." I turned my cup in my hands. "But they've got my boat's papers, Dess. Not seized—*held*. There's a difference, and the difference is I can't legally float so much as a skiff until this is done. He didn't threaten me. He didn't have to. He just showed me the lock he'd already built around my life and offered the key."

Odessa was quiet a while. She had a gift for quiet; most people fill silence like a hull fills with water, but she let it sit there and do its work.

"You could run," she said finally. "There are rivers south that don't know your name."

"There are rivers south where a boat without papers gets impounded and a captain without papers gets worse. And there's Marisol's rent, and Pip and Rook eating like growing boys who happen to be twenty-six, and your shop lease." I said it flatly, because dressing it up would've been insulting to both of us. "I'm not running. I'm hiring."

"Ah." She nodded slowly. "The famous Vane crew of strays."

"They're not strays."

"No?" She counted on grease-blackened fingers. "A cook who fled a marriage with nothing but knives and a grudge. Twins nobody else would take because they steal anything not bolted down—and some things that are. An engineer the guild blacklisted for telling the truth about a ferry that killed nine people. And you. Captain Felix Vane, who came down out of the high river seven years ago with no history and no last port." She smiled, and it wasn't unkind. "Strays, Felix. The best kind. But strays."

I couldn't argue. Arguing with Odessa is like arguing with a river gauge; it just tells you the level again.

"I need you," I said. "Engine room's going to be hell above the gorge. Early monsoon, maybe. Cold water, silt, debris. I need somebody who can keep a heart beating when everything else says stop."

"You need somebody who'll come," she corrected gently. "That's different from needing me specifically."

"It isn't."

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her get up and start pulling tools from the wall rack, sorting them into a canvas roll with the economy of long habit.

"Pay?" she asked.

"Whatever's left after the tax man. Which is to say, glory."

"Glory sinks." She cinched the roll tight. "I'm coming because three years ago you paid my guild fine out of your own pocket and never once mentioned it, and because whoever these missing people are, someone ought to go find them who isn't doing it for a flag." She paused at the door of the back room, where she kept a cot and forty years of engine manuals. "And because you told me the run smells wrong, and you're still going. Somebody has to be aboard who watches the water instead of the money."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a *yes, and*." She disappeared inside. "Give me two hours. If we're going up into cold country I'm taking the good injectors, and if anyone asks, they were always yours."

* * *

The twins found me, which was how it always went. You don't summon Pip and Rook; you simply exist in a location, and within the day they accrete to you like iron filings to a magnet with a bad reputation.

They were sitting on my dock bollard when I came back at midmorning, sharing one cigarette between them the way they shared everything—badly, with commentary. Pip spotted me first, as usual. Rook always pretended he hadn't, then looked anyway.

"Captain Vane," Pip said, sliding off the bollard. "We heard a rumor."

"You heard a rumor."

"A rumor involving the *Kestrel*, a government contract, and the escarpment run." Her eyes were bright as coins. "In that order of interest."

"Where'd you hear that?"

"Marisol," both twins said together, which figured. In a town the size of ours, news travels fastest through kitchens.

I looked at them a moment. Twenty-six years old and identical only in the ways that mattered—same lean hunger, same crooked grins, same habit of standing slightly angled toward each other like two blades of one scissors. They'd come aboard four years ago caught stealing my spare anchor chain off the dock, and instead of turning them in I'd put them to work hauling it back, and then hauling everything else, forever after. It remained the best disciplinary decision of my career.

"The run's bad," I said. "Above the last settlement there's no pilotage worth trusting, the maps are ten years stale, and the weather's turning early. And the people paying us—" I hesitated. Here was the part where a smart captain lied to her crew, and here was the part where I'd promised myself I wouldn't. "The people paying us want this done quietly. Quiet work goes wrong loud. I can't promise you come back with more than you leave with."

Rook spoke first, which was rarer than rain in the dry season. "You ever lie to us yet?"

"Not yet."

"Then we figure you're not starting now." He shrugged, elaborately casual, though his eyes weren't. "Bad run, quiet pay, wrong-smelling job. Sounds like every job we've ever survived."

"We're coming," Pip said, in the voice that closed negotiations. She stuck out her hand, and I shook it, and her grip was calloused exactly where mine was. "But I want it noted for the record that we're coming because it's you asking, and not because any of us believes in the mission."

"Noted."

"And the twins' rate doubles past the gorge."

"Your rate doesn't change."

"Then we'll steal the difference," she said cheerfully, and Rook laughed, and that was settled, God help me.

* * *

Marisol didn't make me ask. That was the strange mercy of it. I went to her kitchen at the back of the boarding house expecting to negotiate, and found her already packing dried chilies into tins with the grim focus of a woman arming herself.

"Odessa told you," I said.

"Odessa told everyone. Odessa is terrible at secrets and wonderful at loyalty, which is why the guild hated her." She sealed a tin, set it in the crate, reached for another. "How many days up?"

"Twelve, if the river's kind. Twenty if it isn't."

"How many mouths?"

"Six. Maybe seven." I hadn't decided yet whether to say the seventh aloud. "Halloran wants to send his own man."

Her hands stopped. Marisol's hands never stopped; watching them stop was like watching a clock halt. "His own man."

"A surveyor. Brandt. Cold-eyed, quiet, carries his kit like it's loaded."

"They all carry their kits like that now." She resumed packing, faster. "Felix. Look at me."

I looked at her. She was fifty-one years old and had crossed half the world to escape a man who'd broken her wrist on their anniversary, and she had rebuilt herself in a galley the size of a coffin, feeding dockworkers and drifters and once, memorably, an entire stranded wedding party through a flood week. She fed people the way other women prayed.

"This run is wrong," she said. "You think it's wrong. I can see you thinking it's wrong, the way you stand in my doorway like the floor might be wired." She pointed a chili-dusted finger at me. "So say it plain, and then I'll decide."

"It's wrong. I think the rescue is real the way a funeral is real—the grief is genuine, but somebody planned it. I think we're being used to fetch something, or someone, or to confirm something's dead. And I think if I refuse, I lose the boat, and if I accept, I might lose more than the boat. And I'm going anyway, because I don't see a third door, and I'm sorry, Mari, I hate that I'm asking this of you."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she added a second crate to the stack.

"I'm not coming for you," she said. "Don't flatter yourself."

"All right."

"I'm coming because whoever's alive up on that escarpment has been eating government rations for weeks, and government rations are an abomination, and if I let strangers starve on bad food when I could have prevented it, I won't sleep." She tied the crate shut with a vicious knot. "Also for you. Slightly. Don't let it go to your head."

"Slightly noted."

"And Felix—if your government man puts one hand on my galley stove without permission, feed him to the river."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

Brandt arrived the next morning, which told me he'd been in town all along, watching. Government men always arrive second; arriving first means admitting you needed to scout, and scouting is an admission of uncertainty, and uncertainty is the one thing their uniforms aren't cut to hold.

He came down the dock at eight sharp with a single case and a coat too clean for the climate, and he stood at my gangway like a man auditioning for the role of passenger.

"Captain Vane."

"Surveyor Brandt."

"You know my name."

"Halloran mentioned it. Once. With the enthusiasm of a man reading a warranty." I didn't move from the gangway. We regarded each other across three planks of wet wood. He was perhaps forty, grey at the temples, with eyes the color of overcast water—not cruel, exactly. Calibrated. "Let's understand each other before you step aboard, Mr. Brandt. This is my boat. My crew answers to me. You ride as a passenger with a tasking, and the day your tasking conflicts with the safety of my people, you and I are going to have a conversation, and I promise you won't enjoy it."

Something moved behind his eyes—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Understood."

"And you eat when Marisol feeds you, sleep where you're berthed, and touch nothing in the engine room unless Odessa invites you, which she will not."

"Understood."

"Do you have questions?"

"One." He stepped onto the gangway, unhurried. "The missing team. Do you actually care whether they're found?"

The question caught me sideways, which I suspect was its purpose. Most men open negotiations with money or threats. Brandt opened with a scalpel question wrapped in sincerity, and I still don't know whether it was honesty or bait.

"I care that somebody's been up there starving while the paperwork got filed," I said. "Whether I care about the individuals is beside the point. Dead or alive, they deserve better than a footnote."

He considered that for a long moment, the way he seemed to consider everything—as though filing it somewhere permanent.

"Then we'll manage," he said, and walked aboard.

* * *

We cast off two days later, on a morning so clear it felt like the sky was showing off before the monsoon took its turn. The *Kestrel* sat low in the water with fuel and stores, engines warm, papers temporarily restored under seal—a government escort document that read less like permission than a leash with prettier stitching.

I gathered them on the aft deck before the first bend took us out of sight of town. All six of us: Odessa with her arms crossed, the twins leaning against the rail in mirrored slouches, Marisol wiping her hands on her apron out of pure habit, Brandt standing apart with his case at his feet like a man waiting for a train he suspected wouldn't come.

"I'm not going to give a speech," I said. "You'd all remember it and quote it back at me at the worst possible moments."

Pip opened her mouth. I pointed at her. She closed it.

"Here's the truth instead. I think this job is wrong. I think the men who hired us know more than they've said, and I think what they haven't said is the dangerous part. Every one of you had a reason to stay ashore, and every one of you is here anyway, and I want you to know I noticed. I notice everything. It's my worst quality."

"Second worst," Odessa murmured.

"—and I'm asking one thing. Watch the water, watch each other, and watch our passenger. If anything feels wrong, you bring it to me, not to each other, not to the dark. We go up that river as a crew and we come back as one, or not at all. That's the speech. Pip, you may now ruin it."

"Too late," Pip said. "It was perfect. I'm framing it."

Rook snorted. Marisol rolled her eyes and went below to start lunch, which was her way of saying *amen*. Odessa squeezed my shoulder once, hard, passing by—her entire vocabulary of comfort in a single bruise.

Only Brandt stayed. He stood at the rail watching the town shrink behind us, and when he finally spoke, he didn't look at me.

"For what it's worth, Captain," he said quietly, "you're right to distrust the job. I'd think less of you if you didn't."

And then he picked up his case and went forward, leaving me at the wheel with the current taking hold and the escarpment waiting somewhere north like a wall the sky forgot to finish.

I told myself the knot in my stomach was just the coffee.

I told myself a lot of things, that morning.
