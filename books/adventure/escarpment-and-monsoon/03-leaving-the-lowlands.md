## 3. Leaving the Lowlands

The last friendly port was called Sallow Bend, which tells you everything you need to know about how the river names its towns. Nobody sallows there. It's a wide, muddy, generous place where the dockmaster knows your engine by its cough and the bakery opens before the fog does. We tied up at dawn on our third morning out, and I made a list.

I make lists the way other people pray. It keeps my hands busy and my heart from wandering off into places it can't come back from. This one I kept in the back of my logbook, under a page of tide tables, and it went like this:

*Things we are leaving behind:*

*1. The bakery on Sallow Bend that sells the seed rolls Marisol pretends not to love.*

*2. Tuesday dominoes at the Anchor & Cleat, where Rook cheats and Pip lets him.*

*3. The dog.*

I want to be clear about the dog. The dog is not ours. The dog is a brindled, opinionated animal named Barge who belongs to the fuel depot at Kiln Landing and who has decided, over four years of us buying diesel there, that Odessa is his person. Every time we cast off, Barge walks to the end of the pier and watches us until we're a speck, and every time Odessa says "he doesn't care," in the exact voice of someone who cares enormously. He was on the pier this morning. Of course he was. He always is. That's the whole trouble with dogs: they show up for the departures and never get to come along, and they don't even hold it against you.

*4. The sound the reeds make when the wind comes off the oxbow lakes — like a crowd deciding whether to applaud.*

*5. Being known.*

That last one took me a while to write. I sat on the cargo hatch with my pencil hovering and finally put it down because it was true and lists are no good if they lie. In the lowlands, people know us. They know the *Vespera* by her patched gunwale and her crooked smokestack, know that Felix Vane runs cargo honest and passengers only if she likes them, know that the twins are twins but not identical and will fight you if you suggest otherwise. Known is safe. Known is a harbor. And we were steaming out of every harbor we had, upriver toward the escarpment, carrying a government man who burned paper for warmth and a job that smelled wrong from the first handshake.

I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me do this properly, the way the river taught me: bow to stern, one thing at a time.

* * *

Felix gave us until noon at Sallow Bend, which was generous, because Felix is stingy with time the way misers are stingy with coins — carefully, and only when it matters. Marisol went straight to the bakery. She does this ritual where she buys six seed rolls and complains about the price and then eats three of them standing right there on the cobbles, crumbs down her apron, glaring at anyone who smiles at her. The baker's wife has learned not to smile. She just wraps the other three for later and slips an extra roll into the bag like she's smuggling contraband, and Marisol pretends not to see it, and both of them would deny under oath that any affection occurs.

Rook and I had the water run. That's a two-person job and always has been, since we were eleven and small enough to crawl the spaces between the tanks. You go aboard the water barge, you sign the chit, you haul hose, and while the tanks fill you have forty minutes of doing nothing in public, which in a river town means everyone who owes you gossip comes to pay up. We heard about the customs crackdown at Graywater. We heard about the new survey office stamps, red instead of blue, and what that meant nobody quite knew, only that it meant something. We heard old Tamsin Cotter had finally sold her dredge and moved inland, away from the water entirely, which made Rook go quiet in a way he thinks I don't notice.

"Whole river's twitchy," he said.

"Whole river's been twitchy since Halloran's lot started asking questions," I said. "Twitchy's just weather now."

He grunted. Rook communicates mostly in grunts and in the occasional sentence that lands like a dropped anchor. "You think this run's clean?"

I thought about Brandt. I thought about the way he'd stood on our deck the day he came aboard, not looking at the boat but through it, measuring. "No," I said. "But I think Captain signed, so we're going."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind captains leave us."

He didn't argue, because he'd already decided the same thing I had, back at the dock in Chapter One of this mess, when Felix told us plain: *This run smells wrong. If any of you want off, say it now and I'll not think less.* And none of us said it. Not because we're brave. Because leaving wasn't really on the menu once she'd warned us honest. You don't walk out on someone who gives you a fair door. You just don't.

Odessa spent the morning in the engine room, naturally, saying goodbye to her machinery, which is the closest thing she has to a religion. I brought her a seed roll around ten and found her elbow-deep in the feed pump with grease to the shoulder, talking to it. She talks to engines the way Marisol talks to bread dough — low, continuous, half complaint, half endearment.

"He'll hold," she said, without looking up. Meaning the pump. "She'll hold." Meaning the boat. "We'll hold." Meaning, I understood, all of us, though she'd have denied the arithmetic if I pointed it out.

"Barge was on the pier," I said.

Her shoulders did a thing. "Dogs don't have opinions."

"This one has a whole parliament."

She laughed, short and surprised out of her, and wiped her hands, and ate the roll in four bites, and that was the whole ceremony. Some goodbyes are small on purpose. You keep them small so they fit inside you without breaking anything.

* * *

The customs officer came aboard at half past eleven, and here is where my list got longer and uglier.

He was young, gray-uniformed, new-stamped — one of the red-stamp men, sent up from Graywater with a camera on a tripod strap and a clipboard full of forms nobody had needed filled out five years ago. His name was Ensign Pell or Pell-adjacent; I never caught it clean. He boarded politely enough, asked for papers, and then — this was the part that raised the hair on my arms — began photographing the boat.

Not the papers. The *boat*. Bow profile. Stern profile. The nameboard. The engine hatch. Me, standing at the rail, before I thought to step out of frame. Click. A little mechanical shutter sound, very precise, like a beetle closing its wings.

"Standard registry documentation," he said, when Rook asked, in his most anchor-dropping voice, what exactly he thought he was doing.

"Registry used to be ink and a stamp," Rook said.

"Procedures have been updated." Click. The crooked smokestack. Click. Odessa's tool rack, visible through the open hatch.

Now, I've grown up on this river and I know what official attention looks like. It looks like boredom, usually — a man checking boxes so he can get back to his tea. This was not boredom. This was a man building a file. And files, on rivers, are how boats stop being welcome anywhere, because a photograph travels faster than a reputation and lies flatter than either.

Felix came up from below and handled him the way she handles everything — flat, courteous, immovable as a piling. Papers produced, fees paid, questions answered with exactly as many words as required and not one more. But I watched her face while Pell photographed the wheelhouse, and I saw something I'd never seen on it before, or maybe had seen and hadn't known the name of. It was the look of a woman watching someone take measurements of a cage door.

When Pell finally clattered down the gangway, Felix stood at the rail a long moment, watching him go.

"Pip," she said, without turning. "How many photographs?"

"Eleven. Twelve counting me."

"Mm." She turned. Her face was back to normal, which is to say closed as a strongbox. "Log the time and date. Neatly."

"Captain—"

"Neatly, Pip."

So I logged it. And under it, in pencil, small, I wrote: *They're building a picture of us. Question is: for whom, and for after what?*

* * *

Brandt burned the manifest at one o'clock.

I want to describe this correctly, because it happened fast and quiet and I nearly missed it, and the nearly-missing-it is the part that still itches.

We were casting off. Lines in, current taking the bow, Sallow Bend sliding past with its bakery smell and its gulls and its Tuesday-dominoes dockside chatter. Brandt stood near the stern rail with his document case open — the case he'd carried aboard sealed, the case containing, supposedly, our cargo manifest and transit authorizations, the paperwork that made us legitimate to whatever officials might stop us upriver.

He fed the manifest into a small tin stove he'd set up, one designed for burning — I realized later he must have brought it for exactly this — and struck a match, and the pages went up orange and fast.

Rook saw it same time I did. "Hey—" he started, already moving.

"The manifest is superseded," Brandt said. Calm. Not defensive. He held up a fresh sheet between two fingers, pre-printed, official-looking. "New transit schedule. Sealed orders. The old routing is void." He dropped the second sheet into the stove too, and I swear on the river's mud I never learned what it said, because it curled and blackened before it finished turning over.

"You're burning our *papers*," Rook said, "on our *boat*, without asking."

"Yes." Brandt looked at him. His eyes are the color of shallow water over slate, and they don't blink much. "Ask your captain. She agreed to sealed orders."

And that was the worst part — he was right. Somewhere in the stack Felix had signed on that first terrible day, there'd been a clause, and Felix had read it, and Felix had said nothing to us, and now our manifest was ash drifting off the stern of the *Vespera* over water we'd never see again, and the only version of our business that existed was the one a government man carried in a locked case and could produce or not produce as suited him.

Marisol appeared at the rail beside me. She'd been below; she had flour still on her wrists. She watched the ash flakes settle on the current and get taken downstream, back toward Sallow Bend, back toward everything.

"Well," she said, in her kitchen voice, the one that means someone has touched her knives without permission. "That's a man who's tidying up ahead of something."

"Yeah," I said.

"Tidying ahead of something" is a phrase I grew up hearing from my grandmother, who ran a ferry crossing and buried two husbands and trusted nobody whose boots were too clean. It means: this person expects a moment coming when the record will matter, and they are making sure the record says what they need. Grandmother never once explained the phrase. She just said it, and years later you'd find out why, and you'd wish you'd listened harder sooner.

I listened hard now. I watched Brandt close his case, check the lock, check it again. I watched him glance upstream — not at the water, at the horizon above the water, where the escarpment would eventually stand, days off yet, invisible. He looked at it the way a man looks at a destination he's been to before. Or a destination he's read about in detail. There's a difference between those two looks and I couldn't split them yet, but I wrote the question down anyway, because that's what I do. Lists and questions. My grandmother would say it's a poor inheritance. It's kept me alive so far.

* * *

We cleared the last mooring buoy of Sallow Bend at two in the afternoon, and I climbed to the roof of the wheelhouse — my spot, the one place on the boat that's mine the way Odessa's is the engine room — and I did what I always do at departures, which is count backward.

Barge was long gone; we'd left Kiln Landing behind yesterday. But the gulls were with us, riding our wake, and the reeds were thick along the cutbank, and when the wind swung around they made their sound — the crowd-deciding-whether-to-applaud sound — and I put it on the list mentally, item six, *the reeds, again, because once isn't enough,* and then I stopped listing and just looked.

Here's the thing about the lowlands that you don't understand until you leave them: they're shallow everywhere except where they matter. The water's knee-deep for miles, brown and slow, full of carp and catfish and kids on rafts. Nothing dramatic. But underneath the shallows, the channel runs deep and cold and old, and every pilot on the river carries it in their head — where the deep water bends, where it hides, where it will hold you or drop you. The lowlands look like nothing and they know everything. That's home, if you ask me. Something that looks like nothing and knows everything.

Ahead of us the river changed. You could see it from the wheelhouse roof even this far down — the banks drawing in, the hills starting to gather themselves, the water picking up a gray-green tint that meant rock under the mud instead of more mud. The escarpment country. Survey country. Country where, according to the newspapers, a government team had gone missing, and according to Felix's face whenever she thought nobody was watching, something worse than missing had happened to them.

Behind us, Sallow Bend shrank to a brown smudge with a church spire. Somewhere in it, the baker's wife was wrapping tomorrow's rolls for customers who weren't us. Somewhere on a pier at Kiln Landing, a brindled dog was lying with his chin on his paws, waiting for an engine sound that wouldn't come for weeks, and Odessa was below decks right now not thinking about him, aggressively, with a wrench in her hand.

I took out my logbook and added the final entries:

*6. The reeds (again).*

*7. Ash on the water, going the direction we're not.*

*8. Twelve photographs of a boat that used to be nobody's business.*

Then I shut the book and sat with my back against the wheelhouse wall and listened to the engine change its note as the current strengthened, and I thought about what Rook had said — *that's not an answer* — and how he was right, and how none of us had answers, only a captain who'd signed and a crew who'd stayed and a stranger aft who burned paper like a man clearing his throat before bad news.

The light went gold, then gray. First rain spots hit the roof, fat and warm, the monsoon's scouts testing the ground. Upstream, where the hills had begun, the sky was the color of a bruise deciding.

I went down to tell Felix the rain was coming early.

She was already at the chart table, and she already knew.
