## 4. First Blood in the Water

The last settlement on the Monsoon Coast was called Gravel's End, which told you everything you needed to know about the optimism of the people who'd named it. It was a scatter of stilt houses and drying racks clinging to the north bank where the river stopped being friendly and started being a rumor. We took on water, bought a barrel of salt pork at three times its honest price, and let Marisol trade a sack of coffee for a string of river fish that she eyed with professional contempt.

"Fish are thin," she said, handing me one. "River's already stressed. Rains coming early."

"Rains always come early," I said. "They're showing off."

She didn't laugh. Marisol had spent thirty years reading water the way priests read scripture, and when she didn't laugh at my jokes it was usually because the water had told her something first.

We cast off from Gravel's End just past noon, and I stood at the wheel watching the settlement shrink behind us with the particular feeling of a woman closing a door she can't reopen. Above Gravel's End there were no more customs men, no more telegraph relays, no more doctors or magistrates or anyone whose opinion of us mattered. There was just the river, the escarpment rising blue and enormous in the north like a wall between worlds, and whatever Halloran hadn't told me.

Brandt stood at the rail beside me, making notes. He made notes the way other men breathed—constantly, unconsciously, in a small waterproof ledger with a pencil stub worn down to half its length. I'd been watching him for two days now, and I'd learned three things: he never slept more than four hours, he ate without pleasure, and he looked at the river not like a man looking at a river but like a man looking at a document that was trying to hide something.

"You've run this stretch before," he said. Not a question. Brandt didn't ask questions; he issued them dressed as statements and waited to see if you'd correct him.

"When I was younger," I said. "My father ran cargo up to the mining camps before the government decided the camps were a bad idea."

"And after?"

"After what?"

"After your father." He wrote something as he said it. Small pencil scratch, no bigger than a fly.

I turned the wheel two degrees to starboard for no reason except to have something to do with my hands. "After my father, I stayed south of Gravel's End for eleven years. You might ask why a woman with my reputation never once ran the upper river."

"I did wonder."

"There's your answer," I said. "I wondered too. And then I decided some rivers you don't go back up until someone puts a gun made of debt against your head."

He considered this. I'll give him that—Brandt considered things. Most men would have pushed, or smirked, or pretended they hadn't heard. He just wrote another little fly-scratch in his book and said, "The survey team came up here in the dry season. Four months ago. If any of them are alive, they've wintered above the gorge line."

"That's not survival conditions. That's a eulogy."

"Perhaps." He closed the ledger. "Or perhaps they were better prepared than the official manifest suggests. The manifest lists forty days of provisions for six men. The team was gone eleven weeks before anyone reported it."

I looked at him. "You know an awful lot about a missing team for a man who claims he's just along as an observer."

"I read," he said, "extensively."

* * *

We found the cache on the third morning above Gravel's End, in a cutbank where the river bent hard around a shelf of red stone and the current went slack enough to beach a boat. Pip spotted it first—from the crow's nest she'd rigged out of spite and spare lumber—and came down the rigging faster than Odessa would have liked.

"Marker cairn!" she shouted. "Red-painted stones, bank side! That's survey marking, isn't it? That's official!"

It was. The cache sat under a tarp weighted with stones, set back twenty feet from the high-water line in a hollow beneath the roots of a fallen fig. Standard government issue: a zinc-lined box, stenciled with the department crest, containing rice in sealed tins, salt pork, matches, quinine, a coil of signal wire, and a medical kit with most of its morphine gone.

Here is what I noticed, standing over that box while the crew gathered around me and the rain began to come down soft and gray through the fig leaves:

The seal on the box was broken. Not broken recently—the tin edges were rusted where fingers had pried them, and rust takes weeks, not hours. But the contents had been rearranged. The rice tins were stacked in a pattern no government clerk ever used—heavy ones on the bottom, light ones on top, the way a person who has to carry things arranges them. Someone had taken the morphine. Someone had taken two of the five signal flares. And someone had left something in exchange: a strip of dried fish wrapped in bark cloth, tucked into the corner of the box with a care that made my chest hurt.

"They restocked it," Pip said. She was crouched over the fish, turning it in her fingers. "Captain, look—they took food and left food. That's—" She stopped, because she'd understood it at the same moment I had.

"It means whoever's up there is thinking," I said. "Not panicking. Panicking people take. Thinking people trade."

Rook had drifted to the edge of the hollow and was studying the ground with the flat attention he gave to everything, which was how I knew he'd found something. "Tracks," he said. "One set. Booted, right sole worn on the outside edge. Came in from upstream two days ago, maybe three. Left the same way." He paused. "They circled the cache twice before approaching. Checked their backtrail."

"One person," Marisol said quietly. "Alone. Careful. Alive."

"Eleven weeks alone above the gorge line," Odessa said from the boat, where she'd stayed, because Odessa held the superstitious belief that the engine missed her if she left it, and honestly the engine probably did. Her voice carried flat across the water. "In monsoon country. On what the manifest says was forty days of food."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The rain ticked on the fig leaves. Somewhere upstream the river made a sound like a throat clearing.

Then Brandt stepped forward and put his hand flat on the zinc lid of the cache box.

"This is evidence," he said. "The whole site gets documented and the contents get secured. Nothing stays here."

I felt the words land in my chest like a dropped anchor. "Secured."

"Standard procedure. An active site in a retrieval operation is catalogued and controlled. If the survivor returns to this cache and finds it stripped of official materials, that compromises—"

"Compromises." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Listen to yourself. There's a human being up this river who has been eating dried fish and trading for rice tins for eleven weeks, and your concern is that leaving a medical kit in a hole in the ground *compromises* something."

"The kit is department property."

"So is the team, presumably. Where's your concern for them?"

Something moved behind Brandt's eyes—not anger, exactly. Recalculation. "My concern," he said, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "is for the operation. Which is why I'm here. Which is why *you're* here, Captain Vane, though I notice you keep forgetting that."

The crew went very still. I was aware of them the way you're aware of weather—Pip halfway up the rigging again, frozen; Rook by the treeline with his hand resting easy near his belt knife; Marisol beside me, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman deciding whether a man needed feeding or burying.

"Let me tell you what I see," I said, and kept my voice level, because the first one to shout loses. "I see a cache that's been maintained by someone alive. I see tracks of one careful person moving upstream. I see a man taking flares and leaving fish, which is what you do when you expect to come back to a place and want it to still be worth coming back to. That person needs this cache. Every tin, every match, every last tablet in that medical kit. And you want to haul it onto my boat and lock it away so you can file it in triplicate."

"Evidence establishes the timeline of events—"

"The timeline of events," I said, "is that your department sent six people up this river and then waited eleven weeks to send anyone after them. Eleven weeks, Brandt. In monsoon season. Either your department is incompetent, which I doubt, because you personally seem competent as a blade—or your department wasn't in a hurry. And a rescue that isn't in a hurry isn't a rescue."

The rain picked up. Water ran off the brim of his hat and he didn't move to wipe it away.

"You're speculating," he said.

"I'm a river captain. Speculation is how I stay alive. I read the water before it reads me." I stepped closer, close enough to see the pencil shavings caught in the seam of his coat pocket. "So here's what happens. The cache stays. We take nothing. We mark our own visit so the survivor knows friends passed through—we leave salt pork and quinine from *our* stores, not yours, so they know it wasn't the department being generous. And you write down in that little book of yours that Captain Vane refused a direct instruction, so your masters know exactly who to blame."

"That's insubordination."

"That's Tuesday," said Pip, from the rigging, and even Brandt's mouth twitched.

For a long moment he just looked at me, rain running down his face, and I got the strange sense that he was weighing me on some scale I couldn't see, entering figures into columns I'd never be allowed to read. Then he took out his ledger, wrote—I assumed my name and crimes—and said, "The cache stays. Note that I objected."

"Noted," I said. "Loudly."

* * *

We left the cache richer than we'd found it: a full tin of salt pork, a bottle of quinine pills, and, at Marisol's insistence, a twist of paper containing sugar and dried chilies, because, as she said, "Whoever's up there hasn't tasted anything but fear in eleven weeks, and fear tastes better with chilies."

Pip carved a marker—a flat board with our boat's sign burned into it, the little crooked compass Odessa had painted on the hull the day we bought her—and wedged it upright in the cairn. "So they know it's not a trap," she explained. "Government caches all look the same. Ours looks like somebody's mum packed it."

By the time we cast off, the rain had settled in for the afternoon, and the river had changed its voice. Below Gravel's End it had murmured. Now it spoke in longer sentences, with a gray-green muscle underneath that I could feel through the deck plates and up through my boots. The escarpment filled the northern sky now, close enough that I could pick out individual ravines, the white threads of falls running full with early rain.

Odessa had the engine room hatch open and was talking to her machinery in low tones. I leaned on the rail beside Brandt, because apparently I was a glutton for punishment.

"You wrote something down when I asked about my father," I said. "Back below Gravel's End. What was it?"

He didn't pretend not to know what I meant. "Your service record has gaps in it, Captain Vane. Eleven years, south of Gravel's End, no registered runs above the gorge. Men with gaps are either hiding something or hiding from something."

"And which do you think I am?"

"I think," he said, "that you argued harder for that cache than the situation strictly required. People argue hardest for the versions of themselves they're afraid of becoming." He clicked his pencil twice, a nervous habit I was beginning to recognize as the only part of him that ever moved quickly. "Someone left you in a place like that once. Alone, with a cache, waiting for a rescue that wasn't in a hurry."

The river said nothing. Neither did I. But my hands on the wheel had gone cold, and Brandt saw it, because Brandt saw everything, and he wrote something else down, and I understood with perfect clarity that Halloran hadn't sent me a surveyor.

He'd sent me a reader.

That night, anchored in the lee of a bend with the rain drumming the cabin roof, I got out the chart case and studied the upper river by lamplight, and it was there I found it—the thing that had been itching at me since the cache. The survey team's authorized route, per the copy of their expedition orders Halloran had given me, ended at the confluence of the Sere tributary. But the cache we'd found sat a full day's run above that confluence, on a branch the orders said the team had never surveyed.

Somebody had redrawn the route. Somebody had known where those six people were actually going. And the same somebody had then spent eleven weeks pretending not to know.

I closed the chart case and sat in the dark listening to the rain, and thought about a single set of boot prints walking upstream, checking its backtrail twice, leaving fish in a zinc box for a rescue that wasn't coming.

Hold on, I thought, to whoever you are. Because I'm starting to suspect neither of us was ever meant to be found.
