## 5. The Escarpment's Throat

My sister says I count things when I'm scared. Pip counts out loud—she hums, she chatters, she narrates the world like a market crier—but I go quiet and I count. Rivets in the deck plating. Seconds between lightning and thunder. The number of ways a man can stand on a boat without belonging to it.

I was counting Brandt's ways the morning we entered the gorge.

There were eleven that day. Standing at the rail with his notebook angled away from the wheelhouse. Standing at the bow with his notebook open flat against the wind. Leaning over the side with one hand on the cable and the other writing something you could not read from any angle I tried. He had a way of standing like furniture that someone had arranged in a room where no one lived. Everything correct. Nothing warm.

The escarpment did not arrive so much as it closed on us.

For three days above the last settlement the river had been wide and brown and lazy, braiding itself through floodplain like it had nowhere better to be, and then the hills started leaning in from both sides, and by midmorning the cliffs were walls—black stone streaked with white birdlime, so tall that when you looked up your hat came off—and the river went from a hundred meters across to forty to twenty-five, and Odessa, who does not waste words on anything that isn't an engine, said from below: "That's the throat."

She was right. It looked exactly like a throat. You could see why the old pilots named it that—the water funneled down into it with a sound like swallowing, a long deep gulp that you felt in the hull before you heard it, and past the first bend there was no seeing what came next because the cliff turned the channel like a hinge.

Felix stood at the wheel with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back and the particular stillness she gets when she's about to do something dangerous on purpose. She'd been quiet since the cache. Not angry-quiet. Weighing-quiet. She'd argued with Brandt about those opened supply crates until the argument ran out of air, and she'd lost it in the way you lose arguments with men who carry government paper—they don't win, they just make continuing feel illegal—and since then she'd been doing arithmetic behind her eyes that none of us were allowed to see.

"Rook," she said now. "Bow line. Pip, stern. Marisol, get everything lashed that isn't already. Odessa—" a pause, "—she's going to want more than she has."

"She always wants more than she has," Odessa called up through the engine hatch. "That's why she runs."

"Brandt." Felix didn't look at him. "Hold onto something or get below."

"I'll manage," Brandt said, and found his twelfth way of standing: braced in the corner of the cockpit, notebook shut for once, both hands free, watching the water the way a man watches a door he expects someone to come through.

* * *

I should explain what the throat does to a boat, because I've thought about it every night since, and putting it in order helps.

A river in floodplain is a crowd. A river in a gorge is a mob. Same water, same volume, but you've taken away its room and given it a deadline, and it stops being a thing that flows and becomes a thing that shoves. Our boat—*Kestrel*, sixty feet of riveted stubbornness that my father helped weld before he drank himself off the crew lists—drew two meters loaded, and the throat at this level gave us four. That sounds like room. It is not room. Four meters means the water underneath you is moving faster than the water beside you, which means the surface lies. There are holes in a flooded gorge where the river simply isn't, places where the current dives and drags a column of air down with it, and if your bow goes into one, the river will take the front of your boat and hold it under like a hand on the back of a neck.

Felix knew where they all were. That was the thing I kept coming back to, watching her. She'd never run the throat at this height—nobody had, the gauge upstream was reading a meter and a half over the flood line, the highest mark on the post was underwater—but she knew where the holes *would* be, because she knew the rock underneath, and she'd learned the rock from a man who died learning it.

"Hard left," she said, conversational, like asking Pip to pass the bread.

Pip threw the stern line across herself and hauled. I took the bow line and leaned into it and felt the boat answer—not turn, exactly; nothing that heavy turns, it *consents*—and the cliff face slid past close enough that I could have touched it, close enough that the spray coming off the wall hit us sideways, cold as well water.

Then the second bend, and the second bend was where the river stopped pretending.

* * *

Here is what I remember, in the order my memory filed it, which is not the order it happened:

The roar getting louder in a way that wasn't gradual but stepped, like stairs.

Felix saying, very quietly, "Odessa, everything."

Odessa saying, "Everything," back, which meant full reverse on one screw and full ahead on the other, which meant she was going to torture her engine and she'd already made peace with it.

The hole.

I saw it maybe a second before Felix did, or maybe she saw it first and I only caught up—a smooth place in all that violence, a patch of water lying flat and dark while everything around it stood up in ridges, and I understood in my stomach, before my head got there, that flat water in a gorge is the most frightening thing a river can show you. Flat water means empty space underneath. Flat water means the river has gone somewhere and would like you to come along.

"Port," Felix said, and then, louder, with the first and only crack in her voice I have ever heard, "*PORT*—"

We ported. Or we mostly ported. The bow cleared the hole's edge by what I would later measure, obsessively, against the length of the boat, as about one meter. Maybe less. The stern did not clear it. The stern went in.

I have been on boats my whole life and I had never felt a boat *fall*. Boats sink, boats roll, boats slam. This was different. The whole stern dropped—there was a moment, half a heartbeat, where the propellers were turning in air, and Odessa made a sound I had never heard her make, a short flat syllable that I suspect in another life was a prayer in a language she doesn't speak anymore—and then the screws bit water again and the engine screamed and the boat climbed.

It climbed because Odessa made it climb. Later she'd say the engine did it, that she just kept the revs up and let the old girl pull. But I was holding the bow line and watching the wake, and what I saw was sixty feet of steel rising out of a hole in the river like something deciding not to drown, and whatever decided that, it wasn't the engine alone.

We came out of the throat nine minutes after we went in. Nine minutes. I know because I counted, because counting is what I do, and afterward Pip grabbed me around the shoulders and shook me and laughed the high unhinged laugh she gets, and I realized I was laughing too, the same laugh, our laugh, the twin laugh, and Marisol had both arms around Odessa on the engine hatch and was saying something in her kitchen-Spanish that was either thanks or scolding and possibly both, and Felix stood at the wheel with her knuckles white and her face gray and said, in a completely normal voice:

"Somebody put the kettle on."

That's how I knew it had been bad. Felix asks for tea when it's bad. The rest of the time she drinks the black sludge from the pot that's been on the burner since Tuesday, but when it's bad she wants the kettle, and Marisol was already moving to make it, and nobody said anything about the hole, and nobody needed to.

* * *

Brandt helped.

I want to be fair about this, because fairness matters to me even when suspicion doesn't leave room for much of it. When the stern dropped, Brandt moved. He didn't freeze, didn't grab the rail like cargo. He crossed the tilting deck in three steps, took the stern line out of Pip's hands when she lost her footing—she'll say she didn't lose her footing, she slipped on the hatch coaming, which is the same thing—and hauled with a technique that was textbook and effective and completely without panic. He held that line through the worst of it with his boots skidding on wet steel and his face doing absolutely nothing, and when we leveled out he coiled the line properly, set it on the pin, and picked up his notebook.

And that was the moment. That was the exact moment, and I want to get it right.

We were still shaking. Pip was still laughing wrong. And Brandt stood at the rail, opened the notebook to a fresh page, and wrote. And I was close enough—I was right there, coiling my own line two meters from him—to see what he wrote, or most of it, because his handwriting is the neat mechanical hand of a man trained to make records other people can read.

He wrote a time. He wrote a number that I took for a speed. And then he wrote, in letters small enough that I had to fill in the gaps: *gauge est. +1.6m over flood datum. constriction width approx. 22m at bend two. drawdown feature, depth unknown.*

Drawdown feature. Depth unknown.

He hadn't written *near-capsize*. He hadn't written *crew nearly lost*. He hadn't written a single word about six people on a boat that almost went under, because we weren't data, and the hole almost eating us wasn't data, and the only thing in that gorge worth ink to him was the shape and size and behavior of the water itself.

You could argue it either way. Pip argued it the generous way that night, later, quietly, when Brandt was asleep on deck: maybe that's just what surveyors do, maybe recording is how he processes, maybe some men write numbers when other men pray. And maybe. But here's what I keep snagging on, the burr in the wool of it: Halloran sent a rescue team upriver to find missing men, and the man he chose to send takes measurements of the river like a man who expects the river to matter more than the men. Rescue parties need trackers and medics and strong backs. They don't, as a rule, need hydrology.

Unless you're not rescuing. Unless you're charting.

* * *

We tied up that evening in a fold of the canyon where the current slackened against an inside bank, a slot of green water under a shelf of rock that some previous pilot had clearly used before—old ring bolts in the stone, rusted soft, older than Felix probably. She rigged lines to two of them without hesitation, like greeting old friends, and I wondered how many times she'd sat in this exact eddy with her father, and whether that was a comfort to her or a wound, and decided it was both, because on this boat everything worth having is both.

Marisol fed us. That's her answer to everything and it's the correct one. Rice and the last of the smoked fish and a sauce she makes from dried chilies that could strip paint, and we ate on deck because the cabin smelled of bilge after the day we'd had, and the light came down the canyon in a single gold bar that lasted about twenty minutes and made even the cliffs look kind.

Pip told the story of the hole twice, improving it each time, until in the second version the stern went fully under and Odessa personally wrestled the boat out by the propeller shafts. Odessa let her. Odessa ate with her eyes half-closed and the tiny smile she denies having, and I understood, watching them, that fear had gone through all of us that morning like a current through wire, and what it had lit up was this: each other. That's the alchemy of a near-drowning. It burns off everything except who you'd want in the water with you.

I looked down the table at our crew and counted, because counting is what I do. Felix, who signed papers she hated to keep us afloat and hasn't once made us feel like payment. Odessa, who talks to engines more gently than she talks to people and would die for a machine that carries us. Pip, who is afraid of everything and does everything anyway, which is the only definition of brave I trust. Marisol, who lied smoothly to a customs officer in chapter one of this voyage and would lie smoother for any of us. Five people who owe each other nothing on paper and everything in fact.

And at the end of the table, apart, Brandt, eating carefully, listening to everything, offering nothing.

He caught me looking. He has a way of catching you that doesn't apologize for it. "Your captain knows this water," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Her father's water," I said. "She grew up on it."

"And the sandbars? The shoals?" He turned a page of his notebook, though he wasn't writing in it. "Does she know them all?"

Something in the question itched. It was the wrong shape for curiosity. Curiosity asks *how does she do it*. This asked *what does she have that I need*, and I heard it the way you hear a false note in a song you know.

"Why?" I said. "Looking for survivors, or looking for channels?"

The table went quiet in that way tables do when everyone hears a thing at once. Brandt looked at me for a long moment, and his face did its furniture impression, and then he said, mildly, "Both are useful," and went back to his food, and the conversation moved on around the spot where the question had landed, the way water moves around a rock.

But later, when the dishes were done and the first rain of the evening was ticking on the canopy, I took the middle watch, and I passed the cockpit, and Brandt was there with a lantern shielded low, writing. I didn't stop. I didn't look. I'd seen enough of his pages for one day.

What I did instead was go forward and stand my watch at the bow, facing upriver, toward the escarpment, which was invisible now in the dark and the drizzle but present the way a wall is present in a cell. Somewhere up there, if Halloran's story was true, starving men were waiting to be found. Somewhere up there, if my gut was true, something else was waiting to be found, and Brandt's notebook was going to be its first historian.

The rain thickened. The river talked to itself in the dark, low and continuous, a sound like a crowd before it decides.

And far downstream—so far I might have imagined it, a flicker at the edge of the canyon mouth, there and gone between rain gusts—a light. White, steady, wrong for fishing, wrong for anything honest that would be running this water at night in this season.

I watched it until the rain took it, and then I went and woke Felix, because some counts you don't keep to yourself.
