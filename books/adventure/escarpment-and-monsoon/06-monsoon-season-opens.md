## 6. Monsoon Season Opens

The rain came three weeks early, and it came like it had a grudge.

I was below decks when the first of it hit—flat on my back under the engine housing with a wrench in my teeth, chasing a rattle in the port cylinder that had been nagging me since the last settlement. The sound that came through the hull wasn't rain. Rain patters. This *hammered*, like the sky had picked up the boat by the keel and decided to shake out everything loose. Which, knowing my engine room, was most of it.

"Odessa!" Pip's voice down the companionway, pitched high with the particular excitement Pip reserves for weather that might kill us. "You should see this!"

"I'm looking at a fuel line," I called back.

"You should see this *anyway*."

I should explain something about myself: I do not love machines because they're beautiful. I love them because they are honest. A machine tells you exactly what's wrong with it if you learn its language, and it never lies to make you feel better, and it never asks you to do something stupid for reasons it won't explain. People could stand to take notes. I'd spent eleven years fixing engines on boats run by people who lied about cargo, fares, destinations, and occasionally their own names, and I'd learned to trust the machinery more than the manifest.

Felix Vane was the least dishonest captain I'd worked for, which is a low bar cleared at a jog rather than a leap, but she'd pulled me off a salvage crew where the foreman skimmed wages and blamed the pumps, so I owed her an engine that never quit. That was the arrangement. She got reliability; I got paid enough to send money to my sister in the delta towns; nobody asked anybody questions about their pasts. It was a good system. I recommend it.

So when the captain herself came down the ladder two minutes later, soaked through, and said, "Odessa, how much can she take?"—I knew the honest answer mattered more than the comfortable one.

"She can take whatever the river gives," I said, "if the river gives it slow."

"And if it doesn't?"

I wiped my hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration. "Then we find out what she's made of. Same as us."

* * *

Here is what the monsoon does to a river like ours, for those who've only known polite water. The river doesn't rise. It *arrives*. All those tributaries up in the escarpment country—the ones the maps draw as thin blue threads—turn into ropes hauling brown water downhill all at once, and the main channel swells from a mile wide to a churning inland sea that has opinions about where the banks used to be. Currents you've read your whole life stop existing. Sandbars migrate. Whirlpools open up in places that were calm last Tuesday, spin for a while like they're thinking it over, and close again.

And the boat rides all of it, or tries to.

By midday of the second day of rain, I had both hands full of wheel-adjacent machinery and no time for philosophy. The intake screens were clogging with silt every twenty minutes—I'd rigged a backflush lever so Rook could clear them without shutting down, and Rook, bless him, pumped that lever like his life depended on it, because it did. The bilge pumps ran near-continuous. Water found ways into the engine room that violated several laws I considered fundamental, and I lay on my back in six inches of warm brown slop re-sealing a gasket while the whole world outside tried to come indoors.

Above me, feet on deck plates: Felix at the helm, calling depths to Marisol, who stood at the bow reading the water the way other people read books—fast, and out loud, and mostly right. "Green water left! Log, big one, starboard—*big*—" And the thud when a tree the size of a chapel came down the current past us close enough to touch, rolling end over end like it was late for something.

We were running upstream against a river running down. Think about that arithmetic. Every hour we made good distance, we burned coal and parts and nerve to gain it, and the river took back whatever it liked whenever it liked. Twice we tied off to trees that I genuinely hope were still rooted, because if they hadn't been, we'd have spent the night learning what the gorge looked like from underneath.

That was the other thing. We'd reached the country where the escarpment started making itself felt—not the cliffs yet, but their foothills, ridges crowding the river from both sides, leaving the channel narrow and fast and mean. The water that came down through there didn't spread out. It concentrated. You could hear the difference in the engine's load, feel it in the deck's shudder. The boat was working as hard as I was, and neither of us was enjoying ourselves.

Somewhere in the middle of the third day—it's hard to keep track when the sun quits on you—Brandt came down into my engine room.

He didn't belong there. That was the first thing. Engine rooms are like kitchens and cockpits: there's a territory, and visitors announce themselves, and he just appeared, ducking under the low beam with his surveyor's case held dry against his chest, looking around at my domain with the expression of a man auditing it.

"Mind the third step," I said. "It bites."

He stepped over it without looking. Of course he did.

"The captain says we're putting in tonight," he said. "Shelter. There's a cave marked on the charts, high bank, left side, above the flood line."

"There's a lot of things marked on the charts," I said, elbow-deep in the starboard pump. "Half of them are wishes."

"This one's government survey. Should be accurate."

"Mm." I tightened the packing gland a quarter turn. "Government surveys of the upper river tend to be accurate the way a portrait is accurate. Right number of eyes."

He didn't laugh. Brandt struck me as a man who'd been told jokes were a security risk. He watched me work for a moment—I'll say this for him, he watched everything, filed it—and then he said, "How old are these engines?"

"Older than me. Younger than the boat." I sat back on my heels. "Why?"

"Fuel consumption figures. For the log."

"For whose log?"

"The expedition log. Standard practice."

"Standard practice," I repeated, and went back to my pump, because there was nothing else productive to do with my face. He took his figures—he must have gotten them off the plate by the throttle linkage, or off Felix—and he left, and I lay there afterward listening to the engine hammer and thinking about the word *standard*, and how a man who burns manifests and photographs nothing and reports everything could be so fond of that word.

But that wasn't the thing that kept me up that night. The cave was.

* * *

Marisol spotted it first, because Marisol spots everything first—that woman's eyes are calibrated instruments disguised as a cook's squint. High on the left bank, a black mouth in the limestone, well above the flood line, with a shelf of rock in front wide enough to warp onto if the current cooperated.

The current did not cooperate. The current had never cooperated in its life. But Felix brought us in on a back-eddy with a piece of helmsmanship I'd have called showing off if it hadn't been necessary, and we warped her nose-first to the shelf with lines to two iron stanchions somebody had driven into the rock who-knew-when, and we breathed.

The cave went back maybe sixty feet, dry-floored, high-ceilinged. Somebody had used it before. That was obvious before anyone said anything: there were iron rings set in the walls for mooring lines, a stone fire ring swept clean, and—this is what stopped me—a wooden rack built against the far wall, sized for survey equipment cases, with slots cut to fit. Not roughly. Precisely. Slots cut to standard dimensions.

Pip found the markings first. Or rather, Pip found them and yelled for everyone, because Pip yells for everyone about everything, which is exhausting and also why we love them.

On the wall beside the entrance, someone had painted survey marks in a paint that had gone chalky with age: elevation benchmarks, a date code, a station identifier. Old work. Faded. The kind of thing you'd expect—government crews had been poking at this river for fifty years, benchmarking and charting and generally measuring things to death.

But lower down, at knee height, sheltered under a lip of rock where the rain couldn't reach, there was newer paint. Brighter. And next to it, scratched into the stone with a knife or a geologist's pick, a fresh date code.

Rook crouched over it. Rook doesn't talk much, but he reads numbers the way I read engines. "This says four months ago," he said. "Maybe five."

"The official maps show the last survey through here eleven years ago," Felix said quietly. She was standing very still, the way she does when she's doing fast math on something that isn't numbers. "I've got the chart edition in my cabin. Eleven years."

"Then somebody's been updating," Marisol said. She was at the equipment rack, running her thumb along the slots. "And stocking. Look—there's oil stains in these slots. Recent. Something heavy sat here, and not long ago."

"A cache," Pip said. "Like the one upriver."

"Like the one upriver," Felix agreed, and I watched her look at Brandt, and I watched Brandt look at the marks with an expression I couldn't read, and I thought: *here we go.*

Because here's the thing I want to put down plainly, the way I'd write it in an engine log, because logs don't flatter and neither will I: I am not a suspicious woman by nature. Suspicion is inefficient. It burns fuel you need for actual problems. But I had been watching this expedition the way I watch a gauge cluster—all needles noted—and the needles had been drifting into the red for days.

A rescue party does not need fuel consumption figures logged separately. A rescue party does not include a man who burns paperwork. And a government that genuinely lost a survey team in the escarpment country would not have a supply cache restocked four months after the team vanished—unless the government knew, with precision, where that team had gone and where it would need supplying.

You don't stock a cave for lost people. You stock a cave for a route.

I sat on an equipment crate that evening while the rain hammered the cave mouth and the fire threw our shadows huge against the limestone, and I turned it over the way I turn over a stubborn fault—gently, patiently, feeling for the shape of the break. The survey team hadn't wandered up here and gotten lost. They'd been sent. Sent along a route the government had already mapped, already provisioned, already benchmarked within the last few seasons. The official maps said eleven years of silence. The rocks said otherwise. Which meant the official maps weren't outdated.

They were *edited*.

And if you edit maps for a living—if you maintain a fiction about where your people have been—then the question stops being *what happened to the survey team?* and starts being *what did the survey team see that required the map to lie about it?*

I looked across the fire at Felix. She was turning the journal page she'd pocketed at the wrecked camp—I'd seen her do it a dozen times now, always the same small motion, hand to breast pocket, checking it was still there, like a man checking a wound. Whatever was on that page, she'd read it, and she hadn't told us, and I found I didn't blame her. Some knowledge is a load-bearing wall. You don't knock on it until you know what it's holding up.

Odessa, I told myself, you fix engines. Fix what's in front of you. The intake screens needed clearing; the bilge needed pumping; tomorrow we'd push back out into a river that wanted us dead. Those were real problems with real solutions.

But the needle stayed in the red. Because I'd finally understood the thing I'd been circling for days, sitting in that cave with knife-scratched dates on the wall and government iron rings in the stone:

Nobody had ever been searching for that survey team. From the very beginning—from Halloran's clean deal on the dock to Brandt's tidy figures to the restocked caches strung up the river like breadcrumbs—nobody had been looking for the lost.

They'd been following them. Step for step, cache for cache, along a route the government had drawn years ago and then erased from every map a citizen was allowed to buy.

The team hadn't gotten lost. They'd been *sent*—and everything we'd been told since was a cover story wearing a rescue's clothes.

I fed the fire another stick and said nothing, because Odessa keeps her findings in the log, not on the deck, and some conclusions you hold until you know who else has reached them. Across the flames, Rook caught my eye. Just for a second. Then he glanced, almost imperceptibly, at the knee-high paint marks by the entrance, and back at me.

So he'd done the arithmetic too.

And that was when, out in the rain behind us, far down the flooded channel, a signal flare went up—white, then red—burning through the monsoon dark from somewhere downstream, where nothing but empty water was supposed to be.
