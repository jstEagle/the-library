## 7. What the Survey Found

The river told us before we saw it.

I'd been reading water since I was six years old, back when my father still believed I'd grow up to be a respectable woman with dry shoes, and the water above the escarpment's throat was telling me something had died up there. Not dramatically. Rivers don't do drama; they do bookkeeping. This one had stopped carrying its usual freight of silt and broken branches from the upper valleys, as if something upstream had tidied up after itself. Clean water in monsoon country is like a swept floor in a house of thieves. It means somebody wanted no evidence.

"Current's wrong," I said to Odessa at midmorning, and she grunted the way she does when she agrees but resents agreeing before her second coffee.

The survey camp sat on a gravel bench above a bend, where any sensible expedition would have stopped—water access, high ground, sightlines downriver for a day's travel in both directions. Whoever chose it had known what they were doing. That was the first thing that bothered me. The second thing was the flagpole, standing straight, flying nothing.

We tied off at a dead snag fifty meters out rather than trusting the bank, which turned out to be instinct worth its weight in brass. Brandt was over the side before I finished the order, wading ashore with his boots on and his notebook already out, because of course he was. The man treated every landing like an audit.

"Felix," Marisol said quietly, beside me at the rail. She'd come up without a sound, the way she always did. "Nobody's standing watch."

"I noticed."

"A camp this size, this long abandoned—somebody should've rigged a warning line at least. Traps. Something." Her eyes moved along the treeline with the flat patience she gave to everything except people. "This wasn't left. This was *arranged*."

She was right, and I hated her for it, the way you hate anyone who says the thing you were already thinking out loud.

We went up in pairs—Marisol and me in front, Rook and Pip behind with the crowbar and the lantern, Odessa minding the boat with instructions to cast off if anything came down the bank faster than walking speed. She didn't argue, which told me how the last week had gone for all of us. Brandt walked alone, ten paces to our right, close enough to see everything, far enough to own nothing we said.

The camp had been eight tents once. You could read the layout from the stakes: command tent center, mess to the east, instrument stores under a fly that had been guyed properly against weather by someone who expected to be here through the season. Now the fly lay collapsed over wreckage like a sheet pulled over a face.

And the wreckage was the thing. I've seen camps hit by floods, by animals, by panic at three in the morning when men run and leave their boots because boots take time. Panic is messy in a particular way—it scatters. This hadn't scattered. Every piece of equipment in that store tent had been destroyed *in place*. The theodolite hadn't been dropped or stolen; it had been taken apart with tools and then beaten with a rock, each lens cracked individually, the glass ground into the mud so no fragment bigger than a thumbnail survived. The drafting table had been burned down to charcoal and the charcoal stamped flat. Even the paper—the paper had been burned in a fire pit and the ashes raked, twice, by the look of them.

"You don't burn your own maps unless the maps are the problem," Rook said behind me. He said it lightly, the way he says everything, but his knuckles were white around the crowbar.

"Somebody was very thorough," Brandt said. He was crouched at the fire pit, sifting ash through his fingers with a small brass sieve I hadn't seen him produce. He didn't look up. "Thoroughness takes hours. Hours means calm."

Hours means orders, I thought, and didn't say it.

Pip found the grave.

They called out from the treeline in a voice pitched wrong—not Pip's usual bright alarm, which is mostly theater, but the flat tone they get when the world has stopped being funny. We gathered where they stood. A rectangle of stones, laid neat, with a survey stake driven at the head instead of a cross. On the stake, in drafting-perfect capitals:

**R. OKONKWO — SURVEYOR — HE DID THE ARITHMETIC RIGHT**

No dates. No cause. Just the epitaph, which was somehow worse than either, because it sounded like a eulogy written by people who knew they might not get to write another one.

"He did the arithmetic right," Marisol repeated softly. "That's not grief talking. That's a message."

"It's a headstone," Pip said. "It's just a headstone, Mari, don't make it weird."

But it was weird, and everyone standing in that clearing knew it. Six graves would have meant disaster. One grave meant selection—one man dead of something ordinary, buried with care, while whatever happened to the others happened somewhere else entirely. Or happened deliberately. I stood looking at that stake and felt the same cold I'd felt at fifteen, hiding in a root cellar while men with lanterns called my name in voices full of false kindness. The cold that says: *you are inside the trap now, and the trap has been here longer than you.*

Brandt photographed the grave. Of course he did. He photographed everything—the store tent, the ashes, the arrangement of the stones—and his pen never stopped moving in that little book, and I watched him write and could not have told you a single word he put down.

* * *

It took us most of the afternoon to understand what we were looking at, and we only understood it because Marisol refused to stop looking.

She worked the perimeter the way she works anything—slowly, in widening circles, reading the ground like other people read tide tables. By late light she called us to the mess tent, or what remained of it, and showed us the floor.

"Count the bedrolls," she said.

There were seven frames, seven groundsheets, seven sets of kit laid out with military precision. Seven.

"The manifest Halloran gave us lists nine," Rook said. "Nine names. I memorized them on the second night, it helps me sleep, don't ask."

"Two beds carried out," Marisol said. "Or two people walked away and took their bedding. Either way—" she tapped one of the empty frames, "—this isn't a camp that got wiped out. This is a camp that got *reduced*. Deliberately. Somebody decided who stayed and who went."

"And the ones who stayed," Pip said slowly, "burned all the maps first."

Nobody answered that. The rain started while we stood there, soft at first, the monsoon finding its rhythm again after a week of sulking, drumming on the collapsed fly with a sound like fingers tapping on a coffin lid. Odessa came up from the boat soaked to the collar and reported that the engine was holding, the river was rising four inches an hour, and she didn't like the color of the sky upstream. She looked at the camp for a long moment and then at me.

"We're not sleeping here," she said. Not a question.

"No," I agreed. "We're not."

But we had maybe two hours of usable light, and I spent them doing the thing I'd been avoiding since we landed: going through the command tent properly, alone, while the crew stripped anything useful from the stores. The command tent had been searched before us—I could tell by the way things were stacked, official-style, corners aligned—but the searchers had missed what they missed because they'd been looking for documents, and the person who hid this hadn't hidden a document. They'd hidden a book.

Under the floorboard beneath the commander's cot frame, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed string, I found a field journal. Most of it was water-ruled and useless—survey notes, depth soundings, supply tallies, the boring gospel of competent men doing their work. But near the back, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. Written by someone who knew the value of what they were writing and the price of being caught writing it.

I read it twice, standing in the ruined tent with the rain coming down, and by the second reading my hands weren't steady anymore.

The survey team hadn't been mapping flood defenses. That was the cover story, and it was even partly true—they'd done the flood work, diligently, the arithmetic right, all of it. But Okonkwo, the man under the stones, had found something else while running mineral samples for the road survey. A seam. Iron and something rarer threaded through the escarpment's spine, close enough to the surface to extract cheaply, sitting astride the only reliable route between the lowland ports and the interior plateau.

The journal put it plainly, in that tight hurried hand: *Whoever controls this seam controls the cost of everything that moves on this river for the next hundred years. If it becomes public, every shipping concern, every toll agreement, every land title from the delta to the falls gets renegotiated overnight. There are men whose entire power rests on the current arrangements. They will not let that happen peacefully.*

And below that, dated three days before the last entry in the whole journal: *H. has instructed us to log the seam samples as trace deposits. I have complied. I no longer believe we will be extracted. I believe we were sent to confirm and then to disappear. R. agrees. He did the arithmetic right too.*

H. Halloran. I stood there with the page trembling slightly in my fingers and thought about a well-dressed man on a dock offering to erase my father's debts with a smile that never reached his eyes, and I understood—at last, completely, in the marrow—what kind of job I'd signed onto.

Not a rescue. A retrieval. And if retrieval failed, a confirmation that nothing remained to retrieve.

I tore the page free. I'm not proud of the tearing—a journal is evidence, and evidence belongs whole—but I wasn't thinking like an investigator. I was thinking like a woman who has spent eleven years learning exactly how much paper can destroy a life, and who had just watched a government do to nine surveyors what it had once tried to do to her. I folded the page small and put it inside my shirt, against my skin, where I used to keep the forged papers that kept me alive through two provinces and one very bad winter.

Then I stood in the wrecked command tent of men who had done nothing worse than measure the earth honestly, and I let myself feel it: dread, pure and architectural, load-bearing dread. Because now I knew why no one was meant to come home. And I knew that the man sleeping ten feet from my cabin door, with his little brass sieve and his little black book, was not here to bring anyone back.

* * *

I burned nothing and destroyed nothing else. Let the camp stand as it was; disturbing more of it would only tell whoever came later exactly how much we'd learned. I reburied the journal minus its one torn page, replaced the board, and walked back down to the boat through the rain with my face arranged into the mild blankness that has kept me alive longer than any gun has.

Supper was quiet. Marisol made rice with the dried chilies she guards like dragon hoard, and nobody talked much, and the rain filled the silence the way rain does, generously and without meaning to. Brandt ate with us, as he always did, and thanked Marisol, as he always did, and asked Rook about the gorge ahead with what sounded like genuine professional interest, and I watched him across the fire and thought: *he photographed a grave today and wrote for ten minutes straight and I have no idea what he wrote.* And underneath that thought, unwelcome and persistent, was another one: *he also waded into floodwater in August to pull a drowning boy off a snag, and he didn't hesitate, and I don't know what to do with a man who contains both of those things.*

Later, when the twins had rolled into their blankets and Odessa had gone below to fight her eternal war with the fuel lines, Marisol appeared at my elbow on the aft deck, silent as ever, and stood looking at the dark water with me.

"You found something up there," she said. Not accusing. Just placing it on the table between us, the way she places food.

"What makes you say that?"

"Because you came down that hill looking like my grandmother looked the day the soldiers came through our valley. And because you've been touching your left side every twenty minutes since, like a woman checking a wound." She turned her head and looked at me, and her eyes in the firelight were patient and terrible. "I'm not asking what it is, Felix. I'm asking whether it's the kind of thing that gets us killed, or the kind of thing that gets *them* killed. There's a difference, and I plan for differently."

I thought about lying to her. I want that on the record: I considered it, seriously, for a good five seconds, because lying to Marisol is easy and telling her the truth felt like handing her a lit fuse. But she'd lied to no one on this boat, and she'd tracked a killer's trail through two provinces for a family that wasn't hers, and some debts you pay in currency and some you pay in trust.

"It's the kind of thing that got nine government surveyors erased," I said. "And the kind of thing that means the man who hired us was never planning to rescue anybody."

Marisol absorbed that the way stone absorbs rain—no visible change, but something shifting far down inside. Then she nodded once, slowly.

"So we're past the point where finishing the job is safe," she said.

"We were past that point on the dock."

"Mm." She looked back at the river. "Then tomorrow I start tracking properly. Because somebody walked out of that camp with their bedroll, Felix, and if one of those nine is alive up this river, then the question isn't whether Halloran sent us to find them." Her voice went very quiet. "It's whether we find them first."

Upstream, beyond the dark shoulder of the escarpment, lightning flickered without sound, once, twice—like a signal nobody had asked for. The river rose against the hull, patient and rising, carrying its clean water down from a valley that had been swept clean on purpose.

I touched the page against my ribs and went below to lie awake listening to the rain, doing my own arithmetic, and getting the same answer every time: whatever we did next, we were already counted among the missing.
