## 8. Tracks Against the Current

I learned to track before I learned to read, which my mother said explained a great deal about me. She was a goat woman from the high terraces above San Ferro, and she believed that everything worth knowing could be found in what things left behind—hoofprints, broken stems, the particular way a frightened animal moves through brush. People, she said, were just animals with better excuses.

So when I say I knew something was wrong with the riverbank that morning, you can believe it came from the goats.

We'd tied up below a shelf of red stone where the current slackened enough to make anchoring honest work instead of prayer. Odessa had the engine torn open again—the poor woman treated that engine like a sick child, and the child kept getting sicker—and Felix had sent Pip and Rook up the cut to look for dry wood, because wet wood makes Marisol a sad cook, and nobody wants a sad cook on a boat this small. That left me washing greens in the shallows, which is where I first saw it.

A stone, turned over. Fresh side up, dark with damp, sitting in a stretch of gravel where every other stone wore its pale face to the sky like good honest rocks should.

Somebody had walked here. Recently. And they hadn't wanted to be seen doing it.

* * *

I didn't say anything right away. This is a habit of mine that drives Felix half-mad, but there's a reason for it: a trail is a story, and if you go shouting the first sentence, you'll never hear how it ends. So I finished my greens. I dried my hands. I wandered upstream along the bank the way a bored cook wanders, picking at plants, and let my eyes do the walking while my feet pretended to have nowhere to be.

The story started small. A scuffed mark on a root, too high for an animal, too deliberate for a stumble. A fern bent back and recovering—three days, maybe four, since someone had pushed through. Then, in the soft mud of a seep, the thing that stopped my breath for a moment:

A boot print. Human. Left foot heavier than the right, the way a person walks when one leg has been hurt and healed crooked. The print was shallow but crisp, made by someone light—not a big man's boot—and the edges hadn't yet begun to crumble.

Days old. Not weeks. Not months.

The survey team had vanished, by Halloran's arithmetic, better than two months ago. Every official word that had come downriver spoke of them in the past tense, the way you speak of drowned men. And yet here was somebody walking upstream along this bank within the last handful of days, alone, favoring a bad leg, moving careful as a thief in church.

Alone. That was the part that sat strange in my chest. A party leaves tracks like spilled grain—a wide scatter, easy reading even for a child. This was a single thread pulled through the wilderness, thin and taut and deliberately quiet. Whoever it was had practiced being unseen. Whoever it was was still practicing.

I crouched at the seep and looked at the print a long time. Then I did something I'm not proud of and not ashamed of either: I swept pine needles across it with the side of my hand until it was only mud again.

* * *

Felix was on the stern deck going over charts when I came back, and I told her the way I tell her everything important—in three words or fewer, with my eyes doing the rest. *Someone's alive. Upstream. Alone.*

She went very still, the way she does, the way a heron goes still before it strikes or flees, and with Felix it's always been hard to know which. But her eyes came up to mine and I saw the thing underneath all her captain's calm: hope, raw and unarmored, the most dangerous cargo we carried.

"You're certain."

"I'm certain someone's alive. Whether it's your survey team—" I shrugged. "One set of prints. One bad leg. They're moving up, not down. Toward the escarpment, not away from it."

"Nobody moves toward the escarpment," Felix said quietly. "Not in monsoon season. Not unless they're looking for something or hiding from something."

"Or both," I said, and she looked at me, because that was exactly the kind of arithmetic she'd been doing since the cache, since the gorge, since Brandt started measuring water levels like a man pricing a river instead of searching it.

"We tell Brandt," she said finally, and it wasn't a question, because Felix Vane keeps her bargains even when the bargains are traps. "He's part of this run. He gets the truth."

"He gets the truth," I agreed. "The whole truth is another matter."

She almost smiled. Almost. It's a thing about Felix—you learn to read the weather around her smiles rather than the smiles themselves.

* * *

Brandt took the news standing at the rail, his notebook already out, his pencil already moving. I watched him write and thought, not for the first time, that he wrote the way other men breathe—automatically, constantly, as though a moment unrecorded was a moment lost. There are worse vices. I've cooked for men whose vice was cruelty, and men whose vice was drink, and a man who writes everything down at least pays attention.

"A single survivor," he said. "Moving upstream." His eyes came up, gray and flat as river stones. "Which direction precisely? Show me."

So I showed him. I led him up the bank to the seep, and here is where I want to be very clear about what happened next, because later—when everything came apart and everyone asked questions—I needed to know exactly what I had done and why.

I showed him the trail. All of it. The turned stone, the bent fern, the scuffed root. I showed him everything except the print itself, because I had swept it away, and in its place the mud held nothing but rain-pocks and old deer sign. And then I showed him the direction the trail ran—which was true, upstream—and I let him stand there looking north into the dripping green while I stood behind him doing the fastest lying of my life.

Because the trail didn't simply run upstream. It ran upstream and then, half a mile on where the bank split around a fallen cedar, it climbed—cut sharply east, away from the water, into the high ground where the tributaries braided and the terrain turned cruel. A lone walker with a bad leg would take the low route along the river. It was slower but kinder to a hurt body. Unless the walker knew the high country. Unless the walker was avoiding the river itself, and everyone on it.

That eastern fork I did not show him. I showed him the bank, the waterline, the obvious road any rescue party would search. And when Brandt asked, "Could they reach the escarpment base by following the river?" I said, "If their leg holds, yes. The bank runs clean for miles," which was true of the bank and false of the walker, who had left the bank behind before noon that day.

You'll want to know why. I've asked myself, in the nights since, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it. Part of it was simple: I'm a tracker, and trackers know that a trail is a person. You don't hand a person to someone who writes everything down and reports to boats that follow in the dark. And part of it was the flare.

* * *

It came that evening, after supper, when the rain had settled into its long patient drumming. I was banking the galley fire when I heard the hatch thump of boots on deck and looked out in time to see Brandt at the stern rail with a signal pistol raised toward the southern sky.

The flare went up red and hung there, bleeding light through the rain, and far off—so far I'd have sworn I imagined it except that Rook saw it too—a light answered. Two flashes. White. Then darkness swallowed everything.

Nobody aboard was supposed to know there was anything back there to answer. We'd talked about the shadow boat in whispers, in the galley, with the door shut—Pip had seen it two days past, a low gray shape holding station miles astern, never closer, never farther, like a debt you can't outrun. Felix had gone quiet in the way that frightens me more than shouting, and Odessa had said what we were all thinking: *we're not the rescue party, we're the leash.*

And now Brandt stood at the rail in the rain, telling that distant shape in the only language flares speak: *found tracks. One survivor. Alive.*

I stood in the galley doorway with dishwater cooling on my hands and understood that whatever came up that river after us would come for that lone walker with the bad leg. Not to rescue. You don't signal a warship about a drowning man; you signal a warship about a threat. Everything Halloran's government had done since the dock—the seized papers, the leash on Felix, the surveyor with his endless notebook—had the shape of a hunt wearing a rescue's clothes.

So yes. I lied to Brandt. I'd do it again before the words finished leaving my mouth. My mother taught me tracking, but she taught me something else besides, something she said the goats taught her: *you protect the strays. The herd forgets them; you don't.* Ilsa Rehn—if it was Ilsa Rehn, if the cartographer with the ruined leg was the one threading these hills—was a stray now. Ours. And nobody hands our people to boats that answer flares in the dark.

* * *

Felix found me scrubbing a pot that was already clean. She leaned against the galley frame and watched me for a while, because she always knows, that woman. She knows when the beans need salt and when a silence needs breaking.

"The flare," she said.

"You saw."

"Rook says the light answered. Pip confirms the boat's still back there, holding station." She rubbed her eyes. "Marisol. What did you show him?"

I set the pot down. Here was the fork in my own trail, then—the moment where a lie either grows roots or gets pulled. I looked at Felix Vane, who had taken every one of us strays onto her boat when no other captain would, who owed debts she couldn't pay and paid them anyway in loyalty, and I decided she could carry one more secret without breaking.

"I showed him the riverbank," I said. "The trail runs east into the high country. He thinks it follows the water."

Felix was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the deck above us. Somewhere aft, Odessa swore at her engine in two languages.

"That's a dangerous kindness," she said at last. "If you're wrong about who's up there—"

"I'm not wrong about what's behind us."

"No," she said softly. "Neither am I." She pushed off from the frame and paused at the hatch, her back to me, her voice dropped low enough that the rain nearly took it. "Keep it that way. And Marisol—if we find them first, they're ours. Whatever the government thinks it's retrieving."

"They're ours," I agreed, and meant it the way my mother meant it, the way the goats would have understood: completely, without paperwork, unto the end of the trail.

She went forward. I banked the fire properly this time and stood a moment in the dark galley, listening to the river push us north through the rain, toward the escarpment, toward whoever was limping through the high country believing themselves alone in the world.

They weren't alone anymore. They just didn't know it yet—and neither, I suspected, did the man with the notebook, who had reported a trail that led nowhere, and would soon enough begin to wonder why the river gave him nothing but empty banks.

By morning, I figured, he'd start asking questions.

By morning, I'd better have better answers.
