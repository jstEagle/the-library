## 9. The Boat That Follows

I want it on the record that I saw it first.

Not Rook with his sharp eyes and his sharper mouth. Not Marisol, who can read a mudflat like a confession. Me—Pip Vane-adjacent, deckhand, youngest of the strays, the one everyone assumes is too busy counting river snails to notice anything. I was on the rain watch at four in the morning because I'd lost the coin toss to Odessa for the third time running, which I'm fairly sure means she's cheating, and I saw the light through the squall off our stern quarter: a single white blink, three seconds, gone.

Then again. Three seconds. Gone.

Nobody runs a signal lamp like that by accident. Three-on, three-off is a relay pattern. It's how boats talk to each other when they don't want to shout.

I stood there in the downpour with water running off my chin and my heart doing something complicated against my ribs, and I did the arithmetic every deckhand learns before they learn knots. We were two days above the last settlement. The river behind us was a brown roaring nothing. And somebody back there was blinking hello.

* * *

Let me back up, because if this account ever gets read by anyone official—and given the way this run has gone, I half expect it to—I want the sequence clean.

We'd left the cave shelter at dawn, or what passes for dawn during monsoon, which is to say the sky went from black to a bruised grey and the rain kept on like it had a personal grudge. The survey markings in that cave had sat in my stomach all night like bad fish. Fresh dates. Fresher than any map Halloran's office had given us claimed anyone had been. Which meant the government knew where its people were going before they vanished, and had told us exactly none of that.

Felix hadn't said much about it either. That's Felix's way. She files things away behind those flat grey eyes and you find out what she concluded about three weeks later, usually when it saves your life. But I've crewed with her long enough to read the small tells. She'd been standing at the wheel longer than she needed to. She'd stopped humming. Felix hums when she thinks a thing is manageable, terrible old river songs her father taught her, off-key on purpose she claims. The silence had been going on two days now.

So: rain watch, four in the morning, me under the canvas dodger with a cup of Marisol's chicory substitute going cold in my hand, and the stern of the *Magpie* streaming silver in the dark, and that light. Blink. Blink. Blink.

I woke Rook first, because Rook wakes quiet and Rook wakes mean, and both are useful qualities at four in the morning. He came up beside me, followed my finger, and watched through one full cycle of the signal.

"Three-on, three-off," he said.

"I know what it is."

"Do you know who it is?"

"No," I said. "But I know where it is. It's holding station. It's not gaining."

Rook was quiet a moment. Rain hammered the canvas. Behind us the light blinked its patient little heartbeat, and ahead of us the river ran black toward the escarpment's throat, and somewhere up that throat was a survivor we were supposed to be rescuing, assuming rescue was the actual word for what we were doing.

"It's not chasing us," Rook said slowly. "It's escorting us."

That was the word. Escorting. Like a prisoner transfer. Like sheep.

* * *

We didn't wake Felix.

I want to explain that decision properly, because it was the biggest thing any of us has ever done, bigger than the crossing later, bigger even than the landing, and it didn't look like anything at all. It looked like five people being very quiet on a boat at four in the morning.

Here's the thing about Felix nobody says out loud: she hasn't made a free choice since the dock. She signed Halloran's paper because the tax man had her boat by the throat, and everything since—the cache argument, the journal page she thinks nobody knows she took, the way she stands at the wheel like a woman steering into weather only she can see—everything has been her trying to keep a promise she never wanted to make. She's carrying this run like a debt. And you can't ask a person carrying a debt whether to turn around, because they'll tell you they can't afford to, and they'll be right, and then you'll all keep walking into whatever's waiting.

So we decided—we four—to make the choice ourselves first. To figure out what we thought, so that if it came to it, we could hand Felix an answer instead of a question. It sounds like mutiny written down. It wasn't. It was the opposite of mutiny. It was the most loyal thing I have ever been part of.

Odessa came up from the engine hole at half past four, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last week. We showed her the light. She watched two cycles, jaw working.

"Steam plant," she said. "Or diesel. Too steady for sail in this chop." She spat over the rail, which for Odessa is a full emotional speech. "They're burning fuel sitting still. That's money. Somebody's paying for us to stay on course."

Marisol arrived last, drawn up from below by some cook's sixth sense, and when she understood what we were looking at, her face did the thing it does—went very still and very kind at once, like a church. Marisol had lied to Brandt about the trail direction two days ago. She'd looked him dead in his cold eyes and sent him pointing up the wrong fork, and none of us had said a word about it since, but we all knew, and knowing it had changed the shape of the air between us. We were past the point where secrets among the four of us cost anything. We were a family with a shared felony.

"So," Marisol said. "Council?"

"Council," Rook said.

* * *

We held it in the galley, which is the only place on the *Magpie* where five people can stand close without the rain finding them, and which smells permanently of Marisol's cooking and Odessa's engine grease, which together smell like home, which mattered more than I can say just then.

Felix slept in her cabin aft. Actually slept—for the first time in days, dead asleep, exhausted past dreaming. Brandt had the bow watch, which suited us fine; Brandt watches forward, always forward, like the past might catch him if he glances astern. Whatever he was looking for, it wasn't behind us. His oversight was the one gift his presence gave.

Rook laid it out flat. "Options. One: we turn back. Two: we go on. Three: we do something clever in between, which historically is where this crew lives."

"Turning back means what?" Odessa asked. "We abandon the survivor? Assuming there is one, and Marisol's tracks weren't ghost stories."

"They weren't ghost stories," Marisol said, in the voice that ends discussions.

"Fine. Turning back means leaving Ilsa Rehn—whoever she is—alone up there with whatever killed her team, plus whatever's in that boat behind us. And it means Felix goes home to Halloran empty-handed, and Halloran takes the *Magpie*, and Felix goes to—" Odessa stopped. None of us finished sentences about Felix's past anymore. We'd all felt the edges of it, the locked room in her history, and we'd all agreed without agreeing never to pick the lock.

"And going on," I said, "means we're being herded toward something. You don't spend that kind of money escorting a rescue. You spend it retrieving. Or erasing."

The rain filled the pause. Through the hull I could feel the river working, that deep muscular shove of floodwater, the escarpment pulling at us like a drain.

"There's a fourth option," Rook said quietly. "We go on—but we stop being predictable. We stop reporting position at the relays. We take the survivor and come out somewhere Halloran isn't watching."

"The lower gorges aren't passable in flood," Odessa said. "Except—"

"Except," I said, and my stomach dropped, because I knew where this went. We all did. "Except the sandbar route. The one Felix runs in her head. The one her father died learning."

Silence. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"She's never shown anyone," Marisol said. "She won't. That channel killed Tomas Vane. She keeps it like a grave."

"Then we don't ask her yet," Rook said. "We ask her when there's no other door. But we decide now, tonight, that if it comes to that door, we walk through it together. All of us. Because here's what I actually think." He looked around the galley, and Rook almost never does that—he's a twin, he looks at Pip, that's his whole orientation system—so when he looked at all of us, I felt it land. "I think Felix would trade herself for this crew tomorrow morning and call it fair pricing. I think she signed Halloran's paper thinking she was the only one at risk. And I think if we put this choice in front of her raw—turn back and lose everything, or go on and maybe die—she'll choose alone, because that's how she's built. So we don't give her a raw choice. We do the work first. We find the survivor, we get the truth, and we build her a road home that doesn't run through Halloran's hands."

"That's protecting her from her own decision," I said.

"Yes," said all three of them, nearly in unison, and Marisol added, "Which is what family is for."

I looked at my hands around the cold cup. I'm the youngest. I catalog snails and small rituals and the exact sound the river makes against the hull at different depths, because noticing things is my job and my armor. And what I noticed right then was that I wasn't scared. Not of the boat behind us, not of the gorge ahead. I was scared the way you're scared at a wedding, or a funeral—all of it mattering too much. These three idiots and their sleeping captain were mine, and I was theirs, and the light blinking patiently in the rain behind us could bring its whole government.

"Vote," Rook said.

Four hands, palm-down on the galley table, stacked like planks. Go on. Together. Protect her.

Above us, through the deck, I heard Felix turn over in her sleep, and the boat held her, and we let her.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and with it, complications, because dawn always brings complications on this river—it's practically contractual.

The first complication was Brandt. He came aft at first light, dry-eyed and immaculate as always, as if the monsoon were a personal discourtesy he refused to acknowledge, and he stood at the rail scanning the water with those instruments of his, and I watched him look directly at the patch of grey where the shadow boat should have been visible in a break between squalls.

He looked at it for four full seconds.

Then he wrote something in his waterproof notebook, and closed it, and said nothing.

Now. Here's what I know about Brandt after two weeks of sharing a boat with him: he reports everything. Water levels, bank erosion, cloud formations, the number of times Rook swears before breakfast. If he'd seen the boat and intended to report it, there'd have been a flare already arcing up through the rain. There was no flare. Which meant either he hadn't seen it—which his eyes don't allow—or he'd seen it and filed it somewhere that wasn't the flare gun.

I didn't know what that meant yet. I wrote it down in my head, in the same ledger where I keep the snail counts and the river sounds, under a heading I'm starting to hate: *Things About This Run That Don't Add Up.*

The second complication was Felix waking up.

She came out mid-morning, hair wet, eyes already at the horizon the way they always are now, and she did her rounds—engine room, galley, lines, charts—and she ended up beside me at the stern rail while I coiled line that didn't need coiling, because my hands needed something to lie to.

"Pip," she said. "You've been looking astern all morning."

"Someone has to. Everyone else watches forward."

"Funny thing about this river," Felix said, mild as milk. "Everything in it comes from behind you eventually."

And I swear on the *Magpie*'s rusted old bell, she knew. Not the council, not the vote—but the shape of it. She looked out at the rain, at the grey wall where a government boat was spending its money keeping us honest, and her jaw set in that particular way, and she said, quietly, mostly to the river:

"Whatever you four decided in my galley last night—I'll be wanting to hear it. When I'm ready. And not before."

Then she went forward to relieve Brandt, shoulders square, humming.

Humming. Terrible, off-key, deliberate. First time in six days.

I stood at the stern rail with my coiled line and watched the rain swallow the place where the light had been, and I thought: hold on, Captain. Hold on just a little further. We're building you a road.

Behind us, faint through the squall, the light blinked twice—quick, irregular, wrong, urgent—and then the rain closed over it like a hand closing over a candle, and it did not come back.

It had stopped holding station.

It was moving.
