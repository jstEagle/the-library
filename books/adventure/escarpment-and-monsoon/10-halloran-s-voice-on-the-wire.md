## 10. Halloran's Voice on the Wire

The relay station at Kettle Bend looked like a church that had lost its faith—a tin-roofed box perched on stilts above the flood line, its antenna mast leaning like a drunk against a lamppost. The operator was a boy of maybe seventeen with a carbuncle of pimples across his chin and the bored eyes of someone who had transmitted a thousand messages and cared about none of them.

"Wire's down to one channel in this weather," he said, chewing something that might once have been tobacco. "You want to send or receive?"

"Receive," I said. "There should be a message waiting. Vane."

He flipped through a ledger with a licked thumb. Rain drummed the tin roof hard enough to make conversation a matter of shouting, which suited me fine. I didn't want conversation. I wanted the paper, whatever it said, and then I wanted to walk back down the muddy stair to my boat and never think about Kettle Bend again.

"Vane," he repeated, running a grimy finger down the column. "Yeah. Priority flag. Came through twice—they really wanted you to get it." He tore the flimsy yellow sheet off its spike and held it out, then didn't let go when I took the other end. "You owe me two bits for the handling."

I paid him. He let go. Small mercies.

I read it standing there under the dripping eave, rain coming off the roof in a beaded curtain between me and the river, and by the third line my hands had gone cold in a way the monsoon had nothing to do with.

*CAPTAIN VANE. TRUST THE JOURNAL WAS INFORMATIVE. YOU WERE SEEN AT THE CACHE AND AGAIN AT THE CAMP. YOUR FATHER'S LEDGERS MAKE TEDIOUS READING BUT I AM A THOROUGH MAN. THERE ARE OTHER FILES. WE WILL DISCUSS THEM WHEN YOU DELIVER. H.*

* * *

I stood under that eave for a long time. Long enough for the boy inside to lose interest in me, which took some doing, because seventeen-year-olds at lonely relay stations are connoisseurs of anything that breaks the monotony. Long enough for the rain to soak through my coat shoulders despite the roof, because beaded curtains of water have a way of finding you if you stand still too long. Long enough to read the thing four times, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something more survivable on subsequent passes.

They did not rearrange themselves. Words rarely do me that courtesy.

*Your father's ledgers.* That was the part that snagged like a fishhook under the skin. Not the threat itself—I'd known since the dock that Halloran's generosity came with barbs. Not even *there are other files*, which was the kind of sentence designed to sit in a person's chest and ferment. It was the ledgers. The idea of Halloran, somewhere dry and well-lit in the Lowlands capital, turning page after page of my father's cramped, liquor-blurred bookkeeping, looking for leverage the way a magpie looks for shine.

And finding it. Obviously finding it, because Halloran did not send priority-flagged messages twice unless he was certain of his ground.

Here is what nobody tells you about secrets: they don't stay buried just because you dug the hole deep. They wait. They compost. They turn into soil that other people's questions grow in.

* * *

I walked back down to the boat slower than I needed to. The path from the station wound through Kettle Bend proper, which consisted of eleven buildings, two of which were bars, and a population that watched riverboats the way cats watch birds—with appetite and no intention of exerting themselves. A woman selling fried dough called out to me. I shook my head. My stomach had closed for business.

Odessa was up on deck when I boarded, elbow-deep in the engine housing, a wrench in one fist and an expression that suggested the engine had said something unforgivable about her mother.

"Well?" she asked, not looking up. "Good news from civilization?"

"Thrilling news. The tax code has been amended in our favor and the rains will be gentle this year."

"Liar." She wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands and finally looked at me. Odessa has a face built for skepticism—heavy brows, a mouth that defaults to a flat line, eyes that take measurements. She'd been my engineer for six years, ever since I fished her out of a debtor's cell in Port Anselm by paying a fine I could not afford, and in six years I don't think I've successfully lied to her once. She always knows. I've stopped asking how. "What did it say?"

"Coordinates for the next leg. Weather advisories. The usual bureaucratic poetry."

She kept looking at me. The rain filled the silence, hammering the deck planks, gurgling in the scuppers.

"Captain," she said, which was already bad, because Odessa only says *captain* when she's about to commit insubordination, "you've got your reading face on."

"I don't have a reading face."

"You have four faces. Reading, lying, furious, and hungry. You're wearing reading over furious, which is new, and I don't care for it." She tossed the rag onto the engine housing. "Whatever it is, we're three days past the last place anyone could help us, so either tell me or stop wearing it around."

I should have told her. That's the honest accounting, and I promised myself I'd keep honest accounts on this river even if nowhere else. But telling Odessa meant telling her what was in the files, or at least admitting there were files, and the files were—

Well. The files were mine. The oldest thing I owned, older than the boat, older than my name as people knew it. And I found, standing there with the yellow telegram folded into a square small enough to hide in my palm, that I would rather have handed over the boat.

"Later," I said. "When we're through the narrows. There's nothing actionable yet."

"*Yet,*" Odessa repeated, tasting the word. She shook her head slowly, the way she did at engines that insisted on failing in ways physics forbade. "You know what I do when an engine starts making a noise I don't recognize? I open the housing right then. Because the noise you ignore today is the fire you're swimming away from Thursday."

"Noted, Chief Engineer. Thank you for the parable."

"Anytime." She went back to her engine, but I felt her eyes on my back the whole way to the wheelhouse.

* * *

Pip and Rook were playing cards in the galley with Marisol refereeing from the stove, which meant cheating was rampant and adjudicated by smell—the cook could apparently detect a palmed ace from across the room, a talent she claimed came from years of catching kitchen boys stealing from the till. Brandt sat apart, as he always did, cleaning a instrument case with a cloth and the patience of a man defusing bombs for a living.

I stood in the doorway a moment, watching them. This is not something I do often. Sentiment is a leak, and leaks sink boats. But the telegram was in my pocket weighing forty pounds, and I wanted—I wanted one clean look at the reason I was going to do what I was about to do, which was say nothing.

Pip caught me first. Pip catches everything; it's the twin thing, the shared radar. "Captain's lurking," Pip announced. "That means either trouble or dinner. Marisol, is it dinner?"

"It is not dinner," Marisol said. "Dinner is an hour out and it is going to be excellent, so nobody die before then."

"Then it's trouble." Pip set down the cards. Rook, who had been losing spectacularly, did not set down the cards, on the theory that a winning hand should never be surrendered to mere reality.

"No trouble," I said. "Message from Halloran. Route orders and weather. We push upstream tomorrow at first light—the station master says the tributary at Two Sisters is rising early this year, so we want to be above the confluence before it crests."

Brandt looked up from his case. "Did the message mention the survey team?"

"It mentioned route and weather." Which was true. Everything else was also true, just not mentioned. I was getting good at this particular grammar. "Why? Something you want to ask them?"

"Just noting the priorities." His voice was level, courteous, empty as a swept floor. "A rescue operation that discusses weather before survivors."

"A rescue operation that drowns helps no one," I said. "Weather first. Survivors second. That's been the order of operations since before you were issued a clipboard, surveyor."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger. Assessment. Brandt assesses everything; I sometimes think he assesses his own breakfast. "Of course, Captain. Your boat."

*My boat.* Yes. And my debts, and my father's ledgers, and my files, and my leash, all of it mine, every link stamped with my name.

I left them to their cards and their cheating and their excellent future dinner, and I climbed to the wheelhouse, and I stood at the window watching the rain stitch the river into grey cloth, and I unfolded the telegram one more time even though I knew it by heart now, the way you know a scar.

* * *

Here is the thing about my father's ledgers, since we're being honest, and the rain makes me honest the way some men are made honest by drink.

My father drank. Everyone on the Monsoon Coast knew that; it wasn't a secret, it was a weather pattern. What nobody knew—what the ledgers showed, in entries I hadn't known existed until Halloran's people presumably read them back to me in some airless office—was what happened the night he died. The night of the gorge run. The official account, the one I gave the magistrate at nineteen, shaking, soaked, half-mad with grief: that we'd hit the sandbar shift together, that he'd gone overboard in the white water, that I'd tried everything.

The ledger entry, dated three days later in my father's hand—which meant he wrote it before the run, which meant he'd planned it, which meant—said otherwise. Said things about debts and the boat and *the girl can have her after I'm done*, in his ugly slanted script. Said enough that a careful reader might conclude the run was no accident. Might conclude that his daughter, who was at the wheel, who knew those waters better than any living soul, who could have refused the run or run it differently or simply told him no—

Might conclude she let it happen. Or worse. Might conclude that a nineteen-year-old girl saw a way out of a lifetime of drowning beside a drowning man, and took it.

I didn't. God help me, I didn't—I fought that river until my hands split, I ran rapids at night that I still see when I close my eyes, I did everything except the one thing that would have saved him, which was refuse. But the ledger doesn't show what I did. The ledger shows what a clever prosecutor could say I did. And Halloran had read it, and Halloran had drawn his own conclusions, and Halloran had written *THERE ARE OTHER FILES* with the confidence of a man holding a knife he has already tested on something soft.

That was the leash. Not the debt—that was just the collar. The leash was the version of me that lived in those pages, the guilty daughter, the particle of doubt that could be grown, with enough watering, into a murder charge. I had spent fifteen years building a life where no one asked about that night. Invisible, careful, debts paid on time, crew loyal, name clean. And one government man with a filing cabinet had reached into fifteen years and closed his hand around the one soft place I had.

Shame is a strange cargo. It doesn't weigh anything until you try to move, and then it weighs everything.

* * *

Marisol brought me coffee in the wheelhouse that evening without being asked. She does that—reads hunger the way Odessa reads lies. She set the mug on the chart table and stayed, leaning against the doorframe, arms crossed, watching the rain with me.

"My mother used to say," she offered, after a while, "that the worst letters are the ones you read twice. Once to know, once to hope."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She sounded wise. She was mostly wrong about everything else." Marisol turned her head and looked at me with those flat black tracker's eyes that miss nothing on a trail and evidently miss nothing in a wheelhouse either. "You read yours four times. I counted, through the window."

"The windows here aren't that clear."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "They're not."

I wrapped my hands around the mug. The coffee was terrible, boiled too long, bitter as a customs officer. I drank it anyway.

"Marisol. If a person found out something about someone—something old, something that maybe wasn't even true but couldn't be proven untrue—and that person was using it to steer, would you say the steered one owed the truth to the rest of the crew?"

Marisol considered this with the seriousness she gave to trail signs. "I'd say a trail that goes cold usually isn't cold. It's just waiting for rain to end." She pushed off the doorframe. "Eat something, Captain. Whatever's in that letter, it keeps better than you do."

She left. I stood there with the rain and the dark coming down on the river, and the telegram in my pocket, and I made my decision the way I make all my decisions now: alone, at night, with the wheel under my hands and everyone I'd come to love asleep below decks, trusting me to get them through.

Tomorrow we'd push upstream. Tomorrow we'd follow the trail toward whoever was alive up there in the high country, toward whatever the survey team had found and someone had decided must never come home. And Halloran would watch us do it through whatever lenses he had pointed at us, content, certain, holding his leash.

But leashes run both ways, I thought, watching lightning walk the escarpment's spine far to the north. A leash means he needs me moving forward. A leash means I'm still useful. And useful things get kept alive.

It was the smallest comfort I'd ever taken, and I took it like a woman drinking ditchwater in a drought.

At dawn, Rook came pounding up the wheelhouse stairs two at a time, breathless, eyes huge, and grabbed my arm before I could even ask.

"Captain—you need to come see this. Down on the bank. Someone's been walking."
