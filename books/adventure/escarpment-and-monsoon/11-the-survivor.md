## 11. The Survivor

My sister has a theory about finding people. She says you don't find them—you just stop being surprised by where they are. Pip says it like it's philosophy. I say it's because she once found me in the flour bin when we were six and never let me forget it.

But standing at the mouth of that high valley cave, with the rain coming down like the sky had a grudge and the wind funneling through the rocks making a sound like a throat clearing before bad news, I understood what she meant. Ilsa Rehn wasn't hiding from us. She'd been waiting to be found by someone, anyone, for weeks—and the surprise was only ours.

Marisol smelled her first. Not literally—though I wouldn't put it past her—but she caught the sign: a thread of smoke too thin and too steady to be natural, rising from a crack in the cliffside half a mile up the valley. A fire someone had banked carefully, fed sparingly, kept alive the way you keep a secret.

"Someone's been tending that," Marisol said, crouching over the tracks she'd been reading all morning. "Same spot for days. Maybe weeks."

"Could be an animal den," Brandt said.

"It could also be my grandmother's ghost," Marisol said, not looking at him, "but I'm betting on the living option, since ghosts don't leave boot prints."

The prints were faint—half-worn by rain, small, uneven, favoring one leg. But they were there, leading up the valley toward the smoke, and they were human, and after eleven days of searching for dead men's camps and destroyed equipment and one lonely grave, human was the word none of us dared say out loud.

Felix stood at the base of the slope with her hands jammed in her coat pockets, looking up at the cave like it might look back. Which, as it turned out, it did.

"We go up careful," she said. "Nobody shouts. Nobody rushes. If she's been alone up here since the survey went dark, she's either feral or armed or both." She glanced sideways at Brandt. "And nobody touches anything without asking."

Brandt's face did its usual nothing. I've spent my whole life around people who talk with their faces—Pip's face is basically a broadsheet headline—and I've gotten good at reading the ones who don't. Brandt's nothing is a very specific nothing. It's the nothing of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want anyone to see.

"Understood," he said.

We climbed.

* * *

The cave mouth was maybe eight feet across, screened by a curtain of hanging moss and a rockfall that had been arranged rather than merely happened. Arranged is the right word—I noticed that even before Marisol pointed it out. Someone had pulled stones into a wall on one side, leaving a gap just wide enough for a person to slip through, and had done it in a way that looked natural unless you were the kind of person who'd grown up stacking things to hide behind.

Which I am. Rook and Pip, twins, professional hiders, amateur finders. It's how we survived our first twelve years.

I went through the gap first, because Felix said so and because I'm the quiet one, and the cave opened into a chamber lit by a single small fire and smelling of woodsmoke, wet wool, and something else—something chemical and sharp, like the inside of a surveyor's kit.

She was against the far wall, and I'll be honest, my first thought was *corpse*, because she was so still and so gray and so thin that she seemed more shadow than woman. Then her eyes moved. They were the color of river ice in late winter, and they fixed on me with a focus that had nothing gray about it at all.

Her hand came up holding a knife—a survey knife, the kind with the folding blade and the little brass scale etched on the handle—and though her arm shook with the effort of holding it level, her eyes said she would use it.

"I'm not going to hurt you," I said, which is what everyone says, and which is why she didn't believe it.

"Rook," Felix said softly behind me. "Let me."

I backed up a step. Felix moved forward into the firelight, slow, palms visible, the way you approach a spooked animal or a debt collector. She crouched down to the woman's level, which is something Felix does that always surprises people who think she's cold—the captain who makes herself small.

"My name's Felix Vane. We're from the boat on the river below. We've been looking for your team for two weeks." She paused. "You're Ilsa Rehn. The cartographer."

Something flickered across the woman's face—not relief. Calculation. I know calculation; I grew up on it. She was counting us, weighing us, deciding what we cost.

"How do you have my name?" Her voice was wrecked, gravel and rust, but underneath it was precise. A voice used to being exact.

"Halloran's office gave us the manifest. Names, positions, photographs." Felix reached slowly into her coat, and the knife arm tensed, and Felix froze with her hand halfway in and said, "Photograph. Just the photograph. May I?"

A long moment. Then a nod, barely.

Felix drew out the folded paper and held it up. The rain-damp ink had run a little, but the face was clear enough—younger, fuller, clean-haired, smiling at whoever took the picture with the polite patience of a woman who'd rather be drawing maps than posing for them.

Ilsa Rehn looked at her own face for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed. It was smaller.

"They sent a rescue."

"They sent us," Felix said, and left it exactly there, and I watched Ilsa hear the space between those two sentences and file it away.

Then Brandt stepped into the firelight, and everything in the cave changed.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because I don't think the others saw all of it, and I don't think Ilsa ever forgot any of it.

It wasn't dramatic. He didn't lunge or shout. He just walked forward—past Felix, past me, into the middle of the chamber—and said, "Miss Rehn. Surveyor Second Class Brandt, attached to the Interior Office. You're safe now. We need to recover any documents, instruments, or records the team was carrying, and then we can get you downriver."

And Ilsa Rehn went from sitting to standing in a way that should have been impossible for a starving woman, knife out, back flat against the stone, breathing like a bellows.

"No," she said.

"You're not in trouble—"

"*No.*" Louder this time, cracking on the edges. "You're not rescue. Rescue doesn't ask about documents first." Her eyes raked him, top to bottom, and I saw her lips move around his name like she was tasting something spoiled. "Brandt. I know that name."

"You may be confusing me with—"

"Oduya wrote it down." Her voice shook now, but not from weakness—from fury, the kind that keeps you alive better than food does. "Before the end. Oduya kept notes, and your name is in them, and the last entry says *if anything happens to us, the man from the Interior Office knew the route before we did.*"

The fire popped. Rain hammered the rocks outside. Nobody breathed.

Brandt stood very still. And here's the thing I keep turning over since: he didn't deny it. He didn't bluster or act wounded or do any of the hundred things an innocent man does when accused. He just stood there with his hands loose at his sides and said, quietly, "Those notes are not in your possession."

"No," Ilsa said. "They're somewhere you'll never find them."

"That's unfortunate," Brandt said, and I swear on the twins' shared grave-that-doesn't-exist-yet, he sounded like he meant it as a kindness.

Felix moved then—stepped between them, shoulder to Brandt's chest, not pushing, just present, the way a hull is present between a boat and a rock. "Nobody's recovering anything tonight," she said. "She's half-dead. We get her warm, we get food in her, and we all sleep. Documents can wait for daylight and decency."

For a second I thought Brandt might argue. His jaw worked once, twice. Then he nodded, turned, and walked out through the gap in the rock wall, and the sound of his boots on the scree faded into the rain, and the whole cave exhaled at once.

Marisol was already moving. She'd come through with the pack and she knelt by the fire without asking permission, adding wood, setting the kettle on, pulling out the hard biscuit and the dried fruit and the little tin of rendered fat she hoards like dragon gold. "Eat slow," she told Ilsa, in the voice she uses on sick crew, which is gentler than her normal voice by roughly the width of a continent. "Slow, or you'll bring it back up. Small bites. There's more."

Ilsa stared at the food like it was a map of a country she'd thought was fictional. Then she picked up a piece of dried apple and ate it the way Marisol said, slow, deliberate, tears running silently down her face the whole time, and she never made a single sound about it.

Pip appeared beside me—we always end up shoulder to shoulder eventually, it's gravity—and whispered, "Did you see his face? When she said Oduya?"

"Yeah," I whispered back.

"He didn't say it wasn't true."

"Yeah," I said again, because I'm the quiet one, but somebody has to confirm the obvious.

* * *

Later, when the fire had been built up and Ilsa had eaten enough to stop shaking and Marisol had wrapped her in every dry blanket we owned, Felix sat down across from her and asked, gently, what happened to the team.

Ilsa's hands closed around the blanket edge. For a while I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she started talking, in that rusted voice, in pieces, the way survivors tell it—out of order, circling back, skipping the parts that haven't healed enough to touch.

They'd found what they were sent to find. That much she said plainly. A mineral seam, up in the escarpment, running along the headwaters. Oduya had understood immediately what it meant—what it would do to the river towns, the shipping contracts, the whole economy that hung off the water like laundry off a line. He'd wanted to report it straight and let the government decide. Then the orders had come, relayed down from the Interior Office, and the orders hadn't been about reporting.

She stopped there. She looked at Brandt's empty place by the fire and stopped there, and Felix didn't push, and I loved her a little bit for it.

"What did you keep?" Felix asked instead. "When the equipment was destroyed. What did you carry out?"

Ilsa's hand went, without her seeming to notice, to the breast of her ruined jacket. To something flat and stiff beneath the cloth, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Nothing," she said.

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and said, "Fair enough," and got up to check the watch rotation, and I understood that Felix Vane had just decided not to spend trust she couldn't afford yet.

I stayed on first watch with Marisol. The rain eased toward midnight, and the clouds tore open for a few minutes, and the escarpment stood up against the stars, black and enormous, like the spine of something sleeping.

"She's got it on her," Marisol murmured. "Whatever it is. Right against her chest. Been carrying it so long she probably sleeps on that side only."

"I know."

"And our friend the surveyor knows it too."

"I know that too."

Marisol was quiet a moment. Then: "Your sister's keeping count of the flares, isn't she?"

Pip was. Two nights ago, another flare had gone up from the river far below and behind us—that same patient, methodical red bloom, a boat reporting its position to a boat reporting to something else. Pip had logged it in her little waterproof book, time and bearing, time and bearing. Four flares now. Somebody downstream was following our wake like a dog follows a scent line, and every flare told them exactly where we were and, presumably, what we'd found.

"Marisol," I said, "what happens if whatever she's carrying is worth more than we are?"

Marisol poked the fire. Sparks went up and died young.

"Then we make sure it isn't," she said. "That's the whole job now. Didn't you hear the captain? Nobody's recovering anything."

Down in the cave, Ilsa Rehn slept with her hand pressed flat over her ribs, guarding the flat stiff thing against her heart, and even unconscious she guarded it. Fragile as frost, that woman, and stubborn as bedrock under it. Hope looks different on people who've earned it the hard way—it doesn't shine, it smolders.

Near dawn, I heard boots on the scree. Brandt, coming back up from wherever he'd spent the night, and I watched from the rockfall as he paused at the gap in the wall and stood there a long moment, looking in at the firelight and the sleepers, his face doing its nothing.

Except this time the nothing had a crack in it. Small. Hairline. The kind of flaw you don't notice until the whole structure depends on it holding.

He went inside and sat down by the dying fire, and Ilsa Rehn woke instantly, silently, her eyes finding him across the embers, and neither of them said a word.

Neither did I. But I remembered where I'd put my knife, and I decided to stay awake a little longer.
