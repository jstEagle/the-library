## 12. Brandt's Orders

I have been called many things in my career. Cold-eyed. The government's knife. That man who doesn't blink enough. I have never once been called a monster, and I want to state for the record—whichever record survives this journey, if any record survives this journey—that the distinction matters to me.

Monsters enjoy their work.

I do not enjoy mine. I am good at it, which is a different thing entirely, and I wish more people understood the difference. A surgeon is good at cutting. Nobody calls him a butcher as long as he closes the wound afterward. What I do is surgery on the body politic. Sometimes the body politic has a tumor shaped like a mineral seam, and sometimes the tumor has to come out before it metastasizes into a war that kills ten thousand people instead of six surveyors and one inconvenient cartographer.

That is the arithmetic I carry upriver like ballast. Six dead already. Ten thousand alive if I succeed. It is clean arithmetic. I have done it every night since we left the lowlands, lying in my bunk listening to the river slap the hull, and every night it comes out the same.

Except lately the arithmetic has started talking back.

* * *

The trouble began, I think, with Marisol's cooking.

This will sound absurd. Let me explain anyway, because I have decided that if my head is going to be opened to the page—even a page no one will ever read—I might as well be honest about where the cracks started. Not with the journal page Felix Vane pocketed. Not with the flare I sent up over the survivor's trail. With soup.

We were three days above the last settlement, rain hammering the deck like a debt collector's fist, and Marisol served a fish stew she'd made from nothing—river fish, some root she'd traded for at a fueling stop, a handful of dried peppers she guarded like state secrets. And the crew ate it huddled under the awning while water sheeted off the canvas, and Pip said something that made Rook laugh so hard he choked, and Odessa thumped his back without looking up from her bowl, and Felix sat at the head of the table watching all of them with an expression I did not have a classification for.

I have classifications for most expressions. It is part of the trade.

Hers was not gratitude exactly. It was older than that. It was the look of a woman counting her people the way other captains count cargo—by touch, by weight, by whether anything has shifted since the last check. And every one of them, without exception, was checking her back.

I sat at the end of the table with my notebook and my stew, and I realized I had eaten forty-one meals with these people, and not one of them had been a transaction.

In my line of work, every meal is a transaction. You eat with an informant to buy his trust. You eat with a target to lower his guard. You eat alone because eating alone is the only meal that costs you nothing. Forty-one meals, and nobody at that table wanted anything from anybody except more stew and another story from Pip, who tells stories the way rivers tell bends—one always promising the next.

I wrote in my notebook: *Cohesion high. Morale anomalous.* And then I sat there feeling like a man who has just described a cathedral as "load-bearing masonry" and knows it, and cannot stop himself, because description is the only language he has.

* * *

Let me lay out the case properly, the way I would present it to Halloran, because I want it on the record that my orders are not madness. They are almost reasonable. That is what makes them dangerous.

The seam under the escarpment—if the journal page is accurate, and the survey team's instruments were good—is a lode of ore that would make whoever holds it the arbiter of the entire river economy. Every barge, every mill, every town from the delta to the headwaters runs on contracts priced against materials that seam could flood the market with overnight. The government's plan—the plan I signed, the plan I believed in—was simple: the survey team goes missing, the territory gets designated unstable under emergency powers, the government seizes the upper river "for public safety," and the seam stays buried until such time as its extraction can be managed by responsible parties. Responsible meaning ourselves.

Is that theft? Yes. Is it also the prevention of a land rush that would make the last resource war look like a tavern brawl? Also yes. I have read the intelligence assessments. I have seen what happens when a seam like this leaks—a dozen claim-jumpers, three foreign consulates, two mercenary companies, and a regional governor or two all descend on the same valley, and the river towns burn first because river towns always burn first. The people on this boat—the twins, the engineer, the cook, the captain with her forged-clean past—they live in those towns. My orders exist, on paper, to protect them.

I held onto that. I held onto it through the cache argument, when Felix stood in the rain and told me someone alive needed those supplies and I called it sentiment and she called me a filing cabinet with legs. I held onto it through the gorge, when I recorded water levels while Rook watched me with eyes like a hawk that has spotted something dead. I held onto it through the cave, through the wrecked camp, through the grave, through the journal page I saw her fold into her coat like a woman hiding a knife she hasn't decided whose throat it's for.

Orders are orders. That phrase has carried me through eleven years of service. It is a load-bearing wall in the house of myself, and I have never once tested it, because men who test load-bearing walls generally find out why they built them.

* * *

But here is what the wall is made of, and here is where the inspection gets uncomfortable.

It is made of the assumption that obedience and protection are the same thing. That if I follow instructions, the outcome will be the one the instructions were written for. Eleven years, that assumption held. Then Halloran sent the signal about the shadow boat, and I decoded it by lamplight while the crew slept, and I read the phrase *leave no witnesses*, and I understood—for the first time, fully, in my gut rather than my ledger—that the instructions and the intended outcome had parted company somewhere between the capital and the escarpment.

Leave no witnesses. Not *retrieve the cartographer*. Not *secure the documents*. Leave no witnesses. Which means the cook. Which means the twins. Which means the engineer who talks to her engine like it's a nervous horse. Which means Felix Vane, whose debts Halloran holds like a leash, and who has spent this whole voyage being braver than anyone I've been ordered to betray.

I sat with that decode for two hours. I transcribed it into my cipher book, which is what a good officer does. And then I looked at the transcription, and I thought: this piece of paper is the most honest thing I own, and if anyone found it, everyone on this boat dies sooner.

I burned the transcription. I kept the decode in my head, where I keep everything, where it has been rattling around for six days now like a stone in an empty canteen.

* * *

Today Ilsa Rehn changed the arithmetic again, though she doesn't know it.

We brought her down from the high valley on a litter Odessa rigged from engine strapping. She weighed less than the survey instruments she wouldn't surrender. When Felix offered her water, Ilsa took the flask, checked it, drank, checked Felix's face, drank again—and I recognized the protocol, because it is my protocol. Trust nothing you haven't verified. Verify nothing you can't control. She had been living by my rules for weeks, alone, half-starved, carrying whatever she carries sewn into her clothing, and she was still executing them cleanly.

And then Rook sat down next to her—Rook, who is nineteen and has the emotional subtlety of a landslide—and handed her half of a biscuit without a word, and Ilsa looked at that biscuit like it was a document in a language she used to speak.

That was the moment. I want to be precise about it, because precision is all I have left. The crack did not come from ideology. It did not come from Felix's accusations or Marisol's lies about the trail direction, both of which I noted and filed. It came from a biscuit. From watching a starving woman remember that food could arrive unearned, from a stranger, for no reason except that she was cold and someone had two biscuits.

My orders say she is a security risk to be contained and delivered. My eyes say she is a person who has not been treated like one in two months, and that the first person to treat her like one was a teenage deckhand who owes Felix Vane a debt neither of them has ever named aloud.

I wrote in my notebook tonight: *Subject Rehn responding to humane treatment. Crew cohesion holding under stress beyond parameters.* And beneath it, after a long pause, in smaller handwriting: *Note to self: determine at what point "beyond parameters" became the thing you admire.*

I did not write that sentence intending it. My hand simply produced it. This is what I mean about cracks. You don't decide to have them. You look down one morning and they're there, running through the foundation, and the building stands, but it makes a different sound in the wind.

* * *

Felix found me on watch tonight. Midnight shift, rain gone soft, the escarpment a blacker black against the sky. She climbed to the pilothouse with two cups of something hot and set one beside me without asking whether I wanted it.

"You've been writing in that book since the cache," she said. "Careful. Paper burns."

"So does everything else on this boat, Captain."

"Mm." She drank. We watched the water. Then: "You saved Rook at the crossing. Didn't hesitate."

"The crossing endangered the mission. Losing a crewman endangers it further. It was arithmetic."

"Funny," she said. "Marisol did the same arithmetic pulling Pip out of the current. Odessa did it staying in the engine room when the intake flooded. Funny how everybody's heroism comes out looking like math." She set down the cup. "You're not a filing cabinet, Brandt. I've known filing cabinets. They don't flinch when the crew nearly drowns. You flinched."

"I did not—"

"You stood up so fast you knocked your instruments over. I was there." She moved toward the ladder, then stopped. "Whatever you're carrying in that notebook, whatever orders you're carrying in your head—you should know the crew already voted on you. Last night, while I slept. Pip told me this morning." A pause. "They voted to keep you. Unanimous. I'm told I wasn't given a ballot."

She went down the ladder. I stood my watch with her cup still warm beside me, and I did not touch it for a long time, because touching it felt like it would count somehow, and I was not yet ready to let things count.

They voted to keep me. Unanimous. I have been assessed, evaluated, and retained by every institution I have ever served, and no assessment has ever landed like a vote by four strangers and a captain with forged papers, conducted in whispers over cards while the rain came down.

* * *

Here is where I stand, then, on the eve of whatever comes next. Let the record show my position clearly.

I believe the seam must stay buried. I believe exposure means war, and war means the river towns burn, and I have read enough after-action reports to know what burning towns do to their populations. I believe Halloran is not evil; he is a man solving an equation, as I am, and the equation is real. I believe orders are the only structure standing between men like me and the wilderness of doing whatever feels righteous, and that the wilderness has killed more people than any order ever has.

And I believe—I have believed it for six days now, since the decode, since the biscuit, since the vote I wasn't given a ballot in—that the shadow boat's orders have nothing to do with protecting anyone, and that when we reach the last relay station, I am going to have to choose which structure I serve: the one made of orders, or the one made of a table of people eating stew in the rain.

I know what a good officer does. A good officer transmits the position at the relay, delivers the subject, files his report, and lets the arithmetic stand. Six dead, ten thousand alive. Clean.

I also know that tomorrow we reach the relay station at Kessel Bend, and that Pip has been watching me transmit schedules with those quick bright eyes, and that the boy misses nothing, and that when he catches me—and he will catch me, because I have decided I will not lie well enough to stop him—I do not know what I am going to say.

That is new. In eleven years, I have always known what I was going to say.

I have begun to suspect that the tumor was never the seam. I have begun to suspect the tumor is the part of me that can write *leave no witnesses* in a cipher book and call it protection, and that the surgery I actually need is not the kind performed on other people.

Tomorrow, Kessel Bend. Tomorrow, the wire. Tonight, the rain, and the sound of a crew sleeping below decks who voted to keep me, and the first honest entry I have ever made in a service notebook:

*I no longer know whose side the orders are on.*
