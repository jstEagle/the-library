## 13. The Crossing at Flood Crest

The tributary had a name on Ilsa's charts and a different name in the mouths of the people who lived near it, and both names meant the same thing: *don't be here when the water comes.*

We were here when the water came.

I stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Grace* — my father's boat, my father's debts, my father's river, though by now the river had outlived three generations of men who thought they owned it — and watched the crossing churn itself into a brown fury that looked less like water and more like a argument the land was having with the sky. The tributary came down off the escarpment shoulders in a broad, braided sprawl, and at flood crest it moved like something with opinions. Whole trees went past us sideways, root-balls first, spinning slow and dignified as drowned bishops.

"That's not a crossing," Odessa said beside me. She had her arms crossed and her jaw set, which was her way of saying she'd already run the numbers and hated all of them. "That's a suggestion the river is making."

"We don't have a better one." I kept my voice level, because the crew took its weather from me, and I wasn't going to rain on them before I had to. "The descent route home is on the far side. Everything else adds four days, and we don't have four days."

"We don't have a boat if we do this either," she said. "Not necessarily."

"Not necessarily" was doing a lot of work in that sentence, and we both knew it.

Behind us, the morning was the color of wet slate. The monsoon had settled in for good now, rain coming down in long gray sheets that made the whole world sound like static. Pip and Rook were forward, checking the lines for the fourth time. Marisol stood amidships with her arms folded, watching the water the way she watched everything — patiently, like it would eventually tell her the truth. And Brandt stood at the rail with his notebook, because Brandt always stood at the rail with his notebook, recording God knew what. Water levels. Current speed. The precise angle at which our lives were about to become complicated.

"Captain." His voice carried flat over the rain. "If you're waiting for the crest to pass—"

"It won't pass," I said. "Not for days. Crest isn't a wave, Brandt. It's a mood. The river stays in this mood until the rains ease, and the rains won't ease, and every hour we sit here the shadow boat gets closer." I didn't look at him when I said *shadow boat*. None of us did anymore. It had become a word like weather — acknowledged, unfixable.

"The crossing can be made," he said. "The current sets downstream but there's an eddy line along the far bank where the gorge walls deflect the flow. If you enter upstream of the gravel bar and hold angle—"

"I know how to hold an angle across floodwater, surveyor."

"I know you do." Something flickered behind those cold eyes — not warmth exactly, but the ghost of respect, which from Brandt was nearly the same thing. "I'm saying it aloud so it's on the record that this plan has a basis."

"On whose record?" Rook called back, grinning despite himself. He was soaked through and loving every minute of it, because Rook loved every minute of anything that involved not dying quietly.

"Mine," Brandt said, and wrote something down.

* * *

We entered upstream of the gravel bar, because that part of the plan was correct even if it came from a man I trusted about as far as I could throw his notebook.

For the first ninety seconds, it was almost fine.

I'll say that for the record too, since apparently we were keeping one. The *Kestrel* bit into the current and held. The engine roared under Odessa's hands — I could hear her talking to it through the deck plates, low and filthy, the way she talked to machines she respected. We crabbed across the flow at maybe thirty degrees off straight-across, bow pitched up, spray coming over the rails in solid green-gray slabs. Pip was on the bow line, Rook on the stern, and between them they looked like something carved on the prow of a warship, two skinny twins daring the water to try something.

Then the dead tree hit us.

It came out of the murk without any warning at all — no wake, no shape, just suddenly *there*, a whole cedar the length of our mast, riding high and fast on the crest. I saw it the same instant Rook shouted, and I did the only thing there was to do, which was nothing, because there was nowhere to put sixty feet of riverboat in front of forty tons of floating timber.

It caught us amidships with a crack I felt in my teeth. The *Kestrel* lurched, slewed, and for one horrible moment lay beam-on to the current, which is the position riverboats go to die. Water poured over the leeward rail in a cataract. I heard Odessa screaming something about the engine intake, heard Marisol's voice cutting through it sharp and clear — *"Rook!"* — and then I heard nothing at all, because the river took him.

He went over the starboard rail like he'd been plucked, no splash even, just gone, swallowed by brown water moving faster than any living thing should argue with. The line he'd been holding tore out of its cleat and whipped away.

Time did something strange after that. It stretched and slowed the way it does when your body knows things your mind hasn't approved yet. I had the wheel hard over, fighting to bring the bow around, screaming orders I don't remember giving. Pip was already at the rail with a coil of line, and I saw her measure the throw, see it was impossible, see her start to wind up anyway —

And Brandt went over the side.

No hesitation. No order given, none needed. He stripped nothing off, shed nothing, didn't even set down his notebook — it went into the water with him, pages blooming open like some pale flower drowning. He simply stepped off the rail into floodwater carrying a knife from the galley block and swam for Rook with a stroke so efficient it looked rehearsed, because everything Brandt did looked rehearsed, and I understood in that moment that somewhere in whatever past had made him, someone had taught him to do exactly this.

The current took them both.

I brought the *Kestrel* around in a screaming arc, Odessa nursing the engine through a sound I never want to hear again — metal chewing on something it shouldn't — and we chased them downstream while the gorge walls narrowed and the water got faster and the arithmetic got worse. Twice I lost them. Twice Marisol's arm shot out and pointed — *there* — and each time it was Brandt I found first, hauling Rook by the collar, Rook limp as laundry, Brandt's face doing absolutely nothing at all except the work.

Pip got a line to them on the third pass. I won't dress it up; it was ugly. Rook came over the rail vomiting half the tributary, and Brandt came over the rail after him, climbed aboard, and stood there dripping, looking around for his notebook.

Marisol fished it out of the bilge water ten minutes later. Half the pages had turned to pulp. Brandt took it from her, examined the ruin of his careful record-keeping, and closed it without comment.

"You went in after him," I said. I hadn't left the wheel. My hands were shaking on the spokes and I made them stop.

"He was in the water."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer." He wrung out his sleeve, methodical, like a man folding a flag. "There are orders, Captain, and there is a man drowning in front of you. Some days they're the same order. Today they weren't."

I didn't know what to do with that. Neither, judging by their faces, did anyone else. Pip sat on the deck with her brother's head in her lap, and she looked up at Brandt with an expression I couldn't read — gratitude and suspicion fighting to a draw, neither willing to concede.

Odessa came up from the engine room black to the elbows and delivered her report the way she delivered all bad news: flatly, with numbers. "Intake screen's half torn away. She's running hot and pulling silt. I can keep her alive at half power, maybe. Maybe. And we've taken water aft — the seam above the rudder stock opened when the tree hit. I've got it stuffed and clamped, but it's stuffed and clamped, not fixed."

"How long will the stuffing hold?"

"As long as it wants to. Same as any of us."

* * *

We limped into the lee of the far bank as the light failed, and tied off against the roots of a fig tree old enough to have opinions about empires. The rain eased to a whisper. Someone — Marisol, always Marisol — produced hot food from a galley that had briefly tried to drown, and we ate in a silence that wasn't comfortable but wasn't broken either.

Rook slept in the cabin, breathing, which was the best news of the day and cost nothing. Pip sat outside his door and wouldn't be moved.

Brandt sat apart, as he always did, mending the tear in his sleeve with small, precise stitches. I brought him a cup of Marisol's tea because it seemed like the thing to do, and because I wanted to look at him close and see if I could find the answer to a question I'd been circling all evening.

"Why?" I said.

He didn't pretend to misunderstand. That was one thing I'd give him — he never wasted motion on pretending. "You want me to tell you I've changed sides. That saving him means something."

"Doesn't it?"

"It means I'm not the kind of man who watches a boy drown to protect a filing system." He pulled the stitch tight. "It doesn't mean I'm not still the kind of man who protects the filing system. Both things are true, Captain. You're going to have to hold both of them at once, and so am I."

"And when they stop being the same order? When Halloran's instructions and that boy asleep in there point in opposite directions?" I nodded toward the cabin. "Which way do you step off the rail then?"

His needle paused. For a moment — just a moment — the cold eyes weren't cold at all. They were tired, and young underneath the tiredness, and I thought: *there you are. Somebody's son.*

"I don't know," he said.

Of everything he might have said, that was the one that frightened me most. A loyal man you can predict. A doubting one is a lit fuse, and nobody knows the length.

I left him with his tea and his mending and walked aft, past the patched seam groaning softly under the hull, past Pip standing her silent watch, and stood at the stern rail looking back the way we'd come.

Far downstream, through the last gray light and the thinning rain, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Deliberate. Patient.

Signal code, from a boat that should have been a full day behind us.

I stood in the dark and counted the flashes, and understood that whatever Brandt had decided on that flooded crossing, somebody behind us had already decided what we were worth.
