## 14. Ilsa's Map

I have carried a dead woman's satchel for one hundred and eleven days.

I know the number because counting was the only arithmetic that kept me sane up there in the high valley, where the days blurred into a gray porridge of rain and fog and the slow, patient work of not dying. One hundred and eleven days of rationing a cache that should have fed six people for two months. One hundred and eleven nights listening to the escarpment drip like a leaking tap the size of God. One hundred and eleven mornings deciding, again, to stay alive — which sounds noble until you've done it, and then it just sounds like stubbornness with better publicity.

The satchel belonged to Dr. Amara Osei, our team lead. She gave it to me on the last morning any of them were alive, and she did not give it to me gently. She pressed it into my chest hard enough to bruise and said, "You're the cartographer. You know how to keep things flat and dry. Keep this flat and dry." Then she smiled at me, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on a human face, because it was the smile of someone who has already done all their grieving and is now just doing paperwork.

I didn't understand until later. I'm not sure I fully understand now. But I understood enough to run upstream when everyone else's trail ran down, and I understood enough to let myself get thin and filthy and half-feral rather than walk into the first settlement and say *here I am, sole survivor, ask me anything.* Survivors who talk get written up by survivors who don't. That's not cynicism. That's field notes.

So when Felix Vane finally sat down across from me in the cave — dripping, exhausted, wearing the expression of a woman who has been lied to so often she's begun to find it restful — and said, "All right, Rehn. Whatever you've been carrying for four months, it's time," I found that my hands were already moving before my fear could file an objection.

That's the thing about carrying something that long. Your body learns the route to it better than your mind does.

* * *

The satchel had a false bottom. Of course it did. Amara Osei was the kind of woman who double-checked her own signatures, and she'd built the false bottom herself out of waxed canvas and a strip of bamboo, stitched so fine you'd need to be looking for it. I'd slept on that satchel for a hundred nights with my cheek against the seam, and I'd never once thought about what was under it until the night I heard boots on scree below my cave and realized that whatever happened next, the contents were going to matter more than my life.

I unwrapped it now, in front of Felix, and laid the layers out on a flat stone like a surgeon laying out instruments. Or a fortune-teller. The distinction felt thinner than usual.

Felix watched without speaking. She'd brought no one else — not Brandt, which told me something; not the twins, which told me more. Just her, and the rain hammering the cave mouth behind her like applause from an audience that wanted somebody to die.

"Three things," I said. My voice came out rustier than I expected. "The map. The funding orders. And the part that's going to make you want to throw me off the escarpment."

"I've thrown people off less for less," Felix said, in the tone of a woman making conversation about weather. "Go on."

I put the map down first, because maps are honest in a way people aren't, and I needed one honest thing between us before I got to the dishonest ones.

It was the survey's master chart — my own drafting, my own hand, ink gone slightly bloomed from the damp despite everything. The river system rendered in full: the lowland channels, the gorge, the escarpment plateau, the tributaries feeding down from the high country like veins off a leaf. And along the eastern flank of the plateau, where the rock showed its teeth in a long exposed ridge, I'd drawn the seam.

Not a rumor. Not a promising anomaly. A seam — a mineral lode running eleven kilometers through limestone that the entire river economy is built on top of. The stuff that makes the dredges profitable, the smelters run, the lowland towns pay their taxes and their bribes. Enough of it exposed at surface that extraction wouldn't even require mining, properly speaking. It would require *removing the plateau*, or at least everything on it, and routing the headwaters through processing works that would turn the upper river into something you'd need gloves to touch.

Felix looked at it for a long time. Her face did something complicated and then settled into stillness, the way water does right before it goes over a drop.

"That's not a deposit," she said finally. "That's a verdict."

"It's a verdict against everyone who lives downstream of it," I said. "Which is everyone."

"Who else has seen this?"

"The government. That's the problem." I reached for the second layer. "They didn't send us to find it. They sent us to confirm they'd already found it. We were the receipt."

* * *

Here is what I have had one hundred and eleven days to think about, and what I will now inflict on you:

When the survey was chartered, we were told it was hydrographic. Water levels, flow rates, seasonal variation — dull, respectable, fundable science. Six of us: Amara Osei leading, two geologists, a hydrologist, a field medic who doubled as cook, and me with my theodolite and my ink-stained fingers. We went up in the dry season with official blessing and official paper and the vague sense, common to all government expeditions, that we were being sent somewhere inconvenient to measure something nobody would read.

The geologists found the seam in week three. Amara radioed it in — coded, per protocol, but coded the way a locked door is locked, i.e., only against honest people. Within nine days we had new orders delivered by relay runner. New objectives. Expanded scope. And attached to those orders, because someone in the ministry's logistics chain made a mistake that I suspect cost several people their careers, the original charter documentation.

Amara read it twice. Then she folded it, put it away, and started destroying equipment.

I asked her why, that night, over a fire we kept small. She looked at me with those tired, precise eyes and said, "Ilsa, what do you think happens to a survey team that finds eleven kilometers of gold-grade lode under land the government has been trying to annex for twenty years?"

"They get funded properly?" I said, because I was younger then, in every way that matters.

"They get *unaccounted for*," she said. "Look at the charter. Read the emergency provisions clause. Read who signed the funding authorization." She tapped the page. "This expedition was never meant to come home. We were meant to vanish — lost to flood, lost to the terrain, tragic, regrettable, and *conveniently* establishing that the upper territories are too dangerous for civilian settlement. Which triggers the emergency powers act. Which lets them seize the whole plateau without a vote, without compensation, without anyone downstream ever knowing there was a seam there at all until the dredges arrive."

She let that sit while the fire popped.

"And us?"

"You and me are having this conversation because I intend for exactly one of us to be unaccounted-for-adjacent," she said. "The rest—" and here her voice did the thing I still hear at night, the thing where grief gets flattened into logistics because feeling it at full volume would stop her hands from working— "the rest is already decided. They'll recall us through the gorge in flood season. If we refuse, they won't need to do anything at all. The river will handle it, and the report will write itself."

We argued. Of course we argued. Amara was a scientist; she believed in evidence the way other people believe in saints. She wouldn't run, because running confirmed guilt. She wouldn't publish, because there was nothing left to publish *to* — the relay stations were government, the newspapers downstream were government-adjacent, and the one independent delegate assembly in the river towns met too far away and too rarely to reach before the flood season closed the routes.

So she did the only thing left. She made sure the evidence would survive even if none of us did. False bottom. Wax canvas. And the cartographer, because the cartographer knows how to keep things flat and dry.

The last supply cache we opened — the one your lot found, the one your surveyor wanted seized — wasn't looted. Amara restocked it deliberately and marked it fresh, because she knew any rescue party would check it, and she wanted whoever came looking to know someone alive had been there. A message in the only language a rescue party reads: *keep searching.*

Then she sent me upstream with the satchel and kept the others together as bait, so that any retrieval team would follow the main body's trail and not mine.

I have had one hundred and eleven days to decide whether I hate her for that. I still haven't finished the calculation. Some sums don't resolve.

* * *

I laid the charter papers out beside the map. Funding authorization, ministry seal, the emergency powers clause with its relevant line underlined in Amara's neat hand — *in the event survey personnel are lost or unaccounted for within designated territories, provisional administrative control shall devolve to the Ministry pending recovery operations.* Signed four months before we ever broke camp. Before we'd found anything. Before there was anything to be lost *from*.

Felix read it the way she does everything — fast, sideways, like reading costs money. Then she read it again slowly, and I watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"They wrote the obituary before the trip," she said.

"Before the departure," I corrected. "The trip was the funeral."

"And Halloran's rescue party—"

"Is the recovery operation. Clause seven. They're legally required to attempt retrieval of 'personnel and materials.' Not to succeed. To *attempt*. The attempt establishes diligence. The failure establishes danger. Either way, the ministry wins." I tapped the underline. "You weren't hired to save anyone, Captain. You were hired to be the witness who confirms we're dead."

Felix was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something flat and cold that I recognized, because I'd heard it in my own head for four months. "Brandt's flare signals. The boat following us. The manifest burned at the customs house."

"Retrieval protocol," I said. "They were never letting the search party come home either. Why would they? You've seen the caches. You've seen the camp. You know the shape of it. Every person on this river who can testify that the team was destroyed on purpose is a loose thread, and the ministry has never once in its history left a thread dangling if scissors were cheaper."

I should mention here that I liked Felix Vane immediately, in the way you like a knife — appreciating the edge while keeping your fingers clear. She didn't gasp. She didn't curse. She sat very still and did arithmetic, and I could see the columns adding up behind her eyes: her crew, her boat, her father's debts, whatever leash Halloran held on her that she hadn't told me about yet and that I was polite enough not to ask after.

"What do you want from me?" she said at last.

"Nothing heroic," I said. "I've had enough heroism. Heroism is what killed five good people on this escarpment." I gathered the papers back into their order, hands steadier than they had any right to be. "I want delivery. Downstream, past the relays, to the delegates' assembly at the landing. There's a session convened in three weeks — the river towns' representatives, the ones the ministry can't quite buy. Amara believed if the charter reached them publicly, with the seal intact and the signature dates provable, the emergency powers act collapses. No unaccounted-for personnel, no devolved authority. And once the seam becomes public knowledge, it stops being a secret weapon and starts being a fight the whole river gets to have in the open."

"And if the ministry decides three weeks is plenty of time to make sure we don't arrive?"

"Then I'll have died closer to home," I said, "which is more than my colleagues managed."

Felix looked at me for a long moment. Rain, fire, the two of us and a dead woman's paperwork between us. Then she said, "You should know that the man you don't trust — Brandt — his name is in your colleagues' notes, and he's saved one of my crew's lives since, and I no longer know which of those facts matters more. And you should know that Halloran has something on me, and I don't yet know how much of my spine it owns."

"I figured both of those out around day forty," I said. "I've been waiting for you to catch up."

Something moved across her face that on another woman might have been a laugh. "Day forty."

"I had time," I said. "Time is the one thing the escarpment gives you in surplus."

She stood, rolled the map with a care that surprised me — a captain's hands, respectful of paper — and tucked the satchel's contents inside her coat, against her body, where a knife would go. "Then here's what happens," she said. "Nobody else sees these tonight. Not Brandt, not my crew, not yet. Tomorrow I decide who knows what, in what order, because if we do this wrong we all drown and the ministry writes the report." She paused at the cave mouth, rain silvering her shoulders. "One question, Rehn. The grave at the camp. Whose?"

"Vasquez," I said. "Our medic. Fever, not murder — the one death up here the government didn't arrange. Amara buried him herself." I looked at the fire. "She said it was practice. For burying the truth. She had a very bad sense of humor at the end."

Felix nodded once, the way people nod at graves, and stepped out into the rain.

I sat alone with the fire and did what I had done for one hundred and eleven nights: counted. Except tonight the count was different. Tonight I counted not the days behind me but the river-kilometers ahead — the gorge, the relays, the shadow boat I knew was trailing us, the landing where either the truth arrived or we did, and the mathematics of which of those the ministry would prefer.

And that was when I heard it, faint under the rain: the sound of Pip shouting from the deck below, and Odessa's engine changing pitch, and Rook's voice cracking across the water with a word I couldn't make out but whose shape I knew, because I'd spent four months learning the vocabulary of things going wrong.

Downriver, past the dark bend, a light had appeared where no light should be.

The shadow boat had stopped following us.
