## 15. Betrayal at the Relay

The relay station at Kettle Narrows looked like something the river had tried to swallow and given up on halfway. A tin-roofed shack bolted to a granite shelf, a mast leaning like a drunk against a lamppost, and a wire antenna strung between two dead cedars that had clearly died of embarrassment rather than weather.

We tied off below it in the last grey hour before dark, because Brandt said we had to, and because Felix said fine, and because when those two agreed on anything you didn't argue—you just checked your pockets afterward.

I wasn't supposed to be the one who noticed things. That was Rook's job, or Marisol's, or Felix's with her captain's sixth sense for trouble. I was the deckhand who catalogued port rituals and kept the good coffee hidden from Odessa. But here's what nobody tells you about being the youngest aboard: you learn to watch. You learn that grown people assume you're not paying attention, and that assumption is worth more than any wage.

So I was paying attention when Brandt went up the path alone.

* * *

Let me back up, because if I'm going to tell this properly I have to tell it honestly, and honesty is a discipline I learned from watching Felix lie beautifully for years.

Since the cave where Rook found Ilsa Rehn half-dead among her own maps, Brandt had been—different is too soft a word. Correct. He'd been correct. He carried water for Marisol without being asked. He took the middle watch so Odessa could sleep near her engine. When the crossing at flood crest nearly ate Rook whole, it was Brandt's arm that came out of the white water and hauled him onto the hull, no hesitation, no speech afterward about duty or orders. Just an arm, and then silence.

Rook started saying thank you to him. Rook, who hadn't thanked a government man for anything since the customs office photographed our boat back in the lowlands.

And I wanted to believe it. God, I wanted to believe it the way you want to believe the river's dropped when really it's just holding its breath. Because I'd seen the flare go up over the ridge two weeks ago—I'd seen it, green star burning through the rain, and Brandt standing under it with his signal kit open like a man posting a letter—and I'd told myself there were explanations. Position reports. Protocol. A rescue party reporting progress to its paymasters, nothing sinister, nothing that needed saying out loud.

That's the thing about wanting to believe. It'll build you a whole house out of wet matches and call it shelter.

* * *

Felix posted watches for the night: herself at midnight, Odessa at three, me at dawn. Which meant from supper until midnight I was free, and free time on the *Vane's Luck*—may she rest in pieces, though she didn't know it yet—meant finding somewhere dry and pretending to read.

Instead I followed Brandt up the hill.

Not heroically. Not with a plan. I'd like to tell you my heart was pounding with righteous purpose, but mostly my boots were squelching and I was thinking, *if he turns around I'll say I need to use the relay's privy*, which was true, incidentally. Fear and bladder make poor bedfellows but they travel together.

The station keeper was asleep inside, or pretending to be; these relay men sleep like cats, one ear up. The transmitter room was a lean-to on the seaward side, and its lamp burned yellow through rain-streaked glass, and Brandt's shadow moved across that glass in the particular way a shadow moves when a man is working a key.

Sending. Not receiving. Sending takes rhythm; receiving is just listening, and listeners sit still.

I stood in the wet dark behind the woodpile and did arithmetic I hated every number of. The flare on the ridge. The boat Pip—me, I mean, I keep doing that, cataloguing myself like cargo—the boat *I'd* seen through the rain, running lights doused, holding position astern of us for three days like a shark that had learned patience. The way Brandt always knew which channel to check for weather before anyone asked. The way his questions never sounded like questions.

The key stopped. Started again. Stopped.

And then, because I am apparently the kind of fool who walks toward the thing everyone else would run from, I stepped out of the rain and opened the door.

* * *

He didn't startle. That was the first horror of it. He turned from the key with the lamp behind him and looked at me the way you look at weather you've already read in the barometer.

"Pip." My name in his mouth sounded official. Filed. "You should be aboard."

"You should be asleep," I said, "but here we both are."

Behind him, on the desk, lay the transmission log—a carbon flimsy, half-written, and I could read upside down better than most people could read right-side up, a skill acquired from years of checking whether cooks were shorting the sugar. Coordinates. Our coordinates. And beneath them, a word I knew from the manifests I wasn't supposed to read: *retrieval complete.*

"Is that us?" I asked. Stupid question. I asked it anyway, because asking stupid questions buys time, and time was the only weapon I'd brought up the hill.

Brandt looked at the flimsy. Then at me. And something happened in his face that I'd never seen there—not softness exactly, because I don't think softness lived in that man, but a crack, a hairline fracture running through all that government masonry.

"Yes," he said. "That's you."

My hands were shaking. I put them in my pockets so he wouldn't see, which fooled neither of us. "Reporting our position," I said. "Standard protocol."

"No," Brandt said.

The rain filled the silence for a while. It does that. Rain is the river's oldest liar—it sounds like it's saying something comforting when really it's just counting.

"The boat behind us," I said. "The one with no lights. What's its name?"

"I don't know its name."

"What's its business?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he sat down on the operator's stool, slowly, like a man setting down something very heavy, and he said:

"To leave no witnesses."

* * *

Here's what I should have done, by every code I'd ever learned on the river: gone for Felix. Gone for Marisol, who could break a man's wrist with a ladle and a smile. Gone for anybody, because you don't stand alone in a tin shed with a man who just admitted he's been feeding you to a knife.

But Felix was asleep, finally actually asleep, for the first time since Halloran's voice had come down the wire at the last relay and said things to her that made her face do something I'd never seen it do. And the crew was asleep. And Ilsa Rehn was asleep with her maps sewn into her coat like a second skin.

And Brandt sat on that stool looking like a man who had been carrying a stone uphill for months and had just set it down in front of the only witness available.

"Say it plainly," I said. "All of it. Or I scream loud enough to wake the keeper, the crew, and the dead survey team."

So he said it plainly. That was the terrible thing—he didn't dress it up. He was constitutionally incapable of dressing things up; it was, I think now, his one virtue and his entire damnation.

The survey team had found the seam. Halloran's office had known what the seam meant—the seizure powers, the emergency declarations, the upper river signed away on paper no one would ever get to read. The team was meant to be unaccounted for. Lost. A tragedy that justified everything that came after. And the rescue—our rescue, Felix's debt-paid rescue, Odessa's engine and the twins' rope work and Marisol's beans—was never a rescue. It was a retrieval with a mop attached. Find the survivor, find the documents, and then—

"And then the boat that follows makes sure the story stays clean," Brandt said. "No witnesses. That includes the crew. That includes—" he paused, and the pause cost him something, I saw it cost him—"that includes Captain Vane. Especially Captain Vane. Her past makes her deniable. They chose her for it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the river went on being the river, enormous and indifferent, and I thought about how small our wake must look from up here. A thread of foam. Nothing.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you caught me."

"That's not a reason. People don't confess because they're caught. They lie because they're caught. Everyone knows that. It's practically a port ritual."

He almost smiled. It was awful, seeing it—like watching frost form on a living thing. "Because I watched Rook say thank you," he said. "And I couldn't file it anywhere. There's no form for it." He looked at his hands, which were steady, which had been steady through everything, and I realized that steadiness can be its own kind of drowning. "I believed the seam had to stay buried. I still believe it—expose that mineral strike and you'll have three governments and every syndicate on the coast at war over this water inside a year. I believed the orders served something larger than the people carrying them. That's what obedience is, Pip. It's arithmetic done by someone else, far away, using numbers that include you only as a remainder."

"And now?"

"Now the arithmetic says drown six people who pulled me out of a flooded tributary and fed me and taught me nothing except—" he stopped. Started again. "Marisol gave me the largest portion at dinner. She thought I didn't notice. I always notice. I'm a surveyor. Noticing is the trade." His voice did something rough at the edges. "And I cannot make the portion size balance against six lives. I've tried. The equation won't close."

I should tell you what I felt, because this chapter will be read someday—Ilsa insists history gets written by whoever survives to hold the pen—and I want the record straight. I didn't feel noble. I didn't feel merciful. I felt cold all the way through, colder than the rain, because I understood suddenly that I was standing in front of a loaded decision wearing a human face, and whatever I did next would either save my family or sign them.

Pip, age nineteen, deckhand, keeper of the good coffee. Holding the lives of everyone she loved in a tin shed at the top of a hill.

"What happens at dawn?" I asked. "If that flimsy goes out."

"They come at the narrows below the falls. Two boats. They take Ilsa and the documents. Anyone who resists—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The river finished it for him, the way rivers do.

"And if it doesn't go out?"

"Then I'm a dead man too, eventually. They'll know I broke. They always know." He said it like a man reading a tide table. Factual. Scheduled.

I took the flimsy off the desk.

He let me. That's the part I turn over at night, the part I'll probably still be turning over when I'm old and telling this story to children who won't believe it: he let me take it. He watched my shaking hands crumple his transmission into a wet ball and he made no move at all, and I understood that the move had already been made, days ago maybe, weeks, back at a flooded crossing when his arm reached for Rook without consulting his orders first.

"There's a key on that desk," I said. "Send something else. Something that buys us till dawn and past it."

"Such as?"

"Tell them we lost the survivor. Tell them the flood took her at the crossing—we nearly drowned twice, it's believable, even you can't argue with the water. Tell them the documents are ruined. Tell them—" I was inventing it as fast as I could talk, and some part of me marveled at how easily lying came when it was for family instead of from fear. "Tell them the trail's cold and we're running for home ahead of the crest."

He looked at me for a long moment. "You've done this before."

"I've watched Felix do it before. Same thing."

And then Brandt—the cold-eyed surveyor, Halloran's own man, the leash around our necks—turned back to the key and sent my lie down the wire in his flat official fist, dot and dash, steady as a metronome, and I stood behind him in the lamplight holding a crumpled ball of paper that weighed, I swear, as much as the boat.

When he finished he sat still a moment. Then he said, without turning around:

"They'll verify. Eventually. A day, perhaps two. When the verification fails, they'll stop believing anything on this wire, and then it won't matter what we send."

"Then we spend the day getting somewhere they can't reach."

"Captain Vane will want to know."

"Captain Vane," I said, "is going to want to know a lot of things, and I'm going to be the one who tells her, and I'd appreciate it if you stood next to me when I do, because if she kills anyone tonight I'd prefer it be you."

This time the frost-smile came and stayed a moment longer. "Understood."

* * *

We walked down the hill together through the rain, and I want you to know that I kept him in front of me the whole way, one hand in my pocket around Marisol's fish knife, which I'd taken from the galley an hour earlier for reasons I hadn't let myself examine. Trust, on the *Vane's Luck*, was less a feeling than a logistics problem. You trusted people the way you trusted a mooring line: with constant inspection and a backup knot.

At the bottom of the path I stopped him.

"One more thing. Why spare me the fight? You could've taken me. You're twice my weight and you know exactly where to hit a person—I've seen your service record face, Brandt. Why just—sit there and confess?"

He was quiet while the rain ticked on the tin roof of the boathouse below. Then he said, "Because you walked in the door instead of running for help. Because you asked your questions in order, worst first. Because you're nineteen and you did the arithmetic yourself instead of waiting for someone far away to do it for you." He looked out at the black water where our boat rode at anchor, lampless, breathing. "Halloran would call that insubordination. I'm starting to think it's the only honest rank there is."

Then he went and woke Odessa, because someone had to tell the engine room we'd be running before dawn, and I stood in the rain a minute longer with the crumpled flimsy in my fist, not yet ready to go below and wake Felix Vane and say the words that would change everything:

*I know what's coming. And I know whose fault it isn't.*

Below me, far down the narrows, past the falls, past the dark bend where the river forgot our name—two sets of running lights flickered on, brief as struck matches, and went dark again.

They were moving early.

They weren't waiting for dawn after all.
