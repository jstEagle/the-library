## 16. Running the Gorge by Night

There are three things I know about the gorge that no chart records and no living soul has ever heard me say out loud. The first is that the sandbar below the second narrows migrates east every spring flood, like a dog circling before it lies down. The second is that the whirlpool under Widow's Tooth isn't a whirlpool at all—it's a drain, and if you feed it your port side at the wrong angle it will take the whole boat down with the courtesy of a customs officer taking a bribe. The third thing is that my father died on this stretch of river because he trusted the official charts over his own eyes, and I have spent eleven years making sure I never became a woman who trusts anything printed by a government office.

Tonight I was going to need all three things, plus a fourth I didn't have: luck.

"Tell me we have moonlight," I said.

"We have rain," Odessa said from the engine hatch, her voice coming up through the deck planks like a prayer from a basement. "We have a great deal of rain."

Of course we did. The monsoon had opened two weeks early and had been showing off ever since, and now it chose this night—the night the shadow boat's running lights finally appeared behind us in the dark like a pair of patient yellow eyes—to deliver its most sincere performance yet. The gorge walls on either side of us were black cutouts against a blacker sky. Somewhere above, water was falling off the escarpment in sheets you couldn't see, only hear: a sound like the whole plateau exhaling.

Pip came aft along the gunwale, one hand on the rail, moving with the particular care of someone who has already fallen in once this season and does not intend to make it a habit. "Still there," she reported. "Half a mile back. Maybe less. They're not even trying to hide anymore."

"They stopped trying when they realized hiding costs more than catching," I said. "Rook. Lanterns."

Rook was already at the lamp locker. He'd rigged the boat's lights himself years ago—three circuits, so we could run dark, run lit, or run lit-but-lying, which is what we did in ports where the harbor master liked to count lamps and draw conclusions about cargo. Now he threw all three circuits together and every lamp on the boat blazed at once, bow to stern, turning our little deck into a stage.

Brandt stood at the rail amidships, soaked through, watching the yellow eyes behind us. He hadn't spoken since the relay station. Whatever bargain he'd struck with Pip—and whatever confession had come with it—he was carrying it the way a man carries a wound he hasn't decided whether to show the doctor. His hands were steady, though. I'll give him that. His hands were always steady.

"You're lighting us up for them," he said.

"I'm telling them exactly what they expect to see," I said. "A panicked boat running bright through the worst water on the coast, because a panicked captain wants to see her own bow wave." I looked at Rook. "Kill them on my mark. Not before."

"And after?" Marisol asked. She had come up from below with Ilsa Rehn behind her, both of them wrapped in whatever oilcloth the galley had surrendered. Ilsa's map—the real map, the one worth more than every hull on this river—was sewn into the lining of her coat, because Ilsa Rehn trusted pockets the way other people trust gods, and honestly, pockets have better track records.

"After," I said, "we go where the charts say there is no river."

* * *

Let me tell you about my father, since apparently tonight is the night the river and I settle accounts.

Aurel Vane ran cargo up and down this coast for twenty-two years and died in the gorge on an ordinary Tuesday in ordinary weather, because the survey office had redrawn the channel markers after a flood season and nobody had bothered to tell the boats. He hit the new bar below the second narrows at speed, broached, and the river took him the way the river takes everything: without malice, without ceremony, and without giving anything back. They found the wheelhouse door forty miles downstream. They found his compass three months later, wedged in a gravel bed, its needle still swinging.

I was nineteen. I inherited the debt, the boat, and the knowledge that the government's maps had killed him—and I did what sensible people do with knowledge like that. I buried it. I kept my head down, paid what could be paid, stayed small, stayed invisible, and told myself that surviving quietly was its own kind of victory. Eleven years of that. And then Halloran put a pen in my hand on a dock in the lowlands, and here I am, running his errands until his errand turned out to be a leash, and now his shadow boat is behind me with orders—I'm fairly sure of the orders, given what Brandt confessed to Pip—to leave no witnesses.

So no, I don't feel guilty about what I'm about to do to their evening.

"Odessa," I called down the hatch. "How is she?"

"She's held together with rust and spite," Odessa said, "which describes me too, so we understand each other. Give me full ahead and pray I don't give you full stop instead."

"That's the spirit."

"Felix." It was Brandt again, closer now, rain streaming off his jaw. "The gorge at night, at flood crest, without survey data—that's not navigation, that's suicide with extra steps. Even I can read these water levels, and I've spent the whole trip reading them wrong on purpose."

"There's a sentence I never thought I'd hear," Marisol murmured.

"I know this water," I said.

"You know it by day."

"I know it better than any chart does, which is the same thing as knowing it at night, because the chart doesn't know it at all." I turned and looked at him, and I made sure he saw me look. "My father died here because he believed your office's numbers. I promised myself I would never die of believing. Tonight I get to keep that promise. You're welcome to be terrified; everyone else is."

Pip snorted. Rook almost smiled. Even Ilsa, half-starved and hollow-eyed, made a sound that in a healthier woman might have been a laugh.

Behind us, the yellow eyes grew.

* * *

The mouth of the gorge is a stone throat maybe sixty meters wide in dry season. At flood crest it's wider but shallower in the wrong places and deeper in the worse ones, and the current through it runs at speeds that hydrologists describe with numbers and river people describe with religious language. The trick—which is not a trick, it's just the accumulated grief of every pilot who ever ran it—is that the safe line moves. Every hour, every flood pulse, every collapse of rock somewhere upstream rearranges the arithmetic. A pilot who learned the gorge last month is flying last month's river.

I learned it the year my father died, because grief makes some people careful and made me obsessive. I ran it eleven times that first summer alone, in a borrowed skiff, mapping the bars with a lead line and a notebook I still keep wrapped in oilcloth under my bunk. The notebook has never left the boat. The knowledge in it has never left my head. Until tonight, no living person knew either existed, because a secret like that is the only property a poor captain owns, and I am, above all things, a woman who understands property.

"Mark," I said.

Rook killed the lights. The world went absolute black except for the foam, which glowed faintly gray-green where the current tore it, enough to read the water if your eyes were good and your nerve was better. Behind us, the shadow boat's lights swung wildly—they'd been steering on ours, and now we were gone, and their helmsman had a choice between slowing down in the gorge mouth or driving blind. From the sound of their engine note dropping, they chose slow.

Good. Slow men think. Thinking men hesitate. Hesitation in the gorge is how you end up as a story other pilots tell.

"Hard to starboard," I said, and felt the wheel answer heavy in my hands, the current grabbing the rudder like a pickpocket. "Odessa—one-third. We're going to let them pass the narrows thinking we hugged the north wall."

"Why would they think that?" Pip asked.

"Because that's what the chart says the channel is," I said, "and because their surveyor—" I nodded at Brandt, who had the grace not to flinch, "—has been recording water levels all trip. Whoever's driving that boat will trust the numbers. Numbers are comfortable. Numbers don't ask you to bet your life on a dead man's daughter."

The second narrows came up faster than I wanted, because it always does, because the gorge eats distance the way the tax office eats seasons. I felt rather than saw the bar—there's a texture to the water over moved sand, a smoothness that's too smooth, a lie written on the surface—and I took us wide around it through water the chart marked as rock. The hull scraped something soft. Sand forgiving us. The old bar, migrated east, exactly where my notebook said it would be, right where the chart insisted was open channel.

If the shadow boat followed the chart, they'd find the bar themselves. If they followed us, they'd find the drain under Widow's Tooth. Either way, I intended to be elsewhere.

* * *

Here is the part I have never told anyone, the part that lives deeper than the notebook: for eleven years I have dreamed about this run. Not nightmares—dreams. In them my father stands beside me at the wheel, silent, the way he stood at the wheel in life, trusting me with the small stretches and taking the hard ones himself. In the dreams I always wake before the narrows. I never got to show him that I'd learned it. That I'd learned it better than the office that killed him. That his daughter could read moving water the way Marisol reads tracks—instinctively, totally, without translation.

Tonight the dream and the river were the same thing, and my father wasn't beside me, and I understood finally that he didn't need to be. He was under me, in the bar he'd shown me by dying on it. He was in the drain under Widow's Tooth, which he'd warned me about a hundred times in the voice fathers use for warnings they hope their children ignore forever. The river had taken him and then, in its indifferent way, had spent eleven years teaching me everything it knew about itself. That's not comfort. But it's not nothing.

"Widow's Tooth," I called. "Port side high. Feed it starboard, shallow angle, and whatever happens, nobody touches the wheel but me."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Rook said, and stepped back from the helm, and that small act—trusting me with all of it, in the dark, at flood crest—did something to my chest that I decided I could examine later, or never.

The Tooth announced itself as a pressure change, a wall of spray, a roar that climbed into a frequency you felt in your teeth. Then the drain. I gave the river exactly what it asked for—not fighting it, not yielding, the way you handle a large animal that could kill you but mostly wants to be left alone—and the boat slewed, and the stern lifted, and for one long second we were surfing on the edge of a hole in the world, and then the current spat us out the far side like a seed.

Nobody screamed. I counted that as the crew's finest professional achievement.

"Everyone alive?" I asked.

"Define alive," said Odessa, from below, over a sound I did not like: metal, complaining.

"Moving. Bleeding optionally."

"Then yes. Barely. Felix—the shaft bearing's singing. Whatever you're planning, plan it fast."

* * *

We lost them properly at the third bend, where the gorge splits around a mid-channel island that appears on no chart because officially the island doesn't exist—it surfaced in a rockfall six years ago and the survey office, being the survey office, simply declined to acknowledge it. The south channel looks like the main channel. It isn't. It ends in a quarter-mile of submerged talus that shreds hulls, and the north channel looks like a dead-end slot barely wider than the boat, because it is—but past the slot the gorge opens into a flooded side canyon that connects back to the lower river eight miles on, through water deep, slow, and utterly uncharted.

I took the slot.

It took four minutes. Those four minutes contained more concentrated terror than the rest of the voyage combined, and I include the crossing at flood crest, and I include the night I signed Halloran's paper. The walls came close enough that Rook could have touched them, close enough that the sound of our engine came back off the rock doubled, tripled, a crowd of engines. Pip lay flat on the bow with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, calling depth to me in a whisper, as though the gorge were sleeping and we were burglars. Once—only once—the hull kissed stone, a long grinding kiss along the starboard strake that Odessa answered with a word in her grandmother's language that I will not repeat but intend to learn.

And then the walls fell away, and the rain came down into open air, and we were through, drifting in black glassy water while the gorge roared behind us like a door slammed on a argument.

I put us aground gently on a gravel shelf, because the alternative was sinking, and sank we soon would anyway.

* * *

The damage, when Odessa brought the lantern down into the bilge, was honest and total. The strake grind had opened a seam. The shaft bearing was finished. We were taking water at a rate the pump managed only while the engine ran, and the engine could not run much longer, and none of it mattered anyway, because a boat is not a boat without a working hull, and ours had been unmade by a canyon wall in the dark.

"She's done," Odessa said. She said it the way a doctor says it. No drama. She put her hand flat on the stringer, the way you'd touch a body. "She got us through. That's more than I'd have bet."

I looked at my boat. My father's boat, technically, though the debt office had owned her more years than either of us. Eleven years of quiet, invisible, careful living, sitting in the rain on a gravel bar in a canyon no map admits to, listing to starboard, taking the river in through her wounds.

I found I wasn't sad. That surprised me. I found I was furious, and relieved, and lighter than I had any right to be, all at once, like a knot coming undone in the wrong direction.

"The shadow boat?" I asked.

"Gone quiet," Rook said from the rail, listening. "Either they took the bar at the narrows or they took the talus at the split. Either way they're having a much worse night than us."

"Small mercies," Marisol said. "Large ones, actually."

Ilsa Rehn stood at the rail with the rain running off her hood, looking back at the gorge, and then at me. "You knew that slot existed," she said. "It's on nothing. I've mapped this entire watershed and it's on nothing."

"It's on my chart," I said. "The only copy. Which means, as of tonight, I'm the most accurate cartographer on this river, and Ilsa, I mean that as no insult to your work."

Something moved across her exhausted face that was almost humor. "None taken. Come morning, I'd like to see it."

"You'll see it. Everyone will." I heard myself say it and realized it was true, and that some door inside me had come open with the hull. "That's rather the point of everything now, isn't it."

* * *

We gathered in the lee of the wheelhouse, seven of us and a dying boat, and I laid it out plainly, because they'd earned plain speech.

"Come dawn we walk. South bank, two days to the landing at Harrow's Mouth, where the delegates are gathering and where Halloran will be waiting, because Halloran is many things but never surprised. We carry Ilsa and the map and Brandt's testimony, and we walk into the middle of all of it, and we finish this in front of witnesses or not at all." I looked around at them—Odessa with grease to her elbows, the twins dripping, Marisol with her arms crossed, Brandt standing slightly apart the way reformed weapons do, Ilsa with her hand pressed flat over the pocket where the truth was sewn. "The boat bought us this. She's the last thing I had that was mine, and I'd call it a fair trade, which should tell you how badly I want to finish it."

"Felix," Pip said softly. "You okay?"

"No," I said. "But I'm through. Both kinds of through. Ask me again at the landing."

Marisol smiled and started, absurdly, unfailingly, to talk about food—what was left in the galley, what could be cooked on a shore fire, whether anyone had opinions about rice. Because that's what Marisol does when the world ends: she plans breakfast. And the crew began to move, and the rain went on falling on the escarpment above us, and the river ran past our wrecked home in the dark, carrying everything it carries toward the sea.

I stayed at the rail a moment longer. Somewhere upstream, under the black water, was a sandbar with my family name on it, and a compass needle pointing at nothing, faithful forever.

"Hold on, Papa," I said, to the river, to the rain, to nobody. "I'm bringing it home."

Then I went to help Marisol with the rice, because tomorrow we were walking into the teeth of the government, and nobody argues well on an empty stomach.
