## 18. What Loyalty Weighs

I have cooked for men who were dying and called it breakfast. I have stretched one salted fish into a meal for nine and made them thank me for it. I know how to feed people through a crisis. What I don't know how to do is cook for a family that's deciding whether to hand one of its own to the wolves, and that was the task in front of me on the third night after we lost the boat.

We'd dragged what we could ashore into a fisherman's lean-to half a mile below where the gorge spat us out—cracked hull, drowned engine, Odessa mourning the engine like a widow at a wake. The rain had eased to a steady gray drizzle, which on this coast counts as a holiday. Ilsa Rehn sat wrapped in two blankets near the fire pit, eating the way starving people eat: slow, deliberate, like she was relearning the shape of food. Felix stood at the edge of the lean-to with her back to all of us, watching the river, which is what Felix does instead of sleeping.

And Brandt sat apart, hands visible, saying nothing, because Pip had told us everything.

That was the poison in the pot. Not the shadow boat somewhere behind us in the dark. Not Halloran waiting at the river's mouth with his clean deal and his clean records. The poison was knowledge, and Pip was carrying it alone until last night when she finally set it down in front of all of us: Brandt transmitted our position at the relay, and the boat behind us has orders to leave no witnesses.

No witnesses. Which meant Ilsa. Which meant, if they were thorough—and government men doing dirty work are always thorough—the rest of us too.

So there we were. Six of us around a dying fire, plus one defector nobody had decided whether to forgive, and one question nobody wanted to say out loud.

I said it out loud. That's my role in this crew. Felix thinks; Odessa fixes; the twins scout and scheme; I say the thing.

"If we give them Ilsa," I said, stirring a pot of nothing much, "maybe they let the rest of us walk."

The rain ticked on the tin roof. The river muttered. Nobody looked at me, which is how I knew every single one of them had already thought it.

Good. Better said than festering.

"I'm right here," Ilsa said. Her voice was rough as river gravel but level. "You can talk about me. I've spent six weeks being talked about by dead men."

"That's not—" Rook started.

"It is exactly what we're doing." She set down her tin cup. "So let's do it properly. I'm a witness. They need me gone. Some of you might survive if you give me up. I did the arithmetic myself, lying in that cave, listening to you come up the valley. I know how it ends for me either way. The only question is whether you're the kind of people who do their arithmetic out loud."

Silence. Even the rain seemed embarrassed.

Then Odessa spoke, without looking up from the engine part she was cleaning though there was no engine left to clean. "My mother sold me to a dredging crew when I was eleven. Sold me. And the foreman who bought me worked me three years before anyone asked why a child was in the stokehold." She set the part down. "Nobody ever traded me to make their lives easier. Not once, not even when it would've been smart. I'm not starting the tradition now."

"You didn't answer the question," I said gently.

"Yes I did."

Pip went next, because Pip always goes next, right after the hard thing, to prove she isn't afraid of it. She sat cross-legged by the fire, small and sharp-faced, and she didn't look at Ilsa at all. She looked at Brandt.

"I caught him," she said. "At the relay. He was sending us to die, or he thought he was, and then he wasn't, and I had him cold and I could've—" She stopped. Started again. "I've been carrying it for two days and it's been sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook. So here's my vote. No. We don't trade her. Because the day we trade one person to save four, we stop being a crew and start being a cargo manifest. People you count. People you can lose."

"And if losing her means losing us?" I asked, because someone had to keep the question sharp.

"Then we lose," Pip said simply. "But we lose as us."

Rook put an arm around his sister's shoulders. He'd barely spoken since the gorge—near-drownings will do that, quiet a man for days—but when he talked, it came out low and certain. "When I went under at the crossing, the water had me. Properly had me. And something hit my arm like a falling tree and it was Brandt, no hesitation, no thinking about it first." He nodded across the fire. "I don't know what that makes him. But I know what it makes the rest of us if we hand a starving woman to men with guns so we can go home and sleep well. It makes us the kind of thing I've been afraid of my whole life."

"So that's three noes," I said. "Odessa, Pip, Rook. And me—I'll get to mine. Ilsa?"

"I don't get a vote," Ilsa said. "I'm the merchandise."

"You're a cartographer with a head wound and frostbitten toes who hasn't stopped working since we found you," Odessa said. "You get a vote."

Ilsa looked at the fire for a long moment. "Then no. Obviously no. But not obviously—not because I want to live. Because if they bury me, they bury the map, and the map is the only thing keeping those towns upstream free. My life is worth exactly what the paper in my coat is worth. Nothing more. I've made my peace with that arithmetic." She almost smiled. "It's the surveyor in me. Everything gets measured."

"Everything except loyalty," I said.

She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the six weeks of terror behind her eyes and the stubbornness that had carried her through it. "Those weren't measured either," she said quietly. "What you're doing. None of it adds up on any ledger I know."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't. That's rather the point."

*

Brandt cleared his throat. Every head turned. The man had spent the whole voyage being furniture with opinions, and now here he was, about to speak, hands still flat on his knees like a man at a hearing.

"You should know what I know," he said. "The unit behind us is eight men, well armed, led by a Captain Dree who does not negotiate and does not take prisoners when the orders say otherwise. They'll reach the mouth of the gorge by tomorrow night. There is no version of running that outruns them on foot. If you surrender Ilsa and claim ignorance, some of you might—"

"We heard your speech," Pip said. "Two days ago. In the relay station. You gave it better then."

"I'm giving it worse now," Brandt said, "because I'm hoping you'll interrupt me again." He paused. "For the record, since everyone's voting: no. I won't help you surrender her. I'll help you fight, if fighting comes to it. I've spent twenty years believing that obedience was the same thing as service. Watching this crew has cost me that belief, and I find I don't miss it." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and I pretended not to notice, because a man deserves his dignity while he's dismantling himself.

Four noes and a defection. Five, counting Ilsa's own.

Which left Felix, standing at the edge of the lean-to with her back to us, and I realized—with the particular clarity that comes to cooks, who spend their lives reading hunger in faces—that Felix had known this conversation was coming since the dock in the lowlands. Since Halloran put the papers in front of her. Maybe since before that.

"Felix," I said.

She didn't turn around. "I heard."

"You get a vote too."

"My vote was cast a long time ago." Still facing the river. "The day I signed Halloran's paper. I read the terms, Marisol. All of them. I knew what retrieval meant. I went anyway, because the alternative was losing the boat, and the boat was your wages, and Pip and Rook's bunks, and Odessa's engine, and your galley. I went because I decided then that if it came down to me or any of you, it would be me. Every time. That's not a vote. That's just the standing order I've been operating under since the day I hired a bunch of strays nobody else would take."

The rain filled the silence.

"You absolute fool," Odessa said, and her voice was thick. "You think we stayed for the wages?"

Felix turned around then. In the firelight her face was wet, and I will swear until I die it was only the rain leaking through the tin roof, because Felix Vane does not cry, and a crew protects its captain's fictions the way it protects its charts.

"I owe you all debts," Felix said. "My father's debts, my own, ones you don't even know about yet. I took this job to pay them and I planned to pay them alone. That was always the plan. Keep you fed, keep you ignorant, keep you safe, and stand in front of whatever came."

"Well, the plan's ruined," Pip said. "Terrible planning. You surrounded yourself with people who refuse to be kept safe."

Rook laughed—a real laugh, rusty, the first since the crossing. "Worst crew discipline I've ever seen."

"The worst," Odessa agreed, wiping her eyes with a rag that had definitely been touching engine grease.

Ilsa was watching all of us with an expression I recognized from mirrors in old kitchens: the look of someone who had forgotten what belonging felt like and was remembering it painfully, like blood returning to a numb limb.

"So that's settled," I said, and ladled out the nothing-much stew, because ritual matters, because feeding people is how I vote. "Nobody trades anybody. We're all going to the mouth of this river together, and we're going to be witnesses, all seven of us, alive and loud."

"And if Dree's men catch us first?" Brandt asked.

"Then they catch seven witnesses," I said, "and discover that a crew of strays with nothing left to lose is the most dangerous cargo on this river."

Felix finally sat down among us, took the bowl I handed her, and ate. And I watched her do it—watched this woman who had spent years making herself invisible, unmissable, untouchable, sit inside a circle of people who had just refused, one by one, to survive without her—and I understood something I'd been circling since the dock.

She hadn't gathered us because she needed a crew.

She'd gathered us because she'd already decided to die for somebody, someday, and she'd wanted to choose worthy somebodies.

Well. She'd chosen. And tomorrow we'd walk into Halloran's landing, all of us, and show her how wrong she'd been about who pays whose debts.

Above the trees, far downstream, a signal flare went up—white, then red, then white again. Dree's men, announcing themselves to each other in the dark.

Closer than Brandt had guessed.

Felix set down her empty bowl and stood, and the tiredness fell off her like a dropped coat.

"Get some sleep," she said. "All of you. Tomorrow we stop running."
