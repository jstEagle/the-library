## 19. The Truth at the Landing

I have run from exactly one thing in my life, and I ran for eleven years, and it did not work. So when we came down out of the high country with the river in flood behind us and Halloran's shadow boat somewhere on our wake, I did not run. I stood on the scorched deck of my dying boat and told my crew something I'd been rehearsing since the relay station, and it came out steadier than I expected.

"We're going to the landing."

Marisol looked at me like I'd suggested swimming to the sea. "Felix. There are soldiers there."

"There are witnesses there," I said. "That's the whole point."

Pip had that look—the one where the freckles bunch up and you can see a person doing arithmetic they don't want to do. "You're going to read it. Out loud. To everyone."

"Ilsa's documents don't do any good in my pocket," I said. "In my pocket, they're evidence against me. Read aloud in front of forty river delegates and their clerks and their cousins and somebody's grandmother selling meat pies off a cart—" I shrugged. "Then they're just true. And truth is harder to confiscate."

Odessa wiped her hands on a rag that had long ago given up being clean. "The engine won't take us anywhere fast anyway. You know that."

"I know that."

"So this isn't tactics." She said it flatly, not accusing. Odessa never accused; she diagnosed.

"No," I said. "This is what's left when tactics run out."

Rook was at the rail, watching the brown water slide past, and didn't turn around when he spoke. "My brother died in a place like this. Not this river. A different one. Same kind of men, though." He turned then, and his face was calm in a way that scared me more than shouting would have. "If we're going to do this, do it loud. Don't let them edit us."

* * *

We came around the last bend an hour before dusk, and the landing opened up below us like a held breath.

Salt Landing wasn't much—a shelf of timber and stone where the river widened and the escarpment finally let go of its grip—but today it was full. The delegates' assembly happened every few years whether anyone wanted it or not, and by catastrophic luck or Halloran's design, it was now. Barges moored three deep. Lanterns already lit against the coming rain. A crowd moving along the boardwalks: men in delegate gray, clerks with document cases chained to their wrists, women from the fishery co-ops, dredge crews still muddy from the bars, a knot of survey school students who'd come downriver to gawk at government pomp.

And at the head of the main pier, under a canopy that some clerk had probably ordered erected to keep the sun off official paperwork, stood Halloran.

He saw us coming. Of course he did. His shadow boat had been reporting our position since the gorge, and I imagine he'd spent the last day arranging the stage—because that's what it was. He'd have preferred to take Ilsa quietly at some lonely bend, but if quiet failed, an audience was nearly as good. A captain arrested for piracy at a public landing looks guilty. Guilty is useful.

He raised one hand as we came in—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment between professionals. I hated how much I understood it.

"Bring her in slow," I told Odessa. "Port side to the far pier. Not his pier."

"Far pier puts us two hundred yards from the canopy," she said.

"Two hundred yards is a speech," I said. "I've checked."

We came in with the current doing most of the work because the engine couldn't, and the hull groaned the whole way like an old man kneeling, and when we kissed the pilings the sound ran through the boat like a verdict. Pip threw lines. Rook stood at the gangway looking deliberately unthreatening, which for Rook meant standing very still. Marisol put herself between Ilsa and the rail without being asked, the way she'd been doing for days, a wall made of flour-dusted forearms and stubbornness.

Halloran's men were already moving along the boardwalk. Six of them, then eight, rifles slung but hands near, spreading out with the unhurried confidence of people who'd been told the paperwork was handled. Behind them, at the canopy, Halloran descended the pier steps onto the open boards of the landing proper, and I watched him make his calculation: too many eyes to shoot, not yet enough to stop him walking over.

So he walked over.

"Felix Vane." He stopped at the foot of our gangway, hands clasped behind him, and gave me the smile he used on people he genuinely liked, which was somehow worse than the other one. "You look terrible."

"Monsoon does that."

"You were supposed to bring me one survivor and a sealed case." His voice stayed pleasant, pitched just for me, while his eyes catalogued my crew, my wrecked boat, the absence of Brandt at my shoulder. "Instead I get a damaged vessel, a fugitive cartographer, and a crew standing around looking loyal. Do you know how loyal looks from where I stand? It looks like conspiracy."

"It looks like a crew," I said. "You wouldn't recognize it."

Something flickered across his face—real regret, maybe, or its well-maintained impersonation. "Felix. Come down here. Just you. We'll talk about your father's debts. We'll talk about the other thing, the thing you think I only suspect." He lowered his voice further. "Walk away from this, and by tomorrow morning there will be no record of Felix Vane anywhere in the archives. Clean paper. A woman who never owed anyone anything. You could start over."

Eleven years I'd wanted exactly that. I could feel it like heat off a stove—the erasure of every mistake, every name I'd signed that wasn't mine, the whole architecture of shame I'd built my invisibility on. All he was asking in exchange was one exhausted woman who trusted no one, and four strangers' silence.

"That's the offer?" I said. "Same as on the wire?"

"The same. It doesn't expire, but it should."

"Then here's my counteroffer." I turned to face the landing, filled my lungs with river air and terror in equal parts, and shouted—not to Halloran, not to his soldiers, but to the crowd. "DELEGATES OF THE SALT LANDING ASSEMBLY! You'll want to hear this before your evening session! It concerns the upper river, the emergency powers act, and land most of you hold title to!"

The crowd turned the way crowds do—slowly, then all at once. A clerk frowned. A fishery woman set down her basket. Somebody's grandmother stopped selling meat pies mid-transaction, which I took as a good omen.

"Felix," Halloran said quietly, and the pleasantness was gone. "Don't."

"Captain Vane!" A voice from the crowd—a heavyset man in delegate gray with a chain of office I recognized from customs forms: Alderman Brac, who'd hated the Survey Bureau since they fined his dredge operation into the mud. "On whose authority do you address this assembly?"

"On nobody's," I called back. "That's rather the point. But I'm holding documents bearing the Bureau's own seal, and I'd hate for them to be lost in a boating accident. I've heard those happen."

A ripple of laughter, ugly and nervous. Halloran's jaw tightened.

"Those documents are stolen government property," he said loudly, turning to the crowd, recovering beautifully, the bureaucrat's instinct for a podium asserting itself even without one. "This woman is a smuggler under investigation. Everything she says is—"

"—is verifiable," I finished. "Because I'm going to read it slowly, and your clerks can copy it down, and half of you have friends in the upper territories whose names appear on these pages. Delegate Osei—you hold grazing rights above Kettle Bend, yes? Then you'll want to hear paragraph three."

I climbed onto the rail of my ruined boat, which was insane, and Marisol braced my elbow without making a show of it, and I unfolded Ilsa's papers with hands that shook until I made them stop.

I read.

I read the funding authorization: emergency powers invoked, survey costs drawn against the Territorial Security line, not the Mapping Office. I read the directive appended beneath it in Halloran's own departmental hand—the team was to chart the upper valleys and file no final report from the field; personnel would be "accounted for administratively upon conclusion." Accounted for. Four words that meant graves with no names on them. I read the mineral seam assessment, the one the survey had found and someone upstairs had decided the river could never be allowed to find: a seam rich enough to reprice every barge, every dock, every fishing right from the escarpment to the sea—and scheduled, per the annex, for seizure under emergency jurisdiction, compensation to existing titleholders to be determined after the fact by the seizing authority.

"After the fact," I read aloud, letting the words sit in the wet air. "Meaning after they take it. Meaning after they take it from you."

The crowd had gone very quiet. The bad kind of quiet, the kind with weight.

"Those are forgeries," Halloran said, but he said it to Brac, not to the crowd, and Brac was already reading the page a clerk had copied, lips moving.

"They're originals," said a new voice, dry and level, and Brandt stepped out from behind the fish co-op warehouse where he'd been standing for the last hour, hat in his hands like a man arriving late to his own funeral. "I countersigned two of them myself. I'm Surveyor Brandt, seconded to Captain Vane's expedition. The directives are authentic. The seizure schedule is authentic. And the survey team was not lost." He paused, and I watched him spend something he'd never get back. "They were expendable. I helped plan an expedition designed to fail. I am prepared to say so under oath, in writing, in whatever form this assembly finds binding."

Halloran looked at Brandt for a long moment. What passed between them wasn't hatred. It was worse—an acknowledgment, officer to officer, that one of them had broken and both of them knew why.

"You understand," Halloran said to him, almost gently, "what you've done to your career."

"I've done to my career what your bureau did to six surveyors," Brandt said. "Accounted for it administratively."

And that was when the soldier nearest the gangway shifted his rifle sling forward—not aiming, not yet, just adjusting, the way a man adjusts when he's been waiting hours for permission—and the whole landing seemed to lean toward that small motion like water toward a hole in a boat.

Rook moved first. Not toward the soldier—away, out to the end of the pier, arms spread wide, putting himself in the sightline between rifle and crowd. Pip was beside him a heartbeat later. Then Marisol, planting herself at the gangway foot with her fists on her hips like the landing owed her money. My strays. My idiots. Forming up around a truth like it was a hull taking water.

The soldier looked at his sergeant. The sergeant looked at the crowd—at the grandmother with her pie cart, at the fishery women, at forty delegates and twice as many clerks with copying hands—and I watched him do the arithmetic that armies pretend doesn't exist: orders versus witnesses. Rifles fire in seconds. Reputations burn for decades, and his superiors' reputations were already burning, and no sergeant alive volunteers to add fuel in front of an audience.

He slung the rifle back.

"Stand down," he said, to his own men, quietly, as if hoping Halloran wouldn't hear.

Halloran heard. I watched him take it in—the soldiers, the clerks scribbling, Brac reading the seizure schedule aloud now in a voice built for auction houses, the crowd pressing closer instead of dispersing—and I watched him arrive, with what looked like genuine professional sorrow, at the same conclusion I'd sailed eleven years to reach: there was nothing left here to win quietly.

"Captain Vane." He straightened his coat. "When the inquiry convenes, I'll cooperate fully. You have my word."

"Your word," I said. "From the man whose directives I'm holding."

"Yes." And he smiled, tired and real, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a leashed dog than a man who'd finally been taken off one. "You should know—I liked you, Felix. From the dock, that first day. I chose you because you were good, and I told myself that was the reason." He glanced at the papers in Brac's hands. "It seems I was wrong about which of us was the instrument."

He walked back up the pier through the parting crowd, and nobody stopped him, and nobody thanked him, and the lanterns came on along the boardwalk one district at a time like the landing exhaling.

Brac climbed onto a crate and started calling the assembly into informal session, right there, in the rain that had just begun to fall, and clerks unchained their cases, and the grandmother resumed selling pies at double volume because crisis is good for business.

I climbed down off the rail. My legs informed me they had been operating on credit and were calling the loan.

Marisol caught my arm. "You did it," she said. "You actually—"

"I read some papers out loud," I said. My voice cracked on the last word, which I pretended was the rain.

Ilsa came down the gangway slowly, wrapped in Odessa's coat, and stood beside me looking at the crowd, at the copies multiplying hand to hand, at her dead colleagues' findings finally breathing open air. She didn't cry. She just closed her eyes for a moment, the way you do when a pain you've carried so long it's become structural suddenly sets down.

"It's not over," she said. "An inquiry means hearings. Means lawyers. Means they'll come for your license, your boat, everything you—"

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway."

Behind us, the *Marguerite's Pride*—my father's boat, my father's debts, my whole crooked inheritance—settled against the pilings and groaned, and I put my hand on the rail and felt the shudder come up through the wood like a pulse getting weaker.

Pip appeared at my elbow, rain-soaked, grinning and crying at once, which is a look only nineteen-year-olds can manage. "Felix. They're chanting your name."

"They're chanting the documents' name," I said. "I'm just the loud one."

But they were chanting, and the rain was falling, and somewhere across the water Halloran's shadow boat sat dark and empty at its moorings, its work finished whether it knew it or not. I stood on the deck of a condemned ship in front of a crowd that loved me for exactly as long as the news cycle, and felt the strangest thing underneath the exhaustion:

Not triumph. Relief. Eleven years of looking over my shoulder, and the thing behind me had finally stepped out into the light where I could see its face.

Which is when Odessa came out of the engine room with a wrench in one hand and an expression I'd learned to dread, and said, "Captain? We need to talk about the hull."
