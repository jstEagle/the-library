## 20. Ashes of the Compass

The hearing took four hours. The verdict took eleven minutes to read aloud, and I counted every one of them because counting was the only thing keeping me from laughing.

*Condemned.* The *Vespera*, my father's boat, my boat, the only address I'd had in nine years—condemned by a tribunal of three men who had never run water rougher than a harbor ferry. Hull compromised below the waterline at frame fourteen. Stress fractures in the keel shoe. Irreparable damage sustained during, and here the clerk's voice went flat and official, "the subject vessel's unauthorized nocturnal transit of the Kestrel Gorge."

Unauthorized. As if the river had posted office hours. As if the shadow boat full of men with orders to leave no witnesses had filed a float plan.

I sat in the gallery between Marisol and Odessa while they read it out, and I kept my hands flat on my knees like a well-behaved woman, because if I didn't give them something to do they were going to start applauding or strangling someone, and I hadn't decided which.

"Pending further inquiry," the chairman added, almost as an afterthought, "the captain's license of Felix Vane is suspended."

Suspended. Not revoked. Suspended, pending inquiry, which is bureaucratic weather-vane language meaning: *we don't know yet whether you're a hero or a problem, so we'll keep you in a drawer until it's decided for us.*

I thanked the tribunal. My voice came out level. Somewhere in the last month I'd learned to do that—thank the people dismantling your life in a tone that suggests you might send them a fruit basket.

Outside the customs house, the rain had stopped for the first time in days, and the light on the wet street was that particular lowland gold that always looked like an apology the sky never quite finished making.

"Well," said Odessa.

"Well," I agreed.

"That was garbage," said Pip.

"Pip," said Rook.

"It *was*. Frame fourteen is fine. I looked at frame fourteen. Frame fourteen has opinions about how fine it is."

"The hull's done," I said, and hated how easily the words came. "You all know it's done. She took the gorge at night at flood crest. No hull comes back from that. The tribunal just wrote down what we already knew."

Nobody argued, which was its own kind of kindness. We stood there on the steps while clerks and delegates streamed past us into the building where the future of the upper river was being argued over in committee rooms that smelled like ink and carpet. Delegates from Harrow Bend. From the netting towns. From places upriver whose names I'd only ever known as sandbars and deadfalls. They'd come because of what I'd read out loud on the landing three weeks ago, and now they were writing new charters, and I was standing outside the door like a dog tied to a rail.

Marisol put her hand on my shoulder. She didn't say anything. Marisol's silences were better than most people's speeches, and she knew it, and she deployed them accordingly.

"I need to go look at her," I said finally. "Before they tow her."

* * *

They'd beached the *Vespera* on the mud flats below the breaker yard, hauled up on skids like a whale nobody wanted to claim. The survey crews had already been through her—chalk marks on the planking, orange tags fluttering off anything worth salvaging. Her name was still painted on the stern, though the last storm had peeled the gold leaf until it read *VESP RA*, and I thought there was something honest in that. A boat missing her heart.

I walked her length alone. The crew let me. That was another kindness; they were learning when to leave me in a room with my grief.

The wheelhouse still smelled like us. Like Marisol's pepper stew soaked into the wood, like Odessa's engine oil tracked in on her boots, like the twins' contraband card games and the smoke Brandt had never once complained about even in the weeks after he'd stopped being our enemy and started being—whatever he'd become. Something without a clean word for it. The compass was still bolted to the binnacle, and I put my hand on it the way I used to put my hand on my father's shoulder when I was small enough that his shoulder was the whole horizon.

"You did it," I told the boat. "Whatever they write down. You did it."

The tide was coming in under the flats, and the *Vespera* shifted on her skids with a groan I knew better than most voices I've loved. Boats talk. People just stop listening.

That was where Ilsa found me.

She came down the flats in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, walking carefully on the mud like a woman who'd spent too long on solid rock and forgotten how ground moved. Which, I supposed, she had. Six weeks in a high valley cave will do that to a person. So will watching your colleagues die one at a time while you carry a satchel of documents that could start a war or end one, depending entirely on who read them first.

"They're condemning her," she said. Not a question. News traveled fast in a town that size, especially news that cost somebody something.

"Frame fourteen," I said. "Irreparable."

"It's not frame fourteen."

"No," I agreed. "It's not frame fourteen."

She came and stood beside me at the rail, looking down the length of the deck the way you'd look at a body. "My father had a boat," she said. "A little gill-netter, before the surveys took him inland. He used to say a boat isn't property. It's a witness. It remembers everything you did on the water, and it tells the next person who owns it, if they know how to listen." She paused. "This one would have a lot to say."

"She'd exaggerate," I said. "Boats always do. Make the storms bigger and the landings sweeter."

Ilsa smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen from her that reached all the way up. On the river she'd smiled like a woman rationing something scarce. Down here, with the documents public and the seizure plan dead in committee and her name cleared in every paper from the mouth to the escarpment, she could afford to spend one.

"Felix. I want to show you something."

"If it's more documents, I've had my fill."

"It's a letter of commission." She pulled it from inside the coat—a folded sheet, real paper, sealed with the delegate council's stamp. New wax. New authority. "The council voted yesterday. There's going to be a new survey of the upper territories. Ours. Funded openly, published completely, every chart and finding released to the river towns within the year. No emergency powers. no sealed annexes. Everything." She held it out. "And they need a pilot who knows the water the way the old maps never did. Someone who knows every sandbar the government charts missed on purpose."

I looked at the letter and didn't take it.

"You're cleared," I said. "Your name's clean. You could work anywhere. Why come ask a woman with a suspended license and a condemned boat?"

"Because you read the journal page and pocketed it instead of handing it to Brandt. Because you ran the gorge at night rather than lead those men to her. Because your crew stood on a mud flat and refused to trade a stranger's life for their own." She shrugged inside the enormous coat. "Because everything I watched you do for two months is exactly the kind of judgment a survey needs and a bureaucracy can't teach."

"The license—"

"Is suspended, not revoked. Pending inquiry. And the inquiry panel includes two delegates whose towns exist right now because of what you exposed. Do the arithmetic."

I did the arithmetic. I was good at arithmetic; it's most of what running a river is. But numbers weren't the problem. The problem was standing behind me on the mud in the shape of a dead boat, and ahead of me in the shape of a life I'd spent nine years making sure I'd never have to live again—the visible life, the named life, the life where people know who you are and what you did and where to find you.

I'd built the *Vespera*'s route specifically to stay small. Small ports, small cargo, small debts. Invisible. That was the whole architecture of my survival, laid plank by plank after the thing I'd done back east, after the fire and the names in the newspaper and the long slow swim away from my own history. And then Halloran had found the loose thread anyway, and pulled, and the whole garment had come apart in my hands—and the strange, terrible truth was that once it was off, I could breathe.

Halloran. I should say something about Halloran, since everyone keeps asking.

He vanished. That's the whole story, and it's the most Halloran story imaginable. One week he was giving testimony to the inquiry, smooth and reasonable, genuinely regretful in that way of his that made you want to hit him precisely because it wasn't fake. The next week his office was empty, his files reassigned, his name absent from the reorganization memos. He'd been absorbed. Somewhere in the ministry's vast gray digestion there was a desk with his name on it, or soon would be, and he'd sit at it and approve freight schedules until retirement, and no one would ever say the word *escarpment* to him again. They didn't even charge him. He'd built the machine but he'd built it deniably, and the machine protected its parts.

I hated him for it. Then I caught myself envying him. Then I hated myself for that, and gave up on the whole emotional project as a bad investment.

"He'll surface somewhere," Ilsa said, when I mentioned it. "Men like that always do."

"Not on my river."

"No," she agreed. "Not on yours."

*Yours.* She said it like it was settled fact, and I turned back to the boat because it was easier than turning around.

* * *

The crew was waiting at the breaker yard gate when I came back up the flats, all five of them, arranged along the fence rail like a jury that had already deliberated. Brandt stood slightly apart, which was habit now more than necessity. His own inquiry had gone differently—suspended pay, sealed testimony, a future that depended on how much of his orders he put in writing. He'd put it all in writing. Every page. He'd told me once, on the flats at flood crest with Rook coughing river water out of his lungs, that obedience was a debt you could only repay by refusing it the next time it came due. Apparently he meant it.

"Well?" said Odessa.

"She offered me a job," I said.

Pip's head came up like a bird dog's. "What kind of job?"

"A survey job. Upriver. Full publication, open charts, the works." I looked at each of them in turn. "It doesn't include a boat. Mine's chalk-marked and tagged. It doesn't include a license, technically, until the inquiry clears. And it definitely doesn't include any of you, because none of you signed up to spend another monsoon above the gorge, and if you did sign up for that you're idiots, and I say that with love."

Silence. The breaker yard clanked behind us—men stripping copper off a derelict trawler, the sound of boats being unmade.

Rook spoke first, which surprised me. Rook rarely spoke first about anything except food. "What's she paying?"

"Rook," said Marisol.

"What? I'm asking practical questions. Somebody has to. Captain's having a moment."

"It's fair," I admitted. "Survey rates. Better than cargo, honestly."

"And the boat situation," Odessa said slowly, in the voice she used when she was three steps ahead of everyone and being polite about it, "is a problem you'd solve how? Exactly? Since you've clearly already solved it, because you've got the face you get when you've done math you don't like."

"I haven't solved anything. I was going to tell you all to go home."

"Home," repeated Pip.

"To your lives. The ones you put on hold for this. Marisol's got a sister in the delta. Odessa, you've had offers from the ferry line since before any of this happened. Twins, your mother—" 

"Our mother sold the house," Rook said. "While we were upriver. Sent a letter care of the harbor office. Very sweet about it."

I closed my mouth.

"We pooled it," Odessa said, and nodded at Brandt, who reached into his coat and produced a bank draft with the reluctance of a man producing a live frog. "All of us. What we saved, what the council paid out in witness fees, what Brandt had left when he decided he wasn't going to need a bribe fund anymore. It's not much. It's a hull."

"A hull," I said.

"A *battered* hull," Pip corrected, grinning now, unable to help herself. "Down at the third slip. A former coastal freighter called the *Maren Lee* that sank at her moorings twice, which Odessa says is a feature, because anything that's sunk twice and floated again knows things."

"Odessa says that?" I looked at her.

"Any hull that's been drowned and came back," Odessa said, "has already survived the worst day of its life. Same as people." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "We bought it this morning. Before the hearing. While you were in there getting condemned."

"You bought a boat before you knew—"

"We knew," Marisol said simply. "We knew how you'd walk out of there. We've known since the landing."

I stood there on the mud with the wind coming off the water and five people looking at me like I was the one who needed rescuing, and I understood, finally, the thing Ilsa's letter had been circling and the tribunal had missed entirely. They could condemn the *Vespera*. They could suspend a piece of laminated paper with my name on it. They could dissolve Halloran into the bureaucracy like sugar into black coffee. But they couldn't touch this—this ragged, stubborn, unpaid-for arrangement of people who had watched me sacrifice everything I had and responded by deciding it was contagious.

"You're all fired," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, which ruined it completely.

"Accepted," said all five of them, more or less in unison, and Pip started laughing, and then Rook, and then it was over, we were all of us laughing on the breaker yard mud like lunatics while the salvage men stared.

* * *

I went back to the *Vespera* alone at dusk, one last time, because some accounts you settle in private.

The tide had come all the way in and lapped at her skids, and she rocked a little, restless, wanting the water. I climbed aboard and went to the wheelhouse and unbolted the compass from the binnacle with Odessa's screwdriver, which I'd stolen from her kit years ago and never returned, and which she'd pretend not to notice me keeping.

The compass was old brass, older than me, older than my father probably. Its glass was clouded and the needle swung lazy and drunk, the way needles do near iron and grief. I'd learned navigation on this instrument. I'd learned that a compass doesn't point home—it points north, and home is a decision you make afterward, over and over, every time you read it.

I carried it down the gangplank and stood there on the flats while the light went out of the sky, and behind me the *Vespera* settled on her skids with a long creak, saying whatever boats say at the end. *Witness,* Ilsa's father would have called her. She'd witnessed everything. The debts and the blackmail and the gorge at night, the journal page in my pocket and the truth read out loud to a crowd with guns in it. She'd carried us all through it and come apart doing it, which is more than most witnesses manage.

"Burn her or break her?" the breaker foreman asked, coming out with his ledger. "Condemned hulls go either way. Fire's quicker. Breaking pays a little scrap."

I looked at her a long time. Gold leaf peeling. Chalk marks. Orange tags.

"Break her," I said. "Sell the fittings to river families. Somebody else's kid should learn knots on her cleats." I turned the compass over in my hands. "But the bell comes with me. That's not negotiable."

He wrote it down without argument. Everyone, these days, seemed reluctant to argue with me. I hadn't decided how I felt about that.

I walked up the flats toward the third slip, where a battered hull sat low in the dark water with five sets of lantern light already glowing aboard it, and the monsoon clouds were stacking up over the escarpment far to the north, right on schedule, indifferent and magnificent, and the needle in my hand steadied at last as I turned it toward them.

North, it said. Home, I decided.
