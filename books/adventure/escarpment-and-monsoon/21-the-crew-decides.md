## 21. The Crew Decides

I have rebuilt engines in floodwater, in the dark, with a wrench held between my teeth and a prayer I don't believe in on my lips. None of it prepared me for the particular misery of watching Felix Vane try to be alone.

She was bad at it. That's the thing nobody tells you about proud people — they're terrible at solitude because they've never practiced. They practice being needed, and then when the needing stops, they stand around like an engine missing its flywheel, spinning fast and going nowhere.

Three weeks after the landing, after the inquiry board had taken her license "pending review" — which is bureaucratic for *sit down and be small* — she took a room above a chandlery in Saltmere and told us, very reasonably, that we should all get on with our lives.

We told her, also very reasonably, to go to hell.

Well. Marisol told her that. The rest of us were politer about it, but the sentiment was unanimous, and if there's one thing I've learned from four years of keeping a boat alive under a captain who treats machinery like a temperamental relative, it's that unanimity is rare and you build on it.

* * *

The money came together the way river money always does: in small, damp increments, each one carrying a story nobody wanted fully told.

Pip went first. Pip always goes first; it's a twin thing, Rook hangs back until Pip has tested the ice. She came to my workshop — I'd rented a corner of a dry-dock shed where I was doing contract repairs for fishermen who couldn't tell a fuel line from a fishing line — and she put a canvas pouch on my bench.

"That's everything," she said.

I looked at the pouch. It did not clink. It made more of a soft, apologetic thud, like a bird hitting a window.

"Pip. That's not enough to buy a rowboat."

"It's not supposed to be enough. It's supposed to be a start." She hopped up onto a crate of spare pistons and swung her feet like a kid, which she technically wasn't anymore, though the courts of the river towns ran on their own calendar. "Rook's got some too. And Marisol sold her knives."

That stopped me. Marisol's knives were not cutlery. They were a collection assembled over years, traded for, won, inherited from a grandmother who had apparently been a terrifying woman, and Marisol sharpened them every Sunday morning like other people attend church. Selling them was not a financial decision. It was an amputation.

"She what?"

"Said she can always get new knives. Said captains are harder to come by." Pip shrugged, but her eyes weren't joking. Nothing about Pip is ever fully joking; the joke is just the paint over the wood. "Odessa. She's not eating. Have you seen her? She sits in that room and reads the inquiry transcripts like they're going to change if she reads them enough times."

I had seen it. That was the problem. I'd been bringing bread by the chandlery every second day and leaving it on her step like feeding a half-wild cat, and every time the bread was gone by morning and there was no note, because Felix Vane would rather starve than say thank you out loud. Gratitude, for her, was a debt, and debts were the thing that had broken her life open twice now — once with her father, once with Halloran. She kept a ledger in her head and hated owing anyone anything, and here we were, four people preparing to hand her the largest entry she'd ever have to carry.

Which meant the trick wasn't giving her a boat. Any fool could give her a boat. The trick was making sure she understood she wouldn't owe us, because you can't owe family. Family isn't a transaction. Family is the thing that happens when transactions fail.

I didn't know how to explain that yet. I'm an engineer. I know how things hold together, not how to tell people they do.

"I'll match whatever you two have," I said. "Between the three of us and Marisol, maybe we can afford something with a hull and no engine. We'll worry about the engine after."

"The engine's the expensive part," Pip said.

"Everything's the expensive part. Welcome to boats." I wiped my hands on a rag that had been clean sometime during the previous administration. "Go find out what Marisol got for the knives. If it was less than they're worth, someone at that pawnshop owes me a conversation."

* * *

The hull we found was, generously speaking, a hull. It sat on the mudflats below Saltmere's lower yards, listing hard to port, its name scraped off the stern by some previous owner with a grudge or a conscience. Forty feet if she was lucky, riveted plates gone orange with rust along the waterline, deck planks warped into a topography that would have interested Ilsa Rehn. There was a hole in the port side amidships you could have put a barrel through, and judging by the weed growing inside the cabin, someone had tried.

The broker was a man named Coss, who had the honest face of a man who had long ago decided honesty was a luxury good he couldn't afford.

"Salvage," he said, patting the rail affectionately, dislodging a flake of rust the size of a saucer. "Sound bones, though. Good lines. She was a working girl in her day."

"She was a working girl in somebody's day," Rook said. Rook had come along because Pip refused to inspect anything without him, and because twins who grew up diving wrecks develop an instinct for which wrecks are lying to them. He crouched at the waterline, ran his thumb along a seam, and looked up at me with the expression he reserved for engines that had been run without oil. "Plates are thin here. Here too. She's been aground more than she's been afloat."

"She's a project," Coss said.

"She's a coffin with ambitions," Rook said.

Coss smiled like we'd complimented him. "Forty marks."

"Twelve," I said.

This went on for two hours. This is the part of boat-buying nobody writes songs about: the haggling, the tea that tastes like the harbor, the broker's tragic tales of maintenance costs he never actually incurred. But somewhere in the middle of it, while Rook was tapping plates and Pip was crawling through the bilge with a lamp, calling out findings in the flat voice she used when she didn't want Coss to hear hope, I stood on that rotten deck and felt it.

Not the deck. The feeling. The one where a wreck stops being a wreck and starts being a boat, the way a pile of parts stops being parts and starts being an engine the moment you decide it will run. I'd spent my whole life on the mechanical version of that moment. Standing there, I finally understood the human one.

Felix had looked at four strays — a disgraced engineer, two orphaned dock-rats, a cook with a warrant in three territories — and decided we were a crew. Not a liability. A crew. She'd never once acted like we cost her anything, even when we demonstrably did, even when my engine failures stranded us and the twins ate through the stores and Marisol feuded with every customs officer between here and the escarpment. She'd just adjusted course and carried on, and she'd done it so smoothly that none of us noticed we'd been adopted until it was too late to object.

Twenty-two marks. That's what we paid, plus Coss throwing in a tarpaulin "out of respect for the dead," by which he meant his margins. We shook on it, and Pip wrote her name on the bill of sale with a flourish, and then we all stood around looking at our purchase the way new parents look at a screaming infant: with love, terror, and the dawning realization of how much work this was going to be.

"We need an engine," Rook said.

"We need a miracle with a crankshaft," I said. "Fortunately, I know a man."

"You are the man."

"I am the man. Get me a barge and six weeks."

* * *

Marisol found us at the dry-dock the next morning. She came down the ramp carrying her knife roll — empty now, the leather gone pale where the blades used to sit — and a crate of provisions, and she looked at the hull for a long moment with her arms crossed.

"It's ugly," she said.

"It's affordable," I said.

"It's both. God help us." She set the crate down and climbed aboard, testing the deck the way she tested everything, with her weight and her skepticism. Then she stopped at the wheelhouse, laid her palm flat against the frame, and was quiet in a way Marisol almost never was. "It smells like her boat. A little. Rust and diesel and old rain."

"Felix's boat is at the bottom of the gorge," Pip said gently. "Along with most of our savings, if we're honest about the accounting."

"I know where it is." Marisol turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, which for Marisol was the same as crying. "I helped sink it. We all did. Every plank of that run was a choice, and I'd make every one again, and I still want to hit something about it. Both things are true. Nobody tells you that either."

Nobody tells you a lot of things. I was starting a list.

* * *

The naming argument lasted four days and nearly ended two friendships, which for a committee of five people — Felix abstaining, on the grounds that she claimed not to be part of this and we let her maintain the fiction because it was kinder than arguing — was better than average.

Marisol wanted something fierce. *Vengeance* was proposed and rejected on legal advice, mine. *The Unrepentant* was proposed by Pip and rejected on the grounds that customs officers have no sense of humor and we'd be dealing with them weekly.

Rook wanted something practical. "Name her for what she does. *Riverwright.* *Current.* Something a harbormaster can shout without embarrassment."

But it was Pip, on the fourth night, sitting cross-legged on the new deck planks I'd laid that week, who said the thing that ended it.

"We should name her for them," she said. "The survey team. The ones who died so Ilsa could carry the map out."

We all looked at her.

"There were six of them," Pip said. She'd gotten the names from Ilsa, back at the landing, when the two of them had sat up all night going over documents. Pip remembers everything; it's her curse and her weapon. "Anselm Voss, the team lead. Teo Brandt — no relation to ours, before anyone asks, I checked, it spooked me too. Kess Ondar, the geologist. Lira Senn, the medic. Dov Marchetti, the boatman who got them up there. And Wren Halloway, the youngest, twenty-three years old, who's in the grave at the camp because the others buried her proper even while they knew what was coming for them." Pip's voice stayed level, but her hands had gone still in her lap, and stillness in Pip is louder than shouting. "Six people the government sent up a river knowing they might not come down, so it could take land that was never theirs. Everybody at the landing heard about the plan. Nobody says their names. It's been months and nobody says their names."

The harbor slapped against the pilings. Somewhere across the water a bar band was murdering a folk song, and the smell of frying fish came off the quay, and it was ordinary evening in Saltmere, and none of us moved.

"So we say them," Pip said. "Every day, on the transom, painted where every customs officer and every delegate and every kid skipping stones can read it. Six names. Let the government look at its own paperwork every time it boards us."

*Voss*, I thought. *Ondar*. *Senn*. Names that would ride the water instead of rotting in a file.

"Too long," Rook said, but softly, the way you concede. "All six won't fit on a forty-foot stern."

"One will," Marisol said. "*Halloway.* The youngest. The one they buried proper. Let her be the figurehead."

And that was that. Some arguments end in votes; the good ones end in silence that everyone understands the same way.

* * *

We launched her six weeks later, on a gray morning with the monsoon season still a month out but the air already tasting of it, that green metallic edge the river gets before the rains. I'd rebuilt the engine from three wrecks and stubbornness — a hundred-and-twenty horsepower of mismatched cylinders that ran rough at idle and smooth as silk under load, which I considered a personality trait rather than a flaw. The hull plates Rook had patched were ugly as scar tissue and twice as tough. Marisol had stocked the galley. Pip had painted the name herself, white letters tall as her forearm:

***HALLOWAY***

Felix came down to the quay because Marisol kidnapped her, essentially — showed up at the chandlery with two porters and a story about a delivery Felix needed to sign for, and the delivery was herself, deposited on the dock with her bag and her father's compass and an expression of profound betrayal.

"You could have just asked," she said.

"You'd have said no," Marisol said.

"I'd have said no," Felix agreed.

"Then kidnapping was the correct engineering solution," I said, and offered her the gangplank.

She didn't take it right away. She stood there looking at the boat — at the patched plates, the mismatched funnel cap, the fresh white letters — and I watched her do the arithmetic she couldn't help doing, the ledger in her head, four people's savings converted into rusted iron, the debt column climbing with every rivet. I watched her start to refuse. I saw the words form.

So I said, before she could: "Before you calculate anything, captain, understand the terms. You're not buying passage. You're not borrowing. There's no debt on this boat. We checked the hull personally." I nodded at Rook, who grinned. "You're crew. Crew doesn't owe crew. Crew shows up. That's the whole contract. If you need it in writing, Marisol will cook it onto a pie."

"That's not how ownership works," Felix said hoarsely.

"It's exactly how ownership works. Four signatures, one share each, and the fifth share—" Pip stepped forward and pressed the ship's papers into Felix's hands, folded around something else: a page from the bill of sale, the fifth line left blank except for one word written in Pip's careful hand. *Captain.*

"—is yours, and always was, and the only payment we ever want is that you keep pointing her upriver."

Felix Vane, who had stared down Halloran on a public dock, who had run the gorge at night over her father's grave, who had burned her own life down to ash to tell the truth — that woman stood on the Saltmere quay and cried in front of us. Just briefly. Just enough that we all pretended to check the rigging, and Marisol loudly declared the tide wouldn't wait, and Rook cast off the lines with great ceremony and no necessity whatsoever.

Then she came aboard. She put her hand on the wheel that wasn't hers yet and already was, and she looked at the six letters on her stern, and she said, quietly, so only those of us in the wheelhouse heard it:

"Halloway. Underway."

The engine caught on the first turn — I'll be modest about that for exactly as long as it takes you to stop reading — and the current took us, and the escarpment stood far off to the north like a promise the sky had made and intended to keep.

We had charts to correct, a river to learn honestly, and a monsoon coming on schedule. For the first time in her life, I think, Felix Vane wasn't running from anything.

She was running the water.
