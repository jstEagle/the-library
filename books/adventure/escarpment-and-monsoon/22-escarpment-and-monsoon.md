## 22. Escarpment and Monsoon

The monsoon came back on the day it was supposed to, which after everything felt like a personal courtesy.

I was on the foredeck of the *Vela Rehn* with a cup of coffee going cold in my hand when the first fat drops hit the planking. Behind us, upriver, the escarpment had gone the color of wet slate, its crown wrapped in cloud like an old man pulling a blanket over his head and refusing to discuss the weather. Ilsa's charts said the rains would arrive on the fourteenth. The rains arrived on the fourteenth. I'd spent so many years assuming that anything a government touched arrived late, over budget, or not at all that punctuality from an official document still startled me.

"Captain." Odessa's voice through the engine-room tube, tinny and contented. "She's holding true at cruising. You want more?"

"I want you to stop asking me questions you already know the answers to."

"That's my favorite kind of question," she said, and the tube went quiet with the smugness of a woman whose engine was purring.

The new boat wasn't new. That was the joke none of us said out loud. She was a battered forty-foot hull Odessa had found rotting at a salvage slip outside Corren's Landing, bought with a shoebox of crumpled notes the crew had pooled together while I was sitting in a hearing room watching them suspend my license pending inquiry—an inquiry that, as far as I could tell, consisted of one man reading one file for eleven months and occasionally sighing. They named her before I even saw her. I came down to the dock and there it was on the transom, fresh paint over old scars: *VELA REHN*. Named for the survey's lost members, all of them, Vela and Rehn and the three whose names never made the newspapers because the newspapers were busy with the scandal of men who'd rather we hadn't come home.

I stood on the dock and looked at that name for a long time, and Marisol, who misses nothing, put a hand on my shoulder and said, "You can cry about it later, Captain. Right now we need someone to sign the insurance forms."

So that's how I came to be standing here, months later, coffee in hand, rain starting, running a river I knew better than I knew my own face—except that now the river had honest maps. There's something strange about navigating with Ilsa Rehn's charts spread open beside you. Every sandbar I'd learned by nearly dying on it was inked there in her small, precise hand, with depth soundings and seasonal corrections, and every time I checked one against my memory and found she was right, I felt a little less like a smuggler of secret knowledge and a little more like a captain who happened to have a very good cartographer aboard.

Not that I'd told her everything. Some sandbars you keep. A captain is allowed one or two secrets, if only for professional dignity.

* * *

Pip found me at the rail an hour into the rain.

"You're doing the thing," Pip said.

"What thing."

"The staring-upriver thing. Where you look at the escarpment like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money. It cost me a boat."

"It cost you a boat you hated by the end," Pip said, leaning beside me, elbows on the rail, watching the water go silver under the rain. "You used to polish that wheel like it was your father's ghost. Now you just steer."

Pip had grown half an inch since spring, which shouldn't be possible for someone already too tall for the cabin bunks, and had developed the habit of saying things that were true in a tone that suggested they were casual observations. Rook did the same thing but meaner. I'd stopped being surprised that the twins had turned out to be the two most perceptive people on my crew; I'd stopped being surprised by a lot of things.

"Any regrets?" Pip asked. "About the landing. About reading it all out loud."

I thought about it honestly, because Pip deserved honesty and because lying to my own crew had gotten expensive last year. "I regret that Halloran got to walk away into a filing cabinet somewhere instead of a cell. I regret that the inquiry took my license for eleven months over a rescue I was blackmailed into. I don't regret the rest." I took a sip of the cold coffee. "You?"

Pip was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence. "I dream about the relay station sometimes. Catching Brandt at the transmitter. I always wake up right before I decide whether to hit him."

"And in the dream?"

"In the dream he tells me about the orders again. Leave no witnesses." Pip shrugged, too casually. "Then I wake up and he's in the galley arguing with Marisol about whether cumin goes in river fish, so. I guess I decided."

Brandt was in the galley right now, in fact. He'd come aboard in the autumn with a duffel bag, a letter of resignation from a government that pretended it had never employed him, and the expression of a man reporting for a sentence he'd recommended for himself. I'd let him aboard for reasons I still couldn't fully articulate—something about a man who pulls a deckhand out of floodwater without hesitating, something about hairline cracks becoming faults given enough pressure—and he'd repaid the gamble by being the most relentlessly competent, relentlessly quiet crew member I'd ever had. He fixed things before they broke. He stood watches nobody assigned him. And once a week or so, Pip would catch him doing something ordinary—mending net, peeling potatoes—and watch him with an expression that meant the dream was still running.

"He'll earn it," I said.

"He knows that."

"Good. Then you both know it, and I don't have to referee."

* * *

We made Corren's Landing by midafternoon, riding a river gone brown and muscular with the season's first runoff. The town had changed in the way river towns change when they stop being afraid—or rather, when they trade one fear for another and find the second one livable. Before, the fear had been shapeless: missing survey teams, government boats that didn't answer hails, the sense that the upper river belonged to somebody else now. Now the fear was concrete and manageable: high water, low prices, the usual. Delegates from six towns met quarterly in Corren's Landing to review Ilsa's published surveys and argue about dredging rights, loudly, in public, which is the sound a river makes when it belongs to itself.

Ilsa was waiting at the dock with a crate of new charts and the same expression she'd worn in the cave—wary, precise, like a woman perpetually braced for the world to make sense at any moment and preparing her objections in advance.

"Captain Vane." She handed up the crate. "Revised soundings for the middle gorge. The flood moved the third sandbar east maybe thirty meters."

"I know. I ran past it Tuesday."

"I know you ran past it. I want it on paper so the next captain doesn't die learning what you know."

That was Ilsa in a sentence. She'd taken the thing she carried out of the mountains—the seam, the seizure plan, the whole rotten architecture of it—and turned it into paper that kept strangers alive. She'd been cleared by inquiry, decorated quietly, offered three government posts, and declined all three to run a survey office above a chandlery where anyone could walk in and buy the truth for the price of a printed sheet. She and I understood each other. We'd both learned that the difference between a secret and a map is whether you're willing to let other people hold it.

"Come aboard," I said. "Marisol's making fish with cumin, against Brandt's explicit objection."

"Then I definitely am."

* * *

Dinner that night was loud in the way I'd once believed I'd forfeited forever. Rook reenacted a near-collision with a log boom using bread rolls and a gravy boat, casting himself as the heroic pilot and everyone else as scenery. Odessa described, in loving technical detail, the replacement of a fuel injector, and was allowed to finish, because this crew understands love takes many forms. Marisol fed us until we surrendered. Brandt washed dishes without being asked, sleeves rolled, and when Pip passed him the drying cloth their hands touched for exactly as long as hands touching a dish towel touch, and I pretended not to notice, because a captain learns which silences are load-bearing.

Afterward, I went up alone to the wheelhouse and stood in the dark with the rain on the windows and the river talking to itself below.

Here is what I had expected, in the months after the landing: that it wouldn't last. That the inquiry would reach deeper into my file and find whatever Halloran had held over me—that old survival secret, the one that had made me invisible for years, the leash I'd finally let him yank in public when I chose truth over erasure. I'd walked into that landing knowing the exposure might burn everything, and it had burned some: the license, the old boat, the careful anonymity I'd built like a hull around myself. But the fire had stopped. Halloran vanished into the bureaucracy the way bad weather vanishes into a season—not forgiven, not punished, just redistributed. And the file stayed closed. Maybe because exposing me served no one anymore. Maybe because Brandt's testimony had made the government so eager for the whole business to be forgotten that digging up my past would have meant digging up theirs.

Or maybe—I was slowly allowing myself to believe this—because a person can survive being known. That was the lesson the river had been trying to teach me since I was a girl watching my father read the water. You can't outrun the current. You can only learn it, respect it, and stop pretending you're somewhere else.

My father had died on the night run through the gorge, on sandbars he thought he knew and didn't, in water that lied to him. For years I'd carried that gorge inside me like ballast—grief on one side, mastery on the other, never quite balanced. When I'd run it by night last summer with the shadow boat behind us and my crew trusting me with their lives, I'd finally faced his river as my own. I'd survived it. The boat hadn't. And I'd learned the difference between those two losses, which is that one of them has a name and the other is just wood.

Now the *Vela Rehn* carried his granddaughter's name in spirit and my father's river in her keel, and neither of them haunted me. They just rode along.

* * *

Rook relieved me at midnight and found me still at the window.

"You're doing the thing again," Rook said.

"There's only one thing, apparently."

"The staring thing. But softer tonight." Rook dropped into the pilot's chair and checked the chart, Ilsa's chart, then glanced at the water, then at me. "Middle gorge tomorrow?"

"Middle gorge tomorrow. Then the long pool, then home waters by the weekend."

"Weird, calling it home waters when home moves."

"It's the only kind of home I've ever trusted," I said. "A house stays where it's put. A boat goes where you point it. If trouble finds one address, you've got another."

Rook nodded slowly, the way the twins do when they're filing something away to say back to me at a devastating moment later. "Night, Captain."

"Night. Wake me if the river gets interesting."

"It's the monsoon," Rook said. "The river's always interesting."

* * *

I slept in my bunk with the rain drumming the deck above me, and I did not dream of the relay station, or the landing, or Halloran's voice on the wire, or the grave on the escarpment with its cairn of stones. I dreamed, if I dreamed at all, of ordinary water—brown, patient, going where it was going—and woke to the smell of coffee and the sound of my crew arguing about whether the rain counted as heavy or merely committed.

Up on deck, the escarpment had emerged from cloud for the morning, standing green and enormous against the east, wearing its waterfalls like medals. Somewhere up there was the seam that had nearly killed us all, and the valley where Ilsa had waited, and the camp where good people had been written off as a rounding error. The government had wanted it unaccounted for. Now it was accounted for, published, argued over in town halls, and mine to look at whenever I liked—which was daily, because you can't run this river without looking at it.

I drank my coffee and watched the monsoon do its work, on schedule, indifferent to governments, faithful to nothing but the calendar and the mountains. The river rose beneath my hull, and the *Vela Rehn* rose with it, light on the water, pointed wherever I'd point her tomorrow.

Odessa stuck her head out of the engine room. "Fair warning, Captain—there's a delegation at the next dock asking for passage up to the falls. Six of them, paying well, and one of them says she knows you."

"Nobody worth knowing admits that in public."

"She seemed nice."

"They always do." I set down the cup and rolled my shoulders, feeling the old familiar weight settle into place—not the leash, not the debt, just the work. "Tell them we leave on the tide."

The rain came down. The river carried us. And for the first time in longer than I could measure, I wasn't looking over my shoulder at all—just ahead, at the water, where the next honest mile was already opening up.
