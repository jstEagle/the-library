## 1. Fathoms of Quiet

The freighter had no name worth remembering. Quentin Hartwell—today she was Ren Okafor, licensed salvage operator out of Port Anselm—had checked the registry twice before the dive, and the ship existed on paper only as a hull number and a date of abandonment: *MV Corvane, 41° of list, surrendered to the sea.* That was all a wreck needed to be. A wreck was honest in a way people weren't. It told you exactly what it was, and then it let you take what you wanted.

She hung at fifteen meters, weightless in the green cathedral light, and listened to the ship breathe.

Rust bloomed across the hull like coral that had learned to be ugly. A school of fusiliers poured through a torn cargo door in a silver ribbon, unspooling and rewinding, indifferent to her. Below, the deck sloped into shadow where the cargo holds yawned open, and somewhere down there was the generator coupling Dov had sent her to find—the one part in the archipelago that fit their winch motor, if it hadn't already turned to orange lace.

Quentin moved through the water the way she did everything else: efficiently, without disturbance. Fifteen years of practice. Fifteen years of being a woman who left no wake.

That was the discipline of it. Anyone could disappear once—a forged passport, a new surname, a bus ticket to a coast town where nobody asked questions. The hard part was staying disappeared. It meant never signing anything twice with the same hand. Never letting your face settle into a photograph. Never loving anyone so loudly that they'd remember your real name by accident, at a checkpoint, under pressure. It meant measuring your life not in years but in nautical miles, in fuel receipts from harbors that blurred together, in the slow accumulation of small jobs done well enough to be paid in cash and forgotten by Friday.

She had gotten good at it. She had gotten so good that some mornings she woke and had to lie still for a moment, running through the inventory of who she was supposed to be today, before the answer came: *Ren. You're Ren. You've been Ren for two years and four months. The boat is the Kestrel. Your crew is Dov and Suri. That's all.*

The coupling was where the manifest said it would be, bolted inside the engine room's aft gallery, furred with rust but intact underneath. She worked the wrench slowly, feeling the bolts give one by one, and let herself have the thought she only ever allowed herself down here, where no one could see her face: *This is enough.*

It was. That was the strange, quiet miracle of it. She had a boat that ran, most days. She had work that paid. She had Dov, who hummed off-key in the engine room and made coffee like an act of penance, and Suri, who was nineteen and laughed too easily and had never once asked where Quentin had come from, because Suri understood that the question itself was rude—that on this boat, everyone's past was a locked hold, and you didn't go prying at other people's hatches.

Fifteen meters up, the surface rippled silver and far away, and Quentin floated in the space between, in the fathoms of quiet she had built one deliberate forgetting at a time.

* * *

Dov met her at the swim ladder with a towel and a scowl that meant good news delivered badly.

"Got it?" he said.

"Bolted tight, but she'll turn." Quentin handed him the coupling wrapped in her dive slate bag. "Clean the threads before you force anything."

"I never force anything." He turned it over in his big hands, squinting at the rust. "I persuade."

"You threaten with a hammer."

"That's diplomacy." He grinned, and the grin cracked the scowl, and that was Dov entire: a man built entirely out of complaints held together by affection. He'd been with her six years, since the night he'd walked off a sinking charter in Port Meridian with nothing but a toolbox and a grudge against his former captain, and asked—not where she'd been, not who she was—but whether her bilge pump was as bad as it sounded. It had been. He'd fixed it by morning. Neither of them had ever needed more than that.

Suri was at the helm when Quentin climbed into the wheelhouse, coiling lines with the quick economy she'd learned from watching Quentin do it wrong first and right second. Nineteen, wiry, sunburnt across the nose, hair always escaping its tie. She'd shown up two years ago at a fuel dock asking for work and a meal in whichever order was faster, and Quentin—who had sworn off taking on strays because strays asked questions—had looked at the kid's hands, calloused like her own, and heard herself say *we sail at dawn* before her caution could catch up.

"Corvane give it up?" Suri asked.

"Eventually. They always do." Quentin dropped into the pilot's chair and checked the chart spread on the table. The cay behind them had no name on any chart she owned—just a smudge of green with a depth sounding beside it, which suited her fine. Nameless places were the best neighbors. "How's our fuel?"

"Enough for Anselm if we don't fight the current. Dov says the port filter's due anyway."

"Dov says a lot of things."

"He also says dinner's in twenty minutes and if you're late it goes to the gulls."

Quentin almost smiled. This was the whole architecture of her life now, and she knew its dimensions better than her own hands: a thirty-one-meter salvage tug, two crew, a circuit of small harbors and smaller jobs, moving always, anchored never longer than the work required. Contentment built on forgetting. She had chosen it plank by plank, and every day she chose it again, and every day the choosing got easier, until some days she didn't have to choose at all. The boat just sailed, and she just lived on it, and the woman named Quentin Hartwell stayed fifteen years and four thousand miles away, court-martialed in absentia alongside a brother who—

She stopped the thought. Set it down gently, the way you set down something fragile you had no intention of carrying.

"Dinner sounds good," she said.

* * *

They ate on deck because the evening was warm and the cay gave them shelter from the long swell. Dov produced rice and tinned fish and a sauce he claimed was his grandmother's recipe, which was a lie, because Dov had told her three different grandmothers' recipes for the same sauce over the years, and Quentin had decided the lies were part of the flavor.

Suri sat cross-legged on the hatch cover, eating fast, talking faster—a story about a fisherman she'd bargained with that morning, complete with voices. Dov heckled. The gulls heckled louder. The sun went down behind the cay in a slow burn of copper, and the water turned to hammered pewter, and Quentin sat with her back against the rail and let the noise wash over her.

This was the family she allowed herself. Not the word—she'd never used the word, and neither had they—but the thing itself. Two people who knew which mug was hers. Who noticed when she slept badly. Who had never, not once in eight combined years, asked her a single question about before.

She caught herself memorizing it, the way she sometimes did. The lamplight. Dov's laugh. Suri gesturing with a fork. She didn't know why she did that—took these snapshots, filed them away like evidence—as if some part of her had always known that contentment built on forgetting came with a lease term, and leases ended.

Later, Suri turned in and Dov went below to argue with the port filter, and Quentin took the anchor watch alone, a cup of coffee going cold beside her, the cay a black shape against a sky salted with stars.

That was when she saw the lights.

Two of them, low on the southern horizon, where the deep water channel ran. At this distance they might have been fishing boats—except fishing boats worked the banks to the west, and these were holding station, dead in the water, in a lane where nobody held station. As she watched, one of them moved a degree, sweeping, and the light caught and held: white over white over red, riding high and clean.

A patrol boat. Navy gray, even if the dark wouldn't admit it yet.

Quentin stood very still and watched it for a long time. Patrol boats passed through these waters often enough; that was normal, that was weather. What wasn't normal was lingering. And this one lingered. Twenty minutes by her watch, maybe more, sitting out there in the channel like a man standing outside a door without knocking. Then, unhurried, it turned south and began to move away, its lights shrinking, and the horizon went back to being only the horizon.

She told herself it was nothing. She was good at telling herself things; she'd had fifteen years of practice at that too. Routine transit. A drill. A crew looking at the same stars she was.

But her hands had gone cold around the coffee cup, and the cold had nothing to do with the night.

Because here was the thing about building a life on deliberate forgetting: you never entirely forgot what you were forgetting. It sat in you like the coupling in the wreck—furred over, corroded, but intact underneath. And some animal part of her, older than her caution, older than her aliases, had felt those lights hold station and had known, with a certainty that arrived ahead of any reasoning:

*They're not looking for smugglers. Smugglers run. Nobody on this boat runs.*

They're looking for someone who stays.

Quentin dumped the coffee over the rail and stood her watch until dawn, and told herself again that it was nothing, and did not believe it, and in the morning there was nothing to do but raise the anchor and point the bow toward Port Anselm, where the fuel was cheap and the harbor bar served beer in bottles cold enough to hurt—and where, though she couldn't have known it yet, a woman from fifteen years ago was waiting with a contract and a name Quentin Hartwell had spent half her life trying to bury.
