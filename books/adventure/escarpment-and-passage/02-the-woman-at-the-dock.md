## 2. The Woman at the Dock

The bar was called the Anchorless, which Quentin had always taken for a joke that wasn't one. It sat at the end of the pier road in Port Calloway's lower town, a low concrete building the color of old rope, its windows fogged with salt and decades of cigarette smoke that had outlived every smoker. The kind of place where nobody asked your name because nobody wanted to give theirs.

She went there anyway, twice a month, when the *Marrow* was in port long enough to need provisioning and short enough on comfort to need beer. She took the corner table with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, not because she expected trouble but because fifteen years of habit didn't care what she expected. Habit just kept its watch.

Dov had gone back to the boat to argue with the coolant pump. Suri had gone wherever Suri went when money changed hands—probably to the chandler's, probably to charm a discount out of the old man there, probably succeeding. Quentin sat alone with a bottle of the local lager, which tasted like it had been filtered through a fishing net, and watched the rain come down in gray sheets beyond the doorway.

That was when Mara Kessler walked in.

Quentin knew her before she cleared the threshold. Fifteen years hadn't changed the way Mara entered a room—like the room had been waiting for her and should be grateful the wait was over. She'd shed the water, though; where the rain soaked everyone else flat, it seemed to slide off her, leaving her dry as a ledger. A linen jacket. Hair cut shorter than academy days, silver at the temples now, worn like it was an achievement rather than a surrender. She scanned the bar once, found Quentin in three seconds flat, and smiled the way you smile at something you've already purchased.

Quentin did not smile back. She made herself take a slow pull of the beer instead, and hated that her pulse had already picked up its pace, traitor heart, running ahead of her judgment the way it always had around Mara Kessler.

Mara crossed the room, pulled out the opposite chair, and sat down without being invited. She set a slim waterproof case on the table between them, square and heavy-cornered, and rested two fingers on it like a woman keeping a hand on a dog's collar.

"Quentin Hartwell," she said.

The name landed in Quentin's chest like a dropped anchor.

Not *Vane*. Not whatever alias the harbor master's books carried this season. Her brother's name. Her father's name. Her own, spoken aloud in a public bar for the first time in over a decade, and the sound of it was so strange she almost looked around to see who Mara was talking to.

"You've got the wrong woman," Quentin said. Her voice came out level. She'd have paid money for that.

"I've got exactly the right woman." Mara leaned back, unhurried, letting the case sit between them. "Salvage diver, licensed out of three different ports under three different names, all of them clean, all of them boring. You've built quite the paper trail, Hartwell. I'll admit it took me eleven months to follow it backward. Most people can't cover their tracks that well." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Most people don't have as much practice."

Outside, the rain hammered the tin awning. Inside, Quentin became aware of every other body in the room—the two deckhands playing cards by the window, the bartender polishing a glass he'd been polishing since she arrived. None of them were looking over. None of them needed to. A name spoken at volume carries.

"What do you want, Kessler?"

"A drink, to start. And then I want to talk business." Mara raised two fingers toward the bartender, then turned back, and for a moment—just a moment—the performance thinned, and Quentin saw underneath it: the same sharp, hungry girl from the academy diving tanks, the one who'd beaten her on the deep-salvage trials by four seconds and never once let her forget it. Older now. Tired underneath, in a way that money couldn't fix, because money had clearly tried.

"The Vespera," Mara said.

The word went through Quentin like cold water through a wetsuit.

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms." Quentin set the bottle down carefully, because she wanted very much to set it down otherwise. "Whatever you think you know about me, you don't know that. Nobody touches the Vespera. That's not a rule, Kessler. That's a headstone."

"It's a fiction," Mara said quietly. "A wreck officially struck from naval records doesn't exist, which means nobody guards it, which means anybody who finds it can take from it. That's not a warning, Hartwell. That's an invitation with the navy's fingerprints wiped off the handle."

"And if they catch you?" Quentin leaned forward. "You know what happened to the last crew that went near her. You know what happened to—" She stopped. Swallowed the name. Elias's name sat in her throat like a stone she'd learned to live around, and she would not hand it to Mara Kessler across a bar table in the rain.

"To your brother." Mara said it gently, which was worse. "Yes. I know what happened to Elias Hartwell. Court-martial, disgrace, a sentence served in a prison hospital until his lungs gave out, and a family name dragged through the mud so thoroughly that his sister had to bury hers too just to keep working." She tilted her head. "I read the transcripts. All of them. Did you?"

Quentin said nothing. She had read them. Forty times. Looking for the seam in the story, the place where the official account stopped matching physics, and finding it every time and being unable to prove it, because proving it required evidence sealed inside a wreck the navy pretended wasn't there.

"They say the Vespera went down with all records lost," Mara went on. "They say there's nothing aboard worth recovering. They say it so often, and so loudly, that you have to wonder what they're so afraid someone might find." She tapped the case with one fingernail. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to dive."

"I swore off that job."

"After Elias." Not a question. "So did I swear off things, Hartwell. We all swear off things. Then the thing we swore off is the only thing left that matters, and we find out swearing is just a word we used to buy ourselves some sleep."

The bartender brought Mara's drink. She paid without looking, waited until he'd gone, and opened the case. Inside, nested in foam, lay a chart—not a commercial chart, but a survey sheet, hand-annotated, coordinates circled in red ink. Quentin recognized the notation style. Naval hydrographic office. Stolen or copied, either way it was a document that could put them both in a cell.

Beneath the chart, a photograph: a strongbox, corroded green at the seams, welded into what looked like a bulkhead bracket. Officer's country. Someone had photographed it underwater, and recently—the colors were still true.

"You've had divers down there," Quentin said.

"One. He came back with this and then he came back with nothing else, ever again. His boat burned at anchor two weeks later. Officially: electrical fault." Mara closed the case. "I'm not going to insult you by pretending this is safe. I'm telling you it's necessary, and I'm telling you I can pay what it's actually worth, and I'm telling you that you're the only diver alive who could get into that compartment and back out."

Flattery, Quentin thought. She's good at it. She was good at everything, that was the whole problem with Mara Kessler. Four seconds faster on the trials. First pick of postings. First to make salvage master, first to go corporate, first to forget where she came from—or so Quentin had believed, all these years, hating her comfortably from a distance.

But Mara's hands weren't steady, and Mara had spent eleven months tracking a ghost, and Mara had said *we* just now, small and quick, like a slip.

"Why me?" Quentin asked. "Really. There are better technical divers. There are divers who don't change their names every three years."

"Because they'd sell me." Mara said it flatly, without self-pity. "And because you hate the navy more than you fear it. I need someone whose hatred has structure. Yours does. I watched you build it, tank by tank, year by year, at the academy, while you pretended you were just ambitious." She lifted her glass but didn't drink. "We were the same, once. Don't tell me we weren't."

"We were never the same. You stayed."

"I survived. There's a difference, and it cost me more than it cost you, whatever you think." For a heartbeat something moved behind her eyes—grief, maybe, or its fossil—and then the composure closed over it like water over a sinking thing. "The offer stands for seventy-two hours. The monsoon's coming early this year, Hartwell. After it breaks, the site's undivable for four months, and after four months, I may not be able to protect the contract from other interested parties."

"Interested parties."

"Navy ones."

Quentin stood. Slowly, deliberately, the way you move when you want your body to lie for you. She left bills on the table for her beer, more than it cost, because leaving exact change felt like staying.

"Mara," she said, using the first name for the first time in fifteen years, watching it land. "You came here tonight and said my real name out loud in a public bar. Either you're careless—which you've never been—or you wanted me to understand something."

Mara looked up at her, and the smile returned, thin and true and terrible.

"Invisibility is a lease, Hartwell. Not a deed. Somebody, somewhere, holds the paperwork on all of us." She slid the case across the table until it touched Quentin's hand. "Seventy-two hours. Think about who else knows your name—and ask yourself why they haven't used it yet."

Quentin walked out into the rain without the case. It took everything she had.

* * *

The *Marrow* rode low at her moorings, riding light on fuel and heavier on debt. Quentin stood on the dock in the downpour and watched Dov's silhouette move behind the engine hatch, cursing at the coolant pump in three languages, and felt the shape of her life settle around her again: the boat, the crew, the borrowed names, the miles of open water between her and everything she'd buried.

It had held for fifteen years. One conversation, and she could feel the mortar cracking.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop turning over, there in the rain with her hair plastered flat: Mara had known. Eleven months of digging, and she'd found the thread, pulled it, followed it all the way back to a name Quentin had drowned in a court-martial record half a lifetime ago. If Mara could do it, others could. Others perhaps already had. And Mara's parting question circled back on itself, patient as a shark: *why haven't they used it yet?*

There was only one answer Quentin could live with, and it was the worst one. Because whoever held the paperwork was waiting. Watching her the way you watch a trap you've already baited—waiting to see if she'd swim for the Vespera on her own, so the navy wouldn't have to dirty its hands dragging her there.

Suri came up the dock road under an umbrella she was holding wrong, a crate of parts balanced on one hip, grinning through the rain.

"Boss! You look like you seen a ghost."

"Not yet," Quentin said, and took the crate from her hip, and led the way aboard.
