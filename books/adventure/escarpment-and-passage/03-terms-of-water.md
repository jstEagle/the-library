## 3. Terms of Water

The engine died on a Tuesday, forty miles from anywhere, and by Thursday the account that fed it was dead too.

Quentin sat in the wheelhouse of the *Magpie* with the ledger open on her knee, doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to. The injector pump had cost eleven thousand, most of it borrowed against the next three jobs at rates Dov called "piracy" and the lender called "standard maritime terms." Below her, through the deck plates, she could hear him still working—torque wrench clicking, radio murmuring weather from a station neither of them trusted. Suri was asleep in the fo'c'sle, or pretending to be, which amounted to the same kindness.

Eleven thousand down. Four hundred and change left. A fuel bill due in nine days.

She closed the ledger and looked out at the harbor of Puerto Aliso, where the water lay flat and green as bottle glass and the fishing boats knocked gently against their tires. Somewhere across that water, in a bar called the Rudder Room, Mara Kessler was waiting for an answer.

* * *

Mara hadn't changed in ways that mattered. Still tall, still precise, still dressed like someone who owned the concept of money rather than merely having it—linen the color of bone, a watch thin enough to be an insult. She'd aged the way good hulls age: nothing loose, everything salted.

"You look well," Mara said, which was a lie between rivals and therefore almost friendly.

"I look solvent," Quentin said. "Which is better than I'll look tomorrow."

"Then you've read my contract."

"I've burned it." That wasn't true either. It sat folded in the wheelhouse drawer, under the flare gun, where she'd put it after reading it four times. "You're asking me to dive a wreck the navy sank twice. Once with torpedoes, once with paperwork."

"And I'm paying enough to make the paperwork irrelevant." Mara turned her glass a quarter-turn on the bar's scarred wood. Around them the Rudder Room did its evening work—deckhands arguing about engines, a chart of the coast yellowing behind plastic, a jukebox playing something mournful in Spanish. Nobody looked at them twice. That was the point of bars like this; that had been the point of Quentin's whole life. "One hundred and twenty thousand. Half up front."

"The Vespera is forbidden."

"The Vespera is *forgotten*. There's a difference, and the difference is worth one hundred and twenty thousand dollars to whoever remembers first." Mara's eyes were gray and patient. Academy gray. They'd spent four years competing for the same scholarships, the same berths, the same instructor's nod, and Quentin had won most of it until the year everything else went wrong. "I'm not asking you to be brave, Quentin. I'm asking you to be broke in a profitable direction."

"Don't call me that here."

Something flickered across Mara's face—not apology, but its expensive cousin, acknowledgment. "Old habits. Hartwell was always the name I knew you by."

"That name stopped existing fifteen years ago. You know why."

"I know what the record says happened." Mara leaned forward, voice dropping beneath the jukebox. "The record says your brother disobeyed orders. The record says the *Vespera*'s loss was his fault and the executive officer's fault, and the record says both men were tried, convicted, and erased. I also know records are written by the people who survive them. My father didn't survive his trial, Quentin. He just survived longer."

Quentin stared at her. Fifteen years of careful forgetting, and this woman had walked into a port bar carrying the two names Quentin had worked hardest to bury, laid side by side like evidence.

"Why me?" she asked finally. "There are divers who don't have my history. Cheaper ones. Safer ones."

"Because they'd sell the box to the first man who offered them a clean identity, and I need someone who hates the navy more than she wants to disappear." Mara smiled without warmth. "Also because you're the best salvage diver on this coast, and I've never once enjoyed saying that."

* * *

Dov said no before Quentin finished the second sentence.

They stood in the engine room, where he could point at things—the stripped pump, the empty parts locker, the invoice taped to the bulkhead like a diagnosis—and let the machinery argue for him. Dov was sixty-one and built like something welded rather than born, and he had been with her nine years, since the night he'd found her half-drowned off a job gone bad and never asked a single question about who she'd been before.

"One hundred and twenty thousand," he repeated. "For a wreck the fleet pretends isn't there. You know what happens to boats that touch those wrecks? They get audited. Their licenses get audited. Their *past* gets audited." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being gray decades ago. "I've got a past, skipper. It's small, but it's mine, and I'd like to keep it."

"It's a client contract. Salvage recovery, standard terms."

"There's no such thing as standard terms on a forbidden wreck." He set the rag down. "Who is this woman to you?"

"A rival."

"Nobody pays a hundred and twenty thousand over a rival. What is she?"

Quentin looked at the invoice. Eleven thousand dollars of Dov's competence, hanging there. "Someone whose father was court-martialed next to my brother."

The engine room went quiet except for the bilge pump ticking in its corner like a slow clock. Dov took that in, and she watched him do the same math she'd done—two names, one crime, twenty years of silence—and arrive somewhere she hadn't yet.

"So it's not a job," he said. "It's a debt."

"It's a hundred and twenty thousand dollars, and we are nine days from owing a fuel company more than we own."

"Money's a reason. It's not the reason." He picked the rag back up, which meant the argument was over even if the objection wasn't. "I'll turn the wrenches. But I want it on record that I think this is how boats die. Not storms. Decisions."

"Noted for the record," Quentin said.

From the companionway behind them, Suri's voice: "I say we take it."

They both turned. Suri stood on the ladder with a mug of coffee she'd clearly been using as cover, nineteen years old and watchful as a gull, three years aboard and still the newest thing on the boat.

"This isn't a vote," Dov said.

"Everything's a vote if the skipper says so." Suri came down the last step. "We're broke. The job's real money. And if the navy's so scared of this wreck, then whatever's on it matters, and I'd rather find out why than spend my life hauling anchors off tourist reefs." She shrugged, the shrug of someone stating weather. "Also, I trust the skipper. That's the whole crew meeting, right? Skipper decides, we decide."

Dov muttered something in Latvian that was probably not a blessing.

Quentin felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders, familiar as a harness: two people, one boat, and every choice she made arriving on their doorstep wearing her face. Invisibility had a price. So did belonging. She'd spent fifteen years believing only one of them could bankrupt her.

"All right," she said. "We take the contract. One condition, non-negotiable."

Suri grinned. Dov didn't.

"Mara comes aboard as client," Quentin said. "Client rides, watches, pays, and keeps her opinions above decks. She doesn't command. She doesn't touch the gear. She doesn't give an order on my boat, not even a suggestion phrased like one. If she can't live with that, the contract dies tonight."

"She'll agree," Suri said. "People like her always agree. Then they remember who they are halfway through."

"Then I'll remind her," Quentin said, "whose boat she's bleeding on."

* * *

That night she didn't sleep. She climbed to the flybridge instead, where the harbor lights blurred in the humidity, and let the memory come, because refusing it had never once worked.

Fifteen years ago. A hearing room at the naval station, all pale wood and worse light. Elias in dress whites that no longer fit him right because he'd lost weight during the proceedings, sitting straight the way their father had taught them, hands flat on the table like a man showing he held nothing. Quentin, twenty-three, in the gallery, watching the captain testify.

The captain had a voice like a tide chart—calm, official, rising and falling exactly as required. He described the storm. He described the orders. He described, with visible sorrow, how Lieutenant Elias Hartwell had failed to execute them properly, how the *Vespera* had been lost with all hands below, how the executive officer, Kessler, had compounded the failure. He never raised his voice. He never hesitated. Lies told slowly sound like truth; Quentin learned that at twenty-three and built a life on it.

What she alone knew—what Elias had told her in the forty minutes she'd been allowed with him before sentencing—was that the orders themselves were unlawful. That he'd followed them anyway because the alternative was refusal in front of a crew, and refusal would have sunk them faster than the sea did. That when it went wrong, the captain had rewritten the night in real time, and everyone alive had agreed to the new version because agreeing was survivable and remembering wasn't.

"They'll ask you to forget," Elias had said through the mesh, smiling the way he smiled when he was terrified. "Forget fast, Q. Forget so completely they can't find you. Promise me."

So she had. New name, new papers, new trade. She'd measured her life since in nautical miles instead of years, because miles didn't accumulate into a history. And now a woman with the other convicted man's surname was offering to buy the forgetting back at market rate.

Below her, the *Magpie* rocked on its mooring lines, patient as a dog. Dov's lamp still glowed in the engine room—he never slept well before a job, and he slept worst before a job he distrusted. Suri's silhouette crossed the foredeck once, checking lines that didn't need checking, the way young sailors bless a boat before hard water.

Quentin thought about the fuel bill. About the box. About a captain with a tide-chart voice, presumably old now, presumably decorated, presumably certain the sea had kept his secret the way it kept everything.

The sea kept nothing. That was the whole problem with the sea. It only held things until someone came down for them.

At dawn, Mara Kessler stood on the dock in the same bone-colored linen, fresh as if sleep were a service provided to her, and watched Quentin walk the gangway down with the signed contract rolled in her fist.

"Terms accepted?" Mara asked.

"Client rides," Quentin said. "Client obeys. First order I countermand, I put you ashore at the nearest rock, refund prorated, no discussion."

A pause, one heartbeat long. Then Mara extended her hand, and her grip was cool and dry and strong. "Then let's go raise a ghost."

Behind Quentin, Dov started the engine, and it caught on the third try, rough but running, and Suri cast off the lines, and the *Magpie* turned her bow toward open water and the coordinates Mara had paid for—coordinates that sat in Quentin's pocket like a splinter, pointing at a wreck the navy had struck from every record but its memory.

Two hours out, Suri appeared at the wheelhouse door with the binoculars.

"Skipper," she said. "We've got company. Gray hull, no flag showing. Been holding our wake since the channel."
