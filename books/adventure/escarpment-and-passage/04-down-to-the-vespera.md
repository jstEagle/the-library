## 4. Down to the Vespera

The Vespera announced herself before Quentin saw her.

The depth sounder had been stuttering for a quarter mile, throwing back echoes that broke apart and reassembled, and then the bottom simply rose to meet them—a black shoulder of steel canted into the sand at twenty-two degrees, her superstructure sheared away, her hull furred with two decades of coral. On the sonar screen she looked like a sleeping animal. In Quentin's experience, most wrecks did. The difference was that this one was supposed to be a rumor.

"Nothing on charts," Dov said from behind her, not looking up from his console. He said it the way he said everything—flat, factual, a man reporting weather. "Nothing on any chart. I checked three registries. She doesn't exist."

"She exists," Suri said from the rail, binoculars down, scanning the empty horizon. "We're floating over her."

Quentin let them talk and watched the water instead. It was the wrong color here—not the bright shelf-teal of the cay country they usually worked but a deep, bruised indigo where the seafloor dropped away east of the wreck field. Water like this held things. She'd spent fifteen years learning what different water held: silt held secrets badly, blue water held them well, and this water—cold, deep, patient—held them best of all.

The navy patrol boat from two days ago was gone. That should have been a comfort. It wasn't. A boat that lingers is watching; a boat that leaves has finished watching and gone to report.

"Buoy," Suri said suddenly, and pointed.

Quentin found it with the glasses: a yellow float perhaps four hundred meters north, riding the swell, its antenna mast glinting. Unmarked. No flag, no strobe, no registration numbers. Just a buoy doing what buoys do, which is nothing, convincingly.

"Hydrophone?" Dov asked.

"Could be a fish aggregator," Suri offered, without conviction.

"It could be," Quentin said. "Dov, how's our noise profile?"

"Quiet as we get. Compressor off, generator at idle." He paused. "You're thinking we shouldn't talk on deck."

"I'm thinking we shouldn't talk at all." She handed the glasses back to Suri. "From here on, hand signals underwater, slate on deck if you have to say something. Assume everything above the waterline is being recorded."

Suri lowered the binoculars slowly. "By who?"

"That's the question I'd like answered before we touch anything."

* * *

She went down alone. That was the plan they'd agreed to—first dive solo, recon only, no gear for work, just lights and slate and the small camera rigged to her helmet. If the wreck was clean, they'd come back tomorrow with cutting equipment and a lift bag. If it wasn't—

If it wasn't, she wanted to be the one who found out first, with no one else's breathing in her ears.

The water took her by degrees. Ten meters, and the indigo turned to ink. Fifteen, and the temperature fell through her wetsuit like a hand. Her light carved a cone ahead of her and the cone found nothing, then sand, then the long pale slope rising toward the hull, and then the hull itself, vast and sudden, a wall of rust and life rising out of the dark like a cliff face.

The Vespera had been a naval auxiliary—Mara's documents said a stores and transport vessel, three hundred feet, sunk in the last year of the war with eleven hands lost. Officially, a mine. Officially, an accident, mourned and filed. Officially, there was no reason on earth for the navy to forbid divers from a wreck this far off any shipping lane, in water too deep for casual sport diving, in a region where no minefield had ever been charted.

Officially, Quentin thought, a great many things.

She swam the length of the hull with her light low, reading it the way she read all wrecks: bow to stern, damage first, history second. The mine—or whatever it had been—had opened her forward of midships, a wound the size of a bus, edges peeled outward. Two decades of growth had softened it into something almost beautiful, orange and violet cup corals spilling from the torn plate like flowers from a broken vase. Fish moved through the gap in silver threads.

It was aft, along the officer's quarters, that the wreck stopped being beautiful.

Mara's information had been precise—the box was welded into the executive officer's cabin, behind a bulkhead panel, because the man who put it there had wanted it findable by exactly one kind of person: someone with the plans, the nerve, and the right to know. Quentin found the cabin by counting frames from the stern, exactly as the old schematic showed, and slid her light through the doorway.

The bulkhead panel was there. So were the marks.

Not rust. Not coral. Cuts—clean, deliberate, recent. Three gouges in the plating beside the panel, each the width of a chisel, each still bright at the center where the metal hadn't yet had time to bloom orange. Fresh water had gotten into those cuts; salt hadn't finished its work. Quentin pressed her glove against the nearest one and felt the edge of it, sharp enough to catch.

Someone had been here within weeks. Maybe days.

She hung in the black water of the cabin, her own bubbles ticking upward past her face, and made herself think it through the way Dov would. A salvage crew working quietly would have cut the panel out already—they wouldn't leave marks and go. A navy recovery team would have taken the box entirely. These cuts looked like survey marks. Like someone measuring, confirming, marking the spot for later.

Or like bait, said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like Mara Kessler. Like someone who wants you to know they've been here. Who wants you nervous. Nervous divers make mistakes.

She unclipped her camera and photographed the cuts, the panel, the frame numbers, the doorway. Then, because fifteen years of erasing herself had taught her one iron habit—document everything, trust nothing—she photographed the corridor outside, the sediment on the deck plates, and finally the sediment itself, close up.

That was where she saw the fin prints.

Two sets, overlapping, pressed into the silt of the passageway. Not hers. Larger fins, longer stride, a diver moving with purpose rather than care. The prints' edges had begun to slump, softened by the slow rain of marine snow, but not erased. Days, again. Maybe less.

Quentin backed out of the cabin without touching anything else. In the corridor she stopped once more, light angled low across the deck, and counted: two divers, one pass each direction, no work done, no box disturbed. A look. A measurement. A message written in a language she'd spent half her life hoping never to receive.

They know the box is here. They know where it is. They're waiting.

* * *

She ascended slow, watching her computer tick through the required stops, and used every second of them. At ten meters she hung in the open blue and swept her light around in a full circle, and that was when she saw it: the buoy's shadow, far above, a thin dark cross against the bright ceiling of the surface. It had moved. When they'd anchored, it had been north. Now it sat almost directly overhead.

Buoys didn't move against the current. Buoys didn't drift two hundred meters upwind in six hours.

Hydrophone buoys listened. And something else occurred to her then, cold and complete, the way bad realizations always arrive—assembled all at once, needing no further thought: a listening buoy over a forbidden wreck wasn't surveillance. Surveillance was reactive. This was a tripwire. Someone had ringed the Vespera with ears and waited years for anyone foolish enough to come make noise. The wreck wasn't just forbidden. It was kept. A locked room with a microphone in it, and she had just spent forty minutes inside, breathing.

Her regulator hissed. Even that sound, tiny, private, swallowed by the sea—she thought of it traveling upward through two hundred feet of water into a hydrophone's ear, being logged, timestamped, flagged.

She finished her stops in silence anyway. Rushing would make more noise. But every meter of the ascent now felt like climbing out of a mouth.

* * *

She came up the ladder and Suri pulled her mask off for her, an old habit between them, and read her face before Quentin said a word.

"How bad?"

Quentin held up one finger—wait—and walked to the helm, where Dov stood, and wrote on the slate in block letters:

FRESH CUTS ON HULL. FIN PRINTS INSIDE. 2 DIVERS, RECENT. BUOY MOVED OVERHEAD. HYDROPHONE. WE ARE MADE OR WILL BE.

Dov read it twice. His jaw worked, the only part of him that ever moved quickly. Then he took the slate and wrote:

LEAVE NOW?

Quentin looked north, where the buoy rode the swell, patient as a held breath. She looked at the water over the wreck, indigo and innocent. Somewhere under that water lay proof that could pull her brother's name out of the mud where the navy had buried it twenty years ago. Proof that Elias Hartwell had followed lawful orders and been court-martialed for following them. Proof that the captain who gave those orders had needed a dead wreck and two scapegoats, and had gotten both.

And proof, apparently, that other people had been waiting for her to come collect it.

"Not yet," she said aloud, and then remembered herself, and took up the slate. IF WE RUN, WE CONFIRM. THEY MAY ONLY SUSPECT. WE'RE A SALVAGE CREW ON A ROUTINE JOB IN AN UNCHARTED FIELD. WRECKS LIKE THIS ARE EVERYWHERE OUT HERE. ACT NORMAL. WORK THE FREIGHTER TWO MILES WEST TOMORROW LIKE PLANNED. LOUDLY.

"And the box?" Suri whispered.

Quentin wrote: SECOND DIVE, STORM WINDOW BEFORE MONSOON. WE GO FOR IT FAST AND QUIET AND WE PLAN FOR COMPANY.

Dov stared at her. Then he wiped the slate and wrote one line: YOU THINK IT'S A TRAP.

Quentin took the slate. Her marker hovered. She thought about the fin prints in the silt, laid down neat as signatures. About a buoy that had drifted against the wind. About a wreck struck from every record, guarded by silence and microphones, holding a secret someone had decided, decades ago, was worth killing eleven sailors to keep.

She wrote: NO. I THINK IT'S A TRAP THAT WAS SET BEFORE WE WERE BORN. WE JUST WALKED INTO IT.

Out on the water, the buoy rocked gently in the failing light, its little antenna catching the last of the sun, blinking—no, not blinking, she told herself, it isn't blinking—but glinting, glinting, glinting, like something patient keeping count.
