## 6. Monsoon Rising

The barometer began lying to them at dawn.

Quentin stood in the wheelhouse of the *Magpie's Rest* with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the needle slide down the dial like a man easing out of a room he didn't want to be caught leaving. Behind it, the sky over the Vespera grounds had gone the color of old pewter—flat, heavy, wrong for the season. The monsoon was supposed to be three weeks out. The forecasters had said so twice, in the confident voices of men who read weather from a room with no windows on it.

Dov came up the companionway ladder with grease to his elbows and doubt all over his face. "Port engine's holding," he said. "Barely. She's running hot if I push her past twelve hundred RPM."

"Then we don't push her." Quentin set down the mug. "One dive. In and out. If the box doesn't come free in forty minutes, we cut the lift bag loose and come back after the front passes."

"And if the front doesn't pass?"

She looked at him. He looked back. Neither of them said what they were both thinking: that fronts like this one sometimes settled in for a month, and that Mara Kessler's money was already spent, and that the navy's hydrophone buoy had been drifting over the wreck site like a patient eye since yesterday afternoon.

"Forty minutes," Quentin said again, as if repetition were a kind of anchor.

Suri was already at the stern rail, coiling the tag lines with the quick economy she'd learned from watching Quentin do it wrong first and then right. Nineteen years old, all elbows and certainty. She'd signed on two seasons ago off a dock in Tanjung Rhu because Quentin fed her and Dov argued about it and then stopped arguing, which was how most things aboard got decided.

"Water's going to be soup down there," Suri said. "Surge is already rolling through at ten meters."

"Then you stay topside." Quentin pulled her wetsuit from the hook. "I go down alone. You run the winch. Dov runs the boat. Nobody else gets wet."

"You always say that."

"It's always true."

* * *

The Vespera sat in sixty feet of green murk, and the murk was moving.

Quentin felt it before she saw it—the whole water column breathing, pushing her against the hull and pulling her away again, a slow tidal hand kneading everything above the seafloor. Her torch beam swung across the officer's quarters' torn plating and found the strongbox where she'd left it two days ago: welded into the bulkhead behind the captain's desk, a black rectangle furred with coral, its seams still sharp under twenty years of growth.

Someone had wanted this thing to survive the sinking. That was the part that kept her up at night. Not hidden—welded. Displayed, almost, to anyone who knew where to look.

She clipped her pry bar to the lifting harness and went to work.

The surge made it brutal. Every time she set the bar's edge into the weld seam, the water shoved her sideways and she lost her bite and had to start again, breath rasping through the regulator, hands working by feel more than sight. The welds were good—navy work, done in a hurry but done right—but twenty years of salt had gotten into them anyway, and rust flakes rose around her like red snow.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. The bar groaned. A seam gave with a sensation she felt in her teeth.

Above her, muffled through forty feet of water, she heard the *Magpie's Rest* change its note. Engine pitch climbing. Then Suri's voice on the comm, thin and distorted: "—coming in fast, Q, I can see it coming—"

Quentin looked up. Through the murk the surface was a ceiling of hammered tin, and beyond it, far to the southwest, the horizon had gone a color she had only seen once before, off Sumatra, the year she'd learned that the sea could take everything a person owned in an afternoon and not consider it work.

The squall line. Black as spilled ink, and eating the distance between them.

"Q." Suri again, sharper now. "Barometer fell thirty points in twenty minutes. Dov says we have to move *now*."

"Two more minutes." The second seam split. The third held. She braced both boots against the bulkhead and put her whole weight into the bar, and the weld tore with a shriek she felt along her spine, and the strongbox sagged free into her arms—heavier than it should have been, dense with whatever secrets it had been keeping.

She clipped the lift bag, dumped air into it, and the box rose off her hands toward the pale smudge of the surface. She kicked after it.

* * *

She broke the surface into a world already ending.

The wind hit her face like a slap—forty knots, maybe fifty, rain coming horizontal and hard enough to sting. The *Magpie's Rest* was pitching hard at the end of her drift line, and Suri was leaning out over the stern rail with a boat hook, screaming something the wind took apart before it arrived. The lift bag bobbed twenty meters off, dragging the strongbox, being carried away from the boat with every swell.

Quentin swam.

It was the hardest swim of her life. The sea had turned to chaos around her, wave trains crossing from three directions, shoving her under and spitting her up, filling her mouth with salt every time she tried to breathe. She caught the lift line on a swell crest and wrapped it twice around her fist and let the box's weight anchor her while she clawed hand over hand toward the stern, and the stern kept rising and falling four feet either side of her reach like something taunting her.

Suri got the boat hook into her harness on the third try.

"I've got her—" Suri's voice, raw, close. "Dov, I've got her, come take—"

Quentin got one arm over the rail. Got a knee up. And that was when the wave came.

It wasn't the biggest wave of the storm. It was just the one that arrived at the wrong angle, catching the *Magpie's Rest* broadside and rolling her hard to starboard, and the deck went vertical under Quentin's hands, and Suri—who had never let go of the rail, who had never once let go of anything—went over it.

No scream. Just a flash of orange slicker, there and gone, swallowed by the gray.

"*SURI!*"

Quentin went back over the rail without deciding to. The cold hit her like a wall of fists. She surfaced, spun, found nothing—gray water, gray rain, gray sky, all of it identical, all of it indifferent. Somewhere below her hung a strongbox full of truth worth more than any of their lives, and she did not think about it even once.

There. Off the port quarter—a flicker of orange, a hand.

She swam. The storm shoved her back; she swam anyway, arms burning, lungs full of broken glass. When she reached Suri the girl was treading water with the terrible calm of shock, eyes too wide, lips already graying.

"Got you." Quentin got an arm across her chest. "Got you. Kick. *Kick.*"

They made it to the stern together, somehow, the two of them and the sea taking turns deciding whether they lived. Dov appeared over the rail like a miracle with grease on his forearms, hauling Suri up and over in one motion, and Quentin came after on her own strength because her hands refused to do anything else.

* * *

The winch jammed at 11:40.

They heard it happen—a shriek of metal from the drum housing, then silence, then the sickening slack-clunk of the cable settling wrong. The strongbox, half-raised, hung somewhere beneath the hull, swinging against the keel with every roll. Dov got the housing open and swore at what he found inside: the drum warped, the pawl sheared, the whole mechanism twisted by the same wave that had taken Suri overboard.

"We can't raise it," he shouted over the wind. "We can't lower it. It's stuck where it is."

"Cut it loose?" Suri asked. She was wrapped in a blanket in the wheelhouse, shaking, her teeth chattering around the words. "Just—cut the cable?"

"And drop it into eighty feet of open water during a monsoon?" Dov shook his head. "We'd never find it again. Never."

Quentin stood at the helm with both hands locked on the wheel and made the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since dawn. The *Magpie's Rest* was taking water—not fast, but taking it, a slow seep through a seam aft that Dov had patched twice this year and would not survive another beating. The port engine ran hot. The barometer was still falling. And hanging under their keel was a box they couldn't raise, couldn't drop, and couldn't abandon, dragging their draft deeper with every mile.

"The cove," she said. "North point of Karang Tiga. Three hours if we can hold eight knots."

"In this?"

"In this."

Dov looked at her for a long moment, rain streaming off his jaw. Then he nodded once and went below to fight the engines.

* * *

They limped north through the worst of it.

Quentin drove with the instinct of fifteen borrowed-name years, reading the water by feel, finding the channels between the wave sets the way she'd once found the gaps between surveillance patrols. Suri bailed. Dov nursed the engines and sang to them, low and tuneless, the way he did when machines were dying. Below the keel, the jammed cable sang its own note—a low groan through the hull that none of them mentioned, because mentioning it would mean discussing what was on the other end of it.

Twice the seas came aboard. Once the bilge alarm screamed until Dov silenced it with a wrench. The leak gained on them by inches, and the cove gained on them by miles, and the race between those two numbers was the whole of the world for three hours.

They came around Karang Tiga's north point at 15:10 with the hull groaning and the wind dropping, as if the storm had spent itself just enough to let them live. The cove opened up ahead—green water, black rock, palms bent double along the ridge—and Quentin brought the *Magpie's Rest* in slow, feeling the drag of the box beneath her, and dropped anchor in eight meters of sheltered calm.

Silence, after. The ringing kind.

Suri slid down the wheelhouse wall and sat on the deck, blanket and all, and started laughing—a shaky, unhinged sound that Quentin recognized because she'd made it herself once, after Sumatra. Dov came up from the engine room wiping his hands and looking ten years older.

"Hull's leaking worse at rest," he reported. "I can patch it proper tomorrow, dry conditions. Tonight I keep her pumped."

"The box?"

"Still hanging under us. Still stuck." He hesitated. "Q—we can't open it, can't move it, can't leave it. We're anchored to it. Literally."

Quentine looked down through the deck plates, as if sixty feet of clear green water were transparent enough to show her what waited below: a sealed steel box, welded shut by dead men, full of the truth about Elias Hartwell and the Vespera and everyone who had buried them both.

A prize they'd nearly died for, and couldn't touch.

She was still standing there when Suri pointed through the wheelhouse glass toward the southern entrance of the cove, where the rain was thinning, and said quietly:

"Q. There's a boat."
