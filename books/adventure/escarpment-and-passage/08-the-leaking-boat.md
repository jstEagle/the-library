## 8. The Leaking Boat

Suri had learned the boat by sound before she'd learned it by name. The *Cormorant* talked in her sleep—a tick in the cooling line, a rattle where the galley drawer never quite shut—and Suri had spent two years learning the language of her. So when the bilge pump cycled a third time in an hour, she didn't need the gauge to tell her what the storm had done.

She was on watch alone when the numbers came up wrong. Water over the sole, six inches and climbing slow, seeping through a seam aft that the monsoon had worried open like a dog working a bone. She woke Dov first because that was protocol, then Quentin, then Mara Kessler, who slept like a cat in a strange house—light, coiled, one eye already open.

By dawn they had the leak slowed but not stopped, the *Cormorant* wedged into the green shelter of a cove whose name wasn't on any chart Suri owned, and Calloway's cutter sitting offshore like a sentence waiting to be pronounced.

That was when the arguing started.

* * *

It began quietly, which was how the worst things aboard always began. Dov in the engine room, his voice pitched low so only Suri would hear it, his hands still moving on the patch job even as he talked, because Dov's hands never stopped moving when his mind was working.

"We take the deal." He didn't look at her. "You heard him. Immunity. A clean identity—for all of us, he said. New papers, new names, no record. Suri, I've got eleven years of debts following me around like a tide. You think I want to spend them running from a navy cutter?"

"He said *her*, Dov. The box and Mara. We were an afterthought."

"An afterthought with paperwork attached." He tightened a clamp harder than it needed. "Quentin's not going to say it, so somebody has to. We are three people in a leaking boat against a government. There's no brave version of this math."

Suri leaned against the bulkhead and watched him work. Dov was the oldest thing on the boat besides the hull itself—older than Quentin's command, older than the name painted on the stern. He'd been there when Suri came aboard at nineteen with nothing but a duffel and a lie about her experience, and he'd taught her engines without ever once asking why a girl that young knew how to disappear.

"I'm not saying you're wrong," she said carefully. "I'm saying ask her first."

"She won't answer. Have you looked at her?"

Suri had. That was the problem. Since the cove, since Calloway's visit, Quentin Hartwell had gone somewhere none of them could follow—not belowdecks, not off the boat, just *away*, standing her watches in perfect silence, eating nothing, answering questions with a nod or nothing at all. Fifteen years of borrowed names, Mara had said, and Suri finally understood what that cost: a person who had practiced vanishing so long she could do it standing right in front of you.

* * *

Mara made her case at noon, in the cockpit, with the rain coming down in gray sheets and the cutter a smudge on the horizon. She'd spread a paper chart across the table and weighted the corners with mugs, and her finger traced a line west with the confidence of someone who had memorized distances the way other people memorized prayers.

"International waters. Two hundred and ten nautical miles. If we run tonight, ahead of the squall line, we make it by tomorrow nightfall. Once we're out, the box goes to whatever tribunal will take it, and Calloway's career becomes the story instead of ours."

Dov stood at the companionway with his arms crossed. "In a boat taking water. With a jammed winch, a cracked seam, and a crew that hasn't slept."

"In a boat that's taken worse," Mara said. "I've read this hull's history. She was built to run."

"She was built to *salvage*," Dov shot back. "There's a difference, and it's the difference between choosing your fights and dying in someone else's."

"And what's your proposal? Sit here until he comes alongside? Take his immunity and hand over my father?" Mara's voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and Suri saw her close her fist around it. Twenty years of buying access, twenty years of patience, and it all came down to a leaking boat and a man in a pressed shirt offshore.

Neither of them said the obvious thing: that both plans required Quentin, and Quentin wasn't there.

Suri climbed the ladder to the flying bridge, where Quentin stood at the rail in the rain, watching the cutter the way you watch a shark fin—without blinking, without hope, just measuring.

* * *

"You're going to have to pick," Suri said.

Quentin didn't turn. Rain ran off the brim of her hood in a steady thread. "Pick what."

"Them. Us. Dov wants to deal. Mara wants to run. And you're—" Suri groped for the word and hated the one she found. "You're *gone*. You're standing right here and you're gone. I've seen you tired, I've seen you scared, I've seen you after the Vespera dive when your hands wouldn't stop shaking. This is different. This is you deciding whether we're worth coming back for."

The silence stretched long enough that Suri counted the swells between them. Four. Five.

"You don't get to do that," Suri went on, and her voice shook, and she let it. "You don't get to teach me engines and charts and how to read weather, and then vanish when it counts. I signed onto a crew, not a ghost. So somebody has to say it out loud, and apparently it's going to be the youngest person on this boat: *what is this crew for?*"

Quentin turned then. Up close, in the flat gray light, Suri could see what the silence had been costing her—the hollows under her eyes, the jaw set like something braced against a load. When she spoke, her voice was rough, like it hadn't been used in days.

"When I hired you, you told me you'd left home because staying meant becoming someone else's idea of you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer." Quentin stepped closer, rain streaming between them. "Fifteen years ago I decided that being invisible was the price of being safe. No records, no names, no ties. I told myself it was freedom. It was a tomb with good ventilation." She looked past Suri, down into the cockpit where Dov and Mara still stood over their competing futures. "Then I picked up a broke engineer who couldn't hold a job anywhere with a payroll, and a kid with a fake résumé and hands like she'd been born in an engine room. And somewhere along the way I stopped being a woman hiding on a boat and started being a *crew*. Do you know the difference?"

Suri shook her head.

"A boat is where you sleep. A crew is what you'd flood a compartment for." Quentin's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "This crew is for each other. Not the box. Not Elias's name, though God knows I want it back. Not Mara's father, not my clean slate, not Dov's debts. Each other. That's the cargo. Everything else is ballast."

"So we don't deal. We don't run."

"We patch the hull," Quentin said. "And then we decide how to leave. But we leave as us—all four—or we don't leave at all. Go tell Dov I said the math includes things he can't put numbers on. Tell Mara—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, old grief wearing new clothes, "—tell Mara she doesn't have to buy her father's honor anymore. Tonight she'll see why."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Dov found the crack's true length by candlelight and swearing, a foot longer than the daylight survey suggested, wandering along a seam the yard should have welded properly a decade ago. Suri pumped while he patched—epoxy and canvas and a steel plate ground down from the spare bracket, driven home with bolts scavenged from the winch housing, which meant the winch stayed dead, which meant if they ran they'd be doing it light. Nobody said that part out loud. Everybody thought it.

Mara worked too. That surprised Suri most of all. She'd expected the client to stand clear, hands folded, preserving the distance money buys. Instead Mara stripped to a tank top and held plates and passed tools and learned the boat's language fast, the way clever people do, and somewhere around midnight, wedged in the lazarette passing bolts up to Dov, she started talking about her father—not the executive officer, not the scapegoat, but the man. How he'd taught her Morse with a flashlight through the wall of her bedroom. How the last letter he sent from prison was mostly about the weather, because the censors read everything else.

"My mother burned his papers after the inquiry," Mara said, to no one, to the dark under the deckplates. "All except the copies he'd hidden inside a hollowed-out Bible. He trusted the navy would eventually do the right thing. That was his mistake. Mine was thinking I could do it alone."

Quentin appeared at the hatch then, silhouetted against the cockpit lights, and said nothing at all. But she handed Mara a rag, and took the next shift holding plate, and Suri watched two women who had spent twenty years as rivals learn each other's rhythm in the dark, bolt by bolt, breath by breath.

At 0400 the pump cycle stretched from every eight minutes to every forty. Dov ran a thumb along the patched seam, grunted—which from Dov was a medal ceremony—and said, "She'll hold. In this sea state. Ask me again in a force nine."

They ate cold rice in the cockpit, four of them shoulder to shoulder under the dodger, the cutter's riding lights burning steady and patient out in the black. Nobody spoke about it. Everybody watched it.

It was Mara who finally said, quietly, "He'll board at first light. He has to—he can't risk us making a run in another squall, and the weather window closes tomorrow evening. He thinks we're broken. He thinks we're deciding."

Quentin set down her mug. For the first time in three days, her eyes were fully lit, fully *here*, and Suri felt the whole boat come about on that look like a sail finding wind.

"He's half right," Quentin said. "We are deciding. We decided. Now we need one more piece—something worth more to Calloway than this boat and everyone on it combined." She glanced at Mara, and something passed between them, quick and unreadable and absolute. "How attached are you to your father's originals?"

Mara went very still. "They're all I have of him."

"They're also the only leverage in this cove heavier than his cutter." Quentin's voice was gentle and merciless at once. "I'm not asking yet. I'm telling you to think about it. Because at first light, we're going to give Calloway exactly what he came for—and it isn't going to be us."

Out in the dark, the cutter's lights blinked once, twice, steady as a pulse. Suri sat among her people, in a patched boat, on a sea that wanted them dead, and felt—for the first time in her life—unmistakably found.
