## 10. Miles and Years

The journalist's name was Okafor, and she arrived the way Quentin had once learned to arrive everywhere: quietly, with a borrowed bag and a face that asked for nothing.

She came out to the boat in a water taxi three weeks after the storm, when the *Corvus* was finally whole again—new plating over the patched hull, a winch that actually winched, Dov's engine running so clean it purred. She climbed aboard with a waterproof satchel held against her chest like a child, and she did not shake hands. She just looked at Quentin for a long moment and said, "You're Hartwell. The real one."

"Yeah," Quentin said. It still caught in her throat, that word. *Yeah.* Like a key turning in a lock she'd welded shut fifteen years ago.

Okafor sat at the galley table while Suri made tea nobody drank, and she read the copies—Mara's father's originals had gone to the sea off the wreck field, but Mara had never been a woman who kept only one copy of anything. The duplicates had ridden out the passage in a dry box under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting. Orders signed by a captain who'd retired to a garden in Corvallis. Hydrophone logs proving the *Vespera*'s distress calls were received and answered with silence. And the recording: Elias's voice, young and steady, reading back lawful commands he was obeying to the letter, forty minutes before the court-martial swore he'd disobeyed them.

Okafor listened to it twice. The second time, she closed her eyes.

"He sounds like you," she said.

"He sounded like my mother," Quentin said. "I sound like nobody."

"You sound like someone who's about to be very famous," Okafor said, and Suri laughed, and even Dov smiled from the engine hatch, and Quentin found she didn't flinch from it the way she would have a month ago.

* * *

The inquiry opened in the capital on a Tuesday, in a hall with bad acoustics and too many flags. They watched it on the boat's small screen, the signal stuttering through the monsoon's tail end, rain drumming the cabin roof like fingers impatient for what came next.

Mara testified first. She wore gray, no jewelry, and she spoke the way she dove—straight down, no wasted motion. When the panel chairman asked her to state her name for the record, she said, "Mara Kessler. Daughter of Executive Officer Aldous Kessler, falsely condemned alongside Lieutenant Elias Hartwell." She said *falsely* like she was setting something heavy down on a table and daring anyone to pick it up again.

Quentin watched her old rival's face on the flickering screen and understood, finally, what Mara had been doing for twenty years. Not avenging. Not even grieving. Just carrying. Walking the same corridor of records and sealed files and polite refusals, year after year, buying access the way other people bought shoes, because stopping would have meant admitting her father had drowned twice—once in the water, once in the paperwork.

"She looks tired," Suri said.

"She looks free," Dov said.

Both of them were right.

When the recording played in the hall—the same forty minutes Quentin had listened to alone in a cove while Calloway's cutter waited offshore—the room went so quiet the microphones picked up the ventilation system. Elias's voice filled a government chamber fifteen years after they'd stripped his name from the rolls. Quentin sat with her hands flat on the table and let it happen. She didn't look away. That was the whole discipline of it now, she was learning. Not looking away.

* * *

Calloway resigned his commission before the second week of testimony. The statement from the navy used the word *retirement*, which everyone understood to mean the same thing *insurance investigator* had always meant. There was talk of charges, quieter talk of pardons, the usual machinery grinding its slow arithmetic. He would not hang. Men like Calloway rarely hung; they relocated, rebranded, resurfaced somewhere the water was warmer and the records were thinner.

"I used to think that meant we lost," Quentin said. She was standing at the rail watching the harbor lights come on, and Dov was beside her with two cups of something that was technically coffee.

"And now?"

"Now I think the losing was never the point. He spent thirty years making sure nobody said the truth out loud. Now it's in a hearing transcript. It's in Okafor's paper. It's going to be in a history book some kid falls asleep on in school." She turned the cup in her hands. "He can retire anywhere he wants. He can't un-say any of it."

Dov considered this with the gravity he gave all things, including engine parts and weather. "Your brother's name comes off the shame list next month. Officially."

"Rehabilitation review," Quentin said. "That's the term. Like he's a building."

"Buildings get restored," Dov said. "It's not the worst metaphor."

Quentin almost smiled. Fifteen years ago she'd have called it exactly that—a renovation, a facade job, paint over rot—and she'd have spat on it. But she'd learned something in the cove, patching the hull by lamplight while a cutter's searchlight swept the water: restoration wasn't erasure done backwards. It was choosing, plank by plank, what you carried forward.

Elias would never dive again. But his name might, someday, be spoken by strangers without flinching. That was more than she'd dared to want since the night the verdict came down and she'd walked into the ocean with a new name and no intention of surfacing as herself.

* * *

Mara left on a Thursday.

No ceremony. She came aboard at dawn with her single bag—the woman had crossed an ocean of years with less luggage than Suri took to market—and stood on the deck looking at the *Corvus* with something almost like affection.

"They offered me a seat on the inquiry board," she said. "Advisory. Permanent."

"Are you taking it?"

"For a while. Someone has to make sure the fixers don't fix it back." She looked out at the water. "Twenty years I thought if I could just get this box open, I'd be done. Finished. Like there was a version of me on the other side of it, whole."

"And?"

"And there isn't. There's just me, and the next thing." She glanced sideways, and for one unguarded moment the academy rivalry fell away entirely and Quentin saw the girl who'd once stayed up all night arguing about decompression tables because being right mattered more than sleep. "You got there faster than I did. Took me twenty years and my father's grave to figure out the box was never going to hold enough."

"What are you going to do with the advisory seat?"

"Bury them in paperwork," Mara said, and this time she did smile. "It's what they taught me."

At the dock she shook Quentin's hand—formal, brief, and then, breaking her own protocol, held it one second longer. "Hartwell," she said. Testing it. Giving it back.

"Kessler," Quentin returned. "Go be loud."

Mara laughed, actually laughed, and walked down the gangway into the noise of the morning market, and Quentin stood at the rail and watched until the crowd took her, and felt—not grief, exactly. More like the moment after a dive when you break the surface and the world is bigger than the one you left.

* * *

They stayed through the season. There was work—there was always work, honest wrecks with honest cargo, none of it forbidden, none of it haunted—and the *Corvus* went out and came back and went out again, and slowly the boat stopped feeling like a hiding place and started feeling like what it had probably always been: a home that happened to float.

Suri repainted the name on the transom herself, and underneath it, small, where only someone at the dock would see it, she painted a line Quentin didn't notice for two days:

*Q. Hartwell, Master.*

"You could've asked," Quentin said, staring at it.

"You'd have said no," Suri said. "So I didn't ask."

"That's mutiny."

"That's Tuesday," Suri said, and grinned, and ducked below before Quentin could decide whether to be angry, which was Suri's oldest trick and still worked every time.

But Quentin stood there a long while, looking at her own name in fresh white paint, drying in the sun, visible to anyone. Anyone. A customs officer. A patrol boat. A man like Calloway, if men like him ever came sniffing again. Fifteen years of practice said cover it, sand it, burn it off before dark. Fifteen years of practice said a name is a debt collectors can find.

Fifteen years of practice was wrong. That was the thing she kept having to learn, over and over, like a knot you tie wrong on purpose for so long your hands forget the right way. A name wasn't a debt. It was an anchor. It only dragged you down if you cut it loose.

She let the paint dry.

* * *

On the last calm day of the season, Quentin went diving.

Not salvage. No contract, no wreck, no box, no bottom even—they'd motored out past the shelf where the seafloor dropped away into blue-black nothing, and she'd gone over the side with a single tank and no task at all, which Dov called irresponsible and Suri called vacation.

She descended into open water. No walls, no wreck, no reference points except the light falling from above and her own bubbles rising. For fifteen years she had measured her life in nautical miles—distance put between herself and the courtroom, the verdict, the name. Miles were safe. Miles were countable. You could always put another one behind you.

But miles ran out. She knew that now. You could run to the edge of the chart and find Calloway waiting, or Mara, or your own brother's voice on a recording, patient as tide. Everything she'd buried had a way of surfacing. The only question—the one the whole terrible year had finally answered—was whether you surfaced with it, or let it come up alone.

She hung in the blue and breathed, and thought about Elias at nineteen, teaching her to clear her mask in the shallows off their grandmother's beach, laughing through his regulator when she panicked and bolted for the surface. *Slow,* he'd told her. *The water's not going anywhere.*

His name came off the shame list in eleven days. She'd be there. In a courtroom, on the record, under her own name, in front of cameras and clerks and whatever fixers still had jobs. Let them look. Let them write it down. *Quentin Hartwell, sister of the accused.* She'd spent half her life making sure no record contained those words, and now she wanted them carved somewhere permanent, wanted them read aloud by strangers, wanted the weight of them.

Below her, the deep went down forever, patient and dark and full of everything the sea keeps. Above her, the surface burned silver, and somewhere beyond it a boat waited with two people aboard who knew exactly who she was—every borrowed name, every buried year, every mile—and had chosen her anyway. Had chosen her *because*, Suri insisted, though Dov maintained it was *despite*, and the argument had become a running joke neither of them would surrender.

Quentin kicked upward, slow, the way Elias taught her.

The light got brighter. She counted her breaths instead of her miles, and for the first time in fifteen years, the number that mattered was not how far she'd run.

It was how long she'd stay.
