## 1. The Flood Gives Back

The river turned against itself on the third night of the melt.

Ada Underhill heard it from his cot in the salvage shed—a sound like a herd of animals moving under the floor of the world—and lay still in the dark, cataloguing it the way his father had taught him to catalogue everything: weight, direction, intent. Water that loud meant water going somewhere new. Water going somewhere new meant ground opened up, and ground opened up meant work.

By dawn the Verrin had chewed a fresh channel through the eastern bend below Lowmark, a quarter mile of raw brown scar where the old meander had slept for fifty years behind a wall of willow. The town's licensed diggers were already down there when Ada arrived, standing at the lip of the cut with their hands in their pockets, watching the flood recede like an audience waiting for a curtain to do something.

"Claim staked," said Bram Ostler, who held the northern half of the bend, and jerked his thumb southward. "Yours is the ugly side."

Ada's side was indeed the ugly side: a slumped terrace of clay and gravel threaded with the pale roots of drowned willows, steaming in the early light. He worked it alone, as he always did, with a long-handled spade, a trowel, a screen, and the particular patience of a man who had learned before he could read that the earth gives back only what you're willing to wait for.

He'd been digging the valley for ten years. Not the way the Provision's surveyors dug—grids and permits and ledgers—but the way his father had taught him, reading the soil like handwriting. Silt told you what the river had carried. Gravel told you how fast. A seam of dark earth inside lighter earth told you men had been here, and burned something, or buried something, or both. His father used to say the valley was a book that kept rewriting itself, and every flood was just the author revising.

Ada had sold eleven years of revisions to Lowmark's pawnbrokers and one discreet dealer in Marrow Ford: coins with dead kings' faces, a bronze pin shaped like a heron, pottery so old its makers' names had worn off the world entirely. Quiet sales. Licensed claims, mostly legitimate, occasionally not. He moved along the river the way his father had moved along it, and the way his father had finally stopped moving, in a trench outside a town whose name Ada made a point of never saying aloud.

He was three hours into the morning, sunburned across the shoulders, when the trowel rang.

Not the dull knock of stone. A clear note, bronze-on-bronze, the sound a bell makes through cloth. Ada went still, the way he always went still, and felt the old animal lift of it rise through him—the thrill that never dulled, the one thing in ten drifting years that had never once disappointed him. Something down there. Something that had waited.

He cleared it over two hours, working outward from the note, refusing to hurry, because hurrying broke things and broken things sold for nothing and, worse, told less. What emerged from the clay was a chest the length of his forearm, cast bronze, green with age but uncracked, uncorroded, its seams still tight enough to hold water out for whatever centuries it had spent drowning. It was heavy past reason. The lid bore a raised device, stamped deep by a die rather than scratched by hand: a circle bisected by a vertical line, with three short bars descending from the line like roots.

Ada sat back on his heels in the mud and looked at it for a long time.

He knew the mark. Not from books—Lowmark's Provision lending house kept exactly four books, and none of them were about anything older than the toll schedules. He knew it from the mouths of drunk old men, the ones who drank at the Ferryman's Rest past closing and talked about the war the way other men talked about weather. *Ostrava*, they said, and spat, or crossed themselves, or laughed too hard. A nation that wasn't on any map. A nation that had been on all of them once, upstream, at the source, until it wasn't—until the name came off the maps and out of the ledgers and out of the school primers so completely that the only place it survived was in the curses of men too old and too soaked to care what the Provision's listeners heard.

*They erased a whole country,* one of them had told Ada once, gripping his sleeve with yellow fingers. *Not conquered it, boy. Erased it. You can't fight what isn't written down.*

And here, in Ada's ugly claim, in clay the river had just decided to give back, was a bronze chest stamped with the unwritten name.

He should have reported it. That was the law, such as the Provision enforced it: finds of significance went to the survey office, were assessed, were taxed, were entered into a ledger, and the finder received a finder's fee calculated to the copper and a receipt he couldn't read without help. Ada had reported finds before. He'd watched a funeral urn worth more than his decade of earnings get carried onto the survey boat while a clerk wrote *ceramic vessel, degraded, nominal value* in a hand like bird tracks.

His hands were shaking. He noticed this with detached interest, the way he noticed weather. Ten years of discipline, and his body had already decided what his mind was still arguing about.

The chest went into his salvage sack, wrapped in his oilskin, buried under tools. The hole went filled. The terrace went raked smooth behind him, and he walked north along the bank at a working pace, nodding to Bram, complaining about the clay, asking after Bram's bad knee, being—for the benefit of anyone watching—exactly the man he appeared to be: a lean, quiet, unremarkable digger with a bad morning and nothing to show for it.

* * *

The rented cellar belonged to a widow named Grett who asked no questions because Ada paid a month ahead and fixed her gate hinge without being asked. It was dry, dark, and smelled of onions, which was more than most hiding places offered. By lamplight he set the chest on a plank table and looked at it properly for the first time.

Up close, the seal was finer work than he'd first thought. The three descending bars weren't decorative; they were lettering, or near enough—characters from some script the valley had forgotten on purpose. Around the rim of the lid ran a band of the same script, tiny, precise, machine-regular, as if stamped by something that did not tire. And the lock—

Ada crouched over the lock for a long time before he touched it.

It was a warded mechanism, but unlike any warding he'd seen, and he'd seen plenty; picking locks was the second trade his father had left him, after reading dirt. The wards weren't the usual simple combs. They were cut in interlocking spirals, and at the center of the keyhole, where a bit would turn, there was a socket shaped like nothing he recognized—elongated, slightly curved, tapered at one end.

Like a bone, he thought. Like a finger bone, or a—

He stopped himself. He didn't know why he stopped himself. There was no reason for the shape to snag in him the way it did, no reason for his father's voice to surface so suddenly, so clearly, ten years drowned: *The best key is the one nobody knows is a key.*

Ada sat down on Grett's onion crate and put his face in his hands.

His father had been dead a decade, and still the man managed to be in every room Ada entered. In the way he screened soil. In the way he checked a lock's wards with a straw before committing iron. In the fact that Ada could not look at a beautiful mechanism without hearing him narrate it. His father had died in a trench with his notes confiscated and his name struck from the guild rolls, and Ada had spent ten years making sure he left no notes and joined no rolls, and here he was, sitting in a cellar, holding a nation that didn't exist, hearing advice from a ghost.

Three days, he decided. Three days to work the lock, learn what the chest wanted, and decide what to do. Report it and take the fee. Sell it whole to the Marrow Ford dealer, who paid for silence and never asked provenance. Or—

Or find out why drunk old men spat when they said the name, and why the Provision's maps had a blank space at the river's source where every other mapmaker in history had drawn mountains.

He worked the lock that night and the next and the next, by lamplight, with picks he ground himself from clock springs. The spiral wards yielded slowly, beautifully, each one a small argument he won. But the central socket defeated him. No pick fit it. No tool he owned fit it. Whatever the chest wanted, it wanted something specific, something shaped, something *carried in a person*—

On the third evening, someone knocked on Grett's street door above him. Three knocks, even, unhurried. Then, after a pause, three more.

Ada blew out the lamp and stood in the dark with a clock-spring pick folded in his fist, listening. Above him, Grett's slow footsteps crossed to the door, opened it, and a woman's voice said, low and pleasant, "Evening, mistress. I'm looking for the digger who rents your cellar. Tall, quiet, sells to Ferren at the pawnshop."

Grett, bless her mercenary heart, said nothing at all.

"I saw him carry something off the east bend," the voice continued. "Something heavy. I'd very much like to see it."

Ada moved to the foot of the stairs, silent on the boards he'd learned to read, and waited in the dark with his heart going like a dredge pump. The street door closed. Footsteps descended—light, deliberate, the steps of someone who had already guessed she was expected to be heard.

She stopped at the bottom of the stairs. Lamplight from the street door above laid a thin gold line down the steps and across the cellar floor, and in it Ada saw her wrist, resting on the rail, and on the wrist a tattoo, small and black and old: a circle bisected by a vertical line, with three short bars descending from it like roots.

"You've had it three days," the woman said into the dark, not unkindly. "I've had it my whole life."
