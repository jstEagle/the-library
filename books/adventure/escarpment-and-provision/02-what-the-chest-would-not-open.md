## 2. What the Chest Would Not Open

The cellar Ada rented cost him four coppers a week and smelled of turnips and old grief, which was more or less the going rate for privacy in Lowmark. It sat beneath a chandler's shop on Rope Street, low enough that he had to stoop, dark enough that he worked by lamp even at noon. He carried the chest down in three trips—first the lid, wrapped in his oilskin, then the body of it slung across his shoulders like a dead animal, then the tools.

It was heavier than bronze had any right to be. That was the first thing he'd noticed, standing in the flood-cut bank with the river still sucking at his boots: not just weight but *density*, as if the thing were packed to its seams with something denser than metal. The second thing he'd noticed was the survey boat coming around the bend, white sail, Provision pennant snapping, and by then he'd already stopped noticing anything except how fast a man could move when he decided not to be seen.

He'd left the chest half-buried overnight in the willow tangle upstream, gone back for it at dawn when the boat had moved on, and told himself the whole time that this was ordinary. Diggers hid things. Diggers always hid things. A find reported honestly was a find taxed, licensed, argued over, and eventually owned by someone whose hands had never touched mud. His father had taught him that lesson without ever meaning to—taught it in the way a man teaches by dying poor while other men grew rich off the maps he'd drawn.

So: the cellar, the lamp, the lock.

* * *

The lock was set into the chest's face behind a hinged bronze plate worked with the seal—a tower above water, a star above the tower, lines radiating outward like something seen or something shining. Ada had seen the mark before, though never clean. Drunk old men scratched it into table tops in river towns and then scrubbed at it with their sleeves, ashamed. Once, in a market two hundred miles downriver, he'd watched a peddler sell a cracked bowl bearing the same star-and-tower, and an old woman had crossed herself with a gesture Ada didn't recognize, three fingers, inward, toward the heart.

The lockwork itself was another matter. He knelt before it the first night with his picks laid out on a folded cloth, and within ten minutes he stopped working and simply looked.

Seven levers. Staggered, not stacked—the way his father built them, the way no guild locksmith in the valley built them, because staggered levers defeated the raking techniques every apprentice learned first. The gates were cut at angles instead of square. And there, at the throat of the keyway, a false ward shaped exactly like the real one, offset by a hair's width, designed to let a pick slide in and convince the picker the mechanism was already open when it wasn't.

Ada knew that false ward. Not the shape of it—he knew the *hand* of it. His father had a way of filing wards so the deception sat slightly deeper than honest work would put it, a kind of signature, a joke only another locksmith would ever hear. Ada had spent his childhood holding lamps in dig shelters while his father filed locks for their caches, humming, and the humming had a rhythm too, and the rhythm of this lock, if locks could hum, was the same.

He sat back on his heels in the turnip-smelling dark and felt something crack open in his chest that had nothing to do with bronze.

"Hello, old man," he said quietly, to no one. To the lock. To whoever had built it, who might as well have been the ghost kneeling beside him.

* * *

Three days. That was what it took, and it should have taken one, and the reason it took three was that Ada kept stopping.

The first day he worked the false ward, found the true keyway beneath it, and got four of seven levers to lift before the fifth refused—not jammed, but *refusing*, springing back with a soft deliberate tension that felt less like mechanics than like opinion. Someone had built this lock to be difficult in a particular order. You couldn't rush it. You had to listen to it, feel for the sequence, and the sequence changed—or seemed to—each time he reset the levers. Either the lock was broken in some way he couldn't diagnose, or it was smarter than any lock he'd ever met, and both possibilities made him want to laugh and put his head against the cold bronze and stay there.

Between attempts he examined the rest of the chest. The seams were poured, not soldered—no torch marks, no repair. The interior, when he finally got the lid plate loose enough to peer through the gap, was lined in lead, and the lead was lined in something darker, wax maybe, pressed with characters he didn't know: angular, dense, running right to left. Not the valley script. Not anything from the old imperial digs his father had worked. Something else entire.

A nation erased from every map. He'd heard the phrase from drunks, from a blind ferryman who claimed his grandmother had sung in a language with no name, from a Provision clerk who'd had too much wine at a licensing dinner and said, *there used to be nine towns upriver, boy, and now there are six, and if you're smart you won't ask where the other three went.* Ada had asked, once, at seventeen, freshly abandoned and stupid with grief, and the clerk had sobered instantly and looked at him the way men look at a lit fuse, and Ada had learned to stop asking.

But the chest hadn't stopped asking. The chest sat in his rented cellar asking all night long.

* * *

On the second night, someone knocked on the chandler's door overhead, and Ada froze with his picks in the keyway and listened to footsteps cross the floorboards above, pause, and move on. On the third night, the footsteps didn't move on.

He heard the shop door open at an hour when no honest customer came. Heard the chandler's voice, puzzled, then placating. Then a woman's voice, low and even, saying something Ada couldn't make out, and the chandler laughing nervously, and then—worse than anything—the sound of footsteps descending the cellar stairs before anyone had invited them down.

Ada closed his hand around his picks and turned on the stool, lamp at his hip, and waited.

She came down sideways, the way people did in cellars built for shorter men, and she came down looking directly at him, which most people didn't bother with. Mid-thirties, maybe. River-weathered, with the forearms of someone who handled rope or oars for a living. Dark hair tied back with what looked like fishing line. She carried no lantern; she didn't need one, because her eyes went straight past him to the chest, and stayed there, and Ada watched her face do something complicated—hunger and fear and reverence fighting for the same ground.

"You've had it three days," she said.

"I've had it two," Ada lied.

"The flood opened the channel Tuesday night. I walked the cut Wednesday morning." She said it without accusation, the way you'd state the weather. "You dug it out Thursday. I know because I was going to, and you beat me to it."

"Then you know I found it on licensed salvage ground."

"I know you found it and ran." She tilted her head. "Licensed diggers don't run."

"Licensed diggers who see a Provision survey boat run," Ada said. "It's practically the license requirement."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth and didn't commit. She came closer, stooping, and stood over the chest, and put her fingers—not her whole hand, just two fingers, delicately, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal—on the bronze plate with the seal.

Tower. Star. Radiance.

Then she pushed back her sleeve.

On the inside of her wrist, inked in faded blue-black, was the same mark. Smaller. Older. The lines slightly different—as if copied by hand, generation after generation, each copy drifting a hair from the last—and yet unmistakably the same sign. Tower above water. Star above the tower.

"My grandmother had it," the woman said. "Her mother had it. Before that they say it was stamped on documents, on doors, on the hulls of boats, on the bells of nine towns upriver." Her fingers were still resting on the bronze. "Ostrava."

The word landed in the cellar like a dropped stone. Ada had heard it only in curses, in the drunk fragments of men who spoke it the way you'd speak a dead relative's name in front of strangers—quickly, quietly, with a glance at the door.

"That's not a real place," he said.

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point of us."

* * *

Her name, when she finally gave it, was Sela, and she gave it reluctantly, the way you hand over something you can't take back. She talked standing up, refusing the crate he offered, arms crossed, eyes moving between him and the chest as if tracking which of them was more likely to bolt.

There were others, she said. Nine, counting her, scattered along two hundred miles of river, some knowing, some half-knowing, some carrying the wrist-mark without knowing why their grandmothers had insisted. They called themselves nothing. Naming things, she said, was how the Provision found them. For a century the consortium had done more than tax and lend and own—it had *edited*. Deeds rewritten. Parish rolls burned. Towns renamed or unmade. A nation didn't die, Sela said; it was deleted, and deletion took clerks, and clerks took wages, and wages came from somewhere.

"And the chest?" Ada asked.

"Is Ostravan work. Is sealed with the high seal—the one on the archive, if the stories are true. My grandfather spent thirty years looking for a chest like this. Died looking." Her jaw tightened. "I'm not going to spend thirty years. I'm going to spend whatever it takes to open this one."

"It doesn't open," Ada said. "Not with force, not with picks. Not quickly."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've spent three days trying."

Her eyes sharpened. "And?"

"And it's the finest lockwork I've ever seen, and I've seen most of it." He hesitated. He had already decided not to tell her about the false ward, the staggered levers, the hand of it. But his mouth went ahead of his caution, the way it sometimes did in cellars at night. "It's built like my father built."

Sela went very still. "Your father."

"A digger. Dead." Two truths and a wall. "Locks were a hobby."

"What was his name?"

"Does it matter?"

"It might." She said it softly, and the softness was worse than pressure. "Names matter to us. We've had ours taken. We keep a list. Every Ostravan name we recover goes in a book my aunt copies out by hand twice a year, in case the first copy burns." She looked at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere—hope, maybe, aimed at a stranger, which was either brave or foolish and possibly both. "If your father was a locksmith with that kind of hand, he might be on our list already. Or he might belong on it."

"He wasn't from here," Ada said. Which was true. Which was also, he understood dimly, the kind of sentence a man says when he is protecting something without knowing what.

* * *

She made the offer plainly, because she seemed like a woman who had learned that plainness survived where cleverness drowned.

"Partnership. You keep the chest—that's yours, flood-found, fair claim, I won't argue it. But we open it together. My people have what your picks don't: context. Names, stories, half a language, a great-aunt who remembers songs. You have—" she gestured at his tools, his stained hands, the neat cloth of picks folded like a surgeon's kit "—obviously, you have the hands. Split whatever's inside. Equal shares. Equal risk."

Ada looked at her for a long moment.

He looked at the wrist-mark, at the faded blue tower and star, and felt the pull of it, faint and foreign and warm, like heat from a hearth in a house he'd never been allowed inside. Nine people. A book of names copied twice a year. A dead nation kept alive by stubbornness and ink. He had spent ten years perfecting the art of belonging nowhere, and here was a woman offering him a place in a country that officially did not exist, and some traitorous part of him—the part that had said *hello, old man* to a lock—wanted it badly.

He said no.

Not cruelly. He was better than cruel, most days. He said he worked alone, that partners were liabilities, that the Provision paid informants in every town on the river and nine strangers with a secret tattoo were nine chances to hang. All true. All reasonable. None of it the reason.

The reason was simpler and uglier: belonging could be taken away. He had proof. He'd been seventeen, and the proof had packed a single bag at dawn and left a note that said *don't follow, this life eats people*, and Ada had followed anyway, all the way to a flooded trench outside a town called Vell, and found the note's author had spoken truly.

So he lied instead. He told Sela he'd report the chest to the salvage office in a week, once he'd catalogued it, take the finder's fee, be done with it. He watched her face close like a door.

"You'll sell it," she said.

"I'll license it."

"Same thing, here." She rolled her sleeve back down over the mark, covering it, and the cellar got colder somehow. "For what it's worth, digger—you don't lock like a man planning to hand this over. You lock like a man trying to get *in*." She climbed the stairs, stooping, unhurried. At the top she paused. "I'm moored at the coal dock until the river settles. If you change your mind, ask for the barge with the patched sail. Everyone knows it. Nobody reports it."

Then she was gone, and the chandler's floorboards creaked under her steps, and Ada sat alone with the lamp and the chest and the lie he'd told sitting in the air between them like smoke.

He worked the lock until dawn. Four levers. Always four. The fifth refused him with that soft, deliberate, opinionated tension, and near morning, fumbling half-asleep, he pressed his ear flat against the bronze to listen—and heard, or imagined he heard, deep inside the mechanism, a faint secondary click, rhythmic, patient.

Like a clock. Like a counter.

Like something counting down.

He sat up, wide awake, heart going, and outside the cellar's high slit of window the sky over Lowmark was turning the color of tin, and somewhere down at the coal dock a patched sail was being rigged by people who kept books of stolen names—and somewhere behind him, in the city offices of the Provision, a survey boat's report was already climbing the chain of command toward a man whose job was arithmetic.

Ada picked up his picks again. The fifth lever waited.
