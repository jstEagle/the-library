## 3. The Provision Sends Its Arithmetic

The knock came at dusk, three measured raps that no debt collector in Lowmark had ever bothered to learn. Debt collectors pounded. This was the sound of someone who already knew the door would open.

Ada froze over the cellar hatch, his palm still dusty from the chest's lid, and listened to the street outside. Rain on the shingles. A cart axle groaning somewhere down the lane. And beneath it all, the particular silence of a man standing very still on the other side of his door.

He almost didn't answer. He'd spent ten years perfecting the art of not answering—doors, questions, names shouted across market squares. But whoever it was had knocked like a man with time to spare, and men with time to spare were worse than men in a hurry.

He opened it a hand's width.

The man on the step was broad without being heavy, gray at the temples, dressed in the undyed wool and oiled leather of someone who had stopped needing to impress anyone decades ago. He held a leather document case against his chest the way a priest holds a book. Behind him, at the mouth of the alley, two younger men stood beside a Provision skiff moored at the salvage dock, their lanterns hooded.

"Ada Underhill," the man said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking."

"Corven Hale." He didn't offer a title. The Provision's enforcers never did; the title was the fear itself, and naming it aloud was considered vulgar. "May I come in? Or we can conduct this at the survey office, where there are witnesses. Your choice."

Ada weighed the cellar behind him—the bronze chest under sacking, the lock picks still warm from his hands—and chose the smaller danger. "In," he said. "Mind the step. It rots."

Hale stepped over the rot precisely, as if he'd already memorized its location, and took the single chair by the cold stove without being offered it. Ada stayed standing by the window, where he could see the alley and the skiff and, if it came to it, the gap between the fence slats he'd measured the day he rented the room.

"You've been working the Lowmark claims for eleven months," Hale said. "Spring license, summer renewal. Salvage class three, riverbed only." He opened the case and drew out a ledger bound in green goatskin, laid it flat on his knee, and turned pages with a clerk's economy. "Your spring license is well drawn. Good paper. Correct watermark. The seal is a shade too red, but seals always are, this far from Verrin City." He looked up. "Your summer renewal, however, was issued by an inspector named Doss. There has been no inspector named Doss in the valley registry for six years. He drowned at Tarn Weir. You bought a dead man's signature."

Ada said nothing. Denial was for men whose names existed somewhere clean.

"I'm not here about the forgery," Hale went on, as though Ada had spoken. "Half the diggers on this river hold papers signed by dead men. It's how the river works. I'm here because your claim sits on ground the Provision has held in trust for one hundred and four years, and because three days ago the flood gave back something from that ground." He closed the ledger. "A bronze chest."

The rain filled the pause. Ada kept his face doing nothing at all, which took more work than any lie.

"A chest," he said.

"Don't." Hale's voice didn't rise. "I have watched men lie to me for twenty years, and you are better at it than most, which tells me you learned young, from someone practiced. But I know what came out of the new channel at Lowmark bend, because my survey boat saw it before you did. My boatman reported it. You reached it first. That is not an accusation—it's arithmetic. The flood cut the channel at dawn. You were on the water by midmorning. The chest was gone by noon." He set the ledger on the table between them, open now to a page dense with columns. "So let us do sums instead of stories."

* * *

The sums were these: amnesty, full and written, for the forged licenses—for every forged license, Hale said, including ones Ada hadn't known were suspected. A salvage grant, legitimate, on any unclaimed stretch of the lower valley. Two hundred crowns, which was more money than Ada had held in his life at any single moment. In exchange: the chest, unopened, delivered to the survey office within the week.

"And if I don't have a chest," Ada said.

"Then you'll want to produce one," Hale said mildly, "because the alternative is that you found Provision property on Provision ground and concealed it, and concealment is not a licensing matter. It goes before the assizes in Verrin City. Men have taken ten years for less. Some haven't come back at all." He said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "I would prefer the chest. Assizes are tedious."

Ada looked at the open ledger. The columns were dates and lot numbers and payments, a century of them, and his eye caught on the earliest entries because the ink changed there—brown, faded, older than anything else in the room except the walls.

"What is all this?" he asked. "Ground held in trust. Trust for what?"

Hale was quiet a moment. When he answered, it was with the careful flatness of a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize so long ago he'd forgotten it wasn't true. "The valley's founding charters reserve certain lands against future claim. Standard provision."

"There's no nation left to claim them."

"No."

"So who's the trust *for*?"

Something moved behind Hale's eyes—not anger, Ada thought. Recognition. The flicker of a man hearing a question he'd once asked himself and buried deep enough to stop hearing the echo.

"That is not a question the Provision answers," Hale said.

"Because there's no answer?"

"Because answering it is above my station." Hale drew out a second document, thinner, and turned the ledger toward Ada fully. "Since you ask after trusts. Look here."

Ada looked. The early page was a payment record, salaries drawn against some account older than the Provision's current charter. Names ran down the margin. Most meant nothing. One did not.

**E. Underhill — surveyor, source expedition — paid in full, final entry.**

His father's name sat in the Provision's oldest ledger like a coin in a dead man's mouth.

Ada's breath went out of him slowly, through his nose, controlled, the way he'd taught himself to breathe when the ground shifted under a dig. His father. Who had hated institutions on principle, who had paid bribes in copper and contempt, who had told him once, drunk on plum brandy in a tent that smelled of wet chalk, that every ledger in the valley was a leash and the trick was to stay small enough that no clerk ever bothered writing your name down.

And there it was. Written down. Paid in full. Final entry.

"When," Ada said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that later, and ashamed of being proud.

"The source expedition," Hale said. "Twenty-two years ago. The last authorized survey of the upper valley." He watched Ada with that same stillness. "You knew him."

"He dug holes for a living."

"Yes," Hale said. "So did the rest of that expedition. Six names. Five of them are also final entries." He tapped the margin. "Do you know what they found up there, Underhill?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone below the rank of director. That is rather the point of the trust." Hale closed the ledger and stood, smoothing his coat. "You have until the week's end. The chest, or the assizes. I'll leave the offer in writing." He placed a folded paper on the table, weighted it with a coin—a Provision crown, fresh-minted, the river goddess on the face wearing her usual blindfold—and moved toward the door.

"Hale." Ada heard his own voice before he decided to use it. "The expedition. Twenty-two years ago. Why did it stop?"

Hale stopped in the doorway. The lantern light from the street caught half his face and left the other half in the brown shadow of the room.

"Because it was concluded," he said.

"That's not an answer either."

"No," Hale agreed quietly. "It isn't." For a moment he stood there, and Ada saw—or thought he saw, and would spend the next hundred miles deciding whether he'd seen it—the enforcer look at the faded brown ink of the old ledger the way a man looks at a grave he helped dig. Then it was gone, shuttered, and Corven Hale stepped out into the rain.

* * *

Ada didn't sleep. He sat on the cellar stairs with a lamp turned low and the chest's seal an arm's length away, and he made himself do the arithmetic Hale hadn't put in the ledger.

One hundred and four years of trust. A nation erased so thoroughly that even its name survived only in drunk old men's curses—and Ada had grown up on those curses, had heard his father spit the word *Ostrava* like a pit, and had never once asked why a man who claimed to care about nothing cared enough to hate that. Six surveyors sent to the source twenty-two years ago. Five final entries. One name that walked out of the valley and kept walking for another seventeen years, dragging a boy behind him, digging holes along a river that had been purchased and shelved like a stolen book.

His father had worked for them. Had been paid in full. And had spent the rest of his life running from whatever the payment bought.

Ada turned the lamp toward the chest. The seal glinted—two keys crossed over a mountain, worn nearly smooth, the mark of a country that officially had never existed. Three days he'd worked that lock and gotten nowhere, and part of him had wanted nowhere, because an unopened chest could be sold or surrendered or sunk in the river, but an opened one made you a man who knew things.

He thought about Hale's face when he'd read the old entries. About the question he'd asked himself and buried.

*Who's the trust for?*

There was an answer. Ada could feel it the way he felt water seeping through clay before the collapse—pressure without shape, coming from somewhere upstream. His father had felt it too. Had followed it to the source with five other fools, and come back alone, and hidden whatever he learned so deep that even his own son had mistaken the hiding for indifference.

The lamp guttered. Down the street, the Provision skiff's hooded lanterns slid away toward the survey office, taking Hale home to his ledgers.

Ada stood, knees cracking, and looked at the sealed bronze a long moment.

"Final entry," he said softly, to his father, to the chest, to nobody. "We'll see about that."

Above him, through the floorboards, he heard footsteps in the street that weren't going away—light, unhurried, circling back past his door a second time. Someone else had done their arithmetic tonight too.

Ada blew out the lamp and waited in the dark, listening, his hand finding the lock picks in his pocket without his permission.
