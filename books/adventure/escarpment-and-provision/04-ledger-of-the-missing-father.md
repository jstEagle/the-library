## 4. Ledger of the Missing Father

The Provision's oldest ledger smelled of tallow and mildew, and it was chained to Corven Hale's wrist.

Ada had expected threats. What he got instead was a table, two chairs, a lamp, and Hale's unhurried hands turning pages as though they were scripture. They sat in the back room of the tollhouse at Lowmark, where the river muttered against the pilings below like something impatient to be let in.

"Underhill," Hale said. "Elias Underhill. Licensed surveyor, class three. Licensed excavator, class two. Deceased." He turned another page. "Your father kept better books than you do."

Ada said nothing. Three days with the chest in his cellar had taught him the value of silence; Hale's visit had taught him that silence was also a kind of arithmetic, and the man across the table was very good at sums.

"Here's what interests me." Hale rotated the ledger so Ada could read it. The ink had gone brown, but the hand was precise, clerkly, indifferent. *E. Underhill — Verrin headwaters survey — permit denied — applicant persistent — file flagged.* "Twice he applied for permits at the source. Twice denied. Then he stopped applying." Hale looked up. His eyes were the color of river ice, pale and patient and hiding whatever moved beneath. "Men who stop asking permission start digging without it. Where did he go, Underhill?"

"He died," Ada said. "That's all I inherited."

"Everything is inherited," Hale said mildly. "Debts most of all."

* * *

Ada walked home through Lowmark's evening mud with Hale's parting words lodged in him like a splinter: *You have six days. Amnesty for the chest, or prosecution for eleven forged licenses. Your father's name stays clean either way. That's a courtesy.*

A courtesy. As if the Provision gave anything back.

His cellar was cold and dark and smelled of wet stone and old bronze. The chest sat where he'd left it, wrapped in sacking, its seal facing the wall because looking at it made his chest tight in a way he refused to name. He'd spent three nights on the lock and gotten nowhere—not because it defeated him, but because the mechanism felt *familiar*. The tolerances, the false tumblers, the little spiteful spring that bit your pick if you rushed. He'd grown up watching hands build locks exactly like this, in lamplight, humming.

He hadn't thought about his father's hands in years. Now they were everywhere.

He went for the lamp oil and found instead the crate he'd never unpacked since Lowmark—the one thing he carried between towns that wasn't tools. His father's field journals, seven of them, water-stained, recovered from a landlord who'd sold a dead man's rooms out from under his son before Ada even knew he was dead. Ada had read them once, at seventeen, furious, and never again. Grief was a country he'd emigrated from. You didn't go back for souvenirs.

But Hale knew his father's name. And the ledger had said *headwaters*.

He opened the last journal by lamplight, and his father's voice came up off the page like heat off stone.

* * *

*Day 41. The river above Weir Hollow is not a river but an argument the mountains are losing. We lost the mule yesterday—Tomas wept over it more than I did, which tells you everything about Tomas and nothing about me.*

*The maps are wrong. All of them. I've compared nine surveys going back two hundred years and every one draws the source as a spring in open meadow. There is no meadow. There is a cliff face of dressed stone, cut so fine you'd take it for geology unless you knelt—and no surveyor ever kneels. I knelt. There are letters in the joints. Ostravan script. The nation that isn't anywhere has been sitting under the source of the valley's whole life, and everyone who drinks from the river drinks past it without knowing.*

*I sent for permits again. Denied within the week. Third time. The Provision does not deny these requests slowly, which means someone reads them fast, which means someone knows exactly what's up there and has known for a long time.*

*Tomas says give it up. Tomas says a man my age should be raising his boy, not chasing a country that died before either of us was born. But here is what Tomas doesn't understand, what none of them understand: they didn't erase Ostrava because it failed. Nations that fail don't scare anyone. They erased it because it succeeded—at something the Provision cannot permit. I've found enough fragments now. Granaries that fed the whole valley free through the famine years. Courts where debt could not be inherited. A library, maybe. An archive. A people who wrote everything down and hid it well, because they knew what would come for paper.*

*The Provision's fortune didn't come from trade. It came from a theft so large they had to erase the victim entirely. You can steal a purse and be caught. Steal a history and there's nothing left to accuse you.*

*Day 44. I have the cipher. Or part of one—a bone-carved key fragment, recovered from the cliff face, and it is the most beautiful piece of work I've ever held. It wants others. It wants to be whole. And I know what happens to key fragments when their finders apply for export licenses. They go into Provision vaults and the vaults burn, occasionally, conveniently.*

*So I've decided where to hide mine. Not in the ground—ground gets surveyed. Not in walls—walls get sold. Somewhere no assessor, no auditor, no enforcer will ever think to confiscate.*

*Somewhere it travels with me always. Somewhere it's already mine by the oldest claim there is.*

*My son broke his leg the winter he was nine. Fell from the mill bridge showing off for girls who weren't watching. The bone set crooked and then set true, and the surgeon in Marrow said the callus would grow thick as a knot in oak. He complained for a year. He walks fine now. He'll walk fine forever.*

*A healed femur is the strongest thing a body builds. Stronger than the bone around it. Nothing gets into a healed fracture. Nothing gets out.*

*Forgive me, Ada. Or don't. You were always better at anger than forgiveness, and anger keeps a man moving, and moving keeps him alive, and alive is what I need you to be. If you're reading this, I'm dead, and the fragment is still where I put it, and you are the only archive the Provision will never search.*

*Go to the source. Or don't. Sell it. Or don't. I only ask one thing: whatever you choose, choose it knowing whose inheritance you're carrying. Not mine. Theirs. Everyone's. That was the whole point of them, and it can be the whole point of you.*

*— E.U., the last entry*

* * *

Ada sat in the cellar until the lamp burned low, one hand flat against his left thigh, feeling the old ache that lived there the way other men lived with weather.

He'd broken it at nine. He remembered the fall, the white shock of it, his father carrying him three miles with the leg cradled like something breakable—which it was, which it apparently had needed to be. He remembered the surgeon in Marrow, the smell of the tincture, his father's hand on his hair saying *hold still, hold still, almost done*, and he had assumed, all these years, that the man was talking about the setting.

He laughed once, a short ugly sound with nothing behind it. Ten years of anger. Ten years of *he left me, he chose the work, he chose the dead country over his own son.* And the whole time—

The whole time the man had chosen him first. Had hidden the most dangerous thing he owned inside his son's body and then walked into the mountains to die so no one would ever look there.

Grief didn't lift. But it changed shape. It stopped being a wound and became a deed of title, signed in bone, and Ada sat with his thumb pressed against the old scar tissue and understood that Hale's ledgers, Hale's amnesties, Hale's whole patient arithmetic had already lost. You couldn't confiscate this. You couldn't buy it, because Ada had spent ten years selling everything he owned and he knew, down to the copper, what things were worth—and this was not a thing.

Above him, footsteps crossed the floorboards of the rented house. Light. Quick. Stopping directly over the cellar hatch.

Then a knock—three soft raps, right above his head, deliberate as a signature.

Ada closed the journal and put out the lamp, and in the dark he heard the hatch creak, and a woman's voice came down the stairwell, low and certain:

"I know what you're sitting on, digger. Both of them."
