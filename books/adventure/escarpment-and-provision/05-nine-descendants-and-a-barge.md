## 5. Nine Descendants and a Barge

Ada came to the barge at night because coming anywhere in daylight had stopped being safe.

He found it moored below the tannery ruins at Lowmark's northern edge, where the river widened into a brown slack that smelled of oak bark and rot. It was less a vessel than an argument between wood and water—a flat-bottomed barge maybe forty feet long, its gunwales patched with planks of three different ages, its single mast stepped crooked. A lantern hung from the stern post, and around it sat nine people who all turned to look at him with the same expression: the wary, measuring stare of people deciding whether he was a burden or a blessing.

Sela stood last among them, arms crossed, and did not look surprised.

"You said you'd think about it," she said.

"I thought about it." He let the canvas-wrapped chest shift on his shoulder so they could see it. "Hale gave me until the new moon to hand it over. I'd rather it be on the water when he comes."

An old man near the lantern snorted. "So we're a getaway now. Wonderful. Sela, we agreed—"

"We agreed to vote, Tomas." Sela took the chest from Ada's shoulder without asking, weighing it like a midwife judging a child. "And I'm voting yes. He knows Provision paperwork better than any of us. He got past Weir Hollow's toll clerk twice last year under two different names—I checked."

"You *checked* him?"

"I check everyone." She looked up at Ada then, and there was no warmth in it, only arithmetic. "You'll pull your weight. You'll eat what we eat. And if Hale offers you a better deal on the river, you tell us before you take it."

"He won't offer me a better deal," Ada said. "He offered me amnesty and a ledger burning. That was his best."

"Then welcome aboard." She turned to the others. "This is Ada Underhill. He digs things up for a living. Tomas, stop glaring, you'll strain something."

Tomas glared anyway. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a fence post left out in weather, with hands knotted worse than tree roots. "Underhill," he said. "There was an Underhill digging at Kessel Bend twenty years back. Loud man. Broke a mummy's jaw and apologized to it."

"That was my father."

Tomas's glare softened by one degree. "He bought my drinks after. All right. He can stay."

That was the whole ceremony. Ada had expected questions—the seal, the chest, why a stranger would burn his life down over a locked box—and instead the nine of them simply made room on the deck, the way water makes room for a dropped stone, and went back to arguing about rope.

* * *

Sela watched the digger sleep the first night and decided she still didn't trust him.

He'd taken the spot farthest from the lantern, curled against the cargo rail with his boots on and his knife hand free even in sleep, which told her more than his words had. The others had done their rituals around him as though he weren't there—Marette's salt line across the gangplank, the twins' argument over whose turn it was to sing the evening verse, old Tomas tapping the hull three times before bed, once for the river, once for the dead, once for whoever was listening. Ada hadn't joined in. But Sela had seen his eyes track each ritual, cataloguing them the way he probably catalogued strata in a cut bank: older than they looked, deliberate, load-bearing.

Ostravan habits, worn smooth by a century of hiding. Her grandmother had salted doorways in a country that wasn't on maps anymore, and had never once explained why.

In the morning she put a bowl of barley porridge in his hands before he was fully awake.

"We eat together," she said. "All nine. It's not optional."

"I've eaten alone for ten years. It's efficient."

"It's also how you disappear." She sat down across from him, close enough that retreat would be obvious. "My grandmother used to say the Provision doesn't hunt people. It hunts *separate*. One digger alone is a fine to pay, a name to cross off. Nine people eating from one pot—" she gestured at the deck, at Marette laughing at something the twins said, at Tomas pretending not to listen, "—that's harder arithmetic. So we eat together. Even the ones who don't want to."

Ada looked at the porridge like it might be a trap. Then he ate it, fast and ungraceful, the way men eat when they've been hungry longer than they admit, and Sela marked that down too.

The barge got underway an hour after dawn, poling up the slack water past Lowmark's last warehouses while the town still slept. By noon they'd reached the first shallows and everyone worked—poles and sweeps, hauling the heavy hull over sandbars, cursing in three dialects and one dead language that only Tomas still spoke fluently. Ada worked silently and well. He read water the way he read soil, Sela noticed; he could see the channel under the surface before anyone else felt it, calling depths and snags in a flat voice that saved them two groundings in the first day.

By the second day the twins were asking him questions about riverbeds. By the third, Marette had repaired the tear in his coat without being asked, and he'd pretended not to notice, and then worn the coat anyway.

Sela allowed herself, cautiously, to begin hoping.

* * *

The leak found them on the fourth night, as leaks do—in the dark, in the rain, at the worst possible bend of the river.

Ada woke to shouting and found the hold taking water through a seam that had opened along a patched plank, gray water welling up over the ankles of the twins who were bailing with buckets and a cooking pot. Lightning walked the ridgelines upstream. Everyone screamed instructions at everyone else.

"Ballast off the port side!" Marette shouted.

"Bail, don't argue!"

"Tomas, sit *down*, your heart—"

Ada didn't shout. He pulled the lamp low, lay flat on the wet decking, and followed the seam with his fingers the way he'd follow a wall trench in failing light—by feel, by logic, by the memory of how things fail. The patch over the seam was newer than the planks around it. Someone had caulked it badly, in a hurry, years ago.

"There." He drove the point of his knife into the old caulking, levered it loose, and held out his hand. "Wick. Pitch. Anyone have pitch?"

Marette had pitch. Of course Marette had pitch; she seemed to have everything, produced from the endless pockets of her coat like a peddler's trick. Ada heated it over the bailing bucket's flame, packed the seam, pressed a strip of canvas in while it was soft, sealed it again. His hands knew this work the way they knew lockwork—patient, exact, indifferent to the rain hammering his back.

It took forty minutes. When it was done, the bailing slowed, stopped, and the nine of them stood dripping in the lamplight, breathing hard, looking at the seam and then at him.

"You've caulked a boat before," Tomas said.

"I've dug drainage channels through flood silt. Same problem. Water finds the lie in anything solid."

Tomas laughed—a dry bark, surprised out of him. "Your father would've said that exactly."

Something moved through Ada's chest that he refused to name. He busied himself coiling rope.

Later, when the others slept, Sela found him at the stern with his feet in the river, watching the current slide black past the hull.

"You could have run," she said. "During the leak. Barge grounded, everyone busy, Hale's skiff somewhere behind us. You didn't."

"The barge has my chest on it."

"The chest is in the hold. The hold was flooding. You could have taken the chest and swum."

He was quiet a long time. The rain had stopped; the river talked to itself in the dark, patient, going where it went.

"My father used to say a man should know what he's carrying before he decides where he's going," Ada said finally. "I'm still deciding."

"No, you're not." Sela sat down beside him, not touching, leaving the choice open the way her grandmother had taught her—all doors unlatched, all invitations unforced. "You're just not ready to say it out loud. There's a difference. My family survived a hundred years on that difference."

"And what happens when someone finally says it out loud?"

"Then the Provision can hear you too." She smiled, tired and sharp. "That's the cost. That's always been the cost."

* * *

On the sixth morning, Tomas cooked.

This was apparently an event of liturgical significance. He rose before dawn, commandeered the galley box, and produced from somewhere a hoard of dried fish, wild onions, and a clay jar of honey that he guarded like relics. The smell woke the whole barge. By full light all nine of them plus Ada sat in a circle on the cargo deck eating fish and onions fried in honey, and Tomas stood over them like a priest and recited, in the dead language first and then in valley speech, the names of the dead.

"Sela's grandmother, Yeva, who kept the seal alive in her wrist. My brother Dano, who drowned at Weir Hollow running the lock at night. The Vessin family, gone in the census purge of '61. The archivists of the source, names unknown, who carried what we carry now." He went on, thirty names, forty, some just first names, some just descriptions—*the woman who buried the ledgers under the chapel floor*—and the others answered after each one, quietly, all together: *"Remembered."*

Ada ate and listened and did not answer, and no one made him.

But when Tomas finished and the circle sat in silence while the river pulled them upstream, Ada heard himself ask, "The archivists. How long has the source been sealed?"

"A hundred and twelve years," Sela said. "Since the last expedition failed."

"What happened to them?"

"They never came back downriver." She met his eyes across the circle, and Ada understood that she had waited six days to say this, that every shared meal and mended coat and caulked seam had been building toward it. "Except one. A digger named Underhill. He came back alone, half-dead, wouldn't say what he saw. Sold his gear in Lowmark and vanished north."

The honey went to iron in Ada's mouth. His father's face, the last time he'd seen it at seventeen—turned away, already leaving, saying *stay here, stay small, stay nothing*—rearranged itself into something else. A man coming back from the source. A man who had seen what was up there and spent the rest of his life making sure his son would never follow.

"Did he have anything with him?" Ada asked. His voice came out level, which cost him. "When he came back down?"

Sela shook her head slowly. "The records say nothing. But Ada—if your father reached the source and turned back, he's the only person alive who's seen the way in." She leaned forward. "Whatever he hid in you, whatever you're carrying—we need to know what he knew. Because Hale's skiff is two days behind us, the river drops another handspan every day, and in a week none of us will be able to reach the source at all."

Upstream, faint through the morning haze, the gorge walls closed in ahead of them like a book beginning to shut.
