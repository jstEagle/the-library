## 6. The Toll at Weir Hollow

Sela had counted the locks of Weir Hollow a hundred times from the far bank, back when she was a girl, when her grandmother still pointed across the water and said *that's where they keep the river hostage*. From the barge, drifting down the last quiet bend before the works came into view, the lockhouse looked almost gentle in the morning light—grey stone, slate roof, the tall black gates standing open like an invitation. The Provision flew no flag. It didn't need one. The toll board nailed above the gatehouse said everything: rates per ton, per head, per cargo manifest, each figure stamped with the consortium's seal—a closed hand gripping a sheaf of grain.

A closed hand. Her grandmother had spat when she described it. *They never open. They only take.*

"Nine souls, one barge, and not a coin between us," muttered Dagny at the tiller, her knuckles white on the worn wood. "We could run the weir."

"And die doing it," Sela said. "The drop's four meters and the channel's choked with flood timber. Hale would not even need to chase us. The river would do his work for free."

At the mention of Hale, the deck went quiet the way it always did. Nine of them now, crowded among crates of dried fish and coils of rope: Dagny at the tiller; old Marta and her twin grandsons, Ferro and Pell, who bickered like crows; Ivo, who hadn't spoken more than ten words since Lowmark; young Wren, who was fourteen and pretended not to be afraid; Tomas, whose knees had gone bad but whose memory of the old river songs remained perfect; Anselm, who cooked; and Ada.

Ada stood at the bow with his arms folded, watching the lockhouse the way he watched everything—with the flat, patient attention of a man reading a room for exits. He'd been with them six days. Six days of eating their food, taking his watch shifts, saying almost nothing about himself, and yet somehow, impossibly, being there every time something needed doing. He'd re-lashed the cargo net that Ferro had botched. He'd found Wren's stolen knife before the girl knew it was gone. He'd mended the pump valve with a strip of leather and a bone pin, working by lantern light without being asked.

He did these things the way other men breathed. And then he stepped back, as if embarrassed to have been caught alive among them.

"We pay," Sela said. "We bluff. Or we fight. In that order."

"And if none of them work?" Pell asked.

Ada spoke without turning around. "Then we sink the barge in the lock and let them explain to the Provision why a quarter mile of the Verrin is backed up behind a dead hull during high season." A pause. "But it won't come to that."

"You've been through Weir Hollow?" Dagny asked.

"I've been through every lock on this river," Ada said. "Some of them twice, under different names."

* * *

They tied up at the lower mooring wall an hour later, and Sela went up to the tollhouse alone, because that was the arrangement—she carried the papers, she did the talking, and the others stayed out of sight of the lockkeeper's window. The lockkeeper was a soft-bodied man named Osk Vell, with ink-stained fingers and the particular pallor of someone who spent his life indoors counting other people's money. His office smelled of lamp oil and vinegar. Behind him, shelves of ledgers rose to the ceiling, hundreds of them, the Provision's memory bound in calfskin.

Sela laid out their manifest—the one Ada had written the night before, in a careful clerk's hand she hadn't known he possessed—and their salvage license, likewise Ada's work, so convincing that her own hands had shaken holding it.

Vell read slowly, running one finger under each line. "Cargo fish, salted. Rope, hemp. One barge, forty-foot." He looked up. "Toll comes to eleven marks, eight pennies. Plus the inspection fee."

"The inspection fee was abolished in the spring schedule," Sela said. "Provision Circular Forty-One. It's posted at Lowmark."

Something flickered in Vell's face—the small, trapped annoyance of a man caught padding the take. "Circulars don't always reach the upriver stations," he said.

"Then you'll want to send for it," Sela said pleasantly. "I can wait. Though my crew will want their berth fees refunded while we sit, and there's a form for that too, I believe."

She watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes: the risk of a complaint filed against his station versus the certainty of a smaller bribe. The Provision trained its people well. They were all bookkeepers at heart, all of them, right down to the enforcers with their iron-tipped staves. Everything was a ledger. Everything balanced or it didn't.

"Two marks," Vell said finally, "and I'll pass you through the morning cycle without unloading."

"One mark," said a voice from the doorway, "and I'll fill out the circular verification myself, since I happen to know the reference number."

Ada stood there with his cap in his hands, wearing the expression of a minor functionary three days past his patience. Where he'd gotten the grey coat Sela never learned—he seemed to produce garments the way a stage magician produced doves—but he wore it like a man born into it. "Station code WH-4," he went on, stepping past her as if he owned the floorboards. "Your last audit was March, and your inspector noted irregularities in the toll variance column. I'd hate to see another one."

Vell had gone the color of porridge. "And who might you be?"

"Underhill. Provision Survey Office, River Division." Ada set a folded paper on the desk—Sela caught a glimpse of an official-looking seal, rendered in red wax that could not possibly be real. "We're conducting compliance checks on the upper locks ahead of the dry-season schedules. Your name appears in nothing concerning, Master Vell. Keep it that way and this stays a routine visit." He tapped the manifest. "One mark, no inspection, morning cycle. And I'll need your counter-signature on the transit chit, because my office requires duplicate documentation for every passage above Weir Hollow."

It was beautiful. It was terrible. Sela kept her face still while inside her something turned over—because she recognized what she was watching. This was the same flat competence he brought to picking a padlock or skinning a fish or lying to her face in a Lowmark cellar. Ten years of drifting had honed this man into a tool of pure escape, a knife that could slip any lock, talk past any door, vanish from any ledger. He had learned the Provision's paperwork the way a fox learns a henhouse—not to live there, but to rob it.

Except now he wasn't robbing it. He was using it for them. For nine ragged Ostravans and a leaky barge full of salted fish. And worse—she could see it in his shoulders, in the way he stood a half-inch taller than he had that morning—he was enjoying it.

Vell signed the chit. Of course he signed it. Men like Vell had spent their whole lives learning which paper to fear, and Ada's paper frightened him perfectly.

* * *

The lock cycle took two hours. They waited in the upper chamber while the water rose, the black gates dripping, the stone walls close enough to touch on either side. Sela leaned on the rail beside Ada, who was watching the winch-crews with an odd, distant expression.

"My father worked a lock once," she said. "Before the erasure. Before I was born. He said the Provision bought the whole river in a single summer, and nobody remembers anyone objecting."

"People object," Ada said. "They just do it quietly, and then they forget they did. That's how it works. You don't ban a thing. You make it expensive to remember." He flexed his left hand, the one that always seemed to trouble him. "My father used to say records are just stories with better lawyers."

"Is that what he taught you? The paperwork?"

"He taught me that every system has a shape, and every shape has a seam." Ada's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but near enough that it startled her. "Locks, ledgers, borders. You find the seam, you put a thin blade in it, and the whole thing opens like it was never locked at all." He glanced at her. "Your grandmother would have hated me."

"My grandmother would have fed you until you couldn't move," Sela said. "That's how Ostravans handle useful people. We fatten them and then we never let them leave."

Something crossed his face then—quick, quickly buried. She pretended not to see it. Six days she'd been watching for it: the flinch whenever the word *stay* came near him, the way he catalogued exits even in conversation. A man raised on leaving. She'd known men like him on the river, drifters who mistook motion for safety, and she knew the only cure was neither argument nor kindness but repetition. Meals. Watches. Small obligations, stacked day upon day, until leaving cost more than staying.

The gates opened. The barge slid out into the upper reach, where the river ran green and slow between willowed banks, and for a mile everyone relaxed. Ferro broke out the last of the Lowmark bread. Wren climbed to the roof beam and dangled her legs. Even Ivo said three words, which by his standards constituted a speech.

Sela allowed herself one hour of it. Then she took the glass and swept the towpath behind them, because her grandmother had also taught her that relief is when the wolf eats you.

The towpath was empty. The lockhouse was shrinking astern. But on the rise above the lower mooring wall, where the road bent away from the river, a single rider sat motionless on a grey horse, and even at this distance Sela could see the way he held himself—not looking at the barge, not looking away from it either, just watching, with the stillness of a man who had already sent word and was now merely confirming the arithmetic.

She didn't recognize the rider. But she recognized the posture, because she'd seen it once before, across a burning warehouse in Lowmark, on a man in a long dark coat: the posture of Corven Hale's people, who never chased, never shouted, never hurried. They simply noted, and reported, and arrived.

"Ada," she said quietly.

He took the glass. He looked for a long moment. When he lowered it, his face had gone smooth and unreadable, the functionary vanishing, the drifter resurfacing.

"How fast can this barge go?" he asked.

"Not fast enough," Sela said. "Not against a skiff with eight oars and a fair wind. Which means we stop pretending the river will hide us." She turned to the others, already moving, already reaching for the boat-hook to bring in the towline. "Dry season's coming early. We beach her at Gorge Mouth tonight and take to the land."

Behind them, small and patient on its rise, the rider did not follow. He didn't need to. Somewhere downstream, a skiff was already being provisioned, and somewhere in its stern, folding his long frame beneath a canvas awning, Corven Hale was reading a report that contained a name.

Sela watched Ada stare back at the rider until the bend swallowed him, and she saw something she hadn't seen in him before. Not fear. Calculation—and under it, faint but unmistakable, the first ember of something that looked almost like anger on behalf of someone other than himself.

Good, she thought. Anger you can build with.
