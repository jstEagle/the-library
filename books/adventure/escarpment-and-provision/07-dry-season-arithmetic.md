## 7. Dry Season Arithmetic

The river began lying to us on the fourth morning above Weir Hollow.

I noticed it before anyone else—old habit, reading water the way other men read ledgers. The barge drew two feet when we loaded her at Lowmark. Now she drew closer to three, and the current had gone from a shove to a sulk. Where the channel had run brown and muscular past the willow banks, it ran thin and clear over pale gravel, braiding itself into threads that couldn't float a leaf.

"Water's down a hand since yesterday," I told Sela. "Maybe more."

She stood at the tiller with her sleeves rolled and her wrist turned so the Ostravan seal faced the sun, the way she did when she was thinking hard, as if the tattoo were a compass that only worked in daylight. She looked at the gravel bars emerging downstream like the backs of sleeping animals.

"That's not possible," she said. "The melt comes off the high snow for another six weeks."

"The melt's done coming off *this* stretch." I crouched at the gunwale and dipped my arm. Cold enough to ache, but shallow. "There's a gorge ahead—the map my father kept shows it. Narrow walls, no banks, one channel. If the river drops another foot, there won't be a channel."

Behind us, the nine of us had become eight-and-a-half: Sela, me, old Tomas, the twins Ilse and Ruta who finished each other's sentences and each other's chores, quiet Bram who never spoke unless the fire was going out, young Piet who talked enough for three men, Marta the cook whose temper was the only thing on this boat hotter than her stove, and Anselm, who'd been a Provision clerk before he'd burned his own ledger and run. Eight of us and a barge named *Provision Enough*, which Sela swore was a joke and which nobody laughed at anymore.

Tomas came forward, favoring his hip the way he always did in cold mornings, and spat thoughtfully over the side.

"My grandfather ran freight up this river," he said. "He said the Verrin gives with one hand and takes with the other, and you always know which hand is which by the color of the sky." He squinted eastward, where the clouds sat low and dry as old wool. "That's a taking sky."

* * *

We grounded at noon.

Not dramatically—no tearing of planks, no shouting. Just a long grinding shudder through the hull, like the river clearing its throat, and then stillness. We were stuck on a gravel shelf in the middle of a channel that had been deep water a week ago, with white stone rising on both sides into hills that went gold and dry toward the gorge.

Sela called everyone aft. I watched them gather—eight people who ten days ago had been strangers arguing about rations, now standing shoulder to shoulder with the automatic closeness of people who'd shared a small deck too long—and felt the familiar itch start between my shoulder blades. Ten years I'd traveled alone because alone was light. Alone, you could ground yourself and dig yourself out without asking anyone's opinion. Alone, nobody broke a leg.

"We walk from here," Sela said. "The gorge portage. It's four days on foot along the north rim to where the spring country starts. We take what we can carry and we scuttle the barge."

"Scuttle her?" Piet's voice cracked with genuine grief. "She got us past the Hollow."

"She's a hole in the water with opinions," Marta said. "Let her rest."

But Tomas wasn't listening. He'd gone to step down from the cargo hatch to the deck, and his bad hip chose that moment, or his boot caught the coaming, or the deck simply decided against him—I never got a straight story, and neither did he. He went down sideways with a sound I've heard once before, in a mine shaft in my seventeenth year: a wet, deep crack, like green wood breaking under a load.

The sound he made after it was worse.

* * *

Bones are arithmetic. That's what my father used to say, kneeling over some shattered potsherd or some shattered workman, splinting whatever needed splinting. A break is simple math: the body wants to knit, but knitting takes time, and time is the one material no expedition ever packs enough of.

Tomas's leg was broken below the knee, clean but complete. I set it while Bram held him and Marta fed him brandy from the medicinal bottle she pretended didn't exist. He took it better than most men half his age, jaw tight, eyes fixed on the masthead as if it might offer terms. When I bound the splint with strips of sailcloth, he looked down at his own leg like it belonged to a stranger who'd inconvenienced him.

"Well," he said. "That settles the arithmetic, doesn't it."

"It doesn't settle anything," Sela said. "We carry him."

"On a four-day portage? Over scree?" I heard my own voice come out flat and practical and hated it. "One person carrying him means two people carrying his gear. Two carrying him means we move at a crawl, and Hale's skiff is—"

"I know how fast Hale's skiff is." Sela's voice was quiet, which was worse than loud. "I've been counting it since Lowmark."

"So have I." I made myself look at Tomas instead of her. "So has he. Look at him. He already knows."

And he did. That was the thing I couldn't get around. Old Tomas lay against the cargo netting with his leg propped on a coil of rope, and he was watching all of us with the mild, patient expression of a man waiting for children to finish an argument he'd settled in his head hours ago.

"There's a fisherman's hut," he said, "two miles back down the near bank. I saw it when we passed. Stone walls, good roof. Leave me there with food and the brandy, and leave someone to fetch water and keep me honest about the splint."

"And when Hale comes upriver behind us?" Ilse asked.

Tomas smiled. His teeth were few and yellow and entirely unbothered. "Then he finds an old man with a broken leg and whoever's fool enough to sit with him, and he spends a day deciding what to do about it. A day is a day. In the gorge, a day is everything."

Nobody said anything. The river muttered over its gravel, embarrassed at having shrunk.

"I'll stay," Bram said.

Everyone turned. Bram spoke perhaps ten words a day, all of them load-bearing. He shrugged, as if staying behind with a crippled man to face the Provision's enforcer were a chore like any other, like mending a sail.

"And me," said Ruta. Ilse elbowed her. "Both of us or neither," Ruta said. "You know that."

"You don't have to—" Sela started.

"No," Tomas said, gently, in the voice he used for correcting younger people about things they'd understand later. "They do have to. That's the whole point of it. If it costs nothing, Hale smells the lie." He looked around at all of us, and his gaze snagged on me last, and stayed. "You especially, digger. You're the one he wants. You should be the one arguing hardest to leave me."

It was true. Every practical bone in my body was screaming it. Leave him, move fast, survive; survival was the only currency I'd ever carried, and I'd carried it alone across ten years of valley because carrying it alone meant never having to watch it spent on someone else.

"He's right," I said. My voice came out strange. "You should argue. I'm the one Hale follows. Every hour I'm on this bank is an hour he's on our wake."

"Then it's fortunate," Tomas said, settling back against the netting, "that I've been slow since before you were born, and intend to go on being slow regardless of who's chasing whom. Go open your archive, boy. Someone ought to get some use out of that leg of yours before it kills you."

I hadn't told him about the femur. None of them knew, except Sela, and she'd sworn silence. But old men who'd spent their lives on rivers learned to read cargo by the way a boat sat in the water, and I suppose I'd been sitting wrong in myself for days.

* * *

We left at first light, which is the hour when leaving is easiest, because the light hasn't yet shown you exactly what you're walking away from.

Marta packed Tomas a week of food and lied that it was two. Sela gave him the barge's signal lamp, the brass one, though it had no oil. "For looking at," she said, and he understood, because he was the kind of man who understood that some things you carry aren't for using. The twins rigged a litter between two oars so that Bram and Ruta could move him the two miles to the hut without jarring the splint, and then they shook hands all around, formally, like diplomats, which made Piet cry and pretend it was dust.

I stood apart, the way I always stood apart, doing the arithmetic. Four days' march. Hale's skiff maybe a day and a half behind, slowed by the shallows, slowed—if it worked—by an old man in a stone hut who would tell him, with great theatrical reluctance, that the remnant had come through two days ago heading upstream, all of them, every last one.

"You," Tomas said. He'd had them set him down at the top of the bank, and he beckoned me over with the imperiousness of a man who'd earned the right to summon anyone. "Come here."

I came.

"Hold out your hand."

I held it out. He put something in it: a small bronze fitting, green with age, shaped like a river delta—a piece of the chest, pried loose during those first days in the cellar, before any of us knew what the chest was. I'd seen Sela turn it over a hundred times.

"Why are you giving me this?"

"Because I stole it," Tomas said placidly, "and I'm not sorry, and I'd rather confess to you than to her. And because when you get to the source, and you stand in front of whatever's left of Ostrava, I want you to remember that a foolish old man on a gravel bar thought it was worth a broken leg." He closed my fingers over the fitting with both of his hands, dry and strong as rootwood. "Your father dug his whole life, they tell me. Did he ever tell you what he was digging *for*?"

"He never told me anything. He just left."

"Mm." Tomas looked out at the shrunken river, at the gold hills, at nothing. "Then dig for both of you. And when you find it—when you're standing there and the whole dead nation is breathing on the back of your neck—remember that we paid for it gladly. All of us. Even the ones who only paid a day's delay."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything in me that could come out as words. So I did the only thing I've ever known how to do with something that matters: I put it away carefully, in the inside pocket, next to my father's journal, and I turned and walked uphill after the others, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you learn whether you can afford what you've spent.

By midday the gorge walls rose on either side of us, red and close, and the path narrowed to a ledge above a thread of dying water. And that was where Sela stopped, so suddenly that Piet walked into her back, and pointed down at the sand of the ledge below.

Footprints. Fresh ones, small and even, made by boots issued by the Provision's survey corps.

Someone was already ahead of us, walking toward the source.
