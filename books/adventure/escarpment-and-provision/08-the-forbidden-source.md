## 8. The Forbidden Source

The river ended the way all rivers end—not with a wall but with a whisper.

We climbed out of the gorge on the fourth morning, Sela ahead of me with her hand flat against the wet stone like she was greeting an old animal, and the sound of running water changed. It thinned. Spread. Became a hundred small voices instead of one large one. I had spent ten years listening to the Verrin the way a man listens to a creditor—always aware of it, always calculating what it might take from him—and now it was going quiet beneath our feet, breaking itself into tributaries no wider than my boot.

"Close," Sela said. Not to me. To all of us, and none of us. Her wrist, where the seal was inked, had been bare of its wrapping since Weir Hollow. She didn't hide it anymore. That frightened me more than Hale did.

The nine had become five. Tomas stayed behind in the high camp with Marta and young Pell, his leg splinted with a barge plank, insisting he'd always wanted to learn to fish. He'd said it laughing, and Marta hadn't laughed back, and I had turned away because looking at them felt like reading someone else's letters. Decoys. That was the word nobody used. Three people staying behind so that Hale's skiff would follow their smoke instead of our trail, buying us days we hadn't earned.

I kept counting the days anyway. Four since the portage began. The skiff would have found them by now, if it was going to.

"Here." Sela stopped at a shelf of rock where the hillside had slumped sometime in the last century—a scar of raw earth half-healed with alder and moss, as if the mountain itself had tried to bury whatever lay beneath. She knelt and pulled away moss in handfuls, and underneath was bronze. Green with age, furred with lichen, but bronze: a door laid flat into the slope, wide as a wagon bed, worked around its edge with the same interlocking pattern as the chest in Lowmark, as the tattoo on her wrist, as everything Ostrava had left behind like fingerprints on the world's furniture.

In the center, where a handle should have been, there was a lock.

I knew locks. I had known them since before I could write, crouched under my father's workbench while he explained tumblers the way other fathers explained scripture. But this lock stopped me. It had no keyhole. It had a socket—a round recess the size of a walnut, polished smooth by rain that had never reached it, and carved into the bottom of the socket, visible only when you tilted your head against the light, was a spiral. A groove winding inward, thinner than a hair at its center.

"It's not a keyhole," I said. "It's a cast. Something fits into it exactly. Bone, maybe—" My hand went to my thigh before I could stop it. Old habit. The ache came every cold morning, deep in the femur where the break had healed wrong, thickened, strange. The break I'd gotten at nine, falling from a dig scaffold, the one my father had set himself, alone, refusing the doctor in three towns.

*Where no one confiscates it.* His own words, from the journal we'd burned pages of just to keep them out of Hale's hands.

Sela looked at me. Behind her, old Wren and the twins and quiet Anselm looked at me too, and I understood that they had all done this arithmetic already, days ago, on the barge, and had simply been waiting for me to catch up.

"You don't have to," Sela said. "Whatever it costs."

"Everything costs," I said. "That's the first thing the Provision teaches you for free."

But I knelt. And I took out the knife.

* * *

My father never told me why he refused doctors. I'd built a dozen stories over the years and discarded them all. Now, with the blade heating over Wren's small flame and Anselm holding my leg steady with hands that shook only a little, I finally understood: a doctor would have set the bone clean. Clean bone heals smooth. Smooth bone holds nothing. He'd needed the fracture to heal *around* something, the way a tree grows around a nail, and he'd needed his son too young and too trusting to ever ask what the hard knot in his thigh was.

He carved the cipher into a sliver of his own making—I recognized the material now, the same dense yellowed bone as the chest's seal-plate—and he packed it into the break before it closed, and then he carried me out of that dig and never spoke of it again, and I grew up around the secret the way the bone had grown around the cipher, without knowing, without pain until the weather turned.

Anselm cut. I will not describe it fully. There was fire, and there was Wren singing something low and old in a language I didn't know but my body seemed to, and there was the moment when the knife's tip touched the knot in my femur and the knot *rang*—a clear small note through my whole skeleton, like a struck bell—and then it was loose in Anselm's forceps, a curved fragment the length of my smallest finger, grooved along its length in a spiral that wound down to a point.

Blood everywhere. My blood, ordinary and red, and I laughed at that, I couldn't help it. I'd half expected it to come out green with age, bronze with history.

Sela took the fragment from me with both hands, the way you take bread in a famine, and pressed it into the socket.

It fit the way a word fits a sentence.

Something moved inside the door—a weight shifting after a hundred years of stillness—and the spiral drew the fragment down, down, swallowing it to the last groove, and the door exhaled. Dust breathed out of seams I couldn't see. And the hillside opened.

Not violently. The bronze panel sank and rolled aside on some patient mechanism, and beyond it a passage sloped down into the dark, and up out of that dark came air that tasted of stone and cold water and, impossibly, of cedar—wood sealed away so long it had become a smell older than memory.

"The source," Sela whispered. "It's under the vault. The spring feeds the whole river. Ostrava built its archive around the beginning of the Verrin." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet, and I felt the pull of belonging again, that current I'd been fighting since Lowmark. "Ada. We're standing on the headwater of everything."

We went down together.

* * *

The vault was not a room. It was a drowned cathedral.

Our lamps found columns, dozens of them, carved with the interlocking pattern running floor to ceiling, and between the columns—shelves. Racks. Cases of leaded glass gone cloudy with time, and behind the glass, documents. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Scrolls, folios, maps drawn on skin, ledgers bound in boards that had once been painted and now showed only ghost-colors. The spring rose through a basin at the far end, welling up silent and glass-clear through a ring of carved stone, feeding a channel that ran the length of the floor and disappeared into the rock—the first inch of the Verrin River, born indoors, under guard, among its own people's memory.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Wren knelt at the channel and put her fingers in the water and left them there like a woman praying.

Then the lamps went out.

All but one. Someone had killed the others—snuffed, not dropped—and in the sudden dark I heard the thing I had trained ten years to hear: a boot finding dry stone with intent. More than one boot. At least four.

"Lanterns up," said Corven Hale's voice, mild as a man discussing freight rates. "Slowly. I'd hate for anyone to trip in a place this old."

Light bloomed. Hale stood at the head of the passage with six men behind him and a crossbow that wasn't pointed anywhere in particular, which was worse than if it had been. He looked different up here. Out of his Provision grays, in plain travel leathers, with dust on him and a scratch healing on his jaw, he looked—tired. Like a man who had walked a long way to be somewhere he didn't want to arrive.

"Tomas is alive," he said, before anyone could ask. "Marta and the boy too. I keep my arithmetic honest; dead informants tell no further tales." His eyes moved across the shelves, the cases, the spring, and something crossed his face that I would spend the rest of my life trying to name. Recognition. Grief. Homesickness, maybe, in a man who had no home. "You opened it. Of course you opened it. Underhill's boy."

"You knew my father."

"I read his file." Hale stepped forward, and his men held position, and Sela's hand found my arm. "Elias Underhill, licensed surveyor, license revoked, last expedition unregistered, presumed drowned at the gorge. The Provision has a file on everyone who ever came looking for this place. Do you know why?" He smiled, and it was the saddest use of a smile I've ever seen. "Because I've seen the founding ledgers. The ones locked under the Counting House. The fortune that built the Provision—the loans, the locks, the whole arithmetic we enforce—it came out of this valley a hundred years ago. Out of *these* shelves. They didn't erase Ostrava out of spite. They erased it because they're spending its inheritance, and they can't afford for anyone to count."

"Then you know what this is worth," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "You know what the Provision would pay—"

"I know exactly what they'd pay." He took another step. Close enough now that I could see the sweat at his temple, the way his gaze kept snagging on the spring and pulling away. "That's why I'm not offering it to them. I'm offering it to *you*, Underhill. Walk away. All of you. Seal the door, forget the coordinates, and I report the vault collapsed, unrecoverable. In exchange—" he reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, heavy stock, Provision watermark, "—full amnesty. Clean licenses. A salvage claim on the richest stretch of the lower river, deeded, legal, yours. You'd never run again. You'd never owe anyone."

The paper hung in the lamplight between us, and I swear the vault went quiet enough that I could hear the spring breathing.

Ten years. Ten years of rented cellars and forged stamps and selling relics quietly to men who paid in silence, ten years of waking with my boots by the door ready to leave before anyone learned my face. Everything I had drifted toward and away from, priced at last, itemized, offered free.

"That's how they got you," I said.

Hale's smile died.

"The amnesty. The claim. The clean ledger." I took a step toward him, and his men's weapons shifted, and he held up two fingers and they stilled. "They didn't buy you with threats. They bought you with *provision*. Made you the enforcer of the debt so you'd never have to admit you were in one. That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's how you keep a remnant son counting their money." I nodded at his wrist, bare below the pushed-up sleeve, where an old faded mark sat in the lamplight—the seal, the interlocking pattern, worn nearly to nothing by years of hiding under cuffs. "You're Ostravan."

The silence stretched so long that the spring filled it, that small eternal voice.

"My grandmother," Hale said finally, "came down this river with nothing but a recipe and a name she wouldn't teach my mother, because names get people found." He folded the amnesty paper slowly, precisely, and put it away. "I was eight when the Provision's clerks found us anyway. Debts my grandfather owed. Debts that were invented. They gave my mother a choice between the debtor's yard and my employment, and I have been doing their arithmetic for thirty years, and I told myself it was survival, and I told myself it was strategy, and I stood in the Counting House last month and watched clerks burn a cartload of pre-Provision records because the new audit required it, and I have come a very long way up this river to find out whether I am still the man who watched them burn."

His hand went to the crossbow. My heart stopped. Then he reversed it, butt-first, and drove it into the jaw of the man beside him, and the vault exploded into motion—lamps falling, Sela shouting, Wren hurling a case-lid, Hale wading into his own men like a man settling a thirty-year account.

And above us, faint but unmistakable, drifting down the passage from the bright mouth of the day: oars. Many oars. The Provision's skiffs had reached the source, and they were landing.
