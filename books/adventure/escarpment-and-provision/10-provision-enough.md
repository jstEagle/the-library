## 10. Provision Enough

Sela had spent her whole life learning to read the river, and in the weeks after the vault opened, she watched it carry something down its length that no barge of hers could have hauled: paper.

Copies of the archive went out on every current. Ada's doing—his design, his stubborn arithmetic. He'd calculated the flood season's reach, hired every raft-hand and fisher who would take a sealed oilcloth packet for coin or kindness, and sent Ostrava's history sliding down the Verrin into forty towns before the Provision understood what was happening. By the time Corven Hale's skiffs began pulling packets from the water, children in Weir Hollow were already reciting the names of Ostravan kings like a skipping rhyme.

The Provision's answer came as it always did: not with soldiers but with silence. The toll houses closed. The ledgers stopped being shown. In Lowmark, the consortium's factor simply locked his office one morning and was gone, leaving behind a strongbox of contracts that Sela's people pried open and read aloud in the market square until dusk—every debt, every forged signature, every farm quietly swallowed over ninety years.

"They'll come back," Tomas said. His leg had healed crooked, set by a Provision surgeon who charged him nothing and asked no questions, which told Sela more about the state of the consortium than any ledger. "A beast that size doesn't die. It just changes shape."

"Then we change faster," Sela said.

She stood on the low bluff above Lowmark with the settlement plans spread on a plank table weighted with stones. Not a camp—a town. That had been her word at the first council, spoken before nine descendants grown suddenly to sixty, before farmers who'd lost their fields to Provision debt, before raft-hands and diggers and two of Hale's own former enforcers who arrived with their confessions written out and their hands shaking. *A town,* she'd said, *built on what we dug up instead of what they buried.*

The first foundation trench went in where the flood had given back the chest, because Ada insisted the town should begin at the place where the river had started telling the truth.

* * *

He worked the trench himself, of course. Sela found him there at dawn on the day they'd chosen to lay the first stone, sleeves rolled, boots caked, moving earth with the economy of a man who had done nothing else his whole life. He didn't look up when she stopped at the edge.

"You're supposed to be at the ceremony," she said.

"I don't do ceremonies."

"You founded this."

"I dug a hole." He straightened, wiped his hands on his thighs. "The hole was already here. The river made it. I just—" He stopped, and Sela waited, because she'd learned that Ada Underhill finished sentences only when he trusted the listener not to walk away. "I used to think staying was the thing that got you caught. You dig somewhere long enough, someone comes with a ledger. So you go. That was the whole philosophy. Ten years of it."

"And now?"

"Now I keep thinking about my father." He looked down into the trench, at the soil dark and wet and honest. "He stayed too long at the source. Or maybe he didn't stay long enough anywhere else. I can't decide which killed him."

"He hid the key in you," Sela said. "That's not a man planning to be forgotten. That's a man planning an heirloom."

Ada almost smiled. It was the closest thing to grief she'd seen him allow since the vault—the grief of a son discovering his father had believed in a future after all, and had staked it on a boy he abandoned without explanation. They had talked about it once, on the barge, in the dark: the journal's last pages, the line where Aldous Underhill wrote *the cipher goes where no one confiscates it* and then, beneath it, smaller, as if ashamed: *forgive me the method.* Ada had read it aloud twice and then put the journal away and not touched it for a week.

"I want to bury it," he said now. "The journal. Here. Not at the source—that's Ostrava's ground, and it should hold their dead, not mine. Here, where the chest came up. Where everything started." He glanced at her, quick, wary, the old reflex. "Is that allowed? Burying private things in a public foundation?"

"It's required," Sela said. "Every town needs something true under its first stone. Most get by with a coin and a lie. We can afford better."

* * *

They buried the journal at noon, with everyone watching, which Ada endured the way a man endures cold water—one breath at a time. Sela spoke the old words, the ones her grandmother had kept alive in a language the maps had erased, and then Ada knelt alone at the trench's edge and said something no one else heard. When he rose, he pressed the earth flat with both palms, deliberately, the way a man closes a door he intends to remember the location of.

Tomas laid the first stone. The masons took over. And Ada Underhill walked to the survey stakes at the northern boundary and began measuring the next trench, because ceremony was over and there was work, and work was the only prayer he knew.

* * *

*The thing about a foundation trench,* Ada thought, driving the first stake, *is that you can't lie to it.*

The ground told you everything. Where it had been turned before, where water ran, where nothing would grow. Ten years of drifting had taught him to read ground the way other men read faces, and this ground—Lowmark's bluff, the flood-carved channel, the rich silt the river had been paying out for centuries—this ground said *stay* in a language even he couldn't pretend not to speak.

He worked through the afternoon. Word traveled the way it always did in a new settlement, which was to say badly and fast, and by evening half the town had opinions about where the granary should go. A woman named Petra argued for the high ground; a former enforcer named Doss argued for defensibility; Sela argued for the river, because towns that turned their backs on water turned their backs on their own food. They shouted pleasantly over the survey map until full dark, and Ada noticed that he had opinions too, and that he had voiced them, and that no one had used them against him.

Strange, that. Strange, how long a man could live on the wrong side of a simple fact.

Afterward he climbed the bluff alone, past the fresh-laid stone, to where the journal slept under six feet of earth. The valley spread below him: the Verrin running silver in the moonlight, torchlight in Lowmark's new streets, and far downstream, faint as a scar, the dark shape of the Provision's remaining stronghold at the weir—still manned, still watching, still owed.

Hale had gone to ground after the vault. No one had seen him since the skiffs retreated. But Ada had read enough of the man's face in that standoff to know the difference between surrender and retreat, and Corven Hale had not surrendered. Somewhere in that stronghold was a man with Ostravan blood and Provision training, holding whatever fragments of the old ledgers the consortium hadn't burned, deciding what shape the beast would take when it changed.

Ada crouched and laid his hand flat on the cool earth above the journal.

"Dad," he said. "I'm staying. You'd laugh."

The river answered, moving in the dark, carrying the valley's first honest year toward the sea. Ada stood, brushed the dirt from his knees, and walked back down toward the lights—not toward the road, not toward the water, not away.

There was digging to do in the morning.
