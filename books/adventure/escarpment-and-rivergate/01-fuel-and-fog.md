## 1. Fuel and Fog

The fog came off the river at four in the morning the way it always did in October, rising like something exhaled by the valley itself, and Odile Dunmore was already awake to watch it happen.

She stood on the porch of her cabin with a tin mug of coffee going cold in her hands and listened to Escarpment wake up below her: the generator at the fuel depot coughing into its morning rhythm, a dog somewhere near the hotel making its single complaint about the day, the creak of the wind sock bearing as the first breath of thermal air moved up the slope. The town was less a town than an argument between the mountain and the river—a scatter of roofs and hangars wedged onto the shelf of land where the escarpment dropped five hundred feet to the water, as if someone had once tried to build a staircase out of the cliff and given up halfway.

Nine years she'd lived here. Nine years, and the fog still told her things the town never had.

She could read it the way other people read weather reports. This morning's was a ground fog, shallow and well-behaved, pooled in the low bends of the river with maybe two hundred feet of visibility down at the strip and clear air above three hundred. It would burn off by eight, reluctantly, from the edges inward, the way ice left a pond. Which meant she had a window—not a generous one, but a real one—to get the Kessler girl down to St. Aubin before the pediatric ward closed its last bed to medevacs for the day.

Odile drank the cold coffee anyway. Waste nothing; that was the first law of the interior. Then she went inside, dressed in the dark without turning on a light—another habit so old it had stopped being a habit and become simply the shape of her—and loaded her kit into the truck.

* * *

The Cessna 208 sat in Hangar Two like a patient animal that knew her footsteps. She'd named it *Vesper* eleven years ago, back when she'd bought it with the insurance money and the last of her father's estate, and the name had outlived every reason for it. She ran her preflight by feel as much as by checklist now, hands moving over the cowling fasteners, the pitot tube, the tire sidewalls, while the fog pressed against the open hangar door like a gray animal wanting in.

Fuel first. She dragged the hose across the apron, the smell of avgas sharp enough to cut through the damp, and watched the gauge climb while the sky behind the escarpment went from black to a bruised charcoal that wasn't quite dawn. There was no one else on the field. There was rarely anyone else on the field at this hour, which was precisely why she chose it. The whole aerodrome belonged to her for ninety minutes a day—the silence, the wet gravel, the mountain standing over everything like a chaperone who'd long since stopped caring what she got up to.

Her father had built half these hangars. That was the joke nobody in Escarpment made anymore, because everyone who remembered him had learned not to say his name around her, and everyone who hadn't known him didn't care. Dunmore Aviation, the sign had read, in letters her father had painted himself the summer Odile turned six, standing on a ladder while she held the bottom and Theo, four years older and already restless, chased gulls along the fence line. The sign was gone now. The company had died with him, and the strip had passed through three owners since, and Odile had stayed on as the only pilot willing to fly the medicine runs year-round, monsoon included, for a wage that would have embarrassed her if she'd ever let herself look at it directly.

She didn't look at it directly. She didn't look at most things directly. That was the art she'd perfected: the woman who flew in, delivered the insulin or the antibiotics or the pregnant teenager bleeding too early, signed the clipboard, and flew out again. The woman whose cabin had no photographs on the walls. The woman who answered questions about herself with questions about cargo weight and runway conditions until people stopped asking.

It had taken years to build, this invisibility, and she maintained it the way Wren maintained engines—with daily, unglamorous attention, tightening the small fasteners of her life before they could work loose.

"Morning, Odile."

She didn't jump, but it was close. Wren Adeyemi came around the nose of the plane carrying two mugs and the particular walk of someone who'd been awake all night and saw no reason to pretend otherwise. Grease to the elbows, coveralls unzipped to the waist over a faded shirt that said *ASK ME ABOUT MY TORQUE VALUES*, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of safety wire. Wren had a genius for looking like a man who'd been assembled hastily from spare parts and yet was somehow the most reliable object on the entire airfield.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Odile said.

"I'm supposed to be checking your number two cylinder, which I did, at three a.m., because somebody's flying a medevac through fog today and I couldn't sleep knowing she'd be doing it on yesterday's compression readings." He handed her a mug. "Compression's fine, by the way. You're welcome."

"You could have called."

"And interrupted your beauty sleep? You'd have bitten my head off." He leaned against the strut, blowing steam off his mug, and looked at her with those level dark eyes that saw entirely too much. "Fog's lifting off the river already. You'll be wheels-up by seven if you want."

"That's the plan."

"Course it is." A pause, comfortable, unhurried. Wren was the only person in Escarpment who could stand next to her in silence without either of them needing to fill it. "Kettle Falls reported ice on their taxiway last night. Might be worth a call ahead."

"I'll raise them on the way."

He nodded, drained the mug, and pushed off the strut—but he paused, hand on the fuselage, and gave the airplane a pat the way you'd steady a horse. "Bring her home," he said. "And yourself."

"Always do," Odile said, and hated how much the words sounded like a promise she was keeping only in the narrowest technical sense.

* * *

The fog broke apart under her wheels at 6:52 and reformed behind her like water closing over a stone.

She climbed out over the river on instruments for the first ninety seconds, the world outside the windshield reduced to a soft gray nothing, and then punched through the top into a sunrise so clean and violent it felt like a secret. The cloud deck rolled away beneath her to the north, white and endless, and above it the escarpment range stood up in blue-black ridges with the first light gilding their eastern faces. Somewhere beyond those ridges, forty miles as the crow flew and nine years as the soul did, lay Rivergate.

She didn't look that direction. She was very good at not looking that direction.

The flight to St. Aubin was fifty minutes of the kind of flying that had hollowed her out and rebuilt her into something functional. She flew the route the way a blind woman walks her own hallway—by count, by feel, by the particular way the air buffeted the wings over the narrows where the river bent around Silver Rock. Her hands moved on the yoke in small corrections, constant, almost invisible, the aircraft and the pilot breathing together. Passengers always thought a smooth flight meant calm air. Only pilots knew it meant someone working.

In the right seat, wrapped in blankets and trying very hard to be brave about it, sat the Kessler girl. Nine years old, appendix angry and getting angrier, face pale as the fog they'd left behind. Her mother rode in the back, holding her daughter's hand across the seat gap.

"How long?" the girl asked, when they leveled off above the clouds.

"Forty minutes," Odile said. "You'll be eating hospital pudding before lunch."

"I hate pudding."

"Then you'll be refusing pudding before lunch. Even better." She glanced sideways. "You want to see something?"

The girl nodded. Odile banked gently left, and the sun came through the side window and lit the cloud tops on fire, gold running to rose running to a violet that no painter would dare put on canvas because no one would believe it.

"Whoa," said the girl, forgetting to be sick for exactly four seconds.

"There's a whole country up here," Odile said. "Most people never see it. They live down underneath, in the weather, and they think the sky is just where rain comes from." She leveled the wings. "But it goes on forever. You can go anywhere."

The mother caught Odile's eye in the mirror and mouthed *thank you*. Odile nodded and turned back to her instruments, and the moment folded itself away into the place where she kept such moments—there were hundreds of them by now, thousands, a whole archive of small salvations filed in a part of her heart she visited only at night, alone, when the work of being invisible was done for the day.

Because that was the arithmetic she'd worked out, nine years ago, standing in the wreckage-light of the worst day of her life: she could not save everyone, but she could save some of them, one flight at a time, and if she asked for nothing in return—if she took no thanks she couldn't deflect, formed no attachments she couldn't break, let no one close enough to be lost—then the saving wouldn't cost her anything she couldn't afford to pay.

It was a lie, of course. It cost her everything, continuously, and she paid it gladly, and called the arrangement freedom.

* * *

St. Aubin's hospital had a helipad that doubled as a parking lot and an ambulance crew that doubled as orderlies, and Odile had the girl transferred and stable-bound inside twenty minutes. She declined the coffee in the staff room. Declined the doctor's invitation to wait out the fog burn-off in comfort. Signed the transfer forms with a signature that was barely legible and entirely anonymous, and walked back out to the apron while the fog was still peeling off the runway in ragged ribbons.

On the way south she stopped at Kettle Falls to drop a crate of veterinary supplies that had ridden up with her, dead weight on the outbound leg. The strip there was bigger than Escarpment's—two hangars instead of four shacks, a fuel agent who wore a collared shirt, a café that opened at seven and served food a person could identify. She ate eggs at the counter and listened to the local pilots talk about the season coming on.

"Early monsoon this year," said one, a freight hauler with a face like a dried riverbed. "My brother-in-law's got a barometer older than me and it's been dropping for three days straight. Says the pattern's wrong. Says it's coming in from the northwest, which it doesn't do."

"They always say that," said another.

"They're always wrong," agreed a third, "until the one time they're right, and then we're all stuck on the wrong side of the range for four months wishing we'd listened to somebody's brother-in-law."

Odile ate her eggs and said nothing. She'd flown the interior through nine monsoons. She knew the rhythm of it the way she knew her own pulse—the building pressure, the first storms that arrived like scouts, then the wall, the weeks of solid gray when the passes filled with cloud and rock and moving water and nothing flew anywhere except at prices that made grown men swear. Every year the same. Every year the towns on the far side of the range stocked up in October or took their chances.

Every year, Rivergate stocked up or didn't.

She paid for her breakfast and walked out to the plane, and found herself standing on the apron for a moment longer than necessary, looking north. The escarpment range ran across the horizon like a locked gate—ridge after ridge after ridge, blue in the distance, each one higher than the last, and the passes between them were just shadows, just suggestions, thin places where the mountains had grudgingly allowed the idea of a road. One pass in particular, second from the left, sat lower than the others. Wider. Flyable, in good weather, by any competent pilot.

Nobody flew it.

Officially, this was because of the disaster nine years ago—the slide, the lost planes, the town cut off for a winter. Unofficially, it was because of what the slide had uncovered, though Odile had spent nine years refusing to think about what it had uncovered, and she wasn't going to start on a Tuesday morning on a fuel apron in Kettle Falls.

She looked away. She was very good at looking away.

* * *

Escarpment came up out of the afternoon haze the way it always did, sudden and modest, the strip appearing between the tree line and the river bend like something the landscape had misplaced and then remembered. She set the *Vesper* down soft, taxied in past the fuel depot, and shut down in front of Hangar Two, where Wren was waiting with a wrench in one hand and an expression on his face that stopped her before her wheels had fully stopped rolling.

Wren did not have expressions like that. Wren's face was a study in mildness—wry, warm, unbothered. Whatever was on it now was new.

"What," Odile said, climbing down.

"Two things." He wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "One, your number three spark plug fouled on final, probably, based on how that last approach sounded. I'll pull it tonight. Not urgent."

"And two?"

Wren hesitated. In the nine years she'd known him, Odile could count on one hand the number of times Wren Adeyemi had hesitated before saying something, and she'd never needed more than two fingers.

"There was a man here this morning," he said. "While you were gone. Chartered in from the coast on a King Air—corporate type, suit, the whole costume. Didn't give a name. Asked a lot of questions about the airstrip. Who owned it, what the debt situation was, whether the owner was in poor health." He watched her carefully. "I told him the owner's business was the owner's business, and he smiled like I'd confirmed something, and then he asked one more question."

The wind sock creaked overhead. Down the slope, the river ran silver and indifferent toward the sea.

"He asked," Wren said, "whether you were any relation to the Dunmores. And Odile—I've known you nine years, and I know you don't talk about your family, and I've never once asked, and I'm not asking now. But the way he said it—" He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't curiosity. It was like a man checking an item off a list."

Odile stood very still beside her airplane, one hand on the warm cowling, and felt something shift far beneath her feet, the way the mountain sometimes shifted in the deep night—a settling, a warning, the sound of a thing beginning to move that would take a long time to stop.

"What did you tell him?" she asked.

"Nothing," Wren said. "But Odile—he already knew. That's the thing. He wasn't asking to find out. He was asking to see what my face did."
