## 2. The Ledger

The news came up the valley on the morning flight, the way all news came to Escarpment: carried in a mail sack, half-crushed under a crate of insulin and someone's birthday parcel from a sister in the capital.

Odile learned of it at the fuel shed, where she'd gone to log her hours before the fog burned off. Wren was already there, sitting on an overturned oil drum with the radio handset pressed to one ear, and something in the set of those shoulders—Wren had shoulders like a doorframe, always slightly braced against the world—told her before a word was spoken that the day had changed shape.

"What is it?" she asked.

Wren lowered the handset slowly. "Emmett died."

The name landed strangely, the way names of permanent things do when they stop being permanent. Emmett Sorrel had owned the airstrip for thirty-one years. He had owned it longer than Odile had been alive, longer than the hangar had had its second bay, longer than the coffee urn in the office had gone unwashed. He was not a man so much as a fixture—a condition of the landscape, like the escarpment itself, like the fog that pooled in the river bend each morning and burned away by nine.

"Died how?"

"Heart, they think. His sister found him in the kitchen." Wren set the handset down on the drum with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate. "There's more."

"There's always more."

"His debts didn't die with him."

Odile leaned against the fuel pump and waited. She was good at waiting; it was most of what flying medicine into the interior taught you—that everything urgent could be survived by standing very still and letting it arrive.

"They're saying the strip's collateral," Wren said. "On his loans. Has been for years, maybe. Nobody knew except Emmett and the bank, and now—" Wren spread both hands, palms up, grease-blackened nails catching the grey light. "Now it belongs to whoever holds the paper."

"And who holds the paper?"

"A bank in Kettle Falls. And whoever they sell to." Wren looked at her then, directly, and there was something underneath the mechanic's usual flatness that Odile recognized and did not want to name. "They're saying he owed a lot, Odile. More than the strip's worth. More than anything here is worth."

* * *

Escarpment was not a town that believed in death until it saw the body. By noon, though, the whole settlement had arranged itself around the fact of Emmett's absence the way water arranges itself around a pulled stone. The café kept his table empty. The postmistress flew the flag at the field at half-mast, which was technically against regulations and which nobody mentioned. Men who hadn't spoken to Emmett in a decade stood at the fuel shed recounting arguments they'd won against him twenty years ago, their voices gone soft with the particular tenderness people reserve for the dead once the dead can no longer argue back.

And through all of it moved the daughter.

Odile watched her from across the apron: a narrow woman in a city coat too good for the gravel dust, standing in the doorway of the office her father had ruled like a weather system. Her name, the postmistress reported within the hour, was Junie Sorrel. She had come up from the coast on the mail plane, alone, with one suitcase. She had not cried where anyone could see. She stood in the doorway of the office and looked out at the strip—the two rust-streaked hangars, the windsock hanging limp in the still air, the gravel runway running a thousand meters toward the wall of the escarpment—and her face did nothing at all, which told Odile more than weeping would have.

She knew that face. She wore it herself, most days.

It was Wren who went over first, because Wren was constitutionally incapable of leaving a person standing alone in a doorway, and because grief, in Wren's economy, was just another machine that needed someone to hold the light while you worked on it. Odile followed at a distance she told herself was respect and knew was cowardice.

"Ms. Sorrel," Wren said. "I'm Wren Adeyemi. Mechanic. We're—all of us here—we're sorry about your father."

Junie Sorrel looked at Wren, then past Wren's shoulder at Odile, and her eyes were dry and red-rimmed and terribly awake.

"Are you," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't gratitude. It was arithmetic. "Then I suppose you'll want to know where you stand. Come inside. Both of you. There are papers."

* * *

The office smelled of Emmett—engine oil and peppermints and forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the walls—and of paper. Paper everywhere: fuel receipts banded with rubber bands gone brittle, maintenance invoices, correspondence in envelopes furred with dust. Junie sat behind the desk that had been her father's and laid out a folder with hands that trembled only slightly, only at the edges, and Odile understood that the trembling had been practiced down to almost nothing, the way her own had been, once.

"My father borrowed against this place," Junie said. "For eleven years, apparently. Equipment, mostly. A new grader for the runway. Roof repairs. Some of it I don't know what it bought." She turned a page without looking at it. "The strip is pledged as security. All of it. The land, the hangars, the lease on the frequency rights. If the debt isn't serviced—or bought out—by the end of the quarter, the holder takes possession."

"The holder being the bank," Odile said.

"The holder being whoever the bank sells to." Junie looked up, and for a moment the practiced flatness cracked and Odile saw underneath it a woman roughly her own age, drowning in a man's paperwork in a town she clearly hated. "Banks don't want an airstrip in the middle of nowhere. They'll sell the paper to someone who does. Someone who thinks a monopoly on the only supply route into four hundred kilometers of interior is worth owning." She closed the folder. "So. That's where you stand. That's where everyone stands. I'm sorry. I didn't make it true; I just inherited it."

Everything her father left her is a debt. The thought arrived in Odile unbidden, unwelcome, wearing a voice she hadn't heard in nine years—her own father's voice, low and tired, reading figures aloud at this same kind of desk in another lifetime. She pushed it down. There would be time for it later, or there wouldn't, and either way the work would continue, because work was the one inheritance that never defaulted.

"We'll keep flying," Odile said. "Whatever happens with the paper. The medevac runs don't stop because ownership changes."

"No," Junie agreed quietly. "They don't. That's rather the point, isn't it? Whoever buys this place buys the flying too. Buys *you*." She said it without malice, which made it worse.

* * *

That night Odile did not sleep. She lay in her room above the hangar listening to the building tick and settle, and at some hour past midnight she gave up, dressed, and took the lamp down the outside stairs to the old freight room at the back of the main hangar—the room that had been her father's, that she rented from the strip for storage and entered perhaps twice a year, each time braced like a woman approaching a grave she wasn't sure she had the right to visit.

Her father's things were exactly as she had left them. Crates of charts gone soft with damp at the corners. A tool roll she never used because Wren's tools were better. On the shelf above the workbench, between a tin of spare fuses and a jar of screws sorted by no system any living person could reconstruct, stood the ledger.

It was a fat book bound in green canvas, its spine broken, its corners rounded by decades of hands. Her father had kept it from his first year flying the interior—every route, every contract, every account, entered in a small precise hand that Odile had spent her childhood watching move across pages by lamplight. After he died she had put it on the shelf and not opened it, because opening it meant reading the last entries, and the last entries were about Rivergate.

Tonight she took it down.

The lamp threw a circle of yellow on the bench. She set the ledger in the center of it and stood over it for a long moment, the way you stand at the edge of cold water, and then she opened it—not at the end, but at the beginning, because she was not ready for the end and might never be, and beginnings were safer.

Route accounts. Fuel costs per kilometer, tracked season by season with an accountant's patience and a pilot's marginalia: *headwind bad in March, take the east side.* Rates negotiated, sometimes hard-won—she found a page where a rancher's price had been crossed out twice and her father had written in the margin, in letters pressed deep enough to dent three pages beneath: *He can afford it. The cattle can't fly themselves.*

She read for an hour. It was like hearing his voice again, and it was unbearable, and she did not stop.

The routes marched north through the book, year after year. Kettle Falls. Cold Larder. The mission at Sainte-Anne. And Rivergate—Rivergate appeared constantly in the early pages, a regular run, grain and mail and medicine and once, memorably, an entire wedding party ferried over the escarpment in three loads, her father's note beside it: *Bride got sick over the pass. Groom never stopped talking. Good people. Worth the trouble.*

Good people. Worth the trouble.

Her throat tightened. She turned pages faster now, wanting to reach the end of the story she already knew, wanting it over with, and the entries grew sparser, the handwriting tighter, and then—

She stopped.

The page was dated the spring of the disaster year. Where the Rivergate column should have been, there was no entry. There was ink. Heavy, deliberate, applied with a brush or a broad-nibbed pen, laid on so thick it had soaked through to the next leaf. A single black bar, drawn straight across the route name, and beneath it, in her father's hand, pressed hard:

*Sworn off. Not negotiable. God forgive me.*

Not *we no longer fly it.* Not *the contract lapsed.* Sworn off. The language of oaths. The language of men who had decided something in the night and would carry it like ballast for the rest of their lives.

Odile stood in the cold hangar with the lamp burning and understood, finally and completely, what her father had left her. Not the ledger. Not the tool roll, not the crates of charts, not the memory of his voice reading figures by lamplight. He had left her a debt—the same debt Emmett Sorrel had left Junie, written not in a bank's figures but in a dead man's ink: the debt of a promise she had never been asked to make, secured against everything she had built since, callable at any moment, held by the dead.

And somewhere out beyond the fog line, past the escarpment, Rivergate sat in its valley with its winter coming, holding the other copy of the account.

She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin, and put it back on the shelf, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the green canvas cover as if taking a pulse.

Behind her, in the dark of the hangar proper, a floorboard creaked.

She turned, lamp raised. Wren stood in the doorway between the bays, silhouetted against the faint glow of the night-light over the tool wall, a mug in one hand, steam rising off it into the cold air.

"You weren't at your window," Wren said. "Your light's usually on till two. I brought tea." A pause. "You've been crying."

"I've been reading."

"Same thing, some nights." Wren came forward and set the mug on the bench beside her, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and looked at the ledger with the expression Wren reserved for engines that had been run badly by people who should have known better. "That's his, isn't it. Your dad's book."

"Yes."

"Can I ask what's in it?"

Odile looked at the mug, at the steam curling up between them, at the face above it—patient, watchful, offering warmth the way Wren offered everything: steadily, without conditions, expecting nothing back.

"A route," she said. "Blacked out."

Wren nodded slowly, and did not push, and that—more than the tea, more than the presence—was nearly her undoing. They stood together in the lamplight, not touching, not speaking, while outside the fog crept back up the river bend to wait for morning.

Neither of them knew yet that the paper on the strip was already moving south through the banks like blood through a heart, that it would change hands within the week, that the person buying it had a name Odile had known since flight school and a smile like a filed tie-rod.

But the ledger knew. Ledgers always know first.
