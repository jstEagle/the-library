## 3. Wren

The engine came apart the way it always did, in layers, like a confession.

Wren Adeyemi worked by lamplight in the hangar's north bay, where the corrugated roof leaked only in two places and both drips had been tamed with coffee cans and wire. The Cessna 208 that Odile Dunmore flew sat on jacks with its cowling off, and Wren stood hip-deep in its open heart, hands black to the wrist, easing the number three cylinder free with a patience that would have surprised anyone who'd only seen her argue with suppliers or curse at the fuel truck. Patience was easy here. Patience was just love with a torque wrench.

The engine was tired. That was the truth of it. Nine years of Odile flying heavy loads into short strips, nine years of hot starts and marginal fuel and that one season she'd run it lean over the escarpment because the alternative was landing at Kettle Falls after dark, and the engine remembered all of it the way a body remembers a hard life. The borescope showed scoring on the cylinder walls. The oil analysis had come back with iron in it, quiet as a warning whispered through a closed door.

So Wren was rebuilding it. Not because Odile had asked—Odile never asked for anything, that was the whole architecture of the woman—but because Wren had read the oil report three weeks ago and started ordering parts before she'd finished reading it.

"You're doing it again."

Wren didn't look up. She knew the voice, knew the particular way it arrived without footsteps, because Odile walked soft everywhere except inside her own life. "Doing what?"

"Rebuilding my engine without being asked."

"I'm inspecting your engine," Wren said. "Inspection is routine. Maintenance is scheduled. Anything else is slander."

There was a pause, and then the sound of Odile setting down the thermos she'd brought, which was how she apologized for things too small to say out loud. Coffee appeared at the edge of Wren's workbench, in the dented tin mug Wren had owned since her first year at Escarpment, the one with the handle epoxied twice.

"I inspected it in June," Odile said.

"You flew it in June. I inspected it in June. Those are different verbs." Wren finally straightened, working the ache out of her lower back, and looked at her. Odile was still in her flight jacket, fog-damp at the shoulders, hair escaping its braid in the way it did after four hours of instrument work when there was nothing to look at but gray. She had the particular exhaustion of a pilot who'd just threaded the river valley in visibility measured in wingspans, and she wore it the way she wore everything—like it was nobody's business but hers.

"How was the medevac?" Wren asked, though she'd been listening to the radio traffic all morning with one ear, the way she always did when Odile was up in weather.

"The baby's going to be fine. Mother too." Odile wrapped both hands around her own mug. "Old Man Teller's daughter. She named the baby already. Wants to name the next one after the plane if it's a boy."

"The plane's name is a registration number."

"She doesn't know that."

Wren laughed despite herself, and saw the corner of Odile's mouth move—the almost-smile, the one that cost her something every time it got out. Nine years Wren had worked this strip, and in all that time she could count the full smiles on one hand and have fingers left over. It wasn't coldness, exactly. It was more like Odile had decided somewhere back down the line that taking up space in other people's days was a debt she couldn't afford, and so she paid her way in competence instead. Flights flown. Engines kept running. Patients delivered breathing. Never a favor asked, never a story offered.

Wren had spent years learning to read her anyway. It was the same skill as diagnostics: you learned what normal sounded like, and then you listened for the deviation.

Right now Odile's voice had a deviation in it, low and buried, and Wren filed it away without naming it. Something had happened out there in the fog besides a baby. Something was sitting behind her eyes.

"Cylinder three's scored," Wren said instead. "I'm rebuilding the whole top end while I've got it open. You'll be down four days."

"Four—" Odile's jaw tightened. "I've got charters booked."

"You've got charters I'll rebook. Marta can take the Tuesday run in the 206." Wren wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago. "Or you can fly it as-is and I can spend the winter explaining to the transport board why I signed off an engine with iron in the oil. Your choice."

It wasn't a choice and they both knew it. Odile drank her coffee and stared at the open engine like it had personally betrayed her, and Wren let the silence do its work. This was their oldest rhythm: Odile pushing against the limits of things, Wren standing between her and the consequences. Neither of them had ever said it in those words. Saying it would make it a thing, and things could be lost.

"You could've told me last week," Odile said finally.

"And you'd have done what? Stopped flying?"

"I'd have scheduled it properly."

"You'd have flown until the engine made the decision for you." Wren held her gaze, steady. "I know you, Odile Dunmore. You treat maintenance recommendations like weather forecasts. Advisory only."

Something flickered across Odile's face—not quite guilt, not quite amusement. "And you treat me like one of your engines."

"No," Wren said, and the word came out more honest than she'd planned, so she covered it by turning back to the workbench. "Engines I understand completely."

She heard Odile huff—a real laugh, small and startled out of her—and then the sound of her boots crossing the concrete, and then, unexpectedly, nothing. When Wren glanced over, Odile had settled onto the old bench by the hangar doors, the one under the window where the morning light came through gray and clean, and she was just sitting there, drinking her coffee, watching Wren work.

That was new. Usually she delivered her grievances and left, the way she did everything: efficiently, without lingering. But today she stayed, and Wren found she had to concentrate harder than usual on the valve train, because being watched by Odile Dunmore was a sensation with weight to it, like a change in barometric pressure.

* * *

The logs lived in the office at the front of the hangar, in a green steel cabinet that predated everyone currently employed at Escarpment. Wren went in for them around noon, when Odile had gone up to the house to sleep off her early start, and pulled the maintenance records for the 208, because a rebuild meant paperwork and paperwork meant history, and Wren was one of those mechanics who believed an aircraft's logbooks were its biography.

Dunmore Aviation had kept meticulous books. That was the father—Elias Dunmore, dead six years now, though his handwriting was still everywhere at the strip, on placards and checklists and the faded sign over the fuel pump that said CHECK TWICE, FLY ONCE. Elias had been the kind of man who annotated everything, whose entries read less like records and more like letters to whoever came after him. *Left mag dropping 40 rpm, monitor. Cold snap coming, drain the truck lines. Tell O. the flaps stick when cold—she'll argue, show her this.*

*Tell O.* Half the logbook was messages to a daughter who had apparently never stopped long enough to read them. Wren had noticed that years ago and had never once mentioned it, because some griefs you don't poke.

She flipped backward through the years looking for the escarpment entries, the Rivergate route. Odile's brother had flown it before her—Theo, the older one, the one from before Wren's time—and Elias himself before that. The route was the spine of the company's history. Every year, the winter resupply, the pass crossings, the whole liturgy of it written out in service entries: prop de-ice checks, oxygen bottle hydro tests, the special equipment the high crossing required.

Except.

Wren turned the pages slower. The entries thinned out nine years ago. There was a service entry dated late summer—the annual inspection, signed by Elias, complete and ordinary—and then the next page began with the following spring, and between them there was nothing. No winter resupply entries. No pass-crossing prep. A blank season where the busiest season should have been.

She checked the page edges. Ran her thumb along the binding.

Three pages had been torn out. Not removed cleanly with a blade—torn, hurriedly, the stubs ragged, the tear-marks overlapping the gutter of the binding like something done fast and in feeling. And the stubs themselves were old; the paper had yellowed evenly with the rest, which meant they hadn't been torn yesterday. They'd been torn nine years ago, or close to it, and no one had ever replaced the pages or noted the omission, which any licensed engineer worth her stamp would have done, because a gap in the logs was itself a record. An aircraft with missing history was an aircraft you grounded until you understood it.

Nobody had. For nine years.

Wren stood in the office with the logbook open on the desk and the light going long and amber through the window, and she felt the specific cold that comes from touching a thing you weren't meant to find. She thought about Elias Dunmore, who annotated everything, who wrote notes to his daughter in government-mandated maintenance records because he couldn't help himself—and who had apparently torn three pages out of the official biography of his own airplane.

You don't tear pages out of a logbook over a mechanical failure. Mechanical failures get documented to death. You tear pages out over a decision. Over something that happened that had nothing to do with the machine and everything to do with the man holding the pen.

Rivergate. The word surfaced unbidden, the way it did around the strip sometimes, always sideways—old-timers mentioning the valley in the past tense, charts with the airstrip marked but no one ever filing for it. Wren had been at Escarpment eight years and had never once heard anyone say they were flying there. She'd assumed weather, economics, the slow arithmetic that kills remote routes. Now she looked at the torn stubs and thought: *no*. This was amputation, not attrition. Someone had cut this route out of the company's body and cauterized the stump.

Footsteps on the gravel outside. Wren closed the logbook, and then—her hands moving before her mind finished deliberating—slid it back into the cabinet, underneath the newer volumes, face-down.

Odile appeared in the doorway with her hair rebraided and her jacket zipped, looking rested and unreachable again. "Truck's back from the fuel farm. We good for the parts order?"

"Should be here Thursday," Wren said. Her voice came out level. She was good at level; she'd had practice. "Four days, like I said."

"Thursday." Odile nodded, and paused, and Wren watched her decide whether to say whatever was queued up behind her teeth. This was the other thing about Odile: you could see the sentences she chose not to say, stacked up behind her eyes like aircraft in a hold. "Wren."

"Yeah."

"Why are you really rebuilding the engine?"

Wren leaned against the doorframe of the parts room, arms crossed, and considered lying, and discarded it, because lying to Odile had never once worked and trying had a certain insulting quality. "Because I read the oil sample in September and I didn't want to spend the monsoon season listening to that engine and wondering."

"Wondering what?"

"Wondering if I'd signed off something that was going to quit on you over the trees." She said it plainly, the way you say the true things, and watched it land. Odile looked away first, out at the strip, where the fog was burning off and the escarpment stood up blue and enormous against the clearing sky, beautiful and indifferent, the wall that defined every horizon here.

"That's a lot of worry," Odile said quietly, "to carry for somebody else's airplane."

"It's not the airplane I'd be carrying it for."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the ones before it. Heavier. Warmer. Odile's ears, Wren noticed, had gone faintly pink, and she was studying the horizon with the concentration of a woman performing an instrument approach in clear weather.

"Well," Odile said. "Thursday."

"Thursday."

She walked out to the truck, and Wren watched her go, and let herself feel, for exactly as long as it took Odile to reach the vehicle, the full unguarded weight of what she'd just said. Then she buttoned it back down, because that was the arrangement, the unspoken treaty they'd both signed: the truth could surface in small doses, diluted, deniable, and neither of them would ever make the other swallow it whole.

* * *

She went back to the office after supper, when the strip was empty and the hangar lights hummed and the mountains had gone black against a bruised sunset.

The logbook lay where she'd hidden it. Wren sat with it for a long time before she opened it again, because she understood that opening it was a decision. Whatever Elias Dunmore had torn out, he'd torn out for reasons that had kept his daughter silent for nine years and a whole town's memory shut like a fist. It wasn't her history. It wasn't her wound.

But it was her airplane. Three pages missing from a logbook was an airworthiness question, and airworthiness questions were hers by trade and by law, and she clung to that framing even as she knew it was a raft built on thinner water.

She opened it. The torn stubs were exactly as she'd remembered—ragged, old, final. She tilted the last remaining page toward the lamp, the final entry before the gap, and read Elias's handwriting one more time. Annual inspection, all items completed, aircraft returned to service. Ordinary. Except at the bottom, below the signature line, in smaller script, squeezed in like an afterthought that wasn't:

*Route suspended. All north operations cancelled effective immediately. Do not reopen without me.*

No date beyond the inspection date. No reason. *Do not reopen without me*—and Elias Dunmore had been six years dead.

Wren sat very still in the pool of lamplight. Outside, the evening wind had come around to the northeast, smelling of snow that was still weeks away but coming, the way these things came, whether anyone was ready or not. Somewhere across the valley the escarpment held its dark shape against the last light, keeping whatever it kept.

Nine years of silence, three torn pages, and an order from a dead man that someone had obeyed without ever asking why. Wren thought about the deviation she'd heard in Odile's voice that morning, the thing sitting behind her eyes that she'd filed away and not named.

Tomorrow, she decided, closing the logbook softly, she was going to ask.

And then headlights swung up the access road—two vehicles, moving fast, dust hanging in their beams though the air was damp—and Wren stood at the dark window and watched them come, and felt the hair rise on her arms, because nobody drove to Escarpment airstrip at night unless they wanted something, and the last time strangers had arrived unannounced, her father had told her, the Dunmores had ended up owing the bank.

The trucks stopped. Doors opened. Four figures in the headlights, and one of them—a woman, tall, walking like she owned the ground under her feet—looked up at the hangar lights and smiled.

Wren reached for the hangar master switch, and waited.
