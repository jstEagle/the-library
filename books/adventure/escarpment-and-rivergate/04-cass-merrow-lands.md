## 4. Cass Merrow Lands

The Cessna came in low over the fuel depot at 9:47 in the morning, and Odile knew it before she saw the tail number.

She was under the wing of her own plane with a dipstick in one hand and a rag gone gray with years of oil, doing the morning check she'd done four thousand mornings running, when the sound reached her across the flat: that particular off-key drone of an engine leaned too rich on descent, a pilot who trusted horsepower over finesse. Most people heard an airplane. Odile heard a signature.

She straightened up slowly, wiped her hands, and watched Cass Merrow's plane drop out of the broken cloud cover north of town, sideslip the last two hundred feet like she was showing off for an audience of nobody, and touch down on Escarpment's single strip with a chirp of tires that said *I own this runway now* even though she didn't. Not yet. The paperwork hadn't landed. But Cass had always flown like the future was already signed.

Odile stood very still while the plane rolled out, long and unhurried, and turned at the far end, and taxied back toward the apron with its shadow sliding along the gravel beside it like a dog that had learned to keep its distance.

Nine years. Nine years since the last time they'd stood on the same piece of ground, and Odile's body remembered it the way a scar remembers weather — not as thought but as pressure, a tightening behind the sternum, a taste like pennies at the back of the throat.

"You going to greet her," Wren asked from the hangar door, "or should I put out the fire extinguisher now and save us the trip?"

"Neither." Odile hung the dipstick on its hook. "I'm going to finish my preflight."

"You've been doing your preflight for twenty minutes."

"Then I'm being thorough."

Wren looked at her a moment longer — that steady, unreadable look Wren did so well, the one that took things in without demanding them back — and then shrugged and went inside, which was Wren's way of saying *your funeral* without spending any words on it.

The Cessna shut down. The door swung open before the prop stopped turning, because of course it did, because Cass Merrow had never once in her life waited for anything to stop moving before stepping into it.

She'd changed. That was the first thing Odile noticed, and she hated herself for noticing it first instead of second or tenth. Cass had been lean and hungry at twenty-six, all elbows and ambition, hair hacked short for practicality and then grown out again three times in the year they'd flown together, depending on which version of herself she was auditioning for. Now she was forty-something and expensive — a wool coat that belonged in a city with valet parking, boots that had never seen real mud, hair cut with the kind of precision you paid for monthly. But the walk was the same. The walk was pure Cass: chin up, weight forward, like every step was a landing she intended to stick.

Behind her, two people climbed down from the rear seats. Suits. Actual suits, in Escarpment, where the dress code topped out at canvas and fleece. They stood by the plane looking around at the mountains, the fog burning off the tree line, the strip itself — gravel and frost heave and a windsock faded to the pink of old meat — with the expressions of men cataloguing assets.

"Odile Dunmore." Cass said it like a verdict. She stopped six feet away, close enough to talk without raising her voice, far enough to make a point about it. "Still flying that relic."

"She's older than both of us and she's never missed a medevac." Odile kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used with frightened passengers. "You're a long way from the coast, Merrow."

"I'm exactly where I'm supposed to be." Cass smiled, and it was almost the old smile, the one that used to make Odile laugh against her will in crew rooms and dive diners, back when rivalry had still been fun. Almost. There was something underneath it now, something held carefully in place, like a patch over a structural crack. "You look good, Dunmore."

"Liar."

"Yeah," Cass agreed easily. "You look tired. You look like a woman who's been drinking bad coffee alone for nine years."

"That's specific."

"I remember how you take it. Black, burnt, standing up." Cass glanced past her shoulder at the plane, at the strip, at the town strung along the river below the escarpment wall — taking inventory, Odile realized. Taking inventory of everything. "Where's your father?"

The question went through Odile like a downdraft.

"He died," she said. "Eight months ago."

Something moved through Cass's face then, quick and genuine and immediately suppressed — grief, or guilt, or the memory of a man who'd once bought them both burgers at Kettle Falls and told them, laughing, that if they ever stopped competing they might actually be good pilots. Whatever it was, Cass folded it away fast, the way she'd always folded away anything that slowed her down.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and hated meaning it, and Odile could see all three layers of it plain as paint.

"Are you." Odile crossed her arms. "You didn't come here to pay respects. You came here with lawyers."

"They're not my lawyers. They're the bank's." Cass tilted her head toward the suits. "Technically I'm just the new name on the paper. But yes. We need to talk business, and I'd rather do it inside than on your apron in the wind."

"There's nothing to talk about. The strip runs fine. Dunmore Aviation is current on—"

"On nothing, Odile." Cass said it quietly, and that was worse than shouting. Quiet was how Cass delivered the killing blows; Odile had learned that a decade ago and never unlearned it. "Your father carried debt for fifteen years. Equipment loans, the fuel contract, the mortgage on the hangar, the second mortgage he took after—" She stopped herself. After. They both knew what came after. "It doesn't matter why. It matters that it's called due, and that I'm holding it."

The wind shifted, coming down off the escarpment in the long cold slide it made every morning once the sun cleared the ridge, and Odile felt it move through her jacket and into her bones and thought, absurdly, of her father's ledger, the blacked-out line, the route he'd struck through so hard he'd torn the page.

"What contract," Odile said.

Cass smiled again, and this time there was no warmth in it at all, only the flat professional satisfaction of a woman reading terms aloud.

"Inside," she said. "Coffee first. Then the part where you hate me."

* * *

They used the crew room, because it was the only room in the operation with chairs enough and a table that wasn't covered in parts. Wren appeared with a pot of coffee and four mugs and set them down without a word, and Odile caught the look that passed between Wren and Cass — brief, assessing, two people who'd heard of each other for years and never shared a room. Wren poured, distributed, and then leaned against the doorframe with arms crossed, radiating the specific silence of someone who intended to hear every word and be believed on none of it.

One of the lawyers opened a leather folder. The other laid out documents in a neat fan, like a card trick nobody wanted to see.

"The asset group I represent," Cass began, "acquired the Dunmore paper last month. All of it. The mortgages, the equipment notes, the fuel supply agreement. In total—" she named a number, and it was bigger than Odile had guessed, big enough that her father must have been robbing Peter to pay Paul for most of a decade, flying cargo routes she'd never known about to service debts she'd never seen. Big enough that the whole airstrip — the hangar, the pumps, the gravel, the tower with its cracked window Wren kept promising to fix — sat underneath it like a foundation.

"The strip is collateral," Odile said. Not a question.

"The strip is collateral," Cass confirmed. "Along with the aircraft registered to the company. Including yours."

Odile looked at her own hands around the coffee mug. Steady. She made them stay steady.

"So this is a foreclosure with extra steps."

"This is an offer." Cass slid a single page across the table. One page, which somehow made it worse than the lawyer's fan of documents. "One contract. One season. Rivergate winter resupply — provisions, medical stores, fuel for their generators, mail. Four round trips minimum before the passes close, more if the window holds. You fly it, the debt restructures, the strip stays yours, and I walk out of here having ruined neither of our lives."

The word landed in the middle of the table and sat there between them, heavy and radioactive.

Rivergate.

Odile had not said the name aloud in nine years. She'd flown around it, routed around it, refueled an hour out of her way rather than overfly the valley, and everyone in Escarpment had understood without ever discussing it, the way small towns understand the shape of a wound without poking it. Her father had sworn off the route with an oath Odile had witnessed — his hand flat on the hood of the plane, his voice cracked down the middle, *never again, not me, not mine* — and she had inherited that oath along with the ledger and the debt and the cracked tower window, and she had kept it the way you keep a grave.

"No," she said.

"Odile."

"No." She pushed the page back across the table with one finger. "Find another pilot. There are charter outfits in Kettle Falls."

"There aren't, actually. Not ones who'll fly Rivergate in monsoon season at rates anyone can afford, and not ones Rivergate will accept." Cass's voice stayed level, but Odile heard the effort in it now, the strain of a woman holding a line she didn't fully believe in. "The town council specifically requested Dunmore Aviation. Your father flew them supplies for eleven years before—" Again the stop. Again the cliff edge. "Before. They trust the name. And the Halvard Group, which holds the paper, has agreed to restructure only against this specific performance. It's not negotiable. Believe me, I tried."

"Halvard." Odile had never heard the name. It sounded like money from somewhere else, money with no face. "Who are they?"

"A buyer of distressed assets. Which is what you are now, Odile. Distressed." Cass spread her hands. "I know how this looks."

"Do you." Odile stood up. The chair scraped. Somewhere outside, Wren's apprentice was running up an engine, and the sound filled the pause like a held breath. "Because it looks like you flew nine hundred miles to stand in my crew room and tell me that either I fly the pass that killed my brother, or I lose the only thing my father left me. And you're smiling while you say it."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're always smiling. It's what you do instead of telling the truth."

For a moment — one raw, unguarded moment — the smile dropped entirely, and what was underneath it looked almost like fear. Then it was gone, shuttered, and Cass was the account manager again, the woman in the coat that cost more than Odile's annual fuel budget.

"Fly it or don't," Cass said quietly. "But understand what happens if you don't. The Halvard Group forecloses Monday. They sell the strip to a logging outfit out of the provincial capital that wants the gravel rights. Every pilot and mechanic in this building is unemployed by Christmas, and Rivergate goes into winter with no resupply and a road that's already snowed shut. That's not me threatening you, Odile. That's just the arithmetic. I checked it twice."

"And if I fly it? What's your cut, Cass? What did they pay you to carry this paper up the mountain and put it in front of me?"

Cass held her gaze for a long moment. Outside, the engine run-up wound down into silence, and in the silence Odile heard the tick of the cooling coffee pot and the wind working at the loose flashing over the hangar door, the small domestic sounds of a place that was about to stop being hers.

"I'll be at the airstrip office until five," Cass said. "The lawyers fly out tonight either way. You don't have to decide in front of me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got today." Cass gathered her coat off the chair back, and paused, and for a second her voice lost its polish entirely, dropped down to something older and rougher, something from the years when they'd shared a crew room not unlike this one and split a bottle of terrible wine after hard flights. "Theo was the better pilot of the two of us, you know. I always knew it. I think maybe that's why I couldn't stand him."

And then she was gone, out the door and across the apron, the lawyers trailing her like gulls after a boat, and Odile stood alone in the crew room with four mugs of coffee going cold and the contract lying on the table like something that had fallen out of the sky.

* * *

She found the ledger open on her father's desk that evening, though she didn't remember opening it.

That was a lie, and she knew it. She'd gone looking for it. She'd climbed the stairs to the rooms above the office where her father had lived alone for nine years among his maps and his radio manuals and his dust, and she'd taken the ledger down from the shelf where it had sat since the funeral, and she'd opened it to the back pages where the handwriting got shakier and the entries got shorter, and there it was again, the same line she'd found the week after he died:

**RIVERGATE — NO.**

Struck through. Struck through so hard the pen had torn the paper, and beneath the strike, in smaller letters, pressed deep enough to read from the back of the page, her father had written a date and a single word: *sworn.*

Below it, in different ink, added later, the entry that had kept her up nights for eight months: a payment received. A large one. From a client listed only by initials — **H.G.** — dated three weeks before he died. And beside it, in her father's cramped hand, a note that might have been a flight plan or a confession or an apology, except that the last line had been blotted beyond recovery, one word drowned in what was either spilled coffee or something else.

Odile sat in her father's chair in her father's darkening office with the ledger open on her knees, and downstairs she could hear Wren locking up the hangar, moving through the evening routine, pausing — she would bet anything — at the bottom of the stairs, deciding whether to come up.

Outside, the escarpment caught the last of the light and held it, mile after mile of gray stone going gold and then violet, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful, indifferent the way they always are. Somewhere up there was the pass. Somewhere up there was the corridor where Theo's transponder signal had walked off the edge of the world nine years ago, and somewhere down in the valley beyond it was a town full of people who had trusted her father's name, waiting on supplies that were never coming unless she flew them in herself.

The contract lay on the desk beside the lamp. She hadn't signed it. She hadn't thrown it out either.

Her hand was on the pen before she'd decided anything at all.
