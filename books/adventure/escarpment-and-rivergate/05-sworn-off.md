## 5. Sworn Off

The rain came in the night, and by morning Escarpment smelled of wet gravel and creosote, the whole town rinsed and hung out to dry like laundry on a line. Odile stood at the open door of her father's old office, a mug of coffee going cold in her hand, and looked at the ledger lying on the desk where she'd left it three days ago. She had not opened it since. She had not needed to. She knew what was written there, or rather, she knew what was not written—the Rivergate route, blacked out with a bar of ink so heavy it had bled through three pages, as if her father had pressed down hard enough to hurt the paper.

She'd been avoiding that desk for nine years. Now Cass Merrow's contract sat on top of it like a stone on a grave marker, and avoidance was no longer among her options.

Outside, Wren was under the Cessna with a trouble light, and the sound of a wrench ringing against the tarmac carried across the strip the way it always did—steady, unhurried, a heartbeat for the whole airfield. Odile listened to it for a long moment. Then she set down the coffee, sat down in her father's chair—it still creaked at the same joint, still smelled faintly of pipe smoke though he'd been dead six years—and opened the ledger to the page before the blackout.

* * *

Nine years ago, in the spring of the disaster, Odile had been twenty-four years old and certain of almost everything.

She remembered the morning of it with a clarity that felt less like memory than like standing in the room again. The escarpment had been clear that day—clear the way mountains are clear before they turn on you, the light so clean off the snowfields that the rock faces showed every seam and scar, every fold of the old thrust faults her father had spent his surveying career mapping. Theo had come out to the strip before dawn to fuel his own plane because he didn't trust anyone else to do it right, and he'd leaned in her cockpit window while she ran up the medevac Twin Otter for a routine run south, and he'd said, "Tell Kettle Falls I want my wrench back," and she'd said, "Buy your own wrench," and that was the last full sentence they ever exchanged.

He was twenty-eight. He flew the supply run north twice a week, the Rivergate route, the one their father had surveyed and Theo had grown up hearing about at dinner like other children heard about sports teams. Their father, Aldous Dunmore, had walked that escarpment on foot for two years with a theodolite and a mule, and he talked about the passes the way some men talk about a woman who left them—with reverence and grievance in equal measure. He'd found the corridor himself, the low one, the one no airline would certify because the margins were too thin: eleven hundred feet of clearance in the notch below the hanging glacier, a dogleg around the tower called the Old Man, wind that funneled through the gap at forty knots when the pressure gradient was wrong. *Flyable*, Aldous had always insisted, *if you respect it.* But the company that held the mail and freight contracts wouldn't touch it, and so the official route went over the high pass instead, the broad saddle to the east where the air was thinner but the geometry was honest.

Theo flew the high pass. Everyone flew the high pass.

Odile was over the southern lakes when the mountain came down.

She never heard it—of course she never heard it; sound doesn't travel like that—but she saw the plume, later, from sixty miles, a gray-white column standing off the escarpment like smoke from a house fire, except it kept rising and rising and did not thin. She turned back without being told to. She remembered pushing the throttles up until the engines sang and telling herself it was nothing, a rockfall, a cornice collapse, the escarpment shed rock all spring—and all the while some animal part of her brain already knew, already reading the size of that column and doing the arithmetic of tons.

The high pass was gone. Not blocked—*gone*. The whole saddle where the route crossed, four hundred vertical feet of mountainside, had let go and slid into the valley on the far side, and the debris run-out had buried the river flats below, and the river itself had dammed and then burst, and by the time Odile got there, circling in the settling dust with her hands shaking on the yoke, the town of Rivergate was under a wall of water and mud, and the airstrip at its edge was a brown smear, and somewhere under all of it—under the slide, or under the flood, they never did recover enough to say which—was her brother's plane, and her brother.

They never found the aircraft. That was the thing nobody outside the family understood. There was no wreckage to bury, no twisted spar to hang grief on. There was a hole in a mountain and a name on a memorial that hadn't been built yet, and a father who stood at the edge of the debris field for three days without speaking, and then came home and took the ledger off the shelf and drew a single black line through the words RIVERGATE — ESCARPMENT PASS, and said, in a voice Odile had never heard him use before or after:

"Nobody flies it. Nobody. Not while I'm alive, and not after."

And Odile, twenty-four years old and hollowed out, had said yes. She'd have said yes to anything. It was easier than saying the true thing, which was that she wanted the whole mountain burned down to its roots.

* * *

In the present, the rain ticked against the office window, and Odile realized she had been staring at the same page for ten minutes.

The ledger was more than an account book. Her father had kept everything in it—fuel receipts, crew wages, the strip's mortgage payments—but interleaved with the money were the flight notes, the weather observations, decades of his small precise handwriting recording wind speeds at the notch and snowpack depths on the shoulders. He had been, she understood now in a way she hadn't at twenty-four, still studying the pass even after swearing off it. Still writing down what the mountain did. A man does not take notes on a thing he has truly renounced.

She turned pages backward, away from the blackout, into older entries, and stopped at one dated the spring before the disaster. The handwriting was different—not her father's careful drafting hand but Theo's looser slant, the letters leaning forward like a man walking fast. Her brother had kept his own notes inside the family ledger, tucked between the fuel accounts where no one would look.

*Wind at the notch 35 kts SW, gusting 50 in the afternoon thermals. Flew the dogleg twice. The Old Man throws rotor off the east face you don't see until you're in it. Dad's numbers hold if you stay below 9,200. Going again Thursday.*

Going again Thursday. Odile closed her eyes. Theo hadn't just flown the high pass. In those last months he'd been flying their father's forbidden corridor, the low route, the impossible one—practicing it, refining it, writing down its moods. And Aldous Dunmore, who read every entry in this ledger, must have known. Must have drawn the black line partly to stop his son from doing exactly what he was already doing.

Her chest did something complicated and painful. All these years she'd built her grief on a simple foundation: Theo died on the high pass, doing the safe thing, and the mountain killed him anyway, and that was the whole story, senseless and complete. But the ledger was quietly suggesting the story had another floor to it—that her brother had been chasing something, proving something, and she had no idea what.

She was still holding the book open when someone knocked on the doorframe.

Wren stood there with grease to the elbows and rain beaded in her close-cropped hair, and Odile watched her take in the scene—the ledger, the blackout page visible even from the doorway, Odile's face—and do the math without asking any of the questions.

"Storm cell's building west of Kettle Falls," Wren said, which was not what either of them was thinking about. "Cass wants a weather brief at noon. Thought you'd want the heads-up." A pause. "You okay?"

"I'm reading my dead father's homework," Odile said.

Wren came into the office—she rarely did; the office was Odile's territory the way the hangar was hers—and looked down at the ledger. Her eyes caught on Theo's slanted handwriting, and stayed there.

"That's not your dad's hand," she said.

"No."

"Whose is it?"

Odile turned the book toward her. "My brother's."

Something moved behind Wren's expression—she'd grown up in these parts too, everyone had lost someone to Rivergate or knew someone who had—and she reached out and very lightly touched the edge of the page, the way you'd touch a wound to check if it was still tender.

"He was flying the low route," Wren said. Not a question. Her father had been a bush pilot too; the corridor was local legend, the route no one flies. "Before the slide."

"So it says."

"And your old man blacked it out after."

"After," Odile agreed, and heard how thin her own voice had gone.

Wren straightened up and wiped her hands on a rag that only redistributed the grease, and for a moment she seemed to be deciding something. What she finally said was: "When you're ready to talk about it, I make decent coffee. Better than whatever that is." She nodded at the cold mug. "And when you're not ready, I'll be under the Cessna."

Then she left, boots ringing on the boards, and Odile sat alone with the ledger and the rain and the sound of a wrench resuming its steady heartbeat across the strip.

* * *

That evening she walked out past the fuel farm to the bench her father had built facing the escarpment, because grief has its appointed stations and this was hers. The clouds had torn open in the west and the last light came through them in long slanting bars, lighting up the range the way a spotlight finds a defendant. From here you could see the high pass—or the absence where it had been; nine years of erosion had softened the scar, but the mountain still wore it, the way a face wears a burn.

"You swore us off it," she said aloud, to a man six years dead. "And he was flying it anyway. Was that your fault or his? Was it mine?"

The mountain declined to answer. Mountains are good at that.

But sitting there, cold in the failing light, Odile made herself assemble the facts like cargo she'd have to weigh and balance: Cass Merrow owned their debt now. The contract named Rivergate. The route in ran over the high pass—what remained of it, the rebuilt saddle crossing the mining company had engineered after the slide, wide and graded and, everyone said, perfectly safe. And beneath that safe route, hidden in a family ledger, was evidence that her brother had spent his final weeks mastering a different way through—a way her father had loved and feared and finally forbidden, a way that had died with him.

Nine years she'd kept her past sealed behind the cockpit door. Nine years of competent loneliness, of being excellent at her job and invisible in her life, and she had told herself it was discipline, it was survival, it was the inheritance her father left her: *don't look back, don't fly north, don't feel it.*

The ledger suggested a different inheritance. The ledger suggested her father had never stopped looking. Neither, apparently, had Theo.

Tomorrow the crew voted on the contract. Tomorrow she would have to stand in front of people who trusted her and decide whether to fly toward the exact coordinates of the worst day of her life—and now, thanks to a dead man's slanted handwriting, she couldn't even tell herself she knew why it had happened.

The light went off the escarpment. Somewhere below the ridge, in the darkening valley, the river that had once drowned Rivergate ran on toward the coast, keeping what it kept.

Odile pulled her jacket tighter and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any downdraft ever had:

*Theo wasn't running from anything. He was going somewhere.*

And she was suddenly, desperately afraid that if she opened that flight log—if she followed her brother's last Thursday all the way in—she would find out that the crash was no accident at all.
