## 6. The Crew Decides

The call went out the way all serious calls at Escarpment went out: a scrap of paper thumbtacked to the board outside the mess hall, in Wren's blocky handwriting, because Wren was the only one whose printing everyone could read without squinting.

**CREW MEETING. TONIGHT. 1900. EVERYONE. YES, EVEN YOU, PETROS.**

By seven o'clock the mess hall was full past its capacity, which had never been designed for anything but eating and arguing on a small scale. The woodstove ticked. Someone had made coffee strong enough to strip paint, and it sat in dented urns along the counter like ammunition. Outside, the fog had come down off the escarpment again and pressed against the windows, so that the whole building seemed to float in white, cut loose from the world, a roomful of people deciding their own weather.

Odile took a place against the back wall, where she could see every face and no face could settle on hers for long. It was a skill she'd refined over nine years—choosing sightlines the way other pilots chose altitudes. From here she could see old Petros with his hearing aid turned up too loud, whistling faintly like a radio between stations. She could see the Okonkwo brothers, who flew cargo and finished each other's sentences. She could see young Ilsa Brandt, twenty-three and hungry, who'd washed out of flight school on the coast and come north to learn the real thing. She could see Wren at the front, arms crossed, watching the door.

And she could see Cass Merrow, seated at the head table like she owned it already, which by the letter of the law—by the ledger, by the debt—she very nearly did.

* * *

Wren had spent the afternoon not working, which for Wren was harder than any work.

There was always something to do at the strip. The Cessna's left magneto needed attention. The hangar's south door had been sticking since the thaw. There were oil samples to send off and a tire with a slow leak that had developed opinions. But tonight none of it would sit still in her hands. Every time she picked up a wrench her mind slid sideways to the meeting, to the contract lying on the table in Cass Merrow's briefcase like something dead that hadn't finished smelling yet.

So instead she walked the line.

She did it without deciding to—the way she did most of the truest things she did. Down the row of aircraft under the last grey light, checking tie-downs, running her palm along cowling edges still warm from the day. The Otter. The two Cessnas. The old Beech that Petros swore would fly again if anyone ever gave him six uninterrupted months and a budget, neither of which had ever materialized. This was hers, all of it—not on any paper, nothing so clean as ownership, but in the way a body belongs to the work it does. Nine years she'd kept these machines alive. Nine years of cold mornings and burned fingers and Odile Dunmore appearing at her elbow at ungodly hours saying *something doesn't sound right*, and being right, and standing close enough that Wren could smell avgas and cedar smoke in her hair while they diagnosed it together.

She knew what the vote would be before anyone cast it. That was the thing nobody wanted to say out loud. Eleven people worked this strip, counting part-timers. Eleven paychecks came out of an operation that was already bleeding. You could ask them to choose principle over payroll, but you'd be asking them to feed their kids on principle, heat their houses with it, ship their parts in on it. Economics didn't care about oaths. Economics cared about whether the fuel bill got paid.

Still. When she thought about that contract—about the word **RIVERGATE** typed across its header, plain as a headstone—her hands closed into fists without consulting her.

She'd read enough of the maintenance logs to know what route that was. She'd found the gap in them herself, months ago: three years of entries ending mid-sentence, pages razored out clean, the binding scarred where the knife had slipped once. She'd never asked Odile about it. Some questions you don't ask a person; you wait for them to become answers on their own schedule, or you accept they won't.

Tonight, though, the question was going to be asked by someone else, in front of everyone, and there would be no waiting.

* * *

Cass let them talk first. It was a technique she'd learned from her father, who'd learned it from thirty years of foreclosing on people: let them exhaust themselves on the reasons it can't happen, then show them the arithmetic. Grief burns hot and fast. Arithmetic burns slow and long, and arithmetic is what's still burning in the morning.

So she sat through forty minutes of it. Petros declaring that no plane he touched would fly that route, not over his dead body, though everyone noted his dead body would presumably not be aboard. Ilsa Brandt asking practical questions in a voice that trembled only slightly—what were the terms, what was the tonnage, what was the pay—which Cass answered precisely, because precision was a kind of mercy. The Okonkwos arguing in low rapid Yoruba-tinged English, Emeka for taking it, Chidi against, the same argument they'd apparently been having since noon. The ground crew, silent, because ground crew always understood first that pilots' principles landed on their shoulders too.

Then the hall quieted, and Cass opened the briefcase, and laid the contract flat.

"Here's the shape of it," she said. "Rivergate winter resupply. Medicine, dry goods, parts, mail. Four runs minimum before the passes close, more if the window holds. Halvard Group pays triple the standard charter rate, plus a completion bonus that clears the strip's debt in full." She let that land. She watched it land, in the shoulders of the men who had mortgages in town, in the face of Ilsa Brandt who had exactly four hundred dollars to her name and a license she'd kill to use. "You refuse, and I'm legally obligated to liquidate the collateral. Which is the strip. All of it."

"You could refuse anyway," said Chidi Okonkwo quietly. "You could eat the loss. Everyone knows you didn't buy that debt to make money on it."

The silence that followed had texture. Cass felt it settle on her skin like the fog outside.

"Everyone thinks they know why I do things," she said. "It's the great luxury of being me. Let me tell you what I actually know." She stood. "I know that eleven people work here. I know that half of Escarpment's economy moves through this strip—mail, medevac, freight, the school supplies that come in twice a year. I know that if I fold this operation, the town doesn't lose a business. It loses an artery." She looked around the room, deliberately, letting her gaze touch each of them. "I am not going to stand here and pretend I don't want something from this. I do. I want the strip. I've wanted it since I was nineteen years old and your chief pilot out-flew me in a crosswind with half her flaps failed, and I went home and cried about it, and I have never entirely forgiven her or myself. But I am telling you—" and here her voice did something none of them had heard it do, dropped its armor halfway, "—I am telling you I did not buy this debt to destroy you. Whatever else is true, that isn't."

"Then say the rest," said Petros. "Say the name of the route."

"I just did."

"No." The old man rose slowly, one hand on the table, hearing aid whistling. "You said Rivergate. Say the whole of it. Say *the pass*. Say the name of the boy who died on it, in front of the woman who hasn't spoken it in nine years, and then ask us to vote."

Every head in the room turned toward the back wall.

Odile had known this moment was coming since the contract hit the table that morning. She had rehearsed it in the air, in the shower, in the grey space before sleep—rehearsed being calm, being distant, being the professional she'd built herself into, rivet by rivet, over nine years. What she had not rehearsed was the sound of Theo's name in this room, in this voice, and the way the whole hall seemed to lean toward her like grass toward wind.

"Theo Dunmore," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of that. Almost. "My brother. He died on the escarpment pass nine years ago this October. My father swore off the route the week after, and I signed that oath alongside him, and he's dead now, and I'm the one still holding the pen." She pushed off the wall. "That's the whole of it. That's everything I have to say."

"It isn't," said Cass softly. "But it'll do for now. Vote's open. Abstentions count."

* * *

They voted by raising hands, because Escarpment did things plainly when it did them at all.

For the contract: eight hands. Emeka Okonkwo's first, then Ilsa's, then the ground crew almost as one body, then, after a long moment in which his brother stared at him with something like grief, Chidi's. Against: two—Petros and a ramp hand named Suki who'd lost family in the valley towns and wouldn't say more than that. One abstention.

Eleven workers. Eight plus two plus one. The math was merciless and public and everyone in the room did it at the same instant, and every eye went again to the back wall, to the woman who had raised no hand at all.

"I need to check the Otter's boots," Odile said, which fooled no one, and left.

The door swung shut behind her and the fog took her, and inside the hall the silence stretched until Wren broke it.

"She abstained," Ilsa said, wonder and judgment mixed in her young voice, "on her own brother's—"

"You don't know what it cost her," Wren said, sharper than she meant to, sharp enough that the whole table looked at her, and she looked back, daring them, hands flat on the wood. "Any of you. Not one of you knows. So think hard before you spend your judgment, because it's cheap to you and it wasn't free to her." She grabbed her jacket off the bench. "Meeting's adjourned. Contract's accepted. Somebody tell her, because I'm done being the messenger."

* * *

Odile walked the perimeter road in the fog until the strip lights behind her were smudges and the escarpment ahead was a rumor of black against black.

Abstention. She turned the word over like a stone with something dead underneath. Nine years she'd told herself the silence was discipline—that a pilot who felt everything and showed nothing was the safest pilot, that her father's oath had been a kind of armor and she'd simply put it on early. And now she'd stood in a warm room full of people who knew her, who ate her terrible coffee and borrowed her tools and trusted her with their lives at fifteen hundred feet, and when the moment came to be one of them—to raise a hand, either hand, to be *counted*—she had chosen the one position that required nothing of her except absence.

Petros would fly the route if the vote said so, and hate every minute, and she'd let him. Ilsa would fly it green and scared, and she'd let her. Wren—

Wren had defended her, she'd heard that much through the door before the fog swallowed it. That was somehow worse. Being defended by the person you most wanted to deserve.

The mountain loomed somewhere ahead of her, invisible, patient. Somewhere up there was the pass. Somewhere up there was the exact coordinates where a nineteen-year-old boy's transponder had blinked out between one radar sweep and the next, and she had spent nine years flying everywhere on earth except toward it, calling the avoidance professionalism, calling the silence strength.

Eight hands had gone up tonight. Two had gone up against. Hers had stayed in her lap like something broken she didn't know how to hold.

She stood in the fog a long time, until she was soaked through and shivering, and then she turned back toward the lights—not toward the mess hall, but toward the hangar, where a single lamp still burned and a familiar silhouette moved among the shadows of the planes, doing at midnight what Wren always did when the world went wrong, which was fix something, anything, with her hands.

Odile started toward the light. Then stopped. Because pinned to the hangar's outer door, fluttering in the damp, was a fresh sheet of paper in blocky capitals—and even at fifty feet, even in the fog, she could read it:

**ODILE — HANGAR. NOW. WE NEED TO TALK ABOUT WHAT YOU'RE NOT SAYING. — W**
