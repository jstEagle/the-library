## 7. Pre-Flight

Wren Adeyemi had a theory about machines and lies, and it was this: an engine could not lie to you. A person could smile at you across the mess hall table and mean nothing by it, could say *sure, Wren, no problem* while their eyes went flat as standing water, but a connecting rod bent under load told you exactly what it thought of you. Metal was honest. Metal kept records. Every stress fracture was a confession written in a language she'd spent fifteen years learning to read, and she trusted it the way other people trusted scripture.

Which was why, at four in the morning, alone in the hangar with the smell of solvent and cold iron, Wren stood very still in front of the *Kestrel* and felt her stomach drop through the concrete floor.

She had been at it since midnight. Odile's note—her own note, pinned to the door in blocky capitals because she'd been too angry and too tired to write anything gentler—had gone unanswered. Odile hadn't come. Wren had watched from the hangar's open mouth as that soaked silhouette stopped fifty feet out, read the paper, and turned away. Not toward the hangar. Away. And Wren had gone back inside and picked up a wrench, because that was what she did when the world went wrong, and the world had been going wrong for three days straight: Cass Merrow's lawyers, the contract nobody wanted, the vote that had split the crew down the middle like a bad weld, and Odile abstaining, Odile who never abstained, who always knew exactly where she stood on everything except, apparently, the one thing that mattered.

So Wren worked. She worked through the fuel system first, draining sumps, checking for water in the tanks, because if someone wanted the Rivergate run to fail, contaminated fuel was the elegant way to do it. Clean. She checked the magnetos, the spark plugs, gapped them all fresh. Clean. She pulled the cowling panels off the number two engine and ran her hands along the oil lines looking for chafe, for a nick, for anything. Clean, clean, clean. The *Kestrel* was old—older than Odile, older than the airstrip's paved runway—but Dunmore senior had maintained her like a religion, and Wren had kept the faith after him. The plane was sound. The plane was *sound.*

Then she got to the hydraulics.

The reservoir on the main system should have read full. It read a third. Now, a system could lose fluid—seepage at the packings, a weeping seal, normal attrition over weeks. But Wren had serviced that reservoir eleven days ago. She remembered it specifically because she'd done it the same afternoon the debt news broke, working with her hands to keep from saying something unforgivable about Cass Merrow in front of the younger pilots. Eleven days. Even with a slow leak, even with the worst packing in the fleet, she should have been down maybe ten percent.

A third. Two-thirds of the fluid gone in eleven days, and no puddle under the aircraft, no wet streaks down the gear doors, no telltale film on the belly where fluid would catch the light and run backward in the slipstream. Wren crouched under the nose with a trouble light and looked for a long time.

There. Behind the reservoir itself, tucked up against the firewall where only a hand on its back would ever reach: a fitting backed off half a turn. Not loose enough to spray. Loose enough to weep—and here was the beautiful, filthy part—a weep that would accelerate with heat and pressure. On the ground, taxiing, everything fine. Get the system hot at altitude, working hard through a pass with downdrafts trying to fold your wings, and the bleed would open up. By the time you needed full pressure to haul out of a sinker, you'd have a pedal going soft under your boot, and soft pedals in the escarpment passes meant—

Wren sat back on her heels on the cold concrete and let the trouble light hang from its hook, swaying, throwing her shadow huge and wrong across the fuselage.

Somebody had done this. Somebody with access, somebody with knowledge—half a turn was not vandalism, vandalism was a slashed tire or sugar in the tank. Half a turn behind the firewall was craft. It was the work of someone who understood hydraulic systems well enough to make failure look like wear.

She made herself finish the inspection. That was the discipline her father had taught her before he taught her anything else: *when you find one snake, check the whole grass.* So she went over the rest of the aircraft the way a surgeon goes over a body, and forty minutes later, on the port main gear, she found the second snake.

The tie-rod end. She'd have missed it if she hadn't been lying flat on a creeper with the light jammed into the wheel well, looking up at the drag link from below—the angle you almost never used, because nobody inspected from underneath unless they were chasing a specific complaint. But there it was, catching the light wrong. A file mark. Fine, parallel scratches running across the machined shoulder of the rod where the metal should have been smooth as lake ice, a groove cut deliberately, patiently, into the steel until what remained was maybe sixty percent of what it should be. Sixty percent would hold. Sixty percent would hold through taxi, through takeoff, through a dozen unremarkable landings. And then one day—preferably, for whoever held the file, on a rough strip, under heavy load, with a valley waiting below—you'd plant that gear and sixty percent of a tie-rod would become zero percent, and the gear would fold, and the prop would eat the ground, and physics would handle the rest.

Wren lay there under the airplane for a long time, listening to the hangar tick and settle around her, the night wind worrying the big door, and she thought about who.

That was the poison of it. That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over. Because the list of people with hangar access at three in the morning was short, and every name on it was a name she ate breakfast with. The night watchman, old Piet, who'd known her since she was seventeen and greasing wheel bearings for pocket money. The junior mechanics, both of them good kids, both of them terrified of losing their jobs to Merrow's restructuring. The pilots. Cass Merrow herself, who had keys to everything now, who owned the debt that owned them all.

And Odile.

No. Wren shut that door immediately, slammed it, threw the bolt. Odile flew that route's shadow in her sleep; Odile had voted against the contract with her whole silent body; Odile had more reason than anyone to want the flight to succeed, if only so it would be over. Whatever was broken between them right now—and things were broken, Wren knew, had known since the vote, since Odile stood there with her hand down and her face like a woman watching her own house burn—it wasn't this. It couldn't be this.

But that was exactly the trap, wasn't it. Love was a blind spot you built yourself, brick by brick, and called it trust.

Wren slid out from under the gear, stood, wiped her hands on a rag that was already black, and looked at the filed tie-rod for a long moment. Then she did the thing she was already sure she'd regret, and also the only thing she could live with.

She said nothing.

Not forever. Just—not yet. Because think it through, Adeyemi. You raise the alarm, and what happens? You get a meeting. Piet, the juniors, the pilots, maybe Merrow's people. Everyone stands around the tie-rod looking grave, and everyone looks at everyone else, and the person who did it watches Wren Adeyemi watch them, and learns exactly how much she knows and how she knows it. And meanwhile the sabotage stops—or worse, gets subtler—and the next failure happens somewhere she hasn't looked yet, at altitude, with Odile's hands on the yoke. No. If someone wanted this flight to fail badly enough to file steel, then the smart play—the only play—was to let them believe they'd succeeded. Fix everything. Say nothing. Watch.

So she fixed it. She torqued the fitting back down and flushed the hydraulic system end to end, refilled the reservoir with fresh fluid, and bled it until the pedals were rock. She pulled the compromised tie-rod and replaced it with a new one from stores, machining the castle nut's cotter pin hole herself because the spare's was drilled a hair off-center and she was not, tonight, willing to accept a hair. She kept the filed rod. Wrapped it in a shop towel, wrote the date and the words *PORT MAIN — DO NOT SCRAP* on the towel in grease pencil, and locked it in the bottom drawer of her toolbox, under the torque wrenches, where nobody ever went without asking.

By the time she finished, the windows above the hangar doors had gone from black to the color of dishwater, and the fog was lifting off the field in rags. Somewhere a crow was making its opinions known. Wren washed up at the utility sink, scrubbing the hydraulic fluid out from under her nails, and caught her own reflection in the dark glass—hollow-eyed, jaw set, a woman who looked like she'd been arguing with someone all night and lost.

She went and stood next to the *Kestrel*, one hand flat on the cold aluminum of her flank, the way you'd steady a horse.

"Nobody touches you," she told the airplane quietly. "You hear me? Nobody."

The airplane said nothing, being honest.

* * *

At seven, the crew drifted in for the pre-flight briefing, and Wren performed the longest act of her life.

She gave the standard report—engines borescoped, hydraulics serviced, control surfaces rigged and checked, *she'll fly better than she has in years*—and she watched faces while she said it. Piet, nodding along, half-asleep, harmless. Dev and Marcus, the juniors, trading a look when she mentioned the hydraulics—curiosity, not guilt, she thought, though she thought it with her heart going hard. The pilots: Ruiz, chewing coffee, already bored. Cass Merrow, immaculate even at seven a.m., listening with that lawyerly stillness of hers, giving away nothing, giving away everything, depending on how paranoid you were. Wren was learning that paranoia was just attention with the volume turned up, and she intended to keep the volume up until this contract was flown or dead.

And Odile.

Odile came in last, hair still damp from the fog, and took the chair farthest from Wren, which was itself a message, and wouldn't look at her, which was another one. There were shadows under her eyes like bruises. When Wren ran through the maintenance summary, Odile listened the way she did everything—completely, totally, as if the words might change if she blinked—and when Wren said *hydraulics serviced and bled,* something flickered across her face. Not suspicion. Recognition, maybe. Or just the particular exhaustion of a woman who'd spent the night deciding whether to come to the hangar and hadn't.

Afterward, as the room emptied, Odile paused by the door. Didn't turn all the way around. Said, to the doorframe rather than to Wren:

"You were up all night."

"Somebody has to be." Wren kept her voice level, kept her hands busy stacking charts that didn't need stacking. "First leg's Thursday. Kettle Falls, then north. I want the *Kestrel* signed off by Wednesday night, and I want a full walkaround with whoever's flying second leg."

Odile's jaw moved. "Wren—"

"Don't." Quiet. Not sharp, just final, the way you close a cowl panel. "Whatever it is you're not saying, you've decided I don't get to hear it yet. Fine. That's your call. But you're going to be sitting in my airplane over that pass, and my job is the airplane. So let me do my job."

For a second—one second—something in Odile's face cracked open, and Wren saw all the way down into it: fear, and grief, and something that looked unbearably like *I'm sorry,* and then it closed again, smooth as a hung door, and Odile nodded once and walked out into the gray morning.

Wren stood alone in the briefing room with the stacked charts and her own heartbeat.

* * *

Wednesday night she did the final sign-off herself, alone, because she didn't trust anyone else's signature anymore and didn't pretend to. Fuel samples clear. Borescope clean. Hydraulics holding pressure overnight—she'd taped a witness mark across the reservoir cap, a strip of white tape with a line drawn on it, so that any loss of fluid would show as a broken line. Control locks out. Tie-rod new, torqued, safetied, logged.

She was closing the toolbox drawer—past the wrapped, filed rod sleeping at the bottom like evidence in a trial that hadn't been scheduled yet—when she heard footsteps in the hangar doorway.

Not Odile's. She knew Odile's step the way she knew the idle of a healthy engine, and this wasn't it. Lighter. Quicker. She turned.

Cass Merrow stood at the mouth of the hangar in the sodium light, coat collar up, hands in her pockets, looking at the *Kestrel* the way a gambler looks at a table where the stakes have gotten away from her.

"Late night," Cass said.

"They usually are, this week." Wren didn't move from the toolbox. "Something I can do for you?"

"A question." Cass came in a few steps, boots ringing on the concrete, and stopped, and for the first time since she'd landed with her lawyers and her contract, she looked—Wren searched for the word and didn't like any of them—*uncertain.* "Your inspection. The hydraulics. Before you serviced them—how low was the reservoir?"

The hangar went very quiet. Out on the field, the wind sock hung dead straight out, pointing north, toward the passes, toward Rivergate, toward whatever was waiting up there with a file and a half-turn wrench and all the time in the world.
