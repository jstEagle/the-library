## 8. First Leg North

The loading began before dawn, under work lights strung along the Escarpment hangar like a string of tired yellow beads. Odile walked the line of crates with a clipboard she barely needed—she had counted the manifest three times the night before, lying awake in her room above the office, listening to the wind test the eaves. Rice and flour in sealed drums. Diesel for Rivergate's generator, forty gallons to a drum, strapped two by two. A crate of veterinary antibiotics that smelled faintly of hay even through the plywood. And at the back, lashed down last and most carefully, a pallet of medical stores: penicillin in glass vials packed in straw, sutures, four cases of plasma expanders, a portable nebulizer Wren had rebuilt from parts.

Two hundred and sixty pounds over gross, if you believed the manifest. Odile did not believe the manifest. She believed the scale Wren had rigged out of a hydraulic jack and a bathroom gauge, and the scale said they'd be fine with an hour of fuel burned off, so she planned to climb light and cruise high, where the air was thin but honest.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said, appearing at her elbow with a torque wrench and a look of fond accusation.

"What thing."

"The thing where you touch every strap twice and apologize to the cargo." Wren patted the fuselage of the old De Havilland—the *Kestrel*, her father's name for her, painted on the nose in letters gone pale as bone. "She's ready, Odile. I checked her twice myself. Third time's superstition."

"Superstition keeps planes up."

"Ball bearings keep planes up." But Wren checked the tie-downs anyway, one hand trailing along the cargo netting, and Odile pretended not to notice that the mechanic was checking them because Odile had touched them first. That was the grammar of the two of them now, after years of it: neither said anything, and everything got said.

Cass Merrow's plane sat fifty feet down the apron, a younger machine, a Twin Otter conversion with a paint job like a bruise—deep violet fading to silver at the tail. Cass had bought it used from a mining outfit in the territories and rebuilt it herself, or nearly; everyone knew she farmed out the engine work, and everyone knew Odile knew, and neither of them had ever said a word about it in eleven years of sharing the same sky.

Cass came across the apron in flight boots and a canvas jacket, hair tied back, carrying two thermoses. She handed one to Odile without ceremony.

"Black tea," she said. "I remembered you don't take sugar."

"I take sugar."

"You stopped taking sugar the winter your father died. You've never started again." Cass said it flatly, not cruelly, the way she said everything—as though feelings were weather reports and should be delivered without comment. Then she turned and walked back to her own aircraft, leaving Odile holding the tea and hating, with a slow professional hatred, how well the woman still knew her.

They were wheels-up at first light, Cass off the numbers ninety seconds behind her, and the valley unrolled beneath them in the gray-green half-dark, the river braiding itself through the floodplain like something alive and indecisive. Odile flew the route from memory and didn't let herself think about which memory. North-northwest, up the trough between the first ridges, following the river until the river gave up on curves and went straight and cold. Kettle Falls lay six hours north at the edge of the high country, the last town with a paved strip, the place where pavement ended and the escarpment country began. Everything beyond Kettle Falls would go in by air or not at all.

She keyed the radio once an hour. Cass answered each time in four words or fewer. It was, Odile reflected, the longest civil conversation they'd had since the funeral.

* * *

The trouble showed itself at the two-hour mark, a smudge on the northern horizon that Odile watched with the particular attention pilots reserve for things that are going to get worse. By the time they'd crossed the timberline lakes it had organized itself into a proper cell—a towering anvil of cloud sitting astride the valley like a fat man in a doorway, its base green-black, hail shafts visible even at twenty miles as gray curtains that reached all the way down.

"Merrow," she keyed. "You seeing it?"

"Seeing it." A pause, the crackle of Cass thinking. "It's parked on the pass ahead. We can go around west through the col, or we can wait it out at the lake strip."

Odile studied the cell. Storm cells had moods, and this one had the sullen, self-satisfied mood of a system feeding on warm valley air with no intention of moving before afternoon. Going around west meant adding forty minutes and threading the col with a loaded airplane while the cell's outflow built the wind against the far ridge—which it would do, because outflow always piled up against terrain, and terrain always funneled it somewhere inconvenient.

But waiting meant landing at the lake strip, a gravel bar barely long enough for either plane at gross weight, and burning their schedule while the monsoon season ate the calendar day by day.

"There's a third option," Cass said, before Odile could answer.

"I'm not going over it."

"I didn't say over. I said through the notch." The notch—the low saddle east of the cell, between the storm and the granite wall of Mount Corben. It was a real feature. Odile's father had flown it in whiteout conditions twice, and both times come home looking like he'd aged a year per minute. "The outflow is blowing northeast. If we cut through the notch low, we stay out of the precip and ride the tailwind down the far side. It'll be ugly for four minutes."

"It'll be ugly for exactly as long as it takes to kill us."

"That's what ugly means, Dunmore."

Odile looked at the notch. Looked at the cell. Did the arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nineteen years old, when her father put her hands on the yoke and said, *the sky doesn't care what you feel, so stop bringing it up here.* Wind aloft out of the southwest at thirty. Cell base maybe nine thousand, tops well above anything they could use. The notch saddle at seventy-two hundred. Gap between cloud base and saddle: eighteen hundred feet, minus turbulence, minus the mountain wave spilling off Corben, minus the fact that loaded airplanes don't climb like the ones in the brochure.

Eighteen hundred feet. It was enough. It was enough the way a rope bridge is enough.

"Notch," she said. "You first. I want to watch your line."

A silence on the radio, and then Cass laughed—one short bark, genuinely surprised. "You want to *watch my line.*"

"If you're wrong, I want to be wrong somewhere else."

"Copy. Merrow's going in first. Stay a mile back and a thousand up, and keep your eyes open, Dunmore. This is going to be instructive."

* * *

It was instructive.

Cass flew the approach to the notch the way a surgeon closes—not fast, not showy, just exact. She came in high on the near side, bled speed in a shallow S-turn that used the valley instead of fighting it, and threaded the saddle with perhaps six hundred feet of margin, her wingtips rocking once in the rotor turbulence off Corben's flank and then steadying, and then she was through, small against the sunlit country beyond, and the cell's black shoulder slid past Odile's window close enough to read the texture of the hail falling inside it.

Odile followed her line almost exactly—and that was the humbling part. Not approximately. Almost exactly. Where Cass had made her S-turn, Odile made hers. Where Cass had tucked in close to the rock to steal the smooth air off the lee side, Odile tucked in the same way, close enough to see the lichen on the granite. It was like being made to fly in someone else's handwriting and discovering it was better than yours.

On the far side the outflow caught them as promised, a thirty-knot shove from behind that turned the last forty minutes into a gift, and they came into Kettle Falls under a washed blue sky with the storm raging impotently behind the ridge, and Cass touched down first and cleared the runway and said nothing on the frequency, which from Cass was applause.

* * *

Kettle Falls smelled of diesel, sawdust, and the particular sweetness of a town that lived at the end of the road and knew it. The strip ran along the river terrace, gravel and patched asphalt, with a fuel farm, a freight shed, and a hotel called the Landing that served food until late and gossip until later. Half the town seemed to turn out to watch them unload—two loaded airplanes arriving together was an event here, the way a circus was an event anywhere else.

Wren had come north in Cass's plane, riding shotgun in the right seat, ostensibly as loadmaster, actually because someone had to be there to meet them with tools, and actually-actually, though no one said it, because Wren did not like letting Odile fly toward the escarpment alone. Odile found this arrangement both infuriating and secretly, shamefully comforting, and had decided years ago never to examine that contradiction too closely.

They worked through the afternoon, transferring freight to the shed, refueling, checking tie-downs against the evening wind that came down the river valley punctual as a train. The Kettle Falls agent, a leathery woman named Sorrel who had known Odile's father, signed for the freight and kept looking at Odile with an expression she couldn't quite file.

"Your dad used to run this leg," Sorrel said finally, handing back the manifest. "Him and Theo both. Before."

Before. In the interior, *before* had only one meaning, and everyone knew it, and everyone also knew you didn't say the rest out loud unless the person it belonged to said it first. Odile took the clipboard and said, "He knew this country," and left it there, and Sorrel nodded slowly, and the conversation closed like a door on a dark room.

Cass appeared at her shoulder as Sorrel moved off. "Everyone up here has a story about your family."

"Everyone up here has a story about everybody. It's a town of two hundred people and forty years of aviation."

"No. It's more than that." Cass lowered her voice, and there was something in it Odile hadn't heard before—not mockery, not the old schoolyard edge. Something almost careful. "Dunmore. When I took this contract, I did my homework. I read everything I could find on the Rivergate route. Newspaper archives, transport board filings, the accident report. Do you know what I couldn't find?"

Odile's hands went still on the tie-down strap.

"The final investigation report. There's an initial incident filing—weather-related, aircraft lost on the escarpment crossing, pilot deceased—but the full report was never published. Nine years, and the file's still marked pending review. Files don't stay pending for nine years unless somebody wants them pending." Cass was watching her face as she said it, watching the way a pilot watches weather building on a horizon. "And then there's the contract itself. Resupply rates double the market standard, paid in advance, no inspection clause. Nobody pays that kind of money to move rice. Somebody's paying it to move *something*, or to make sure something moves, or—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening. "I've been flying contracts for fifteen years. This one stinks like a fuel spill."

"So why did you sign it?"

"Because I wanted to know who was paying." Cass held her gaze. "And because I figured whoever it was would have to deal with me eventually, and I'd rather they dealt with me than with some kid they hired off a bulletin board who'd ask no questions and die asking none."

The wind came down the valley and rattled the tie-down chains, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and Odile understood that something had shifted between them on the far side of that storm cell—some border quietly redrawn. They had spent eleven years competing for the same sky, and it had taken one shared notch and one shared suspicion to reveal that they had been reading the same weather all along.

"My father blacked out the Rivergate pages in his ledger," Odile heard herself say. "Every entry. Ink, not pencil. He wanted it unreadable."

"And you think that was grief."

"I know it was grief."

"Do you?" Cass asked softly. "Because ink isn't grief, Dunmore. Grief burns things or buries them. Ink is a decision. Somebody decided those pages should never be read again—and your father was many things, but he wasn't a man who wasted decisions."

Odile wanted to argue. Eleven years of habit rose in her throat like bile, all the old ammunition—Cass's shortcuts, Cass's ego, the way Cass had once, unforgivably, at the memorial service, said *at least he died doing what he loved* in a voice that made it sound like a verdict. But standing here in the diesel-smelling dusk with the escarpment invisible to the north and the storm muttering behind the ridge, she found the ammunition wouldn't load. Because Cass had flown that notch today like a poet, and poets don't usually lie about weather.

"We'll talk after the run," Odile said. "When the freight's delivered."

"When the freight's delivered," Cass agreed. Then, turning away, over her shoulder, almost too casual: "Nice flying today, by the way. Your father's line, mostly. But the last ten miles were yours."

* * *

That night the Landing's common room was loud with trappers and freight haulers and a survey crew drinking off a season in the bush, and Odile sat in the corner with a plate of trout and a beer she was using as a prop, listening. Listening was the other thing her father had taught her—that a pilot who only listened to radios was flying half-blind, that towns talked, and that the talk at the end of the road was always one week ahead of the official word.

Most of it was ordinary. Fuel prices. A bear problem at the fish camp. The monsoon, always the monsoon—two weeks early this year, the old-timers said, maybe three, and the passes would shut like a trap.

Then, at the table nearest the stove, a voice she half-recognized: one of the survey crew, a heavyset man in his sixties with a white beard and forearms like dock rope, saying to his companions, low but not low enough, "—that's the third blasting permit registered to a company nobody's ever heard of, all of them within five miles of the escarpment headwall. You don't blast five miles of claims for nothing. Somebody's taking that mountain apart in pieces and calling it exploration—"

"Halvard," said another man, and spat, or made the gesture of it. "Same crowd buying up the Rivergate lots. Been buying them for years, cheap as frost. Whole town's mortgaged to strangers nobody's met."

Odile kept her eyes on her plate. Her pulse had gone strange and slow, the way it did in turbulence.

The bearded man glanced around the room as he spoke, and his gaze crossed Odile's face and stopped. He frowned, the frown of a man retrieving something from deep storage. Then he said, to no one in particular, in a voice pitched just loud enough to carry:

"Dunmore. Now there's a name I haven't heard up here in nine years. Used to be a surveyor by that name. Drew the best maps of the escarpment headwall anybody ever saw. Wonder whatever happened to him."

Nobody answered him. The fire ticked. Odile lifted her beer so the motion of drinking would explain her stillness, and set it down, and did not look up—and in not looking up felt, with terrible clarity, that she had just failed some kind of test whose questions she hadn't yet heard.

Across the room, Cass was watching her over the rim of her glass, and the look on Cass's face said plainly: *you heard it too.*

The old surveyor stood, settled his tab, and on his way to the door paused beside Odile's table—just for a moment, just long enough to set something down beside her plate, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine, worn soft at the corners by decades of pack straps and map cases.

"You'll be wanting this," he said. "Your brother would have wanted you to have it before you fly the pass."

Then he pushed open the door into the cold, and was gone, and the package sat on the table between the salt and the ketchup, small and heavy as a stone, waiting for her hands.
