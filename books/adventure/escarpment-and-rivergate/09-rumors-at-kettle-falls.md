## 9. Rumors at Kettle Falls

Kettle Falls smelled like diesel and spruce rot and the particular sweetness of a town that had learned to live on what the river brought it and nothing more. Odile stood on the boardwalk outside the Northern Air depot with a paper cup of coffee gone cold in her hand and watched the weather build over the western ridgeline like a debt coming due.

The staging operation had taken most of the day. Cass's plane had come in an hour behind hers, riding the edge of a cell that had looked, from altitude, like a bruise spreading under skin, and now both aircraft sat lashed down on the gravel apron while the ground crew from Kettle Falls—three men and a woman named Petra who ran the fuel truck like she was feeding a temperamental animal—worked through the manifest by lantern light. Crates of flour and salt. Diesel in drums. The medical pallet, which Odile had personally strapped and double-strapped because she did not trust anyone else's knots with that one.

Rivergate's winter stores. Nine years' worth of someone else's silence, stacked on a gravel strip two hundred miles north of anywhere.

She drank the cold coffee anyway.

"You always drink it dead?" Cass appeared beside her, jacket collar up against the wind that came off the falls in long cold breaths. She nodded toward the cup.

"It stops tasting like coffee either way."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Odile agreed, and Cass made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were something she still paid for.

They stood together without speaking for a moment, which was new. On the flight up they had spent four hours trading radio calls across twenty miles of broken air—Cass calling the turbulence before Odile hit it, Odile finding the smooth lane through the cell that let Cass's heavier load climb—and somewhere over the divide between Escarpment and Kettle Falls the rivalry had shifted into something almost like conversation. Not friendship. Odile wasn't sure she had the material for friendship with Cass Merrow. But a shared language, maybe. Two pilots discovering they'd been reading the same sky all along.

"Petra says the hotel has rooms," Cass said. "And that the bar has whiskey."

"Which are you after?"

"Both. In that order." Cass pushed off the rail. "You coming?"

"I've got the medical pallet to check."

"You checked it twice."

"Then I'll check it a third time." Odile said it lightly enough, but Cass looked at her a beat too long—the look of a pilot reading weather she didn't like—before she went inside.

* * *

The hotel was called the Landing, though there was no landing anymore except the airstrip; the river steamer had stopped coming up this far when Odile was a child. The building leaned slightly east, as if listening for water that no longer ran past its door. The common room was low-ceilinged and warm, lit by lamps that buzzed, and it held perhaps fifteen people: freight handlers, a couple of Ministry road engineers drinking beer out of bottles with the labels sweating, an old man playing solitaire with a deck worn soft as cloth, and a knot of locals near the stove who went quiet—not fully quiet, but quieter—in the way small towns go quiet when strangers walk in carrying news from outside.

Odile took a stool at the end of the bar. The bartender, a broad woman with forearms like dock rope, poured her something amber without asking and said, "You're the pilots. North run."

"South run, mostly. This is a detour."

"Detour." The woman said the word like she was tasting it. "That's one way to put Rivergate."

Odile turned on her stool. "What do you mean?"

The bartender wiped the counter where nothing needed wiping. "I mean nobody goes there. Nine years, nobody goes. Weather closes the pass, fine, weather does that. But weather opens too. And nobody comes down the valley road, and nobody flies in, and the Ministry doesn't ask why, and the supply contracts—" she shrugged, a big rolling shrug that moved her whole shoulders— "the supply contracts just stop getting renewed. You ever see a contract just stop? Contracts don't stop. People stop them."

"Maybe the town moved."

"Four hundred people? Where to?" The bartender set the rag down. "My cousin married a man from Rivergate. Before. He used to say it was the best valley on the range—good soil, good water, timber, a hydro plant the old company built back when companies built things to last. Nobody leaves a place like that because of snow."

"And yet."

"And yet." She leaned in, voice dropping under the lamp-buzz. "Freight men talk, you know. We hear things. There's been land bought up around that valley—quiet, through lawyers, parcels here and there, going cheap since the slide. Cheap like somebody knows something. Cheap like somebody's waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

The bartender straightened, and whatever had loosened her tongue closed again like a valve. "Waiting for the price to be right, I expect. That's all anybody waits for." She pulled a bottle down and topped off Odile's glass unasked. "You're flying in there?"

"Tomorrow, weather holding."

"Then keep your ears open and your mouth shut. It's advice that's kept me alive in towns smaller than this one." She moved off down the bar, and Odile understood she'd gotten everything the woman meant to give.

At the table by the stove, the conversation had resumed but changed key. She caught fragments now, pitched deliberately low: *—saw the road crew turn back at the washout, no orders to repair it, three seasons running—* and *—my brother-in-law flew charter out of the capital, said there was a NOTAM on that whole grid, said it read like it was written by a lawyer, not a weatherman—* and then, softer, a woman's voice: *—the Dunmore girl is sitting right there, so mind your tongues.*

The name went through the room like a stone through a window.

Odile kept her back to them and her eyes on her glass and felt every ear in the place swivel toward her and away again, the way birds all lift at once and settle. *Dunmore.* Nine years she had worn the name thin, worn it into something neutral, something that belonged to a dead man and a closed ledger and not to her. She had paid for her anonymity in installments—no photographs, no hometown listed on her license renewal, a signature that had simplified itself over the years until it was barely a mark. She had paid, and she had assumed the debt was settled.

But names travel north the way seeds do. On the wind. In the mouths of freight men.

* * *

She took her drink to the corner near the window, where the glass ran with condensation and the dark beyond it was absolute except for the runway lights, two rows of orange points swallowed by cloud. From the corner she could watch the room without being watched, an old habit, an art she had refined the way other people refined golf swings or pie crusts.

The old man with the solitaire deck was watching her.

He sat with his back half to the stove, a lean, dried-out man with a white mustache stained amber at the edges and hands that lay flat on the table like tools set down carefully. He'd folded his cards. He wasn't pretending otherwise. He simply looked at her, unhurried, the way you look at a landmark you haven't seen in years and are checking against memory.

When she looked back, he gathered his cards, rose, crossed the room with a gait that favored his left knee, and set himself on the stool beside hers without asking.

"Dunmore," he said. Not a question. A weighing.

"It's a common name."

"Not spelled that way, it isn't. Not pronounced that way, either." He signaled the bartender for whatever she was having. "Elias Vannick. I surveyed the escarpment in sixty-eight, seventy-one, seventy-six. Your father cut the original pass line with me. Ila Dunmore. Flew like he was part of the airplane."

The name landed in Odile's chest with a weight she hadn't braced for. She had spent nine years making sure no one said her father's name to her in a warm voice. "You knew him."

"Everybody on the range knew him. Best mountain pilot there was, and the only honest surveyor I ever worked with—he wouldn't sign a line he hadn't walked." Vannick accepted his drink, lifted it, didn't drink. His eyes stayed on her, pale and steady as winter light. "So. The daughter's flying the resupply. After all these years."

"The company won the contract. I fly what the company tells me."

"Mm." He drank finally, a small measured sip. "Funny thing about that contract. Been floated twice before, you know. Twice some outfit or other tried to put a supply run back into Rivergate, and twice it died in paperwork. Permits, insurance, environmental review—one year it was a bird nesting survey, if you can believe that, in November. And now it goes through in six weeks, clean as a whistle, to a strip owner drowning in debt." He turned the glass slowly on the bar. "Contracts don't stop, girl. And contracts don't start, neither. People start them."

The bartender's words, coming back north out of another man's mouth. Odile set her own glass down. "Who are you, Mr. Vannick?"

"Retired. Mostly deaf in one ear and opinionated in both." He smiled, and it was a good smile, worn smooth by decades of use. "I'm a man who liked your father and never believed the story they told about his son."

The room's noise seemed to recede. Odile heard the lamp-buzz, the stove ticking, her own pulse.

"What story is that?"

"That Theo Dunmore got eager, pushed the pass in bad air, and killed himself through impatience." Vannick said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you'd read coordinates off a map. "I knew that boy. He checked his fuel three times and his charts five. Eager wasn't in him. Careful was in him so deep it was nearly a vice." He paused. "And I knew the rock. I'd surveyed that face. What came down on him that day came down wrong—it came down from above the tree line, from a bench nobody quarried, in a pattern that doesn't happen natural. I said so. Once. To a Ministry man who wrote it down and thanked me, and I never heard another word, and a month later my firm lost its provincial retainer, and I learned how these things go."

Odile's hands had gone cold around the glass. "You're saying the slide that killed my brother—"

"I'm saying I'm an old man who keeps his mouth shut, and I've kept it shut so long I sometimes forget there's anything in it." He set the glass down and looked at her, and in his face she saw something that undid her a little: not pity. Recognition. The look of a man meeting the one other person in the world who had been paying the same installment plan. "And then you walked in tonight, Ila Dunmore's daughter, flying the route nobody's allowed to fly, and I thought—well. Maybe the mouth's been shut long enough."

"Mr. Vannick—"

"Elias." He raised a hand, stopping her, and glanced once around the room—at the freight men, at the engineers, at the corners where corners hold ears. When he spoke again his voice was lower, and every word was placed like a foot on scree. "There's a thing I should give you. Something of your father's. I've kept it thirty years thinking someday somebody from that family would come north asking questions, and I told myself if they never did, it would die with me, and that would be that. But you're here. And you're flying in tomorrow. And I find I don't want to die with it after all."

Odile's heart was doing something she didn't authorize. "Give me what?"

Vannick looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind came off the falls and the runway lights shivered in the murk, and somewhere over the western ridge the monsoon's leading edge was grinding south toward them like a slow black tide.

"Come by my cabin in the morning," he said. "Before you fly. First light—I'm an early man. It's the blue house past the church, you'll smell the woodsmoke." He finished his drink and stood, favoring the knee, and gathered his soft-worn cards. Then he paused, one hand on the bar, and added quietly:

"Bring your questions, Miss Dunmore. And bring your brother's name. It's time somebody said it out loud in this country."

He went out into the dark, and the door swung shut behind him, and Odile sat alone at the bar with her cold glass and the loud buzzing of the lamps, feeling the strange, terrifying sensation of being seen—thoroughly, precisely, by name—for the first time in nine years.

* * *

She found Cass in the hotel's narrow hallway outside their rooms, leaning against the wall with her boots crossed, clearly waiting.

"Well?" Cass said.

"Well what?"

"You've got the face. The one you had over the divide today when you found that smooth lane through the cell—like you'd seen something nobody else could see yet." Cass tilted her head. "Something happened down there in the corner. Old man Vannick sat with you twenty minutes and the whole room pretended not to watch."

Odile weighed it. Every instinct she owned said *keep it sealed, keep it yours, carry it alone the way you've carried everything.* But the bartender's words were still in her ear—*keep your ears open*—and Vannick's voice under them, and beneath both, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a trap clicking shut around a contract she hadn't wanted to fly.

"Cass," she said slowly, "why did you really take this job?"

Cass held her gaze for a long moment. Somewhere below them, a floorboard creaked; the storm pressed its first fat drops against the window glass.

"Ask me again tomorrow," Cass said. "After we've flown the pass."

Odile climbed the stairs to her room, set her alarm for first light, and lay in the dark listening to the rain begin, rehearsing a dead man's name until she could say it without flinching.

*Theo,* she practiced. *Theo.*

It still didn't come out right. But in the morning there would be a blue house past the church, and woodsmoke, and whatever her father had left behind that she'd never known to look for—and for the first time in nine years, Odile Dunmore was afraid not of what she remembered, but of what she was about to learn.
