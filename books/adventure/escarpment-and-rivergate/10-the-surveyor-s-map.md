## 10. The Surveyor's Map

The rain found Kettle Falls an hour after dawn and settled in like a tenant who intended to stay the season. It came down in gray sheets off the sawtooth ridges, drumming on the corrugated roof of the survey office, filling the gutters, turning the mud street into a brown river that carried leaves and bottle caps and one abandoned boot past the window where Odile sat waiting.

The office smelled of paper and lamp oil and forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the walls. Maps hung from every vertical surface—topographic sheets, timber cruises, mineral claims, cadastral plats of towns that had burned down or washed away or simply given up—and they hung there curling at their corners like old leaves, patient, certain someone would come back for them eventually.

Odile had been sitting in the straight-backed chair by the stove for twenty minutes, watching the old man work.

His name was Aldous Prentiss, though everyone at Kettle Falls just called him the Surveyor, as if he were the only one, which for three hundred miles in any direction he very nearly was. He was somewhere north of eighty, built like the terrain he'd spent his life measuring: low, dense, weathered to the color of granite. His hands shook now—not much, but enough that when he unrolled a map across his desk he did it with both of them, the way a man handles something fragile or holy.

He hadn't spoken since she'd arrived. He'd only looked at her.

That was the thing about coming back to the country your father had flown. People looked at you. They took your face apart looking for him in it, and then they either smiled or went quiet, and Odile had learned over nine years to dread the going-quiet more than anything else the mountains could do to her.

Prentiss was going quiet.

"You have her chin," he said finally. "Dunmore's girl. You have her chin exactly."

"I don't know what you mean," Odile said, because she didn't. Her mother had died when Odile was six; her face was her father's face, sharp through the jaw, all angles and stubbornness, and she had spent her adult life not thinking about it.

"Your mother." The old man's eyes were pale as river ice. "I surveyed the Dunmore parcel in '61. Your mother stood on the porch with you on her hip and told me I was trespassing until your father came out and laughed and put coffee in my hands." He turned back to the map he'd been unrolling. "You were two years old. You spat at me."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Best review I ever got." He weighted the corners of the map with four brass objects that might once have been instrument parts. "Sit forward, girl. This is yours, and I've kept it too long already."

Odile leaned forward, and the room seemed to lean with her.

It was a survey sheet—the original, she understood that immediately, the master copy, drawn in ink on linen-backed paper that had gone the color of weak tea with age. The title block read ESCARPMENT RANGE — SOUTH APPROACHES, and beneath it, in her father's blocky, unmistakable capitals: A. DUNMORE, LICENSED SURVEYOR, COMMISSIONED BY THE PROVINCIAL LANDS BOARD, and a date nine years and two months before the crash.

Her father's handwriting. She had seen it last on a fuel receipt she kept folded in her flight bag behind her license, and seeing it here, large and confident and alive, laid out across a dead man's desk in a town she'd sworn never to need, hit her somewhere below the ribs and stayed there.

"This is his?"

"Every line." Prentiss touched the sheet without touching it, hovering a fingertip above the ink. "Commissioned to run the escarpment approaches after the timber companies started agitating for road access. Your father walked every contour himself. Took him fourteen months. I ran the triangulation for the high stations—I was younger then, and stupid enough to enjoy the cold." He tapped the western edge of the sheet. "This is the pass. The one they fly now. The one your boy went down in."

*My boy.* As if Theo were a colt she'd raised. Odile said nothing. Outside, the rain hammered the roof, and somewhere down the street a dog was losing an argument with the weather.

"Look at the margin," Prentiss said.

She looked. Along the lower edge of the map, in red pencil, faded almost to rust, someone had drawn a line.

Not a route—a *line*, ruled freehand, starting at the valley floor south of Rivergate and climbing northwest in a long, improbable diagonal, threading between contour lines that Odile's eye, trained by nine years of mountain flying, read even before her mind caught up. The line crossed terrain no pilot would choose. It climbed through a notch in the secondary ridge—a notch so narrow the contour lines nearly kissed—then followed a bench of flat ground along the escarpment's shoulder, a bench barely wide enough to exist, let alone to fly, and then it cut across the headwall itself, the great rock face where the wind came off the icefields like water off a dam spillway, and dropped into Rivergate from the northeast, out of the sun, out of the wind, out of everything.

"Nobody flies that," Odile said. Her voice came out flat. "There's no approach off the headwall. The downdraft alone—"

"There wasn't, in your father's day." Prentiss sat down heavily across from her, and the chair complained. "But maps are arguments, girl. Every line on this sheet is your father saying *here is the truth of the ground*, and somebody else saying *no, here is what we'll pretend*. That red line isn't a flight path. Not yet. It's a question. He drew it because the contours said it *might* be possible, if a man flew it enough times to learn its temper. He never got the chance to answer it."

"Why not?" But she knew why not. She could feel the answer coming toward her like weather.

"Because the Lands Board shelved the survey." Prentiss's voice dropped, and the old man's shaking hands went still on the desk, the way hands go still when they're gripping something. "Shelved it six weeks after delivery. No explanation. Your father wrote them eleven letters—I have copies of nine. Then the blasting permits started coming through for the upper valley, and your father stopped writing letters and started drinking, and I stopped asking him questions, because a man who has watched a government eat his own work learns to keep his mouth shut and his instruments oiled."

"The blasting permits." Odile's mouth had gone dry. "For what?"

"For a mine that isn't on this map." Prentiss reached across and turned the sheet over, and there, on the reverse, in her father's hand again, was a list. Eleven entries. Dates, claim numbers, permit references, each one annotated in that same blocky capital script, and beside the last entry, underlined twice, three words that made the room tilt:

**NOT ON RECORD.**

"A mine above the pass," Odile said slowly. "Above the escarpment."

"A mine that shouldn't exist." The old man nodded. "And nine years ago, the week before your brother died, your father came here. Sat in that same chair. Drank my coffee and refused my whisky, which was unlike him. And he asked me to hold this map. Not file it—*hold* it. He said, 'Aldous, if anything happens to me or mine, give this to the one who comes asking.' And then he flew home, and eight days later the mountain came down on your brother, and he never spoke of the map again, and neither did I, and that silence is the one regret of my life I can't survey my way around."

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the window in ropes. Odile became aware that she had not breathed for some time and corrected this.

"He thought the crash was coming," she said. "Before it happened. He knew something about that mine."

"I think your father knew a great many things," Prentiss said, "and told us all of nothing, because telling things is how people die in this country. But he left you more than the map." The old man opened a drawer, and his hands were shaking badly now, and he didn't seem to care. "He left you this. I was to give it only with the map, and only to family, and God forgive me for the nine years I sat on both."

It was an envelope, soft with handling, sealed with tape gone amber. On the front, in her father's writing: **FOR O. IF SHE ASKS.**

*If she asks.* Nine years, and she had never asked. She had flown around the escarpment, around the grief, around the whole blacked-out country of her family's history, and the map had waited here the entire time, patient as the mountains, because her father had known her better than she knew herself. He had known she would have to come asking eventually. Or he had hoped. With her father it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She broke the seal.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, folded in thirds, and when she opened it she expected her father's hand and found another's instead.

Theo's.

She would have known it anywhere, asleep, blind, underwater. Her brother wrote the way he flew—fast, slanted, leaning hard into every line as if the page were a turn he was banking through, letters tumbling over each other in their hurry to arrive. She had thirty years of his notes in her memory: grocery lists, birthday cards, marginalia in flight manuals, the last note he'd ever left on the kitchen table at Escarpment, *back by dark, save me some of whatever Wren burns for dinner.*

The note was dated five days before the crash.

*Odi —*

*Dad won't say it straight so I'll say it for him. There's blasting going on up top of the escarpment that isn't on any permit we can find. I hiked the upper bench Tuesday. There's a working face cut into the ridge maybe two kilometers northeast of the pass, hidden in the scree, and it's big, and it's recent, and whoever's running it doesn't want it found. Dad thinks the vibration is loading the slopes above the flyway. He's right. I stood on that bench and the whole hillside hummed like a struck string.*

*I'm going to fly the corridor on the red line next week. If it works, we take the survey to the Lands Board ourselves, with photos, and make them shut it down. If I don't come back, don't let Dad bury this map with his pride. Give it to someone with worse sense than him.*

*That's you. Fly safe. Come argue with me about it Sunday.*

*T.*

The paper trembled. Not her hands—she checked—they were steady, iron-steady, the way they went in turbulence, the way they'd been steady at Theo's funeral while the whole town of Escarpment watched her not cry. It was the paper trembling, or the light, or the world.

*Don't let Dad bury this map with his pride.*

Her father had buried it anyway. In a stranger's drawer, in a town he never visited again, behind an oath and a blackout and nine years of silence. And Odile had helped him bury it—had carried the silence like cargo, had made invisibility her trade, had flown nine years of medicine runs with her brother's last words folded inside a stranger's envelope two hundred miles north and never once come asking.

*Give it to someone with worse sense than him.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, half sound and half wound, and Prentiss had the grace to look at the window while she did it.

"Theo was mapping a safe corridor," she said, when she could speak. "He wasn't hot-dogging. Everyone said—he flew that pass like he owned it, they said he was showing off, pushing weather—"

"Boy was careful as a church deacon," Prentiss said. "I lent him my barometer for that hike. He returned it calibrated and wrapped in an oilcloth." The old man's pale eyes held hers. "Whatever brought him down, girl, it wasn't recklessness. And it wasn't only weather. Your father believed that to his grave. Now you've got his map and your brother's letter, and I'd guess you've got a contract in your pocket that smells wrong, and a rival flying your second plane, and a monsoon coming early. So the question isn't whether you believe it. The question is what you're going to do with a piece of paper that says the mountain was murdered."

Outside, the rain intensified, as if the sky had been listening.

Odile folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, the way you fold something that has already survived more than it should have. She rolled the survey sheet and sleeved it in the oilcloth Prentiss offered, and the weight of it in her flight bag was nothing—an ounce, less—and also everything, the heaviest thing she had ever carried, heavier than the medevac litter, heavier than the debt, heavier than the urn of her father's ashes she had scattered into the river at Escarpment because he'd left instructions and she'd obeyed, the way she always obeyed the dead.

At the door, she stopped.

"The red line," she said. "The corridor. Has anyone ever flown it? Since him?"

Prentiss was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Down the street, the dog had won or lost or simply stopped.

"One man tried it, the summer after the crash," the old man said. "Local bush pilot, thought he'd prove the boy right and the mountain wrong. Turned back at the headwall. Said afterward there was a wind coming off that face that sounded like voices." He met her eyes. "He never tried again. Most men don't, after hearing that."

"What happened to him?"

"Retired to the coast. Flies tourists over glaciers now, tells them the mountains are peaceful." Prentiss almost smiled. "Men lie about mountains the way they lie about women, girl. Politely, and to protect themselves."

Odile stepped out into the rain and let it hit her, cold and honest, washing the smell of old paper off her skin. Across the street, Cass Merrow's second plane sat tied down at the fuel dock, dripping, and beyond it the gray wall of the monsoon's leading edge stood over the southern ridgeline like a verdict.

In her bag, against her hip, Theo's letter pressed its folded weight against her side, five days older than his death and nine years younger than her grief.

*Come argue with me about it Sunday.*

"Ask me anything," she said quietly, to the rain, to the mountain, to the brother the world had decided was reckless because the truth was inconvenient. "Ask me anything, Theo. I'm done not asking."

Then she pulled up her collar and walked toward the planes, and behind her, in a drawer in a survey office in Kettle Falls, the space where the map had slept for nine years lay empty for the first time—and somewhere south, in a town that didn't know yet that it was about to be saved or sold, a radio crackled awake on a frequency no one had monitored in years.
