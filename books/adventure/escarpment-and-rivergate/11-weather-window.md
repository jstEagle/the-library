## 11. Weather Window

Wren Adeyemi woke before the alarm, which was how she knew something was wrong. Her body had a way of keeping the airstrip's time even when her mind wanted sleep, and it never woke her early unless the world had shifted in the night—unless some machine, somewhere, had begun to fail.

She lay in the narrow bed above the hangar office and listened. Rain on the tin roof, steady but not heavy. The compressor cycling down in the shop below, its pressure bleed hissing like a long exhale. Nothing broken. Nothing screaming.

And still she lay there with her heart going too fast, staring at the ceiling where the light of the runway beacon came through the gap in the curtain and swept its slow white arc across the rafters, and thought: *six days*.

The number had arrived whole, the way bad numbers do, before she could account for it. She'd fallen asleep over the satellite printouts at two in the morning, coffee gone cold beside her elbow, and somewhere between waking and sleeping her mind had finished the arithmetic without her permission. The monsoon was making landfall early. Not by hours. By days. And the passes—the escarpment passes, the ones that stood between Kettle Falls and Rivergate like a locked gate—would close behind it.

Six days. Maybe five and a half if you were honest about margins, and Wren was always honest about margins. It was the dishonesty of other people that kept her up at night.

She got up, dressed in the dark out of habit, and went down the iron stairs into the hangar's cold cathedral air.

* * *

The hangar at four in the morning was Wren's favorite church. The sodium lights were off; only the work lamps she'd left burning over the Cessna's open cowling threw their yellow circles into the dark, and beyond them the big cargo plane sat in shadow like a whale beached in a barn, its fuselage catching the work light along one curved flank. The smell was oil and kerosene and cold metal and, underneath it all, the particular mineral scent of Escarpment itself—granite dust blown in from the ridge, ground fine by nine thousand landings.

She put her hand flat on the cargo plane's skin. Still warm from yesterday's test run, faintly, the way a stove stays warm after the fire's out. She liked that. Machines held on to what you gave them, if you paid attention.

The sabotage was what she hadn't been able to stop thinking about. Three days now since she'd found it, and she had told no one—not Odile, not Cass, not old Baptiste who ran the fuel truck and had known her since she was seventeen and greasy-knuckled and lying about her age to get on the night crew. She'd turned it over and over like a stone in her pocket: the drained hydraulic reservoir, topped back up with fluid so no casual check would catch it, but drained enough that under sustained high-pressure demand—under a go-around, say, or a heavy load flaring hard on a short strip—the system would cavitate and go soft when it mattered most. And the tie-rod. The filed tie-rod on the nose gear steering. Someone had taken a hacksaw to it nearly through, then painted over the cut with such care, such patience, that only a torque wrench and a suspicious mind would ever find it.

A suspicious mind. That was the problem, wasn't it. Because suspicion, once you let it in, didn't stay politely confined to strangers. It got into everything. It got into the way Baptiste had been short with her Tuesday, and the way the new line boy, Ferro, kept his locker padlocked when nobody else did, and the way Cass Merrow's second plane had needed an unexplained parts order from the coast—perfectly ordinary parts, probably, but probably was doing a lot of work in that sentence lately.

It got, worst of all, into the thought she refused to finish: that whoever had done this had done it quietly, competently, with access and time. Which meant crew. Which meant someone whose face she saw every day.

Wren pulled a stool to the nose gear, set her lamp, and looked at the tie-rod again. She'd replaced it already—new rod, new castle nuts, safety-wired and torqued and logged—but she'd kept the old one, wrapped in a rag in her toolbox, because evidence mattered and because she couldn't quite bring herself to throw away a thing that had almost killed them. She touched the paint-blistered cut now through memory rather than metal, and felt the same cold move through her chest that had moved through it the first time: not fear exactly. Something more personal than fear. *Someone reached into my airplane.* The plane was hers the way a house is yours—not on paper, maybe, but in the way that counts, in the way of knowing every sound it makes.

And Odile had been scheduled to fly it.

That was the part she kept circling back to at three in the morning. Not the money, not the contract, not even the strip they might all lose. Odile Dunmore, who flew like the sky owed her nothing and gave her everything anyway, strapped into a machine someone had quietly arranged to fail.

Wren picked up her wrench and went to work, because work was what her hands knew how to do with feelings too large for them.

* * *

She started with the hydraulics and gave the system the kind of attention most mechanics reserve for engines about to carry their own mothers. Flushed the reservoir completely, refilled with fresh fluid from a sealed drum she opened herself, watched for particulates in the filter with a jeweler's loupe. There—fine gray glitter caught in the pleats. Metal. Not much. Enough. She bagged the filter and labeled it in her small square handwriting: *date, tail number, found during routine inspection*. Routine. Let anyone who read the log assume routine. The truth could wait in the bag until she knew whose ears deserved it.

Then the rest of the plane, top to bottom, panel by panel, because once you've found one knife in the dark you check every room. Control cables: tensioned true, no frays. Fuel lines: clean, clamps tight, no sign of tampering. She pulled the cowl panels and ran her fingers along the firewall looking for chafe or cuts, checked the battery box, the avionics rack, the pitot lines, the stall vanes. She drained a cup of fuel from each tank sump and held it to the lamp—clear as gin, no water, no grit. Whatever the saboteur had intended, they'd been surgical about it. One system weakened just past the margin. One gear component thinned just shy of visible. A failure that would look like weather, or like age, or like pilot error.

Like grief, she thought, and hated the thought. Like the kind of accident people would shake their heads over and say *well, that route, that mountain, you know*, and no one would ever look twice.

By six the light through the hangar's high windows had gone from black to charcoal to the bruised pewter of an Escarpment dawn, and Wren straightened up with her spine crackling and found Baptiste standing at the hangar doors with two cups of coffee and the expression of a man who had been watching her work for an hour without interrupting.

"You sleep?" he asked.

"Enough."

"That's a no." He handed her a cup. He was seventy if he was a day, built like the fuel drums he rolled, Senegalese French accent worn smooth by forty years of English. He nodded at the cargo plane, its cowling panels stacked in their careful order. "You're inspecting like a woman expecting to find something."

"I inspected like a woman who inspects," Wren said. "That's the job."

"Mm." He drank. Beyond him the mist was lifting off the river flats in long slow banners, and the escarpment stood above the town with its head in cloud, gray and enormous and indifferent. "Weather's turning," he said. "Coast report came in on the telex overnight. Storm's made landfall two days ahead of the forecast. They're calling it early monsoon."

Wren's stomach dropped, confirming the number her body had already delivered. "How early do the passes close?"

"Kettle Falls says five days, six if the front stalls." He shrugged, a gesture that contained multitudes: pity, fatalism, forty years of mountains. "Cass wants everyone briefed at eight. Odile's already in the office with the charts spread out. You should eat something first."

"Did you tell her?"

"About the storm? Everyone knows by now. Small town." He paused, and his eyes, shrewd and kind, settled on her. "Something else you're not telling anyone?"

Wren held his gaze and lied, or half-lied, which is the same thing said slower. "When I know, you'll know."

Baptiste studied her a moment longer, then nodded slowly, and she understood that he had accepted the lie the way you accept a passenger's weight—knowing it's there, adjusting for it, saying nothing. He drained his coffee. "Eat something," he said again, and walked out into the wet morning.

* * *

The briefing was Cass's show, and Cass ran it like she ran everything: fast, precise, daring you to object. She'd pinned the satellite imagery to the corkboard in the office—a great spiral arm of cloud swinging up from the coast like a scythe—and she stood beside it with a pointer and the coiled energy of a woman who had decided that fear was a fuel and she intended to burn hers efficiently.

"Landfall happened twelve hours ago, here"—the pointer tapped the coast—"and the leading edge reaches the escarpment in roughly ninety-six hours. Behind it, sustained rain, embedded cells, winds gusting sixty plus over the ridgeline. After that, the passes are closed. Not difficult. Closed. Nobody flies them, including us, including idiots, until spring."

"So we have four days," Odile said. She was sitting on the desk's edge with a chart across her knees, and Wren, standing by the door with her coffee, watched her the way she always watched her—sideways, hungrily, guardedly. Nine years Wren had worked within ten feet of Odile Dunmore and nine years she had not said the thing, and the not-saying had become its own structure in her life, load-bearing, familiar. Some days she thought the silence was cowardice. Most days she thought it was mercy: Odile guarded her interior the way Wren guarded a hangar, and you didn't pick a lock out of love.

"We have four days to get the winter resupply into Rivergate," Cass went on. "Which means we fly tomorrow, both planes, full loads, and we fly again the day after, and we keep flying until the window shuts or the town's stocked. Questions?"

"The staging at Kettle Falls," Odile said. "Fuel's thin there. I want drums ferried up on the first load."

"Noted. Wren?"

"Both aircraft are ready," Wren said. "I'll have the cargo plane signed off by tonight. Full inspection, fresh fluids, new nose gear components." She let that last word sit there a half-second longer than it needed to, watching faces. Cass didn't blink. Odile glanced up sharply—she'd hear the emphasis, she always heard everything—but Wren gave her nothing, and Odile looked back down at her chart.

Ferro, the line boy, was in the corner with his arms crossed, and Wren watched him watch Cass, and told herself that a nineteen-year-old locking his locker proved nothing, that suspicion was a solvent and it ate the container holding it before it ate anything else. But she wrote his name in her head anyway, in the same column where she'd written everyone's name, because the saboteur had access, and access was a short list, and she intended to shorten it.

* * *

After the briefing broke up, the office emptied except for the two of them, because it always did—Odile rolling her chart, Wren pretending to inventory the tool cabinet, the conversation they weren't having filling the room like heat.

"New nose gear components," Odile said at last, not looking up.

"Wear item. Due anyway."

"Due anyway." Odile set the chart down. In the gray morning light her face was all angles and tiredness, and there was something raw under the tiredness that Wren recognized and ached at. "Wren. Look at me and tell me there's nothing wrong with that plane."

Wren looked at her. This was the moment, and she knew it was the moment, the one where a person either hands over the stone in their pocket or keeps carrying it. But the stone was heavy with a question she couldn't answer—who—and handing Odile a question with no answer in it, on the eve of the flight that had already cost her brother, that had been pried out of her father's grave by a contract and a rival—that would not protect her. It would just make her afraid in a different direction, and afraid pilots died differently than careful ones.

"There's nothing wrong with that plane," Wren said. "Not anymore. I've been over it twice, panel by panel. It is the safest aircraft on this field, and I will stake my license on it."

Odile searched her face for a long moment. "Not anymore," she repeated softly. "Meaning?"

"Meaning I fixed everything that needed fixing." Meaning, also, *ask me again when I know who*, and Wren watched Odile decide—visibly, the way she decided things, like trimming an aircraft—not to push. For now.

"Okay," Odile said. Then, quieter, in the voice she used perhaps twice a year: "Thank you. For the way you take care of things."

"Things," Wren said, and smiled despite everything. "Is that what I am."

"You know what you are." Odile gathered her charts, and at the door she stopped, half-turned, and for one unguarded second the whole guarded architecture of her face fell open, and Wren saw in it nine years of the same unsaid thing, mirrored. "Fly safe tomorrow," Odile said. "Both of us."

"Both of us," Wren agreed, and then Odile was gone into the rain-smelling morning, and Wren stood alone in the office with her heart going like an engine at full throttle and nowhere legal to land it.

* * *

She spent the afternoon making the plane not just safe but *known*—every system witnessed, every log entry squared, the maintenance record rebuilt page by page. It was while cross-checking the historical entries, hunting a discrepancy in the hydraulic service intervals, that she found the torn pages.

She'd noticed the damage weeks ago and filed it under sloppiness: the big green maintenance binder for the cargo plane, its spine cracked, several pages near the middle raggedly removed—torn, not cut, in haste. Old news, she'd thought. Paperwork lost in some frantic season. But now, with a filed tie-rod wrapped in a rag in her toolbox and gray metal glitter in a labeled bag, sloppiness had stopped being an available explanation.

She smoothed the stubs flat and read what remained. The surviving fragments covered a stretch of nine years ago—the year of the Rivergate disaster, the year before Wren had come to Escarpment, the year Odile's father had still owned the strip. Torn from the record: the maintenance history covering the months before Theo Dunmore's last flight. What remained were the edges of sentences, halves of part numbers, the ghost of a signature. And on one stub, in the margin, in pencil, faded almost to nothing, a single legible fragment:

*...pressure vessel not OEM — flag to D.*

Flag to D. Flag to Dunmore. Someone, nine years ago, had found something wrong with this aircraft—or one like it—and had tried to tell Odile's father about it, and the pages telling that story had been torn out.

Wren sat very still in the office chair, the binder open on her knees, rain beginning against the windows in earnest now, the first outriders of the monsoon arriving like scouts ahead of an army. Outside, the beacon swept its arc. Somewhere across the field Odile was preflighting her own plane for tomorrow, trusting the machines, trusting the crew, trusting the world to have become simpler in nine years.

It hadn't. It had just gotten better at hiding.

Wren closed the binder gently, as if it were sleeping, and added a new column to the list in her head. Not *who*. A harder question now: *how long*. How long had someone been working this angle—nine years, or three days, or continuously, patiently, the whole time, while she and Odile and Baptiste and all of them kept the strip alive on faith?

She looked at the bagged filter, the wrapped tie-rod, the mutilated binder. Evidence. It needed a keeper, and until she knew who to trust, the keeper was her.

Tomorrow they flew north. Six days of window, four of them already spent by the sky. And somewhere in the rain coming down over the escarpment, whatever hand had reached into her airplane was still reaching, patient as water, waiting for the weather to do the rest.

Wren switched off the office light and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the storm arrive.

*Let it come,* she thought. *We'll see whose clock runs out first.*
