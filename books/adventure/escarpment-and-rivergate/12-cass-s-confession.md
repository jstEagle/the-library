## 12. Cass's Confession

Cass Merrow had learned a long time ago that the truth was like fuel: you didn't spill it all at once, and you never spilled it near an open flame. She'd been rationing hers for three months, measuring it out in drops, and tonight—sitting across from Odile Dunmore in the back room of the Kettle Falls roadhouse with rain hammering the tin roof like a fist on a locked door—she decided the tank was finally empty enough to show what was left.

She'd asked Odile here with a lie, of course. A lie about engine logs, about cargo weights, about the second plane's center of gravity. Odile had come anyway, which told Cass something: the woman was hungry for answers too, even if she'd rather chew glass than admit it.

The roadhouse was nearly empty this late in the season. The stove ticked and settled. Somewhere behind the wall, the owner's dog dreamed in short, unhappy bursts. Cass turned her cup of coffee in slow circles without drinking it—a habit she'd picked up from watching her father do the same thing at kitchen tables where bad news lived.

"You want to know why I really bought the debt," she said.

Odile's face did what it always did when Cass mentioned the debt: closed like a hangar door. "You wanted the strip. You've wanted it since flight school."

"I wanted to *beat* you since flight school," Cass said. "That's different. Wanting to beat someone doesn't cost two hundred forty thousand dollars."

"Then what does it cost?"

"Everything." Cass set the cup down before her hands could betray her. "Sit down, Odile. Actually sit down. This is going to take longer than your patience usually lasts."

Odile stood for another moment—the way she always stood, weight forward, ready to be gone—and then she sat, pulling her chair around so that her back was to the wall and she could see both doors. Cass almost smiled. Nine years of flying into the interior and the woman still checked her exits like a courier running contraband.

*Maybe she is,* Cass thought. *Maybe we all are.*

* * *

It had started, if she was honest about the beginning, eleven months ago in a hotel bar in the provincial capital—a bar too clean for the city around it, all brass and dark wood and men whose suits cost more than Cass's first airplane. She'd been there to drink alone after losing a contract she'd needed badly, and a man had sat down next to her uninvited, ordered her exactly what she'd been drinking, and said:

"Cassandra Merrow. The woman who out-flew Dunmore at Verrall Creek."

"I didn't out-fly anyone. I got there first because my plane was lighter."

"That's not the story people tell." He'd smiled like a man who collected stories and kept only the useful ones. His name, eventually, was Mr. Renner. He had no card. Men like him never did; cards were evidence.

He'd talked for twenty minutes about nothing—weather, fuel prices, the dying economics of bush aviation—and then, standing to leave, he'd dropped the hook as casually as a man tossing a fish head off a dock.

"The Escarpment airstrip carries a lot of debt. Debt gets sold. Someone should buy it who knows how to fly."

She'd thought about it for four days. That was the part that still shamed her—not that she'd taken the money, but how little persuasion it took. Her own operation was bleeding out. Two planes, one airworthy. A lease on a hangar she couldn't afford past spring. And here was a stranger offering to make her the owner of the one airstrip she'd coveted since she was nineteen years old and watched Odile Dunmore land a load through fog so thick the tower swore it was impossible.

Renner's terms had seemed simple. Buy the debt. Call it. Force the Rivergate resupply contract onto the books—specifically, onto Odile Dunmore's books. Then fly the route yourself, twice, and report back.

"Report back what?" Cass had asked.

"Whatever you see up there," Renner said. "Roads. Equipment. Who comes and goes. Rivergate's been cut off nine years and nobody asks why. Curious people ask why. We're curious people."

"We."

He'd smiled again and slid an envelope across the table, thick as a Bible, and she'd hated herself even as she took it, and she'd taken it anyway.

* * *

"So let me get this straight." Odile's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a pilot reading back a clearance she didn't trust. "Someone paid you—an amount you won't say—to buy out Halvard's note on the strip—"

"Not Halvard's note. That's what I thought at first. It isn't." Cass leaned forward. "Odile. Listen to me. The debt wasn't Halvard's. Halvard Group *sold* it. Sold it to a shell, the shell sold it to me, and Renner paid me to do the buying. Three hands between them and us, and every hand wearing a glove."

"Why would they sell their own leverage? They owned the strip. They owned *us*."

"Because owning a strip is worth pennies." Cass tapped the table once, hard. "Owning a *pilot* is worth everything. They didn't want the airstrip, Odile. They wanted you flying north. They wanted the contract forced, specifically, in writing, with your name on the flight plan. When I called the debt, the terms weren't mine—I tried to write them myself and Renner's lawyers rewrote them overnight. One clause mattered. One. *Rivergate resupply, primary pilot of record: O. Dunmore.* Everything else was negotiable. That wasn't."

The silence stretched. Outside, the rain eased from a hammering to a long steady hiss, like the sky catching its breath.

Odile had gone very still. Cass knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

"They named me," Odile said quietly. "In the contract."

"In the contract. Before I ever walked into your office. Before your father's ledger was even cold." Cass watched her take that blow—it landed visibly, somewhere behind the ribs—and made herself keep going, because stopping now would be mercy, and mercy now would get them both killed. "I told myself it was spite. I told myself they knew the rivalry would sell it, that forcing you to fly Rivergate was just a rich man's idea of a joke. But then I flew the first leg, and I started looking at what they actually wanted reported. Not weather. Not loads. Roads, Odile. Equipment movements. And a specific question, verbatim, that Renner asked me on the phone last month: *has anyone been up to the old survey markers above the pass?*"

Odile's eyes came up fast. "Survey markers."

"There's a survey of that escarpment older than either of us. Done by a man named Dunmore." Cass held her gaze. "Your father's. And somebody very expensive doesn't want it read."

* * *

She'd flown the staging runs to Kettle Falls twice now, and both times she'd done what Renner asked and more than he knew. She'd photographed the roadhead at Miller's Cut and seen graders that belonged to no road crew she recognized. She'd counted fuel drums—far too many drums for a town of four hundred that supposedly had winter stores already cached. She'd watched a helicopter, unmarked, gray-green, lift out of the valley above the pass at dusk and vanish south, flying low enough to stay under every radio horizon, and she'd written down its tail configuration in her kneeboard and lied to Renner about seeing anything at all.

That was the night she understood she wasn't working an investigation. She was inside one. The difference, she'd realized somewhere over the black teeth of the escarpment, was that an investigation has an exit.

"You're telling me," Odile said slowly, "that you took money from these people, forced me onto a route that killed my brother, and now you're confessing because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, worse: "Because you found out it's real. Because it stopped being a job."

"No." Cass shook her head. "I'm confessing because I ran the numbers and I don't survive them. Renner pays well and Renner's principals leave no loose ends—that's not paranoia, that's pattern. Ask yourself why Rivergate's been dark nine years. Weather doesn't last nine years. Grief does, but grief doesn't grade roads. Somebody wants that valley empty or desperate, and somebody used me to put you in the cockpit because if the Dunmore daughter dies on that pass, it reads as history repeating. No investigation. No questions. Just tragedy, the kind people look away from."

"And if I live?"

"Then you fly their supplies and keep the town alive just enough to stay cheap, and you never talk, because talking means admitting what your family's silence covered." Cass spread her hands. "Either way, we're the deniable part. We're the gloves."

Odile was quiet for a long time. The stove ticked. The dog behind the wall whimpered and went silent.

When she spoke, her voice had changed—stripped of the armor, and all the more dangerous for it. "My father blacked out the Rivergate pages in his ledger. Tore the maintenance logs. He swore off that route on his knees, Cass. I watched him swear it. If someone wanted that route flown, they needed someone who could force it past a dead man's oath." Her jaw tightened. "They needed his daughter. Or his daughter's enemy."

"Or both," Cass said. "Maybe the rivalry wasn't a bonus. Maybe it was the design. Make sure the two pilots most likely to watch each other can't stand each other. Divided, we audit nothing."

Something moved through Odile's face then—something old and tired and furious—and Cass recognized it because she'd been carrying the same thing for weeks: the sick vertigo of realizing your worst assumptions were installed in you by someone else. Every snub she'd catalogued, every cold shoulder, every year of keeping score—how much of it had she built herself, and how much had been handed to her, pre-fabricated, by men who profited from two women too busy hating each other to look up?

"I flew against you for nine years," Odile said, "because beating you was the only thing that felt like mine."

"I know," Cass said. "I think that's what they counted on."

* * *

There was one more thing, and it was the hardest one, and Cass made herself say it plainly because Odile deserved plainness even if plainness ended everything between them.

"The sabotage," she said. "Wren found drained hydraulics and a filed tie-rod on the cargo plane. I know about it because I've been checking my own ship the same way since Kettle Falls. Here's what I need you to hear: it wasn't me. And I don't think it was Renner's people either—at least not the way you'd think. Sabotage that fails quietly, that gets caught in pre-flight, that grounds you for a day and makes you paranoid? That's pressure. That's a message to whoever's watching that the operation is fragile. They may want the flight to fail *visibly*. A crash raises questions. A breakdown delays you into the monsoon, and the monsoon buries everything for six months."

"You've thought about this a lot."

"I've had nothing else to think about at altitude." Cass finally drank her coffee. It was cold and tasted like the bottom of the pot, and she was grateful for it, because it gave her mouth something to do besides shake. "Here's where I am, Odile. I can call Renner tomorrow, report what he wants, collect the rest of the payment, and spend the rest of my life knowing what I sold. Or I can go dark, and we find out together who's grading roads above a town full of people who trusted your father. Those are the options. I've done my arithmetic."

"And if I say I don't trust you? If I say this is the longest con ever run, that the confession itself is the play?"

Cass nodded slowly. She'd expected the question. She'd earned the question.

"Then test me," she said. "Take the survey your father's friend gave you—I know about the map, Odile, I saw the surveyor's face when he looked at you, and I've been in this game long enough to know what recognition costs—and don't tell me what's on it. Fly the leg tomorrow. Watch what I do. If I'm theirs, I'll steer you wrong, and you'll know by the third waypoint. If I'm not..." She shrugged, and it cost her more than she let show. "If I'm not, then we're the only two people in the world who know the shape of this trap, and we'd better learn each other's language fast, because the monsoon's making landfall early and neither of us flies alone anymore."

Outside, the rain had stopped altogether. In the new silence they could both hear it: far to the north, faint under the wind, the river running high and loud, carrying snowmelt down from the escarpment like a message neither of them could yet read.

Odile stood. For a moment Cass thought she'd leave without a word, and braced for it.

Instead, Odile reached into her jacket and laid something on the table between them: a folded sheet of drafting paper, soft with age, corners worn round by handling. Old ink. Older mountains drawn in section lines. And along the upper edge of the escarpment, in a hand Cass didn't recognize—younger than the surveyor's, quicker, confident—a penciled corridor marked through terrain no sane pilot would cross, annotated in small block letters:

*T. DUNMORE — FLOW CHECKS — IT HOLDS.*

"My brother drew that," Odile said. "Nine years ago. Three days before he died on it."

Cass looked at the map, and then at the woman holding it, and understood that whatever came next, neither of them was flying solo again.

"Then we'd better find out," Cass said quietly, "what Theo knew that got him killed."
