## 13. The Escarpment Approach

The weather held for three days, which in the high country was less a mercy than a loan.

Odile spent those days the way she spent all waiting—not resting but rehearsing. She walked the escarpment route on paper until she could fly it with her eyes closed: out of Kettle Falls on a heading of 341, climb through the river valley's cold sink to nine thousand feet, thread the notch between Mount Corben and the shoulder of gray rock the surveyors had never bothered to name, then drop into the long green throat of the Rivergate valley. Forty-one minutes of flying. Nine years of not doing it.

She knew every number by heart because her father had made her memorize them when she was nineteen, before she'd ever soloed a loaded plane, standing in the Escarpment hangar with his finger on the chart like a man pointing at a scar.

"Wind over the notch comes down, not across," he'd said. "The mountain doesn't push you away, Odile. It pulls you in. Respect that or it will take the decision out of your hands."

She had respected it for nine years by never going near it.

Now, on the fourth morning, with the barometer falling and Wren's voice on the radio reading her the Kettle Falls altimeter setting one more time than necessary, she rolled the old cargo plane onto the strip and let the mountain have its chance.

* * *

The first twenty minutes were kind.

Kettle Falls fell away beneath her in a wash of gold—larches turning, smoke from the sawmill rising straight up in the windless cold, the river braiding itself silver through the flats. The plane was heavy: flour, diesel, batteries, a crate of vaccines packed in dry ice that Mara Voss at Rivergate had begged for by name. Two thousand pounds over gross would have been a crime; two hundred under was a prayer. The engine pulled it all without complaint, and Odile found herself almost grateful to the machine, the way you're grateful to a friend who doesn't ask why your hands are shaking.

Cass was somewhere behind her, thirty miles south, flying the second load in the other plane. They'd agreed on the radio last night with a formality that fooled neither of them: Cass would hold at the falls until Odile called the pass open or closed. If it was open, Cass would come up behind her. If it was closed—

If it was closed, they'd both go home and do arithmetic about how many more windows the season would offer, and neither of them would say aloud what the arithmetic was really counting.

"Escarpment One, Kettle Falls," the tower operator said, though Kettle Falls barely deserved the word *tower*—a shack, a man named Denny, a radio older than Odile. "Winds aloft light and variable. Corben reporting clear."

"Escarpment One, roger. Going up the valley."

"Good luck," Denny said, and then, as if hearing himself, "I mean—good flying."

There was no luck in it, Odile thought. That was the lie she'd told herself for nine years, and she felt it come apart now the way fog comes apart, in rags, revealing exactly what she'd been avoiding. There was no luck in flying. There was only skill, and weather, and the small space between them where people lived or didn't. Theo had been skilled. The weather had been weather. And the space between them had closed in four seconds on a Tuesday morning in October, and she had been on the ground at Escarpment, fueling another plane, when the radio went quiet.

The valley narrowed. She began to climb.

* * *

At six thousand feet the air changed texture.

Anyone who hadn't flown the interior would have missed it—the way the smooth morning lift turned grainy, then rough, the way the light off the snowfields above took on that hard mineral glare. But Odile's body knew it the way a sailor's legs know a swell coming before his eyes find it. She was entering the mountain's weather, the private climate the escarpment made for itself, and everything from here to the notch would be argument.

She checked her watch. Eleven minutes to the notch at this rate of climb. She keyed the radio mostly to hear a human voice.

"Cass, you copying?"

Static, then Cass's voice, thin and clear and infuriatingly calm. "Loud and clear, Dunmore. How's it look ahead?"

"Grainy at six thousand. No buildups yet."

"Yet," Cass repeated. "Poetry."

"I learned from watching you file flight plans."

A pause, and then Cass laughed—a short, real laugh, the first one Odile could remember hearing from her in years. "Call me at the notch," Cass said. "Out."

The valley walls rose around her, granite streaked with old avalanche chutes like tear tracks down a face. The river below shrank to a white thread. She was alone with the sound of the engine and her own breathing, and her breathing was wrong—too shallow, too fast—and she made herself slow it, made herself run the checklist again not because anything needed checking but because procedure was a handrail and she needed something to hold.

Fuel: sufficient. Mixture: rich. Carb heat: as required. Seatbelt: snug.

Brother: dead nine years this October.

That wasn't on the checklist. It never left the checklist anyway.

* * *

At eight thousand feet she saw the notch for the first time in nearly a decade, and it looked exactly the same, which felt like an obscenity.

Two walls of rock with a slot of sky between them, maybe half a mile wide, maybe less—the charts said three-quarters but charts were drawn by optimists. Snow on the upper shoulders, sun on the eastern face, shadow pooling blue-black in the western cleft. From here, at this distance, it looked almost gentle. A door standing open in the wall of the world.

Her father had flown that door four hundred times. Theo had flown it—she'd never known how many times; the logbook pages were gone, torn out, and Wren had found the gaps and said nothing, and she had loved him a little more for the silence.

And somewhere in that slot of sky, at a point she could have marked on a chart if she'd ever let herself mark it, a Cessna 180 with her brother's laughing voice on the radio had stopped transmitting mid-sentence.

*"—corridor looks good, Dad, I think I've got—"*

That was all. That was the whole recording. Her father had kept the tape in the hangar office for years, and once, drunk on the anniversary, he had played it for her, and she had heard her brother's voice stop the way a heart stops, and she had understood finally why her father never flew north again, why he drank, why he died with a ledger full of debts and a blacked-out line in his route book where RIVERGATE used to be written in his own square hand.

*"Corridor looks good."*

He'd been mapping it. Everyone in Escarpment said Theo died showing off, pushing weather, flying the impossible pass to prove something—but she'd been in the office that night, she'd heard the tape, and there had been no bravado in his voice at all. Only work. Only the flat, focused calm of a pilot doing a job. Whatever he'd been doing up there, he'd believed it was safe.

The memory hit her at eight thousand two hundred feet, and the downdraft hit her at eight thousand five hundred, and for one bad second she couldn't tell them apart.

It came off the eastern shoulder without warning—the mountain pulling the air down its flank the way her father had warned, gravity given a runway of rock. The nose dropped. The altimeter unwound—eight thousand, seventy-five hundred, seven thousand—and the trees on the valley floor swelled toward her with terrible patience, and Odile did what her hands had been trained to do while the rest of her was still nine years away and ten years old inside: full power, nose to the horizon, carb heat, wings level, fly the airplane.

The plane flew. That was the bargain of machines—they obey even when you don't deserve obedience. At sixty-five hundred feet the sink released her, spat her back into merely turbulent air, and she climbed again with the engine roaring and her heart slamming against her sternum like something trying to get out.

"Escarpment One," she said on the radio, and was surprised her voice came out level. "Hit significant sink off the east shoulder. Recovered at sixty-five hundred."

Static. Then Cass, closer now, must be close: "Copy. Coming up behind you. Your call, Odile."

Your call. As if it were a decision about weather.

* * *

She flew on. Of course she flew on. Turning back now, with the notch in sight, with Cass behind her and medicine in the back and a whole winter closing like a door—that would be the end of something she couldn't afford to end. She climbed back to eight thousand, then eighty-five hundred, giving herself margin, and the notch grew in the windshield until it filled it, and the wind across the saddle streamed snow off the eastern peak in a long white banner pointing away from her, which was good, which meant the wind was crossing, not pouring, which meant today the door might be open.

She was at the mouth of the notch at 10:42 in the morning.

She knew the time because she wrote it down. She always wrote down the times that mattered; it was a habit from her father, who believed that a pilot's logbook was a form of testimony. Later she would understand that 10:42 was the exact minute, to the minute, that the tower at Escarpment had logged Theo's last transmission nine years ago. She didn't know it yet. Some part of her knew it anyway. Some part of her had always known it, the way a body knows a room where something happened, and that part of her spoke now, not in words but in weight, settling over her chest like ballast as she crossed the threshold of rock and the walls closed in on either side.

Half a mile wide. The sun went behind the western wall and the world turned the color of iron. The air in the notch was still—unnaturally still, holding its breath—and the engine noise flattened against the rock and came back at her doubled, her own sound thrown in her face.

She was through the worst of it. That was the thing. She was actually through it—the saddle was ahead, she could see the far side dropping away, the green country beyond, the valley of the Rivergate opening like a cupped hand. Another ninety seconds and she'd be over, descending into light, done, the first person from Escarpment to make this crossing since her father swore it off with his hand on her shoulder at the funeral.

Ninety seconds.

And then she saw it.

Not the mine—she didn't know about the mine yet, wouldn't know for weeks. What she saw was smaller than that and worse. On the eastern wall of the notch, just below the saddle line, at the precise altitude she was flying, there was a scorch of broken timber and pale rock where a slide had come down—old, years old, healed over with grey lichen, but shaped like a wound. Shaped, her mind supplied helpfully, like the debris fan they'd photographed from the search planes, the one they'd shown her father when they told him there was nothing to recover, that the mountain had kept what it taken.

She was looking at the place. Not near it. Not in the neighborhood of it. She was at the coordinates—her own body, her own hands on her own yoke, at the exact point in the sky where her brother had stopped existing, and the knowledge arrived not as thought but as physics, a force with direction and mass, pressing her down into her seat, narrowing her vision to a tunnel with the scorch mark at the center of it.

*"Corridor looks good, Dad, I think I've got—"*

The plane drifted. She felt it drift—ten degrees right, fifteen, her hands loose on the wheel, her feet slack on the rudders, the aircraft wandering off the centerline of the notch the way a sleepwalker wanders toward a stairwell. The western wall slid closer in her peripheral vision. Somewhere very far away, the disciplined pilot who had flown nine thousand hours without a scratch was screaming at her to correct, to climb, to fly the airplane, and she could hear the screaming perfectly and could not move.

The mountain pulled. It always pulls. Her father told her that.

What broke the spell was the vaccine crate shifting in the back—a soft wooden knock, the dry ice box sliding an inch against the tie-down, the small ordinary sound of cargo doing what cargo does. It reached her like a hand on the shoulder from behind. Someone was counting on this airplane. Someone in a fever ward she'd never seen was counting on the cold box in the back, and the woman flying it had frozen at the door of the sky like a child at a dark hallway.

Odile corrected. Hard. Wings level, nose centered, climbing, the walls falling away on either side as she hauled the plane back to the middle of the notch—and then, with the saddle fifty feet above her and the green country visible past it, close enough to touch, close enough to *make*, she banked left and turned around.

She didn't decide to. She would say later, honestly, that she didn't decide anything. Her hands simply did the thing they had done at every emergency for nine years, the thing they knew better than her heart did: they got her out. She turned in the notch with maybe two hundred feet of clearance on each wingtip, dove for the valley floor to get out of the sink zone, and ran south down the river valley with the throttle forward and the mountain shrinking in her mirrors and tears freezing in the corners of her eyes in the wind through the vent, and she did not wipe them, because wiping them would have meant admitting they were there, and she was still flying the airplane.

* * *

She landed at Kettle Falls at 11:31 and sat on the strip with the engine running until Denny waved her off the active, then taxied in and shut down and did not get out.

Cass landed fourteen minutes later. Odile watched her through the windshield—watched the other woman's plane touch down smooth and fast, roll out, taxi up beside hers, and stop. For a long moment neither of them moved. Two airplanes parked nose to tail on the gravel, engines ticking as they cooled, two pilots sitting in their separate silences with a mountain between them and a mountain behind them.

Then Cass dropped out of her cockpit, walked over, and stood at Odile's wing root. She didn't open the door. She just stood there, arms folded against the cold, looking at the scorch-marked east wall of the escarpment, visible even from here, forty miles south, a grey smudge on the skyline.

"My turn tomorrow," Cass said through the glass.

Odile shook her head. "You don't know what it does to you up there."

"No," Cass agreed. "But I know what it's doing to you down here, and it's worse." She unfolded her arms. "Nine years is a long time to pay interest on a debt nobody can collect, Odile. Tomorrow I fly lead. You follow me through, or you don't. Either way, the medicine goes."

"You don't owe me that."

"No," Cass said, and something moved behind her eyes, some ledger of her own opening briefly and shutting. "But somebody does. Get some sleep."

She walked away toward the shack and the coffee and whatever passed for a bed in Kettle Falls, and Odile sat alone in the cooling plane as the afternoon light went long and gold and then grey, and she was still sitting there, still not asleep, when Denny came out of the shack waving his hat and shouting her name across the gravel, saying she'd better come quick, come quick, there was a station trying to raise her on the HF, weak signal, breaking up, calling from Rivergate.
