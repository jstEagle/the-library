## 14. Rivergate on the Radio

The turnback sat in Odile like a stone swallowed whole.

She had flown the leg back to Kettle Falls in silence, hands moving through the shutdown checks with the mechanical grace of someone whose body had stopped consulting her, and when the props finally wound down to stillness she stayed in the pilot's seat a long moment, watching condensation crawl across the windscreen, feeling the airframe tick as it cooled around her. Somewhere behind her, Cass was climbing out of the second plane, and somewhere ahead of her was the mess hall, and somewhere beyond that was the question everyone would be too polite to ask aloud: *how close did you get?*

Close enough. Close enough that she had seen the pass itself—a black notch in the escarpment's shoulder, wearing its first snow like a fresh bandage—and close enough that the downdraft had taken the yoke out of her grip for two full seconds, two seconds in which the mountain had held her life loosely in its palm and she had felt, with terrible clarity, exactly what Theo must have felt in his last ninety seconds of sky.

She hadn't told anyone that part.

* * *

Kettle Falls in late afternoon was a town built for transience: a gravel strip, a fuel farm, a mess hall that smelled permanently of diesel and fryer oil, a bunkhouse where the beds were measured in nights rather than years. The locals called it the Last Dry Sock before the North, and they said it without affection. Everything here was a threshold. Nobody's boots came off.

Odile took her supper standing at the counter because sitting meant conversation, and conversation meant questions. The cook, a broad woman named Etta who had fed pilots for thirty years and trusted none of them, slid a plate across without comment—beans, flatbread, something with onions doing its best to be stew. Odile ate half of it and carried the rest outside.

The evening had gone the color of pewter. To the north, the escarpment stood against the horizon like a wall someone had raised between the world and whatever lay behind it, and the clouds were already stacking on its flanks, gray on gray on gray, building toward something. Monsoon season didn't arrive so much as assemble. You could watch it happening the way you watched a debt come due.

Wren found her at the fuel drums, where she'd gone ostensibly to check the tie-downs and actually to stand somewhere her hands had honest work.

"You turned at the notch," Wren said. Not an accusation. Wren never accused; Wren observed, the way a good mechanic observed, cataloguing the sound an engine made before it failed.

"Two miles short."

"I know. I listened." Wren leaned a hip against the drum, arms crossed, grease worked so deep into the knuckle creases it looked like tattooing. "You flew it clean, Odie. The radio chatter—the Kettle Falls guys were impressed. Even Cass said—"

"Cass can say whatever she likes."

"She said you flew it cleaner than your father ever did."

That landed strangely, somewhere between gift and wound, and Odile looked away toward the mountains because it was easier than letting Wren see her face do whatever it was doing. Nine years of practice at being unreadable, undone by one sentence from a mechanic who knew her engines better than her expressions.

"The route doesn't care how clean you fly," she said finally. "It cares whether the mountain is in a mood. And the mountain—" She gestured at the cloud stack building on the escarpment, slow and monumental as a cathedral going up. "The mountain is getting into a mood."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, carefully, in the tone of someone handling a part they suspected was cracked: "You'll try again tomorrow?"

"We have six days. Maybe five now, looking at that sky. We fly until we can't."

"And if you can't?"

Odile picked up the plate, the cold half-stew, the small domestic wreckage of her evening. "Then Rivergate winters on what it has, and we all learn to live with that. Some of us already know how."

She walked away before Wren could answer, which was becoming a habit, and habits like that were how people ended up alone at fifty with nothing but logbooks for company. She knew this. Knowing a thing and not doing it—that was practically the family crest.

* * *

The radio room at Kettle Falls was a converted shipping container bolted to the side of the operations shack, lined with foam that had been peeling since the previous administration, and it belonged to a dispatcher named Hollis who was seventy if he was a day and who treated the frequency the way a priest treats a confessional—with patience, discretion, and a certain weary familiarity with human folly.

Odile went there after dark because she couldn't sleep, and because the bunkhouse ceiling pressed down on her, and because there was a specific comfort in the radio room's amber lamplight and the low murmur of voices threaded through static—the whole northern country whispering to itself through the wires. Bush pilots' lullaby. She'd grown up falling asleep to it through the wall of her father's office.

Hollis nodded her in without surprise. Pilots drifted to the radio room at night the way moths drifted to porch lights; he'd long since stopped asking why.

She wasn't transmitting. She was just listening, headset loose around her neck, one ear in the current of traffic: a charter out of Fort Nelson reporting tops at eleven thousand, a mining camp complaining about their resupply, the weather broadcast cycling through its litany of fronts and freezing levels. Routine. The ordinary hum of a world that mostly worked.

Then, under it, faint as a voice heard through a floor:

"—any station, any station, this is Rivergate community net, do you copy—"

Odile went still.

Rivergate didn't call out. That was the thing. In nine years, the valley had been a hole in the map, a silence on the frequency, a place that existed only in freight manifests nobody filled and in the blacked-out pages of her father's ledger. Towns cut off by weather still called out—they begged, they bargained, they listed their needs into the static on the off chance some passing aircraft might relay them. Rivergate had never done even that. Whatever pride or policy kept the valley quiet, it had kept it quiet through nine winters.

Until tonight.

"—any station, this is Rivergate. We are reading you weak but readable. If any station copies, please respond. Please." A pause full of static, and then the voice again, and this time Odile caught the texture of it: a woman, young-ish, worn thin, speaking with the careful over-enunciation of someone who has rehearsed composure and is losing the rehearsal. "This is Mara Voss, Rivergate health post. Requesting relay to any provincial authority. Our medicine stores are gone."

*Gone.* Not depleted. Not running low. Gone.

Odile's hand found the transmit key without her deciding anything. She didn't press it. Her heart was pressing it hard enough for both of them.

Hollis had swiveled in his chair, eyes sharp over his spectacles. He'd heard it too. He reached for his own set, but Odile was faster—keyed up, voice level by some miracle of muscle memory.

"Station calling, this is aircraft Dunmore, Kettle Falls. I read you. Say again your situation."

Static bloomed and collapsed. Then, closer now, clearer, as though the woman on the far end had physically leaned toward her set: "Dunmore." A breath. Something moved through the name—recognition, or dread, or both. "Are you—are you *the* Dunmore? Escarpment?"

The question hung there. Odile looked at the transmit key. Nine years of being no one's daughter, no one's sister, just a competent stranger who landed, unloaded, and left. She could deny it. Denial was a skill she'd maintained better than her instrument rating.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "I'm Odile Dunmore."

Another silence, longer, and when the voice came back it had changed—not warmer, exactly. Rawer. As if the name had stripped something off it.

"Miss Dunmore. My name is Mara Voss. I run the health post in Rivergate. I need you to write this down, because our transmitter is old and I don't know how many more times I can hold this connection." Papers rustled across nine hundred kilometers of wilderness. "Three weeks ago our storage cellar flooded. Freeze-thaw, spring melt backing up through the foundation—we lost nearly everything. Insulin, antibiotics, the antipyretics, the whole cold chain. We requisitioned replacement through the regional office in March. They confirmed the order. It never came. When we inquired again, we were told the road was closed and air service to Rivergate was, quote, suspended pending review."

"Suspended by whom?" Odile asked, though she already felt the answer coming the way you feel a downdraft before the altimeter shows it.

"That's what I'd like to know. There's a company that's been very helpful to us since the disaster—the Halvard Group, they bought up the damaged properties, they've been sending engineers, making promises about rebuilding the road. Everyone assumed they were arranging the supply runs too. But last month a man from Halvard came through and told the council that outside medical resupply was, quote, no longer economically viable for the valley, and that relocation assistance was available for any family willing to sell." The voice wavered, steadied. "We have four hundred people here, Miss Dunmore. And ten days ago the fever started."

"Fever. What kind?"

"They're calling it flu in the council minutes. It isn't flu. It started in the children near the old mill quarter and it's spreading house to house. High fevers, four, five days, then pneumonia in maybe one in five. I've got eleven patients in my six-bed ward. Two dead—one of them was seven years old." The words came out flat and precise, each one placed like a stone in a wall, and Odile understood suddenly that this woman had been building this report alone in the dark for days, saying it to a dead frequency, waiting for anyone at all. "And the winter road closed early this year. Snowed shut at the divide three weeks ago. Nobody is walking out. Nobody is walking in."

Outside the container, the wind had come up, worrying the guy-wires of the antenna mast with a low plucked-note sound. Inside, Odile became aware that Hollis had pulled the logbook toward himself and was writing, timestamping, doing the bureaucratic work that made things real. Good man. Someone had to make it real while she did the other thing, the harder thing, which was simply to keep listening.

"Mara." First names seemed suddenly necessary; formality felt obscene across that much distance and that much grief. "Mara, listen to me. We have supplies staged here at Kettle Falls—general freight, but there's medicine in the manifest, antibiotics, broad-spectrum. We were contracted for your winter resupply. We attempted the pass today and turned back at the notch—weather. But we will try again. I give you my word we will try again."

"Aircraft Dunmore." A pause, and then, quietly, with something underneath it that Odile couldn't name at first and then could: it was fear. Not of the fever. Of her. "Your father swore off this route. After the crash. After your brother. Half this town remembers that oath. Some of them—" The transmission crackled, recovered. "Some of them blame him. For flying that day at all. For what the investigation did afterward. I'm telling you this because you should hear it from me and not from the council chamber."

"I know," Odile said. "I've known for nine years."

"Then you know more than we do about why the stores stopped coming." Static surged, and through it the nurse's voice came fast, urgent, a woman spending her signal like her last coin: "Miss Dunmore, I'm going to say something I shouldn't say on an open frequency, and then I'm going to sign off because the generator fuel won't last the hour. The flood in our cellar—it didn't happen the way the report says. The drainage was altered. Cut, not clogged. Cut from above, from the mine road. I walked it myself in April and I saw the cuts. Somebody wanted our stores ruined, and somebody wanted this valley sick enough to sell and well enough to work. I don't know who. But your family knew these mountains better than anyone alive, and if anyone can find out who's been doing this to us—" The signal sagged. "Come north, Miss Dunmore. Bring what you can carry. And bring the truth, if you've got room for it beside the cargo."

The carrier wave thinned to a whistle, then to nothing. The frequency settled back into its ordinary murmur—charters, weather, the mining camp still complaining—as though Rivergate had never spoken at all.

Odile sat with the headset ringing faintly against her skull. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking, and she folded them in her lap like a child taught to sit still in church.

*A seven-year-old.* Theo had been twenty-six. He'd had twenty-six years of sky and river and laughter, and the town had buried him in absentia, in effigy, in anger, and now there was a grave in Rivergate with a child in it who had died of something as ordinary as pneumonia while men in offices three hundred miles south decided her valley was no longer economically viable.

*Duty arriving as a human voice.* All these years the Rivergate route had been an abstraction—a blacked-out line in a ledger, an oath, a wound shaped like a coordinate. Now it was a tired woman named Mara Voss counting beds she didn't have, and the abstraction was dead, and good riddance, and God help her, the weight of the real thing was so much worse.

"Log entry's made," Hollis said gruffly. He pushed a mug of coffee across the desk—black, terrible, offered like communion. "Timestamped, word for word as best I could take it. That's a legal record now, girl. Whatever happens next, that call happened, and I heard it, and it's written down."

"Thank you, Hollis."

"Don't thank me." He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes, and for a moment he looked every one of his seventy years. "I flew relief into that valley once, back before the disaster. Finest little town on the north slope. Somebody's been starving it on purpose, and everybody on this strip knows it and nobody says it." He put the spectacles back on and met her eyes. "You going to say it?"

Odile looked at the coffee, at the logbook, at the dark window where the escarpment's cloud-cathedral was still under construction, lit from within now by the occasional silent flicker of heat lightning.

"I'm going to do more than say it."

* * *

She found Cass in the mess hall, alone at the corner table with a chart spread out and a bottle of the local rye standing open beside it like a paperweight. Cass looked up, read Odile's face in the way rival pilots learn to read weather—fast, total, no wasted motion—and pushed the chart flat with both palms.

"What happened?"

"Rivergate broke radio silence tonight." Odile sat down across from her. "Their medicine stores are destroyed. Deliberately, by the look of it. There's a fever spreading, the winter road's shut, and a company called Halvard has been telling them air service is suspended." She laid it out flat, fact by fact, the way Mara Voss had given it to her, because the facts were strong enough and dressing them would only weaken them. "They're dying up there, Cass. Slowly, quietly, on schedule. And we're the only aircraft on this side of the range."

Cass was very still. Then she reached for the rye, poured two fingers into each of the tin cups on the table, and slid one across.

"Halvard," she repeated. The name came out of her oddly—carefully, the way you handle a wire you suspect is live. She stared into her cup for a long moment, and Odile watched something move behind her face, some calculation arriving at a sum it didn't like. "Odile. Tomorrow. Before we load. Before anyone else hears that transmission. You and I need to talk. Not here."

"Why not here?"

Cass lifted her eyes, and for the first time in nine years of rivalry, Odile saw fear in them—fear, and beneath it, unmistakably, guilt.

"Because the man who paid me to force this contract," Cass said quietly, "signed his letters *H.*"

Outside, the wind rose, and the first rain of the monsoon began to fall on the tin roof of the mess hall like applause for something terrible about to start.
