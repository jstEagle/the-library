## 15. Grounded

The paperwork was the first thing Wren Adeyemi checked every morning, the way other people checked the sky.

She'd learned it from her mother, who'd kept books for a fishing cooperative in Lagos and said that every disaster she'd ever witnessed had announced itself in advance—in a column of numbers that didn't add, in a signature where no signature should be. *The paper always knows first,* her mother used to say, tapping the ledger with one blunt finger. *People lie with their mouths and their faces and their whole shining hearts. Paper only lies on purpose.*

So Wren stood in the Kettle Falls operations shack at six-forty in the morning, coffee going cold in her hand, reading the airworthiness file for the *Merrow's Luck*—Cass's old cargo bird, now pressed into service hauling Rivergate's winter stores—and feeling the particular cold that comes up through the soles of your boots when you find something that has been done to you rather than by you.

The weight-and-balance sheet was wrong.

Not sloppy-wrong. Not tired-pilot-at-the-end-of-a-long-day wrong, which Wren could forgive, because she'd been that pilot more than once. This was wrong in the way a forged painting is wrong: every stroke correct, every proportion plausible, and the whole thing false. The cargo manifest listed twelve hundred pounds of dried goods and fuel drums distributed across stations three through seven. The actual load they'd flown north two days ago—Wren had weighed it herself, had watched it come off the truck at Escarpment, had argued with the loader about the drum placement for twenty minutes because twenty minutes was what it took to do it right—had been fourteen hundred, weighted aft.

Someone had rebuilt the document. Someone who understood aircraft well enough to make the forgery flyable-looking, and understood bureaucracy well enough to know exactly which form would matter if anyone ever looked.

And someone had signed it with her initials.

*W.A.* In blue ballpoint, in a hand that was almost hers—the slant right, the loop on the A closed too tight, but close. Close enough for an inspector who didn't know her. Close enough for a lawyer.

Wren set the coffee down very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to let them, and went through the rest of the file the way she'd go through an engine after a prop strike: systematically, without hope of finding nothing.

The annual inspection certificate was current—she'd done it herself in June, and it was genuine. The hydraulic service record showed the flush she'd performed at Escarpment before the first leg north, which meant whoever built this forgery had access to their real maintenance logs. The tie-rod entry—the filed tie-rod she'd found three weeks ago, the one she'd replaced at two in the morning and told no one about, not even Odile, because she'd told herself it might have been vibration fatigue, might have been metal doing what tired metal does—was recorded as *inspected, serviceable.*

Which meant the paper wasn't just covering a falsified load sheet. The paper was establishing a history. A pattern. *This aircraft has been maintained negligently by its mechanic, who signs documents without performing the work.* If anyone ever pulled the maintenance records—and someone would, someone had arranged for someone to—they'd find a mechanic whose initials were all over a plane that should never have been in the air.

Her. They were building the case against her out of her own name.

Wren stood in the doorway of the operations shack and looked out at the strip. Kettle Falls in the early light was all gravel and diesel smoke and the smell of the river coming up off the flats, mist burning off the water in long gray ribbons. The *Merrow's Luck* sat at the fuel island, nose into the wind, dew silvering the wings. Beyond it, Odile's plane—the *Dunmore*, the old girl, forty years of patched skin and honest dents—was tied down with fresh chocks, loaded for today's run into the valley. Odile had preflighted her before dawn. Wren had watched her do it from the bunkhouse window, moving around the aircraft in the dark with a flashlight held in her teeth, hands finding the surfaces by memory, and had thought, as she thought most mornings: *there is the truest thing I know.*

They were going to take it away from her. Whatever *it* turned out to be—the planes, the route, the license, the name. Someone was dismantling them on paper first, the way you'd dismantle an engine before stealing it, and they'd started with the parts nobody sees.

Wren picked up the phone and called the district aviation office, because that was the other thing her mother taught her: when you find the lie, walk toward it. Liars count on you circling.

* * *

The inspector arrived at nine, and he arrived with a folder, and Wren knew before he opened his mouth.

His name was Prosser. She'd met him twice before, at annual inspections down in the capital, and he'd seemed like a decent man then—methodical, humorless, the kind of bureaucrat who took genuine comfort in regulations because regulations were the only thing between small airplanes and the ground. He shook her hand now with a formality that hadn't been there in June, and he didn't meet her eyes, and Wren understood that whatever was in his folder, he didn't like carrying it.

"Miss Adeyemi. I'm afraid I've got a complaint to act on."

"About?"

"Documentation irregularities on CF-MRK." He said the registration the way priests say names of the dead. "Falsified weight-and-balance data. Maintenance entries signed but not performed. And—" he paused, and Wren watched him decide whether to say the next part, watched him lose the argument with himself, "—an anonymous report alleging that the operator has been flying commercial loads without valid insurance certification."

"Anonymous," Wren said.

"The complainant's identity is protected under—"

"I know what anonymous means, Mr. Prosser. I'm asking who benefits."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small recoil, as though she'd raised her voice, though she hadn't; she'd made it quieter instead, which her mother also taught her, and which worked better. Around them the strip went on being a strip—a loader shouting at a forklift, gulls arguing over something spilled near the fuel farm—but Wren felt the day contract down to the space between herself and this tired man with his folder full of other people's handwriting.

"Miss Adeyemi, my job isn't—"

"Your job is airworthiness." She said it evenly. "Mine too. So let's do our jobs. You want to inspect the aircraft? Inspect it. Pull a panel, borescope the cylinders, weigh it empty if you want—I'll open the cowling myself and stand there while you work. But you're grounding a plane that's carrying medicine into a valley with a fever in it, based on a piece of paper somebody forged, and I'd like you to look me in the face while you tell me that's what the regulations are for."

Prosser looked at her then. He had gray stubble and eyes like a man who'd spent thirty years finding cracks in things and had stopped being surprised by any of them. He opened the folder, and Wren saw the photocopied load sheet with her own initials sitting on it in borrowed blue ink, and felt something in her chest go hot and narrow.

"The order stands," he said quietly. "Both aircraft. Pending investigation. I'm sorry. For what it's worth—" and here he did something she didn't expect, leaning in, voice dropping below the wind, "—I've read this file twice. The load sheet's dated the fourteenth. The fourteenth was a Sunday, Miss Adeyemi, and Kettle Falls was fogged in till noon. Whoever flew that load couldn't have. Whoever wrote it wasn't here." He straightened, official again, already turning away. "You'll have your hearing within ten days. Don't leave the district."

Then he walked out to the *Merrow's Luck*, and began, methodically, to take her apart.

* * *

Odile found her an hour later in the hangar, sitting on an oil drum with the forged load sheet flattened on her knee, going over it with a magnifying loupe the way she went over everything—slowly, from the edges inward.

"You heard," Wren said.

"Heard? Half the province will have heard by lunch. Cass is on the phone to her lawyers, if you can call that man in Escarpment a lawyer, and Mara's radioed twice asking why the medicine stopped moving." Odile crouched down beside her, forearms on her knees, close enough that Wren could smell avgas and river damp on her jacket. "Tell me what you see."

"Familiar initials."

"Whose?"

"Mine." Wren handed her the sheet. "Look at the A. Closed loop. I don't write it that way—never have, you can check anything I've ever signed. But whoever did this studied my hand. Studied our logs. They knew the drum weights, Odile. They knew the tie-rod entry. That's not a stranger with a grudge. That's someone who's been inside our paperwork for months."

Odile read in silence. Wren watched her face do what it always did—not much, a stillness like deep water, the grief and fury both sinking below the surface where they did their work unseen. But her thumb pressed white against the paper's edge, and Wren knew that pressure. It was the same grip Odile used on the yoke in downdrafts.

"The Halvard Group," Odile said at last.

"You've seen the name?"

"Cass has. On the debt papers. She's been pulling threads since Escarpment—says the money behind her buyout doesn't smell right, says she's been flying this contract to find out who's holding the other end." Odile looked up. "Why are you telling me this instead of her?"

"Because you're the one flying into that valley tomorrow if we get these orders lifted, and because—" Wren stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because someone wants this operation dead, and they're willing to build it out of me. My initials, my inspections, my name on a crash report someday. I need you to understand that when I say I'm going to trace this, I'm not talking about a phone call. I'm talking about following paper until it runs out of places to hide."

"And if it runs out somewhere dangerous?"

"Then I'll know where dangerous lives."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she reached out and took the loupe from Wren's hand, and held it, and didn't give it back—an exchange Wren would think about later, the small theft of it, the intimacy of a tool passed between people who fixed things for a living.

"My father kept ledgers," Odile said. "Everything written down, everything blacked out where it hurt. I used to think the blacking-out was shame. Now I think some of it was evidence." Her eyes came up, and there was something in them Wren hadn't seen before—not the guardedness, not the grief. Aim. "Whatever you find, Wren—you bring it to me before anyone else. Before Cass. Before the lawyers. Promise me."

"Why you?"

"Because I'm the one they actually want." She said it without drama, the way you'd read a weather report. "I'm the Dunmore daughter flying the route her father swore off. I'm the deniable part of this story. If they can break us, it has to happen through me. So I should be the one holding the map."

Wren looked at her—at this woman who had spent nine years making herself invisible, asking now to be aimed at—and felt the thing she never said out loud rise in her throat like warm air off a sunlit runway. She swallowed it. There'd be time. There had to be time.

"Promise," she said.

* * *

The shell company took her four days to find, and she found it the way she found anything: by refusing to stop looking.

Prosser's grounding order bought her the time—both planes chained to the earth, crews idle, the fever in Rivergate climbing according to Mara's nightly radio reports, each one a little thinner than the last. Wren spent those days in the back office of the Kettle Falls hotel, surrounded by borrowed directories and freight manifests and the patient, unglamorous machinery of paper trails. Freight companies registered their aircraft through holding companies; holding companies answered to leasing agents; leasing agents had directors; directors had addresses. Most of it was legitimate. All of it was layered. But fraud was lazy at its roots, because fraudsters got bored, and boredom left fingerprints.

The complaint against the *Merrow's Luck* had been filed through a firm in the capital—Voss & Associates, aviation regulatory counsel, suite address above a shuttered restaurant. Voss & Associates had eleven active clients. Ten were ordinary operators: charter outfits, a flight school, a heli-logging company. The eleventh was a company called **A.R.M. Holdings Ltd.**, incorporated eight months ago, registered agent a law clerk named nobody, share structure a maze with no center.

*A.R.M.*

Wren sat with the name for a long time, the hotel room dark around her, the river muttering below the window. Initials again. The conspiracy liked initials—it had used hers, after all, stamped them onto forgery like a brand. And initials were how men like this thought: small, tidy, deniable. Three letters that could stand for anything, that pointed nowhere, that—

That pointed at **Alaric R. Merrow**.

Cass's father. Dead two years, God rest him, and Wren had known the man—had rebuilt his Cessna's landing gear in '19, had drunk his terrible whiskey, had listened to his stories about the early days on the escarpment routes. Alaric Merrow, who'd owned half the debt paper in the interior before the banks ate him alive. Whose name, attached to a shell company, would mean nothing to a stranger and everything to anyone who knew that Cass Merrow had inherited her father's debts along with his airplanes.

*There it is,* Wren thought, cold settling through her like lake water. *There's the frame. Cass bought the airstrip's debt with money she was given. The money traces to a company wearing her dead father's initials. When this unravels—and it will unravel, that's the design—it unravels onto the Merrows. Cass takes the fall for hiring the pilots, the pilots take the fall for the paperwork, and the Halvard Group walks away owning a valley.*

She thought of Odile's face in the hangar, the aim in it. She thought of Mara's voice on the radio, thinner every night. She thought of the tie-rod, filed clean through, and the man or woman who'd done it standing somewhere in the dark with a hacksaw, patient as erosion.

Wren gathered the pages together, squared their edges on the desk, and bound them with a rubber band the way her mother had bound every ledger she'd ever kept. Then she pulled on her jacket and went out into the rain to find Odile, because a promise was a promise, and because the paper had spoken first, just like her mother always said it would.

What it said was: *they know we know. And they are not finished.*

Behind her, in the hotel office, the phone began to ring—Kettle Falls exchange, local number, someone who'd let it ring eleven times before giving up. On the desk, beneath the lamp, the directory lay open to the page she'd marked days ago and never followed: a listing for a surveyor retired to Kettle Falls, a man who'd once worked the escarpment with a partner named Dunmore.

The rain came down harder. Somewhere north, past the closing passes, a valley waited with its fever and its dead and its winter stores rotting on a grounded airplane, and Wren Adeyemi walked into the storm carrying the only weapon anyone had given her: a rubber-banded stack of paper, and the truth it held, and the name of the woman she was sworn to deliver it to.
