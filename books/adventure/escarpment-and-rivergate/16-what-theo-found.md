## 16. What Theo Found

The surveyor's map smelled of cedar and old smoke, the way paper smells when it has lived a long time in a room where someone burned wood every winter of their life. Odile laid it flat on the table in her room above the Kettle Falls freight office, weighted the corners with four coffee mugs, and turned the lamp down low so the pencil lines would show. Old graphite hid in lamplight. It came alive in shadow.

Her father had drawn this map twenty-two years ago, before she was born, before Theo could walk. She knew his hand the way she knew her own heartbeat—the way he lettered *N* with that little serif tail, the way he drew contour lines in pairs like he was braiding them, the way elevation numbers always sat slightly left of their line as if shy of commitment. Dunmore surveys. Two generations of them. Her grandfather had walked these ranges with a brass transit on a tripod; her father had done it with a theodolite and a mule named Biscuit; Theo had done it from the air, because by then the world had changed and so had the family.

And there, in the upper right quadrant, past the last marked contour, past the place where the escarpment's north face dropped away into the cloud-shadowed country above Rivergate—there was the route.

It was drawn in red now, but Odile could see beneath the red that the original line was pencil, faint, corrected twice. A corridor through the pass that no one flew. The impossible one. The one that killed him.

She did not touch it yet. First, the note.

* * *

Theo's handwriting was harder to read than her father's, because Theo wrote the way he flew: fast, confident, with a certain contempt for convention that only occasionally ended in disaster. The note had come folded inside the map's backing, pressed between two sheets of waxed linen that the surveyor—old Anders Vik, retired forty years to Kettle Falls, who had gone quiet when he heard her surname and quieter still when he'd handed over the tube—must have kept for nine years without ever opening, or opened once and never again.

The note was dated eleven days before the crash.

*O —*

*If you're reading this I've either landed it or I haven't, and either way you're going to be furious at me, so let me get ahead of it.*

*I found something on the north face. Not weather. Not rockfall. Somebody's been blasting up there — above the pass, in the headwall, where there's nothing on any lease map. I flew the scarp three times this month and each time there's fresh rubble in the gully below the third rib, and each time the rubble is wrong. Rock doesn't shatter like that from frost. Frost cracks along the grain. This is cut. You can see the drill lines if you know what you're looking at, and I know what I'm looking at, because Dad taught us both on the same kitchen table with the same sugar cubes.*

*I'm going back up tomorrow with the camera rig. If I don't come back, don't come looking. That's not me being dramatic, that's me doing the math. Whoever's up there went to a lot of trouble to be invisible, and they'll have gone to more trouble to stay that way.*

*Take care of Dad. He'll pretend he's fine. He won't be.*

*T.*

Odile read it four times. On the fourth read her hands were steady, which surprised her, because on the first read they had not been, and she had set the page down on the table among the coffee mugs and stood at the window for a long while watching the monsoon light stack itself against the western ridges like slate being unloaded from a barge.

Somebody's been blasting up there.

Nine years. Nine years she had carried the other story—the one everyone told, the one her father never contradicted because contradicting it would have meant talking about it at all. Theo had pushed the weather. Theo had flown the route no one flew when no one should have been flying anything. Theo, brilliant and impatient, had finally outrun his own luck, and the mountain had closed its hand. Grief needs a shape, and that was the shape grief had been given: a boy who flew too hard into a pass that ate the reckless.

And it had been wrong. It had been wrong the whole time.

She thought about her father in those first years after—the silence that filled the house like floodwater, the ledger with the Rivergate route blacked out in ink so heavy it had torn the paper, the oath he'd sworn at the graveside with half the town listening: *No Dunmore flies that pass again.* She had thought it was fear. She had thought it was superstition, the particular superstition of pilots, who are rational people until they aren't. Now she understood it was neither. Her father had known something. Maybe not all of it—but enough. Enough to know that the pass hadn't killed his son. Something on the pass had, and whatever it was had been strong enough, or well-connected enough, that a man like Aldous Dunmore—who argued with provincial inspectors for sport—had chosen silence over war.

He had chosen silence, and he had made his daughter inherit it.

* * *

She worked until two in the morning.

The map gave her the geography; the note gave her the intent; what she needed now was the proof, and Theo, being Theo, had left her a trail of breadcrumbs scattered across three different kinds of document. She started with the flight logs—not his final log, which she still couldn't open, but the maintenance records Wren had flagged months ago, the pages torn from the Escarpment strip's books. Wren had noticed the gaps back when the contract first landed, and had said nothing, and Odile had filed the observation away in the same locked drawer where she kept everything else about Rivergate. Tonight she opened the drawer.

The torn pages corresponded to dates. She wrote them out on the back of a fuel slip: March 4th. March 19th. April 2nd. April 11th. Five flights in six weeks, all logged out as survey work for a client whose name had been torn off with everything else. But fuel purchases didn't tear, and the fuel slips lived in a separate box, and her father had been a bookkeeper before he was anything else. She cross-referenced. Every one of those flights had taken full tanks and returned having burned nearly all of them—a round trip to the escarpment and back with loiter time. Camera work. Theo had said he flew the scarp three times *that month*. The logs said five times in six weeks, and those were just the flights that originated from Escarpment. There could have been others from Kettle Falls, from anywhere.

Then the photographs. This was the part that stopped her breath.

In the bottom of the map tube, beneath the linen, rolled tight as a cigarette, was a strip of aerial film negatives. Twenty-four frames. Anders Vik must have developed them—or someone had—and then hidden them, and Odile understood suddenly why the old man had gone quiet at her surname, why he had kept this thing for nine years like a coal banked in ash. He had been afraid. He was probably still afraid. And he had waited for a Dunmore to come asking, because some debts you can't hand to just anyone.

She taped the negative strip to the window glass and held her phone's flashlight behind it, frame by frame, photographing each one and blowing it up on the small screen until her eyes watered.

The first frames were the escarpment's north face as everyone knew it: gray wall, gray sky, the pass cutting through like a healed wound. Frame seven changed everything.

Frame seven showed the headwall above the pass, and in the headwall, tucked into the shadow of the third rib exactly where Theo's note said to look, was an adit. A mine entrance. Timbered, shored, with a spoil heap below it that had to hold ten thousand cubic yards of broken rock—and drill lines. Even at this resolution she could see them: parallel scars running across the face like comb marks, the unmistakable signature of systematic blasting. Not one charge. Not a claim jumper with a stick of dynamite. An operation. Schedules, crews, equipment hauled up terrain so steep it couldn't be reached except from above—which meant air support, which meant someone had been flying in there too, someone with a plane or a helicopter and a reason to keep it off every book in the province.

Frames twelve through nineteen were close-ups of the gully below the spoil heap. Frame fifteen showed what frost-shattered rock looked like—angular, fractured along natural seams. Frame sixteen showed the other kind: the smooth-faced, conchoidal breaks of drilled and shot stone, lying in windrows, sorted almost neatly, as if someone had planned to come back and haul it away and then hadn't.

Frame twenty-two was the one she stared at longest. It showed the adit again, closer, and beside the timbering, stenciled on a crate half-buried in spoil, a mark. She photographed it, enlarged it, enhanced it until the pixels surrendered. Three letters, block capitals, partially obscured:

**H L V**

Halvard.

The name arrived in her mind with a physical sensation, cold water finding a crack. Halvard Group. Cass had said the word weeks ago in the freight office at Kettle Falls, in her flat, bitter way—*someone paid me to force this contract, and the money traces back to a holding company that doesn't answer mail.* Odile had filed that away too, in the drawer, next to everything else. But Cass had meant the debt, the contract, the trap. Cass hadn't known about the mine. Nobody living knew about the mine except a retired surveyor and a dead boy and whoever had put the adit in the headwall.

And now her.

She sat back down at the table and made herself think it through, because thinking it through was the only alternative to putting her fist through the wall, and the wall had done nothing wrong.

The sequence assembled itself with terrible ease. Nine years ago, someone was mining the escarpment's headwall illegally—high-grade ore, most likely, or gravel rights, or something rarer; the *what* mattered less than the *where*, because the where was unleased, unsurveyed, and invisible from below. Theo found it. Theo documented it. Theo wrote a note to his sister saying he was going back with the camera rig, and eleven days later his plane went down in the pass, and the official record called it weather, and the blasting company kept operating long enough to haul out whatever they'd come for, and then—here was the part that made her teeth ache—they hadn't even left. They'd stayed. They'd become respectable. They'd spent nine years buying Rivergate's land at fire-sale prices from a town too broken and too isolated to know what its grief was worth, positioning themselves as benefactors, waiting for the day the valley's stores ran thin and its title deeds got cheap. And when the timing was right, they'd bought a rival pilot's debt and forced the one contract that would bring a Dunmore back to the pass—because if the route reopened, land values moved, and if the route failed, a Dunmore died in the attempt and the whole story stayed buried under another nine years of weather.

Either way, they won. Either way, she was the instrument.

*You were the perfect deniable pilot,* Cass had said, and Odile had thought she understood. She hadn't. Understanding arrived now, at two in the morning, in a rented room smelling of lamp oil and cold coffee, and it arrived not as a revelation but as a weight settling onto her shoulders with the specific gravity of cargo properly loaded.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around four she heard Wren's boots on the stairs—he never slept properly before a maintenance day, and the grounding order had given him nothing but maintenance days—and she opened the door before he could knock.

He took one look at her face and came in without being asked, which was its own kind of fluency. He saw the map, the mugs, the negative strip still taped to the dark window. He picked up Theo's note and read it standing, very still, the way he read torque specifications, and when he finished he set it down and said, "Okay," and then, "Tell me from the beginning."

She told him from the beginning. The drill lines. The adit. The film. The letters on the crate. The math of it, the shape of the trap, the nine years of a lie that had fed on her family like dry rot feeds on a beam—from the outside sound, from the inside hollow.

Wren listened the way he fixed engines: completely, without interruption, hands quiet. When she finished he was silent for a moment, and then he said, "Your brother mapped the safe corridor. Didn't he. That's what the red line is."

"I don't know yet." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "I haven't read his final log. I've had it nine years and I've never—" She stopped. Started again. "I couldn't. It's the last thing he wrote and I couldn't make myself read past the first page."

"Then maybe tonight's the night." Wren said it gently, but he said it plainly, the way he said everything that mattered. "Because if the mine's still active—if they're still shooting that headwall—then the escarpment isn't just history, Odile. It's a structure under load. Everything they've blasted loose since your brother died is sitting up there waiting for the monsoon to pull the trigger. And there are four hundred people in that valley drinking from the river below it."

She had gotten there herself, hours ago, alone in the dark. But hearing it in Wren's voice made it real in a different way, the way a second altimeter confirms the first. She looked at the map on the table—at her father's careful contours, at Theo's red line threading the impossible pass—and felt something shift in her chest that she recognized, eventually, because she had felt it only once before in her life, the day she soloed: fear turning over, mid-fall, into lift.

Grief had been her ballast for nine years. Dead weight, carried because she didn't know how to jettison it and didn't dare. But ballast, she remembered now from her father's lessons, is just weight in the wrong place. Put it where it belongs and it becomes stability. Put it to work and it becomes purpose.

"Theo went back up with a camera rig knowing what might happen," she said. "Eleven days before the end, he wrote *don't come looking*, and then he went anyway, because somebody had to see it and write it down." She began rolling the map, carefully, along its original creases. "So we're going to do what he did. We're going to see it and write it down. All of it. The adit, the spoil, the drill lines, the letters on the crates. Photographs, bearings, elevations—everything a lawyer and a newspaper and a provincial minister would need to burn the Halvard Group down to its foundations."

"And the town?"

"The town gets the truth too. Before anyone offers to buy their silence." She looked up at him. "I'm done being invisible, Wren. It didn't protect Theo. It didn't protect anybody. It just gave them nine clear years to finish the job."

Wren nodded slowly, and then, because he was Wren, he reached past her and tapped the negative strip on the window. "Frame twenty-two. Get me a clean print of that crate before dawn. Stenciling has batch codes, usually—lot numbers, blast dates. If we can date the most recent shots, we can prove the mine's active *now*, not just nine years ago. Active means criminal. Closed means tragic. I want them criminal."

It was such a mechanic's sentence—evidence as a maintenance record of evil—that despite everything, despite the hour and the grief and the mountain waiting in the dark, Odile laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

They worked side by side until the windows went gray, printing frames, tracing contours, building the case brick by brick. And when the light was finally strong enough to read by, Odile took the last thing out of her flight bag: Theo's final logbook, bound in green canvas, corners worn soft, smelling faintly of kerosene and nine years of her own avoidance.

She set it on the table between the coffee mugs.

Outside, the monsoon's leading edge had begun to arrive—cloud stacking on cloud, the pressure dropping in her ears, the birds going quiet. Six days, maybe five, before the passes closed. Somewhere above the escarpment, men were probably loading charges into the headwall right now, betting that the weather would bury their evidence the way it had buried hers.

Odile opened the cover. The first page was dated the spring of the crash year, and her brother's handwriting marched across it, fast and confident, contemptuous of margins.

She began to read.
