## 17. Night Flight

The storm came in from the west like something with intent.

Odile stood on the Kettle Falls apron at dusk and watched it build—a wall of cloud stacked against the escarpment's shoulder, bruise-purple at the base, lit from within by lightning that flickered without sound, too far away yet to carry its thunder. The air had that taste she'd learned as a child in her father's hangar: ozone and wet stone and the particular metallic tang of a sky holding more water than it could bear.

"Six hours," Cass said beside her, arms crossed, watching the same horizon. "Maybe five. After that, nothing flies north for a week."

The fever in Rivergate wouldn't wait a week. Mara's radio voice was still lodged in Odile's skull like a splinter—*we've lost the stores, we've lost the cold chain, I have eleven children running temperatures and nothing left but willow bark and prayer.* Eleven children. Odile had counted them against the dark on the flight back from the pass, the turn-back, the place where her courage had failed at the exact coordinates of her brother's death. She'd spent the whole return leg doing arithmetic she couldn't stop: how many doses, how many hours, how many degrees of fever before a child's body tipped past what willow bark could hold.

So: tonight. The leading edge of the monsoon was still an hour out, maybe two, and the corridor between Kettle Falls and the Rivergate valley ran low along the river gorge where the storm hadn't yet reached. It would be flying with a lit fuse. But Wren had the plane loaded—twelve crates of antibiotics, serum, IV fluid, a portable refrigerator unit strapped down like a sleeping animal—and Wren had signed off every line of the pre-flight twice, and Wren had looked at Odile across the cargo bay and said only, "Bring it back. Both of you." Which was Wren's way of saying everything.

"You don't have to come," Odile said now, not looking at Cass. "It's my family's mess. My route."

Cass snorted. "Your route. You turned back at the pass this morning so hard you nearly bent the yoke." She pushed off the rail of the fuel shed and walked toward the plane, talking over her shoulder. "Besides, it's my contract. If Halvard wants this run made, I want to be the one who makes it. And if someone wants this run *not* made—" she patted the fuselage as she passed under the tail, "—I want to be there when they try."

That was the thing about Cass Merrow that nine years of rivalry had let Odile forget: underneath the sharpened edges and the expensive sunglasses and the way she said *my* about airplanes the way other people said it about children, there was steel of the old kind. The kind their fathers' generation had been forged from. Cass had confessed it all three nights ago in the Kettle Falls bunkhouse, her voice low and furious in the dark—that she hadn't bought the debt for profit, that someone had paid her to force the Rivergate contract, that she'd been flying the job ever since trying to find out who. Two pilots caught in the same snare. It should have made them allies instantly. Instead it had made them careful, circling each other like dogs deciding whether the other one was pack or threat.

Tonight, apparently, they were going to find out at ten thousand feet in the leading edge of a monsoon.

* * *

They lifted off at 21:40, into a sky already going wrong.

The river gorge swallowed them whole. Below, black water; above, cloud deck lowering by the minute; ahead, the faint gray seam of the valley where the last daylight was being pressed out of the world. Odile flew instruments-and-instinct, the way her father had taught her—*trust the needle, trust your hands, and never trust the mountain*—while Cass rode the right seat with the radio log open on her knee, calling position reports into a void that mostly didn't answer.

"Kettle Falls relay is degrading," Cass said twenty minutes in. Static had crept into the frequency like frost across glass. "We'll lose them inside the gorge."

"We always do." Her father used to say the gorge ate radios for breakfast. He'd said a lot of things about this route, most of them in the imperative mood: *never fly it at night, never fly it in weather, never fly it alone.* She was breaking all three oaths in a single departure, and some old, obedient part of her kept flinching, waiting for the sky itself to object.

The first turbulence hit them at the gorge's second bend—not the sharp-edged chop of thermals but a long, rolling heave, the air moving in masses, like something enormous turning over beneath them. Lightning strobed inside the cloud deck above, silent, illuminating the underside of the storm the way a hand held behind a lantern illuminates the fingers.

"There's our leading edge," Cass said quietly.

Odile climbed two hundred feet and threaded east of a cell that glowed on the weather radar like a wound. The rain found them then—not falling so much as arriving, horizontal, hammering the windscreen until the wipers labored and the world beyond the glass became a gray-green smear shot through with the ghost of the gorge walls. She flew by radar and memory and the dim amber glow of the panel, and the plane shook around them like a dog shaking off water, and she felt—God help her—the first loosening in her chest she'd felt in nine years.

Because this was the flying her father had actually loved. Not the tidy medevac hops in morning fog, not the milk-run charters. This: the machine alive under her hands, the storm arguing with her, every control input a sentence in a conversation the sky was having whether she joined in or not. Theo had loved it too. That was the terrible inheritance of the Dunmore bloodline—they didn't fear weather, they *recognized* it.

"Your hands are steady," Cass said, watching them on the yoke. There was something in her voice Odile couldn't name. Not surprise exactly. Recalculation.

"Nine years of fog landings at Escarpment. Fog doesn't negotiate either."

"No," Cass said. "I remember. I used to watch you come in on those mornings and think—" She stopped. The rain filled the pause with static-hiss against the fuselage.

"Think what?"

"That you were showing off." A short laugh, stripped of anything warm. "And then I grew up enough to realize you weren't showing off. You were hiding. Best pilot on the strip, flying the dullest routes in the province, and everyone just accepted it because you made dull look like a choice." She turned in her seat, and even in the red glow of the night lighting Odile could feel the weight of her stare. "It wasn't a choice, was it. It was penance."

The plane dropped through an air pocket, sickening, weightless, then caught. Odile corrected with two fingers and a wrist, barely a movement, and waited until her voice would come out level.

"What do you know about penance, Cass?"

"I know I've been doing it since I was nineteen." Cass faced forward again. For a while there was only the engine's drone and the rain's drumming and the radar's slow pulse. Then, in the flat voice of someone reading coordinates off a map of herself: "My father lost the strip in '09. Gambling debts, bad contracts, the usual slow bleed. Everyone in Escarpment thought I hated him for it. I did. But what I never told anyone—" her jaw worked, "—is that the man who bought his paper was the same man who later came to me with the Halvard money. And I took it. Knowing it smelled wrong. Because taking it meant getting close enough to find out what happened to my father's books, and his books had Rivergate pages torn out too, Odile. Same as your maintenance logs. Same pages. Same years."

The lightning came closer now, close enough to carry sound—a long rolling concussion that Odile felt in her sternum rather than heard. She flew through it. Somewhere below in the dark, the river ran north toward the valley where eleven children burned with fever, and she held the plane steady on the corridor and let Cass talk, because she understood suddenly that this flight was carrying more than medicine. It was carrying two women's confessions, and the storm was the confessional, and altitude was the only place either of them had ever been able to say true things.

"I told myself I was investigating," Cass went on. "I told myself I was going to burn whoever set this up. But some nights, in the bunkhouse, I'd wake up and know the truth. I took the money because I wanted back in. Back into the game, back onto the strip, back into—" she gestured vaguely at the cockpit, at Odile, at the whole airborne architecture of their lives, "—this. And the men who paid me knew that. They knew exactly which hunger to feed. That's what I can't forgive myself for. They didn't trick me. They *shopped* me."

"They shopped both of us," Odile said. "A Dunmore and a Merrow. The two names in the province with history on this route. Deniable, motivated, and too proud to compare notes." She shook her head slowly. "We were perfect."

"We were *available*." Cass's laugh was bitter and real. "There's a difference."

* * *

Past the halfway bend, the storm closed its hand.

The cell they'd been threading beside drifted across the corridor ahead, and Odile saw it coming on radar thirty seconds before she saw it with her eyes—a solid core, hail-topped, sitting across the gap like a door slamming. Going around meant climbing into the shear layer where the monsoon's upper winds lived. Going back meant the children waited another day. Going through meant—

"Through," Cass said, before Odile had even spoken. "You're thinking through. I can hear you thinking through."

"I'm thinking that the gap between the core and the ridge narrows to maybe half a mile, and that my brother died in weather half this bad, and that I promised myself—"

"Promises made to grief aren't binding," Cass said. "I've checked." She reached over and tapped the radar display. "Half a mile, moving east at fifteen knots. We go in on the trailing edge, ride the outflow, and we're through in ninety seconds. Your father flew worse than this in a taildragger with no radar at all."

"My father swore off this route."

"And look how much good the oath did him. It didn't bring Theo back. It just taught his daughter to fly scared." Cass's voice gentled by a degree, which from Cass was more shocking than shouting. "Fly the airplane, Odile. Not the oath. Not the grave. The airplane."

Something in Odile went very quiet and very clear.

She rolled in toward the gap. The rain became a solid thing, a white roaring wall, and the lightning stopped being scenery and started being navigation—each flash printing the gorge walls on her retinas, half a second of geography at a time. The updraft hit first, shoving them upward, and she countered, and then the downdraft came like a fist, eight hundred feet a minute, and she countered that, full power, nose down, the stall warning chattering once and going silent as the airspeed bled and recovered and bled again. The airplane groaned around them. Cargo shifted in the back with a sound like furniture moving upstairs. Cass called off numbers—airspeed, altitude, closure—and her voice never rose, never cracked, just ticked through the emergency like a metronome, and Odile understood that this was the language they'd been learning to speak all along, through nine years of rivalry and three weeks of suspicion: not words at all, but *numbers*, exchanged under load, the only honest currency pilots had.

"Ninety seconds," Cass said. "Fifty-five."

The downdraft tried to put them into the gorge wall and Odile refused it—not with heroics but with patience, trading altitude for margin, letting the river's cold outflow cushion under them like a hand. Forty seconds. Thirty. The rain's roar thinned. Ten. Five—

And then they were through, out the far side into a sudden cathedral of clearing air, the storm behind them glowing like a city on fire, and ahead—below—the Rivergate valley opening in the dark, a scatter of lights along the river, small and stubborn and impossibly fragile. Relief lights at the strip. Someone down there had lit the runway with kerosene flares, dozens of them, laid out in a trembling orange line against the black.

"Look at that," Cass breathed. "They lit it themselves. No radio codes, no tower. Just—flares and hope."

"Theo used to say that was the best approach lighting in the province." Odile's throat was tight. "He said the mountains give you nothing, so the people give you everything."

She brought the plane down through light rain onto the flare-lit strip, smooth as a signature, and the whole town seemed to be there in the dark—figures running alongside with flashlights, hands waving, someone laughing, someone crying, Mara's tall shape sprinting for the cargo door before the props had fully stopped. When the crates came out and the refrigerator unit hummed to life under a tarp, an old man took Odile's hand in both of his and held it and said, "Dunmore. You're a Dunmore," and there was no blame in it and no welcome either, only recognition, heavy as ballast.

* * *

They sat on the loading ramp afterward, side by side, drinking tea someone had pressed into their hands, watching the storm they'd crossed smolder on the western ridgeline. The flares guttered along the strip. Somewhere a generator coughed and steadied.

"I used to hate you," Cass said conversationally. "Properly hate you. Nine years of it. Do you know why?"

"Because I beat you into Kettle Falls by four minutes in '16 and never let you forget it?"

"Because you got to leave." Cass stared into her tea. "When it went bad here—when Theo died and your father swore the oath—I stayed. Escarpment, the strip, the gossip, all of it, every year, watching everyone look at me like I was the one who'd failed at something. And you got to vanish into fog and become a legend nobody could touch. I resented the hell out of your invisibility." She glanced sideways. "Took me until tonight to see what it cost you. You paid for that fog with everything else."

Odile didn't answer right away. Down the strip, Mara was directing the unloading by flashlight, and a boy of maybe ten stood at the edge of the light holding a crate of serum like it was made of eggshell, walking with exaggerated care, solemn as a priest. Eleven children. Maybe ten, now, who'd see morning.

"My brother mapped a route through the escarpment," Odile said finally. "Before he died. Nobody knows it. Nobody's flown it. It's in his handwriting on my father's survey, and I think—" she turned the tin cup in her hands, "—I think it might be the only way to move these people out before the passes close. And I think the men who killed him know it too, which is why they need me grounded, or gone, or bought."

Cass was quiet for a long moment. Then she drained her tea and stood, and offered Odile her hand up off the ramp—an ordinary gesture, pilot to pilot, that landed like a treaty.

"Then we'd better read that route tonight," Cass said. "Because that storm's not slowing down, and neither am I."

Beyond the eastern ridge, deep in the black, thunder rolled—long, patient, like something turning over in its sleep. The monsoon was coming for the valley. And somewhere above the tree line, in the dark, the mine was still blasting.
