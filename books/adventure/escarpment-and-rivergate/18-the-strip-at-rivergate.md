## 18. The Strip at Rivergate

The valley opened beneath them like a held breath finally let out.

Odile had seen it a hundred times in the nine years since she'd last seen it—in the gray hour before sleep, in the space between radio calls, in the mirror when her face went strange and young without her permission. But memory had flattened it, the way memory flattens everything it can't bear to keep at full resolution. The real Rivergate was worse and better than the one she carried: worse because the slide scar was still there, a pale raw stripe down the eastern wall of the valley where the mountain had shrugged off its own skin nine years ago and taken the road, the bridge, and her brother with it; better because the town was still *there*, still stubborn, still smoking its morning fires into the mist like a handful of lit candles someone had refused to blow out.

"Rivergate strip, Rivergate strip, this is Merrow One, inbound from the south, four miles out, requesting wind and conditions." Cass's voice on the headset was steady as a metronome, and Odile loved her a little for it—for not letting the silence on the other end stretch into something either of them had to explain.

The radio crackled. A man's voice, older, roughened the way voices get when they spend decades talking over river noise. "Merrow One, Rivergate. Wind's out of the north at eight, gusting fifteen off the scarp. Strip's wet but solid. We—" a pause, long enough that Odile's hands tightened on the yoke, "—we didn't think anyone was coming."

"We almost didn't," Cass said, and let that stand.

The strip sat on the valley floor where the river bent wide and slow, a ribbon of gravel and old asphalt that her father's generation had carved out of the floodplain with bulldozers and sheer orneriness. She could see the windsock—new cloth, orange, snapping hard—and the cluster of buildings at the north end: a hangar with a sagging roof, a fuel shed, a low building that might have been a terminal once and was probably a storage shed now. Someone had painted numbers on the threshold. Someone had *kept* it. Nine years of no scheduled flights, no mail runs, no medevac contracts, and somebody had swept the gravel and painted the numbers and kept the windsock in fresh cloth, the way you'd keep a grave, or a promise, or a wound you weren't ready to let close.

"Merrow One, you are cleared to land," the voice said, and then, quieter, almost lost under the static: "Welcome back to Rivergate."

*Back.* Odile's throat did something complicated. She keyed the mic and found she had nothing. Cass glanced over, read her face in the way pilots read instruments—quickly, completely, without commentary—and took the radio herself.

"Merrow One, thanks, Rivergate. It's good to be—" Cass hesitated, honest to the last, "—here."

The flare. The gravel singing under the tires. The plane settling into itself, heavy with medicine and fuel and nine years of everything unsaid, rolling out past the painted numbers toward the hangar while the mist closed around the wings like water closing over a stone.

* * *

They were waiting.

Odile had expected maybe the strip keeper and a dog. Instead, when she dropped from the cockpit onto wet gravel, there were twenty people, thirty, standing in the drizzle at a careful distance—close enough to see, far enough that it wasn't quite a crowd, the way people stand when they haven't decided yet whether what's arrived is a rescue or an accusation.

An old man stepped forward. He was tall the way river trees are tall—weathered down but not beaten, with a white beard and a canvas coat gone soft as cloth can get. He stopped six feet away and looked at Odile for a long time, and she watched the recognition move across his face like weather.

"You're Dunmore's girl," he said.

Not a question. Her name, the one she'd spent nine years burying under call signs and contract signatures, spoken aloud in the valley air for the first time since she was twenty-four years old. Somewhere behind her, she heard Cass's boots on the ramp, deliberate, a presence at her back.

"I'm Odile Dunmore," she said. It came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "We've brought medicine. Antibiotics, fever reducers, IV fluids. Enough to start."

The old man didn't reach for the manifest. He kept looking at her, and his eyes were wet, and his jaw was set, and Odile understood with a lurch that both of those things were true at once and would stay true, here, in a way they never were in Escarpment, where people let you be two things only if the second one stayed hidden.

"Tam Voss," he said. "I kept your father's strip." He gestured at the hangar, the painted numbers, the windsock. "Kept it every season. Told myself he'd come back one day and want it ready." His voice caught, ground itself flat again. "He never came."

"No," Odile said. "He didn't."

"And then he died," Voss said, "and I thought, well, maybe the daughter. And you didn't come either." There was no cruelty in it. That was the worst part. It was only grief, old and settled, the kind that has stopped hoping to be answered and just states its accounts to whoever finally shows up.

"I know," Odile said. "I'm sorry. I—"

"Mrs. Aldous is dead," a woman's voice cut in from the edge of the group. Middle-aged, arms crossed against the drizzle, eyes like struck matches. "Marlene Aldous. Fever took her three weeks ago. And the Kessler boy, and old Ruthie Pine, and we sent word south every which way we knew, and nobody came." She stepped forward, out of the careful distance, close enough that Odile could see the tremor in her folded arms. "So you'll forgive me if I don't weep over the Dunmore name showing up now, with the frost already on the passes."

"Petra," Voss said, low, a warning.

"No. Let her say it." The woman—Petra—turned on Odile fully. "Your father flew this valley for twenty years. Your brother died on that mountain and the whole world said *what a tragedy*, and then the world went away, and we learned what the tragedy was really for. It was for nothing. It was for a road that never reopened and a strip nobody used and a family that swore the place off like we were the sickness." Her voice cracked and she hated it, you could see her hate it. "You want to hand me medicine? Hand it over. But don't stand there waiting for a welcome."

The drizzle ticked on the plane's aluminum skin. Somewhere up the valley, a dog barked twice and quit. Odile stood in the wet gravel of her father's strip, in the town her family had sworn off, and felt the full weight of the arithmetic Petra Aldous was doing in front of everyone: nine years of silence, measured against one plane full of antibiotics. There was no exchange rate. There never had been.

"You're right," Odile said. "About all of it. My father didn't come back. I didn't come back. There isn't a good reason, there's only reasons, and they don't help you, so I won't waste your morning on them." She turned and climbed back into the plane and began handing crates down the ramp, one after another, into Voss's arms, into other arms as people moved to help, and she kept handing and kept talking, level, over the work. "Fever's in the lower town, Voss told me on the radio—I heard it in his voice. Take me to whoever's nursing it. I flew medevac nine years out of Escarpment; I can triage, I can start IVs, I can do the work. I'm not asking you to forgive the name. I'm asking you to use the hands."

Petra Aldous stared at her. The match-strike eyes flicked to the crates, counting—Odile could see her counting, the way you count when your child might be the one who needs the third crate and not the fifth—and something in her face shifted, not softening, exactly. Recalculating.

"The nurse is Mara Voss," she said at last. "Tam's daughter. She's got eleven in the schoolhouse. Two of them won't see the week out without the antibiotics." A pause, and then, grudging as gravel: "The hands, then. Leave the name at the strip."

* * *

They worked the unloading in a chain that formed itself without anyone ordering it—Cass in the cargo bay tossing crates, Odile and Voss and a dozen townspeople passing them hand to hand down the ramp, a boy of maybe sixteen stacking them on a flatbed wagon behind a tractor that coughed blue smoke but ran. The drizzle thickened toward rain. Nobody left.

Odile learned the town's shape through the work. The schoolhouse sat up-slope, whitewashed, its bell tower leaning. The church was new-roofed—she could see the pale lumber from the strip, a scar of repair on an old building. The slide scar above it all, on the eastern wall, wore its nine years of green regrowth like a bandage over a break that had never been set. And at the valley's head, half-lost in cloud, the escarpment pass: the notch in the ridgeline where the road had run south, where the trucks had come and gone twice a week her whole childhood, where Theo had been flying survey when the mountain came down.

She caught herself looking at it the way you probe a broken tooth. Made herself stop. Failed.

"Your father built that hangar door," Voss said, coming to stand beside her while the last crates went down. He nodded at the big sliding door, its frame of blackened timber, iron straps gone to rust-bloom. "Winter of '61. Came up with a crew of six and did it in nine days before the snow closed the pass. He was a bull of a man, your father. Loud. Laughed like a rockslide." Voss smiled, and it hurt to look at. "You have his hands."

"Everyone says so."

"You don't have his laugh, though." Voss studied her, mild and merciless. "I wondered, over the years. What happened to the girl who used to ride shotgun with him at eleven years old, hollering the calls into the radio before she could spell half the words. Then the crash came, and Theo went, and I stopped wondering. Grief does what it does." He picked up a crate, hefted it. "But I'll tell you what I told him, the last time he stood on this gravel. This valley doesn't forget its own. It just waits."

"Maybe it waited too long."

"Maybe." Voss shrugged, unhurried as the river. "Mountains don't keep clocks. Neither do we."

* * *

It was Petra Aldous, of all people, who said the other thing.

The wagon was loaded, the plane light and empty-feeling, and the crowd was breaking up into the rain when Petra stopped in front of Odile, arms still crossed, rain running off her elbows.

"You should hear the rest of it," she said. "Since you're going to be walking around this town with that name."

"The rest of it."

"There are people here who say your father knew." Petra's voice dropped, not from shame but from the care of handling something sharp. "That the blasting company that was working above the pass—Halvard, the name was, Halvard's people—had been warned off. That your father flew a complaint to the district office that spring, about the charges they were setting, about the cracks in the scarp above the road. And that when the mountain came down anyway, some folks decided he'd been paid to stop asking. Or that he'd asked too loud and they dropped the mountain on him for it. On *Theo*." Her jaw worked. "I never believed it. Tam never believed it. But nine years is a long time to be cut off with nothing but a story, and stories grow roots. There are houses in the lower town where the Dunmore name is a curse. You'll hear it. I'd rather you heard it from me than from a thrown rock."

Odile stood very still. The rain ran down the back of her collar. *Halvard.* The name went through her like a fishhook finding old scar tissue—Cass's shell companies, the debt, the contract, the men who had paid to force this route, and now here it was again, nine years early, waiting in her father's valley like it had never left.

"Thank you," she said. "For telling me straight."

"Don't thank me." Petra unfolded her arms at last. "Come to the schoolhouse. See what nine years of nobody coming looks like. Then decide what you thank."

* * *

The schoolhouse smelled of woodsmoke and camphor and the particular sweet-sour heaviness of sickness that Odile knew from a hundred medevac runs, and had hoped, stupidly, never to smell again. Mara Voss met them at the door—a woman of forty-odd with her father's height and a nurse's flat, competent calm, sleeves rolled, hands red from washing.

"Eleven patients," she said, before hello, before names, already walking. "Fever, coughing, two with the rash. It started six weeks back, slow, and it's moving faster now that it's in the children. I've been treating with willow bark and prayer and it's not enough." She pushed open the door to the main room, and Odile saw the cots in rows, the small bodies under blankets, the woman—another Voss cousin, maybe—wiping a child's forehead with a cloth, and the arithmetic Odile had refused at the strip arrived anyway and sat down in her chest. "Tell me what you brought."

"Amoxicillin, ceftriaxone for the bad ones, antipyretics, IV kits, rehydration salts." Odile was already unzipping the medical crate. "It'll treat eleven. It'll treat thirty. Mara—" she looked up, and the nurse's eyes were sharp and tired and waiting, "—how many in the town are sick, total? Not just here."

Mara Voss went still in a way Odile recognized, because it was her own stillness, the stillness of a person standing at the edge of a number they've been carrying alone.

"Sixty that I know of," Mara said. "And I don't know the lower town proper. And the winter road is snowed shut, and the pass—" her gaze went, like everyone's gaze went, to the eastern wall, to the scar, "—the pass is gone. So whatever you brought, whatever you *are*," and here her voice did not harden so much as sharpen, a blade laid flat, "it had better be enough to come back with. Because one plane is a headline. A supply line is a rescue. Which one are you, Odile Dunmore?"

Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and up the valley, under the cloud, the escarpment held its silence—the loaded, listening silence of a thing that is not finished falling.

Odile looked down at the crates, at the small fevered faces, and heard herself answer before the fear could vote.

"A supply line," she said. "Whatever it costs."

From the doorway behind her, where she'd followed the wagon up through the rain without being asked, Cass Merrow said quietly, "Both planes."

And down in the valley, faint under the rain, Odile would swear for the rest of her life that she heard the mountain shift.
