## 19. The Fever Ward

The nurse's house sat at the crook of the river, where the water bent around a stand of black cottonwood and the town's one paved road gave up and became gravel. Odile followed the sound of coughing the way she'd follow a beacon—unwillingly, inevitably—up the porch steps of a building that had once been Rivergate's clinic and was now, judging by the plywood over the back windows and the generator exhaust pipe jutting through the wall, Mara Voss's entire hospital.

Mara opened the door before Odile knocked. She was fifty-something and built like driftwood, all weathered grain and hidden density, with a stethoscope around her neck worn smooth as river stone. She looked at Odile for a long moment—not at her face but at her hands, then her boots, then finally up.

"Dunmore," she said. Not a question. Not a welcome either.

"Mara Voss." Odile had rehearsed this on the flight in, three versions of an apology, none of them adequate. "I came to help."

"Did you." Mara stepped aside. It wasn't hospitality; it was triage. "Then come in and be useful. Talking can wait or it can rot, I don't much care which."

* * *

The main room had been a waiting area once. Now twelve cots stood in rows where the reception desk used to be, and every cot was full. The air was thick with the smell of camphor and damp wool and the sweetish wrongness of fever sweat. A wood stove ticked in the corner, doing its inadequate best. Children lay two to a cot in some cases, small bodies curled against their mothers' spines like commas against a line of text.

Odile stood in the doorway and let it hit her.

Nine years. Nine years she'd been flying medicine into the interior—insulin, antibiotics, vaccines, whole pallets of it—to towns whose names she could list alphabetically from memory. Anvil Creek. Cold Harbour. Two Rivers. She'd flown penicillin to places with populations smaller than this room. And Rivergate, forty minutes north over the escarpment, forty minutes from Escarpment's own strip, had gone without.

Not because no one asked. Because no one flew.

Because her father had sworn off the route, and she'd inherited the oath along with the ledger, and neither of them had ever said aloud what the silence cost. They'd just paid it, quietly, year after year, in the currency of other people's fevers.

"You're standing there doing arithmetic," Mara said behind her. "I can see it. Stop. There's work."

She put a basin of cool water in Odile's hands and pointed her at a boy of maybe seven, flushed scarlet, his mother fanning him with a folded newspaper gone soft as cloth from handling. "Sponge him down. Keep it under the jaw and at the wrists. When that basin's warm you bring it back and take cold."

The boy's skin burned under the cloth. His eyes were half-open, fixed on nothing, and when Odile touched his forehead he flinched and murmured something in a language she didn't know—one of the valley dialects, old as the river. His mother looked up at her, and there was no blame in her face, which was somehow worse than blame would have been. There was only exhaustion, and a kind of desperate courtesy, as if Odile were a guest who might still leave.

"What is it?" Odile asked Mara, low, between cots. "What are we looking at?"

"Started as influenza. You know how it goes up here—influenza walks in healthy and stays to kill the old ones. But then it turned. Pneumonia in maybe a third of them. And the stores—" Mara's jaw worked. "We had a proper pharmacy once. Antibiotics, antivirals, the works. The resupply stopped coming nine years ago and we stretched what we had, and stretched it, and by last winter we were down to aspirin and prayer. The fever took the medicine cabinet before it took a single person."

Nine years ago. The number landed like a downdraft.

"The winter road," Odile said. "Couldn't you—"

"The winter road closed the same year your family's planes stopped coming. Coincidence, the council called it for a long time." Mara wrung out a cloth with hands that didn't tremble, though Odile suspected they wanted to. "We sent runners south twice. First time they turned back at Kettle Falls—told the passes were unsafe. Second time a man went all the way to Escarpment, to your airstrip, and asked at the office. He came home with a story about weather and liability."

Odile remembered the office. Remembered her father in those years, gray-faced, drinking coffee he never finished, staring north out the window at a sky he wouldn't fly into. She'd thought it was grief. Maybe it had been. But grief made a poor excuse for a locked door, and somewhere in nine years of silence a locked door had become a policy, and a policy had become a graveyard filling one cot at a time.

"How many have died?" she asked.

"This month? Eleven. Two of them children." Mara said it the way she did everything, flat and precise, a woman who had learned that numbers carried better than tears. "It would have been thirty if old Henrik hadn't kept the fever ward separate. It'll be more before this breaks. Unless someone flies me antibiotics tonight, in which case it'll be fewer, and I will personally build a statue to whoever does it."

"I can fly tonight."

Mara looked at her then, really looked, and Odile saw the question arrive in her face—the one everyone in this valley must carry: *where were you?*

But Mara didn't ask it. She was too busy for grief's etiquette. "You can't," she said instead. "Weather's already chewing the lower pass. And even if you got out, Kettle Falls has nothing—Halvard's people bought up the medical depot there two years ago, 'consolidated regional supply,' and now everything routes through the provincial capital on a six-week schedule. Six weeks." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Six weeks is a funeral schedule."

* * *

The afternoon went by in basins and blankets and the small brutal economies of nursing. Odile learned the cots by their occupants: the grandmother in the corner who sang hymns in a whisper when the fever climbed, keeping herself company; the teenage girl who pretended to sleep whenever Odile passed so she wouldn't have to be seen sick; the millworker with pneumonia rattling in his chest like gravel in a hub, whom Mara checked hourly with a stethoscope pressed to skin gone mottled.

At some point Odile realized she'd stopped thinking about flying at all. Her hands knew this work—she'd done medevac long enough—but here it wasn't transport, it was presence. Here you couldn't hand a patient off to a hospital in the capital and climb back to altitude where the air was clean and nobody's suffering could reach you. Here the suffering was the room. You breathed it until it broke or you did.

Near dusk, Mara sat down for the first time all day, on a stool by the stove, and held out her hand. Odile put a cup of tea in it without being asked.

"You want to know why I'm not angry at you," Mara said.

"I've been wondering."

"Because anger needs somewhere to go, and I spent mine years ago—on the council, on the province, on God, in roughly that order. Didn't help a single patient." She blew on the tea. "And because I knew your father. Slightly. Before. He flew us flu vaccine every October, regular as migration. Good man. Careful man." Her eyes came up. "So when the flights stopped, I didn't think *coward*. I thought *something happened to that family*, and whatever it was, it was big enough to stop a careful man mid-route."

"It was my brother." Odile heard her own voice go strange, thin, like radio static. "Theo. He went down in the pass. My father swore off the route the same week. And I—I let the oath stand. Nine years. I flew everywhere else. I told myself the escarpment was genuinely dangerous, and it is, I'm not lying to myself about that, but—" She stopped. Started again. "There were ways. Charter outfits. Provincial runs. I never once picked up a phone and asked anyone to break the silence. That part's mine. Whatever happened to my father, the nine years are mine."

Mara drank her tea and considered this with the unhurried attention she probably gave symptoms.

"Do you know what I tell families, when someone dies up here?" she said. "I tell them: guilt is a fuel. It burns dirty, it fouls plugs, and if you let it idle too long it floods the engine. But it *is* fuel. Some people run clean on love alone. Most of us need the dirtier stuff." She set the cup down. "You're burning it right now. I can smell it. So don't waste my time apologizing—convert it. Tell me what you need to get medicine into this valley before the passes close, and I'll tell you what I need, and we'll see if the sums work."

Odile stared at her. Of all the conversations she'd imagined on the flight north—recrimination, coldness, the handshake that offered belonging and refused it in the same motion—she had not imagined being handed a task list and absolution in the same breath.

"The sums don't work yet," she said slowly. "Kettle Falls has no stock. The capital's six weeks out. Our own supplies are staged for the evacuation math, not a fever ward. But—" and here her mind, the pilot's mind, the one that solved problems in three dimensions, caught hold of something. "Cass has contacts. Old cargo outfits that don't ask paperwork questions. And Wren's coming north with the plane, defying the grounding order, and if he's already breaking one rule he won't blink at a second. If I can get word to him tonight—"

"Radio's in the back room. Generator powers it four hours a night." Mara was already standing, energy returning like a tide. "And while you're at it, ask him for oxygen. We've got two concentrators and both are dying. And lidocaine. And—" she began counting on fingers, and Odile left her counting, walking fast toward the back room with her heart going hard, not from fear this time but from something adjacent to it, something with heat and direction.

Shame, metabolizing. Mara was right—it burned dirty. But burning was burning, and an engine didn't care what fed it so long as it fed.

* * *

The radio room was a closet with a chair, a ham set older than Odile, and a window fogged with condensation. She keyed the mic and raised Kettle Falls on the third try, static shredding the edges of Wren's voice when he came back.

"Say again, Odile—you're broken and crackled—"

"Fever ward," she said, and spelled it out, and read Mara's list into the storm noise: amoxicillin, azithromycin, oxygen concentrators, lidocaine, IV fluids, quantities that made her own voice wobble. "Whatever you can lift. Cass may have a source. I know what I'm asking."

There was a pause on the channel, long enough that she checked the frequency. Then Wren's voice came through, stripped of banter, which from Wren meant gravity.

"I'll strip the cargo bay to the frame if I have to. Give me till tomorrow night." A pause. "Odile. How bad is it in there?"

She looked through the fogged glass, back toward the main room, where eleven people breathed too fast on cots under wool blankets, where a boy murmured in a dead language to a mother who fanned him with yesterday's news.

"Bad enough that I'm staying," she said. "Bad enough that I should have been here nine years ago."

Another pause. When Wren spoke again his voice was quiet and very steady, the voice he used with engines, the voice that meant he'd already decided something.

"Then I'll see you tomorrow night. Fly safe till then."

She signed off and sat a moment in the dark closet, listening to the generator hum and the rain start against the roof, and understood that something in her had permanently changed course. For nine years she had flown away from this valley. Now every calculation in her body pointed north-to-south, back here, again and again, for as long as the weather allowed and past it.

Behind her, in the main room, a child coughed. Mara's footsteps crossed the floorboards, steady as a metronome, going toward it.

Odile wiped the window clear with her sleeve and looked out at the escarpment, black against a bruised sky, its upper reaches already wrapped in cloud. Somewhere up there was the pass. Somewhere up there was Theo. And somewhere beyond it, tomorrow night, a plane carrying medicine and a stubborn mechanic would come through weather that should ground it, because she'd asked, because asking was finally something she knew how to do.

She went back to the ward, took up a basin, and started on the next fever.
