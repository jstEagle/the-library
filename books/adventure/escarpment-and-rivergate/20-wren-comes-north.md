## 20. Wren Comes North

The grounding order came down at four in the afternoon, on paper, which was how Wren knew it was serious. Nobody bothered with paper for small lies.

*Certificate of Airworthiness suspended pending investigation. Aircraft C-GROD grounded at Kettle Falls Municipal until such time as—*

Wren read it twice under the flickering fluorescent tube in the FBO office, then folded it into quarters and put it in a pocket over her heart, where she kept things she intended to deal with personally. The clerk behind the counter—a young man with a provincial government lanyard and the posture of someone who had never been rained on—watched her fold it with the wariness of a man watching a mechanic handle a tool he didn't understand.

"You'll want to sign the acknowledgment," he said.

"I'll want a lot of things," Wren said. "Where's your supervisor?"

"Gone home."

"Of course he has." She signed the acknowledgment, because refusing would only give them a second charge to wave around, and walked out onto the apron into weather that smelled like the whole sky had been wrung out and hung up to dry again.

C-GROD sat on the tie-down where they'd left her, chained by regulation, looking less like an airplane than like a hostage. Rain sheeted off her wings in silver ropes. The tail number was half-hidden under a government inspection sticker some functionary had slapped on without even cleaning the surface first, and that—more than the order itself, more than the falsified-paperwork charge, more than the shell company initials Odile had traced on a napkin two nights ago—that casual disrespect made something go off in Wren's chest like a circuit breaker tripping in reverse.

They had filed a complaint about *her* airplane. About the engine she'd rebuilt with her own hands, torque-wrench and feeler gauge, every bolt kissed once before it went in. They'd claimed the maintenance logs were falsified, and the worst part—the part that kept her up at night staring at the motel ceiling—was that the logs *were* incomplete. Because someone had torn pages out of them nine years ago, back when Wren was still learning to bleed brakes, and Odile's father had carried the gap in those books like a stone in his boot until the day he died.

Halvard hadn't forged anything. Halvard had just found the wound and pressed on it.

* * *

She spent the evening doing what she always did when the world turned illegible: she worked.

The FBO hangar had a corner the manager rented out to transients, and Wren rented it with cash and no conversation. She laid out her tools on a rubber mat in the exact order of use, left to right, the way her mother had taught her in a garage in Lagos that smelled of solvent and fried plantain, and she started pulling panels off GROD's port side by lamplight while the rain played percussion on the roof.

Not because the plane needed it. Because thinking required her hands to be busy, and because somewhere in the back of her skull a very quiet voice had been repeating the same sentence since the grounding order landed:

*They don't ground you unless they're afraid of where you're going.*

That was the thing about paperwork. It looked neutral. It looked like process, like procedure, like the boring machinery of bureaucracy grinding along its rails. But Wren had spent fifteen years listening to machines tell the truth underneath what people said about them, and she could hear this one clearly. The complaint had been filed eleven days ago—before the fever call from Rivergate, before Odile and Cass's night flight, before any of it became urgent enough to matter. Someone had set this trap in advance and simply waited for them to walk into it. Which meant the goal was never safety. The goal was delay. Ground the planes, burn the calendar, let the monsoon close the passes, and by spring there'd be nothing left in Rivergate to save and nothing left to prove.

Weather as a weapon. Weather as an alibi. Somewhere in a glass office, a man was watching satellite loops the way other men watched horse races.

Wren tightened a bungee cord, tested a fuel line, and let herself think the thought she'd been circling for three days.

The order said the aircraft couldn't fly. It did not say the aircraft couldn't be *moved*. And it named one aircraft, by registration—C-GROD. It did not name the second plane, the battered old Skywagon Cass had ferried north and left parked at the far end of the field with a cracked windscreen and an engine that ran rough above eight thousand feet. An engine Wren had been quietly rebuilding in stolen hours for a week, on the theory that Cass would need it eventually, because Wren had learned long ago that in this business you fixed things before you knew why.

The Skywagon wasn't grounded. The Skywagon wasn't even mentioned.

Wren sat back on her heels in the lamplight and looked at her hands, grease in every crease, and understood that she had already decided. She'd probably decided the moment she read the order. Maybe the moment Odile's radio call from Rivergate had come through thin and crackling, describing fever wards and children with wet lungs and a nurse named Mara working twenty-hour shifts with expired antibiotics—and Odile's voice, steady as a level flight, except Wren had known that voice for six years and could hear the fault line running under it.

Love, her mother used to say, is not a feeling. Feelings are weather. Love is what you build that stands in it.

Wren wiped her hands, packed her tools, and went to find a fuel truck operator who owed her a favor.

* * *

The favor cost her a bottle of rye and a promise she'd help re-seal his roof come June, and it bought her four hundred liters of avgas pumped into drums after midnight, plus the truck itself parked where the cameras didn't reach. The field security at Kettle Falls consisted of one dog and a floodlight with a dying ballast, and the dog liked her, so that was settled.

At two in the morning she preflighted the Skywagon by flashlight, rain hammering the hangar doors, and gave the engine the kind of examination most people reserved for suspicious packages. Magneto check. Compression check. The number-three cylinder she'd honed and re-ringed was holding pressure like a held breath. The cracked windscreen she'd stop-drilled and sealed with epoxy and a plate of clear acrylic cut from a skylight panel—ugly, legal-adjacent, and structurally sound, which described most of Wren Adeyemi's life.

There was one more thing, and she stood in front of the open cowling for a long moment before she did it.

In her toolbox, wrapped in a shop rag, was the tie-rod. The original one—the one she'd found filed nearly through, hidden in the depths of GROD's nose gear weeks ago, the first whisper of sabotage. She'd meant to turn it over to the authorities. Then the authorities turned out to be a clerk with a lanyard and a supervisor who went home at four, and the tie-rod had stayed wrapped in its rag like evidence in a trial that would never be scheduled.

She unwrapped it now and set it on the workbench under the light. Filed metal gleams a particular way. You can't fake it; you can't un-file it. Anyone who knew metalwork would look at it and know exactly what it was: intent. Somebody had wanted that landing gear to fail, wanted it at speed, wanted it on a runway or worse, and had been patient about it, drawing the file across the steel night after night like a man whittling toward a conclusion.

Wren photographed it from six angles with the old camera she kept for exactly this purpose—documentation being the mechanic's version of testimony—and then wrapped it back up and stowed it in the baggage compartment behind the spare oil. If she went down in the mountains, somebody should find it. If she didn't, she'd put it in front of the right eyes herself.

Then she climbed in, lit the engine, and taxied out into the dark with no flight plan, no clearance, and no permission, which was, she reflected as the tailwheel came light and the rain blurred the runway lights into smears of amber, either the bravest or stupidest thing she'd ever done, and she genuinely could not tell which.

The Skywagon lifted off at 2:47 a.m., heavy with fuel and medicine crates she'd bought with the last of her savings, and the weather swallowed her whole.

* * *

Flying at night in the monsoon's leading edge was not flying so much as negotiated passage through a series of arguments between air masses, none of which cared about her opinion.

The rain came in curtains you could hear before you hit them—first as static on the airframe, then as a sound like gravel thrown against the fuselage, then as white noise so total it erased the engine. Lightning stitched the cloud deck ahead in silent flashes, each one printing the storm's interior architecture on her retinas: towers, anvils, canyons of black. The radar in the Skywagon was older than some of her tools and painted the world in smeared greens and yellows, and Wren flew it the way her mother had taught her to drive in Lagos rain—by feel, by rhythm, by reading the machine's mood through her fingertips on the yoke.

Between cells, the air went glassy and strange, and the moon broke through in patches, lighting the cloud tops below her like a drowned country. She talked to herself on the intercom because there was no one else to talk to.

"Number three's running hot but honest," she told the empty right seat. "Windscreen's holding. Fuel's fine. You're fine." A pause. "We're fine."

The empty right seat did not answer, but it was not entirely empty in her mind. Six years of Odile sat in it—Odile in the hangar at Escarpment, arguing torque specs with her hands jammed in her coverall pockets; Odile asleep in the crew room with a maintenance manual tented over her face; Odile saying, three months ago, in the flat careful voice she used for the truest things: *"I'm not good at staying, Wren. I want you to know that going in."*

And Wren had said, because she was also not good at the truest things: *"I know how to fix things that shake loose."*

Which had sounded like banter. It had even felt like banter. But here she was at eleven thousand feet in the teeth of a storm system that the forecasters had given names to, flying an ungrounded-but-not-exactly-legal airplane north toward a valley full of sick people and one specific stubborn pilot, and it occurred to her with the clarity of lightning that she had never once considered turning back. Not at the fuel stop. Not at the cell that closed over her like a fist. Not now, with ice starting to glaze the wing roots and the outside air temperature falling through freezing in a way the charts said shouldn't happen until further north.

Love as a decision made in bad weather. Her mother again. The woman had been full of lines like that, and Wren had spent thirty years pretending they embarrassed her.

She climbed to stay out of the icing layer and the engine coughed—the mixture too rich in the thin cold air—and for eleven seconds, eleven seconds that lasted approximately the length of her entire life, the propeller windmilled and the world went silent except for rain and her own heartbeat, loud as a mallet on a hangar door. Then she got the mixture leaned, and the engine caught with a bang that she felt in her spine, and the Skywagon surged forward like a dog hitting the end of its rope.

"Right," Wren said aloud, to the engine, to the storm, to the federal aviation administration, to the Halvard Group and all its initials and shell companies and filed tie-rods. "Right. We're not done."

* * *

Dawn came up gray-green and violent over the escarpment foothills, and with it the last hundred miles, the ones the charts marked with hatched lines and euphemisms like *terrain rises abruptly*.

She'd planned to approach from the west, over the low shoulder of the range, following the river valley the way supply flights were supposed to come in. But the valley was socked solid—she could see the fog bank sitting in it from twenty miles out, a white river thicker than the actual river—and the ceiling was pressing down toward the ridgelines like a lid closing on a pot.

So she did the thing Odile would have done, which was the thing Odile's father must have done, which was the thing nobody was supposed to do: she flew the contour. Low along the foothills, in the gap between the fog below and the rock above, threading a corridor maybe six hundred feet wide in places, her eyes burning from not blinking, the windscreen wiper—hand-cranked, absurd, essential—squeaking its arc across the acrylic patch.

Twice she turned. Twice the terrain rose faster than the airplane could climb, and she banked away with her stomach in her throat and found another line. The third time, there was no other line, just a saddle between two peaks with cloud pouring over it like dry ice, and she made herself say it out loud:

"Theo flew this."

Nobody knew exactly where Theo Dunmore had gone down. The official report said *weather-related loss of control in the pass*, which was a sentence built to end conversations. But Wren had spent years around pilots' families, and she knew that the survivors kept their own maps, their own coordinates, their own private geography of grief. Odile had never shared hers. But Odile had gone rigid, once, over a chart of this exact quadrant, her finger stopping a centimeter short of a spot on the ridge and then lifting away like the paper was hot.

Wren crossed the saddle at full power with fifty feet of margin and the cloud swallowing her tail, and she did not let herself think about whose shadow she might be flying through. There would be time for that later, or there wouldn't, and either way the only thing that mattered was the next sixty seconds of flying, and the next, and the next.

The valley opened beneath her like a held breath released.

Rivergate from the air was smaller than she'd imagined—four hundred souls strung along a river bend, rooftops dark with rain, fields gone to seed, and at the south end a strip of gravel that some optimistic soul decades ago had called an airstrip. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys. On the schoolhouse roof, someone had painted a huge white cross as a ground reference, fresh paint bleeding in the wet, and beside it, arranged in rocks the size of armchairs, a word:

*DUNMORE*

Wren's eyes stung, and she blamed the wind through the cracked seal, and began her descent.

* * *

The landing was ugly and she knew it before the wheels touched—crosswind gusting forty off the ridge, the strip soft near the midpoint where water had gotten under the gravel, the tail swinging twice before she wrestled it straight. She rolled out at the far end with the engine sputtering on the last dregs of the left tank, and shut down, and sat for a moment in the sudden enormous quiet, listening to the rain tick on the cooling cowl and her own breath sawing in and out.

Then she saw them coming across the field—figures in rain gear, running, and at the front of them a woman in a green flight jacket moving with that stride Wren would have known in a crowd of ten thousand, that stride that was somehow both hurry and refusal-to-hurry at once.

Wren popped the door and swung out onto the gravel on legs that had stopped consulting her sometime over the escarpment, and the adrenaline that had carried her four hundred miles chose that exact moment to file its resignation. The ground tilted. She caught the strut.

Odile reached her first, and grabbed her—not a hug, exactly, more like an inspection conducted with both hands, checking wings and fuselage and face for damage—and her voice came out wrecked and furious and shaking:

"You flew *through that?* Alone? In *that?*"

"Somebody had to bring the amoxicillin," Wren said. "You were busy."

"You could have—" Odile stopped. Started again. Stopped. Nine years of sealed cockpit doors, and Wren watched her fail to find a single sentence adequate to the moment, and loved her so much it felt structural, load-bearing, like something the whole airframe now depended on.

"You're grounded," Odile managed finally. "Officially. If anyone finds out you flew—"

"They'll have to catch me." Wren straightened up, pried open the cargo door, and started hauling crates of medicine into the rain. "Besides, I brought evidence. The tie-rod. Photographed, dated, chain-of-custody written out in my own hand. Whatever they've got on us, we've got something better on them."

Behind Odile, the townspeople had formed a line to the crates without being asked, passing them hand to hand toward the clinic, and an older man with a surveyor's stoop took one look at Wren's mud-caked tailwheel and said, in a voice pitched to carry: "Flies like her father did."

Odile flinched. Wren stepped closer, shoulder to shoulder, and took the next crate herself.

It was Mara, the nurse, who brought the news that dropped the temperature ten degrees colder than the rain—a woman running from the clinic with her hood blown back, shouting over the wind: "The upper pass—it's moving! Rockfall this morning, big one—the trail to the mine road is buried, and Odile, the scarp above the water intake, it's *cracking*—"

Wren looked at Odile and saw the color leave her face, and understood, with the certainty of a woman who had just flown through a storm to get here, that the clock they'd been racing had just started running much, much faster.
