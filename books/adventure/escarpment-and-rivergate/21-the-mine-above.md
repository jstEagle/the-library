## 21. The Mine Above

They left before first light, when the valley still held its breath and the river ran gray with snowmelt, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge Odile could see the scar on the mountainside that no one in Rivergate had been willing to name.

It sat above the pass like a wound that had never closed—a pale slash of exposed rock and talus where the timberline gave up trying to hold on. Nine years ago it had been green. She knew that the way she knew her own hands. Theo had flown over that slope a hundred times, and once, laughing across the radio from Kettle Falls to Escarpment, he'd told her the hillside above the pass was so thick with pine that from altitude it looked like a rug someone had thrown over the mountain's shoulder. *You could land a plane in that trees if you were stupid enough*, he'd said. *I'm not stupid enough. Yet.*

The word *yet* had come back to her for nine years, arriving at odd hours, patient as weather.

Now the slope was bare as a skull.

"You're staring," Cass said beside her. She had a pack on, an actual hiking pack with a bedroll strapped under it, because Cass Merrow did nothing halfway and had never once in her life packed light for a short trip. Her boots were new—city boots, bought in some provincial capital—and she was already favoring her left ankle on the scree.

"I'm looking at the mountain."

"You're staring at the mountain. There's a difference. One is reconnaissance. The other is grief." Cass adjusted her pack straps and nodded up the trail. "We can grieve at the top. It'll keep."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. "Since when do you hike?"

"Since I started suspecting that everything worth knowing about this valley is written somewhere you can't fly to." Cass set off up the switchback without waiting, which was also very Cass. "Come on, Dunmore. Daylight's a currency and we're spending it."

* * *

The trail was not really a trail. It was a surveyor's line, cut decades ago and half-swallowed since, marked every hundred meters by iron posts gone orange with rust. Odile's father had walked this line once—she understood that now, walking it herself, feeling the rhythm of the grade settle into her legs the way a familiar approach pattern settles into your hands. He'd walked it with his instruments and his two children trailing behind him, and he'd taught them to read a mountain the way other fathers taught their kids to read scripture: reverently, suspiciously, always aware that the text could kill you.

She remembered being eleven, standing on one of these switchbacks, and her father crouching to press his palm flat against the rock.

"Feel that?" he'd said. "Cold comes out of the stone in summer. That means there's water moving inside it. A mountain is never solid, Odile. It's a held breath. Your job is to know how long it can hold."

Theo, eight years old and already incapable of leaving anything alone, had asked what happened when it couldn't hold anymore.

Her father had looked up the slope—at the green hillside, then, whole and breathing—and said, "Then everyone downstream finds out what they're made of."

She hadn't thought about that morning in twenty years. Now it walked beside her the whole way up, quiet as a third hiker.

Cass, to her credit, didn't fill the silence. That was another thing nine years of rivalry had taught Odile about her: Cass Merrow talked constantly when she wanted something and went silent as ballast when she was working a problem, and right now she was working one. Every fifty meters she stopped, pulled a battered handheld GPS from her jacket, took a reading, and wrote it in a waterproof notebook balanced on her knee. She'd been doing it since the valley floor. Odile had watched her do it forty times without asking why.

Finally she did ask.

"Ground truth," Cass said, not looking up. "I flew this ridge twice last month, on the contract runs. From the air I logged subsidence along the upper bench—three centimeters between passes, maybe four. You don't get three centimeters of settlement on stable rock. You get it when something underneath is being removed." She snapped the notebook shut. "I wanted to see if the ground agreed with my instruments."

"And?"

"We're not there yet." But her jaw was set in a way Odile recognized from cockpit arguments, the set it got when Cass already knew the answer and was furious about it.

* * *

They reached the treeline at midmorning and the old mine adit just after noon.

Or rather, they reached where the adit should have been, because the entrance had been rebuilt. The original opening—the one from the disaster-era surveys, the one that should have been sealed, collapsed, forgotten—had been squared off with fresh-cut timber. New lagging. Steel plate over the portal mouth, bolted, with a sliding gate that had been oiled recently enough to shine. A generator shed squatted against the hillside beside it, camouflaged in drab paint that fooled no one, its exhaust pipe venting a thin thread of smoke into the cold air.

And the sound. Odile stopped dead twenty meters below the portal because the sound reached her through her boot soles before it reached her ears: a deep, rhythmic concussion, felt more than heard, rolling out of the mountain at intervals of perhaps ninety seconds. Each one made the scree shift faintly around their feet, a whisper of small stones finding new positions, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"They're blasting," she said. The words came out flat, stripped of surprise, because some part of her had known since Kettle Falls, since the surveyor's map, since Theo's note. Knowing a thing and standing inside it were different countries. "Nine years later. They never stopped."

"No," Cass said quietly. "They industrialized."

She moved forward along the slope with the GPS held out in front of her like a divining rod, taking readings every ten meters now, and Odile followed, and together they worked their way around the shoulder of the hill to the overlook her father's survey had marked with a simple triangle and the notation *BENCH OBS. PT.*

From there they could see down into the working.

It was bigger than an operation. It was an industry. Two terraces had been cut into the upper bench, haul roads spiraling between them, a crusher plant humming behind a berm, muck piles stacked in neat geometric windrows. An excavator the size of a house was loading trucks whose tires stood taller than Odile. Everything was orderly. Everything was efficient. Everything was designed, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the altitude, to be invisible from below—from Rivergate, from the pass, from any angle a supply pilot would ever approach from. The ridgeline itself hid it. You would have to fly the impossible route, the route no one flew, the route that killed Theo Dunmore, to see this from the air.

That was why the corridor mattered. That was why the map mattered. Her brother hadn't died stumbling onto this. He'd died *finding* it.

"He saw this," Odile said. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, like a radio call from far out on the fringe. "He was mapping the corridor and he saw this, and he turned back to report it, and they brought the slope down on him."

"Maybe." Cass was crouched at the edge of the observation point, notebook open, writing fast. "Or maybe he saw the beginning of it and they decided he'd seen too much. Either way—" She looked up, and Odile saw something in her face she'd only seen once before, the night Cass confessed who had paid her to force the contract: fury wearing competence like a mask. "Either way, look at the bench, Odile. Look at it properly. Not as evidence. As a pilot."

So Odile did what she'd spent her whole life doing. She read terrain.

The upper terrace had been undercut. She could see it plainly from here—the haul road carved along the base of the headwall, the excavation biting back into the slope's foundation, the resulting face unsupported, fractured, weeping groundwater in a dozen silver threads that hadn't been there in any survey. The waste rock had been dumped on the downhill margin of the bench, tens of thousands of tonnes of it, loading the slope exactly where the slope was weakest. And below all of it, below the terraces and the muck and the fractured headwall, the mountainside funneled down into a single steep gully that ran—

Her stomach dropped.

—that ran straight down to the river. Not near the river. To it. To the bend where the water pooled slow and deep behind the natural gravel bar that Rivergate had drawn its drinking water from for a hundred years. The intake pipes were visible even from here, small dark lines running from the riverbank toward the town's pumphouse, thin as stitches.

Every gram of that slope, if it let go, would arrive in the river. The slide wouldn't hit the town—it wouldn't need to. It would bury the intake, dam or divert the water, choke the valley's artery with mine spoil, and Rivergate would die of thirst with its houses still standing. Or worse: the slide mass would plug the channel, pond the river behind it, and then fail catastrophically a day or a week later and take the lower town out with the flood.

"It's not abandonment," Odile heard herself say. "It's not even carelessness. This is geometry. They've built a loaded gun pointing at the town and they've done it with engineering drawings."

"Yes."

"The Halvard Group owns the land cheap because nobody wants to live downstream of a disaster. So they make the disaster." The words tasted like copper. "They don't need to buy Rivergate out. They just need to wait for the mountain to evict everyone. Survivors sell for nothing. Dead men sell for less."

Cass closed the notebook. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the mountain spoke instead—another deep concussion rolling out of the portal behind them, the scree whispering, a hawk somewhere below them riding the updraft off the heated rock with what seemed, absurdly, like indifference.

"There's more," Cass said finally. "And you're not going to want to hear it."

"Say it anyway."

Cass turned the notebook around. On the page was a hand-drawn cross-section, quick but precise—she'd sketched it while Odile was reading the terrain, Odile realized; the woman worked like a machine. The slope profile showed the excavation, the waste dump, the failure surface she'd inferred, and then a dashed arrow arcing down the gully to the river.

"Monsoon arrives in four days, maybe five. Sustained rain on a saturated, fractured, overloaded slope." She tapped the failure surface. "This isn't a someday problem, Odile. The groundwater is already weeping at the crown. First real storm cell parks over this ridge and dumps two hundred millimeters, and the pore pressure alone will finish what the blasting started. We are standing on a countdown, and the clock is weather."

Odile looked at the intake lines, small as hairs against the river's gray braid. She thought of Mara in the fever ward, hauling water bucket by bucket because the gravity feed couldn't be spared for washing. She thought of the town meeting, four hundred people voting with their chins up to stay and fight for the valley. Fight for it with what? With water full of mine tailings?

"How long have they known?" she asked. "Halvard. Their engineers. How long has somebody signed off on this?"

Cass was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its edge and gained something heavier. "My contact in the capital sent me the environmental filing last week. Buried in an appendix. Their own geotechnical consultant flagged the slope stability in year two. Recommended de-stocking the waste dump immediately. The recommendation is initialed, acknowledged, and filed." She met Odile's eyes. "They've known for seven years. They've been waiting out the clock, same as us. Except their plan for when it goes is lawyers, and ours has to be airplanes."

* * *

They started down at one o'clock, because Cass said so—because daylight was a currency and the descent was slower than the climb and neither of them trusted the trail in the dark—but at the second switchback below the overlook, the mountain made its argument.

The concussion came different that time. Deeper. Longer. Not the rhythmic work-blast cadence they'd been hearing all afternoon but a single grinding report from higher on the headwall, followed by a sound Odile knew from nightmares: the hiss-and-rattle of rock beginning to move.

"Down!" Cass grabbed her arm and hauled her against the cut bank of the switchback, both of them pressed into the slope, packs over heads, as the dust came down the gully above them—not the main mass, not yet, just a tongue of it, a few hundred tonnes of talus letting loose from the toe of the waste dump and running out downslope in a gray boiling wave that passed a hundred meters to their left and kept going, and going, and going, down and down, until it reached the bottom of the gully and stopped with a sound like a door closing.

The dust hung in the cold air for a long time. Through it, faintly, they could hear the mine generators still humming. Nobody up top had even paused.

Odile found she was shaking. Not from fear—or not only from fear—but from the specific, nauseating recognition of scale. That had been a sneeze. A minor slough, a maintenance event, the kind of thing a site foreman would note in a logbook and schedule grading for. And it had traveled a kilometer and a half.

"That wasn't monsoon," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "That was today. Ordinary Tuesday. No rain, no trigger. The slope is already failing."

"I know." Cass was brushing dust off the notebook, checking it, checking the GPS, her hands doing their work while her face did something else entirely. "First wave of the evacuation leaves tomorrow at dawn. We tell them tonight. All of it—the mine, the timeline, the water. No more deciding what the town can bear. They bear what they bear; they decide with the whole board showing."

Odile looked back up the slope one last time. The dust was settling. The scar on the mountain caught the afternoon light, raw and pale, and she understood suddenly that she had spent nine years treating it as a grave—as finished, as past, as the fixed coordinates of the worst day of her life. It wasn't a grave. It was a fuse. The past wasn't behind them at all. It was overhead, loaded, and burning downward toward everything she'd sworn to protect.

"Your father used to say a mountain is a held breath," Cass said quietly, watching her.

"I remember."

"Well." Cass shouldered her pack and started down. "It's exhaling."

They descended fast after that, trading the lead on the bad sections, saying little, the town growing below them with every switchback—smoke from the cookhouse, the airstrip's windsock hanging limp, the tiny bright rectangles of windows coming on as the light failed. By the time they reached the valley floor the sun was gone and the first stars were out, hard and cold, and somewhere above them in the dark the mountain shifted again, a low groan rolling down the valley like a voice remembering an old grievance.

Odile was still fifty meters from the strip lights when she saw Wren come out of the hangar, lantern in hand, scanning the trailhead. Watching for them. Had been watching, she'd have bet anything, since sundown.

Wren raised the lantern, and the light caught their face—worry first, then relief, then something sharper as they read what the walk down had written on both women.

"You'd better come inside," Wren called across the dark grass. "Both of you. Because an hour ago a truck came through from the north, and the man driving it asked a lot of questions about who went up the mountain today."
