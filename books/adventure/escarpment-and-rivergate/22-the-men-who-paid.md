## 22. The Men Who Paid

Cass had been in a lot of rooms where bad news was about to happen, and she'd learned to read them the way she read weather: by the pressure in the air before anything actually broke.

The Rivergate nurse's quarters—Mara's quarters—was a small room with two cots, a kerosene lamp turned low, and a window that rattled every time the wind came off the escarpment. Wren sat on the edge of one cot with their arm bound and their knuckles split, refusing to lie down the way only Wren could refuse: by simply not doing it, with tremendous dignity. Odile stood at the window with her back to all of them, watching the dark, which was what Odile did instead of pacing. And Mara moved between them with bandages and boiled water like a woman who had long ago stopped asking why trouble kept arriving on her doorstep and had started simply budgeting for it.

The man from the truck was gone. He'd driven north an hour ago, back toward Kettle Falls, after asking his questions—who went up the mountain, how many were on the strip, whether the survey party had found anything worth reporting—and after Cass had lied to him with a smile so smooth it should have been illegal in three provinces. But his questions had done what questions do. They'd opened a seam, and everything Cass had been carrying since Escarpment was now pressing against it.

She looked at Odile's back—at the set of those shoulders, the way her hand rested on the windowsill as if the sill might bolt—and understood that she could not carry it alone anymore. Not because she was weak. Because the trap only worked on people who were isolated, and the whole architecture of the thing depended on each of them believing they were alone inside it.

"Cass," Odile said, without turning. "You've got your lawyer face on."

"I don't have a lawyer face."

"You have three faces. Pilot, rival, and the one you make when you're deciding how much truth a room can hold." Odile turned then, and the lamplight caught the exhaustion under her eyes, nine years of it stacked behind tonight's fresh layer. "How much can this room hold?"

Cass took a breath. Somewhere outside, the river said its one long word against the banks.

"All of it," she said. "It has to be all of it, because I think we've been flying for them this whole time. Both of us. Since the beginning."

* * *

"Start with the debt," Wren said. They'd finally allowed themselves to sit properly, but their good hand stayed closed around a wrench they'd apparently brought into the room out of pure habit, like a talisman. "The airstrip debt. That's where it started for me."

"That's where it started for everyone," Cass said. "That's the point. It starts small and boring. Nobody gets suspicious of paperwork." She pulled the folded telegram from her jacket—the one she'd carried for months, read a hundred times, never shown anyone—and laid it on the cot between them. "Eight months ago I was broke. Not struggling-broke. Finished-broke. My charter operation in the south had gone under, my name was mud in half the licensing offices, and I was drinking coffee in a Kettle Falls diner trying to decide which province to fail in next. A man sat down across from me. Never introduced himself. Suit too good for the town, shoes too clean for the road. He put a folder on the table and said, 'Merrow Aviation is a going concern again, if you want it.'"

Odile had come away from the window. She lowered herself onto the opposite cot, elbows on knees, and Cass watched her go very still in the way she did when she was listening with her whole body.

"The folder had a purchase agreement for Dunmore Field's debt," Cass went on. "Every note, every lien, the collateral clause on the strip itself. Pre-negotiated. All I had to do was sign my name as buyer and show up. There was money behind it—a lot of money, routed through something called the Halvard Group, though the man never used the word *group*, he just said Halvard like it was weather, like it was something that simply existed and you planned around it." She tapped the telegram. "And there was one condition. One line item in a contract forty pages long. The new owner would take the Rivergate resupply contract, and the new owner would require Odile Dunmore to fly it."

The lamp hissed. Wind pressed at the rattling window.

"I told him no," Cass said. "I want that on the record, Odile, because I know what you're thinking right now, and I need you to hear it straight. I told him to get out of my booth. And he smiled—he had a smile like a filing cabinet closing—and he said, 'You will. When you understand what's in the rest of the folder.' And the rest of the folder—" Her voice caught, not from grief exactly, but from the old anger of a person who has been handled. "The rest of the folder was me. Every mistake I ever made, every license suspension, every debt, every letter of complaint, some of them true and some of them not, all of it documented, all of it ready to land on the aviation board's desk the week before my recertification. He didn't threaten me. He just showed me the weather report and let me draw my own conclusions."

"So you signed," Odile said quietly.

"So I signed." Cass met her eyes. "I told myself I'd fly the contract, keep the strip alive, and find out who Halvard was and what they wanted with Rivergate, and then burn them down with whatever I found. I told myself that in the diner, and I believed it for about six weeks. Then I started pulling records, and I stopped believing anything except that this goes deeper than one man in good shoes."

* * *

She got up and paced, because some truths wouldn't come out sitting down. She'd learned that in depositions.

"Halvard Group. Registered holding company, provincial capital, incorporated eleven years ago—which means it was created *after* the disaster, not before, which tells you something. On paper it does land acquisition and resource development consulting. In practice?" She counted on her fingers, and her voice flattened into the register she used for weight-and-balance calculations, cold and exact. "In practice, since the year after the slide, Halvard or its subsidiaries has bought four hundred and sixty parcels in and around Rivergate. Farmland. Timber rights. Water access. Commercial lots on the main street. Bought from families who lost everything in the disaster and couldn't pay their mortgages on a valley with no road in. Bought at panic prices—I pulled the assessment rolls, Odile, they paid thirty cents on the dollar, sometimes less. And they bought through eleven different shell companies so no single name ever appears twice."

Wren had stopped pretending to fidget with the wrench. "Four hundred parcels. How much of the valley is that?"

"By my math? Better than half the private land. And here's the part that kept me up nights." Cass turned to face both of them. "Nobody develops it. Eleven years of ownership and there's no construction, no logging, no mining claims worked. The houses they bought are falling down. The fields are going to brush. A company doesn't sit on assets like that unless the assets are waiting for something. Unless the price is about to change."

"The mine," Odile said.

"The mine," Cass agreed. "Which we haven't proven yet—we saw blasting, we saw fresh workings, we saw a man in a truck asking questions—but if there's an active operation above that pass, then the escarpment isn't stable and everybody in this valley knows what happens when it isn't stable. Another slide. Another disaster. And another round of land sold for nothing by people who've already buried one generation of their dead." She let that sit in the room like ballast. "Halvard isn't buying Rivergate despite the disaster, Odile. Halvard has been farming the disaster. It's their crop. It comes up every nine years or so, whenever the mountain gives them a harvest."

Odile's face had gone the color of the lamp's weak light. Cass watched her arrive at the arithmetic—the same arithmetic Cass herself had hit at two in the morning in a Kettle Falls motel, staring at parcel maps until the numbers swam.

"And the first disaster," Odile said. Each word placed carefully, like cargo being loaded. "Nine years ago. Theo. The pass. You're telling me—"

"I'm telling you what the pattern says, and you can hate me for saying it. If Halvard was incorporated within a year of the slide—if they started buying distressed land immediately, at scale, with financing nobody can trace—then either they got very lucky, or they knew the slide was coming. Or." Cass made herself finish it. "Or they helped it along. We don't have proof of that yet. What we have is a mine above the pass that shouldn't exist, a company that profits every time the mountain moves, and a resupply contract designed to put a Dunmore back over that escarpment at exactly the moment the story needed a Dunmore in it."

"Why?" The word cracked out of Odile harder than she'd meant it; Cass saw her regret it and press on anyway. "Why me? Why would they want me anywhere near that pass?"

And there it was. The center of it. Cass sat back down across from her, because this part deserved eye contact, deserved to be delivered like a diagnosis.

"Because you swore it off," she said. "Because your father blacked out the route in his own ledger and tore the pages from the maintenance logs, and because for nine years you have built yourself into a woman who does not look at that mountain. Think about what that makes you, from the outside. A pilot with a family connection to Rivergate—which gives the operation legitimacy, *history*, the kind of story a company loves. But also a pilot who'll never dig, who'll never ask old-timers about the slide, who'll fly the route with her eyes forward and her grief locked in the cockpit. You're not just a good pilot, Odile. You're the perfect deniable one. If anything happens to you up there—an accident, a disappearance, a plane that goes in at the same coordinates as your brother's—they don't even have to explain it. Everyone in two provinces will say *that family and that pass, it was always going to happen.* Your tragedy is their alibi. It was pre-built. Nine years in the making, free of charge."

The silence afterward had texture. Cass heard Wren's breath go ragged and even heard, distantly, the fever ward's generator cycling somewhere across the compound. Odile hadn't moved. Then, slowly, she raised one hand and pressed it flat against her sternum, as if checking that the blow had landed where aimed.

"They didn't force me onto the route," she said. "They *inherited* me onto it. Like the debt. Like the ledger."

"Like everything your father left," Cass said, gentler now. "I'm sorry. I've known for months and I've been flying beside you letting you walk into it, because every time I tried to say it, it sounded insane, and because—" She stopped. Made herself be honest. "Because I was afraid that if I told you, you'd refuse the flight, and if you refused the flight, whoever's running this would know I'd talked, and I've seen what happens to people who talk. So I did the cowardly version of the right thing. I flew the job and gathered evidence and told myself I was protecting you by lying to you. Which is exactly the kind of sentence men in good shoes want women like us saying."

* * *

Wren spoke into the quiet, voice rough. "The sabotage. The hydraulics, the tie-rod. And then the paperwork complaint that grounded us, and the engine fire at Kettle Falls, and my arm." They lifted the bandaged forearm slightly, evidence entered into the record. "Same hand behind all of it?"

"Follow the shape of it," Cass said. "Somebody wants the Rivergate flights to fail—but not loudly. Not with a crash, not yet. Drained hydraulics that a good mechanic catches on preflight. A filed tie-rod. Falsified-paperwork complaints that ground us legally instead of killing us. An engine burned on the ground rather than in the air." She ticked them off. "That's not sabotage meant to destroy. That's sabotage meant to *delay*. To burn our days while the monsoon closes the passes, so that when the window shuts, the failure looks like weather. Act of God. Nobody's fault. Rivergate stays cut off, the fever runs, the town gets desperate enough to sell anything to anyone who offers a road out—and guess who owns the roads out, on paper, already."

"Or desperate enough to evacuate," Mara said from the doorway.

Cass hadn't heard her come back in. The nurse stood with a tray of instruments she'd already used, looking at the three of them with the flat patience of someone who had spent nine years watching a valley be slowly taken apart and calling it misfortune.

"If they evacuate," Mara said, "they evacuate onto Halvard land. There's a camp site past the north ridge—company-owned, surveyed last spring, nobody's ever explained why. I've seen the stakes." She set the tray down. "Whatever you three are circling, I've been watching it circle us for a decade. Say the names. Out loud. Names are the one thing they can't buy back."

Cass looked at Odile. Odile gave a single nod—not permission, exactly; more like a woman stepping off a ledge and inviting company.

"Halvard Group," Cass said. "Holding company, provincial capital. Behind it, as far as I've traced: a director named Edmund Voss, who was a land surveyor in this valley twenty years ago and knows every parcel worth having. Under him, an acquisitions man named Corliss—you've met his type, the truck driver today answered to him, I'd bet my license on it. And above Voss, the money trail goes dark into a trust registered in the capital, but the trust's filing agent shares an address with—" she paused, savoring the ugliness of it, "—the law firm that represented the blasting company cited for safety violations on the original escarpment mine. The same mine your father surveyed around. The same mine Theo was flying when he died."

Odile made a sound that wasn't quite a word. Wren reached across the space between cots and took her hand, and she let them, and Cass looked away to give them the privacy of it, out the rattling window, at the black mass of the escarpment barely visible against the storm sky.

"One more thing," Cass said. "The man in the truck today. Before he drove off, he asked me one question I didn't like. He asked whether the Dunmore girl had been up to the mine yet." She turned back. "He didn't ask if we'd found anything. He asked if *she'd been up there*. Which means somewhere in a filing cabinet in the capital there's a page with your name on it, Odile, and somebody just updated it. We're past the point where this trap closes slow. Whatever they do next, they do it soon—and I don't think it's going to come by truck."

Outside, far off toward the north ridge, a light flickered once in the darkness where no light should have been. Twice. Then gone.

Wren saw it too. Cass knew by the way they were already on their feet, wrench still in hand, heading for the door.

"That'll be the camp site," Mara said quietly. "Somebody's arrived."
