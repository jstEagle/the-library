## 23. Sabotage, Second Round

Wren Adeyemi had always believed that a hangar at night told the truth. Daylight let a plane lie—let the paint hide the corrosion, let the shine fool the eye—but at night, with a work lamp and a bare hand, an engine confessed everything. So Wren worked nights now, had worked them since Kettle Falls, since the drained hydraulics and the filed tie-rod, since the quiet certainty that someone in this valley of mud and fuel drums wanted their airplanes dead.

The spare engine sat on its stand under the lean-to behind the Kettle Falls depot, wrapped in canvas against the drizzle that had been falling for three days like the sky couldn't decide whether to commit to the monsoon or merely rehearse it. It was a good engine. Wren had rebuilt it twice—once in Escarpment with Odile's hands helping hold the torque wrench, once again on the ferry north when the vibration came back and wouldn't be argued with. It was the difference between flying the Rivergate shuttle and not flying it. Between Mara's fever ward getting penicillin or getting prayers.

Wren checked it every night before bed. Not because it needed checking. Because checking was a way of touching something without having to say what it meant.

Tonight the canvas was wrong.

It took a moment to register—the particular wrongness of fabric that had been moved by hands rather than wind. The tie-down knots were re-tied, and nobody retied Wren's knots; even Odile left them alone, a small superstition between them, the mechanic's signature respected like a pilot's preflight ritual. Wren stood in the drizzle with the flashlight off and listened. The depot yard was quiet except for the generator next door and the river talking to itself below the bank. Somewhere up the street, the hotel porch light made a yellow smear in the wet dark.

The Halvard man's name was Corrigan. He'd arrived nine days ago on the mail flight, a soft-spoken man in a rain jacket too clean for this country, carrying a leather portfolio and a smile he rationed out like fuel during a shortage. He represented "regional development interests." He bought drinks at the hotel and didn't drink his own. He asked questions about schedules and weather windows and how many loads the planes could carry, and he wrote everything down, and Wren had watched him write and thought: *there is a man taking inventory of us.*

Wren had said nothing. That was the mistake Wren kept making—saying nothing, watching, cataloguing, hoarding suspicion like a miser while the evidence walked around in daylight wearing a clean rain jacket. Saying nothing had almost killed them on the first leg. Saying nothing had grounded them. And now saying nothing had led to this: a stranger's hands on Wren's knots.

* * *

The decision took four seconds. Go back for Cass, who was asleep at the hotel? Go find the constable, who was two towns over and half a bottle into his retirement? Or open the canvas and see what was being done to the one engine that mattered?

Wren opened the canvas.

The work lamp came up on the second pull, and by its light Wren saw the whole shape of it at once, the way you see a whole storm cell at once from altitude—too big and too late. The fuel line had been loosened at the junction block, not cut—loosened, so it would weep and chafe and fail somewhere over the escarpment where a fire would have no witnesses. And beside the engine stand, stacked neat as cordwood, were three jerry cans that did not belong to Wren. The caps were off. The smell of avgas cut through the rain like a blade through fruit.

Corrigan stepped out of the shadow of the lean-to with the calm of a man who had already finished his arithmetic.

"Evening," he said.

Wren's flashlight was in hand, heavy, aluminum, the kind with a knurled grip. Everything else happened fast and also slowly, the way violence does—the mind recording details it never asked for. Corrigan moving first, not toward Wren but toward the jerry cans, tipping the nearest one so the fuel sheeted across the concrete apron and the engine stand's legs. The match flaring in cupped hands, absurdly domestic, a man lighting a stove. Wren lunging—not for Corrigan, never for Corrigan, for the match, for the flame, because a mechanic knows which enemy kills fastest—and catching him across the shoulders as the match dropped.

The world went orange.

Avgas doesn't burn like fire in stories. It burns like a decision already made—a soft whump, a bloom of heat, and then the light is everywhere and the night is gone. Wren got the second can kicked away, spinning, unlit, and felt the heat take the hair off one forearm and then take the sleeve, and then there were hands on Wren—Corrigan's hands, surprisingly strong, surprisingly practiced—shoving, and the ground coming up, and the edge of the engine stand finding Wren's temple with a bright, ringing honesty that blanked the world to white and then to static.

When the static cleared, Wren was on the wet concrete with blood in one eye and the lean-to burning like a signal pyre, and Corrigan was walking away down the slope toward the dock road, unhurried, a man whose errand was done. He paused once, halfway to the dark, and looked back—not at Wren, at the fire—and adjusted his collar against the rain.

Then he was gone, and the radio shack door stood open, and the code book—the frequency cards, the weather net authentication tables, everything that let them talk to Rivergate and to each other through the storm—was not inside it.

* * *

Fighting the fire alone was stupid and Wren did it anyway, because the engine was right there and the drums were twenty feet away and twenty feet might as well have been a mile. Dragged the stand clear by main strength, scorched both palms through the gloves, beat the flames off the canvas with a wet tarp until the tarp itself burned and the rain finally, grudgingly, did the rest. By the time the depot's night watchman came running with a bucket line, the thing was mostly smoke and ruin and one very much alive engine, singed but sound, standing in a black lake of its own near-death.

Wren sat down hard on the loading ramp afterward and let the watchman's wife pour tea that tasted like woodsmoke and apology. The temple was already swelling. She—Wren had stopped correcting people years ago about the pronouns, let the town call her what it liked, she'd earned the ambiguity honestly enough—she pressed a cold rag to her head and took inventory the way she took inventory of engines: methodically, unsentimentally. Ribs: bruised, probably cracked, one. Hands: burned, functional. Head: concussed, likely, mild, don't sleep yet. Voice: working, which surprised her, because when she tried to speak all that came out at first was Wren's father's name, which made no sense and perfect sense at once.

And the codes were gone. That was the part that sat in the chest like a swallowed stone. Fire could be fought. Burns healed. But the frequencies, the authentication tables, the weather net—those were in a leather folder now walking toward a boat or a truck or a shortwave set in some room where men counted money, and without them Rivergate was a voice in a canyon they couldn't answer. Without them, Mara's fever ward would radio into static and hear nothing back and draw its own conclusions.

Odile landed at dawn.

Wren heard the plane before seeing it—that particular off-key hum of the old cargo hauler with the rebuilt engine, the one Wren knew better than her own heartbeat—and stood up from the depot bench despite everything, because some things outrank injuries. The plane taxied in through ground fog, and Odile dropped from the cockpit door before the props had fully stopped, and Wren watched her face do the thing faces do when they find wreckage and a person still standing in it.

"What happened."

Not a question. Odile's voice had gone flat and precise, the voice she used for weather decisions, and Wren understood suddenly that this was worse than shouting. This was Odile calculating.

"Corrigan," Wren said. "He torched the lean-to. Took the radio codes. I stopped him from burning the engine." A breath. "Mostly stopped him."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. Sit down." And then Odile did the thing Wren would remember longer than the fire: she put both hands on either side of Wren's face, careful of the burn, careful of the temple, and looked at her the way she looked at instruments—reading, actually reading, none of the deflection permitted. "Tell me exactly what you saw."

So Wren told her. All of it. The knots. The loosened fuel line. The match. The unhurried walk away. The open radio shack. And Odile listened without blinking, and Wren watched the flatness in her face crack along one seam—not grief exactly, not rage exactly, something molten underneath both—and when Wren finished, Odile said:

"He took the codes so we can't coordinate the evacuation. He burned the spare so we can't afford a failure. He wants us deaf and one engine short going into the worst weather of the year." Her thumb moved once, unconsciously, along Wren's cheekbone. "You should have come for me first."

"There wasn't time."

"There's always time to not die alone in a fuel yard." And then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe once a season: "I need you functional, Wren. I need you more than I've ever needed anything with wings on it. Don't make me choose between the mission and you. Don't you dare make me choose."

There it was. The choice, laid on the wet concrete between them like a dropped tool. Wren had known it was coming—had known since the filed tie-rod, since the grounding order, since the night flights and the confidences traded across headsets—that loving Odile Dunmore meant eventually standing in a doorway between her and the sky. She just hadn't expected the doorway to be on fire.

"The mission needs the codes replaced," Wren said. "And the codes are replaceable. I built half that net myself. Give me a day and a shortwave and I'll have Rivergate back on the air." She held up her bandaged hands. "These still turn wrenches. These still tap keys. You fly. I fix. That's the deal we've had for nine years and I'm not renegotiating it in a burn ward."

Odile laughed—one broken note, half sob, quickly mastered—and pressed her forehead to Wren's, careful, so careful, and for three seconds neither of them was a pilot or a mechanic or a target. Just two people in the smoke-smell morning, holding on.

Then Odile straightened, and the flatness came back, and Wren saw her look past Wren's shoulder at the burned lean-to and do the arithmetic that pilots do.

"Cass is flying the proof run Thursday," she said. "Survey data, mine records, Theo's log, straight to the capital. Corrigan will know the schedule by tonight—he always knows. Which means he'll move against the plane, not the people, because a wrecked plane looks like weather." She turned back. "And which means somebody has to guard this strip, and the radios, and you, and I can't be in two places."

"Then don't be," Wren said. "Fly your runs. I'll hold the strip."

"Wren—"

"Hold the strip," Wren repeated, and picked up her tool bag with her burned hands, and did not let the wince reach her face, because love, she had learned, was mostly a series of things you refused to show.

* * *

By noon the constable had come and gone, taken statements, pronounced it "vandalism, possibly drunken," and driven back toward his bottle. By afternoon Wren had a shortwave borrowed from the mission church, a soldering iron, and a plan: rebuild the authentication tables from memory, rotate every frequency, and put out word through the grapevine—the trappers, the mail boat, the church network—that the planes would run regardless.

Cass found her under the repaired lean-to at dusk, working by lamplight with her ribs strapped and her hands wrapped.

"You look terrible," Cass said, sitting down on an oil drum without invitation.

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. He's on the evening boat south, first class cabin, drinking something amber." Cass's jaw was tight. "I followed him as far as the dock. He tipped his hat at me. Tipped his hat, Wren. Like we're in a play."

"We are. We're just the only ones who didn't get scripts."

Cass was quiet a moment, watching the lamplight catch the scorch marks on the stand. Then she said, in a different voice, low and stripped of all her usual polish: "They hurt you to slow us down. That's what I keep coming back to. Nine years I've flown circles around this woman—" she nodded toward the fog where Odile had gone, "—and these people, they just reach for a match. It's obscene."

"It's efficient," Wren said. "That's what makes it obscene."

Cass stayed an hour, helped hold the antenna wire straight while Wren soldered, argued amiably about grounding points, and left with a promise to fly cover over the strip tomorrow, low and visible, just to remind Corrigan's employers that their deniable pilot had become an undeniable witness.

And late that night, alone, ribs aching, hands throbbing, Wren keyed the rebuilt transmitter for the first time and called Rivergate on the new frequency, fingers clumsy on the key, and waited through the static with her heart in her throat.

The reply came at 0230, faint, wavering, half-swallowed by the storm building over the escarpment—but unmistakable. Mara's voice, exhausted, reading numbers: the fever count, doubled since the last report. The medicine gone. The water source downstream of the mine, and the mine, Mara said, had lights on it again last night, machinery sounds, blasting after dark.

Wren wrote it all down with a burned hand, letter by letter, and then sat looking at the page, and then reached for the microphone to raise Odile on the night frequency, because there was a message that couldn't wait for dawn, and the words forming themselves in Wren's chest were the ones she'd been refusing to think:

*The mountain isn't waiting for the monsoon. It's already going.*
