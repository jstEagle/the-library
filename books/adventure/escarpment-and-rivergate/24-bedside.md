## 24. Bedside

The infirmary at Rivergate had been the schoolhouse once, back before the disaster, back when there were enough children to fill it. Now it was two rooms and a corridor that smelled of carbolic and woodsmoke, and Wren Adeyemi lay in the far bed with her right hand wrapped in gauze like something newly cast, and Odile stood in the doorway at four in the morning unable to make her feet move across the floor.

She had flown through downdrafts that could fold a wing like paper. She had landed on gravel strips the length of a church aisle in crosswinds that made grown men walk away and be sick in the grass. She had, nine years ago, identified her brother's remains by the serial number on a fuel pump, because someone had to and her father could not. And now she stood in a doorway, frozen, because the woman in the bed had a burned hand and a fever of a hundred and one and it was, Odile understood with a clarity that felt like vertigo, entirely her fault.

Not directly. Not in any way a court would name. Wren had gone after the Halvard man herself, had tackled a stranger with a kerosene can in the dark behind the hangar at Kettle Falls, because the spare engine was burning and the radio codes were in a lockbox and because—Odile knew this the way she knew her own blood type—Wren had been protecting the mission, which meant protecting Odile, which meant that the geometry of blame came all the way home no matter how many intermediaries you stacked between.

Mara looked up from the basin she was wringing out. "She's been asking for you," the nurse said, not unkindly. "For six hours. She's been asking for you since the radio call came through, and you've been standing in that doorway for ten minutes, and I have a fever ward with eleven people in it, so either go in or go away, but do one of them."

"I'm going in."

"You're a terrible liar for a pilot. Everyone thinks pilots are good liars. You're not. You fly straight lines and you talk straight lines and it's very easy to read you." Mara pushed past her with the basin, and paused, and said, more quietly: "She flew through weather that should have killed her to get here. Whatever you have to say to her, say it like it's the last load you'll ever carry. Because for her it might have been."

Then she was gone down the corridor, and Odile crossed the floor.

Up close, Wren looked wrong. That was the thing no one warned you about, loving a mechanic: you learned the exact topography of their competence, the grease-dark seams of their knuckles, the particular set of their shoulders when they were listening to an engine the way other people listened to music. And now the hands were bandaged, the left one only at the palm where she'd caught the lockbox edge, the right one wrapped to the wrist, and the shoulders were flat against the mattress in a way that had nothing to do with rest and everything to do with the fever working through her like weather through a pass.

Her eyes opened. They found Odile immediately, no searching, the way a compass finds north, and Odile felt that in her chest like a physical event.

"You got the message," Wren said. Her voice was sandpaper.

"The mountain's already going. I got the message." Odile sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the bandaged hand, and did the thing she had watched Mara do and hated herself for needing instruction in: she tucked the blanket higher over Wren's shoulder. "You're supposed to be resting."

"I rested. I flew a plane through a thunderstorm with a hand that looked like a melted candle. I've earned the right to be a bad patient." A ghost of the usual grin, but it cost her; Odile could see the cost. "How's Cass?"

"Asleep in the hangar office, in a chair, with her boots on. She flew the last shuttle run at eleven and landed in rain so heavy I could hear it on the roof from the mess hall. She's earned the right to be a bad patient too, but she'd fight me on it, so I let her have the chair."

"And the town?"

Odile looked toward the window. Beyond the glass, past the infirmary's yard, Rivergate lay in its bowl of valley dark, and above it—she couldn't see it, but she could feel it, the way you feel a storm cell building behind a ridge—the escarpment stood with its new wound. The rockfall at the upper pass had come down three days early, three days before anyone's math said it would, and the sound of it had rolled across the valley like the mountain clearing its throat.

"The town's holding a meeting tonight," she said. "At the grange hall. Evacuation math. Four hundred people, two planes, maybe two hundred seats before the corridor closes for good. They're going to vote on who stays." She heard her own voice go flat and procedural and hated it. "Cass thinks we can stretch it to two-fifty if we strip the planes and fly the corridor at weights that would make a transport inspector weep."

"And you think?"

"I think the corridor holds. I've read Theo's log four times now, cover to cover, and the man was mapping a lifeline, not chasing a thrill, and the numbers are sound. I think the corridor holds for six more crossings, maybe seven, and after that the monsoon sits on the range like a lid and nothing goes over, either way, in either direction." She stopped. Her hands had made fists in her lap without her permission.

Wren watched her with the patience of someone who had spent years waiting for engines to tell the truth. "That's not what I asked."

"It's what I answered."

"Odile." Just the name. Wren said it the way she'd say a part number—precise, certain, meaning exactly one thing in all the world—and it undid something in Odile's chest that had been held together with safety wire for nine years.

* * *

Here is what Odile Dunmore knew about herself, and had known for a long time, and had arranged her whole life around never saying: she was a woman who kept moving. Her father had taught her that the sky forgave nothing and remembered everything, and she had taken the lesson further than he ever intended. She had made herself into a plane in constant flight—medicine north, gossip south, always a load, always a schedule, always a reason to be at altitude where no one could ask her a question she couldn't answer with a heading and an ETA. Nine years of it. Nine years since Theo, since the escarpment, since the oath her father swore with his hand on the old ledger and his voice broken down to gravel: *we don't fly that route, we don't speak that valley, we don't go back.* She had obeyed him the way you obey a dead man—completely, and without ever once being asked whether you agreed.

And in those nine years there had been Wren, in the hangar, with her sleeves rolled and her forearms inked with grease and a way of talking to engines that Odile had slowly, without deciding to, started wanting aimed at her.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you fly a pattern in your head instead of talking. I've watched you do it for six years. You circle, you circle, you never land."

"The last time I landed somewhere I was supposed to avoid," Odile said, "my brother was on the mountain and my father stopped speaking to the sky, so forgive me if I've developed a preference for circling."

Wren's good hand came out from under the blanket and lay palm-up on the sheet between them. An offer of runway. Odile looked at it the way she'd looked at the corridor on Theo's map, the impossible route, the one that killed him, the one that was the only way through.

"I keep almost losing you," Odile said. It came out lower than she intended. "Do you understand that? Three days ago you fought a man with a kerosene can. Before that you flew through a storm to get here. Before that you found the sabotage and said nothing because you didn't know who to trust, and you carried that alone, and if the tie-rod had let go at altitude I would have—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She pressed on. "I have spent nine years being very good at losing people. I lost my brother. I lost my father a year before his body caught up. I lost a whole valley. I got so good at it that I built a life where the losing was built in, where I could be useful to everyone and known by no one, because known people can be taken away and anonymous people just—keep flying. And then you."

The infirmary was quiet except for the tick of the woodstove and, far off, the sound of the valley river running high with snowmelt and rain.

"And then you," she said again. "In the hangar, with your hands inside my engine like you were listening for its heartbeat. Six years. Six years of Tuesday mornings and coffee gone cold and you saying *she'll fly* about the plane and me hearing something else every single time and never once saying it back. I have flown into weather that should have killed me and I have never once been as afraid as I was standing in that doorway ten minutes ago, and I am done, Wren. I'm done circling. I love you. I have loved you since—God, I don't even know when, since the day you told me the number three cylinder was running rich and you'd fixed it and then made me tea like it was nothing, like fixing things and feeding people were the same act, because for you they are. I love you. I love you in level flight and I love you in a stall and I love you lying in a fever bed with your hand wrapped up because you went into the dark after something that was trying to hurt us, and I am saying it out loud, in a room, to a person, which is something I have not done since I was nineteen years old, and I need you to be alive for a very long time now, because I don't think I survive saying this twice."

The silence afterward had texture. Odile sat in it with her heart going like an overspeeding engine and nowhere to divert.

Wren's eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them; her hands were accounted for. "There she is," she said finally, and her voice cracked on the second word. "Nine years of the most competent woman I've ever met, and it turns out the whole time she was just waiting for a night with bad enough weather that she couldn't fly away from herself." She turned her good hand over and Odile took it, and Wren's grip was fever-hot and strong. "I love you too. I have since the tea. I love you in a way that has made me check the tie-rods twice, and then a third time, because I want you to land every single time, Odile, I want you to land until we're old and the planes are museum pieces and some kid asks us what it was like flying the old corridor and we lie to them about how brave we were."

Odile laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and unsteady. "We were brave."

"We were terrified and we did it anyway. That's better. Bravery's just terror with good maintenance."

* * *

They stayed like that while the fever peaked and broke, Mara coming and going with water and willow bark and the unsentimental tenderness of a woman who had spent nine years watching a valley go without. Toward dawn Wren slept, really slept, her breathing evening out, and Odile sat in the wooden chair Mara had dragged over and held the unburned hand and watched the window go from black to iron to the pale washed grey that passes for morning in monsoon country.

And that was when the arithmetic came back.

It came back the way it always did, patiently, without malice, the way the mountain did its work. Two hundred and fifty seats. Four hundred people. The corridor holding for six crossings, maybe seven. The grange hall meeting at noon. Cass's voice in her memory, flat with fatigue and certainty: *every load is a triage decision, Odile. Somebody's grandmother and somebody's baby and one seat between them, and you're the one holding the manifest.* And underneath all of it, the thing she had said to Wren with her whole chest an hour ago: *I need you to be alive for a very long time.*

Because Wren couldn't fly. Not for weeks. The right hand was a ruin of blisters and the left wasn't much better, and even if the pain allowed it, the infection risk at altitude, the dexterity a yoke demands—Mara had been clear, and Wren had argued, and Mara had won, and Wren only ever lost arguments when she was too sick to make them well. So the shuttle runs would be Odile and Cass, two pilots, two planes, and the loading, the manifest, the triage, would fall to whoever on the ground had the standing to decide—and Odile knew, watching Wren sleep, exactly who that was. The town would look to the Dunmore girl. The Dunmore girl, whose father had sworn off this valley, whose brother had died mapping their way out, who had come back after nine years with medicine and a grudge and a map with a wound drawn on it.

*You can't have both,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the cold clarity of a stall warning. *You can't sit at this bedside and hold the manifest. You can't love someone this much and spend every crossing betting the plane. That's the trade. That's what duty is—it's the thing that takes the hand you're holding and puts it back under the blanket and walks you out the door.*

She had said it plainly, finally, out loud, after nine years—and the world had not paused to let her keep it. The fever would break, the meeting would convene, the corridor would close, and love would have to live at the bedside while duty flew the weather. She understood now why her father had gone silent. Not because he stopped loving the valley. Because he loved things on both sides of the mountain and could only be in one cockpit at a time.

Wren stirred. Her eyes opened a slit, found Odile, and narrowed with the instant, infuriating perceptiveness of the very sick.

"You're leaving," Wren said.

"The meeting's at noon. Cass and I have to strip the second plane before then, re-rig the tie-downs, recompute the corridor weights with the new density altitude. There's a list." She heard herself reciting and stopped. "Yes. I'm leaving. In an hour. Maybe two."

"Okay." Wren's good hand tightened on hers. "Odile. Look at me." She waited until Odile did. "You said it. It's said. It doesn't un-say itself while you're flying. It'll be here at the end of the day, and the day after, and every day you come back down through that corridor with a load of people who get to grow old. That's not love losing to duty. That's love *doing* duty. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I need you to know that I know it, so you can't make it mean that I come second. I don't come second. I come *after*, the way a runway comes after the flight—everything you do in the air is so you can find me at the end of it."

Odile bent and pressed her forehead to Wren's fever-warm brow, and stayed there for a long time, longer than the list allowed.

When she straightened, Wren was already half asleep again, but her hand held on one more second.

"Come back with a full plane," Wren murmured. "And Odile—when the town votes today. Whatever they decide. Don't abstain. Not this time. Not here. They need to hear your voice say who goes first, because if you don't say it, the men who caused all this are counting on the silence, and silence is the one thing you've finally run out of."

Odile stood in the doorway again, on her way out this time, and looked back at the bed, at the bandaged hands, at the woman who had flown through the impossible to reach her.

Outside, the escarpment stood grey against the racing monsoon cloud, and from somewhere up in the broken country came a sound like far-off thunder that was not thunder at all—the mountain, settling, shifting, deciding what it would take next.

The grange hall bell began to ring.
