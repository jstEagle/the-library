## 25. The Town Votes

The news came down the mountain the way bad news always came to Rivergate: on foot, out of breath, ahead of itself.

Mara Voss heard it first, because Mara heard everything. She was in the fever ward, third row of cots, wringing a cloth into a basin when the door banged open and young Piet Aldous stood there with rain streaming off his oilskin and his eyes too wide for his face.

"They found it," he said. "The mine. It's not dead up there. Odile Dunmore and the Merrow woman—they went up yesterday, and they found men working it. Blasting. Right above the pass."

Mara's hands stopped in the water. Around her, the ward went quiet in that particular way sick rooms go quiet—not silent, but listening, every cough suspended, every fevered breath held by people too weak to hold anything else.

"Say it plain," said old Henrik from the corner cot, who had been dying slowly of his heart for six years and refused to do it any faster.

"The slide nine years ago," Piet said. "It wasn't weather. There's been a mine working the escarpment head this whole time—illegal—and the blasting is what shook the mountain loose. And now they're at it again, and the next one will come down on the reservoir."

The basin water had gone cold around Mara's wrists. She thought of the crates of medicine that had never come, year after year, the requisitions that vanished somewhere between Kettle Falls and the valley, the polite letters from the provincial health office signed by men whose names she'd never heard of until last week, when Odile Dunmore spread them across her kitchen table like a hand of losing cards. *Halvard Group.* She'd written to Halvard. She'd thanked Halvard, once, in writing, for their continued interest in Rivergate's welfare.

She took the cloth out of the basin and wrung it dry with great care, because if she did not give her hands something precise to do she was going to put them through the window.

"All right," she said. "Get Tom Aldous. Get everyone. Meeting hall, tonight."

* * *

Tom Aldous had been mayor of Rivergate for eleven years, which meant he had spent eleven years presiding over a town that was mostly a memory of itself. He stood at the front of the meeting hall that evening and looked out at four hundred faces—or three hundred and eighty, he counted later, though some were children asleep against their mothers' shoulders—and understood that whatever happened in the next hour would be the only thing anyone remembered about him.

Odile Dunmore sat near the front, beside Cass Merrow, and Tom still wasn't used to the sight of either of them. A Dunmore in the hall. The name alone had been enough to empty rooms for nine years. Old man Dunmore had flown out of here the week after the slide and never come back, and the town had decided in its grief that the pilot's silence was its own kind of confession—that if Dunmore had nothing to hide, why had he never come back to look them in the eye?

Now his daughter sat among them with a survey map spread across two folding tables, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs, and the whole room leaned toward it like plants toward light.

"This is my father's original survey," Odile said. Her voice carried without effort; Tom noticed she didn't raise it, the way you don't have to raise a blade. "Nineteen years old. He mapped every approach to the valley, including the upper pass—the one Theo was flying when—" She stopped. Started again. "When the mountain came down. Look at the contour lines above the pass. This shelf here." Her finger touched the paper. "That's where the mine is. That's where it's always been. The blasting loosens the headwall, and everything above the reservoir comes down in one piece."

"And the men doing the blasting?" someone called from the back. "They just walk away?"

"They've already walked away," Cass Merrow said, standing. She was taller than Odile and harder-edged, and Tom had watched her work the crowd before the meeting started, shaking hands, learning names, spending charm like a woman who knew exactly what it cost. "The company is called the Halvard Group. They bought your land titles through shell companies starting eight months after the slide, when prices were at the bottom. They own, outright or through proxies, sixty percent of the valley floor. If the mountain buries the reservoir—if the town becomes uninhabitable—they acquire the rest for nothing, and no one ever has to answer for the first slide or the second one."

A sound went through the hall then, low and ugly, the sound of four hundred people understanding theft all at once.

Tom raised his hands. "Questions first. Decisions after."

But there was really only one question, and everyone knew it, and it was Del Marek—a farmer, a practical man, a man who had buried his wife in the first slide—who stood up and asked it.

"How long?"

Odile met his eyes. "Cass and I measured the new cracking ourselves. The monsoon arrives in five days, maybe six. Once the ground saturates, the headwall goes. Could be during the storm. Could be the day after. But it goes."

"And the road?"

"Snowed shut since October. Won't open till spring."

"So we fly out," Del said, "or we drown in mud. That's the arithmetic."

"That's the arithmetic," Odile said.

* * *

Wren Adeyemi stood at the back of the hall with her arms crossed and her shoulder still bound from Kettle Falls, and listened to a town argue for its life.

She had fixed planes in a dozen towns like this one—smaller, meaner, more frightened—and she knew the shape of what was coming. First the denial. Then the anger. Then the arithmetic, which was always crueler than the disaster, because the disaster at least pretended not to be about money.

Two hundred souls per plane load, Odile had told them, if they stripped the cargo plane to seats and flew the corridor round the clock in the weather windows. Two hundred per crossing, maybe three crossings a day if the mountain and the monsoon cooperated, which mountains and monsoons famously did not. Four hundred people. Six days. Wren had done the math on the flight north and done it again tonight, and the math kept coming back the same way: *it fits, but only barely, and only if nothing goes wrong,* and something always went wrong.

An old woman near the front—Wren didn't know her name, gray braid, hands like tree roots—stood up and said, "My mother is buried in this valley. My husband is buried in this valley. I am seventy-three years old and I will not be carried out of my home like luggage so that some company can have an empty valley to themselves."

There was applause for that. Real applause, hard-handed, and Wren felt her stomach drop, because she could see where it was going.

"You'd rather be buried twice?" That was Piet Aldous again, young and furious. "You'd rather the whole town die for a principle? Take the flight. Come back in spring when it's safe."

"It won't be safe in spring," the old woman said. "It'll be owned. You think they'll let us return? You think the titles come back?" She turned to face the room. "If we leave, we leave as refugees. We'll scatter to the towns, and Halvard's lawyers will pick our claims apart one family at a time, and in ten years Rivergate will be a company town with a company church. I've seen it happen. My sister's valley. So don't tell me leaving is the safe choice. Leaving is just the slow way of losing."

Silence after that. The kind of silence that has weight.

Tom Aldous looked at Odile. "You've flown the corridor. Your brother mapped it. Can you get four hundred people over that range in six days?"

Wren watched Odile take that question the way you watch someone catch a falling blade—by the handle or by the edge, no telling which until the blood shows.

"I can get two hundred over tomorrow," Odile said. "I can't promise the weather past that. Nobody can promise the weather past that."

"But can it be done?"

"If we start now, and if nothing breaks, and if the mountain holds six days instead of five—" Odile paused, and Wren saw her choose honesty over comfort, saw it cost her. "Then yes. It can be done. Barely."

"Barely," repeated a voice from the middle of the room, and it might have been despair or contempt; with small towns it was usually both.

* * *

Mara Voss voted with her feet before the vote was ever called.

She stood up in the middle of the argument—old woman against young man, stay against go, grief against grief—and she said, "I want to say one thing and then I'll sit down and let you decide."

The hall quieted. Mara was not a woman people talked over; she had set too many bones and closed too many eyes for that.

"Nine years ago we lost thirty-one people to that slide, and for nine years we told ourselves it was the mountain's temper, and we endured it, because that's what this town does. We endure." She looked at Odile, and Odile held the look. "And the medicine never came, and the road stayed shut, and we got smaller and sicker and prouder, and we called it independence. It wasn't independence. It was a cage somebody built and locked from the outside, and we polished the bars and called them ours." Her voice cracked, just once, and she let it. "I lost patients I could have saved. Every winter. For nine years. Because men in an office somewhere decided our valley was worth more empty than full."

She turned back to the room.

"So vote how you like. But understand what you're voting on. Not whether to leave your homes. Whether to keep letting other people decide what your lives are worth. The ones who want to fight—fight. The ones who want to fly—fly. But don't any of you call staying brave if it's really just being tired. And don't call leaving cowardice if it's really just loving your children more than your pride." She sat down.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then the old woman with the gray braid—her name was Signe Holt, Tom would remember afterward, Signe Holt who had lost everything twice—raised her hand and said, in a voice gone quiet and clear:

"I'll fly out with the first load. And I'll come back. And I'll stand on my husband's grave and dare them to stop me." She looked around the hall. "We don't have to choose between the valley and each other. We choose each other now, and we take the valley back after. That's the vote I'm making. Who's with me?"

Hands went up. Slowly at first—Del Marek, then his sons, then Piet Aldous, then half the room, then most of it, the hands rising like something growing in time-lapse, and Tom Aldous stood at the front of his town and felt the decision move through it like weather, and knew it was done.

Not unanimous. Nothing in Rivergate would ever be unanimous again. A dozen families would stay regardless, dug in like stones, and Tom marked their names in his mind for the evacuation list's end, and prayed the mountain gave him time to argue with them one house at a time.

"Then it's settled," he said. "First load at dawn. Everyone able to work—packing, loading, carrying—get to the airstrip tonight. Mara, triage order for the planes. Odile—" He turned to her, and the whole hall turned with him, four hundred people looking at the daughter of the man they had blamed for nearly a decade. "How many crossings can you and Merrow actually make?"

Odile stood. Beside her, Cass rose too, and behind them both, at the back of the hall, Wren Adeyemi pushed off the wall and started forward, and Odile looked at the three of them arranged around her like points of a compass and said:

"As many as the weather allows. And one more than that."

The hall laughed—short, surprised, alive—and broke apart into motion, chairs scraping, families gathering, the whole town becoming a single animal with a single purpose, and outside the rain had already begun.

* * *

Later, in the yellow light of the airstrip shed, Wren found Odile with her father's map spread on the workbench, her finger resting on the corridor Theo had drawn, the impossible line through the teeth of the range.

"One more than the weather allows," Wren said. "That's a hell of a thing to promise four hundred people."

"I didn't promise them." Odile didn't look up. "I promised myself."

Wren came closer, close enough to see the tremor in the finger on the map, the only tremor Odile permitted anywhere on her body. "Your brother's route. You're really going to fly it. With a plane full of passengers."

"With a plane full of *my people*," Odile said, and heard herself say it, and did not take it back. Nine years she had kept Rivergate at arm's length, a wound she flew around, and now she had claimed it in a single sentence in a smelly shed, and there was no unclaiming it.

Wren put her good hand over Odile's on the map. Outside, the rain thickened against the roof, and far to the south, though neither of them could hear it yet, the monsoon was coming up the valley like a debt being called in.

"Dawn, then," Wren said.

"Dawn," Odile said, and began to fold the map along her brother's lines.
