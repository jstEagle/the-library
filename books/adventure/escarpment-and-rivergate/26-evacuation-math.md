## 26. Evacuation Math

The chalkboard had been Mara's idea. She'd dragged it out of the schoolhouse storage room at dawn, propped it against the fuel shed where everyone could see it, and written two numbers at the top in her careful nurse's hand: **400** and **200**.

By midmorning the numbers had acquired annotations. Wren added a third figure—**6**, for the days of flying weather Cass's barometer promised before the monsoon's leading edge swallowed the escarpment—and underlined it twice. Someone else, no one would say who, had drawn a small coffin beside the 400 and then, ashamed or defiant, had not erased it.

Odile stood in front of the board with a cup of coffee going cold in her hand and did what she had been doing since first light: she refused to do the arithmetic.

It wasn't that she couldn't. That was the thing people misunderstood about pilots—the assumption that the hard part was the flying. The flying was easy. The flying was hands and eyes and the old litany of numbers, manifold pressure and airspeed and the width of the pass ahead of you, and all of it answered to you if you answered to it. The hard part was standing on the ground with chalk dust in the air and four hundred names in a ledger and two hundred seats that existed only as arithmetic: nine souls per load in the Otter if they stripped the cargo racks, six in Cass's older Goose with its bad door seal, three runs a day each if the weather held, which it would not, because it never had.

Two planes. Six days. Two hundred souls, best case, if nothing broke, if nobody panicked, if the passes stayed open one day longer than any pilot's judgment said they would.

She drank the cold coffee anyway. It tasted like the inside of a thermos that had survived a war.

* * *

The town meeting convened in the grange hall at ten, and Odile sat in the back row because sitting in the back row was a skill she'd perfected over nine years of being nobody's daughter in Escarpment. But Rivergate didn't let her be nobody. Word had traveled from the strip within an hour of her landing—the Dunmore girl, Theo's sister, come north after all these years—and faces turned toward her when she entered like compass needles finding a bad pole.

Alderman Prosser ran the meeting, a big man with a forester's shoulders gone soft and a voice built for calling across valleys. He stood at the front beside the chalkboard's smaller cousin, a paper list taped to the wall, and he said the numbers out loud so that everyone had to hear them spoken in a human voice rather than read them in chalk.

"Four hundred and eleven," he said. "That's every soul in Rivergate, counting the fever ward. We have lift capacity for perhaps two hundred before the passes close. Maybe less. The council has asked Miss Dunmore and Miss Merrow to explain how the loads will be chosen."

Every head in the hall turned back toward Odile.

She felt the old reflex rise in her throat—the reflex to be small, to defer, to let someone else carry the weight of deciding. For nine years she had flown medicine into places like this and never once been asked to say whose grandmother went first. The manifest was always somebody else's cruelty. She just flew the plane.

"Miss Dunmore?" Prosser prompted.

"I'm not choosing," she said, and heard her own voice come out flatter than she intended. "I want to be clear about that. I'll fly whoever this town puts on my manifest. But I won't stand up here and rank human beings."

A murmur moved through the hall—not angry, exactly. Tired. These were people who had spent nine years doing without, who had learned to ration diesel and antibiotics and hope, and they recognized rationing when they saw it even when the person rations came wrapped in principle.

An old woman in the third row—Odile knew her now, Hetta Voss, who had wept over her father's name at the memorial wall—said quietly, "Then who does?"

And there it was. The question Odile had been dodging since dawn, delivered without malice by a woman who had buried a husband in the disaster and had no patience left for anyone's clean hands.

Odile looked down at her boots. She thought about refusing again. She thought about how easy it would be, how principled, how exactly like her father, who had sworn off this valley rather than face what it asked of him. She thought about Theo, who had died mapping a way through for other people, and about how his logbook had taught her, in the last week, that courage was not a feeling but a series of decisions made with incomplete information and full knowledge of the cost.

"The medical triage comes first," she heard herself say. "Mara sets that order—it's already set, it's how nurses do it, and nobody argues with a nurse about triage. Fever ward goes first, then the elderly, then children with their mothers." She paused. "After that, the town decides. Not me. A committee—Prosser, Mara, Hetta, and two others you choose today. They make the lists. I fly the lists."

"And if the committee can't agree?" someone called from the back.

"Then I'll take the argument up in the plane with me and let the mountain settle it," Odile said, and the hall laughed—a short, startled sound, the laugh of people remembering that gallows humor was still permitted—and the meeting rolled forward into the ugly, necessary work of forming a committee.

She sat through all of it. She made herself sit through all of it, every amendment and objection, every bitter exchange between the mill families and the farm families about whose needs were greater, because she understood now that looking away was its own kind of choice, and she had made enough of those to last several lifetimes.

* * *

*

The first manifest was posted at noon on the fuel shed door, beneath the chalkboard, and Odile read it twice and then walked out past the windsock to the end of the runway and stood there until her breathing steadied.

Forty-one names. Mara's triage section took the top nineteen—the entire fever ward, ambulatory patients first, the two worst cases lashed to improvised stretchers in the Otter's cabin with IV bags taped to the ceiling strut. Then the elders. Then eleven children, listed with their mothers, though the mothers' own names appeared further down the list, a bureaucratic fiction everyone understood: no child flew alone, so the children pulled their mothers up the list like anchors pulling a boat.

Forty-one names, and Odile knew she could carry maybe thirty-five of them on the first run if the weight and balance worked, which it might not, because stretcher patients sat badly in a load sheet and the Otter hated a tail-heavy cabin.

Wren found her at the runway's end. She heard the uneven footfall before she saw him—his hip still bound from the torch fight at Kettle Falls, ten days healed and lying about being healed—and then his hand settled warm between her shoulder blades, and she leaned back into it the way she had finally, in the fever ward's long nights, allowed herself to do.

"You've been standing here twenty minutes," he said.

"Has it been twenty minutes?"

"I counted." He came around to her side, and she saw he was holding the manifest, folded into quarters. "I checked your weight and balance for the stretchers. You'll need to shift the aft rack forward two stations and take the second stretcher out. Thirty-three souls, not forty-one. The rest go on the second run."

"Thirty-three." She said it the way you'd read a casualty figure. "Out of four hundred and eleven."

"Yeah." Wren was quiet a moment. Below them the river ran high and brown with snowmelt, carrying the valley's whole winter downstream whether the valley was ready to go or not. "You know what I keep thinking about? The night I ferried the plane north. Every mile of that weather, I wasn't scared of dying. I was scared of arriving too late to matter." He tapped the folded manifest against his thigh. "This is what mattering looks like. It's a list with names you can't fit on it."

"When did you get wise?"

"About the time a certain pilot started letting me see her flight plans." He smiled, but it didn't reach far. "Odile. There's something else. Cass wants both planes running the corridor tomorrow, which means no ferry back to Kettle Falls for spare parts, which means if anything breaks—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "I've done everything I can with what's here. The Goose's number-two cylinder is running hot and I've got no spare. If it lets go over the pass with a full load—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," she admitted. "But if I refuse to fly it, the alternative is leaving sixty people on this manifest for a mountain to bury. So the cylinder flies, and you'll have it torn down and re-ringed tonight, and I'll watch the gauges like a hawk with a grudge." She turned to face him fully. "That's the math, Wren. That's all the math is—choosing which risk you can live with afterward."

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once, slowly, the way he nodded over an engine he'd decided to trust.

"Then I'd better get the cowling off," he said. "Come find me when you land. I'll have coffee that isn't terrible."

"You've never once made coffee that isn't terrible."

"Motivation," he said, and limped back toward the hangar, and she watched him go and understood that she had just described her own heart to him using the language of load sheets, and that he had understood her perfectly.

* * *

*

The afternoon went to loading drills. Odile ran the Otter through three practice cycles—engines turning, ramp down, passengers shuffled aboard in manifest order, timed—with Mara directing the flow and Hetta Voss, unexpectedly, proving herself a drill sergeant of the first order, moving elders along with a cane and a voice that brooked no dawdling. Best time: eleven minutes from engines-start to taxi. Odile wanted eight. Eight minutes mattered when the weather window was measured in hours and the cloud base in hundreds of feet.

Between cycles she stood at the chalkboard and made herself study it.

**400. 200. 6.**

Two hundred and eleven people who would not fit. That was the number she kept circling back to, the number that sat in her chest like ballast. Two hundred and eleven neighbors, millworkers, farmers, children she'd watched playing in the flooded street below the strip that morning—people who would still be standing in this valley when the monsoon arrived and the escarpment, destabilized by nine years of Halvard's blasting above the water source, began to give. The survey data in her flight bag said the slide zone reached the lower terraces. The survey data said a lot of things she wished it didn't.

There had to be another way. There was always supposed to be another way; that was the lie she'd carried since childhood, the comfortable fiction that cleverness and effort could make any equation balance. But equations didn't care about cleverness. Four hundred minus two hundred was two hundred, and no amount of flying skill changed it.

Unless the math was wrong.

She stared at the chalkboard until the numbers blurred, and then she took the chalk from the ledge and, before she could talk herself out of it, wrote beneath Wren's underlined **6**: **what if the window is longer than we think?**

Cass appeared at her shoulder—silent as ever on her feet, a habit from years of walking up behind rivals she intended to unsettle. "What are you doing?"

"Checking our assumptions."

"Our assumptions are satellite imagery and a barometer."

"Our assumptions are that the corridor Theo mapped is only good in stable conditions." Odile tapped the chalk against the board. "His log says he flew it in weather worse than this. He was testing it. He wrote marginal notes—ceiling minimums, wind limits, times of day when the downdraft off the east face dies down. If half of what he logged is accurate, we're not limited to six days of fair-weather flying. We could run loads in conditions we're currently writing off."

Cass was quiet for a moment. Odile had learned to read her silences the way she read weather: this one was calculation, not doubt.

"If you're wrong," Cass said finally, "you don't get a second mistake. The corridor doesn't forgive errors, and neither does a loaded airplane."

"I know."

"And if you're right, we might move another eighty, ninety people before the passes close." Cass took the chalk from her hand, and for a moment their fingers overlapped on it, two pilots gripping the same instrument, and something passed between them that had taken nine years and a war's worth of rivalry to arrive at. "Tonight," Cass said. "We go through his log line by line. All of it. And tomorrow morning, before the first passenger run, I take the Goose up the corridor alone and fly his numbers exactly. If I come back, we trust them."

"Cass—"

"Don't." Her voice was gentle and absolutely final. "You've got a town to load and a man in the hangar who makes terrible coffee. Let me be the one who tests the map. It's what lead planes are for." She stepped back, chalk in hand, and added a fourth number to the board in her sharp engineer's hand: **+90?**

The question mark hung there among the certainties, and Odile looked at it and felt something she hadn't felt since she'd landed in this valley: not hope exactly—hope was too soft a word—but the specific, electric vertigo of an equation that might, against all odds, have a solution she hadn't seen yet.

* * *

*

Dusk came down the valley like a lid closing. Odile walked the perimeter of the strip one last time, checking tie-downs against the evening gusts, and stopped at the chalkboard to look at the day's final tally, which Mara had updated in fresh chalk:

**Evacuated: 0. Manifested: 41. Days remaining: 5.**

Zero. After all of it—the confession, the mine, the refusal, the town's vote to fight—they had moved not one single soul out of harm's way, and the mountain above the water source creaked and settled in the dark like an old man turning over in bed.

Headlights swept the strip. A vehicle coming up the valley road, fast, kicking dust even at this hour, and Odile's hand went instinctively to the radio clipped at her shoulder before she recognized the shape of it: the Halvard Group's white SUV, the one that had been parked outside the grange hall all afternoon like a patient predator.

It stopped at the edge of the light. The representative got out—Kessler, the smooth one, the one who had offered her father's debts cleared and Theo's name restored if she flew and stayed silent—and he stood beside his open door for a moment, looking at the chalkboard, at the numbers, at the question mark.

Then he looked at Odile across fifty yards of gravel and failing light, and he smiled, and he raised one hand in a gesture that might have been greeting or might have been checkmate, and began to walk toward her.

Odile did not move. Behind her, five days ticked down to four at midnight, and two hundred and eleven names waited in a valley that was running out of ground to stand on, and the man from Halvard came on through the dusk with an offer in his pocket that she already knew she was going to have to hear.
