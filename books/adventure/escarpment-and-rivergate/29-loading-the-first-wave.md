## 29. Loading the First Wave

Wren Adeyemi had never believed in orchestras. Too many people, too much ego, too much standing around waiting for a conductor to tell you what you already knew. But by the second hour of the evacuation, standing on the wet gravel of Rivergate's strip with a clipboard going soft in the drizzle, she understood the appeal. Everything here was timing. Everything was one instrument after another coming in on cue, and if she missed a beat, the whole thing turned into noise.

"Load four's ready," called Petra Osei from the tailgate of the flatbed, her voice carrying over the idling engines. "Six elders, two litters, one infant. Mara says the infant goes with the mother, no exceptions."

"No exceptions," Wren repeated, and wrote it down even though she wouldn't forget it. Writing it down was the ritual. The ritual was what kept her hands steady.

The strip at Rivergate had never been meant for this. It was eleven hundred feet of packed earth and river gravel, built by men who assumed nothing heavier than a mail plane would ever want it, and it softened in rain like bread in milk. Wren had walked it at dawn with a shovel and a bag of crushed rock from the quarry road, filling the worst of the ruts, and she'd do it again at dusk, because the Cessna loads were chewing the surface faster than the weather could be blamed for it. Odile had taken off twenty minutes ago through cloud that came down to two hundred feet, and every minute that plane sat on the ground was a minute the ground got worse.

* * *

The first wave had been chosen the night before, in the schoolhouse, by lamplight, and Wren had sat against the wall while the town argued itself into a machine.

Mara Voss had started it, laying out the fever ward numbers on butcher paper: forty-one sick, nine critical, three who would not survive the flight but would not survive the week either. The elders' council had countered with their own list—the oldest families, the ones whose grandparents had built the valley, who refused to leave and were refusing still. And then old Henrik Solheim, ninety-one years old, deaf as a post and sharp as an awl, had stood up on his cane and said, in a voice that filled the room: "Children first. Then the sick who can be saved. Then the old ones who consent to go. In that order, or not at all." He'd looked around the room like a man daring anyone to improve on arithmetic. Nobody did.

So that was the order. Children, then the salvageable sick, then the consenting old. Four hundred souls, maybe two hundred seats before the passes closed, and everyone in the room had done the division and nobody had said the number out loud.

Wren had watched Odile during that meeting. She'd expected flinching—Odile, who kept her past sealed tighter than a fuel cap, asked now to triage a town. But Odile had sat very still with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea gone cold, and when they'd come to her and asked how many loads she thought she could fly, she'd said, "As many as the weather gives me," and that was all. Wren had recognized the voice. It was the voice Odile used when feeling would break something if it got loose.

Wren's job was everything that happened on the ground, which meant everything that mattered and none of the glory. Fueling. Weight and balance. Loading order. The strip itself. The radio, such as it was—a battered set wired to a truck battery, crackling like a fire. And the people, which was the hardest part, because people under fear became either their best selves or their worst, and there was no telling which until the moment arrived.

* * *

By midmorning the drizzle had thickened into honest rain, and the cloud deck dropped again, and Wren made the call she'd been dreading: single-engine starts only, no taxiing, planes pulled onto the strip by hand with the tractor and the tow rope. It cost them ten minutes per departure. Ten minutes, times however many loads remained, was a math problem she refused to finish.

"You're being careful," said Cass Merrow, appearing at her elbow with the particular silence of a woman who'd spent thirty years walking up behind mechanics. She had mud to the knee and didn't seem to have noticed.

"I'm being slow," Wren said. "Careful is what I'd call it if we had time."

Cass studied the sky the way other people read correspondence. "She'll hold till evening. Maybe. That cell over the Kettle Range is moving south faster than the forecast said, and the forecast was already lying."

"Then we fly faster."

"That's not—" Cass stopped herself, and Wren saw her decide, deliberately, to say the kinder thing. "You've done well with this strip. It shouldn't be taking this traffic. Someone built it to be forgotten."

"My father's generation built half the things in this valley to be forgotten," Wren said. "It's sort of the regional style."

That earned her half a smile, which from Cass Merrow was practically a medal. Then the radio crackled and Odile's voice came through, thin and doubled with static: *"Rivergate, Escarpment One, five miles out, descending through eight hundred, I have the field."*

And the whole strip changed key. The loaders straightened. The litter teams lifted their stretchers. The mothers holding children moved forward in the queue with that terrible patient shuffle, and Wren raised her hand and the tractor dragged the flatbed into position, and the machine of mercy took another stroke.

* * *

Escarpment One touched down at 11:40, wheels kissing gravel so gently that Wren felt pride move through her chest like warmth through cold hands. Nine years of flying into strips like this one, and Odile Dunmore could put a loaded plane down on wet gravel softer than most pilots could land on tarmac.

They unloaded in four minutes flat. Out came empty oxygen bottles and a crate of antibiotics Mara had begged for by name; in went six children, two litters, a grandmother named Aune Virtanen who gripped Wren's sleeve at the door and said, "Tell my son I'll see him on the other side, and tell him not to fly angry," and Wren promised both things without knowing whether either was possible.

Then Odile was out of her seat, doing her own walkaround in the rain, checking the tires, the struts, the tie-downs Wren had replaced twice since Kettle Falls. Their eyes met across the engine cowling. There wasn't time for anything else. There was never time for anything else anymore. Wren had learned in the last three days that love under evacuation pressure was conducted entirely in glances and torque wrenches: *I see you. The left main looks good. Go.*

"Cloud's building over the north saddle," Odile said, low, just for her. "I don't love it."

"I don't love any of it. Fly anyway?"

"Fly anyway." And she was back in her seat, and the door slammed, and Wren stood clear and gave the thumbs-up, and Escarpment One rolled into the murk and was gone, and Wren turned immediately to the next group waiting under the tarpaulin and said, "Load six, let's move, we're burning daylight we don't have."

* * *

The afternoon was where it got hard.

The children went first, as promised, and the children went easily—they were excited, mostly, thrilled by the plane, waving out the windows until the glass fogged. It was the parents who broke Wren's heart, standing in the rain watching their kids shrink into the sky, some of them not knowing if they'd follow tonight or tomorrow or ever. One father, Jussi Ranta, held his daughter up for a last look at the valley before handing her through the door, and Wren heard him say, "Remember the shape of the mountains. You'll want to know them when you come home." As if coming home were a settled thing. As if the mountain weren't actively trying to kill them.

Because it was. At 2:15 the escarpment spoke—a deep percussive boom from up-valley, felt in the chest more than heard—and a plume of dust rose off the upper slopes where the Halvard blasting had been chewing for weeks. The whole strip went quiet. Even the engines seemed to listen. Old Henrik Solheim, waiting on a stretcher he'd fought his way onto, looked up at the mountain and said, clearly, to no one: "It's early. It wasn't supposed to go until the thaw."

Wren wrote down the time in her log. She didn't know why. It felt like something a record should exist of.

After that, the pace changed. People who had been reluctant to leave found new enthusiasm. People who had been eager began to panic, and panic was the thing Wren had been hired, in effect, to prevent. She posted Petra at the queue with strict orders: no pushing, no cutting, families stay together or they wait together. She put the biggest man in town, Bo Karppinen, at the litter station, because Bo could lift anything and frighten no one. She ran the fueling crew herself between loads, dragging hoses through standing water, keeping the drums under canvas, counting every liter like a miser because the resupply they'd flown in was now also their fuel supply and the arithmetic was ugly.

At 3:30, Cass landed hard—harder than she'd have liked, the crosswind gusting off the river in ways the windsock couldn't make up its mind about—and taxied clear with her jaw set, and Wren met her at the door.

"Tire?" Wren asked.

"Tire's fine. Pilot's annoyed." Cass swung down. "The corridor's getting narrow, Wren. Theo's route is real—I'll give his ghost that—but the window's shrinking around it. Odile's threading a needle that's closing. We get maybe three more loads each before I won't sign off on it."

"Three more loads each," Wren repeated. "How many does that move?"

Cass did the sum in her head, and Wren watched her arrive at the answer and hate it. "Not enough," Cass said quietly. "Not nearly enough."

* * *

Toward evening the rain turned serious, drumming on the tarpaulins, and the light went the color of pewter, and Wren stood at the edge of the strip watching the west, where the weather came from, and made herself name the thing she'd been not-naming all day: they were going to run out of runway before they ran out of people.

Petra appeared beside her, soaked through, hoarse from calling loading counts. "Bo says the quarry road's washing out. If it goes, we lose our fill for the strip."

"Then we stop using fill and start praying the gravel holds."

"That's not much of a plan."

"It's the plan we have." Wren rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. She hadn't slept more than three hours at a stretch since the grounding at Kettle Falls, since the drive north, since the torch-lit wreckage of their spare engine, since the hospital cot where Odile had sat beside her and finally, finally said the words out loud—and then gotten up at dawn to fly anyway, because duty was winning, barely, and they'd both agreed to let it win for now. Wren carried that agreement in her chest like a splinter. *For now.* She intended to collect on it. She intended, when the last plane was parked and the last person was out, to stand in front of Odile Dunmore in the rain and refuse to let her disappear into the sky again. But that was later. Later was a country you flew to. Right now there was load nine.

The radio crackled: *"Rivergate, Escarpment Two, inbound, ten miles. Weather's deteriorating over the saddle, I'm coming in below it, expect me visual in four minutes."*

Cass. Wren keyed the mic. "Rivergate copies, Escarpment Two. Wind's shifted, favoring two-six. Gravel's soft past the midpoint—touch long, roll easy."

*"Touch long, roll easy,"* Cass repeated, and there was something almost fond in it, the way a pilot repeats a mechanic's instructions when she trusts the mechanic more than she lets on.

The plane materialized out of the murk exactly when promised, and Wren felt the strange gratitude that had been arriving all day, load after load: the gratitude of watching competence defeat weather, over and over, a small stubborn miracle performed on schedule.

* * *

They flew until they couldn't.

The last safe load went out at 6:50, Odile at the controls, twelve souls aboard including Mara's three critical patients and old Henrik, who had consented at last with the air of a king granting a border concession. Before boarding, Henrik had gripped Wren's wrist with a strength that belied his ninety-one years and said, "You keep the strip. Strips are promises. Whoever holds the strip holds the way home." She'd nodded as if she understood, and she did understand, and it terrified her.

Then the light failed, and the cloud came down to the treetops, and Odile stood in the rain beside her plane with the engine ticking cool and said the words Wren had been bracing for all day.

"That's it for today. I can't see the saddle. Nobody flies the corridor blind—not even for this."

"How many are left?" Wren asked, though she knew.

"A hundred and eighty." Odile's voice was level. Her eyes weren't. "Maybe less, if the count from the up-valley farms comes in."

"And the weather?"

"Monsoon makes landfall tomorrow. Properly, this time. After that, the corridor closes and stays closed." Odile looked up at the escarpment, black against the bruised sky, where the dust plume had finally settled. When she spoke again her voice had changed—flattened into the tone Wren knew meant a decision had already been made somewhere below speech. "Which means tomorrow isn't shuttle runs. Tomorrow is everything, all at once, every plane, every hour of light, and if we miss—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Up-valley, faint under the rain, the mountain boomed again, longer this time, and both of them turned toward the sound, and neither of them said the word *slide*, and the word sat there between them anyway, enormous, patient, waiting for morning.

Wren reached out and took Odile's hand, right there on the strip, in front of God and the ground crew and the rain. Odile let her. That was new. That was three days new.

"One more thing," Wren said. "Before you sleep. There's something about the corridor I checked in Theo's survey notes—something I haven't told anyone yet. And you need to see it before you fly tomorrow."

Odile turned to her fully, rain running off her chin, and Wren watched the exhaustion in her face give way, inch by inch, to something sharper.

"What kind of something?"

"The kind," Wren said, "that changes the math."
