## 30. The Slide Begins

The mountain had been talking all morning.

Odile heard it first as a sound she couldn't name—a low percussion under the wind, like someone knocking on the inside of the world. She was on her fourth shuttle run of the day, climbing out of Rivergate with nineteen souls in the back and the monsoon's leading edge already bruising the western sky, when she felt it through the airframe: a tremor in the air itself, the way water shivers before it boils.

"Did you feel that?" Cass's voice in her headset, from the second plane holding short below.

"I felt it."

"That wasn't blasting."

"No," Odile said. "It wasn't."

Blasting had a rhythm to it—counted charges, human hands, the staccato signature of men who thought they owned the rock. This was longer. Deeper. A single exhalation from something that had been holding its breath for nine years, or maybe ninety, and had finally decided to let it out.

She banked south over the valley and looked at the escarpment with the eyes her father had given her, the ones that read terrain the way other people read weather. The upper pass—the one they'd been flying over twice an hour since dawn, threading evacuees through Theo's corridor toward Kettle Falls—sat beneath a face of grey limestone that had always looked solid to her. Solid the way her father's ledger had looked solid. Solid the way anything looks right up until it isn't.

There was dust coming off it now. Not cloud-dust, not spray off the ridgeline where the wind tore at loose scree. A brown-gold plume rising from the base of the cliff itself, hanging in the cold air like smoke from a fire nobody had lit yet.

"Cass, look at the toe of the east face."

A pause while Cass brought her plane around. When she came back on the radio her voice had changed—flatter, faster, the voice of a pilot doing arithmetic she didn't like. "That's a tension crack opening. I can see daylight in it from here."

"How wide?"

"Wide enough that I don't want to be downwind of it."

Odile keyed the radio for the strip. "Wren. Come in."

Static, then Wren's voice, ragged with a day of loading planes and shouting over engines. "Go ahead."

"Shut down operations. Get everyone away from the east end of the valley floor. The escarpment is moving."

Silence for three seconds. She could picture Wren standing on the strip, looking up, doing the same calculation every person in that valley was about to do: how far is safe from a mountain that has decided to fall?

"Copy," Wren said. "How long do we have?"

And there it was—the question with no honest answer. Odile had spent her whole life around mountains, and the one thing she knew about them was that they kept their own schedule. They did not negotiate. They did not extend the window because four hundred people still needed to cross.

"I don't know," she said. "Hours. Maybe less. Move them anyway."

* * *

The town moved the way water moves when a dam breaks—not fast, but everywhere at once.

By the time Odile set down and shut down, the airstrip had become something between a field hospital and a refugee camp. Tarpaulins strung between fuel drums. Children sitting on crates of tinned peaches, guarded by old women who had lived through one disaster in this valley already and were not going to be surprised by a second. Mara's fever ward had been decanted into two trucks and a barn loft, the sickest patients carried out on doors pried off their hinges, and Mara herself moved among them with the terrible calm of a woman who had stopped expecting help years ago and was simply doing what was in front of her.

Wren found Odile before Odile found anyone else. There was a bandage on Wren's forearm, fresh, the gauze already spotting red, and Odile felt her chest tighten around something sharp.

"You're hurt."

"It's nothing. The Halvard man's friends got creative last night—that's handled." Wren said it fast, waving it off, and Odile understood: not now. Later. If there was a later. "The count is bad, Odile. We've moved two hundred and six people out since dawn. There are better than three hundred and eighty left. And the corridor—"

"The corridor's still open."

"For now. But if that face comes down while a plane's in it—" Wren didn't finish. Didn't need to. They both saw it: a plane in the throat of the pass when the mountain exhaled, folded into the slide like a leaf into a closing book.

Cass landed behind them and walked up the strip with her helmet under her arm, hair plastered flat, and for once there was no irony anywhere on her face. "I've been watching the crack through my glasses," she said. "It's propagating north. By my read, we've got the corridor for another three hours, four if the rain holds off. After that, the whole eastern approach is a dice roll."

"Then we fly until then," Odile said.

"With what load? Every minute on the ground is a minute the window closes. We're running nineteen passengers a trip because of the weight and the downdrafts. Three hundred and eighty people at nineteen a run—" Cass shook her head. "That's twenty trips. At forty minutes a round trip, that's thirteen hours of flying. We have three."

The numbers sat there between them, ugly and indivisible. Odile had done this math once already, back when the evacuation began, and had hated herself for doing it. Now the math was doing her.

"We triage again," she said. "Sickest first. Smallest families first—one parent can carry the paperwork for a family of five. Anyone who can walk to the winter road walks; the road's still open below the snowline for another day, maybe. We fly the ones who can't walk."

"And the rest?" Mara had come up quietly, a stethoscope looped around her neck like a garrote she'd made peace with. "The rest who can't walk and can't fit?"

Nobody answered. The wind came down the valley and carried with it a smell Odile had never smelled before and recognized instantly anyway—stone dust, raw and mineral, the smell of rock that had been sealed since the world was young and was about to stop being sealed.

* * *

They flew anyway. Of course they flew.

Odile made five more runs in the dying light of the afternoon, each one through the corridor with her eyes flicking between the instruments and the east face, reading the mountain the way she'd once read her father's ledger—looking for the line where the numbers stopped adding up. The crack widened. She could see it now without glasses: a black seam high on the face, weeping dust, growing by inches she could measure against the pine trees below it. Each trip it was longer. Each trip the plume at its base stood a little taller.

On the fifth run she carried the Delacroix family—grandmother, mother, three children—and old Tomas Vey, who had helped bury her brother's memory in silence for nine years and who gripped her shoulder on the way past the cockpit and said only, "Your father would be proud," which undid her more than any accusation could have.

On the sixth run, the mountain called in its debt.

She was thirty seconds into the corridor, the walls rising on either side, the plane heavy with twenty-two people and a full tank because Cass had insisted they top off in case the staging ground closed—when the sound came. Not the knocking from the morning. Not the long exhalation. This was a roar, deep and total, the sound of several thousand tons of limestone deciding simultaneously to stop being a cliff, and it came through the airframe not as vibration but as pressure, as if the sky itself had been struck.

"Get us out—" someone screamed from the back, and Odile was already doing it, already rolling the plane hard left and diving for the valley floor, the stall warning shrieking because there was no time to trade altitude for speed politely. Behind her and above, the corridor filled.

She saw it in the mirror of her rearview glance, and she would see it for the rest of her life: the eastern face folding outward like a door kicked open, the white-brown torrent of it pouring down across the route they had flown eleven times that day, burying Theo's corridor, burying the survey markers, burying the exact coordinates where her courage had failed her a lifetime ago and where, today, it had held. Dust boiled out across the valley in a rolling wall, swallowing the treeline, swallowing the light, and Odile flew out of it into sudden clean air with her hands shaking so badly she had to lock her elbows to hold the wings level.

Twenty-two people breathing behind her. Twenty-two people alive because they had been ninety seconds earlier than the mountain's patience.

And below, on the strip at Rivergate, better than a hundred and fifty people who hadn't been.

* * *

She landed with the dust still falling like grey snow. It coated everything—the tarps, the crates, the faces turned up toward the escarpment, which now wore a new shape entirely. Where the eastern face had been there was a raw amphitheatre of fresh stone, and at its foot a rampart of rubble two hundred feet high, sealing the upper pass the way a cork seals a bottle. The winter road beyond it: gone. The corridor: gone. Everything they had flown through all day, everything Theo had mapped with the last hours of his life, buried under a hundred thousand tons of mountain.

The silence on the strip was worse than screaming. Two hundred people standing in falling stone dust, looking at the place where their way out had been, and not one of them making a sound.

Mara broke it first, turning from the escarpment to the crowd with her voice pitched to carry: "Head count! Everyone touch the person next to you and say your name!" Because of course her first thought was the wounded, the missing, the ones who might have been caught outside when it came down—and Odile loved her for it, fiercely, helplessly, even as her own mind went somewhere colder.

Because Odile's first thought was arithmetic.

One hundred and fifty-three people. That was the number Wren shouted back ten minutes later, hoarse, having counted twice. One hundred and fifty-three still in the valley, plus the walking party that had gone out on the winter road that morning—twelve souls whose fate was now written somewhere under the same rubble, unless they'd cleared the snowline in time, and no one would know until someone could get a radio to them.

One hundred and fifty-three people. And no pass.

Odile stood in the middle of the strip with dust sifting out of her hair and did the thing she had sworn on her father's grave she would never do again: she opened his survey map, right there in the wind, and looked at the route marked in his hand. The impossible one. The one that went not through the escarpment but over it—the high crossing, the one no pilot had ever survived, the one Theo had been flying when the mountain killed him.

Her father had drawn it with a note beside it, in handwriting gone brown with age: *Only in desperation. Only with the wind from the west.*

The wind was from the west. It had been all day. It was the only reason the dust was blowing away from them instead of over them.

"You're thinking about it." Wren had come up beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and there was blood seeping through the fresh bandage and dust in Wren's eyelashes and no judgment anywhere in that voice—only the question.

"I'm thinking there's no other way out."

"There might be no other way in, either. Odile, that route killed your brother."

"The blasting killed my brother." She heard her own voice come out level and strange, a stranger's voice. "Theo died mapping a corridor that was sound. His log proves it. What killed him was Halvard's dynamite, and what just sealed our pass was nine years of Halvard's dynamite loosening what should never have been touched." She folded the map along its worn creases, over and over, until it was small enough to close her fist around. "The route didn't fail. We never finished proving it."

Cass joined them, helmet off, face grey with dust and something older than fatigue. She looked at the buried pass for a long moment, then at the map in Odile's fist, and Odile watched her arrive at the same conclusion, watched her hate it, watched her refuse to look away from it.

"The high crossing," Cass said quietly. "In monsoon season. With loaded aircraft."

"With the wind from the west," Odile said.

Cass was silent. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound with no humor in it at all—and put out her hand, palm up, asking for the map.

"Give me that," she said. "If we're going to die on your brother's route, I want to study it first."

Behind them, the mountain settled with a long grinding sigh, sending a last curtain of dust drifting down the valley, and somewhere above the fresh scar, the clouds of the monsoon's leading edge began, at last, to rain.
