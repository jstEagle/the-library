## 31. The Route No One Flies

The rain came in sideways off the escarpment that night, and Odile sat in the Rivergate airstrip's single-room terminal with the map spread across two folding tables and a coffee can holding down one corner and her elbow the other.

The lamp above her buzzed. Somewhere behind the partition wall, Mara's fever ward breathed—forty-one souls now, up from thirty-six at sundown—and beneath the rain she could hear the generator laboring like a heart with a murmur. Outside, the Cessna and Cass's old Twin Otter sat tied down on the flooded apron, their shapes soft blurs under the field lights, looking less like aircraft than like animals hunkered against weather they understood better than she did.

She had been staring at the survey for three hours.

Her father's hand was unmistakable even after nine years—the heavy drafting pencil, the letters leaning forward as if impatient to arrive wherever they were going. He had drawn the standard route first: the southern pass, the wide one, the sane one, the one every freight pilot between here and Kettle Falls had flown since before Odile was born. He'd marked its ceiling heights in his neat column of figures. Two thousand feet at the saddle. A mile of clearance on either side of the ridge line. A road through the sky, wide enough to drive a truck through.

Then he had crossed it out.

Not once. Three times, each stroke harder than the last, until the pencil had torn the paper and he'd gone over the gash in ink. And below it, in the lower right quadrant of the sheet where the escarpment bent north toward the glacier country, he had drawn something else—a thin line threading a corridor so narrow it looked less like a flight path than a pulse. A heartbeat drawn by a man who knew he was running out of them.

*The North Notch,* the old surveyor had called it, back in Kettle Falls, when he'd pressed this map into her hands with an apology twenty years overdue. *Nobody flies the Notch, girl. Nobody ever did. Your father surveyed it because the mining company wanted a route over the top for their ore trucks' supply line, and he told them no, and then he drew it anyway, because a surveyor draws what exists whether he likes it or not.*

Odile leaned closer. The lamp threw her shadow across the northern third of the range, and she shifted to let the light fall into the Notch itself.

There it was. The wound in the paper.

The corridor ran between the main escarpment wall and a secondary ridge called the Sentinel—a granite fin that rose out of the valley country like the dorsal blade of some drowned animal. The gap between them was, according to her father's own figures, eleven hundred meters wide at the narrowest point. Eleven hundred meters, with walls rising sheer on both sides to four thousand feet, and the floor of the gap itself not empty but terraced—a staircase of rockfall debris from some prehistoric collapse, each step a few hundred feet higher than the last, all the way up to the saddle at eight thousand six hundred.

A canyon. A stone throat. You didn't fly through it so much as you climbed it, banking from wall to wall like a bird working up a chimney, gaining altitude in increments because there was no room to gain it any other way.

And at the top—the saddle. Eight thousand six hundred feet, which meant the ceiling of the whole route was set by the saddle, and the saddle's weather was set by whatever the monsoon decided to do with it.

No wonder her father had blacked it out. No wonder he had crossed it off the map with such violence that he'd torn the page. She could see, when she tilted the sheet against the light, the ghost of older markings beneath the ink—his original draft of the Notch route, complete with wind annotations, escape headings, notes on the downdraft behavior along the Sentinel's eastern face. He had studied it. He had almost loved it. And then—

Then Theo.

Odile closed her eyes and let the memory come, because she had learned that fighting it only made it worse. Nine years ago, in the week before the disaster, her brother had been obsessed with the Notch. He'd talked about it at dinner until their father left the table. *It's the future, Dad. It cuts forty minutes off the resupply run. Forty minutes is fuel, and fuel is margin, and margin is everything.* He'd borrowed the survey from the office. He'd flown the approach twice—twice, she knew now, though she hadn't known it then—from the valley side, low, careful, mapping the turns with a camera strapped to the passenger seat. Their father had found out and there had been a fight Odile had heard through the wall of the house, their father's voice low and terrible: *That route is a coffin with a view, and I will not bury my son in it.*

And then the slide had come down off the escarpment's southern face and taken the pass and taken the road and taken Theo—not in the Notch, never in the Notch, but on the standard route, at the standard altitude, doing nothing wrong except being in the air above a mountain that a blasting crew half a ridge away had spent six months hollowing out.

The official story said weather. Her father's oath said *never again.* And the Notch had stayed what it had always been: the route no one flew, the line on the map that everyone agreed was impossible, the wound her father had drawn and then tried to kill.

Odile opened her eyes.

The rain had thickened. Against the window, the field lights smeared into long yellow wounds. She thought about the upper pass—the southern pass, the only other way out—sealed since yesterday noon by the rockfall, a thousand tons of new stone sitting across the saddle where the freight planes used to cross, and beyond it the monsoon making landfall early, a wall of gray moving up from the coast at a pace Wren had described, over the radio from Kettle Falls, in words Odile had never heard him use.

And she thought about the arithmetic. Four hundred people. Two planes. Sixty hours, maybe, before the passes closed for good—and now only one plane, hers, since Cass had taken the Otter south with the second wave of evacuees and was due back tomorrow if the weather allowed, which the weather showed no sign of allowing.

The southern pass was shut. The road was shut. The river was rising too fast for boats. There was nowhere to go but up, and the only way up was the corridor her family had sworn off in blood.

*Inheritance as a dare,* she thought, and almost laughed. Her father had left her a debt, an oath, a blacked-out map, and a brother's grave. It turned out he had also left her a door. He just hadn't been able to bear telling her where it was.

* * *

She worked through the night, and the work felt like prayer, or like surgery—something done slowly, with clean hands, over a body that might still be saved.

Her father's original annotations were still legible beneath the ink if she held the page at an angle and let the lamp graze across it. Wind behavior along the Sentinel: *downdraft severe on east face after 1400; fly the west wall morning only.* The first terrace: *rockfall field, keep 300 ft above step lip, do not cut the corner at the bend.* The second terrace: *turbulence pocket in lee of the fin, expect 40-knot shear, commit before you enter or don't enter at all.* And at the saddle itself, in handwriting tighter than the rest, almost cramped: *Ceiling hard limit 9,500. If cloud sits on the saddle, the Notch is a trap with one mouth. There is no turning around past the second terrace. Say it again: NO TURNING AROUND.*

He had written it like a man leaving instructions for someone he hoped would never need them.

Beside the survey she laid Theo's logbook, the one she had finally read cover to cover two nights ago in the fever ward's back room while Mara slept sitting up against the wall. His last entries weren't reckless. That was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that undid her a little more each time she read them: they were meticulous. He had flow the Notch approach twice and logged both flights with the dispassionate precision of a surveyor himself—wind estimates, turn radii, groundspeed checks against known landmarks. In the margin of the final entry, dated four days before the slide, he had written: *Dad's figures check out. Route is sound within limits. Limits are honest. Tell him.*

*Tell him.* Four days later the mountain came down, and Theo never got the chance, and their father spent the rest of his life believing his son had died proving him right about the coffin with a view.

Odile sat back. The lamp buzzed. The rain hammered.

The route was sound within limits. Her father had known it—had drawn it, annotated it, tested its numbers against the mountain itself—and had buried that knowledge under ink and grief. Theo had confirmed it. And now the limits were the question, because the limits were weather, and the weather was coming down on them like a lid.

She got out the radio log and checked the barometric readings Mara's son had been taking at the schoolhouse twice a day. Pressure falling steadily for thirty hours. Freezing level dropping. By tomorrow evening, the cloud base in the valley would be under six thousand feet, and the saddle at eight-six would be inside the cloud deck, and the Notch would close like a fist.

Which meant the window wasn't sixty hours. The window was tomorrow. One day. One morning, maybe, if the cloud lifted off the saddle at dawn the way valley cloud sometimes did before a front—burning off for an hour, two at most, before the monsoon swallowed it whole.

One morning. One plane. One route.

She did the arithmetic again, slower this time, letting each number hurt. The Cessna carried nine passengers in evacuation trim, ten if she stripped the cargo pod and nobody minded sitting on their duffels. Two crossings at dawn, maybe three if the cloud held off past midmorning. Thirty people. Out of four hundred.

It wasn't enough. It was so far from enough that the number felt like an insult, and she put her palm flat on the table and breathed until the fury settled into something she could use.

But it was a start. And a start was a thing the town hadn't had since the rockfall sealed the pass, and a start was a thing Halvard's men had spent nine years and a great deal of money ensuring Rivergate would never get.

She thought about the offer she had refused on this same airstrip five days ago—the deed, the cleared debts, Theo's name polished and returned to her like a bribe wearing his face. *Fly the evacuation and say nothing about the mine.* They had wanted her flying because she was deniable, because she was the daughter of the man they'd blamed, because a Dunmore at the controls made the whole catastrophe look like an act of God with a family grudge attached. They had never imagined she'd refuse. They had certainly never imagined she'd take the route her father had crossed out—the one route they couldn't have planned for, because no one flew it, because it was impossible, because it killed Dunmores.

Well. It hadn't killed Theo. They had. And the route was still there, waiting in the paper like a held breath.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the tables, the map now crowded with her own annotations in red grease-pencil—turn points, altitudes, go/no-go gates—layered over her father's ghost-ink and Theo's marginal faith. She had slept perhaps ninety minutes in the chair, and her neck knew it, and she found she didn't care.

Mara came in at first light with two cups of tea and the particular walk of a woman who had been on her feet in a fever ward all night. She set one cup by Odile's elbow without asking, looked at the map for a long moment, and said nothing, which was its own kind of permission.

"It's the Notch," Odile said anyway. "My father's route. Through the gap past the Sentinel."

"I know what it is." Mara wrapped both hands around her cup. "Everyone in this valley knows what it is. My grandfather helped carry your father's survey gear up the Sentinel when I was a girl. He said Aldous Dunmore stood on that fin for a whole afternoon, just looking at the gap, like a man reading a letter he wasn't sure he wanted answered."

"What did he decide?"

"He decided the mountain was honest," Mara said. "That was the word he used. Honest. He said the southern pass lied to you—looked easy and killed you when the company loosened its teeth. But the Notch, he said, tells you exactly what it is, and lets you decide." She looked at Odile over the rim of her cup. "Your brother believed him."

Odile's throat tightened. "You knew about Theo's flights."

"Half the valley knew. We watched him working the approach that summer, low and slow, careful as a man defusing something. We were proud of him." Her voice roughened. "And then the slide came, and the company's story stuck, and it seemed cruel to say otherwise. So we didn't. That's our shame, Odile, not yours. Nine years of silence, and we let your family carry it alone."

Odile looked down at the map—at the thin red line she'd drawn through the Notch, climbing the terraces, cresting the saddle, descending the far side into the green country beyond the range where the staging fields waited. It looked fragile as a hair. It looked, she thought, exactly like what it was: the only door out of a burning room.

"The cloud," she said. "What does the valley say about tomorrow?"

Mara was quiet a moment. "Old Ferro says the mountain breathes out at dawn before a big storm. An hour of clear air, maybe two, like it's sorry." She set down her cup. "He's been wrong twice in fifty years."

"And the freezing level?"

"Dropping. The schoolhouse gauge says snow on the saddle by tomorrow night." Mara met her eyes. "So it's dawn or it's never."

*Dawn or never.* Odile looked out the window, where the rain was thinning into that gray suspended drizzle that preceded real weather, and the escarpment's southern wall stood invisible somewhere above the cloud, holding its rockfall across the pass like a hand pressed over a mouth. Somewhere up there was the Notch, and the Sentinel, and the saddle at eight-six, and her brother's last honest work.

She had spent nine years learning to be invisible, and invisibility had nearly cost her everything—Wren, the strip, the truth, the town. Tomorrow at first light she would fly the most visible line in the sky, up a stone chimney with no room to turn around, carrying the first thirty of four hundred people, betting her life and her father's name and her brother's vindication on an hour of clear air and a dead man's figures.

*Route is sound within limits. Tell him.*

"I'll tell him," she said quietly, to the map, to the mountain, to the boy who had written those words four days before the world ended. "Tomorrow. At the top."

Mara touched her shoulder once, briefly, and went back to her ward.

Odile began packing the map, folding it along her father's original creases, and that was when she heard it—over the rain, faint and stuttering, cutting through the static on the field radio she'd left tuned to the Kettle Falls frequency all night.

Cass's voice. Broken, wind-torn, barely readable—but unmistakable, and saying words that dropped the bottom out of Odile's stomach:

*"—Otter down safe, repeat, down safe at the falls—but Odile, listen—they've got the field here locked, Halvard's lawyers and a provincial writ, nobody's flying north—I'm coming up the Notch myself at dawn, do you copy? I'm taking the corridor. Save me a slot at the top—"*

Static. Then, fainter, almost lost: *"—and Odile. Your dad's figures. I read them all night. They're good."*

Odile stood very still in the buzzing lamplight, the folded map pressed to her chest, and outside the rain began, very softly, to stop.
