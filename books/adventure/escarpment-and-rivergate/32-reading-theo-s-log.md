## 32. Reading Theo's Log

The lamp in the Rivergate nurse's quarters buzzed like a trapped insect, and Odile sat beneath it with her brother's flight log open on her knees for the first time in nine years.

She had carried it since Kettle Falls—carried it, in truth, since the surveyor had pressed it into her hands along with the map, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a length of frayed surveyor's twine. She had carried it through the night flight, through the landing at Rivergate, through the fever ward where Mara's hands moved from one burning child to the next with the economy of someone who had learned to ration hope. She had set it on the shelf beside her cot each night and told herself she would read it when there was time. When the weather held. When the medicine arrived. When she was stronger.

There was no more *when*. The monsoon was forty hours out, maybe less. The upper pass was sealed behind a wall of fresh rockfall. And tomorrow morning, if anyone was going to fly Theo's corridor at all, someone would have to believe in it first.

So she untied the twine.

The logbook was the same make as hers—the same stiff blue cover, the same ruled columns, the same faint smell of kerosene and cedar that clung to everything their father had ever touched. Dunmore & Sons Aviation Supply, the embossing read, though there had never been any sons but Theo, and their father had ordered the books anyway, optimistic about a future that lasted exactly four years past this one.

Her thumb found the corner of the cover, worn soft and pale. He had worried that corner with his fingers the way he'd worried everything—pencil erasers, zipper pulls, the hem of his sleeve when he was thinking. She could see him doing it. Nineteen years old, sitting cross-legged on the hangar floor at Escarpment, filling out his hours by lamplight while she pretended to study approach plates across from him and actually watched his face.

*You log the weather too,* twelve-year-old Odile had said once, frowning at the columns. *Nobody logs the weather.*

*Everybody logs the weather,* Theo had said. *I log what the weather* meant.

She opened to the first page and began at the beginning, because she could not bear not to.

* * *

The early entries were almost unbearably young. Student solos logged in careful block capitals. A first cross-country to Kettle Falls with the wind entered twice because he'd forgotten to close the column the first time. A note in the remarks section: *Dad says I flare high. He's right.* Beside it, a smaller annotation in their father's hand: *He flares high.*

She laughed—a broken sound in the empty room—and pressed her knuckles to her mouth until it stopped being something else.

Page by page, hour by hour, the boy became a pilot. The handwriting tightened. The remarks grew longer and stranger: observations of lenticular clouds over the escarpment, of the way the morning fog pooled in the Rivergate valley "like water finding its level," of downdrafts on the north face that didn't match anything in the textbooks. He'd begun flying the escarpment routes before he was legally allowed to carry passengers for hire, running light freight for their father, and every flight over the range came back annotated with wind speeds he'd estimated by the bend of the trees, by the smoke off the smelter, by the behavior of snow cornices he could name individually the way other pilots named their airplanes.

*East cornice slumped overnight. Wind must have been 60+ up top while we sat on the ground drinking coffee. The mountain does its real work while we sleep.*

Odile turned pages with increasing care, as if the paper might bruise.

Then, eleven months before the crash, the entries changed character entirely. The flights became systematic. Same departure times, same headings, same altitudes flown in sequence—050, then 055, then 060 over the saddle between the twin peaks, each pass logged with temperature, wind, cloud base, and a notation she hadn't understood until tonight:

*Corr. test 14. Marginal below 8,000. Corridor holds if entry made east of the notch and descent begun before the second rib. Dad's route is wrong. Not unsafe—wrong. It fights the terrain instead of using it.*

*Dad's route is wrong.*

Their father had flown the escarpment for thirty years. Their father had taught half the bush pilots in the province. And Theo, twenty years old, had written in his own logbook—in ink—that the old man's line through the passes was not the best line, and had set out to prove it.

She remembered now, memory arriving like floodwater finding an old channel: the arguments at the dinner table. Her father's jaw going tight whenever Theo mentioned the corridor. *The mountain doesn't care about your cleverness,* he'd said, more than once. *It cares about its own opinion.* And Theo, grinning, unquenchable: *Then I'll learn its opinion.*

He hadn't been showing off. He hadn't been reckless. He had been doing the most careful thing a pilot could do—building, flight by flight, a documented, repeatable, safe passage through terrain everyone else crossed by folklore. Every entry was a brick. He was building a road.

* * *

The last three months of entries were written in weather that should have kept him grounded, and Odile felt her chest tighten with the old grief, the old anger—until she read closer and saw what he was actually doing. He wasn't pushing through storms. He was flying the corridor's edges in deteriorating conditions deliberately, methodically, logging how the route degraded, establishing margins. *Corridor test 31. In cloud from the notch to the second rib. Entry fix holds. Descent angle steep but stable. This route will work in weather that grounds the main pass. That's the whole point.*

That's the whole point.

A route that worked when the main pass didn't. A backup line through the escarpment for the days the mountain closed its front door—so that Rivergate would never again be cut off, so that medicine and mail and people could always get through.

He was building it for them. For the town. For the valley full of strangers who called their father stubborn and their son promising. Nine years ago, when the official report came down—pilot error, VFR into IMC, a young man overreaching—Odile had believed it, because believing it was the only shape her grief could hold. Her brilliant, restless brother had finally outrun his own skill. It was terrible, but it was comprehensible, and comprehensible grief can be buried.

The truth in these pages was harder and cleaner: Theo Dunmore died doing the most conservative flying of his life, mapping an escape route for people who would spend the next nine years blaming his family for his death.

She turned to the final week.

* * *

October 3rd. *Corr. test 44. Full profile in gusting crosswind. The notch entry is the key—everything else follows. Wrote it all up for Dad. He didn't read it. Mom would have read it.* Their mother had been two years gone by then; the sentence sat there without self-pity, just a fact logged alongside the winds aloft, and it undid Odile completely. She let the tears come this time, let them fall on the page and blotted them carefully with her sleeve, because this book had survived enough.

October 9th. *Blasting on the ridge above the pass again. Third time this month. Who blasts above a flyway? Shook the whole face loose below the notch. Flew wide. Logged the rockfall field—new debris in the gully, big blocks, fresh faces on the stone. Somebody needs to ask questions.*

October 11th. *Asked at the company office in Kettle Falls. They said it's Halvard Group, survey work, all permits in order. Permits for WHAT? There's nothing up there to mine. Nothing legal, anyway. Dad says drop it. Dad says a lot of things.*

October 13th. His last entry. The handwriting was steady, unhurried, a man writing down facts.

*Final corr. test planned for tomorrow, full profile with the new entry fix. Also going to photograph the blasting site from the air—someone has to have a record of what they're doing up there. If the face comes down above the pass the way it's shedding now, it won't be pilots who pay for it. It'll be Rivergate. Their whole watershed sits under that slope. Nobody else seems to see it. Maybe I'm wrong. But I've flown that face sixty times and I know how it breathes, and right now it's holding its breath.*

*If anything happens tomorrow, tell Odie the corridor works. Tell her the numbers are all in here. She'll know what to do with them.*

Odie.

She closed the book and held it against her sternum and wept—not the dry, controlled grieving of nine years but the raw crying of a girl whose brother had promised to teach her lazy eights over the lake in spring, who had kept his promise in the only way left to him, in ink, in numbers, in a message that had taken nine years and a dead surveyor's courage to reach her.

*She'll know what to do with them.*

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough that the lamp's buzz became the room's whole weather. Long enough that when a knock came at the door, dawn had gone from black to the color of wet slate.

"Odile." Cass's voice, low, stripped of its usual armor. "Mara sent me. The fever's spread to the mill families. She needs the sulfa tonight or she starts losing people she can't lose." A pause. "And the barometer's falling faster than the forecast said. Whatever window we thought we had is closing."

Odile wiped her face with both hands, opened the door, and held out the logbook.

Cass took it, glanced at the cover, and looked up sharply. "Is this—"

"Theo's. All of it. Every test flight." Her voice came out rough but level. "He mapped the corridor, Cass. Flight by flight, sixty-one profiles, wind data, entry fixes, descent angles. It's not a dare and it's not a gamble. It's a surveyed route that happens to be written in a dead man's hand." She swallowed. "He died photographing the mine. Halvard's mine. He saw what they were doing to the slope and he flew up to document it, and they brought the face down on him and called it pilot error."

Cass stood very still. Rain had begun somewhere above them—you could hear it starting on the tin roof, tentative, like fingers testing a drumhead. When she spoke, her voice had the flat precision Odile had learned to hear as respect.

"The numbers are good? You've checked them?"

"I checked them against my own flights over that range for nine years. Against the surveyor's map. Against everything I know about how that mountain behaves." Odile nodded toward the window, toward the black wall of the escarpment invisible beyond the rain. "The corridor works. It works *because* of weather like this—it was built for weather like this. The main pass will be suicide by tomorrow noon. Theo's line will still be flyable."

"And you want to fly it."

"I want us to fly it. Tonight, ahead of the worst of it, with Mara's sulfa and every radio battery in town." She met Cass's eyes. "But someone goes first. Someone proves it with a load that isn't patients. Someone who isn't me, because if I'm leading and it goes wrong, no one behind me will trust the route—they'll just think the Dunmores die on that mountain, that's all, it runs in the family."

Something moved through Cass's face—nine years of rivalry, of borrowed planes and beaten ETAs and a history neither of them had ever bothered to narrate—and settled into something quieter and far more dangerous.

"Give me an hour to preflight," Cass Merrow said, handing back the logbook like a relay baton. "I'll take the lead plane."

* * *

Outside, the rain thickened. Odile stood on the nurse's porch with her brother's log under her coat, watching the valley lights waver in the wet dark, and spoke to him the way she used to through the hangar wall when he was working late and she couldn't sleep.

"You built the door, Theo. I'm just the one walking through it."

Above the valley, unseen, the escarpment breathed its held breath, and the monsoon came on.
