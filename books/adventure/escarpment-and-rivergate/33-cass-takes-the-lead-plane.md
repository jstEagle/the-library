## 33. Cass Takes the Lead Plane

Cass Merrow had never been afraid of silence. She'd grown up inside it—the kind that lived in hangars after hours, in the space between a father's disappointment and his next sentence. Silence was where she did her best arithmetic. And she was doing it now, standing at the edge of the Rivergate strip with Theo Dunmore's flight log open on her knee, while the mountain across the valley shed its first real argument of the season.

The escarpment was talking. That was the only word for it—a low grinding somewhere above the tree line, like a jaw working something over, deciding whether to swallow. Every few minutes a crack rolled down the rock face and the ravens lifted off the scree in a black ragged sheet and settled again, as if they'd heard it all before.

Behind her, the town was loading its second wave. She could hear Wren Adeyemi's voice carrying across the gravel, hoarse from three days of it—*lighter, lighter, we go by weight not by want*—and the slap of cargo straps and a child crying in a way that wasn't frightened so much as furious at being carried away from home. The planes were running up their engines in staggered turns, two at a time, because Cass had insisted on staggering them ever since the upper pass sealed.

She had read Theo's log four times now. Not the way Odile read it—with her finger hovering over the handwriting like it might still be warm—but the way a pilot reads another pilot's work, which is to say critically, looking for the flaw, the place where the dead man got it wrong. Because there was always a place. Cass had built a career on finding those places in other people's flying.

There wasn't one.

That was the problem. She'd gone through the corridor route line by line, altitude by altitude, wind call by wind call, and the man had flown it clean. He'd mapped a slot through the eastern wall of the range that no survey acknowledged and no chart showed—a notch above the glacier shelf where the terrain funneled the katabatic flow into a predictable downdraft you could ride if you committed early enough, and died if you hesitated. It required entering the gap at eleven thousand two hundred feet with the mountain on your left wingtip close enough to count the strata. It required trusting that the air behind the first ridge behaved the way he said it did, based on six passes flown nine years ago in weather no sane person would launch into.

It required believing that Theo Dunmore hadn't died because he was reckless. He'd died because Halvard's blasting brought half the cliff down on top of him while he was doing the most careful flying of his life.

Cass closed the log and looked at the mountain for a long time.

* * *

She found Odile in the fever ward's anteroom, which had become, in the last seventy-two hours, the closest thing Rivergate had to a planning office. Maps taped to the walls. A radio handset on a crate. Mara's patients visible through the doorway behind, rows of cots, the smell of carbolic and woodsmoke. Odile stood over the evacuation manifest with a grease pencil, and Cass watched her for a moment before speaking—watched her cross out a name, add it back, cross out another family, her mouth moving slightly around numbers.

Two hundred souls per plane-day, give or take. Four hundred and change in the town. Six loads each if the weather held, and the weather was not going to hold. The monsoon's leading edge was a gray wall on the southern horizon that grew visibly taller every time Cass looked at it, like a tide coming in over the whole sky.

And the upper pass—the good pass, the wide one, the one any charter pilot could fly drunk—was gone. Sealed under forty tons of new rockfall since yesterday morning. Which meant every remaining load went through Theo's corridor or nobody went anywhere.

"We need to talk about the crossing," Cass said.

Odile didn't look up. "I've got the weights worked out. First wave is elders and the fever cases, Mara goes with them—"

"That's not what I want to talk about."

Odile looked up then, and Cass saw the thing she'd been seeing more of every day: not the rival, not the woman who'd spent nine years perfecting the art of being elsewhere, but a pilot at the end of her reserves, running on fumes and duty, eyes red-rimmed and absolutely level.

"Say it," Odile said.

"Someone flies it first." Cass kept her voice flat, professional, the register she used for pre-flight briefings. "Not the first load. Before the first load. Empty ship, or near enough. We prove the corridor before we put two hundred people's mothers and children in it."

"Prove it how?"

"Fly it. Report back. If the air behind the first ridge doesn't match Theo's notes—if the downdraft's shifted, if the blasting's changed the thermal profile—we'll know before it matters."

Odile set the grease pencil down very carefully, the way people do when they're keeping their hands busy so their hands don't do something else. "I'll fly it."

"No."

"It's my brother's route. My family's map. My—" Her voice caught, and she pushed through it. "My name on this. I should be the one."

"That's exactly why it can't be you." Cass stepped closer, close enough that the ward smells gave way to avgas and cold air that clung to her jacket. "You turned back at the pass once already. At the exact coordinates. Don't look at me like that—I'd have done the same, probably worse. But if you're in that cockpit with the mountain on your wingtip and your brother's ghost in the right seat, and the air does something the log didn't predict, you will hesitate. Half a second. One second. And in that corridor, one second is the difference between a story and an obituary."

"You don't know that."

"I know pilots." Cass heard her own voice do something it almost never did—soften. "I've spent nine years knowing you, Odile. Watching you. Racing you. Hating you, if we're honest, though I couldn't have told you why for most of it. And I'm telling you: you're too heavy with this one. You need someone in front of you with nothing in the airplane but fuel and nerve."

"And that's you?"

Cass smiled, and it cost her something, and she let him see that it cost her, which was maybe the point. "I've been flying other people's dirty jobs for two years. Halvard hired me to force this contract, and I took the money because I wanted to find out who was paying and why, and every leg of that I flew, I flew toward the answer. This is the same flight. Last leg. Except this time I picked the job myself."

* * *

They argued for another hour. Of course they did. They argued in the anteroom, and then outside in the rain that had started coming down sideways off the glacier, and then in the hangar with the lead plane's cowling open between them like a lectern. Wren listened from the workbench with a torque wrench going quiet in her hand, saying nothing, watching the two of them with the expression of someone who has learned that some negotiations between pilots are like weather—you don't intervene, you just secure loose objects.

The argument was never really about who was the better pilot. Both of them knew the answer to that varied by day and aircraft and how much sleep either of them had gotten. It was about worth. About whose life the corridor got to test first. And underneath that, unspoken, nine years of accumulated ledger entries—every race won by inches, every insult traded across airstrip bars, every year Cass had spent being the successful one while telling herself she didn't miss the friendship that had come before the rivalry, back when they were both nineteen and shared a bunkhouse at Kettle Falls and traded paperback novels and called each other by nicknames neither would admit to now.

Cass remembered the exact moment the friendship broke. A regional championship, a fogged-in final, and she'd taken a departure window Odile wanted and won the leg by ninety seconds, and Odile had looked at her across the ramp with something that wasn't anger—it was recognition, the terrible kind, the kind that says *you are me and I am you and one of us is going to spend her whole life trying to be the other*. They'd been rivals ever since because rivalry was easier than intimacy. Rivalry had rules. You could lose at rivalry and survive.

"I'm not asking your permission," Cass said finally. "I'm informing my second."

Odile stared at her. Rain dripped off the hangar's open door in a bright stitched curtain. Somewhere out in the town a generator coughed and steadied.

"If you buy it in there," Odile said, "I will never forgive you."

"I know." Cass checked the fuel totalizer on the panel without looking at it, needing her hands on something. "But you'd forgive me if I stayed on the ground and the first load went in blind and lost kids? You'd carry that better?"

Silence. The wrench on the workbench clicked softly as Wren set it down.

"No," Odile said. Barely audible. "No, I wouldn't."

"Then this is the trade." Cass zipped her flight jacket. "One empty crossing for two hundred certain ones. Best exchange rate in aviation."

* * *

Pre-flight took forty minutes and Cass performed every item herself, waving off the ground crew, because this was one inspection she wanted in her own hands. Wren hovered anyway—not interfering, just present, handing her tools before she asked, which was its own kind of conversation. When Cass pulled the aileron cables through their travel and felt them sing true, Wren finally spoke.

"Theo's log. The entry about the second ridge. There's a line crossed out."

"I saw it."

"You read what's under it?"

Cass nodded. She had held the page to a lamp at an angle until the impression of the pen came up through the strike-through like a bruise. *Wind is lying*, the buried line said. *Trust the sound of the water, not the flags.* Below the second ridge there was a streambed, invisible from altitude except as a darker seam in the rock, and the water's pitch changed a quarter mile before the final gap. Theo had trusted his ears over the surface indicators. It was the kind of detail only a pilot who expected to die would bother recording—and only a pilot who expected to live would bother correcting.

"He was mapping a rescue route," Cass said quietly. "For other people. He just ran out of sky before he finished proving it."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coverall pocket and pressed something into Cass's palm: a small brass whistle on a lanyard, the kind river guides wore. "Rivergate made these," she said. "Mara gave it to me. If you're down in there and walking out, blow it at the glacier toe—they'll hear it over the water. And if you're not walking out—" Wren's jaw tightened. "Then it comes back to Odile. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Cass." Wren's voice dropped to something that wasn't mechanic's talk at all. "She'll follow you in whether you clear it or not. So don't make her. Come back and land, and let her follow you the boring way."

Cass laughed despite everything, a short rough sound. "Adeyemi, you're wasted on engines."

"I keep telling people."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of wet slate. The monsoon wall had eaten half the southern sky overnight, and the light over the escarpment was thin and metallic, the kind of illumination that makes mountains look like X-rays of themselves. Cass sat in the lead plane's left seat and ran the start sequence with hands that were perfectly steady, which surprised her. She'd expected fear. What she felt instead was something older and stranger—clarity, terrible and clean, the way the world looks in the last seconds before a stall when you finally stop fighting the aircraft and simply fly it.

Odile stood at the wing root. She'd argued right up until the engines started, and then she'd stopped arguing, because pilots understand that at some point argument becomes ballast.

"Cass." Odile leaned in over the sill, rain beading on her collar. Whatever she'd prepared to say, she abandoned it. What came out instead was: "When we were nineteen. Kettle Falls. You left a book on my bunk. You never admitted it."

Cass looked at her. Nine years fell away like chaff off a climb-out.

"*Stormglass*," she said. "Annotated. You were supposed to return it."

"I still have it."

"Well." Cass pulled her headset on, and her voice went thick and she let it, one time, in front of one person. "Keep it. Fly when I'm through."

She taxied out with the town watching from the strip's edge—a ragged silent line of four hundred people, the ones who'd drawn low numbers and the ones who hadn't, all of them understanding exactly what the empty plane rolling toward the runway meant. Old men who remembered her father. Children on shoulders. Mara with her sleeves rolled, arms crossed against the cold. Nobody waved. It was better than waving. It was the whole town holding its breath in common.

Cass lined up on the wet gravel, ran the engine to full song, and released the brakes.

The corridor swallowed her in nineteen minutes. She flew it exactly as the log described—eleven thousand two hundred at the notch, the stratified wall sliding past her left wingtip close enough that she could see frost in the joints of the stone, the downdraft taking the plane in its fist at the second ridge exactly as promised, a fifteen-hundred-foot-per-minute hand pressing her down toward the glacier shelf while she rode it rather than fought it, throttle cracked, nose trimmed, trusting a dead man's arithmetic with her life. The stream appeared below, a silver thread in a slot of shadow, and she opened the window to hear it over the engine roar, and its pitch rose, and rose, and she banked into the final gap with the water singing the note Theo had written down nine years ago, and the corridor opened ahead of her like a door.

On the far side, above the clouds, in light the valley would not see for days, she leveled off, wiped her eyes with the back of one glove, and keyed the radio.

"Escarpment lead, corridor is good. Repeat—corridor is good. Wind's lying, water's honest. Send them through."

Behind her, faint through the static, she heard Odile's voice break on the acknowledgment—and then steady, and then rise into the crisp cadence of a pilot with a job, calling for the first load of passengers to bring them forward.

Cass turned the plane south and started back, alone in the bright cold, carrying the whistle in her pocket and nine years of rivalry settling behind her like contrail—gone, dispersed, weightless.

Below, at the edge of hearing, the mountain was still talking.

But for the first time since she'd arrived in this valley, Cass Merrow noticed that it sounded less like a threat than like a countdown—and the monsoon wall on the horizon had begun, very quietly, to move.
