## 34. The Crossing

The monsoon arrived the way a debt collector arrives—not early, not late, but exactly when it could do the most damage.

Odile stood on the Rivergate strip at four in the morning and watched the leading edge of it come down the valley like a curtain being drawn across the world. It was not cloud, not really. It was weather with intent, a gray wall of rain and wind stacked from the river to the peaks, and behind it, somewhere over the escarpment, the passes were already filling with snow that would not melt until spring. The radio had given them six days. The sky was giving them one.

"Fuel's topped," Wren said beside her, voice pitched low under the wind. They had not slept. Nobody had slept. The whole town seemed to be standing in the dark holding lanterns, watching two old cargo planes be loaded by lantern light with everything that couldn't walk out: the fever ward's sickest, the oldest of the old, children wrapped in quilts against a cold they hadn't felt yet because they'd never left the valley.

"How many?" Odile asked, though she knew.

"Twenty-two souls in yours. Nineteen in Cass's." Wren's hand found her elbow, brief, grounding. "Mara triaged all night. She said—" A pause. Wren didn't finish it, and Odile was grateful. She didn't need to hear what Mara had said about who was too far gone to fly. That arithmetic lived in another chapter, one she refused to open before dawn.

She turned to the plane. *Kestrel*, her father had named her, forty years ago, before Odile was born, before Theo, before any of it. The paint was flaking off the cowl and the number three cylinder still ran hot if you let her lean out, and this morning she was the most beautiful machine Odile had ever looked at, because this morning she was a door.

Theo's route lay folded in her breast pocket, inside a plastic sleeve she'd taken from the med kit. She had read it eleven times since midnight. She could recite it: up the north fork to the notch below Sentinel, then hard left where the terrain funneled, riding the lee-side updraft along the escarpment's shoulder for nine miles of corridor no wider than the wingspan's margin for error, then the saddle at eleven thousand two hundred feet—the saddle Theo had died trying to prove—and down the far side through the cloud deck to Kettle Falls. His log called it *the throat*. His last entry, written in the careful block letters he'd used since he was twelve, said: *Corridor is sound. Winds cooperative before 1100. Tell D—* And there it ended, mid-sentence, mid-word, the way his life had.

Tell Dad. She knew. She'd always known what the unfinished word was going to say.

"You don't have to fly first," Wren said. "Cass offered."

"Cass is flying point. I'm flying wing." Odile checked the tie-downs on the forward pallet herself, though Wren had checked them twice. "I've spent nine years letting other people go first through my own family's weather. I'm done with that."

Wren studied her for a long moment. In the lantern light their face was all planes and shadows, grease still in the creases of their knuckles from an engine they'd rebuilt by feel in the dark. "Then come back," Wren said simply. "That's the whole flight plan. Come back."

Odile kissed them—quick, hard, in front of God and the whole town and the mountain, because if the mountain was going to take anything from her today it would have to take it while she was looking straight at it—and climbed aboard.

* * *

Engine start at 0440. Cass's voice came through on the radio, stripped of everything except function: *"Kestrel, Merrow. You ready?"*

"Ready."

*"Then let's go meet your brother."*

They took off into the north fork as the first gray light came up, and for twenty minutes it was almost kind. The valley floor was calm, the air smooth, the river below them running high and brown with the storm's first meltwater. Odile flew formation off Cass's right wing close enough to see the rivets, close enough to see Cass glance over twice and nod once—the nod meaning *you're steady, we're good*—and Odile thought, absurdly, of every race they'd ever flown as girls, every dare, every time Cass had pushed her to fly one degree closer to the edge than sense allowed. All of it had been rehearsal. All of it had been two pilots learning each other's hands so that someday, on a morning like this one, they could trust each other without speaking.

At the notch below Sentinel, the mountain stopped being kind.

The wind came off the escarpment in a single solid gust that shoved the *Kestrel* sideways thirty feet, and Odile corrected without thinking, hands moving the way her father had taught them to move, the way Theo's hands must have moved in the last seconds of his life when the same gust found him. No. Not the same gust. Different day, different hour. *His corridor is sound.* She said it aloud to herself, alone in the cockpit, and heard her own voice crack on the second word.

*"Merrow to Dunmore. Turning in on my mark. Three, two—one."*

Cass banked left into the funnel and vanished into shadow, and Odile followed her in.

The throat closed around them.

It was exactly what Theo's survey promised and nothing like what her imagination had feared, which was somehow worse, because now she knew he had flown this true. The walls rose sheer on either side, granite streaked black with nine years of runoff, close enough that she could count the strata. Above them the ridge line ran like a jagged seam of stitching between a sky going the color of wet slate and a valley drowning in cloud. The corridor itself was a channel of moving air, and the air was alive—lifting along the lee wall where the wind spilled over the top and curled, sinking in the middle, turbulent at the edges where eddies spun off the rock ribs. Theo had drawn it all on the map. *Ride the lift high on the left. Stay out of the middle. The middle is where the mountain keeps its teeth.*

She rode the lift high on the left.

Nine miles. At ninety knots it was six minutes. It was the longest six minutes of her life, and it was also, in some way she could not yet name, the shortest—because inside the throat there was no past and no future, only the next hundred feet of rock and the next correction and the sound of her own breathing and, faintly, beneath the engine noise, a feeling she had not had since she was nineteen years old: that her brother was flying with her. Not haunting her. Flying with her. Every mark on that map was his hand on her controls. He had spent his last hours drawing her a road home and she had spent nine years refusing to look at it, and now here she was, inside his handwriting, and it held.

Halfway through, the whiteout came.

It came the way the surveyor had warned it might—a spillover of cloud cresting the ridge like water over a dam, swallowing the light, erasing the walls, erasing the sky, erasing Cass's airplane thirty feet off her wing. One heartbeat Cass was there; the next there was only gray, total and absolute, the kind of gray you could fall into forever. Her instruments swam. The horizon indicator tumbled. Somewhere below and to the right was eight hundred feet of granite that did not care about instruments.

*"Merrow to Dunmore."* Cass's voice came through the static, level, almost conversational, the way you talk to someone on the edge of a ledge. *"I'm showing the saddle bearing zero-four-one, seven miles. My gyro says climb fifty feet and hold the left wall by sound of my engine. Can you hear me?"*

"I can hear you."

*"Then stay on my voice. Don't look for me. There's nothing to see. Just fly the map and follow my voice, and in seven minutes you'll be looking at open sky."*

So Odile flew blind through her brother's corridor with a dead horizon and a rival's voice for a lifeline, climbing fifty feet, holding left, correcting by the feel of the lift on her wings the way Theo's notes said to, the way her father must have taught them both without ever knowing he was teaching for this. The rain began—fat, horizontal, hammering the windshield like thrown gravel. The turbulence came in long rolling waves, and the passengers behind her were silent in a way that was worse than screaming, twenty-two people trusting her absolutely in the dark, an old woman praying in a language Odile half-remembered from her mother, a child crying without sound.

And in the middle of it—in the worst of it, in the moment when the downdraft hit and drove them down two hundred feet in four seconds and her stomach rose into her throat and every instinct screamed *pull up, pull up*—in that exact moment Odile felt something break loose in her chest, something that had been lodged there for nine years like shrapnel, and what rushed into the space it left was not fear.

It was clarity. Terrible, luminous clarity. *This is what he saw. This is what he loved.* Theo hadn't died running from anything. He had died doing the thing that made him most himself, mapping a way through for people who needed one, and the men above the pass had dropped the mountain on him for it, and none of that—not the blast, not the lie, not the nine empty years—could touch what was happening right now, which was that his sister was flying his route and his route was *sound*.

The downdraft released them. The lift caught. She climbed back to altitude with her hands shaking and her heart singing.

*"Two miles,"* Cass said. *"One. Coming out—now."*

The gray tore open like a curtain, and there it was: the saddle, a notch of bruised gold morning light between two black towers of rock, snow streaming off its lip in banners, and beyond it the whole western slope falling away into cloud and distance and the rest of the world. Cass's plane shot through ahead of her, wings rocking—joy or relief or salute, maybe all three—and Odile followed her through the gap at eleven thousand two hundred feet, through the exact coordinates where her brother's transponder had gone dark, and came out the other side into open air.

The radio was full of noise for a moment—her own breath, the engine, the wind—and then she heard herself laughing. Or sobbing. It sounded the same at altitude.

*"Kestrel, Merrow,"* Cass transmitted, and her voice wasn't level anymore either. *"Your brother sends his regards."*

* * *

Kettle Falls materialized out of the murk at 0610, the strip soaked and streaming, ground crew waving them in with both arms. Odile set the *Kestrel* down so gently the passengers applauded, and then she sat with her forehead on the yoke for a full minute after shutdown, unable to move, while behind her the doors opened and the first of Rivergate's people stepped out onto solid ground and into their lives.

An ambulance took the fever cases. Volunteers took the rest. The old woman who had prayed paused beside the cockpit door, gripped Odile's shoulder with a hand like a bird's foot, and said something in her own tongue that Odile didn't understand but received anyway, the way you receive a blessing meant for someone else and passed along the line until it finds you.

Wren's plane was due in from Escarpment within the hour with the second load of fuel and the parts for the strip's broken radio relay. Cass stood in the rain under the wing, wringing out her cap, watching the refugees disappear into the terminal lights.

"Turnaround's two hours if the weather holds," Cass said. "It won't. Front's making landfall early—we've got maybe three more crossings in us before the saddle closes for the season." She looked west, toward the wall of gray advancing on the range, and her jaw tightened. "Three crossings moves sixty more people. That leaves a hundred and eighty still down there in the valley, Odile. Maybe more, if the slide takes the lower road tonight."

Odile followed her gaze. The monsoon's edge had reached the foothills. By nightfall it would be sitting on the escarpment like a lid.

"One crossing at a time," she said, and went to refuel.

Behind them, far to the east, muffled by rain and distance, the mountain made a sound like a door closing.
