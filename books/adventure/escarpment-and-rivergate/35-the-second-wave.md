## 35. The Second Wave

The first crossing had been a miracle. The second was a decision.

Odile stood on the Rivergate strip at first light, watching the fog lift off the valley floor in slow ribbons, and understood that the difference between the two was everything. A miracle you could thank God for and never repeat. A decision you made again, and again, until your hands knew the shape of it better than your fear did.

Theo's corridor lay open above the escarpment, a seam of grey sky between black walls of cloud. It would not stay open. Every hour the monsoon pressed closer, thickening the air, dropping the ceiling by inches she could feel in her teeth. They had maybe two days. Maybe less.

"Fuel's topped," Wren said beside her, voice rough from two nights without sleep. "Cass took the lead plane up ten minutes ago. She's reporting the seam holding."

"Wind?"

"Fifteen knots down the valley, gusting thirty at the notch." Wren paused. "It'll be worse than yesterday."

"It's always worse than yesterday now." Odile pulled her headset up around her neck. "That's just arithmetic."

Wren caught her hand before she could turn away — quick, deliberate, calloused palm against hers. Not a gesture for the crowd of townsfolk stacking crates at the strip's edge. Just for her.

"Come back," Wren said. "Every time. That's the whole job. Come back."

"That is the whole job," Odile agreed, and squeezed once, and let go, because if she held on any longer the letting-go would cost more than either of them could pay today.

She walked to her plane through a gauntlet of quiet purpose. Old men loading blankets. Children carrying sacks of flour nearly their own weight, solemn as acolytes. Mara checking a list of names against a list of seats, her pen moving with the terrible efficiency of someone who has triaged human beings before and hates that it has become familiar.

"Forty-one souls this run," Mara said. "Twelve can't walk unaided. I've got them strapped in the cargo bay with padding. Odile—" She stopped. Her face, which had spent nine years closed to Dunmores, was open now, and it hurt worse than blame. "My mother flew out yesterday. First time she's left the valley since the disaster. She said to tell you—" Mara's jaw worked. "She said to tell you the sky remembers who belongs to it."

Odile nodded, because there was no sentence in her mouth equal to that, and climbed aboard.

* * *

The corridor was worse.

Yesterday it had been a wound; today it was a living thing, breathing cloud in and out of the notch where Theo's plane had gone into the rock. Odile flew it with his log open on her knee between corrections — she'd copied the critical headings onto a card taped to the panel, his handwriting marching across it like a soldier's: steady, exact, unhurried. *Left of the slide scar. Trust the river sound, not your eyes.* He had written that nine years ago, mapping a way out for people he'd never meet, and today forty-one of them were alive inside his handwriting.

"Seam's narrowing," Cass called over the radio from the lead plane, already through, her voice flat and professional and underneath it singing. "You've got about twenty minutes of this before the afternoon build-up. Don't sightsee."

"I never sightsee."

"You're sightseeing right now. I can hear it in your turns."

Odile laughed — actually laughed, alone in the cockpit with forty-one lives behind her — and put the plane into the notch.

The downdraft hit like a hand pressing down on the tail. The mountain leaned over her. For one white instant she was nine years younger, watching a speck that was her brother fall out of this same sky, and then she heard the river — faint, below the engine roar, the sound Theo had told her to trust — and she banked left of the slide scar and the corridor opened its throat and let her through.

Behind her, forty-one people breathed. She could hear a child crying, steadily, unashamed, and an old woman singing to it, some valley hymn worn smooth as river stone. Odile flew toward the sound. It was better than instruments.

They came out the far side into hard sunlight, the staging valley green and calm as if none of it existed, and when she set down on Kettle Falls' muddy strip the ground crew came running, and the doors opened, and the old woman carried the child down the steps still singing.

Every safe landing, Odile thought, taxiing clear while the next load was marshaled. That was the resurrection. Not hers. Theirs. Nine years of silence undone one landing at a time.

By noon she'd flown it twice. By mid-afternoon, three times. Her arms ached from holding the yoke against gusts that wanted to fold the wings. Between runs she sat on a crate at Kettle Falls drinking coffee that tasted like scorched earth, and Cass dropped onto the crate beside her, smelling of hydraulic fluid and rain.

"Your landings are getting sloppy," Cass said.

"Your lead flying is getting showy."

"My lead flying is getting *efficient*. There's a difference." Cass looked out at the range, where the seam was visibly closing, cloud spilling over the notch like water over a weir. "Two more runs each. Maybe three if we're liars about our fuel reserves."

"We're liars about our fuel reserves."

"Good." Cass turned her coffee cup slowly in her hands. "Do you know what I keep thinking? Nine years I raced you. Every contract, every charter, every stupid trophy at the Escarpment fair. And I never once beat you at the thing that mattered."

"What thing?"

"This." She gestured at the mountains, at the planes, at the line of evacuees waiting in the rain. "Flying scared people through weather that wants them dead. You've been doing it your whole life. You just didn't know it."

"Cass—"

"Don't get sentimental, we've got a load to load." But she bumped Odile's shoulder with her own, hard, the way pilots do instead of saying things, and went to check her tie-downs.

* * *

At Rivergate, the water was rising.

Wren stood at the edge of the strip with a surveyor's stake in hand, driving it into the mud at the point where the river had lapped at dawn, and watched the water reach it again by mid-afternoon. Six inches in eight hours. The strip's lower third would be soft by tomorrow morning. Unusable by tomorrow night.

"Drainage ditch on the east side," said Amos Ferro, the town councilman who'd once blamed Dunmore's name for everything and now wouldn't leave Wren's side. "If we cut it deep enough, we can bleed the runoff past the runway end."

"How many shovels have you got?"

"All of them. Every shovel in Rivergate is going to be in that ditch within the hour."

And they were. Wren had never seen anything like it — a town that had spent nine years marinating in grievance, digging together in the rain, old feuds shoulder to shoulder in the muck. The woman whose father had died in the disaster shoveled beside the man who'd blamed him for flying that day. Nobody spoke about it. The ditch spoke for them.

Wren ran the ground like a conductor runs an orchestra that is also on fire. Fuel drums moved uphill. Crates staged by weight, lightest last. The sick and elderly queued under tarps with numbered tags Mara had written, and volunteers fed them soup from the church kitchen, and a boy of maybe ten appointed himself Wren's runner and message system and refused all payment except the chance to sit in the cockpit for one minute between loads.

"One minute," Wren said. "Engine cold. Hands in your lap."

"Yes sir. One minute." The boy's grin could have powered the radio.

But beneath the grace, Wren watched the sky and did the math nobody said aloud. Two planes. Forty-odd souls per run. Three hours round trip in good conditions, longer as the weather built. The seam in the escarpment was closing like a fist. And the water kept rising, patient as a creditor.

Somewhere past midnight, soaked through, Wren stood in the fuel drum shelter and let the exhaustion come, and with it the fear, and with it — steady underneath, like bedrock — the certainty. Odile would fly until the mountain closed or the plane quit. So Wren's job was simple: make sure neither happened before everyone was out.

A hand touched the shelter flap. Odile, back early, rain streaming off her, eyes hollow-bright.

"Load's down safe. Cass is behind me." She looked at Wren a long moment. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other mechanic." Wren reached out and pulled her under the shelter, into the smell of canvas and avgas, and held on. Just held on, for thirty seconds, while the rain hammered the roof like applause.

"The ditch is holding," Wren said into her hair. "The strip will hold tonight. Go sleep two hours. That's an order from your ground crew."

"Ground crew doesn't give me orders."

"Ground crew is the only reason you have a plane to give orders about."

Odile laughed into Wren's shoulder, and for those thirty seconds neither of them was flying, or digging, or counting, or grieving. Then the boy-runner stuck his head in — "Miss Cass is on final!" — and the world resumed, and they stepped apart, and it was fine. It was more than fine. It was fuel.

* * *

The fourth run, the seam fought them.

Odile went in behind Cass at dusk, both planes heavy, the notch half-swallowed in cloud. Halfway through, the updraft that had anchored the corridor's western wall simply stopped — the monsoon swallowing the thermal whole — and the seam began to close ahead of her like a book shutting.

"Cass. Wall's dying."

"I see it. Slide right ten degrees, follow my smoke." Cass's plane wobbled ahead, trailing a thread of orange flare-smoke through the murk, and Odile followed it on instruments and faith and Theo's card — *trust the river sound* — ears straining under the engine noise for water she couldn't see.

She found it. Faint. Below. Left.

She banked toward it and the corridor's last open throat slid past her wingtip close enough to count the pines on the ridge, and then they were through, both planes, dropping into the calm green valley as full dark fell behind them and the escarpment disappeared into the storm's first real advance.

On the ground at Kettle Falls, Cass climbed down and stood in the rain looking back at the wall of cloud where the pass had been.

"That's it for today," she said quietly. "Maybe forever, if that keeps building."

"Two hundred and sixty out," Odile said. "Mara's list says a hundred and forty remain."

Cass didn't answer. She didn't have to. Both of them stood in the rain doing the same arithmetic, and the arithmetic did not work, and somewhere south the monsoon rolled inland like a verdict.

Then Odile remembered the map. Her father's survey. The route marked in red ink along the eastern face — the impossible one, the one no one flew, the one Theo had died proving.

Not the corridor. The other route.

"Cass," she said slowly. "How do you feel about a route that isn't a route yet?"

Cass turned. Rain ran off her chin. In the dark, her smile was slow and sharp and entirely fearless.

"Odile Dunmore," she said, "I have been waiting nine years for you to ask me that."
