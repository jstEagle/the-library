## 36. The Evidence Flight

The last of Rivergate's people had crossed the escarpment at dawn, and by nine o'clock the valley behind her was empty of everything but water.

Odile stood on the strip at Rivergate with the evidence case open on the tailgate of Wren's truck, checking it for the fourth time. Survey data in oilcloth. Mine records—blasting schedules, payroll ledgers, the Halvard Group's own initials stamped into invoices like a signature on a confession. Theo's logbook, its spine cracked, its final pages soft as cloth from handling. And beneath all of it, wrapped in a flight jacket that still smelled faintly of engine oil and forty years, her father's original survey with the corridor drawn in his steady hand.

"You've checked it four times," Wren said. Her arm was in a sling, her ribs strapped, her face still carrying the yellow-green bloom of the bruise from Kettle Falls. She'd flown north through weather that should have killed her to stand here now, half-broke and furious and refusing to sit down. "It hasn't changed since the third time."

"Paper is patient," Odile said. "That's what scares me about it. It waits."

Wren looked out at the river, which had come up over its banks in the night and was licking at the low pasture where the town's cattle used to graze. The monsoon had made landfall two days early. The radio at the strip had been reporting nothing but gray static and the occasional frantic voice from the coast, and every hour they lost, Halvard's lawyers gained ground in the capital.

"Cass says the window closes tomorrow afternoon," Wren said. "After that, nobody flies anything anywhere until spring. If those documents don't land before then—"

"They'll land." Odile closed the case and latched it. "I'm not flying them through the storm, Wren. I'm flying them ahead of it."

"That's a fine distinction you're drawing there at six in the morning."

"It's the only distinction I have."

* * *

Cass met them at the fuel drums, already dressed for altitude: fleece under the flight suit, thermos of black coffee, the hard bright look she got when she'd done her arithmetic and didn't like the answer but trusted it anyway.

"Weather's coming down the pass from the northwest at thirty knots," she said without greeting. "Front edge will be over the escarpment by fourteen hundred, maybe earlier. The corridor Theo mapped runs northeast of the main pass—it'll get hit last, but it'll get hit. You want my estimate?"

"No."

"Four hours of margin. Maybe five if the front stalls on the high country, which it won't, because fronts never stall when you need them to." Cass handed her the thermos. "Halvard's counsel filed their injunction yesterday afternoon. My contact in the capital says the hearing's set for Friday morning. Today is Wednesday."

"So we land tonight or we don't land at all."

"We land tonight." Cass said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered, and for a moment Odile saw the girl she'd known at flight school—the one who'd raced her down the valley in a borrowed Cub, both of them laughing into the headset, neither of them knowing yet what the mountains kept. "One more thing. There's a man at the capital field who works for Halvard. Name of Grell. He'll meet your plane if he can. He'll be polite. He'll offer to help you tie down. He will try to take that case out of your hands with a smile, and if that fails he'll try something less smiling. Don't let him touch it."

"What do I do if he doesn't take no?"

Cass smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile at all. "You're landing at the provincial airfield, Odile. There are forty people on that ramp whose entire job is watching airplanes. Land loud. Land in front of everyone. Make yourself impossible to disappear." She paused. "You spent nine years getting good at being invisible. Consider this your graduation exercise."

* * *

Wren walked her through the preflight even though Odile could have done it asleep, because that was how Wren loved things—by inspection, by torque wrench, by the discipline of hands on metal. She checked the tie-rods twice herself, the memory of the filed one from Kettle Falls sitting in both of them like a stone neither would name. She checked the hydraulics, the fuel lines, the pitot heat. She stood on the wheel strut and pressed her palm flat against the cowling as if taking the airplane's pulse.

"She's sound," Wren said finally. "Sounder than she's ever been. I went through her top to bottom after the second sabotage. Nothing on this aircraft is going to fail you." A beat. "Nothing on this aircraft."

"I know."

"And the pilot?"

"The pilot has been failing for nine years," Odile said. "She's about to stop."

Wren laughed—a short, cracked thing—and then pressed her forehead against Odile's shoulder, careful of the sling, careful of the ribs, careful the way Wren was careful with everything she couldn't fix. "Come back," she said into the fabric of the flight suit. "I know duty's pointing north. But so am I. Both directions at once. That's the whole trick of it, isn't it? They're finally the same direction."

Odile held her there on the wet ramp while the river rose and the mountain waited, and understood that Wren had just said aloud the thing Odile had been circling for weeks: that love and duty were not two headings between which a pilot must choose, but one heading read off two instruments. She kissed the top of Wren's head, tasting rain.

"Hold the strip," she said.

"Hold the sky," Wren answered, and stepped back.

* * *

The corridor opened below her at eleven hundred hours like a sentence finishing itself.

Theo's route ran up a tributary valley no surveyor had bothered naming, threading between two faces of the escarpment that the main charts marked as impassable—and they were impassable, in the ordinary way of reading terrain, unless you flew them exactly as a dead man had drawn them: a quarter-mile off the east wall to stay out of the rotor, climbing at a precise angle through the notch where the rock funneled the wind instead of fighting it, then a hard left over the saddle with the cairn her father had built the summer Odile was twelve. Every waypoint was a handprint. Every turn was Theo saying *here, here, fly here.*

She talked to him the whole way up. She hadn't planned to; it simply happened, the way grief happens, unbidden and necessary.

"Not bad, little brother. Your line holds. Your sister's holding it."

The downdraft came off the west face at the notch exactly as his notes promised—a sharp exhale of cold air that dropped them two hundred feet and then gave them back three hundred in the climb, the mountain breathing around the airplane instead of against it. Odile rode it with her hands light on the yoke, feeling for the first time in nine years that the escarpment was not an enemy but a language, and that Theo had been fluent, and that he had died mid-sentence trying to teach it to the rest of them.

At the saddle she banked left over the cairn. Below, in the green throat of the valley, the river that had taken him ran silver and indifferent. She did not look long. Looking long was for the return trip, if there was one, and for the ceremony afterward, and for the rest of her life.

Then the wall of weather showed itself on the northern horizon, and there was no more time for anything but flying.

* * *

The front did not stall. Of course it didn't.

She hit its leading edge ninety minutes out from the capital, and the world went from gray to white to a churning dark that had no up in it. Rain came across the windscreen sideways, then in sheets, then not as rain at all but as the sky itself relocating. Lightning walked along the ridge to her right, close enough to strobe the cockpit. The old cargo plane bucked and groaned, and Odile flew it by instruments and by faith—by the numbers on the gauges, by the hum of an engine Wren had rebuilt with her own hands, by the weight of the case lashed into the seat beside her like a passenger.

She thought, absurdly, of her father's ledger. All those years reading it as a book of debts, when it had been a map all along. *Everything her father left her is a debt*—she'd written that sentence in her own heart once, and now she unwrote it. Not a debt. An inheritance. The route. The oath, broken rightly. The brother, restored. The woman on the ramp at Rivergate with her arm in a sling, pointing both directions at once.

The turbulence hammered her through the worst cell for twenty minutes that felt like a season. At one point the aircraft dropped so hard the harness bit her shoulders and the case strained its lashings, and Odile put her free hand flat on the oilcloth wrapping and said, out loud, into the roar:

"Not today. None of you. Not today."

Whether she meant the storm, the dead, or the men who had caused all of it, she didn't specify. It applied to all of them equally.

Then the cell was behind her and the air smoothed out like water gone still, and ahead, impossibly, absurdly, the sun was breaking through the storm's trailing edge over the plains, gilding the flooded fields gold, and the provincial capital sat on its river bend glittering like something newly minted.

Her eyes were wet. She let them be. Nobody at eleven thousand feet could see, and anyway, she was done hiding things.

* * *

The capital airfield came up out of the gold at seventeen minutes past six, tower lights winking through the last ragged cloud. Odile called in early, loud, and by the book—full callsign, full declaration, cargo manifest read out over the frequency in a voice pitched to carry: *survey documents, mine records, evidentiary materials for provincial review.* Let it go out over the airwaves. Let it live in a dozen logbooks and a dozen memories before her wheels touched pavement.

Grell was on the ramp. Of course he was.

He was exactly as Cass had described: silver-templed, well-tailored, standing beside a black sedan with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never once been told no in a way that stuck. He raised a hand as she shut down, smiling, already walking.

"Ms. Dunmore. What an extraordinary flight—we've been following you on the weather feeds. The Halvard Group wants you to know how deeply we admire what you've done for those people in the valley. Truly heroic. Now—" his eyes went, smooth as a pickpocket's fingers, to the case— "if I can help you with your cargo, our legal team can have these documents properly filed first thing—"

"Properly filed," Odile repeated. She climbed down, planted herself between him and the aircraft, and did not move. Around them the evening ramp was alive—fuel trucks, a regional airliner boarding, ground crew, two constables by the terminal doors drinking coffee. She'd landed loud. She'd landed in front of everyone. "Filed where, Mr. Grell?"

"The provincial registry, naturally—"

"Because the provincial registry is where I'm headed. Right now. Tonight. With witnesses." She lifted the case out of the plane and held it against her hip like a child. "These are sworn evidentiary materials concerning the deaths of forty-one people at Rivergate nine years ago, Mr. Grell, including my brother. They concern illegal blasting above the escarpment, falsified surveys, and a land-buying operation conducted by your employers on ground they helped make cheap. The hearing is Friday morning. I intend to be there, and so does everything in this case. So you can tell the Halvard Group—" she stepped closer, and watched, with a satisfaction she would savor for years, the smile flicker— "that their deniable pilot declined the role."

For a moment Grell said nothing. The floodlights buzzed. Somewhere a jet whined toward the runway.

"You should understand," he said quietly, "what you're doing to yourself."

"I understand perfectly." She turned and walked toward the terminal, toward the lights, toward the registry, toward Friday. "I'm making myself visible. It turns out I'm very good at it."

Behind her, the storm she'd beaten was closing over the escarpment like a door. Ahead of her, under the floodlights, a clerk in the records office was just locking up for the night—and stopped, keys in hand, when he saw a mud-spattered pilot striding across the wet tarmac carrying a case like it was the most important cargo ever flown, which, as far as Odile Dunmore was concerned, it was.

"Sir," she called. "Is there a notary in this building? I have evidence, and I'm told the hearing starts Friday morning."

The clerk looked at the case. He looked at her face. Whatever he saw there, he unlocked the door.

* * *

By midnight the documents were logged, copied, witnessed, and sealed—three sets, in three places, because Odile had learned from men like Halvard that paper is patient and so must she be. She stood at the terminal window afterward with bad coffee going cold in her hand, watching lightning flicker over the western hills where the escarpment slept under the monsoon, and thought of Wren holding the strip, of Cass somewhere south with the last loads, of Mara's fever ward and the cairn on the saddle and the river running silver through Theo's valley.

Friday morning, the reckoning. Justice arriving not as lightning but as paperwork.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. Wren's voice, thin through the bad connection, half-swallowed by static and wind:

*"Odile—Odile, are you there? The hearing—there's been a development. Halvard's lawyers moved first. You need to hear this before you walk into that room."*

Odile set down the coffee and gripped the phone with both hands.

"Tell me," she said. "Tell me everything."
