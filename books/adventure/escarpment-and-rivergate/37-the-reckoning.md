## 37. The Reckoning

The capital smelled of rain and diesel and old stone, and Odile walked into the Provincial Lands Registry at nine minutes past eight in the morning with a canvas flight bag that weighed less than her father's ledger had and meant infinitely more.

She had not slept. The crossing from Rivergate had taken eleven hours against the monsoon's trailing edge—Cass on her wing for the first three, until the weather split them and Odile flew the last stretch alone through cloud that moved like something alive. She had landed at the capital's municipal field with her hands still trembling on the yoke, taxied to the tie-downs, and sat in the pilot's seat for a full ten minutes doing nothing but breathing, listening to the engine tick itself cool, feeling the airframe settle around her like an animal coming off a long run.

In the bag: Theo's flight log. Her father's original escarpment survey with its marked corridor and its note in Theo's handwriting. The mine records Cass had photographed by lantern light in the foreman's shack above the pass—the blasting schedules, the chemical manifests, the payroll sheets bearing names that would make the morning papers sing. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing Wren had bled for: the Halvard Group's internal correspondence, lifted from the staging office before the fire took everything else, showing nine years of land purchases in the Rivergate valley at prices no honest market could explain.

She had rehearsed nothing. That was the point. Rehearsal was what you did when you wanted to be believed; she only wanted to be heard.

* * *

The clerk at the registry desk was a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular patience of someone who processed grievances all day and believed almost none of them. He looked up at Odile—at the flight jacket, the wind-cracked lips, the canvas bag—and his expression performed the small, tired arithmetic of a man estimating how much of his morning she intended to consume.

"I need to file a complaint," Odile said. "And I need it filed today."

"Complaints are taken in order of—"

"It concerns an unlicensed mining operation above the town of Rivergate. It concerns falsified survey records held by this office. It concerns the deaths of—" Her voice caught, and she let it, deliberately, because the catch was true and true things were the only currency she had left. "It concerns the deaths of eleven people nine years ago, including my brother."

The clerk's pen stopped moving.

Behind him, through the glass partition, Odile could see the reading room where the public records lived—the same room where, she now knew, a forged survey had been quietly substituted for her father's real one, the corridor through the eastern shoulder erased, the instability of the upper pass noted and then un-noted. Paperwork. All of it paperwork. The disaster had been rock and water and gravity, yes—but the permission for it, the cover for it, the cheap buying of a grieving valley afterward, had been done here, in rooms like this one, by men who never got their boots muddy.

The clerk picked up a different form.

* * *

By noon there were four of them in a conference room on the third floor: Odile; a lands investigator named Okafor who had the build of a former rugby player and the eyes of an accountant; a junior minister's aide who kept glancing at the window as though the storm might deliver further instructions; and Cass, who had arrived two hours behind Odile, flying through weather that grounded every other aircraft in the province, because of course she had.

Okafor laid the documents out in rows on the long table, and Odile watched him work the way she watched Wren work an engine—with the recognition of one craftsman studying another. He read the blasting schedules first. Then the manifests. Then he put on a pair of reading glasses and went back to the beginning, slower.

"This is the original survey?" he asked, tapping her father's map.

"That's Dunmore's hand," Odile said. "You can verify it against his other filings. He surveyed half the northern ranges."

"And this is what your office holds." He slid across a photocopy of the registered version. The differences were surgical. A contour line shifted. A note about seasonal instability removed. The eastern corridor—Theo's corridor—simply gone, as if the mountain had never offered it.

Odile nodded. "Someone amended it after the crash. After everyone who could contradict it was dead or gone."

"After," Okafor repeated softly. He made a note. "Ms. Merrow. Your records indicate you were retained by a shell company called Northlight Logistics to fly resupply into Rivergate. Do you know who owned Northlight?"

"I found out last week." Cass's voice was flat and even, the voice she used in downdrafts. "Northlight is Halvard. Has been since incorporation. They paid me to force a contract onto a crew whose senior pilot was the sister of the man whose death made the valley cheap. They wanted the route flown by someone with a reason to fail—or a reason to stay quiet. Preferably both."

The aide by the window stopped looking at the weather.

"And instead," Okafor said, "you flew it anyway, and brought me this."

"Instead," Cass said, "I flew it anyway, and brought you this."

* * *

It did not happen the way Odile had always imagined reckonings happening. There was no courtroom, no dramatic unmasking, no moment when a villain's face went white before a hushed gallery. What happened instead was this: Okafor spent four days making telephone calls. The junior minister's aide spent four days carrying folders between offices with increasing urgency. On the fifth day, a provincial judge signed an emergency order suspending all Halvard land transactions in the Rivergate district pending review, and on the sixth day, the mining license—which turned out to have been issued under a classification meant for gravel extraction, a clerical fiction so audacious it was almost beautiful—was revoked outright.

On the seventh day, the papers came out.

Odile bought a copy from a kiosk outside the municipal field, standing in drizzle with the newsprint going soft in her hands before she'd finished the front page. THE MINE ABOVE RIVERGATE: NINE YEARS OF LIES IN THREE SIGNATURES. Below the fold, a photograph of the escarpment she knew better than her own face, and beneath that, in smaller type, the names of the dead—including THEODORE DUNMORE, PILOT, 27.

Twenty-seven. He had been twenty-seven. She had carried the number for nine years and seeing it in print still knocked something loose in her chest.

"You're dripping on the article," Cass said, appearing at her elbow with two coffees and handing one over.

"They voided the titles." Odile's voice sounded strange to her own ears—hollowed out, lighter than it had been in years. "Every parcel they bought since the crash. Voided. The judge called the purchase pattern 'exploitation of manufactured distress.' Manufactured distress. As if he were describing a coupon scheme."

"He was, sort of." Cass blew on her coffee. "They manufactured the distress. That's the whole architecture of it. You don't steal a valley. You break it, wait for the grief to do the pricing, and buy. It's very tidy. It's the tidiest evil I've ever been adjacent to, and I've been adjacent to some."

Odile looked at her. Nine years ago they had been rivals over contracts and landing slots and, once, badly, over a man neither of them had actually wanted. Now Cass stood in the rain beside her having flown through a monsoon to testify, and Odile understood that whatever the word for what they were now, *rival* had been retired from service.

"The investigator says there will be charges," Odile said. "Criminal ones. It'll take years."

"It always takes years." Cass shrugged. "But here's what I keep thinking about. Somewhere in the Halvard offices right now, there are lawyers drafting statements, executives shredding things too late, and a whole floor of people realizing that the paper trail didn't die in the fire at Kettle Falls because a mechanic with a broken collarbone had already mailed copies to three different agencies." She smiled, thin and sharp. "Paperwork, Odile. It cuts both ways. They built the whole machine out of it. We just fed it back through."

* * *

Wren's letter arrived on the ninth day, carried by the mail plane that also brought spare parts and, folded inside the envelope, a page torn from a maintenance log in Wren's careful block capitals:

*Engine holding. Strip holding. Town holding. Mara says the fever's broken—last new case six days ago. People are planting late gardens like lunatics, saying the land is theirs again. Come home when the sky lets you. Not before. But come.*

*P.S. Your side of the hangar is exactly as you left it. I have dusted nothing. Some things should be evidence.*

Odile read it four times on the bench outside the field office, then folded it along its existing creases and put it in the jacket pocket over her heart, which was where she had decided such documents belonged.

Home. Wren had written *come home*, and had meant Escarpment, and had meant, unmistakably, more than Escarpment. Odile found that the word did not frighten her the way it once had. Invisibility had been her art for nine years—the sealed cockpit door, the surname she gave as just *Dunmore* without history attached, the careful practice of belonging nowhere enough to owe nowhere anything. But invisibility had died at the airstrip in Rivergate, publicly, in front of the whole town, the day she refused Halvard's offer. What grew in its place felt less like exposure than like weight-bearing. Like a strut finally installed where one had always been missing.

There were still depositions to give. Okafor had a list of questions long enough to require its own fuel stop, and the provincial attorney's office wanted her account of the evacuation flights entered into the record load by load, decision by decision—the triage arithmetic she still dreamed about, the forty-one people who crossed in Theo's corridor while the mountain came down behind them. She would give all of it. She owed the record her precision, because somewhere in that record, someday, a lawyer for the dead would find the sentence that mattered.

But the sky had cleared for the first time in a week, and the mail plane's pilot—a round, cheerful woman named Bex whom Odile had known since flight school—was loading her bags and jerking a thumb at the second seat.

"Heard you're heading north eventually," Bex said. "Weather window's open till Thursday. After that, monsoon round two, and nobody flies anything anywhere."

Odile looked at the mountains to the north, faint and blue beyond the field's fence line. Somewhere past them, a valley was replanting its gardens on land that belonged to it again. Somewhere past them, a woman with a healing collarbone was pointedly not dusting her side of a hangar.

"Thursday," Odile said, and climbed in.

* * *

The deposition took three days. She gave it in a room with bad lighting and a stenographer whose fingers never seemed to touch the keys and yet never missed a word. When it was done, Okafor walked her out through the registry's marble corridors, past the reading room where the forged survey had lived its quiet life among honest documents, and paused at the doors.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the amendment to your father's survey was traced to a specific filing date. Three weeks after the crash. Someone walked in here while the valley was still burying its dead and changed the mountain on paper." He shook his head slowly. "I've worked fraud for eleven years. I've never seen anything quite so cold."

"My brother saw the real mountain," Odile said. "That's why he's dead. He flew the truth of it, and the truth of it was unstable because of what they were doing above the pass."

"Then the record will say so." Okafor held the door for her. "Names, dates, signatures. It's not lightning, Ms. Dunmore. It's better. Lightning fades. This goes in the archive."

Outside, the capital glittered under a rinsed sky. Odile stood on the steps a moment, breathing air that tasted of rain and distance, and thought about archives. Her father had kept a ledger with a route blacked out. Theo had kept a log with a corridor drawn in. Halvard had kept files with a valley priced in. Everyone, it turned out, wrote things down. The difference—the only difference that had ever mattered—was what you were willing to sign your name beside.

Her name was beside the truth now. It was in depositions and affidavits and a newspaper photograph caption, and it would be in the judgment when it came, however many years the machinery of justice required to grind its slow and unglamorous way forward. She had spent nine years keeping her name small. There was no putting it back.

She found she didn't want to.

* * *

Thursday dawned clear and cold, the kind of flying day her father would have called *a gift you don't refuse*. Odile loaded the mail plane's second seat with her bag, a crate of medicines Mara had radioed a list for, and a bundle of newspapers she had bought twelve copies of for reasons she couldn't fully articulate until she was folding them into the crate: the people of Rivergate should see their own names in print beside the word *vindicated*. They had waited nine years for anyone to write it down.

Bex swung the prop, and the engine caught on the first blade, smooth as a held breath released.

"Rivergate strip's reporting crosswind gusting eighteen," Bex shouted over the noise. "Your mechanic says, quote, 'tell her if she floats the landing I'm charging her for tires.'"

Odile laughed—out loud, alone in the cockpit, the sound startling her with its own ease—and pushed the throttle forward.

The capital fell away beneath her, then the lowlands, then the first gray shoulders of the range. Ahead, the escarpment stood in the morning light with its scars visible and its passes open, and Odile flew toward it not as a woman returning to a wound but as a courier carrying the end of something: the last leg of a delivery nine years overdue. In the crate behind her seat lay the newspapers. In the pocket over her heart lay Wren's letter. And somewhere below the eastern shoulder, in the corridor only two living pilots had ever crossed, the mountain that had taken her brother was about to learn what her family did with inheritances.

They flew them home.
