## 39. Deeds and Debts

The deed arrived on a Tuesday, which Wren Adeyemi thought was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day nothing ever happened at Escarpment—the day the fog sat longest on the strip and the coffee went stale in the pot and the whole town seemed to hold its breath between one week and the next. A Tuesday was a day built for paperwork. And this was, at last, paperwork worth the waiting.

It came by mail plane, of all things—a small yellow Cub out of Kettle Falls, bouncing down the gravel with its tires throwing spray from last night's rain. The pilot handed the envelope over with a grin and no idea what he was carrying. Wren signed for it because nobody else was around, and then stood in the middle of the empty strip holding it, feeling the weight of a single sheet of paper that had taken nine years, two crashes' worth of grief, one burned engine, one cracked rib, and an entire mountain's worth of stubbornness to arrive.

*Escarpment Airstrip — Title Free and Clear.* The Halvard Group's liens, voided along with everything else the company had touched, struck through in the provincial registrar's neat bureaucratic hand. The debts of Aldous Dunmore, deceased, discharged. The collateral released.

Wren read it three times. Then walked to the hangar, where Odile was underneath the Cessna with her legs sticking out into the grey morning light, torque wrench in hand, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of torn chart.

"Odile."

A grunt from under the aircraft.

"The strip's ours."

The wrench stopped. There was a long silence, the kind Odile Dunmore specialized in, and then she rolled out on the creeper and lay there looking up at the rafters, blinking, her face doing something complicated that Wren had learned over the years to translate: joy arriving through a customs checkpoint, inspected, delayed, but ultimately admitted.

"Ours," she said, testing the word.

"Free and clear. Every lien dead. Your father's debts—" Wren stopped, corrected. "Your father's *debt*. Singular. It was all one debt, dressed up as many. And it's gone."

Odile sat up slowly. She took the paper and held it the way she held charts in turbulence—two hands, full attention—and for a moment Wren thought she might cry, which would have been a first, and then she didn't, which was more usual, and instead she said, quietly:

"He'd have hated how it happened. All of it. The town, the scandal, his name dragged through the inquiry." Her thumb moved across the strikethrough lines. "But he'd have liked this part. The part where the ledger balances."

"Ledgers don't balance," Wren said. "People do. Eventually. If they're lucky."

Odile looked up then, and there it was—that look, the one from the bedside, the one from the night flight, the one Wren had spent years not naming and then, finally, in a hospital room smelling of antiseptic and fear, had named aloud and found the sky didn't fall. "You're getting philosophical," Odile said. "That's my job. You're supposed to be telling me my torque values are wrong."

"Your torque values are always wrong. That's why I love you." Wren crouched, took the wrench from her hand, set it down. "Come inside. There's coffee. There are people. Mara flew in with the mail plane—she wants to see the strip that started all of it. Cass is arguing with the fuel truck again. The crew's all here, Odile. First time since before the monsoon. Come be among them."

"They'll want to talk about the future," Odile said, with the tone of a woman who had recently learned that futures could be survived but still found them exhausting.

"Yes," Wren said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The hangar had been cleaned for the occasion, which meant the oil stains were still there but the charts on the walls had been straightened, and someone—Wren suspected old Pieter, the ground chief, who had worked the strip since before Odile was born—had put fresh bunting up over the tool crib, red and green, the colors of the valley. The whole crew was there: Pieter and his two line boys; Sefa from dispatch, who had kept the radio log through the entire crisis and whose handwriting now filled three notebooks of testimony; the young pilots, Tomas and Ilse, who had flown shuttle runs until their hands shook; Mara Voss, Rivergate's nurse, sitting on an upturned crate with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking, looking around the hangar like a woman memorizing a place she'd only ever heard about in bad news.

And Cass Merrow, of course, standing by the fuel truck with her arms crossed, mid-argument, because some things were eternal.

"—I'm telling you, the pump calibration is off by four percent, I've flown enough hours to feel it in the yoke—"

"You've flown enough hours to feel your own ego in the yoke," Pieter said placidly, which got a laugh, because everyone knew by now that Cass and Pieter had invented a game of insult that neither would ever concede.

Wren watched Odile walk into that room—watched her hesitate at the threshold, one half-second, the old reflex, the woman who had made herself a corridor through other people's lives rather than a room in any of them—and then watched the hesitation die. Odile came all the way in. Mara saw her first and stood up, and whatever passed between the nurse and the pilot was quiet and old and not for the rest of them, a hand on a forearm, a nod, something settled that had needed settling since a fever ward in October.

Then Pieter raised his mug and said, "To the deed," and the hangar roared, and Wren stood at the edge of it with coffee going cold in hand and thought: *this is what belonging looks like when it stops being accidental.*

It had never been accidental for Wren. That was the difference between them, the thing Wren had understood about Odile somewhere over the escarpment in whiteout conditions with both engines coughing: Wren had chosen Escarpment at nineteen, walking off a freight plane with a duffel bag and no references, and had chosen it every day since—every rebuilt engine, every frozen dawn, every year of watching a brilliant guarded pilot taxi past without quite looking back. Belonging, for Wren, had always been a decision renewed daily, like preflight checks. For Odile it had been an inheritance she'd tried to refuse. Now the inheritance was rewritten—strikes through the liens, the oath, the exile—and Wren intended to make sure she signed it.

* * *

Cass waited until the crowd thinned, until it was just the core of them—Odile, Wren, Cass, and later Pieter drifting over with the gravity of a man who already knew what was coming—standing by the open hangar doors in the late light, the strip running gold and wet toward the tree line.

"I sold my plane," Cass said.

Wren blinked. "You *what*?"

"The Merrow Air single. Sold it to cover my share." Cass said it flatly, the way she said everything, but Wren caught the flicker underneath—the cost of it, a woman selling the thing she'd built with her own hands and ten thousand hours. "The Halvard money's frozen pending the inquiry anyway, so it's not like I could touch it. So I liquidated. Which means—" she pulled a folded paper from her jacket, and Wren recognized the format instantly, because pilots thought in these formats, "—I'm buying in. A third share. Odile's got the strip through her father's estate, you've got sweat equity and the maintenance contract, and I've got capital and a second type rating. Three-way partnership. Equal."

Silence. Rain dripped off the hangar roof. Somewhere down the strip a raven commented on the proceedings.

"You hate partnerships," Odile said.

"I hate *your* partnerships," Cass said. "This one has me in it."

"And the route?" Wren asked, because there was always a route with Cass, always a line drawn on a map that explained everything else. "Partnerships need revenue. What are we flying?"

Cass unfolded the paper. It was a schedule—hand-drawn, provisional, the margins dense with figures in her cramped pilot's shorthand. And down the left column, repeated weekly, monthly, seasonal, written like a vow:

*Rivergate.*

"Twice a month, minimum," Cass said. "Medicine, mail, parts, produce going out. Year-round if the weather allows, and after this winter we'll know better than anyone alive what the weather allows. Theo's corridor opens a supply line nobody's had in nine years. There's a town of four hundred people up there who learned this winter exactly what it costs to be forgotten, and there are three pilots in this valley who know the way in better than the survey office does." She looked at Odile. "I didn't buy your father's debt to ruin you. I bought it because someone paid me to, and I stayed in the trap to find out who. But somewhere over Kettle Falls it stopped being a trap and started being the best flying of my life. I'm not giving that up. I'm asking to keep it."

"You could fly it alone," Odile said. "You proved you can."

"I could," Cass agreed. "Alone is what got four hundred people snowed in and a good man's name buried under a landslide. I'm done proving things alone." She held out the paper. "Well? Do we have a company or do I have to go buy another airstrip and make you compete?"

Odile took the paper. Read it. Wren watched her read it—watched the pause at the top of the column, at the word *Rivergate*, where nine years ago there would have been flinching, and found none. Just a woman reading a flight plan.

"Pieter," Odile said, without looking up. "You've worked here thirty years. Any objection to a Merrow on the letterhead?"

"Objection?" Pieter grinned through his beard. "Girl, I've been waiting nine years for someone to argue with Wren about engine hours besides me. Welcome aboard, Miss Merrow."

"Then we have a company," Odile said. She dug in her pocket, produced a pen—the same pen, Wren realized, that had signed the medevac logs for nine years, the invisible woman's pen—and wrote her name on the partnership line. Handed it to Wren.

Wren looked at the line. Looked at Odile. "Sweat equity," Wren said. "You sure? I can match Cass's capital if I mortgage the cottage."

"Your hands rebuilt every engine that brought those people home," Odile said. "That's capital. Sign it."

So Wren signed. And Cass signed, with a flourish that was pure provocation, and then stood there awkwardly for a moment, three names on a page, before saying gruffly, "Someone should name the damn thing," and Pieter suggested *Dunmore-Merrow-Adeyemi Air Transport* and everyone agreed it was too long and Cass suggested *Merrow Aviation* and everyone ignored her, and it was Odile who said, quietly, looking out the hangar doors at the strip, the escarpment beyond it blue and clean-washed in the rain-light:

"*Escarpment and Rivergate.*"

Nobody argued. Some names you know when you hear them, the way you know a runway's aligned before the numbers confirm it.

* * *

Evening came down soft. The crew dispersed to the hotel and the cottages, Mara to the spare room Wren kept for visiting medics, Cass to the hotel with a bottle of something celebratory and an open invitation to argue about fuel policy tomorrow. The bunting came down—or most of it; Pieter left one strand up over the door and dared anyone to comment.

Wren and Odile walked the strip at dusk, because Odile always walked the strip at dusk, and because Wren had learned that the walk was not a ritual of solitude anymore but an invitation. The gravel was dark with rain. The mountains stood up against the last light, the pass visible as a pale notch, Theo's corridor somewhere inside it, mapped in a dead boy's careful handwriting and flown now by the living.

"My father used to walk this strip every evening," Odile said. "I used to think he was checking the surface. Wind socks, drainage, the gravel. Pilot things." She kicked a stone gently off the centerline. "He wasn't. He was saying goodnight to it. To the people it connected him to. I thought inheritance meant the debts and the oath and the silences. Turns out it was this. A man saying goodnight to his work because his work was his people."

"And now you say goodnight to it too," Wren said.

"Now I say goodnight to it too." Odile stopped at the midpoint of the strip, where the numbers were painted, 09 faded but legible, and turned. In the half-dark her face was open in a way it had cost nine years to build. "Wren. When we incorporate—properly, with lawyers and letterhead and all the boring machinery—put my name and yours adjacent. On everything. Not just the business."

Wren felt something in her chest come loose, something that had been braced since a hospital bed in November. "Adjacent," Wren said. "Like rivets."

"Like rivets," Odile agreed, and almost smiled. "Structural. Load-bearing. Impossible to remove without compromising the whole airframe."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said and I'm going to have it engraved on the hangar wall."

"Don't you dare."

"I absolutely dare."

They stood there while the light went out of the valley, and the first star came up over the escarpment, and far to the north—invisible, forty minutes' flying time through a corridor only three people in the world had ever crossed—a valley town lit its windows against the coming spring, four hundred people who knew now that the sky above them belonged to them again.

"We should plan the first run," Odile said eventually. "Cass will want the northbound load finalized by morning. She's insufferable when she's right about schedules."

"She's insufferable always."

"She's family now. We're allowed to say it."

They turned back toward the lights of the hangar, and Wren laced fingers with hers, and that was when the radio in Wren's pocket crackled—the handheld, the one kept for strip duty, Sefa's voice coming through thin and urgent over the static:

"Escarpment, Escarpment, dispatch. Got an odd one on the frequency—Rivergate relay, weak signal, asking for Odile by name. Says—" static swallowed a beat— "says it's about the mine records. Says someone's been up at the Halvard site tonight, and they weren't alone. Odile, they're asking whether we still have copies of everything."

Wren felt Odile's hand tighten around hers—not with fear, Wren realized, but with the particular stillness of a pilot reading weather on the horizon.

"Tell them yes," Odile said into the dark, calm as level flight. "Tell them we kept everything. And tell them we'll be on the frequency at dawn."

She looked at Wren, and the starlight caught the edge of a smile that was equal parts promise and warning.

"Spring's coming," she said. "And apparently, so is the next fight."
