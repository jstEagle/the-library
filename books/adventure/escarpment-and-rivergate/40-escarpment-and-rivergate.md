## 40. Escarpment and Rivergate

The morning of the first run of the new season, Odile woke before the alarm and lay still in the dark, listening to the sound of the river.

It was a sound she had spent nine years not hearing. In Escarpment, the town's water came down a concrete flume behind the fuel depot, a thin industrial trickle you stopped noticing after a week. But here, in the valley, the Rivergate ran loud enough to reach the second floor of the old Dunmore house, a constant silver argument between the water and its banks, swollen with the first melt, carrying ice the size of dinner plates down toward the lowlands. She had grown up inside that sound. She had spent nine years forgetting it. And now it was the last thing she heard at night and the first thing she heard in the morning, and she could not decide if it was a lullaby or a summons.

Beside her, Wren slept the way Wren did everything—thoroughly, with total commitment, one arm flung across the pillow where Odile's head had been. The window was open a hand's width, because Wren insisted on sleeping with air even in a valley that still smelled of woodsmoke and thaw, and through the gap came the river and, beneath it, fainter, the sound of the airstrip waking up. A generator. Someone's boots on gravel. The particular cough of old Halvorsen's truck, which no mechanic in three counties had been able to cure and which Halvorsen refused to let anyone try.

Odile slid out of bed without waking her, dressed in the dark, and went downstairs to make coffee in a kitchen that still didn't feel like hers and was beginning to, which was its own kind of vertigo.

The house had been her father's. That was the strange arithmetic of the last year: the man had owned a house in the valley he swore off, had paid taxes on it through a lawyer in Kettle Falls, had never once slept in it as far as anyone could tell. The deed had surfaced in the final untangling of the estate, along with the survey maps, the ledger, and the letter he'd written to Odile and never sent, which she kept now in the flight bag she carried on every run, because some things you read once and some things you read until they stop being letters and start being instructions. *You will want to make yourself small*, he had written, in the cramped hand that had blacked out the Rivergate pages of his ledger. *Small is safe. But the sky is not small, and neither are you, and I am sorry it took me dying to say so.*

She had forgiven him somewhere over the escarpment, in the whiteout, with Theo's corridor open beneath her and the monsoon's edge chewing at her tail. Forgiveness, it turned out, was not a feeling. It was a heading.

The coffee was just starting to perk when the kitchen door opened and Wren came in wearing yesterday's shirt and today's expression, which was the one that meant she had already been outside once and found something.

"You're up," Wren said.

"I'm always up."

"You're up *early*," Wren said, and kissed her on the cheek on the way to the stove, an economy of motion that Odile had learned was how Wren said most of the important things. "The strip's fine. I checked. Frost heave on the north third, nothing structural. I'd still like to walk it again before we load."

"You walked it at five a.m."

"I walked it at five a.m. because I couldn't sleep, which is a different thing." Wren poured coffee, leaned against the counter, and looked at Odile over the rim of the mug with those level, patient eyes. "How did you sleep?"

"Badly," Odile admitted. "Good badly. The kind where you're not worried, you're just—" She gestured vaguely at the window, the valley, the whole green world coming apart from winter.

"Waiting," Wren said.

"Waiting."

Wren nodded as if this were a diagnosis she'd already made and found benign. "The range will still be there at eight. The town will still be here. The medicine will still be in the crates. Nothing about today requires you to be awake at four-thirty."

"Everything about today requires me to be awake at four-thirty," Odile said, and Wren laughed, and the sound of it went through Odile like warm oil through a cold engine, and she understood, not for the first time and not for the last, that this was what she had been flying away from for nine years and toward for one: a kitchen at dawn, a person who knew the difference between worry and waiting, and the smell of coffee mixing with the smell of the river.

They drank it standing up, the way pilots do, as if sitting down before a flight might use up some ration of luck.

* * *

The strip at Rivergate was not really a strip. It was a meadow that the town had cleared and graded and gravelled in the years after the disaster, when the road closed and the valley learned to live by air, and it was short, and soft at both ends in spring, and it required exactly the kind of flying that Odile's father had taught her and Theo had died refining. She walked it at six with Wren, boots crunching on frost that was already letting go, and the whole valley steamed around them, the sun clearing the eastern ridge and setting the wet meadows alight, and the escarpment stood up over the town to the north, green to the treeline, grey above it, its passes still holding their white shoulders high against the sky.

"Six weeks," Wren said, looking at it. "Maybe eight, and the upper route closes again."

"Then we fly while it's open."

"We fly while it's open," Wren agreed. "And in between we finish the hangar roof, because I am not spending another winter watching the snow come through the rafters onto the number two engine."

"You could spend it in Escarpment," Odile said. It was a joke, mostly. Wren looked at her.

"I could spend it anywhere," Wren said. "That's the whole point of what we built. That's the whole point of what *you* built." She kicked at a frost-heaved stone, flicked it off the runway line with the unconscious precision of a woman who had spent her life making things true. "Cass wants to talk schedules when she's back from the capital. She says the provincial contract's going to renew, and if it does we can afford a third plane, and if we can afford a third plane she wants to put Mara's nephew in the right seat and start training him up, because the valley should have its own pilots, not just us flying in like visiting royalty."

"Mara's nephew is seventeen."

"Mara's nephew is seventeen and has already rebuilt a chainsaw engine with a spoon and a grudge. I'm telling you, the kid's got hands."

Odile smiled. A year ago, the idea of a third plane, of training valley kids, of a permanent line between the escarpment towns and the river towns, would have sounded like someone else's dream—a bigger life than the one she'd allowed herself, the small safe invisible one. Now it just sounded like Tuesday. Somewhere in the last twelve months the horizon of what she expected from her days had quietly widened, the way a pass widens when you finally come around the corner and see that the valley beyond was there the whole time.

They walked the north third together, Wren pointing out the frost heave, Odile nodding, and neither of them said anything about the fact that they were also, both of them, looking north at the escarpment, at the corridor, at the place where Theo's plane had come apart and where, eight months ago, Odile had flown forty-one people out through weather that the books said was unflyable, following a route drawn in her brother's hand on a map that had spent nine years in a stranger's trunk.

The route was marked on the strip's operations board now, inside the hangar, in Wren's neat block capitals: THEO'S LINE — UPPER PASS — VFR ONLY — CHECK WIND AT THE SADDLE. The town had voted on the name. Odile had been out on a medevac when it happened, and had come back to find it already painted, and had stood in front of it for a long time with her cap in her hands, and no one had made a speech at her, because Rivergate was not a town that made speeches. They had just named the line after him and gone back to work, and that had been the right way to do it.

* * *

Loading took until nine. The crates came up from the clinic on old Halvorsen's truck—antibiotics, insulin, the new dental equipment the provincial grant had finally paid for, and, packed in straw at the bottom of the last crate, the thing Odile had carried on every run since winter and would carry on every run always: the replacement memorial plaque, cast in the capital, for the nineteen names that the original flood and the original silence had left half-erased on the stone by the bridge.

The town had buried its dead properly in the fall. That was the ceremony Odile still could not think about without her throat closing—the whole valley on the riverbank, the priest and the elder sharing the words between them, the nineteen names read out in full for the first time, and then her own hands, shaking, screwing Theo's name into the stone while four hundred people watched and nobody looked away and nobody comforted her, because Rivergate understood that some work you have to do yourself. Afterward, old Mrs. Calloway, who had blamed her father for nine years, had taken Odile's arm at the church hall and said, "Your daddy was a coward about this valley, girl, but he raised you honest, and honest is what counts," and Odile had said thank you, and meant it, and had cried in the truck afterward where no one could see, and then stopped hiding that she cried, because hiding was over.

Wren loaded with the ground crew—two of them now, since the contract renewed—and Odile did the weight and balance at the wing, and the numbers came out clean, and the morning was so clear it felt almost like a dare.

Cass had flown down to the capital two days ago for the contract signing and the last of the Halvard hearings, and she'd radioed last night from Kettle Falls, her voice crackling with the satisfaction of a woman who had spent a year being underestimated: *Paperwork's done. They caved on all counts. Tell the valley their land is their land again, and tell Wren if she touches my engine without a work order I'm filing a grievance.* The Halvard Group was in receivership; the mine above the pass was sealed with provincial concrete and provincial lawyers; the land titles were voided and reissued. Justice had arrived as paperwork, exactly as promised, and it had been no less satisfying for that. Odile had read the final judgment twice, slowly, the way you drink something good, and then she had put it in the flight bag next to her father's letter, because some things you keep.

"Ready when you are," Wren said, swinging up into the right seat, and Odile climbed in, and ran the checklist with her mouth while her heart did what it always did at this moment, the old lift, the old hunger, the engine catching and the whole airframe coming alive around her like something waking up.

"Rivergate traffic, Delta-Kilo, departing meadow strip, climbing to the north," she said into the radio, and the valley answered with its own call signs—Mara at the clinic, Halvorsen in his truck, the forestry lookout up on the ridge—all of them voices she knew, all of them watching her go, and she took the strip in four hundred feet and was airborne before the frost was off her wings.

* * *

The escarpment, in spring, was a different country.

In the season of the disaster it had been a wall of ice and wind, and in the monsoon it had been a wall of cloud, and Odile had known it for years only as the thing that killed her brother and the thing she refused to look at. But in May, with the snow retreating up its flanks and the meadows below it gone the green of new money and the river braiding silver through the valley floor, it was simply beautiful, and she flew north along its face with the window cracked and the smell of pine coming in, and let it be beautiful, and found that she could.

Theo's Line opened ahead of them at the saddle, the corridor her brother had mapped with the last hours of his life: the dogleg through the upper pass, the wind gap, the long glide slope down the far side to the staging meadow. It looked, from two thousand feet, like what it was—a way through. Not a miracle. Not a wound. A way through, drawn by a careful man in bad weather, and proven by his sister and his rival in worse.

"Wind at the saddle's light out of the northwest," Wren said, eyes on the instruments, and then, because it was tradition now, because they did it every crossing: "He'd have liked this morning."

"He'd have hated the dew on the wings," Odile said.

"He'd have scraped it off with his sleeve and complained about the paint."

"And then he'd have flown it at two hundred feet just to see the meltwater."

"And then he'd have flown it at two hundred feet," Wren agreed, "and Mara would have had words with him, and he'd have married her, and we'd all be miserable."

Odile laughed, and the laugh went out through the headset to whoever was listening, and she didn't mind. She crossed the saddle at the altitude Theo's log specified, felt the wind lay down exactly the way his notes said it would—*the saddle lies to you for the first mile, then it's honest*—and came down the far side into the wide green country of the north, and the crossing took eleven minutes, and she thought: *nine years I gave this mountain, and it gives me back eleven minutes.*

But that wasn't the truth, and she was done with not-truths. The mountain had never taken anything from her. Men had taken things, and weather had taken things, and her own fear—her beautiful, disciplined, well-flown fear—had taken the most of all. The mountain had only ever been the mountain. And this morning it was green to the treeline, and it let her pass, and on the far side the staging meadow was already full of people waiting for medicine and mail and the new schoolbooks, and a kid ran the length of the fence with his arms out like wings, and Odile waggled the wings for him, because you always did, because someone had done it for her once, because that was the whole inheritance, passed hand to hand at two hundred feet since the first pilot first came over the range: *the sky is open, and you can be in it.*

* * *

They made the return run at two, and were back over Rivergate as the light went long and gold, and Odile brought the plane down onto the meadow strip with the river flashing alongside and the whole town, apparently, out on the grass.

She saw it as she flared: the trucks, the crowd, the long table somebody had dragged out of the church hall, Mara in her good coat, Halvorsen's terrible truck parked at a ceremonial angle. Not an emergency. A party.

"What's this," Wren said, not really asking.

"I think," Odile said, "we're being welcomed home."

The word landed strangely and then landed true. Home. Not Escarpment, where she had a bunk and a hangar and nine years of competent loneliness; not the cockpit, which had been home the way a shell is home; but here, the valley her family had sworn off, the town that had blamed her father and forgiven her daughter, the strip that was a meadow, the river that talked all night. She had spent her whole adult life keeping one foot out the door of every room she stood in, and somewhere between the memorial and the evacuation and the winter of flying supplies through weather that should have grounded them, both feet had come down, and she had not even noticed until this exact moment, taxiing in toward a crowd, that she was not looking for the exits.

They climbed out into a wall of noise. Mara got to her first—Mara always did, the nurse's triage instinct applied to everything—and pressed a mug of something hot and terrible into her hands and said, "The plaque's up. We did it this morning. We wanted you to see it with the light on it."

The light on it. Odile set down the mug and walked to the stone by the bridge with the crowd opening before her and Wren falling in beside her, and there it was: the new bronze, all nineteen names restored in full, and beneath them, in smaller letters, the line the town had voted to add, which she had not known about until this moment and which stopped her breath:

*THEY KEPT THE VALLEY, AND THE VALLEY KEEPS THEM.*

And beneath that, newest of all, still bright from the polishing cloth: *THEO DUNMORE, WHO DREW THE WAY THROUGH.*

The river ran loud behind the stone. The escarpment stood green in the last light. Wren's hand found hers, and Odile stood in front of her brother's name in the town where he died, in the town where she had been born, and felt the last of the old sealed thing inside her—the cockpit-door thing, the invisible-woman thing, the nine-years thing—finally, quietly, come to rest.

"You know what your dad's ledger was really keeping," Wren said, after a while. It wasn't a question. Wren had read the ledger a hundred times helping her decode it.

"The route," Odile said. "He blacked it out so no one else would fly it before it was safe."

"He blacked it out so *you* would be the one to find it," Wren said. "When you were ready. That's an inheritance, Odile. That's the only kind he knew how to leave."

The crowd had drifted back to the table, the food, the fiddles somebody was tuning. The light was going amber on the water. Somewhere north, over the range, tomorrow's weather was building exactly the way spring weather builds, and there would be storms, and there would be winters, and there would always, always be the escarpment between the towns and the world—but there would also be the line through it, and the planes, and the people, and her.

Odile looked up at the sky going pink over the ridge, at the first star, at all that open country where her father and her brother and she herself had spent their lives, and she understood at last what had been left to her. Not the debt. Not the oath. Not the house, or the ledger, or the grief. The sky, and the people worth flying it for. Everything else was fuel.

"First run of the season," Wren said, squeezing her hand. "And forty-one more to go."

Odile laughed, and turned toward the light and the music and the table, and above the valley, patient and green and forgiving for once, the escarpment held the door open to the sky.

*The End.*
