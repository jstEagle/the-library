## 1. Miles, Not Years

The hail came in sideways off the prairie like it had a grudge, and Lena Marchetti drove straight into the middle of it because that was where the money was.

"Two miles," she said to herself, hands at ten and two on the wheel of a truck with a cracked windshield and a transmission that whined in third gear. "Two miles and it turns."

She'd been saying two miles for forty minutes. The supercell crawled north along US-281, dragging its wall cloud behind it like a hem, spitting stones the size of golf balls through the wheat country. Somewhere ahead of her a spotter network was calling rotation, and somewhere behind her was a Vantage Logistics invoice for eleven thousand dollars in hail-damage repairs she hadn't paid, stacked on top of a fuel contract, a radar lease, and a "recovery services fee" from the time their tow crew pulled her truck out of a creek bed in Oklahoma. The debt didn't have a number anymore so much as it had a weather pattern. It rolled in. It stayed.

The hail turned at one and a half miles. It always did. She sat on the shoulder of 281 with her wipers flogging broken glass and watched the storm walk away from her into the empty western half of the state, taking its lightning with it, and felt the particular exhaustion of a person who has chased something for a living and caught nothing for three seasons running.

Her phone buzzed against the dash mount. She almost didn't look. It was either Vantage's collections desk or her mother, and those were the only two entities that called her anymore, and she wasn't sure which conversation she dreaded more.

It was neither. It was a message routed through the chase-network boards, the kind of encrypted drop that meant someone wanted work done quietly and was willing to pay for discretion. The subject line read: SALVAGE CONTRACT — KALYET GORGE — URGENT.

Lena read it twice, parked under a gas station awning while the hail finished its tantrum on the roof.

*Recovery of abandoned scientific equipment, remote river gorge, monsoon region. Government survey team departed site abruptly; assets remain. Concession permits expire at season's end. Contractor will be sole authorized party on site. Generous flat fee plus salvage percentage. Debt consideration available.*

That last line was the hook, and whoever wrote it knew it. *Debt consideration available.* Four words aimed straight at the soft part of her ledger. Vantage didn't forgive debts. Vantage restructured them, which was a word that meant the same thing as *deepened*.

She sat there doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to. Eleven thousand in hail damage. Nine thousand in fuel contracts. A radar unit leased at predatory rates from a company that was, when you followed the ownership chain far enough up, Vantage itself wearing a different hat. She owed them something north of sixty thousand, and every mile she drove added to it, because the truck was theirs in everything but title, and the title was collateral they hadn't gotten around to taking yet.

Twenty years of chasing. Tornado alley, the gulf coast, two seasons in the Philippines, one catastrophic year in Bangladesh that she still didn't talk about. She measured her life in miles the way other people measured it in birthdays or anniversaries. Forty-one thousand miles last year. Thirty-eight the year before. She could tell you what the sky looked like over any county in Kansas in May, and she could not tell you the name of a single neighbor on her street in Wichita, because she'd been on that street four nights total in two years.

Miles were clean. Miles added up in only one direction. You put them behind you and they stayed behind you, and nothing you left back there could reach forward and touch you. That was the whole philosophy, if she was honest, though she never said it out loud: keep moving and the past can't catch your license plate.

Except debts don't work like storms. Debts wait.

* * *

She called the number in the message from a truck stop outside Hutchinson, standing between a diesel pump and a vending machine that ate her dollar. The voice on the other end was smooth and regional-neutral, a man who introduced himself as Corben and who spoke the way people speak when they've been trained to sound helpful.

"Ms. Marchetti. We're glad you responded. Your reputation precedes you."

"My reputation is I haven't turned a profit in three years."

"Which is precisely why you're our first call." A pause, warm as a handshake. "We need someone good enough to do the job and motivated enough to do it fast. The Kalyet Gorge survey team abandoned significant equipment—sensors, comms arrays, a full meteorological station worth several hundred thousand new. The concession holder's permits lapse when the monsoon ends. After that, everything on site becomes legally unrecoverable. We need it extracted before then."

"Who's 'we'?"

"Vantage Adventure Logistics. Extreme-tourism division." He said it lightly, like it was a travel agency. "We run guided expeditions in some of the most remote river systems in the world. The gorge is slated for development next cycle. The equipment is an asset we'd rather not write off."

"And the survey team? They just walked away from several hundred thousand dollars of gear?"

"People abandon things, Ms. Marchetti. Especially in places like that." Something flickered under the smoothness, quick as a fish under ice. "Your concern is the gear, not the people. They're accounted for. Paperwork exists."

Paperwork exists. She wrote that phrase down later, in the notebook she kept in the door pocket, and stared at it in a motel room that night without knowing why it bothered her. Everything was paperwork with Vantage. The debt was paperwork. The truck was paperwork. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in a city she'd never seen, Lena Marchetti herself was probably paperwork—a profile, a risk assessment, a line item marked *motivated*.

The terms arrived by morning. Flat fee against debt reduction, plus fifteen percent of assessed salvage value, plus expenses. Travel within seventy-two hours. The gorge was halfway around the world, in a monsoon belt she'd only read about—a river called the Kalyet cutting through limestone so deep the maps showed it as a shadow. The town at its mouth was called Sarn Khol. She looked it up and found three photographs, all taken by the same government survey team, all captioned with coordinates instead of names.

Seventy-two hours. She packed in twenty minutes, because twenty minutes was all her life required. Two duffel bags, a go-bag, the notebook, a rain shell rated for weather worse than anything Kansas had ever thrown at her. She left the motel key on the dresser and the storm season to whatever chaser picked up her slack, and she did not say goodbye to anyone, because there wasn't anyone.

At the rental counter she hesitated over the return date field. The clerk waited, chewing gum.

"One way," Lena said finally. "I'll figure out the rest down there."

* * *

Thirty hours of flights compressed her into a shape she barely recognized. Wichita to Dallas, Dallas to Doha, Doha to a coastal capital whose name she'd have to check her ticket to remember, then a prop plane west into hill country where the runway was a strip of flattened grass and the terminal was a man with a clipboard under an awning. From there, a bus. Then a smaller bus. Then a jeep hired from a driver who took one look at her duffels and quoted her triple the fair rate, and she paid it, because haggling was a skill for people with time.

The land changed the way weather changes—gradually, then all at once. Dry hills gave way to green so dense it looked wet even in sunlight. Rice terraces stepped down slopes like staircases for giants. And then, around a bend in the road, the air went cool and heavy and she smelled the river before she saw it: silt and stone and something mineral, like rain on hot rock, except the rain here never stopped long enough for anything to get hot.

The Kalyet Gorge opened below the road without warning. One moment there was jungle; the next, the earth simply fell away, a kilometer deep, and the river ran along the bottom of it brown and muscular, carrying whole trees like kindling. Mist rose off the water in columns. Somewhere down in that green dark, a meteorological station worth several hundred thousand dollars sat waiting for her, and a debt sat waiting to be erased, and both of those things felt, for the first time in years, like the same thing: a way out.

Sarn Khol appeared at the gorge mouth as if it had grown there rather than been built—a scatter of tin roofs and stilt houses on a shelf of rock above the water, a single street, a ferry landing worn smooth by decades of hulls. Monsoon clouds stacked up over the ridgeline to the north, gray as slate, patient as creditors.

She stood at the landing with her bags while the jeep driver dumped them and drove off, and she looked at the river and felt the old familiar itch start up in her chest—the one that said *don't unpack, don't learn anyone's name, get the gear, get paid, get gone*. Twenty years of that voice. It had kept her alive. It had also, she was beginning to suspect, kept her from anything else.

A boat was tied up at the landing, a long low ferry with a patched canopy, and a man was working on its hull with a scraper, methodical as tide. He didn't look up when she arrived. He didn't look up when she set her bags down. He scraped paint in long even strokes, and the river talked to itself below the landing, and the first fat drops of the evening's rain began ticking against the canopy above him.

"You the ferry?" she asked.

He considered the question longer than it deserved. "Depends who's asking."

"Lena Marchetti. I need to go upriver. Tomorrow, or as soon as this lets up." She jerked her chin at the sky, which was already answering.

The man set down his scraper and looked at her then, and his eyes were the color of the water—brown, unreadable, moving faster underneath than they showed on top. He glanced at her bags, at the logo on her rain shell, at the phone in her hand with its signal bars that had died an hour ago.

"Upriver's two days in good water," he said. "This isn't good water. And the survey camp—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening around whatever came next, "—isn't a place people go much anymore."

"I'm going anyway. I have a contract."

"Everyone upriver has a contract," he said, and picked the scraper back up. "Come back tomorrow. If the river agrees, I'll take you."

He turned back to his hull. Rain drummed on the canopy. Lena stood there a moment longer than she meant to, feeling dismissed in a language she didn't speak, and then shouldered her bags and climbed the steps toward the town, toward a guesthouse, toward the first night in twenty years she would spend in a place she had no plan to leave quickly—and no plan to stay, either.

Behind her, the ferryman resumed scraping. Under the old paint, she'd notice tomorrow, the hull bore the ghost of another name, sanded down but not gone: *Khol*, in letters faded almost to nothing, like a signature someone had tried very hard to erase.
