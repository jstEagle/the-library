## 2. The Gorge Keeps Its Own Records

The bus from the coast died twice on the switchbacks and had to be push-started by six men who laughed about it, and Lena understood the laughter. It was the laugh of people who lived somewhere the road didn't bother pretending to serve. When they finally rolled into Sarn Khol at dusk, the town was a smear of lamplight along a black gorge: tin roofs, a diesel generator coughing somewhere, and below it all, the river—wide, brown, and moving with the kind of patient violence that made her spine hum.

She'd been in towns like this before. Tornado towns. Fire towns. Places where the landscape had opinions. You could always tell because nobody asked you why you'd come. They just looked at your hands.

The Vantage contract sat folded in her jacket pocket, soft as cloth now from handling. *Recovery of abandoned government survey equipment, Kalyet Gorge concession block seven. Salvage rights transfer upon verified inventory. Completion deadline: end of monsoon season.* Fourteen days, maybe less, depending on how the rains held. The numbers were simple. The gear was worth enough to clear a third of what she owed, and the syndicate's regional man had made it clear that thirds were generous.

What the contract didn't say was why a government survey team would abandon forty thousand dollars of equipment in working condition. People didn't walk away from that. Not geologists, not hydrologists, not anyone who'd spent grant money arguing for every case of instruments. Abandonment meant catastrophe. Catastrophe meant bodies, or at least wreckage, or at least a story.

Sarn Khol had none of these.

* * *

She found the survey camp on her second morning, after a night in the one guesthouse listening to rain hammer the tin roof like applause from an enormous indifferent crowd. The camp sat on a gravel bench above the river, two hours' walk upstream, exactly where the contract's coordinates put it. She smelled it before she saw it—diesel, canvas, wet nylon—and her first thought was professional relief. Her second thought arrived ten seconds later and never left.

The tents were staked. Not collapsed, not torn, not dragged. Staked, guy-lines taut, rain flies secured against weather that hadn't stopped falling in three weeks. Somebody had prepared those tents for a storm and then walked out of them.

Inside the main tent, the mess table still held breakfast. Or what had been breakfast whenever breakfast was: three enamel plates with rice gone to gray mush, a kettle on the stove with water still in it, a jar of chili paste open with its lid set neatly beside it. A paperback novel lay face-down on someone's cot, spine cracked at page two hundred and something. A pair of reading glasses rested on top of it, folded, careful.

Lena stood in the doorway with rain running off her hood and felt the hair rise on her forearms. She'd seen abandoned camps before—chase crews that bailed ahead of a supercell, drilling sites evacuated for wildfire. Abandonment had a texture. It was fast and ugly and selective; you grabbed the hard drives and left the sleeping bags. This was different. This was *tidy*. Glasses folded. Book marked. Kettle filled, as if whoever filled it expected to come back and boil it.

Nobody comes back to boil a kettle.

She worked the camp the way she'd work a storm damage path, methodical, photographing everything, narrating into her phone out of habit even though there was no signal. The instrument cases were stacked under a tarp in the equipment tent, labeled, lashed. Core samples in waxed tubes, racked and numbered. A laptop bag hung on a peg. A satellite phone sat in its charging dock, dead, its cable still plugged into a solar array angled correctly at a sky that offered no sun.

Forty thousand dollars of gear, and not one item packed for transport. Not one item destroyed. Not one item hidden.

And no people. No graves, no blood, no drag marks in the mud. She circled the camp twice, reading the ground the way she read hail fields. The mud told her things. It told her the last vehicle tracks leaving camp were weeks old, grown soft-edged with rain. It told her that whatever footprints had once led away from this place going downstream had long since dissolved—but also that there were no prints leading *up* the trail toward the pass, which is where you'd go if you were leaving voluntarily. The trail up-canyon was the only exit that didn't require a boat, and it was clean. Nobody had used it.

So the team had either gone downriver—which meant boats, which meant the ferryman, which meant the town—or they had gone nowhere at all.

She stood in the mess tent a long time, looking at the three plates. Three plates. The contract said a five-person survey team. She counted cots. Five cots. Five bedrolls, five duffels, five sets of everything, and three plates of cold rice.

Somebody had cooked for five and served three. Or somebody had eaten in shifts. Or—

She stopped herself. That was the thing about her brain, the thing that made her good at this work and bad at everything else: it wouldn't stop. It took a detail and worried it like a dog with a bone until the bone became a door and the door became a hallway and the hallway went down into the dark. In Kansas that instinct kept her alive, reading a wall cloud's rotation before the sirens. Here, in a quiet tent full of cold rice, it was starting to feel like something else. Like the beginning of an obsession she couldn't afford.

Because here was the arithmetic she actually needed to avoid: if the survey team hadn't vanished—if they'd been *made* to vanish—then the gear wasn't salvage. It was evidence. And if it was evidence, then Vantage didn't want it recovered. Vantage wanted it *owned*. There was a difference, and the difference was the kind of thing that got contractors buried in riverbanks.

She zipped the tent flap closed behind her, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping person.

* * *

Back in Sarn Khol, she started asking questions, and discovered that the town practiced a silence so complete it qualified as architecture.

The guesthouse owner, a broad woman named Mrs. Dawa who fed her noodles without being asked, answered the question "Do you know the survey team?" with a shrug that contained an entire legal philosophy. The man who ran the generator co-op looked at his boots. Two teenagers playing cards by the landing didn't stop playing cards, didn't look up, didn't miss a beat—their stillness was so perfect it was clearly rehearsed, a whole generation drilled in the same performance.

Only one person gave her anything, and he gave it sideways, the way people hand you something hot. He was old, mending a net on the landing, and when Lena asked her question for the fourth time that day he didn't answer it. Instead he said, in trade English slow as syrup:

"The river keeps records. But the gorge—" he tapped the rock wall above them, black and dripping, "—the gorge keeps its own."

Then he went back to his net, and that was all.

She climbed to the guesthouse roof that evening and watched the gorge go dark and tried to make herself think like a contractor instead of a storm chaser. Contractor math: inventory the gear, document it, hire transport, file the claim, take the payout, shave a third off the debt. Fourteen days. Simple. Clean. Don't ask about the rice.

But the chaser in her kept reading the sky of it. Three plates. A marked book. A kettle waiting. Footprints that went nowhere. A town that had rehearsed its silence. Every instinct she'd trusted across twenty years of chasing said the same thing it said when a storm went green: *this isn't weather anymore.*

And underneath that, quieter, worse: the coordinates in her contract were precise. Precise enough that whoever wrote it knew exactly where the camp was, knew exactly what was in it, and knew exactly how much time the monsoon would leave on the clock. Someone at Vantage had drawn a map to a disappearance and called it an opportunity. They'd handed it to a woman deep enough in debt that she might not look too hard at what she was carrying out.

She thought about the enforcer's voice on the satellite call from the coast—*no complications, Marchetti, in and out, fourteen days*—and heard it differently now. Not logistics. Instructions.

Rain came back over the ridge line around midnight, and with it a sound she couldn't place at first, low and rhythmic under the drumming tin. Oars. Someone rowing upstream through the dark, against the current, which no sane person did at night in flood season. She leaned over the roof edge and saw a single lantern far out on the brown water, small and steady, moving with unhurried purpose toward the upper gorge.

Toward the camp. Toward the tents with the staked guy-lines and the kettle full of waiting water.

Lena watched the lantern until the bend swallowed it, and then she sat down hard on the wet shingles, heart going like a hail-dented drum, and understood that her fourteen-day contract had just become something else entirely.

In the morning, she was going to need a boat.
