## 3. A Stranger With a Leaking Boat

Tomas Khol knew the stranger was trouble before she reached the dock, because trouble always arrived the same way in Sarn Khol: alone, wrong-footed, and looking at the river like it owed them something.

She came down the mud path from the survey camp at midmorning, when the mist was still burning off the water, and she walked the way outsiders walked here—like the ground was a temporary arrangement. Tall woman, sunburned across the cheekbones, a dry bag over one shoulder that had been patched more times than his boat. She stopped at the top of the ramp and looked at the *Sarn Khol II*—his father's name for her, though there had never been a first—and Tomas watched her do the arithmetic. Hull. Engine. The tarp over the cargo well. He'd seen that look on a hundred faces, and it always ended the same way: *this is not enough boat.*

He was right to assume it. He was wrong about everything else.

"You're the ferryman," she said. Not a question.

"I'm a man with a boat."

"Lena Marchetti." She didn't offer a hand. Good. His hands were wet and he didn't want hers anyway. "I need to go upriver. Past the second bend. As far as the gorge narrows."

"Nobody goes past the second bend this time of year."

"That's why I'm asking you and not someone else."

He let that sit. Out on the water, the current carried a dead branch the size of a dog past the pilings, moving faster than a man could swim. He gestured at it with his chin. "That's why."

She looked at the branch, then back at him, and something in her face shifted—not softened, exactly. Recalibrated. She was the kind of person who treated obstacles as information rather than insult, and Tomas distrusted that in people, because it was the kind of thing he saw in mirrors.

"How much?" she said.

"For what?"

"The trip. Both ways if I have to come back down with gear."

"It's not about money."

"Everything's about money. That's not cynicism, it's just physics."

He almost smiled. Almost. He wiped his hands on his trousers instead and looked upriver, where the gorge walls went green and vertical and swallowed the river into shadow. Somewhere up there was the survey camp she'd already found, because of course she had; the whole town had watched her walk up to it yesterday evening and walk back down before dark, and the whole town had said nothing, and the nothing had been so loud that even she must have heard it.

*The whole town knows,* he thought. *And she's asking me.*

Because his family held the concession papers. Because the permits were expiring with the monsoon. Because whatever the government team had left behind in that camp, it sat on Khol land, and everyone understood—if they thought about it at all—that the stranger's salvage contract was worth less than the paper it was printed on unless a Khol ferried her to it.

Or maybe she just liked the look of him. Strangers were bad at reading this country.

"There's a condition," he said.

"Name it."

"You don't ask me questions about the survey team while we're on the water."

Her eyes narrowed. "That's backwards. You're taking me to their camp."

"I'm taking you upriver. What's upriver is your business. Who was there before you—" he shrugged, "—is mine."

"And if I ask anyway?"

"Then we turn around, and you can explain to whoever holds your contract why you spent three days getting nowhere."

Something flickered across her face at the word *contract*—a flinch, fast, gone before it finished arriving. Tomas filed it away. He was good at filing things away; it was most of what he did.

"Fine," she said. "No questions."

"Load your own gear. And bring rope."

* * *

The *Sarn Khol II* leaked. This was not a secret; it was a feature of their relationship, Tomas and his boat, an old argument renewed every season. She took water through a seam below the waterline on the port side, slow enough to bail twice an hour in calm water, fast enough to bail continuously in current. His father had patched her eleven times. Tomas had stopped counting after that, the way you stop counting a parent's excuses.

Lena discovered the leak twenty minutes out of Sarn Khol, when the river bent north and the current leaned its shoulder into them.

"She's taking water."

"She does that."

"How much is 'does that'?"

"Enough that you should bail. Bucket's under your seat. Bail like you mean it and we're fine. Bail like you're bored and we're swimming."

She bailed like she meant it. That surprised him. Most outsiders bailed the way they did everything else—performing effort for an audience, waiting for someone else to take the chore. Lena Marchetti bailed with the flat efficiency of a person who had been wet before and hadn't enjoyed it. Her strokes were short and rhythmic, no wasted motion, and within ten minutes she'd found the seam's rhythm too, adjusting without being told.

"You've run boats," he said.

"Airboats. Storm chasing, mostly. Kansas, Oklahoma, the plains." She nodded at the gorge walls rising around them. "Nothing like this."

"Then bail like it's new."

The rain started an hour later, because it was the monsoon and the rain always started eventually, and it came down in ropes rather than drops. Tomas ran them into the lee of a rock shelf and killed the engine, and for a while there was only the sound of water on water—the river, the rain, the bucket going down and coming up.

She talked first. They always talked first; silence made outsiders itch.

"Your family's been on this river a long time."

"No questions."

"That wasn't a question. It was an observation."

"It was a question wearing a coat." But he said it without edge, and she heard that, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the space between them with something almost like privacy.

"My father held the ferry concession," he said finally, and didn't know why he was saying it. Maybe because the rain made confessions cheap. "His father before him. The concession covers the upper gorge—ferry rights, landing rights, salvage rights on anything the river gives up between the second bend and the falls. It's been ours for sixty years."

"And now?"

"Now it expires at the end of the monsoon. There's a renewal process. There's paperwork." He turned the engine over, listened to it, shut it down again. "There are people in the district capital who would prefer the paperwork go another way."

"What kind of people?"

"That's two questions."

"That wasn't—"

"Third one's forming behind your teeth. I can see it." He looked at her, and she looked back, and the rain drummed on the canopy like fingers on a desk. "You want to know who scared off the government team."

The words landed in the bottom of the boat like the bucket, heavy and full. Lena Marchetti went very still—the stillness of a person who has hunted things for a living and knows what it means when prey stops moving.

"You know what happened to them," she said quietly.

"I know the river keeps its own records. And I know yours is the third contract this year that smells like Vantage."

He watched her absorb the name. Watched her decide, visibly, whether to lie. He respected that she took a full three seconds to decide, and respected more that she chose truth—or a piece of it.

"They hold my debt," she said. "Salvage contract. I clear it or I don't go home."

"And home is?"

"Kansas. A truck. A hangar bay I rent by the month." A short laugh with nothing in it. "Home is wherever the next mile starts."

Tomas looked at her then, really looked, the way he looked at the river before running a rapid—not to admire it but to read it. Here was a woman who measured her life in distance, who had crossed an ocean to pull strangers' equipment out of a gorge she couldn't pronounce, all to service a debt to men who frightened survey teams off mountainsides. She was either a fool or a prisoner, and prisoners were more dangerous, because they had nothing left to lose except the trip itself.

"We should get moving," he said. "Rain breaks in twenty minutes. We make the narrows before dark or we camp on the water."

"You didn't answer my question."

"I answered all of it. You just didn't like the shape of the answer."

* * *

They made the narrows as the light failed, grey going to violet going to black between walls that rose sheer on both sides, and Tomas put them ashore on a gravel bar that would be underwater by morning if the river rose another meter. They built no fire. Fire drew attention, though he didn't say whose attention, and she didn't ask, which he noticed and filed.

They ate in silence: rice and dried fish from his stores, shared without ceremony. Then she did something he didn't expect. She pulled a waterproof case from her dry bag, took out a folded paper, and studied it by the last violet light—not hiding it, not showing him, just holding it the way a gambler holds cards he hasn't decided to play.

Concession documents. He knew the format without seeing the header. He'd grown up watching his father fill out that format every spring, in careful ink, in the district office that smelled of mildew and indifference.

"You're carrying our land papers," Tomas said.

"Vantage's copies. Part of my contract file. Permits, boundaries, expiry dates." She folded it away. "I read everything I sign. Or everything I'm handed to work under. Habit."

"Find anything interesting?"

"I find a lot of things interesting." She lay back against her pack and closed her eyes, conversation over, and Tomas sat with the engine key in his pocket and the river talking to itself in the dark and the knowledge sitting in his chest where it had sat for four months now, patient as a stone:

He knew who had come upriver in the black raft in the spring. He knew the polite man with the clipboard and the two quiet men with the rifles slung casual, and he knew how the survey team's cook had come down to Sarn Khol three days later, white-faced, buying passage for eleven people and paying triple and never once meeting anyone's eyes. He knew where the team had gone, and he knew what they'd taken with them, and he knew why his mother kept the shrine locked these days in a way she never had before.

He could tell her. One sentence, and the stranger with the leaking bucket would understand exactly what cargo she was really carrying up this river, and whose inheritance was folded inside her contract file.

But trust was a boat too, Tomas thought. You didn't launch it loaded. You tested the seams first, in shallow water, and you watched how it took the weight.

Above them, far up the gorge, thunder rolled—a long, grinding sound, less like weather than like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Lena opened one eye. "How high does this river get?"

"Higher than that gravel bar," Tomas said. "Sleep light."

And downstream, faint through the rain, he thought he heard an engine—low, patient, running without lights.
