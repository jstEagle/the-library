## 4. Monsoon Season Has No Contract Terms

The river rose four feet overnight.

Lena knew because she'd marked the waterline on a boulder with Tomas's chalk before they tied off for the night, a habit from storm season—measure everything, trust nothing. Now the chalk line sat underwater, and the boulder itself was only a suggestion of shape beneath brown, muscular current.

"Camp's moving," she said.

Tomas was already up, barefoot in the rain, untying the boat. He didn't argue or explain. He just pointed upstream to a shelf of rock above the flood line where someone—years ago, by the look of the rusted pitons—had rigged tarps and platforms. A mid-river camp. The kind of place ferrymen built for exactly this: when the gorge decided it owned the water and everything floating on it.

They spent two hours hauling gear up slick stone in rain that came down like thrown gravel. Lena carried crates until her forearms burned and her grip went numb, and twice she slipped and caught herself on the old piton lines, which held, which meant someone had trusted their life to this camp before her. She tried not to think about who.

By the time the last crate was stacked under the tarp, the gravel bar where they'd slept was gone. Not flooded—*gone*, scoured clean, the river having reached up in the night and taken it like a debt collector.

Tomas looked at the empty space where dry land had been and said nothing at all.

* * *

They were trapped. That was the plain arithmetic of it. Upstream, the rapids through the narrows ran too violent to ferry; downstream, the same. The boat was beached and lashed double. The monsoon had closed its hand around the middle of the gorge, and there was nowhere to go but into the crates.

So Lena did what she always did with a salvage contract: she inventoried.

She worked by headlamp inside the tarp shelter while rain hammered the roof like applause from an enormous indifferent crowd. The survey team's equipment was good stuff—government-issue, newer than anything Vantage fielded. Water samplers with calibration logs still clipped to them. Core drills. A sediment analysis kit packed so carefully it looked like it had been put away by someone planning to come back.

That was the thing that kept snagging her. Everything was *stowed*. Not abandoned in panic, not scattered by flight. Stowed, labeled, oiled. In Sarn Khol she'd found meals half-eaten and boots by a door, but here, in the cargo, she found intention. Someone had packed this equipment expecting to return within days.

Nobody packs like that if they're dead.

She pushed the thought down and kept working, because inventory was safe, inventory was numbers, and numbers had never once lied to her the way people did.

Crate nine was heavier than its manifest weight. That was the first wrong thing. She weighed it on her hand scale—eleven kilos against a listed six—and set it apart without comment. Tomas watched her do it from across the shelter, mending a tear in the tarp with waxed twine, and said nothing, but his hands slowed.

Crate nine wasn't government issue. The stenciling was civilian, third-party, the kind of subcontractor marking Vantage used on its own logistics. Her own name had traveled in crates marked just like it, once, on a hail run through Kansas that ended with her owing more than the truck was worth.

She opened it with a pocketknife and a growing coldness in her stomach.

Paper. Eleven kilos of paper, sealed in waterproof sleeves, and beneath the paper, a hard case with a portable scanner and three data drives.

The top document was a concession map. Kalyet Gorge, upper reach—the very stretch of river Tomas ferried, the land his family had worked for generations. The map showed boundaries, and the boundaries were wrong. Lena knew the official registry lines because she'd read the permit file on the flight in, cross-checking the contract the way she checked weather: obsessively, looking for the cell in the sky that would kill her. The registry gave the Khol family rights to the full upper reach.

This map gave them a strip half as wide.

She pulled the next sleeve. Correspondence. Internal Vantage memos, dated years back, discussing a "boundary rationalization"—a beautiful phrase, she had to give them that—for the Kalyet concession ahead of tourism development. A memo noting that the original survey records held at the regional office could be "amended." A follow-up memo confirming they *had* been amended, with a signature she recognized from her own contract. The man who'd offered her this job had signed the erasure of a family's inheritance, and then hired a stranger to haul away the evidence that it had ever existed.

Because that's what this was. Not salvage. Disposal. The survey team hadn't been mapping the gorge for the government—they'd been re-surveying it, quietly, and somewhere in their work they'd found the original records, the true ones, the ones showing the Khols' full claim. And then the team had vanished, and Vantage had needed the physical evidence gone before permits expired and the whole question reopened.

And she was the disposal mechanism. Eleven kilos of proof, contracted to disappear upriver with a woman too deep in debt to ask questions.

Lena sat back on her heels. Rain filled the silence around her, and the headlamp beam wavered slightly, and she realized her hands weren't steady.

"Tomas," she said.

He looked over. He'd stopped pretending to mend the tarp some minutes ago; she understood that now.

"This crate." She held up the map, the true boundary line traced in the surveyor's careful ink. "This is your family's land. All of it. The registry record was falsified."

He didn't move. Didn't blink. Rain drummed. And then he crossed the shelter and took the map from her hands with a gentleness that frightened her more than anger would have, the way you handle something that has already broken you once.

"I know," he said.

* * *

They sat facing each other over the open crate while the river spoke its one long word below them.

"You knew," Lena said. "Before we ever launched."

"I knew the survey team came to my mother with the original records. I knew men from Vantage came upriver after them, polite men, smiling men, and the next morning the camp was empty and the team was gone." His voice stayed level, the way a river stays level over a drop. "I knew whoever Vantage sent to collect this cargo would be carrying away the last proof of what they stole."

"And you let me load it into your boat."

"You were going upriver either way." He folded the map along its original creases, precisely, a man handling an heirloom. "I wanted to see what you were. Debt makes people honest, eventually. So does high water."

Lena laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "What did you see?"

"Someone who inventories everything." He nodded at the manifests spread around her, her neat columns, her obsessive cross-checks. "Someone who reads contracts like weather. You knew something was wrong with this job before you took it. You took it anyway. I wanted to know why."

"Because I owe Vantage more than I'll earn in ten years, and this contract forgives a piece of it, and I told myself that was the same as being free." She said it flatly, factually, the way she logged wind speeds. It was the most honest sentence she'd spoken aloud in years, and it cost her something to hear it leave her mouth.

Tomas studied her for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing; the rain thickened from sheets to walls.

"The permits expire when the monsoon ends," he said. "Thirty days, maybe less. After that, the concession question closes until next season, and Vantage's amended maps become simply the maps. This paper is worth nothing in a crate. It's worth everything filed at the land registry in Sarn Khol, with testimony, before expiry."

"Testimony." She shook her head slowly. "You're asking me to walk into the office of the syndicate that owns my debt and hand them a reason to destroy me."

"I'm asking you to decide whose side of the river you're standing on."

Lena looked at the crate—at eleven kilos of other people's theft, other people's courage, a vanished team that had packed up expecting to come back. She thought about the arithmetic she'd lived by for twenty years: miles logged, debts accrued, exits calculated. Every number in her ledger said take the salvage downriver, make the trade, buy back her life at the price of a family she'd known for six days.

Every number said that. And yet.

"We should get some sleep," she said finally, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it.

* * *

She woke near midnight to a sound that didn't belong to rain.

An engine. Low, patient, running without lights—downstream, somewhere below the narrows, holding position in the dark the way a predator holds position downwind.

Lena lay still in the wet dark and listened to it idle. Whoever it was couldn't run the narrows tonight any more than they could. They were trapped together, all of them, on opposite banks of the same flood.

She thought about the data drives in crate nine, and slid them out of the case, and tucked them into the inner pocket of her jacket, against her skin, where a knife usually rode.

Then she lay back and watched the tarp shudder overhead, doing the only math that mattered now:

How long until the water dropped. How far to Sarn Khol. And whether the engine below them belonged to a rescue—or to the people who had signed her name to this job, waiting to see what she carried out of the gorge.
