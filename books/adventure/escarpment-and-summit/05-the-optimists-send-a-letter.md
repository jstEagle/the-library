## 5. The Optimists Send a Letter

My father used to say the river tells you who's coming before you see them. He meant it practically — a heavy boat displaces water differently, a motor carries through stone, a man poling hard against current is a man in a hurry. But he also meant it the other way. The river knows things. It had known my grandfather's name before the concession papers did.

So when the water below the bend went flat and wrong on the fourth morning of the rains, I knew someone was coming who didn't belong to it.

I was mending net under the lean-to at the mid-river camp while Lena inventoried the salvage crates for the third time. She'd been doing that all week — counting things she'd already counted, as if the numbers might rearrange themselves into something kinder. I watched her hands move over the survey team's equipment and thought about what I hadn't told her yet. What my mother kept wrapped in oilcloth behind the shrine. The reason the Khol family still had any claim to this stretch of water at all.

The flatness on the river broke. A raft came around the bend, riding high, paddled by two men in matching yellow rain shells. Nobody wears matching anything on the Kalyet. Matching means payroll.

"Tomas." Lena had seen them too. She came out from under the lean-to with a crate lid still in her hand, holding it like a shield or a weapon, I couldn't tell which. "Friends of yours?"

"No one's friend."

* * *

They beached neatly, professionally, and the tall one stepped off first and wrung out his sleeve like a man arriving at a garden party rather than a drowned gorge in monsoon season. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that has been photographed a great deal and has learned to arrange itself for it. Behind him, the second man stayed with the raft and said nothing, and looked at everything.

"Ms. Marchetti?" The tall one smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced smiles usually are. "Dev Anand. Vantage Expeditions, Regional Partnerships Division." He pronounced the division name the way some men pronounce their own titles. "We've been so worried about you. The weather reports out here are criminal — nobody could reach you sooner."

"Weather reports," Lena said.

"Monsoon season has no contract terms, I'm afraid. We tried to tell you that in the offer letter." He turned the smile toward me without turning his body, an economy of motion that told me he'd done this in places less forgiving than a riverbank. "And you must be Tomas Khol. Your family's ferry service is legendary. My father took your father's boat up to the falls, must have been thirty years ago. He talked about it for the rest of his life."

I said nothing. My father had never taken a Vantage man upriver in his life. He'd refused them twice, loudly, and the second refusal had cost us a season of permits. But men like Dev Anand don't lie to deceive you. They lie to establish the shape of the world they want.

"You're a long way from anywhere," Lena said. "Even for partnerships."

"Optimism travels well." He gestured at the camp, the crates, the lean-to, with an open palm, a man admiring another man's garden. "May I sit? This rain — it's not really rain anymore, is it. It's a climate."

He sat on the supply crate nearest the fire ring without waiting for an answer, which answered its own question. Lena stayed standing. I moved to stand where I always stand when strangers come to the bank — between them and the boat. Not because the boat matters. Because it's where my family has stood for three generations, and the body remembers even when the papers don't.

"Here's the situation, plainly, because I respect plain dealing." Dev Anand produced a leather folder, dry despite the deluge, which meant it had traveled inside something waterproofed and expensive. "Vantage is prepared to make this very simple for everyone. The salvage contract Ms. Marchetti signed includes a buyout clause — standard language, section eleven, I can show you. We purchase the recovered equipment at fair market value plus twenty percent. Her outstanding debt is forgiven in full. Clean ledger. She walks away owing nothing to anyone."

"And the catch," Lena said.

"There's no catch. There's a scope clarification." He opened the folder and removed a single sheet, holding it out. "Our records indicate the survey team's cargo included certain materials — data storage, field documentation — that were government property on loan to a licensed concession holder. Those materials are outside the salvage scope. They need to come back with me. Everything else is yours to sell, keep, sink, burn — frankly we don't care. Just the documents."

Lena didn't take the sheet. I watched her not take it, and I watched what happened in her face while she didn't — the small war of it, the arithmetic running behind her eyes. I had seen that arithmetic before. On my mother's face, the year the syndicate's lawyers redrew the concession maps and my father stood in a registry office holding papers that had suddenly stopped meaning anything.

"Which documents," Lena said carefully, "would those be?"

"Survey records. Hydrological data. Concession assessments." He said it lightly, a man listing groceries. "Bureaucratic material. Worthless to anyone but auditors, and there are no auditors within four hundred kilometers."

* * *

He made his offer twice more over the next hour, each time softer, each time with more warmth, and I understood that the warmth was the threat. Men who shout are selling fear. Men who smile are selling inevitability.

Lena stalled beautifully. I'll give her that. She asked about the buyout valuation until he produced figures. She asked about the debt structure until he produced schedules. She walked him along the crate rows and let him look at equipment that wasn't what he wanted, and he let himself be shown, patiently, because patience is also a message: *we have time, we have resources, we will still be here when your money runs out.*

I listened from the boat, bailing water that didn't need bailing.

What struck me — what lodged in me like a splinter — was how close it was. Twenty percent above market. Full forgiveness. A clean ledger. I know what her debt looks like; I've carried debts of my own, the kind that compound in the dark while you sleep. He was offering her the thing she'd crossed an ocean chasing: freedom, purchased honestly, receipt included. All it would cost was a folder of paper and the future of a family she'd known for two weeks.

I had no right to resent the temptation. I'd withheld the truth from her myself, every day since Sarn Khol, every night in the leaking boat, every hour she spent counting crates that held the proof of what was taken from us. She didn't know the documents were ours. She didn't know the survey team had come to my mother's door soaked and terrified, that my mother had fed them and hidden their evidence and sent them upriver to the passes before the enforcers arrived. She didn't know why the camp was intact, why the meals were half-eaten, why no one in town would meet her eyes.

She only knew she was drowning, and a smiling man had just thrown her a rope.

"The thing I don't understand," Lena said, near the end, standing at the waterline with the rain coming down her collar, "is why a regional partnerships director personally rafted four days into a flood zone for a folder of bureaucratic paperwork."

Dev Anand laughed, genuinely, and for a moment the smile fell away and something older and tireder looked out of his face. "Because I've been doing this job for twenty-two years, Ms. Marchetti, and I have learned that the paperwork is never just paperwork. Somewhere in that camp is a document that ends careers. There always is. My instruction is to find it before the permits lapse and the registry closes for the season." He picked up his dry folder and stood. "You have until the water drops. After that, the permits expire, the salvage reverts, and whatever's in those crates becomes someone else's problem. Someone less polite than me."

He shook her hand. He offered me his, and I looked at it, and after a moment he withdrew it without offense, still smiling, and I understood he'd expected exactly that and had offered anyway, because the offer itself was a test he could report back on.

At the raft he paused. "Tomas Khol. Your grandfather poled this gorge in water no one else would touch. It would be a shame if the last of that line went down with the concession." He settled onto the raft. "Think of it as inheritance planning."

Then they were gone around the bend, and the river closed over the sound of their paddles, and the flat wrongness left the water the way a fever breaks.

* * *

Lena stood in the rain a long time after they'd gone. I gave her the space. Some decisions need weather.

Finally she came and sat on the gunwale across from me, dripping, and said, "Twenty percent above market. Full forgiveness. That's — Tomas, that's everything. That's ten years of my life handed back to me in a folder."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw that she was angry — not at him, at me. "You knew they'd come. You've known since Sarn Khol. You watch me count these crates like a woman counting her own coffin nails, and you know what's in them, and you say nothing."

"That's true."

"What's in them, Tomas?"

I looked downriver, toward the shrine I couldn't see, twelve kilometers away behind the flood mist, where my mother kept the oilcloth bundle safe. The survey team had trusted us because my father had once pulled two of them out of a whirlpool above the falls, and trust like that doesn't expire with permits. But Lena — Lena I'd been testing for two weeks, weighing whether she was the kind of person who takes the folder and the forgiveness, and I realized, sitting there in the rain, that I'd been letting her take the exam without ever giving her the reading.

"They're our land titles," I said. "Every document in that camp proves the concession fraud. And my family has been hiding the originals since the night the survey team ran."

Lena went very still. Rain ran off her chin. When she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have.

"How long have you known what I'm carrying?"

"Since the day you hired the boat."

She stood up slowly. Downriver, faint beneath the rain, I heard it — or imagined I heard it — the sound of more paddles than two, coming back through the flood.

"Then we have a problem," Lena said, "because I already told them where the crates are."
