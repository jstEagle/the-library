## 6. What the Water Takes

The river told us an hour before it arrived.

I didn't understand the language yet, but Tomas did. He stopped mid-sentence over the inventory ledger—my ledger, my salvage, my numbers—and cocked his head the way a dog does at a frequency humans can't hear. Then he said one word in his own tongue and started kicking dirt over the fire.

"What is it?" I said.

"Water." He was already moving, pulling the tarped crates up the bank with a speed that made me revise every estimate I'd built about the man. "Not this water. Other water. From the high valleys."

"It hasn't rained here in two days."

"It's been raining up there for a week." He pointed at the gorge walls, at the brown smear of cloud sitting on the ridgeline like a bruise. "The river keeps what the mountains send. It just doesn't always announce it."

We hauled. That's what the next hour was—hauling. Crates of survey instruments, the theodolite in its padded case, the sealed document tubes I'd catalogued twice and photographed three times because something about them made my skin itch. The generator. The fuel cans. Forty-one line items of salvage that represented the difference between Lena Marchetti, debt-free, and Lena Marchetti, owned. I carried them up the bank in the rain that had started falling again, fine and needling, and stacked them on the shelf of stone above the high-water line, which I judged by the debris and Tomas judged by something older.

"You should move them higher," he said.

"They're above the mark."

"The mark is a suggestion." He looked at the sky, then at the river, and I watched him do the math I couldn't. "This river doesn't negotiate. I told you that."

"You told me it keeps records."

"Same thing."

The water came up the way a debt collector does—quietly, then all at once. One minute the channel was high and fast and angry; the next, a wall of it rounded the bend upstream, a brown shoulder of water taller than the boat, carrying whole trees sideways, carrying a roof, carrying something that might once have been a footbridge. The sound arrived after it, a rolling boom that I felt in my sternum, in my teeth, in the fillings I'd never gotten around to replacing because Vantage took sixty percent.

"Tomas—"

"I see it."

He was running for the boat. Not away from it—toward it. The ferry was tied at the lower landing, and the flood was going to take it, and he was going to lose the last thing his family owned if he didn't cut it loose and run it into the eddy behind the rock spur, and I understood all of that in the half-second before I understood the other thing, which was that the eddy was already filling with debris and a man alone in a leaking boat in that water had maybe a coin-flip chance.

"Tomas, leave it—"

He didn't leave it. Of course he didn't leave it. I watched him cast off the painter and swing aboard and take the oars against a current that picked the boat up like a toy, and I stood on the bank holding a document tube like an idiot while the man I'd been negotiating trust with for two weeks rowed into a flood.

I did the only arithmetic that mattered. The salvage was on the bank. The salvage was fine. The salvage could drown for all I cared, because Tomas was in the water and the enforcer's raft—the polite smiling man's raft, moored downstream at the camp we'd been avoiding—was going to be swept too, and somewhere in the churning brown between those two facts was a decision I hadn't consciously made yet but my legs had.

I ran downstream along the bank, over slick stone, through water to my knees and then my thighs, and the river had come up four feet in the time it took me to cover two hundred meters. The raft came apart against the rock spur—I saw it happen, the pontoon frame folding around a submerged trunk with a crack I heard over the flood, the enforcer's outboard and his polite little camp stove and his polite little satellite phone all going into the brown at once. Good, some vicious part of me said. And then the boat.

The ferry hit the spur's downstream eddy and the current spun it and Tomas went in. No drama, no movie slow motion—he was just there, and then he wasn't, just a hand on the gunwale and then brown water closing over the place where he'd been. The boat, empty, pinned against the rock, taking water through the leak he'd never fixed because he'd spent the money on his mother's medicine instead.

I don't remember deciding to go in. I remember the cold. I remember that the Kalyet in flood is not water, it's a moving landmass, and swimming in it is a lie you tell yourself while it happens to you. I remember catching his jacket—canvas, heavy, dragging him down—and nearly losing him twice, once to a submerged snag that peeled my grip open and once to the eddy itself, which wanted to keep us both. I remember surfacing against the rock spur with my arm hooked through a crack in the stone and Tomas coughing against my shoulder, alive, heavier than a man should be, his weight trying to take us both under, and me screaming words at the river that I won't repeat even in my own head.

Then hands—his hands—on the rock. Then stone under my knees. Then nothing but rain and the two of us on the spur with the flood roaring past close enough to touch, and the boat pinned and dying beside us, and the entire salvage inventory sitting safe and dry on the bank like a punchline.

We got the boat off an hour later, when the water dropped a foot and the eddy relented. It was wrecked. Not sunk-wrecked—wrecked-wrecked, a cracked strake, a sprung seam, the kind of damage you fix with a season and skills I don't have. Tomas ran his palm along the split in the hull the way you'd touch a wound on a person.

"I can patch it," he said. His voice was scraped raw from the river. "It won't be pretty."

"Will it float?"

"It will float." A pause. "Eventually."

* * *

We dried off under the overhang where the survey team had once cooked their meals, and I did the inventory I'd been dreading. The flood had taken the lower stack—everything I'd judged safe by the debris line. Eleven crates gone downstream, including the generator, the fuel, and half the instruments. The document tubes survived. All of them. They'd sat in my arms through the whole disaster because I'd been carrying the one I'd photographed when the river came.

Forty-one line items became twenty-six. My debt was denominated in the full forty-one. I sat there with the ledger and did the math twice, three times, hoping the numbers would get embarrassed and change. They didn't.

"Half the salvage," I said. "I just lost half the salvage."

"You lost half the salvage," Tomas said, wringing out his jacket. "You also went into a flood for a man who isn't in your ledger. Vantage's accountants will be confused."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, the kind of laugh that happens when the body needs to discharge something the mind won't process yet. He let me have it. He was good at that—letting people have things without making it a transaction.

"The raft," I said, when I could talk again. "The enforcer's raft is gone. His phone, his radio, everything."

"Yes."

"He was polite about it, but he was here to collect. And now he's walking out, or waiting for extraction, or—" I stopped. "Tomas. I just destroyed my employer's equipment in a flood I could have—should have—pulled his gear up the bank first. That's not an act of God. That's negligence, or sabotage, or—"

"That's a story," he said. "And the story you tell depends on which side of it you're standing when it ends." He fed a branch into the small fire he'd coaxed alive under the overhang. "You've been standing on Vantage's side since Sarn Khol. Standing is not the same as belonging."

I looked at the document tubes. Eleven of them, sealed, government-stamped, the ones I'd photographed three times because my gut kept whispering. I'd inventoried them as *contents unknown, priority high*. High priority for whom? For the contract. For Vantage. For the syndicate that had sent a smiling man upriver on a raft to buy them off me before anyone official could look inside.

"Tell me about the survey team," I said.

Tomas didn't answer right away. He watched the fire. Outside, the flood was dropping, the river's voice lowering from roar to growl.

"You already know they didn't drown," he said.

"I know there were no tracks and no bodies and the camp was packed for a move, not abandoned. I know locals go quiet when I ask. I know Vantage's permits expire with the monsoon and their enforcers arrived before my salvage did." I pulled the tube out of the stack and held it up. "And I know whatever's in here is why."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he reached over and took the tube from my hands, and I let him, and he cracked the seal with his thumb.

Maps. Survey maps of the gorge, annotated in three hands. Concession boundary overlays. And beneath them, in a folder marked with a government registry stamp, a chain of title—transfers, amendments, signatures, dates. I read fast; it's the one skill Vantage's contract actually sharpened. The Khol family concession, held for three generations. Then a 2019 amendment. Then a 2021 transfer of rights to a subsidiary with a name I knew, because I'd signed a debt instrument with its sister company. The signatures on the amendment were forged—I didn't need an expert, the dates didn't line up, the Khol patriarch had been in a hospital two provinces away when he'd supposedly ceded his family's inheritance to the syndicate that now owned my contract.

"They didn't vanish," Tomas said quietly. "They found this, and Vantage's men found them finding it, and my family hid them upriver and moved them out over the mountains before the enforcers came back. Four of them. Alive. They left this with us because we're the ones it belongs to." He rolled the map closed. "The survey team didn't run from the river, Lena. They ran from the people who hired you."

The fire popped. The river growled. Somewhere downstream, a polite smiling man was standing in the wreckage of his raft, and every plan I'd built—hand over the salvage, clear the debt, disappear down a highway with a clean ledger—was floating away on the same brown water that had nearly taken Tomas.

"If I bring this in," I said, "I'm not a contractor anymore. I'm a witness. Witnesses don't get debt forgiveness. They get visited."

"Yes," Tomas said.

"And if I hand it to Vantage, the debt clears and your family loses everything a second time."

"Yes."

I sat with the tube in my lap and felt, for the first time in twenty years of measuring my life in miles, the exact weight of a thing that couldn't be outrun.

"The permits expire when the monsoon ends," I said. "How many days?"

Tomas looked at the sky, then at me.

"Enough," he said. "If we run the gorge."
