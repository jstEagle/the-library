## 7. Inheritance Is a Debt You Didn't Sign

The shrine sat above the flood line, which told you everything about how long the Khols had known this river. My grandfather cut the first stair into the rock before the concession existed, before there were papers to hold. The stone steps were worn to a shallow curve in their centers, each one a small argument that his family had been climbing here longer than any syndicate had been writing contracts.

I went up first with the lamp. Lena followed, and I heard her count the steps under her breath—fourteen, fifteen—because she counted everything. Miles, hours, debts. It was how she kept the world from sliding.

The shrine was a single room cut into the cliff face, no bigger than her tent. A shelf of river stones my grandmother had carried up one at a time over forty years. A brass lamp blackened at the lip. And behind the stone shelf, in a cavity sealed with wax and oilcloth, what my father had hidden the night the survey team came down from the gorge with their eyes still wide and their hands shaking.

"They didn't vanish," I said. "You should hear it from me before you see it."

Lena stood in the doorway, dripping monsoon off her jacket onto the threshold stone. She didn't come in. People who chase storms learn a superstition about thresholds; I'd watched it all week. You don't cross until you know what's on the other side is survivable.

"Tell me," she said.

"Six of them. The night Vantage's men came through Sarn Khol with the new concession maps. The team's lead was a woman named Osei. She understood within an hour what the maps meant—that Vantage had redrawn the boundaries, moved the river markers, backdated everything. Her permit was real. Theirs was forged. But forged paper beats honest paper when the forger owns the registry clerks." I lifted the lamp so the light reached the shelf. "She came to my father because his family held the old concession. She said: if they erase you, they'll erase us too. So they ran. Upriver, past the falls, into country nobody surveys. Alive. All six. And she left this."

I pulled the oilcloth packet from the cavity and set it on the shelf between the river stones, where it looked wrong and right at once—an intruder among things placed by hand over decades.

"Open it," Lena said.

"You open it."

* * *

She crossed the threshold then. Of course she did. I was learning that about her: every refusal lasted exactly as long as it took to become unbearable.

Her hands were steady opening the wax. Inside were the survey originals—the field notebooks, the boundary measurements, photographs of the marker stones with the Khol family's registration numbers still legible beneath the fresh paint Vantage had applied. And a signed statement, dated three days before the team fled, in Osei's careful hand: *Concession boundaries as filed by Vantage Holdings do not correspond to physical markers or historical registry records. Alterations appear deliberate. I am withholding final certification.*

"She certified nothing," I said. "That's why they needed her gone quietly. An uncertified survey means the concession reverts at expiry. To the original holders."

"To your family," Lena said.

"To my family. When the monsoon ends. Eleven days."

She read everything twice. I watched her do it—the way her finger tracked the lines, the way she stopped breathing during the photographs and started again after. This was a woman who could read a sky. I wanted her to read this the same way, and I could see that she was, and I could see that it frightened her more than any wall cloud ever had.

"Tomas." She set the statement down flat, aligning its edges with the shelf. "Do you know what this is worth?"

"I know what it costs."

"That's not—" She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "No. Fine. Yes. Both." She turned to face me, and the lamp threw her shadow up the shrine wall, taller than either of us. "If I take this to the registry, I'm not filing evidence. I'm testifying. My name goes on it. I'm the contractor Vantage sent to salvage the site—I'm in their system, Tomas, I signed their indemnity clauses, I took their advance against a debt I can't discharge. The moment I file this, their lawyers don't come after the paper. They come after me. Fraud facilitation, contract violation, whatever charge sticks. I'll spend years proving I didn't know, and even if I win, I lose. Nobody hires a storm chaser who testified against a syndicate. The debt doesn't die with them—it gets sold. Sold debt outlives everything. There are men who buy debt the way you buy boats."

"And if you hand it to them instead," I said, "the debt clears. Clean exit. Your records burn, Osei's stay buried, and my family's concession dies in eleven days with nobody left to claim it."

"Yes," she said. Simply. That was the worst part—how simply she could say it. Not cruel. Precise. She measured life in miles, and this was just the next mile, and she was showing me the map because she thought I deserved the honesty.

"My grandfather held this concession for thirty-one years," I said. "My father died ferrying surveyors through water worse than this. They never once missed a season. And you're standing in their shrine telling me the arithmetic works better if we let it go."

"I'm standing here telling you what it will cost *me*." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it; I saw her hate it. "You want me to pretend the cost is only yours. It isn't. Belonging somewhere has a price, Tomas. I've spent twenty years making sure I never belonged anywhere long enough to get the bill."

"Then this is the bill."

"It's a debt I didn't sign!"

The words hit the stone and came back at her. She heard it too—heard whose sentence she'd borrowed, whose shape of life she'd just described. Inheritance is a debt you didn't sign. I had been carrying mine since I was nine years old, hauling rope on my father's boat, learning the river's moods like scripture. I never chose it. I never once got to choose it. And I would not trade it, not for clean exits, not for anything, because it was the only thing I had that couldn't be sold.

"Neither did I," I said quietly. "Neither did I sign. That's what inheritance is. You pay for people you never met, or you spend your whole life running from a bill that follows you anyway. You think your debt stays in Vantage's ledgers? It's already in you. You've been paying interest on it for twenty years, Lena. Every town you left early. Every season you skipped. Every mile."

She didn't answer. Outside, the rain worked the cliff face, patient as a clerk with a stamp.

* * *

We came down the stairs without speaking, and at the bottom she stopped, rain streaming off her hood, and looked at the river the way she looked at storm cells—reading structure, reading intent.

"The enforcer," she said. "Rask. He gave me until permits expire. Eleven days. If I don't deliver the salvage and the evidence, he takes the boat, the gear, and files the lien. And if I do deliver, he forgives the debt and I walk away whole and your family loses everything."

"Yes."

"And if I testify, I probably lose everything too. Just slower, and in public."

"Yes."

"So the only version where anyone wins is the one where we're faster than both of them." She turned, and under the hood her eyes had that flat bright quality I'd started to recognize—the one that arrived just before she did something reckless and correct. "The registry office is in Haran Kot. Three days' run downriver in flood conditions. Rask thinks I'm cowed. He thinks the water does his work for him now."

"He's not wrong about the water."

"No," she said. "But he's wrong about me. He priced me as a debtor. He forgot I'm a storm chaser. I've spent my whole life reading systems that want to kill me and finding the seam in them." She held out her hand—not for the packet, I noticed. For agreement. "Eleven days. One run. We go together or not at all, Tomas. I'm done calculating alone."

I looked at her hand. Rain fell between us and the river below carried half the gorge toward the sea, taking whatever wasn't anchored.

"If we do this," I said, "you don't get to be a stranger afterward. That's the price. You testify, you belong here—in the worst way, on the record, permanently. There's no clean exit after Haran Kot. Only seasons."

For the first time since I'd known her, Lena Marchetti hesitated longer than a heartbeat.

Then she shook my hand, and her grip was cold from the rain and steady as a mooring line, and above us the last light failed over the gorge, and somewhere downriver a raft lantern swung in the dark—Rask's men, moving early, eleven days suddenly feeling like none at all.
