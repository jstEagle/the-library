## 8. The Price of Staying Invisible

The enforcers came into Sarn Khol on a Tuesday, which Lena only knew because the ferry ledger in Tomas's boathouse was marked by days and she'd been counting them like a woman counting down a sentence.

Three men. Gray rain jackets, gray expressions. They didn't threaten anyone. They bought tea at the riverfront stall, paid too much for it, tipped the boy who served it, and asked—politely, the way Vantage always asked—whether anyone had seen the salvage contractor. The American woman. The one with the debt.

Lena watched them from the upper window of the Khol house, through a gap in the shutters that let in a blade of light and nothing else. One of them stood at the edge of the water and looked upriver for a long time, the way a man looks at something he already owns.

"They'll come here by Friday," she said.

Tomas didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing with her about the syndicate somewhere around the third day after the flood, when she'd finally told him everything—the contract, the debt, the number with all its zeros that Vantage held over her like a hook through the jaw. He'd listened without blinking, then said, *So you've been rowing away from it your whole life,* and gone back to bailing out his boat.

Now he said only: "The registry office closes the concession file in nine days. When the monsoon ends, the permits lapse. After that, whatever they claim is theirs."

"Then I leave before Friday."

He looked at her. In the dim boathouse light his face did what it always did—gave away almost nothing, except to someone who'd spent weeks learning the grammar of it. This look meant: *you're doing the thing again.*

"I can take you downriver," he said. "Past the narrows, past the checkpoint at Dzeli Bend—"

"No." She said it fast, before the offer could become something she wanted. "If they see my name next to yours on anything—a boat log, a witness statement—it buries the concession claim. Your family's case has to be clean. It has to be *mine*, alone, or it's contaminated."

"That's not how testimony works."

"It's how *Vantage* works," she said. "They don't need truth. They need a story. And 'the debtor and the ferryman conspired together' is a much better story than anything in those records."

Tomas was quiet for a while. Rain worked the tin roof like fingers testing a lock.

"You're protecting me," he said finally, "by making yourself bait."

"I'm protecting the evidence by being the only one carrying it."

"And yourself?"

She didn't answer that one, and he didn't make her, and that was somehow worse.

* * *

She left on Thursday night, two days early, because waiting felt like standing still in open water.

The plan was simple, and simplicity was its whole virtue. Take the survey records—not copies, the originals, because Vantage's lawyers would shred anything that could be called duplicate—sealed in waterproof film inside her dry bag. Run downriver alone in the leaky skiff, the small one, the one nobody would miss until morning. Make contact with the Vantage broker at Dzeli Bend before the enforcers swept that far. Trade: the originals for a signed release of her debt, witnessed, notarized, done. Then walk away from the gorge with nothing but her freedom and the particular emptiness that comes after selling something you can't get back.

She'd rehearsed the conversation on the drive north from Kansas, back when the cargo had been salvage and the buyer had been abstract. Now she rehearsed it again in the dark, paddling, and found the words kept changing shape. *I want out.* *This is just business.* *Nobody else gets hurt.* Each version sounded more like a lie told to a mirror.

The river helped. Rivers don't care what you tell yourself.

For three hours it went well, which should have been her first warning. The skiff rode the swollen current with its nose down, taking water faster than she could bail, but the channel was wide and forgiving and the rain fell straight instead of sideways. She passed the burned-out fish weir. She passed the shrine rock where the offerings sat drowned to their chins. She made six kilometers without seeing a single light.

Then the river narrowed, and the dark changed quality.

She knew this stretch. Tomas had walked her along the bank here a week ago, pointing with his chin, naming things the way other men named saints. *The Sleeper*—a submerged shelf that lay quiet in low water and became a standing wave in high. *The Teeth*—basalt fins that showed only as swirls, never as stone. *The Widow's Elbow*, where the current turned hard against the outside bank and ate boats.

In daylight, with Tomas calling the line, it had looked runnable. Barely.

Alone, at night, in a leaking skiff, in water two meters higher than it had been a week ago, it looked like a place where people stopped existing.

She eddied out behind a boulder to think, and the thinking went bad immediately, because every option required knowledge she didn't have. Where exactly was the Sleeper tonight? Had the Teeth moved their swirls with the extra meter of water? Was the Widow's Elbow even an elbow anymore, or had the flood opened some new throat she'd never been shown? The river had rewritten itself since her last lesson, and the only copy of the new edition lived in the head of a man she'd left sleeping on the bank forty minutes upstream.

That was the moment she understood the shape of her mistake, and it wasn't courage or cowardice or any of the words she'd been using. It was arithmetic. She had spent twenty years believing that self-sufficiency meant going alone, and going alone had worked everywhere the terrain was honest. But this river wasn't honest. This river kept its own records, and it only shared them with people who stayed long enough to learn the language.

She thought about turning back. She actually shipped the paddle and started the stroke.

Then the flashlight found her.

It came from the downstream point—a white beam swinging once across the water, deliberate, unhurried, the way you signal someone you already know is there. Behind it, half-hidden in the willows, sat a boat she recognized: the gray-hulled patrol skiff from the Vantage fleet, the kind the enforcers used, riding low with four men aboard.

Not chasing her. Waiting for her.

Her stomach dropped with a strange, cold clarity. They hadn't followed her from Sarn Khol. They'd *predicted* her. Of course they had. A debtor running alone at night toward the one bend with road access—that wasn't a pattern, it was a script, and Vantage had read it a hundred times. Every runner took the river. Every runner went alone, because runners were, by definition, people who trusted no one enough to bring them.

Her loneliness hadn't been a strategy. It had been a signature, and they'd signed her name for her.

The beam swung again. A voice carried across the water, mild and reasonable, the broker's voice she remembered from the contract signing in Wichita—*Ms. Marchetti, we should talk while the terms are still generous.*

Lena looked downstream at the light. She looked upstream at the dark she'd come from, where a man was probably awake by now, reading the empty mooring, knowing exactly what she'd done and why, and saying nothing, because that was who he was.

The skiff took another liter of water over the gunwale, as if voting.

She put her paddle in the water and pulled for the bank—not downstream, not upstream, but sideways, into the willow tangle below the shrine rock, dragging the skiff into the reeds until the hull ground against mud and the darkness closed over her like a hand. She crouched there, dry bag clutched to her chest, listening to the patrol skiff's engine idle at the bend, patient as interest accruing.

They couldn't search the whole bank tonight. But they owned the morning. They owned the road out of Dzeli Bend. They owned, in nine days, the entire legal meaning of the gorge.

And she owned a bag of paper that was worth nothing unless it reached a registry desk, through a flooded gorge, past four men in a gray boat, in less time than a monsoon needed to end.

There was exactly one person alive who knew the way through.

Lena pressed her forehead against the wet gunwale and laughed once, quietly, at the perfect economy of it. She'd run alone to keep him clean, and now the only way to save either of them was to go back and ask him to get dirty.

She waited for the engine note to shift, for the skiff to prowl off downriver toward the bend, and then she hauled the leaking boat back into the current and began the long pull upstream, against everything, toward the one light she'd left burning.
