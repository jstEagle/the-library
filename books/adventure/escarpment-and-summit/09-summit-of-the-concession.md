## 9. Summit of the Concession

**Lena**

The river had stopped pretending to be a river. It was a brown animal now, shoulder-deep in its own gorge, and it wanted the boat.

Lena stood at the bow with the survey evidence sealed in a dry bag strapped across her chest like a heart she'd finally agreed to carry on the outside. Below her feet, Tomas worked the tiller without hurry, reading water the way she read sky—by feel, by habit, by twenty years of mistakes he'd apparently already made so she wouldn't have to.

"Left channel," he said.

She looked left. The left channel was a wall of standing waves with something white and dead turning in the trough of them—a drowned buffalo, maybe, or a raft that had belonged to someone who hadn't read the water as well as Tomas Khol.

"Left channel," she repeated, because arguing was a luxury for people whose permits weren't expiring at sundown.

The registry office sat in the district town of Haran Kot, forty kilometers downriver, and it closed at six. It was five-forty in the morning. Vantage's enforcers had taken the road hours ago in two trucks, carrying a forged salvage claim with Lena's signature on it—a signature she'd never written but which was good enough, the smiling man had assured her, for any clerk who'd been paid not to look closely.

So they were running the gorge. In flood season. In a leaking boat held together with Tomas's father's caulking and Lena's last roll of repair tape.

"You can still put me ashore," Tomas said. "Above the narrows. Walk the ridge road, make Haran Kot by dark."

"And you?"

"I know this river." He didn't look up. "It knows me back. That's different from safe."

Lena checked the dry bag's straps for the fourth time. "Then we both go."

He nodded once, and gave the boat to the current.

* * *

**Tomas**

His grandfather had run this stretch with a wooden hull and no engine, and Tomas thought about him through the first rapid—not sentimentally, but technically, the way you think about anyone who did something hard before you understood how hard it was. The old man would have hated this water. He'd have run it anyway, because the concession papers in his chest pocket were the only inheritance a ferryman ever got, and inheritances don't wait for calm seasons.

The boat hit the first wave broadside and the world went white and loud. Lena threw her weight where he pointed—she'd learned fast, these weeks, learned to move when told instead of asking why—and the hull came around wet and screaming and alive.

They took three rapids like that. On the fourth, the river changed its mind mid-channel, a boil rising where the chart in his head said smooth water, and they went over the lip sideways into a hole that held them, turned them, showed Tomas the sky twice and the water once.

When they punched out, half the cargo was gone and his hands were bleeding where the tiller line had run.

"Lena." He said her name the way you check whether someone is still in the world.

"Here." She was soaked, gray-lipped, grinning like a woman who'd just seen God and found Him mediocre. "Bag's dry."

Of course it was. She'd have let the river take her before the bag.

"There's a shortcut," he said. "Past the temple bend. My family's water—it cuts ten kilometers. But the enforcers will be watching the takeout at Haran Kot, and if we come down the main channel they'll see us an hour out."

"So we take your water."

"It's not marked. It hasn't been run since—" He stopped. Since the concession was stripped. Since Vantage's clerks redrew maps that had belonged to his family for four generations, and the channels his grandfather had named fell off every official chart, became nobody's water, unrunnable by law.

Lena understood. He watched her understand—the small stillness in her face, the way she put one hand flat on the gunwale like she was swearing on it.

"Your water," she said. "Run it."

* * *

**Lena**

The temple bend channel was a rumor with rapids in it. They ran it blind on Tomas's memory, portaging once through leech-thick jungle while the rain came down like a verdict, and came out below the narrows at four in the afternoon with forty minutes of river left and the whole sky the color of a bruise going bad.

That was when Lena saw the trucks.

Not the trucks themselves—their dust, hanging over the ridge road above the final bend, moving toward town at the speed of men who believed they'd already won. And moored at the Haran Kot takeout, riding low and smug: the syndicate's big aluminum barge, the one from the mid-river camp, the polite enforcer's second raft. Men on it. Rifles slung casual, the way you carry a tool you've never had to use seriously.

"They'll board us at the landing," Tomas said quietly. "Take the bag. Take the papers. Take us, maybe, if the clerk has been paid enough not to notice two more disappearances."

Lena looked at the takeout, then upriver, then at the bag against her chest.

Every instinct she owned said what it had always said: *run*. Twenty years of running. Kansas hail, Gulf storms, a marriage she'd left in a parking lot with the engine still warm. There was always another road, another ticket, another clean exit, and the exits had all led here—to a leaking boat, a drowning debt, a man bleeding quietly beside her because she'd finally stopped taking the exit.

"No," she said. "We don't land there."

"Then where?"

"The registry has a river door. Old customs house, before the district moved inland. My salvage contract lists every legal access point—I read it forty times trying to find a loophole." She almost smiled. "First useful thing Vantage ever gave me."

Tomas stared at her, then laughed—one short, disbelieving bark—and swung the tiller. "Customs stairs are under the bridge pilings. Current will try to fold us against the stone."

"Current tries that everywhere."

"Not like my river does."

They ran the last bend together, and the bridge came down on them like a held breath, and the stone piling came up, and Tomas put the hull into a seam of slack water no wider than the boat itself, threading it while Lena lay flat over the evidence bag and prayed to nothing in particular. The stern kissed rock. The bow swung free. And then there were stairs—wet, ancient, half-drowned—and Tomas's hands on the painter, holding the boat against the current while she climbed.

"Go," he said. "I'll tie off and follow."

She went.

* * *

**Tomas**

The registry office smelled of ink and mildew and the particular despair of government buildings everywhere. One clerk behind a counter, one ceiling fan turning at the speed of resignation, and a wall clock that said 5:51.

Lena was already at the counter, dripping a puddle onto the floor, laying documents out with hands that shook only slightly—which Tomas recognized as her version of total composure.

"I'm filing testimony," she said, "and evidence, under the concession fraud provisions, section—" she recited a number she'd memorized somewhere between the temple bend and the narrows, "—and I'm withdrawing my salvage claim, contract number 4471-K, effective immediately."

The clerk looked at the papers. Looked at her. Looked past her, at the door, where boots were starting to sound on the stairs.

"The salvage claim is bonded," the clerk said carefully. "Vantage Consolidated holds the bond. Withdrawal requires—"

"Requires nothing." Lena's voice didn't rise; it dropped, which was worse. "Section twelve. Fraud vitiates the bond. You have the survey records in front of you. Read the concession numbers. Then tell me whose bond it is."

The clerk read. Outside, the boots reached the top of the stairs. A fist hit the river door—once, twice—and the old lock, built for customs officers and smugglers, held the way old things sometimes do out of pure spite.

"Sir," Tomas said, stepping forward, and putting his family's concession papers on the counter beside hers. Four generations of them. His grandfather's signature faded to a ghost. "These are ours. They were always ours. I'm filing too."

The clerk looked at the two of them—at the drowned rat American and the bleeding ferryman standing shoulder to shoulder in a puddle—and something in his face shifted, some long-buried reflex of a man who'd become a clerk instead of what he'd meant to be.

He stamped the withdrawal. He stamped the filings. He wrote a receipt.

The river door burst open on the third blow, and the polite enforcer came through it smiling, two men behind him, rain streaming off their shoulders onto the registry floor.

"You're too late," Lena said, and for the first time since Tomas had known her, she wasn't running, wasn't calculating, wasn't measuring the distance to any exit at all. She was just standing there, in the wreckage of her own escape plan, holding a receipt. "Filed at 5:53. Public record. Your forged claim died the moment mine did—and the survey evidence names every signature on the concession transfers. Including yours."

The smile stayed on the enforcer's face, but it went hollow, a mask over a man doing arithmetic and not liking the sum. Behind him, his men looked at each other. Contracts were dying in this room, Tomas realized—all of them, the whole web of them, every debt and bond and threat, unraveling from one stamped receipt outward.

"You'll be ruined," the enforcer said to Lena. Almost gently. "You understand that. No syndicate will touch you now."

"Yeah," Lena said. "I counted."

And Tomas watched her say it, watched her mean it, and understood that whatever she'd been fleeing for twenty years had just caught up with her—and she'd turned around and shaken its hand.

* * *

**Lena**

They walked out the front door of the registry into the last light of the monsoon day, past the enforcers who had suddenly, thoroughly, run out of jurisdiction. The rain had thinned to a drifting silver. Down at the landing, the syndicate barge sat emptying, its crew melting away into the town the way hired men do when the money stops being certain.

Tomas limped beside her, one hand pressed to his ribs, and said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, it was to ask the only question that mattered to him.

"The concession hearing. They'll want me to speak. My family's claim, in person."

"You'll be good at it," Lena said. "You're the only honest ferryman in the district. That plays well."

"And you? The testimony. They'll ask you about Vantage. About everything you carried for them." He glanced at her. "Miles, not years. That's what you told me. What happens when the miles stop?"

Lena looked downriver, where the gorge ran on toward country she'd never mapped, and felt the strangest sensation—not restlessness, not the itch of the next road, but something underneath it, patient and unfamiliar.

A season, maybe. Maybe longer.

"I don't know yet," she said, and discovered it was the truest thing she'd said in twenty years.

Behind them, in the registry window, the clerk turned off his lamp. Somewhere upstream, the survey team's survivors were walking home. And in Lena's chest, where the debt had lived for so long she'd mistaken it for a heartbeat, there was—for the first time she could remember—nothing at all.

Just room. Just the sound of the river, going on without her permission, whether she stayed or not.

She found she was already thinking about staying.
