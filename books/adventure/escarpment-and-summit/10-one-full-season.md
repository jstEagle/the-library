## 10. One Full Season

The gorge went quiet the way a room goes quiet after an argument—suddenly, and with everyone pretending it hadn't been shouting for weeks.

Lena noticed it first as sound. Or rather, the absence of it. For two months the Kalyet had been a constant animal presence, a low roar that lived in her sternum even when she slept, and now, standing on Tomas's landing at dawn, she could hear individual things again: water over stone, a kingfisher somewhere upstream, the creak of the boat hull drying in the sun. The river had dropped six feet. It had left a brown tideline on the pilings like a mark on a doorframe, measuring how high it had climbed, how far it had fallen.

She'd spent twenty years learning to read skies. She was only now learning to read seasons.

The news had come downriver three days ago, carried by a supply runner who poled his way into Sarn Khol with the smugness of a man delivering a lottery result. The survey team—four of them, minus the one who'd broken his ankle on the escarpment trail—had been found alive at a mission station two hundred kilometers upstream, thin and bearded and furious in equal measure. They'd walked out of the gorge the night the enforcers came, gone up instead of down because going down meant passing Vantage's checkpoint, and they'd spent the monsoon eating mission rice and writing affidavits. The runner said the youngest one, a hydrologist named Okafor, had asked specifically whether "the American salvage contractor" had filed the evidence properly.

Lena had laughed when she heard that. Filed it properly. She'd stood in the registry office with riverwater still dripping off her, hands shaking so badly the clerk had to steady the page, and read her testimony aloud twice because the first time her voice cracked on Tomas's family name. Properly was a generous word for it. But it had held. That was the thing about truth done badly—it still worked, if you did all of it.

Vantage's regional office had closed within nine days. The concession fraud unraveled the way rotten rope does, not cut but simply giving out, strand by strand, until the whole load dropped. Her contract—the predatory, mile-deep salvage debt that had brought her to this gorge in the first place—died with it. A clerk in Sarn Khol had stamped a single line in a ledger: *obligation void, counterparty dissolved.* Lena had stared at the stamp for a long time. Twenty years of running, priced at one ink impression.

She kept expecting to feel lighter. Mostly she felt strange, like a leg waking up after too long asleep.

* * *

Tomas's boat lay on its side on the shingle above the landing, propped on logs, hull open to the sky like something undergoing surgery.

It was worse than she'd remembered. The flash flood had done most of it—tore a plank loose along the starboard strake, sprung three seams, cracked the transom where the boat had swung broadside into a deadfall—but the rest was age and neglect and the particular despair of a man ferrying other people's cargo in a vessel he couldn't afford to fix. When Lena peeled back the first layer of old caulking, it came away in gray ribbons, more chalk than pitch.

"You don't have to do this," Tomas said, for the fourth time that week. He sat on an overturned crate nearby, mending net, his hands moving through the work without his attention. He'd said it every day since she'd dragged the boat up the bank herself, and every day he said it a little less like a refusal and a little more like a question he wanted answered differently.

"I know," she said, and drove a seam ripper under the next plank.

He watched her work for a while. He was good at watching; it was most of what he did, and he did it without making you feel measured. After a time he set down the net and came to crouch at the gunwale, running a thumb along the wood where the flood line stained it.

"My grandfather built this hull," he said. "My father re-planked her twice. I always thought the third time would be mine."

"It's yours now," Lena said. "Concession came through Tuesday. The whole reach, upstream to the falls. That's your boatyard if you want it."

"That's paperwork." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it heavier. "Paperwork is what they used to take it."

"Then make it something else." She tapped the opened hull. "Start here."

He didn't answer, but that evening he showed up at the boat with his grandfather's caulking irons wrapped in oilcloth, tools older than either of them, and he didn't say anything about it, and neither did she. Some agreements are better unsigned.

* * *

The repair took eleven days, and Lena learned that a boat is just a slow argument between wood and water, and that caulking is the art of losing gracefully. She learned the mallet rhythm Tomas's grandfather had taught him—two light, one heavy—and found that her chase-chaser's hands, calibrated for radar screens and hail-dented steel, could learn it too. By the fifth day she stopped flinching when the mallet slipped. By the eighth she could hear a bad seam before she saw it.

They talked, in the way people talk when their hands are busy and there's nowhere either of them is going. He told her about his mother, who had run the ferry through four monsoons alone while his father worked the far camps. She told him about Kansas—not the storms themselves, he didn't need the drama, but the waiting, the long flat hours of a chase crew sitting in a parking lot in Hugoton watching a sky refuse to break. He told her the gorge had those hours too, between rains. She said she'd never stayed anywhere long enough to find out. He said nothing to that, just handed her the pitch pot, and she understood that he'd heard the whole confession inside it.

On the ninth day, Okafor himself came down from the mission road—a lean, sunburnt man with a walking stick and a government envelope he handled like live ordnance. He stood on the shingle looking at the overturned boat and at Lena elbow-deep in its hull, and he laughed once, short and disbelieving.

"They told me you'd filed the evidence," he said. "I didn't believe them. Government contractors don't burn their own contracts."

"This one did."

"Why?"

Lena thought about giving him the clean answer—the right thing, the decent thing, the answer that would look good in the inquiry report he was clearly drafting in his head. Instead she said: "Because a man pulled me out of the water, and the water was my own fault. And because I've spent twenty years measuring everything in miles, and miles don't add up to anything. You can't cash them in. You just die farther from where you started."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment, then at Tomas, who had not looked up from his work and did not intend to. Then he nodded, once, the way one professional acknowledges another, and left the envelope anyway—it was a certified copy of the restored concession, for the Khols' records, since the registry's own copy might take years to travel—and went back up the mission road whistling something tuneless and glad.

* * *

The last plank went in on a morning so clear the escarpment looked close enough to touch. They turned the boat upright together, four weeks of work balanced on two sets of shoulders, and set her on the shingle, and she sat there in the early light looking exactly like what she was: old wood, new seams, seaworthy.

Tomas ran his hand along the transom where the crack had been. The scar would always show—a pale seam in the dark grain, like a healed bone.

"She'll leak a little," he said. "First season, any new caulk does. You bail her and she tightens up."

"Everything does," Lena said, "if you stay long enough."

He glanced at her then, quick, the way he'd glanced at her the night she'd come back from the failed solo run, soaked and empty-handed and finally honest. He didn't ask the question. He never asked the questions that mattered; he just made room for the answers.

But she gave it anyway, because she'd learned that from him too.

"There was a flight out of Sarn Khol Thursday," she said. "Charter, heading south, half-empty. I could have been on it. I stood at the airstrip for an hour."

"What stopped you?"

"The pilot asked where I was headed and I realized I didn't have an answer that wasn't just *away*." She picked up a handful of shingle, let it run out through her fingers. "Twenty years of *away*. I'm tired of being a direction."

The river moved past them, lower now, gentler, carrying the season downstream toward some delta she'd never see. Above the far bank the escarpment caught the sun, and above the escarpment the summit stood clear for the first time in months, snowless and blue and patient.

"So what do you measure in now?" Tomas asked quietly.

Lena looked at the boat, and the landing, and the tideline on the pilings marking how high the water had risen and how far it had fallen, and she found the answer was already there, had maybe been there since the night she'd burned her records in a stove on the wrong side of the gorge.

"Seasons," she said. "One full season, to start."

Tomas nodded slowly, and picked up the caulking mallet, and turned it handle-first toward her—an offer, or a test, or simply the next task, which in his language were often the same thing.

"Then we'd better get her in the water," he said. "Monsoon comes back in eight months, and the ferry doesn't run itself."

Lena took the mallet. Downstream, around the first bend, she could hear the river practicing its roar again, quiet for now, gathering. There would be storms. There would always be storms; she'd bet her life on that fact for twenty years and it had never once let her down. But for the first time she wasn't chasing one, and wasn't fleeing one either.

She was going to be here when it arrived.

* * *

That evening, a runner came down the mission road with a letter—official paper, a government seal, and a request that made Lena read it twice by lamplight: the inquiry board, sitting in the provincial capital, wished to hear the testimony of the contractor Lena Marchetti in person. Travel provided. Return passage guaranteed.

Return passage. She turned the phrase over like a coin she no longer recognized.

Tomas stood in the doorway of the shrine house behind her, saying nothing, watching the lamp flame bend.

"When?" she asked the runner.

"Next month," he said. "Before the dry season ends."

Lena folded the letter along its crease, carefully, and set it on the table, and looked out past the landing to where the boat rode at anchor on the darkening water, riding easy, holding.

One full season. She'd promised herself one full season.

She smiled, and reached for a pen.
