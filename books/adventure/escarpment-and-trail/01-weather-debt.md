## 1. Weather Debt

The supercell had been building since noon, stacking itself against the escarpment like a debt coming due.

Astrid Vane watched it from the shoulder of Route 9, one boot on the truck's bumper, notebook open against her knee. The cloud had that green-black bruise at its base she'd learned to read the way a banker reads a signature — the color of hail, of rotation, of money in motion. She drew its position on the gridded page: forty miles southwest, drifting northeast at twenty-two knots. Then, beneath it, without any sense of contradiction, she wrote down the numbers that actually governed her life.

*Balance remaining: $14,207.33.*
*Interest accrued this month: $311.60.*
*Days until Meridian's deadline: 19.*

Two ledgers, one notebook. The storm and the Syndicate. She'd never thought much about what it said about her that they shared the same pages, that she tracked a wall cloud's movement with the same pencil she used to calculate how many chase seasons stood between her and solvency. Both were things that grew while she slept. Both were things that would find her no matter how far she drove.

The wind came up the escarpment face in long, warm gusts, smelling of dust and sage and the ozone promise of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Somewhere below the rim, the river ran through country she couldn't see — canyon country, forbidden country, the kind of place that showed up on maps with blank spaces where towns should be. She didn't know yet that the river was going to matter. Nobody knew that yet, least of all her.

She got back in the truck, keyed the ignition, and let the radar software spin up on the laptop bolted to the passenger seat. The hook echo was textbook. A supercell like this could track along the escarpment for three hours, dropping hail the size of fists on anything foolish enough to stand still.

Foolish enough to stand still. That was the whole religion of her life, condensed into six words. She'd built it without meaning to, the way you build any creed — one small cowardice at a time, repeated until it looked like conviction.

Astrid Vane did not stop. Stopping was where the collectors caught up. Stopping was where the phone calls found her, where the letters with the cheerful letterhead piled up under the wiper blades, where the arithmetic in the back of the notebook finally demanded to be read aloud. As long as she was moving — chasing the storm east, then north, then doubling back when the cell died over the flats — she was a moving target for everything: the weather, the interest, the men from the Meridian Syndicate who smiled like insurance salesmen and broke fingers like carpenters driving nails.

She pulled onto the highway and fell in behind the storm.

* * *

For two hours she chased it the way other people pray — attentively, hungrily, with a low constant hum of dread underneath. The escarpment unrolled beside her, a rampart of red rock running north-south for a hundred miles, and the storm ran parallel to it like a beast pacing its cage. Lightning walked down out of the anvil in slow white strokes. Hail began at 6:40, rattling off the truck's roof hard enough to leave dents she'd stop noticing by July.

She loved this part. She needed to be honest with herself about that, at least. There was a version of the story where she chased storms purely for the money — storm data sold to insurers, footage sold to news desks, a few hundred dollars a cell if you were positioned right — and that version was true as far as it went. But there was another version underneath, and it was this: inside a storm, everything made sense. Cause and effect. Pressure gradients and inflow boundaries. A storm never lied to her about what it wanted. It wanted to organize, to rotate, to drop its load, and then it wanted to die, and it did all of those things openly, on a schedule she could read in the reflectivity returns.

People were different. People smiled. The Meridian Syndicate smiled more than anyone she'd ever met.

Their first contact had come eighteen months ago, after her mother's second surgery, when the hospital billing office had introduced her — with genuine warmth, the way you introduce old friends — to a "flexible financing partner." Meridian Lending Group, the paperwork said. Flexible was the operative word. Flexible rates. Flexible terms. Flexible definitions of what happened to people who fell behind. She'd signed because her mother was dying and the alternative was watching it happen in a hallway, and she'd told herself she understood the contract, and maybe she had, in the way you understand a storm chart — every symbol legible, the whole system's intent invisible until it's on top of you.

Now the balance grew $311 a month whether she paid or not, and the payments she could make mostly went to interest, and the deadline moved whenever Meridian decided it should move, always closer, never farther. They called it *recalibrating your commitment.* They said it warmly.

At 7:15 the cell began to gust out, its rotation unraveling into straight-line wind and ordinary rain, and the show was over. Astrid sat in the truck on a gravel pullout with the engine ticking and the wipers slapping, and she opened the notebook and did the thing she always did after a chase: she counted.

Footage: usable, maybe $180 from the regional affiliate. Data package: $75 if the insurer's broker was in a good mood. Fuel burned: $61. New hail dents: whatever they cost her at trade-in, someday, if there was ever a someday.

Net for the day: somewhere south of two hundred dollars. Against a debt growing faster than she could shrink it. She closed the notebook and pressed her thumb against the cover as if she could hold the numbers shut.

Nineteen days. In nineteen days, unless something changed, the collectors would recalibrate her again, and the last time they'd recalibrated, a man named Denny had held her hand flat on the hood of her own truck and explained, smiling, what happens to hands that sign contracts and forget them. He hadn't broken anything. He'd just let her believe he might, which was the Syndicate's real product. Not violence. The *availability* of violence, priced into every payment plan.

She started the engine. There was a gas station forty miles east, at the junction where Route 9 met the river road, and it had diesel, bad coffee, and a gravel lot behind the propane tanks where nobody bothered anyone. She'd sleep there. Four hours, maybe five. Then chase the next system, sell the next clip, shave another hundred dollars off a number that wouldn't notice.

That was the plan. Plans were how she kept the fear in its cage.

* * *

The Junction station sat alone where the escarpment finally gave up and rolled down into river country, its single pump lit by one buzzing fluorescent tube and its windows glowing the tired yellow of places that stay open because closing would mean admitting there's nothing here. A hand-painted sign promised GAS • DIESEL • ICE • BAIT. The BAIT had been crossed out and rewritten twice, each time in a different hand, as though ownership itself kept washing out with the floods.

Astrid filled the tank, bought coffee that tasted like hot cardboard, and asked the kid behind the counter — sixteen, maybe, doing homework under the register light — whether the lot out back was okay to park in overnight.

"Nobody cares," the kid said, not looking up. "Sheriff comes through sometimes. He doesn't care either."

"Perfect," Astrid said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

She parked behind the propane cages with the nose of the truck pointed at the road — pointed *away*, always away, so that waking up meant driving, no turning around required. She set her phone alarm for 4:30. She ate half a bag of gas-station peanuts for dinner and told herself it was fine, she'd eat properly tomorrow, she'd do a lot of things tomorrow.

Sleep came the way it always did for her: reluctantly, in shallow installments, with one ear tuned to the parking lot. She dreamed in fragments — the green-black base of the cloud, the ledger page, Denny's smile, her mother's voice saying *you work too hard, Astrid, you have to let some things go* — and woke at 2:10 to headlights sweeping across the propane cage.

Not the sheriff. The sheriff drove a county pickup with a light bar and left it running. These were two black sedans, parked neatly side by side, and the men who got out of them did so unhurried, the way people get out of cars when they own the night and know it.

There were three of them. Astrid knew two by sight. The big one was Denny — Denny Okafor, though nobody at Meridian used last names, last names being for lawsuits — broad and soft-looking in a way that concealed exactly nothing, his smile already in place before his feet hit the gravel. The thin one was Pell, who carried a leather portfolio everywhere and handled documents like a priest handling relics, gently and with total confidence in their power. The third was new to her: a woman, mid-forties, gray at the temples, wearing a field jacket that fit too well to be cheap. She hung back by the sedans with her hands in her pockets, watching, and something about her stillness made Astrid's stomach drop lower than Denny's grin ever had.

Astrid sat up in the driver's seat and turned the key halfway, just enough to wake the dash lights. Engine off. For now.

Denny rapped once on the glass, a friendly little knock, knuckles only. "Miss Vane. You awake in there? Course you are. You're always awake. That's what we love about you."

She rolled the window down four inches. Night air came in smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet. "It's two in the morning."

"It is." Denny leaned his forearm on the door frame, comfortable, proprietary. "And we apologize for the hour. Truly. But we've been looking for you across three counties, and when we heard a storm chaser was working the escarpment tonight, well." He spread his hands, delighted. "You're kind of famous, Miss Vane. Makes you easy to find. That's a compliment."

"What do you want, Denny?"

"Just talk. Pell?"

Pell stepped forward and opened the portfolio with a small ceremonial care. "Ms. Vane. On behalf of Meridian Lending Group, I'm pleased to inform you that your account has been reviewed, and your commitment has been recalibrated." He slid a paper through the window gap. She took it because not taking it wasn't really an option. "In recognition of your consistent partial payments — and we do appreciate them, we say so in the file — the board has authorized an extension of your deadline."

Astrid looked at the page. It took her a moment to find the trick, and then she found it, and it was beautiful, the way a hook echo is beautiful. The deadline had moved from nineteen days to thirty-one days. And the monthly accrual had moved from $311.60 to $428.90. They'd given her more time and charged her for it, retroactively, and the new interest rate meant that in thirty-one days she'd owe more than she owed tonight.

"You extended my deadline and raised my balance," she said.

"We *restructured*," Denny corrected warmly. "Words matter, Miss Vane. You of all people should appreciate that — you write such nice notes in your little book." His eyes flicked to the notebook on the dashboard, and something cold went through Astrid's chest. He couldn't have seen the numbers from out there. But he knew it was there. They always knew what you kept closest.

"The woman by the car," Astrid said. "Who is she?"

Denny's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a hairline shift, like ice settling on a lake. "That's Ms. Voss. She's new with us. Regional. She likes to observe." He lowered his voice to a confiding register, man-to-man, almost tender. "Here's the honest talk, Astrid. Can I call you Astrid? The honest talk is, everybody at Meridian is rooting for you. Genuinely. You pay. Most people don't even pay. But the math is the math, and the math says you can't outrun it. So somebody upstairs had an idea." He tapped the paper in her hand. "Thirty-one days. And in that time, if you could find yourself a lump sum — even a partial one, even ten thousand — the board would look very favorably on rewriting the whole structure. Fresh start. Clean slate. We love a clean slate."

"And if I don't find ten thousand dollars in a month?"

Denny sighed, and the sigh was the most honest sound he'd made all night. "Then Ms. Voss takes over the account. And Ms. Voss does things differently than I do." He straightened up, and the friendliness snapped off him like a breaker tripping, clean and instant. "Recalibration complete. Pleasure as always. Get some sleep — you've got storms to catch."

The sedans pulled out onto the river road and their taillights shrank toward the junction and were gone. From the dark by the second sedan, just before the turn, the woman — Voss — had looked back at the truck once. Not smiling. Taking a measurement.

* * *

Astrid sat in the cab for a long time after they left, the recalibration notice lying on the passenger seat like something dead.

She should sleep. She had four hours. She had a chase day tomorrow, footage to cut, a broker to call. Instead she picked up the notebook and flipped to the ledger page and stared at the numbers until they blurred, and then she did the only thing that had ever reliably quieted the noise in her chest.

She started the engine and drove.

Not toward anything. Away. The river road ran east along the bottomlands, black and empty, the escarpment a darker mass against the stars to her left, and she drove it at sixty with the windows cracked and the radio off, letting the motion do what motion always did for her: dissolve the present into pure forward, no address, no account, no name attached to a file in a Meridian office. This was the truth she'd never said out loud, the one under all the invoices and the storm footage and the careful plans: she wasn't running from the debt. The debt was just the shape the running wore. She was running from the version of herself that existed when she stopped — the one sitting still in a parking lot at 2 a.m., addable, locatable, someone whose whole life fit on a single page of a stranger's portfolio. Stopping was where she became a number. Moving was where she stayed a rumor.

The problem, and she knew it, and knew it the way you know a storm has hooked when the rain starts coming from the wrong direction — the problem was that rumors don't earn. Rumors don't pay down $428.90 a month. Every mile she drove tonight was a mile she'd have to buy back with footage and hail dents and bad coffee, and she drove it anyway, because the alternative was lying in the dark listening to her own heart count.

Near dawn the land changed. The road bent north away from the river, climbing toward a line of bluffs, and the sky ahead went the bruised gray-green she knew better than any color in the world. A new system, building over the high country, already towering. Her hands tightened on the wheel with something that wasn't quite hope and wasn't quite hunger but lived in the same neighborhood.

She pulled over at a flooded stretch where runoff sheeted across the asphalt, brown water moving fast over a dip in the road, and stopped to check the depth — habit, professionalism, the small disciplines that kept chasers alive. Through the open window she heard the water rushing, and beneath it, faintly, from somewhere downstream around the bend, another sound. A voice. Weak, rhythmic, human.

Someone was calling for help in the floodwater.

Astrid Vane, who did not stop, who did not detour, who had a storm building on the horizon and a recalibrated deadline in her glovebox and nineteen — no, thirty-one days, thank you, Meridian, bless your hearts — sat with her foot on the brake and her eyes on the rising water, and felt the old dread and something newer, sharper, unfamiliar, fighting for the wheel.

She put the truck in gear and turned toward the sound.
