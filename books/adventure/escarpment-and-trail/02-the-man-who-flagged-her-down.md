## 2. The Man Who Flagged Her Down

The rain came down like it had a grudge.

Astrid drove with her headlights cutting cones through the dark, wipers slapping at a rhythm that had stopped meaning anything an hour ago. The back road out of the escarpment country wasn't on her map — she'd found it the way she found most things, by taking a wrong turn and deciding to call it instinct. Now the wrong turn was filling with water. Brown water sheeted across the gravel, running downhill in long braided ribbons that caught her lights and threw them back broken.

She should have stopped. Any sensible person would have stopped. But Astrid Vane had a rule about stopping, and the rule was: don't. Stopping was where the interest compounded. Stopping was where the collectors found you. Stopping was where you sat still long enough to hear yourself think, and thinking, for Astrid, mostly meant counting.

Fourteen thousand, three hundred and change. That was what remained of the loan she'd taken from the Meridian Syndicate at twenty-two, when she'd been young enough to believe that a storm-chasing rig and a good camera could outrun arithmetic. Six years of payments, six years of recalculated deadlines, six years of cheerful men in clean shirts explaining — always smiling, always apologetic — how the numbers had shifted against her. She kept the ledger in the same notebook where she tracked barometric pressure and storm cells, because debt and weather were the same thing really: systems that built pressure somewhere out of sight until they broke over your head.

The truck's tires hissed through standing water. She was doing maybe thirty-five, which was stupid, but the alternative was slowing down, and slowing down felt too much like stopping.

That was when she saw him.

At first she took him for a fence post, or a fallen branch — something pale and angled half out of the flooded ditch at the road's edge. Then the branch moved. It raised one arm, weakly, against the glare of her high beams, and Astrid stood on the brakes so hard the trailer behind her fishtailed and kissed the guardrail.

She sat there with the engine idling and the rain hammering the roof, and she told herself every reasonable thing. He's drunk. It's a trap. People don't lie in ditches on flooded roads at midnight unless they want something from you, and everything anyone wants from you costs money you don't have.

But he'd raised his arm like a man signaling a train. Like someone who'd been lying there a long time, watching headlights pass, and had decided this one was worth dying for if it didn't stop.

Astrid swore, put the truck in park, and got out into the rain.

* * *

He was older than she'd expected, sixty maybe, with a face gone gray under the tan and a white beard plastered flat against his throat. His left leg was bent wrong below the knee, and the water in the ditch was pink around him, diluting by the minute into nothing. There was a hole in his side, low, just above the hip — not gushing anymore, which meant either it had clotted or he'd already lost most of what there was to lose.

"Shot me," he said, before she'd even crouched down. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Two of them. Upstream. Took my mule and most of my gear." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a wet cough. "Left me the map, though. Too dumb to know what they had."

"I'm calling this in," Astrid said, already reaching for the radio. "There's a ranger station—"

"No." His hand shot out and closed around her wrist with a strength that surprised them both. His fingers were cold as creek stones. "No rangers. No sheriff. You hear me? They'll find me, and then they'll find whoever I talked to."

"That's not how it works."

"That's exactly how it works." He pulled himself up against the ditch bank, and Astrid saw the effort cost him something permanent. "You're a chaser. I've seen your rig at the Crossroads station. You go where the weather goes and you don't stay anywhere." He said it without judgment, the way a man describes a river. "Good. Good. That's what I need."

"I need," Astrid said, "to get you to a hospital."

"The hospital is ninety miles that way and I'm not going to make thirty." He said it flatly, the way he might have reported a gauge reading. No self-pity in it. Just data. "So we're going to skip past the part where you argue with me, because I've only got so much breath and I intend to spend all of it talking."

Lightning flickered away over the escarpment, silent at this distance, a white pulse inside the cloud. Astrid found herself looking at it the way other people look at a clock.

"Elias Thorn," the man said. "Prospector. And before you ask — yes, that Thorn. If you've heard the name, you've heard it wrong."

She hadn't heard the name at all. But she nodded anyway, because he seemed to need her to.

"My truck's fifty feet up the road," she said. "I can get you into the cab. Out of the water. Will you let me do that much?"

Thorn considered her. Rain ran off the brim of some hat he no longer wore; his hair was flat and streaming. In the lightning-flash she saw his eyes clearly for the first time — pale, sharp, the eyes of a man who'd spent forty years reading ground the way she read sky.

"You're afraid of me," he said. "Not of me. Of this. Of being tied to somebody."

"Don't flatter yourself."

"It's not flattery. It's diagnosis." He let her take his weight anyway, arm over her shoulders, and together they made the worst fifty feet of progress either of them had ever managed, sliding and stumbling through the flood until she got the passenger door open and hauled him up into the cab. He collapsed against the seat with a sound that was almost relief, and Astrid climbed in after, soaked to the skin, engine running, heater blasting cold air that would take ten minutes to warm.

She should drive. She should drive to the ranger station and dump this problem on people paid to have problems dumped on them.

She put the truck in gear and drove nowhere — just up the road a quarter mile to a gravel pullout under a stand of cottonwoods, and stopped. Because he was bleeding again, and because driving with a dying man beside her felt worse than sitting still with one, and because — though she'd never have admitted it — she wanted to hear what he had to say.

* * *

He talked for two hours.

He talked in pieces, between the bad moments, when the pain receded enough to let words through. He talked about silver — a lode, a real one, not a rumor, something he'd found years ago and hidden so well that even the men who'd shot him tonight hadn't known where to dig. He talked about partners who'd become creditors who'd become hunters. He talked about a company called Meridian, and Astrid's stomach dropped when he said the name, because of course. Of course. The world was small and the Syndicate's fingers were in everything, smiling all the way down.

"They fund the claims," Thorn said. "They fund the permits. They fund the men with rifles. Same smile everywhere you go. You know the smile."

"I know the smile," Astrid said quietly.

Thorn turned his head and looked at her, really looked, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly. "Yeah. Yeah, you do." He fumbled one-handed at his jacket, at an inner pocket, and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. It was smaller than a paperback book. He held it in both hands like it weighed forty pounds. "This is the map. This is the whole thing — where the source is, where the lode runs, where the assay samples are cached. Everything those men upstairs are going to spend the rest of their lives trying to get."

"Then why keep it?"

"Because of what it does if it gets out." His breathing was getting shorter. Astrid noticed she'd started timing his breaths without meaning to, the way she timed lightning strikes. "There's a valley downstream. Kestrel's Ford. Farms, a ferry, about four hundred people who'd fight a strip mine with deer rifles if it came to that, and lose, because Meridian owns the judges. The permit dies if nobody can prove where the ore is. This map is the proof. As long as it stays lost, the valley stays whole."

"So burn it," Astrid said. "You've got a lighter. Problem solved."

"Burn it and the knowledge dies with me, sure — except it doesn't, because Halloran and his boys already know enough to keep digging, year after year, decade after decade. And my daughter—" His voice caught, and he coughed, and pressed a hand hard against his side. "My daughter lives in that valley. Wren. Wren Thorn. Runs the ferry crossing, these days." Something moved through his ruined face that Astrid recognized and hated recognizing: love, arriving far too late to be useful. "I haven't seen her since she was nine years old. I walked out. Chose to walk out, so the claim-jumpers would follow me instead of following the ground under her town. Twenty-six years of being followed." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Astrid had seen all night, and she'd watched a man bleed out in a ditch. "Worked, too. Right up until it didn't."

The rain eased a little. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the canyon walls like furniture being moved.

"So here's the trade," Thorn said. "The map, for a promise. You take it to Kestrel's Ford. You put it in Wren Thorn's hands. You tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her whatever you think she can stand. That's your call. I'm not going to be there to see how it lands."

"No," Astrid said.

Thorn blinked. "No?"

"I don't make promises. Promises are debts, and I'm carrying all the debt I can afford." She gripped the wheel with both hands, staring through the rain-blurred windshield at nothing. "Take the map to a ranger. Take it to a lawyer. Take it to anybody whose life isn't already mortgaged."

"To a ranger," Thorn repeated. "To a lawyer." He laughed softly, and this time the laugh didn't turn into a cough, which somehow made it worse. "Miss Vane. Look at me."

She looked at him.

"I've got maybe an hour. Maybe two. Every minute you sit here arguing with a dead man is a minute closer to Halloran's crew finding this truck, and they will find it, because they found mine, and they are patient men with good dogs. You can drive away right now. I'd even say I understand." His pale eyes held hers, steady as surveyor's marks. "But you stopped. In the rain, on a flooded road, with every reason not to. A woman who truly never promises anything would've kept driving. So the question isn't whether you're the kind of person who makes promises. The question is whether you're the kind who keeps them. And I won't know the answer to that, on account of I'll be dead."

"That's a hell of a sales pitch."

"I prospected for forty years," Elias Thorn said. "I know ore when I see it."

Astrid sat with that. The heater had finally warmed; the cab smelled of wet wool and blood and old coffee. Outside, the cottonwoods thrashed. Her notebook sat in the door pocket, fourteen thousand dollars deep, and she thought about how every promise she'd ever witnessed had been a contract with teeth in it, and how this one had teeth too, but for once the teeth were pointed at her own cowardice instead of her throat.

"I'm not staying for a funeral," she said finally. "I'm not staying for the sheriff. I deliver the map, and I leave. That's the deal. No promises beyond that."

"That's the deal," Thorn agreed.

And then, because he was dying and because she was weak in exactly the way he'd diagnosed, she heard herself say the rest of it — heard the words come out of her own mouth like they belonged to a stranger:

"I promise. I'll get it to her."

Thorn closed his eyes. For a moment she thought he'd gone, and panic rose in her throat, absurd and enormous. Then he exhaled, long and slow, and said, "There. Was that so hard?" and had the strength left to grin at her.

* * *

He lasted until a little after four in the morning.

Astrid spent those hours doing the things you do for the dying because there is nothing else to do. She made him drink water. She packed the wound with the gauze from her first-aid kit, knowing it was theater. She listened while he talked about Wren — how she'd looked at nine, gap-toothed and furious, throwing rocks at the river; how her mother had died when she was six; how he'd sent money for a few years through a banker in the Ford until the banker stopped answering, and he'd told himself that meant she was provided for, and had known even then he was lying to himself.

"She'll hate me," he said, near the end. Clear-eyed now, the way people sometimes get. "That's fine. Hate's honest. What I can't stand is her never knowing why."

"Then write it down," Astrid said. "Write it down and I'll give her that too."

He wrote it badly, propping the notebook on his knees, four lines in a shaking hand that she would later memorize without trying. Then he dictated where the assay samples were cached — Miller's Bend, he said, under the third headstone in the ghost camp's deadfall — and made her repeat it back twice.

Near the end he stopped talking about business. He looked out the windshield at the fading rain and said, "Storm's breaking up. Moving east. You'd know."

"I'd know," Astrid agreed.

"Good night for it, though," he murmured. "River'll be up. River's always up when things change." His voice was getting thinner, stretched over the words like ice over moving water. "You take care of my girl. Not the map. The girl. The map's just paper."

"You said the deal was the map."

"I lied," Elias Thorn said. "Dying men get to." And he closed his eyes, and this time he did not open them, and his breathing went from labored to shallow to spaced to still with the patience of all natural processes, and Astrid sat in the warming cab of her truck holding the hand of a stranger until it grew cold, watching the eastern sky go from black to iron to a thin, mean gray.

* * *

She buried him at dawn, because she couldn't not.

The ground was soft from the rain, which was the only mercy in it. She dug with the folding shovel from her gear kit, in the high ground above the ditch under the cottonwoods, and it took her over an hour and she cried the whole time, silently, furiously, furious at herself for crying, furious at him for dying in her truck, furious at the world for arranging itself so that the first person to ask her for anything honest in six years had asked her for the one thing she couldn't afford to give.

She wrapped him in the tarp from her bedroll because she couldn't dig deep enough not to. She stacked stones over the grave because she'd read once that it kept animals off, and because stones were what you did. She stood over it with her hat off, feeling ridiculous and grief-stricken and exposed, and said, "I don't even know your middle name," and then, "I'll get it to her," and then nothing, because there was nothing else, and the river below the pullout ran high and brown and fast with all the night's rain, heading somewhere without any choice in the matter.

Back in the cab, she unwrapped the oilcloth bundle with hands that wouldn't quite steady.

Inside was the map — hand-drawn, dense with contour lines and annotations in Thorn's cramped engineer's script, the river rendered in blue ink from its mouth all the way up to a place marked simply SOURCE, ringed twice. And folded behind it, which he hadn't mentioned, a second document: pages of columns, dates, initials, amounts. A ledger. She read the first page standing in the cab with the door open and the morning birds starting up, and her blood went cold in the warm air, because the names in the margin weren't just Halloran's crew.

Meridian was written there too. Dates. Disbursements. A rigged claim, bought and paid for, with the Syndicate's cheerful fingerprints on every line.

Astrid closed the ledger and sat very still.

Somewhere south, a gas station clerk knew her rig. Somewhere upstream, two men with rifles and a patient boss were working their way downriver toward a body that wasn't where they'd left it. And somewhere east, in a valley town she'd never seen, a woman named Wren Thorn was waking up to a ferry rope and a morning shift, not knowing yet that her father had come back to her the only way he could.

Astrid started the truck. Her notebook lay in the door pocket, weather and debt tangled together in her cramped handwriting, and she understood suddenly, with total clarity, that the numbers had changed — that the promise she'd made in the dark had reset every deadline she had.

She pointed the truck west, toward the valley, and drove.
