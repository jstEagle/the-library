## 3. Terms of Inheritance

The rain had stopped by the time Elias Thorn died, which felt like a courtesy the sky had extended to him alone.

Astrid would remember it in pieces for the rest of her life — the way memory works when it wants to make sure you can't pretend you weren't there. The cab of the truck with its windows fogged and shivering. The smell of river water and blood, iron and silt. The man's breath going shallow as a creek in August, each inhale a little less certain than the last.

"Kestrel's Ford," he'd said, an hour before. "Ferry landing. Ask for Wren."

He had been lying half in the flooded ditch where she'd found him, shot through the side, his boots full of the river that had tried to finish what the bullet started. She'd hauled him into the truck because leaving a man to drown on a back road was the kind of thing that followed you, even for someone who'd made a career of not being followed. He'd pressed the oilcloth bundle against her chest with both hands, like a man passing a coal he couldn't hold anymore.

"You take it to her. Not to anyone else. Not to sell." His eyes had been gray as the water. "Promise me."

"I don't promise things," she'd told him. "It's how I stay honest."

He'd almost smiled. Blood at the corner of his mouth. "Then be dishonest for once."

And then — this was the part she kept returning to, turning it over like a stone looking for what was underneath — she had heard herself say yes. Not thought it. Heard it come out of her mouth, low and strange, a word in a language she used to speak: *All right. I'll take it to her.*

He died at 4:17 by the truck's dashboard clock, though she hadn't looked at the clock then and only knew the time later, when she was writing it down, because writing things down was what she did instead of feeling them. He went out between one rattling breath and the next, quietly, the way a lamp goes when the oil runs out. His hand slid off the bundle in his lap and she caught it without thinking, held it a moment out of some instinct older than her cynicism, then laid it back down and sat in the dark listening to the water drip off the eaves of the world.

She buried him at first light above the flood line, under a cottonwood whose roots gripped the bank like fingers. It took two hours with a folding shovel and the ground fought her every inch, clay and stone, as if the land itself objected. She marked the grave with a flat rock and scratched *E.T.* into it with a screwdriver, and stood over it longer than she meant to, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say to a man she'd known for ninety minutes who had trusted her with the last thing he owned.

Then she got in the truck and did what she always did. She drove.

* * *

By midmorning she was forty miles east, parked behind a gas station called the Lariat — one pump, one window, a diner attached that served coffee strong enough to strip paint — with the oilcloth bundle open on the passenger seat and the door locked.

The map was beautiful. That was the first thing. She'd expected a prospector's scrawl, and instead Thorn had drawn like a draftsman: the river rendered in fine blue ink from Kestrel's Ford up through the gorge, past Miller's Bend, past a place labeled only *the Narrows*, climbing through canyon country drawn in cross-hatched browns until the lines simply stopped at a spring marked with a small, careful star. Above the star, in handwriting so controlled it looked printed: *THE SOURCE OF THE RIVER.*

Around the margins were annotations in three different inks, three different decades. Elevations. Distances paced off in chains. A note reading *quartz stringers, 40 ft below spring line.* Another: *DO NOT BLAST ABOVE WATERLINE — see ledger.* And in faded pencil, older than everything else, almost shy: *enough to buy the valley ten times over.*

Astrid sat very still.

She knew what silver was worth per ounce. She knew what a lode running under a river canyon was worth to men who didn't care about valleys or waterlines or anything except tonnage. She did the arithmetic automatically, the way she did everything — ounces times price times probable yield, minus extraction, minus the cost of moving ore out of a gorge with no road — and the number that came back was large enough to clear her debt eleven times over and leave her a rich woman besides.

Her hands were steady. Her heart was not.

Because here was the thing about a map worth millions: it wasn't worth millions to her. It was worth millions to whoever took it from her. And the dead man in the cottonwood's shadow had said *not to sell*, and she had said yes, and now the most valuable piece of paper in three counties was sitting on the seat of a truck registered to a woman who owed the Meridian Syndicate sixty-two thousand dollars and change, accruing interest daily, and the Syndicate's collectors had already found her once this month.

She closed the map along its old folds. Carefully. Like closing a door on something sleeping.

That was when she noticed the second document.

It had been folded inside the oilcloth, flat against the backing of the map, wrapped in a page torn from a Bible — Ecclesiastes, she saw later, the part about a time for every purpose under heaven. She unfolded it on her knee. It wasn't paper so much as artifact: soft with handling, stained, covered edge to edge in Thorn's drafting hand. But this wasn't a map.

It was a ledger.

Not the Syndicate's kind — not numbers owed and numbers paid. A claim ledger. Dates going back twenty-two years. Names she didn't know yet but would come to hate: Halloran. Deke Marsh. A man listed only as *Pike*. And column after column of entries in that same precise script:

*Aug 14 — Staked claim #4471, north fork. Filed under H.'s name per agreement.*
*Sept 2 — Assay results withheld from county record. True values logged here only.*
*Nov 9 — Meridian capital received. Terms: they hold the paper, we hold the ground. Rigged filing puts the mother lode under syndicate title while surface claims show "marginal."*
*Dec 30 — Told them I want out. They laughed.*

And then, near the bottom, in ink darker than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*They mean to strip-mine the whole upper valley. The permit dies if the source stays unrecorded. As long as no survey pins the head of this river, the ground is worthless on paper. I have hidden what I found. Let them chase a ghost. The valley keeps its water and I keep my silence, and God forgive me, I let them believe my daughter knows nothing, because the safest lie I ever told her was that her father was a coward who ran.*

Astrid read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you reread a sentence that has just rearranged the furniture of your understanding.

Elias Thorn hadn't run from the Syndicate. He'd run *toward* them — deliberately, visibly, dragging their greed behind him like a lure, away from the valley, away from the ferry landing, away from a girl named Wren. Twenty-two years of exile bought with a reputation for weakness. And the map — the treasure everyone would kill for — was itself the trap. Because the moment anyone recorded that source, pinned it to a survey, filed it, the permit lived again and the valley died.

The map was worth millions sold. And worthless — worse than worthless, catastrophic — delivered.

*You take it to her,* he'd said. *Not to anyone else. Not to sell.*

"Oh, you son of a bitch," Astrid said softly, to a dead man, with something that was not quite anger and not quite admiration. "You didn't give me a treasure. You gave me a bomb."

* * *

She spent the afternoon telling herself stories.

That was another thing she did instead of feeling: she built scenarios the way other people built fires, stacking possibilities and lighting them to see which ones caught. Scenario one: deliver the map to Wren Thorn, walk away clean, let the girl decide what to burn. Cleanest option. Also the one where Astrid exited the story entirely, debt intact, collectors still circling, six weeks left on Meridian's reset deadline.

Scenario two: sell. Find Halloran or the partners or the Syndicate itself, name a number, watch the valley get strip-mined on paper she'd signed. Sixty-two thousand gone, plus interest, plus maybe enough left over to start over somewhere the weather was different. She made herself look at that one honestly. She was good at looking at ugly options honestly; it was the decent ones that made her flinch.

Scenario three: burn it all — map, ledger, promise — and drive until the fuel ran out. The oldest scenario. The one shaped exactly like her.

She was on her third cup of the Lariat's terrible coffee, notebook open on the table beside the window, doing what she always did with her notebook — storm tracks on the left page, money on the right. A low-pressure cell was organizing over the western escarpment; if it held its bearing it would dump four inches into the gorge watershed inside two days, and the river would come up fast and dirty. She wrote *flood stage possible, 48–60 hrs* and underlined it, and on the facing page wrote *Meridian: $62,340 @ 19% APR, deadline reset to June 14,* and the symmetry of it struck her the way it always did, faintly obscene: her life balanced between two columns, weather and debt, both of them forces that gathered far off and arrived all at once and never cared what they flattened.

The waitress refilled her cup. "You waiting on somebody, hon?"

"No," Astrid said. "Just the rain."

The waitress smiled the smile of a woman who'd heard better lies and moved on.

It was nearly dusk when the black Suburban rolled into the lot.

She saw it in the window's reflection first — she'd trained herself years ago to read reflections, a habit from the year she'd spent one step ahead of a repo man in Billings — and her stomach dropped through the floor of her body before her mind caught up. Two men got out. Suits, wrong for the country, cut well enough to be expensive and worn casually enough to say *we don't have to try.* The taller one wore his hair slicked back and a smile already installed. The shorter one carried himself like a man who enjoyed his work.

She knew them. Rennick and Cole. Meridian's traveling goodwill.

Sixty miles from the last place they'd found her. One day after she'd buried a murdered man. Her mind ran the math cold and fast: either coincidence — and she didn't believe in those — or someone had seen her truck on that flooded back road. Someone had reported it. Which meant someone might have mentioned the body, the burial, the direction she'd driven. Which meant the question of whether Elias Thorn had died alone was about to become very relevant to her continued health.

She didn't run. Running from a parking lot in daylight was how you turned a conversation into a chase. Instead she closed the notebook, left a five under the cup, picked up the oilcloth bundle — wrapped now in a canvas tool roll, anonymous — and walked out through the diner's kitchen door, past a dishwasher who didn't look up, into the lot behind the dumpsters where the truck sat angled for exactly this exit.

She was backing out when Rennick stepped around the corner of the building, hands in his pockets, smiling like they were old friends at a reunion.

"Astrid Vane." Warm as bathwater. "Small world."

"Rennick." She kept the engine idling. Kept her voice level. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"We're everywhere, Ms. Vane. That's rather the point of us." He strolled closer, unhurried, and leaned one forearm on the truck bed's rail, casual as a neighbor. Behind him, Cole drifted wide, cutting the angle toward the driver's door — not obviously, just enough. "We heard a rumor. Old-timer washed up on the east fork road. Shot, they're saying. You wouldn't know anything about that."

"I pull people out of ditches. It's a hobby."

"Mm." Rennick's smile widened by a millimeter, which on him was a great deal. "Funny thing about hobbies. They put a person at scenes. And scenes put a person in reports. And reports—" he tapped the truck bed twice, lightly, affectionately, "—put a person in front of people like me, having conversations like this one, instead of being home making her payments."

"I'm current."

"You're *current*," he agreed pleasantly. "Current is a word that means *not yet late*. We prefer a different word. We prefer *finished*." He straightened, and for one unguarded instant the warmth drained out of his face and left something older and hungrier underneath, and Astrid understood that the smile was the costume and this was the man. "June fourteenth, Ms. Vane. Mr. Voss wanted me to remind you personally. He's very much looking forward to seeing you again. He says you're his favorite account."

She held his eyes. "Tell Voss I keep my own calendar."

"I'm sure you do." The smile came back up like a shop gate. "Drive safe. Roads are washing out all over. Terrible season for being alone out here." He patted the truck bed once more — right over the wheel well, right where the tool roll lay hidden beneath the seat, though he couldn't have known that, couldn't have, and yet her skin crawled as if he'd touched it — and stepped back, and waved, actually waved, as she pulled out of the lot.

In the mirror she watched the two suits stand together in the failing light, watching her go. Neither of them reached for a phone. Neither of them needed to. Whatever they'd come to confirm, they'd confirmed it: she was here, she was reachable, and she was carrying something she hadn't handed over when two Meridian men asked her a direct question about a dead man.

Cole raised a hand too, at the end. Just a little wave. Fingers wiggling.

* * *

She drove south with the headlights off until the highway curved away, then west, then nowhere in particular, putting darkness between herself and the Lariat, her mind working the problem from every side like a tongue finding a cracked tooth.

Here was the trap she'd walked into with her eyes open and her mouth saying *yes*: the map was worth selling to everyone except the person she'd promised to give it to. The ledger could destroy the Syndicate's claim — and the Syndicate's claim was collateral, and collateral was the only language Meridian spoke, which meant the ledger was worth more to her creditors than any payment she'd ever make. And Thorn's old partners were out there somewhere — Halloran, Marsh, Pike — men who'd spent twenty-two years chasing a ghost and had just, by every sign she could read, stopped chasing and started hunting. Three sets of wolves, one piece of oilcloth, and her.

She pulled off onto a ridge track above the river and killed the engine and sat in the dark with the notebook on her knee, and for a long time she didn't write anything at all.

Below her, the water moved in the blackness, patient, going somewhere it had decided on long ago. She'd always envied rivers that. Everything she'd ever done had been a detour. Every town a gas station, every night a parking spot, every relationship a rest stop with the engine warm. She'd told herself for years it was freedom. Sitting there with a dead man's promise in a canvas roll beside her and a collector's smile still hanging in the air like smoke, she finally admitted what it actually was: she had never once stayed anywhere long enough to find out what staying cost. Running wasn't freedom. Running was just a debt with better scenery.

And now, for the first time, running had a destination she hadn't chosen. Kestrel's Ford. A ferry landing. A daughter who didn't know her father was dead.

Astrid opened the notebook. On the left page, under the storm tracking, she wrote: *Low pressure holding. Gorge flood likely, 48 hrs. River will rise fast.* On the right page, under the debt, she drew a single line through the neat column of figures and wrote beneath it, in letters harder than she intended:

*New terms. Deliver the package. Survive the delivery. Do not sell. Do not burn. Do not stop.*

She looked at the last words a moment. *Do not stop.* It was supposed to be tactical. It came out sounding like a prayer.

Far upstream, thunder muttered along the escarpment — the western cell, arriving early, dragging its skirt of rain across the divide. The river below her answered with a sound she felt through the tires: a deepening, a gathering, the first hunger of high water.

Two days, she thought. Maybe less. Everyone who wanted what she carried would be on that river by the time it crested.

She started the truck and drove toward the storm.
