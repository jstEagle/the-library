## 4. Kestrel's Ford

The road down off the escarpment was the kind of road that punished optimism. It switchbacked through sage and broken rock for eleven miles, dropping through climate zones the way a falling man drops through floors, and Astrid took it in second gear with the truck's engine braking and her hands at ten and two, the way you drive when the vehicle beneath you is the only thing standing between you and a long red smear on the canyon wall.

The oilcloth map rode in the map pocket on the passenger door, where a map belonged, which felt like a joke Elias Thorn would have appreciated. She'd studied it for two hours at a rest area outside Rawlins, coffee going cold, tracing the river's blue thread from its mouth back up into country the cartographers had mostly given up on. The source was marked in Thorn's own hand — a small black X above a cairn symbol, with a note in pencil so faint she'd had to tilt the paper into the sun: *Where the water is born, so is the money. God forgive me for knowing both.*

She'd turned the page over a dozen times looking for more. There was nothing. Just the X, the cairn, and a dead man's handwriting that pressed on her like a thumb on a bruise.

Kestrel's Ford announced itself before she saw it. First the smell — cut hay and river mud and woodsmoke, the smell of a town that heated with what it could cut and burned what it couldn't sell. Then the sound: the river, wide and brown-green here, running loud over gravel bars. Then the town itself, folded into the valley like something the mountains were keeping secret — two streets, a grain elevator leaning like a bad tooth, a church with a steeple that had been re-shingled in a color that matched nothing, a general store, a bar, a one-room post office flying a flag so faded the stripes had gone the color of old blood.

And the ferry. She saw that from the last bend in the road: a flat-bottomed barge on a cable strung across the river, mid-crossing, carrying a pickup and two horses. Someone stood at the stern working the crank, and even at this distance the figure moved with a certainty that made Astrid's hands itch — no wasted motion, no hesitation, the kind of economy you only saw in people who had done one thing ten thousand times and intended to do it ten thousand more.

She parked on the street outside the general store and sat for a moment with the engine ticking, watching the town watch her.

Because it did. That was the thing about Kestrel's Ford. She'd rolled into a hundred towns like this one — poor, proud, and watchful — and she knew the choreography. A stranger's truck drew faces to windows the way a dropped plate draws flies. An old man on the store porch paused mid-shuffle to look her over. A woman hanging laundry turned with a sheet still in her hands. Nobody waved. Nobody frowned, either. They just *took inventory* — the truck's age, the out-of-state plates, the dust on the windshield, the way Astrid sat too long before getting out — and filed it all somewhere behind their eyes, to be retrieved and compared against whatever the next stranger brought in.

Astrid got out, locked the truck, and immediately unlocked it, because locking a truck in a town like this was a confession that you had something worth stealing.

The general store smelled of coffee, feed, and gun oil. The man behind the counter was maybe seventy, built like a fence post, with a mustache that had gone the color of the flag outside. He rang up a box of shells for a teenager who was pretending Astrid didn't exist, and then, when the bell over the door clattered shut behind the kid, he turned to her with the unhurried patience of a man who had all the time in the world and knew she didn't.

"Help you?"

"Gas," Astrid said. "And directions, if you've got them."

"We got gas." He said it the way other men said *we got opinions*. "Directions depends on where to."

"The ferry."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise. Assessment. "Ferry's hard to miss. Big flat boat, middle of the river."

"I meant — who runs it."

He set both hands flat on the counter, which Astrid recognized as a man preparing to enjoy himself. "Wren Thorn runs the ferry. Has since her granddad's knees gave out. You wanting a crossing?"

"Eventually." Astrid put a twenty on the counter. "I'm looking for her, actually. Family business."

The old man looked at the twenty. He looked at Astrid. He did not pick up the twenty.

"Thorn," he said, "is a name with some weather on it in this valley. You kin?"

"Distant," Astrid said, which was the first lie of the day, and it sat in her mouth like a coin.

"Uh-huh." He picked up the twenty, finally, and rang it through with the slow ceremony of a man making a point. "Ferry dock's down Water Street, past the church. Can't miss it." He pushed a brass key across the counter. "Pump's out back. And missus?" He waited until she looked at him. "Wren Thorn don't much like surprises. Fair warning."

"Noted," Astrid said, and meant it, and went out to pump gas anyway, because she was going to do this thing no matter how many old men warned her off, and knowing a thing and being ready for it were two different animals.

* * *

The ferry dock was a plank platform and a gravel ramp, a tin-roofed shed full of rope and chain, and a hand-painted sign that read THORN FERRY — CROSSING $4 — HORSES EXTRA — NO CREDIT, the last two words underlined twice in a different color, by a different hand, in a different year.

The barge was mid-river, coming back. Astrid leaned against the truck and watched Wren Thorn work.

The girl — woman, really, though she couldn't have been more than twenty-three — stood at the stern crank with her weight low and her shoulders doing the work her back refused to. She was small. That was the first thing. Small and brown from a lifetime of river sun, with her dark hair hacked off at the jaw in a cut that said *I cut this myself with a fishing knife and I regret nothing*. She wore rubber waders cut down to fit and a canvas jacket gone soft with age, and she worked that crank the way Astrid read weather — totally, without doubt, her whole body in conversation with the machine and the current and the cable, three forces negotiating through her hands.

The barge slid into the dock. Wren threw a loop over a bollard without looking at it, killed the crank, and looked up — and Astrid felt the look land like a hand on her chest.

The eyes were Elias Thorn's. She knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a photograph you've only seen once. Same gray, same flat calm over something running fast underneath. The dead man's eyes, alive and furious, set in a face that had his jaw and none of his tiredness.

"You want the crossing, it's four dollars," Wren said. "Horses extra. No credit."

"Not here for a crossing."

"Then you're standing on my dock for no reason." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. She was already coiling rope, already turning away, a person with a full day's work queued up behind her eyes. "Dock's public. Stand all you want."

"Are you Wren Thorn?"

The rope stopped. Just for a second. Then it kept moving. "Who's asking?"

"Astrid Vane." She'd rehearsed this on the drive down, three versions, four, and now that the moment had arrived every version fell out of her head like change from a torn pocket. "I — knew your father."

The rope came off the coil. Wren set it down on the planks with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing when the alternative is throwing it. When she looked up, the flat calm was gone and what was underneath wasn't running fast anymore. It was standing very, very still.

"My father," Wren said, "has been gone eleven years. If you're selling something, I'm not buying. If you're a bill collector, you've wasted a tank of gas. And if you're one of *his* people—" she stepped forward, and Astrid was surprised to find herself reassessing the arithmetic of the girl, the reach of her, the coiled thing in her stance, "—then you can get back in your truck and tell them the answer's the same as it's been since I was twelve. We don't want anything he's got. There's nothing he's got we want."

"I'm not selling anything. I'm not collecting anything." Astrid heard her own voice go low and even, the voice she used on herself in bad weather. "I need to talk to you. Not here."

"Then where."

"Somewhere your neighbors can't count my teeth from the porch."

That earned her a flicker of something — not a smile, but the ghost of where a smile would go on a different face, a different life. Wren looked past her, up the street, at the town that was certainly watching, and Astrid watched her make the calculation every kid in a small town learns young: how much is it worth to be seen talking to a stranger, and who's going to ask about it at church on Sunday.

"Supper," Wren said. "There's a bend in the river a half-mile downstream, past the willows. Old picnic table. I eat there when I don't want to be found, which means you'll be the only one there besides me, and if anyone asks, I was checking my lines." She picked the rope back up. "Sunset. If you're late, I'm gone, and don't bother finding me again, because I'll have decided you weren't worth it."

"I'll be there."

"Bring the four dollars," Wren said. "You crossed my dock. That's a crossing."

* * *

Astrid spent the hours until sunset the way she spent all waiting hours: badly, and then well. Badly, meaning she drove the town's one other street, bought coffee at the bar, and let the place read her back. The bar was called the Anchor, which was funny in a town forty miles from anything floatable except the river, and inside it was dim and smelled of decades. A woman behind the bar with reading glasses on a chain took Astrid's order and her money and asked no questions, which in a town this small was itself a kind of question.

"You're the one at the Thorn girl's dock," the woman said, setting down the coffee.

"Small town."

"Small town," the woman agreed. "You knew Elias?"

Here it was again, the lie, waiting. "A little. Long time ago."

"Mm." The woman polished a glass that didn't need it. "He was a good man who did a bad thing, or a bad man who did a good thing. Town's never decided which. I expect he never did either." She set the glass down. "Wren was eight when he left. Old enough to remember everything and too young to understand any of it. That's a bad combination. It makes children into either saints or knives." She looked at Astrid over the glasses. "That one came out a knife. Just so you know what you're walking into."

"Thanks," Astrid said, and the woman said, "Don't mention it," and Astrid understood that she literally shouldn't.

She drove out past the willows an hour early, because she didn't trust the town's clocks or her own nerves, and sat in the truck watching the river run gold in the low light, and did what she always did with a notebook open on her knee: she made lists.

The first list was debts. The Meridian Syndicate, principal and interest, recalculated as of this morning, with the new deadline the smiling men at the gas station had so cheerfully reset — fourteen days now, not thirty, because they'd found her once and wanted her to know the finding was easy. Fourteen days. The number sat in her stomach like swallowed stone.

The second list was the promise. *Take it to my daughter in Kestrel's Ford.* Fourteen words, spoken by a man with river water in his lungs and a bullet hole in him, and Astrid had said yes — she still didn't know why, still couldn't reconstruct the moment, only that he'd looked at her with those gray eyes going dim and she had heard her own voice say *I promise* as if it belonged to someone else. She had never promised anyone anything. Promises were anchors. She'd built her whole life on the principle that the only thing you couldn't survive was being still, being tied, being *kept* — and now she was anchored to a dead prospector's last wish and a town full of people who watched strangers like hawks, and the terrifying part, the part she kept circling without landing on, was that some small cold corner of her had felt *relief* when she said it. Relief, like a fever breaking. Like someone else had finally taken the wheel.

The third list was the hardest, and she wrote it small: *what to tell Wren.*

The truth was a shape she kept turning over. *Your father is dead. He was shot, on a flooded road, by men who wanted what he knew. He died in my truck. His last words were your name.* She imagined saying it and watched, in imagination, the knife the bartender had promised her come out — watched Wren's face do what faces do when the worst thing arrives wearing the face of a stranger. And underneath the imagined grief, Astrid knew, would come the question, the one with teeth: *Who shot him? And why were you close enough to catch him dying?* And under that question, like a reef under dark water, was the map in the door pocket and the ledger in the map's folds and the whole rotten architecture of claims and syndicates and rigged assays that Elias Thorn had spent eleven years and, in the end, his blood trying to keep away from this valley.

Telling the truth tonight would bring all of it down on Kestrel's Ford by morning. Small towns talk. Small towns talk *fast*, and somewhere in the Syndicate's wide smiling network, someone was listening. Voss was already on the river road — she'd seen the black sedan pass the Anchor at dusk, slow, the driver's face turned toward the storefronts, cataloguing. She was sure of it the way she was sure of a green sky.

So: not the truth. Not tonight. A version. *I met your father recently. He asked me to bring you something. He's alive — * no, she couldn't say that, that lie had a half-life of days and would detonate worse when it did. Something between. Something that bought time. She hated it. She wrote in the notebook: *Lie of mercy or lie of cowardice?* and underlined it twice and couldn't answer, and the not-answering felt like the truest thing she'd done all day.

Headlights swept the willows. Wren's truck — an older pickup, river-ruined, a canoe racked on top — pulled in beside her, and the girl got out carrying a canvas bag and a lantern and the four dollars' worth of authority she'd rented Astrid on the dock.

"You're early," Wren said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. Late is a thing that happens to people who expect the world to wait." She dropped onto the picnic table's bench, opened the canvas bag, and produced — with the gravity of a ritual — two tin plates, a loaf of bread, a jar of something amber, and a knife that had been sharpened past all reason. "I brought supper because you look like a person who's been eating gas station food, and because a conversation goes easier when both parties have eaten. That's not kindness. It's tactics." She cut bread. "Talk."

So Astrid talked. She gave her the version, the one built on the drive out, and she gave it well — she'd been lying to smiling men for three years and had gotten good at it, and the awful thing was how well it worked. *I met your father in the north country this spring. He was old and sick and he knew it. He wouldn't say much about this valley, but he talked about you. He said you ran the ferry. He said you were the best thing he ever did and the worst thing he ever left.* Each sentence was a stone with a true center and a false skin, and Wren ate the bread and listened with those gray Thorn eyes and said nothing, and Astrid felt the guilt sharpen as she talked, honing itself on the girl's silence, until it wasn't guilt anymore but something with an edge and a purpose.

When she finished, Wren looked out at the river for a long time.

"You're lying," she said at last, mildly, the way you'd note the weather.

Astrid's blood went cold. "I'm not."

"Parts of it." Wren turned and looked at her, and there was no anger in it, which was worse. "You lie like someone who's had practice. Your eyes go to the left when you build a sentence and to the right when you mean it. You said *he talked about you* with your eyes left. You said *he was old and sick* looking me dead in the face." She set down the knife. "So here's what I know. You met my father. Recently — your truck's got north-country mud in the wheel wells and there's a bullet hole patched with epoxy in the passenger door that's maybe three weeks old, and I know epoxy because I patch my boat with it. Something happened to him, or around him, and you drove eleven hundred miles to my dock because he told you to, and now you're feeding me a story in pieces because you think the whole one would break me." She picked the bread back up and tore it, calm as tide. "I grew up on this river, Astrid Vane. I read water for a living. You're not water, but you're close enough. So I'm going to eat this bread, and you're going to decide how much of the truth you can afford, and I'm going to sit right here until you decide, because I've got nowhere to be and all night to be nowhere."

The lantern hissed. The river ran. Somewhere up the dark valley, thunder muttered — a storm building over the escarpment, Astrid's oldest trade calling to her, and for the first time in her life she didn't want to chase it. She wanted to sit still, in this light, in front of this girl, and get the next thing right.

She opened her mouth to try — to find the middle ground, the partial truth, the merciful fraction —

And Wren's head came up, sharp, toward the road. A vehicle was coming down through the willows, slow, headlights off, and Astrid knew the engine note before she saw the shape of it, knew it the way you know a storm's voice.

Black sedan. River road. Coming on with its lights dark, like something that had learned to hunt at night.

"That's the third time that car's passed my dock today," Wren said quietly, rising, one hand closing around the knife. "You want to tell me who your friends are, Vane? Because it sounds like they've found us."
