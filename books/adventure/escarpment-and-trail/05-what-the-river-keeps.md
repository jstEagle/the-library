## 5. What the River Keeps

The stranger came up the ferry ramp at half past seven in the morning, and Wren Thorn knew three things about her before she reached the top of the planks.

One: she was a liar. Not because she'd said anything yet — she hadn't — but because of how she held her shoulders, the way liars hold them, slightly forward, weight on the balls of their feet, ready to be someone else at a moment's notice.

Two: she was a drifter. The dust on her boots was road dust, not valley dust, and the truck parked crooked at the bottom of the ramp had a storm chaser's antenna mast and a passenger seat buried under maps and notebooks and fast-food wrappers from three states away. Nobody with a home kept their life stacked where they could grab it in four seconds.

Three: she was trouble, and trouble always found the Thorns eventually, the way water finds the low ground.

Wren set down the wrench she'd been using on the *Maggie B*'s outboard and wiped her hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime last summer. "Ferry doesn't run till eight," she said. "And it doesn't run for free."

"I'm not here for the ferry." The woman climbed the last of the ramp and stopped a polite six feet away. She was tall, sunburned across the nose, with eyes the flat gray-green of river ice in February — pretty enough to notice once and then notice they never changed expression. "I'm looking for Wren Thorn."

"You found her."

"Astrid Vane." She didn't offer a hand. Wren noted that too. People who offered hands wanted something small. People who withheld them were saving up for something big. "Your father sent me."

The words landed like a stone dropped into still water, and Wren watched the ripples spread through her own chest while keeping her face as flat as the stranger's. Her father. Elias Thorn, who'd walked out of Kestrel's Ford nineteen years ago with a prospector's pack and a promise to send for them by spring. Who'd sent exactly eleven letters in nineteen years, each one shorter than the last, until the twelfth never came at all.

"My father," Wren said carefully, "is dead to me. So you'll have to be more specific."

Something moved behind those gray-green eyes — not quite flinching, but banking, the way a pilot banks around weather. "He's not well," Astrid Vane said. "He asked me to bring you something."

*There it is.* First lie. Wren had grown up reading people the way her mother read the river — every eddy, every submerged rock, every place where the surface lied about the depth. And this woman was lying with every line of her body except her mouth, which was doing its best.

"He asked you." Wren leaned back against the ferry rail and crossed her arms. "A man who couldn't manage a letter twice a year asked a total stranger to drive into a town he spent two decades avoiding. That's quite a favor. What did it cost him?"

"Nothing I'll discuss standing here."

"Then we won't discuss it standing here." Wren pushed off the rail. "We'll discuss it never. Good morning."

She turned toward the boat, and that was when she saw it — the shape of it, anyway — inside the woman's jacket. An oilcloth-wrapped roll, long and narrow, tucked against her ribs the way you carry something you've been told never to let go of. Oilcloth. Wren's stomach performed a slow, cold roll, because she knew oilcloth. She knew the particular way her father wrapped things, the double fold and the wax-sealed corners, because he'd taught it to her before he taught her to swim. *If you find something worth keeping, Wrennie, you wrap it so the river can't take it.*

"Wait." The word came out of her before she'd decided to say it. She hated that. She turned back slowly, making it look like a choice. "What is it you're carrying?"

Astrid Vane's hand went to her jacket — an instinctive, protective motion, and the most honest thing she'd done since stepping onto the ramp. "That's between me and your father."

"Then give him my regards." Wren picked up the wrench again, mostly so her hands would have something to do besides shake. "Tell him his daughter learned to run a ferry without him. Tell him the *Maggie B* has a new engine. Tell him—" Her voice caught, traitorous, and she hated herself for it, hated him, hated the whole rotten architecture of loving a man who'd chosen a river's source over a kitchen table. "Tell him whatever liars tell each other."

For a long moment the stranger just looked at her. Then, quietly: "You have his handwriting."

"What?"

"The way you write your signs." Astrid nodded toward the fare board hanging off the ferry house — FERRY 50¢, NO CREDIT, NO EXCEPTIONS, painted in Wren's own slanting capitals. "He writes like that. Same hard angles. Same way the T crosses like it's mad at the word."

"You've seen his writing." It wasn't a question. Wren's pulse ticked up. "Recently."

"I've seen it recently."

"And he's alive."

Astrid Vane paused one beat too long. One beat. In the valley, one beat was an answer all by itself.

"No," Wren said. The word came out steady, which surprised her. "No, he's not. Is he."

The stranger didn't confirm it. She didn't deny it either. She stood there on the worn planks of the ferry ramp with the morning fog lifting off the water behind her and let the silence do what silence does, and Wren felt nineteen years rearrange themselves inside her chest — the anger staying, because anger was earned and hers, but something else shifting underneath it, some old child-shaped hope finally, mercifully, dying.

"How?" Wren asked.

"That's his story to tell. Was." Astrid's jaw tightened. "He didn't leave much time for telling."

*So he died badly,* Wren thought. *Badly and recently and somewhere far enough from here that he needed a stranger to finish the trip.* She looked at the oilcloth bundle again, and this time she didn't see treasure or trash or a dead man's excuses. She saw her father's handwriting on whatever was inside it, waiting, patient as sediment.

"I want to see it," she said.

"I don't think—"

"You don't get to think. You drove here carrying my father's last letter, whether you call it that or not, and you don't get to decide how I read it." She held out her hand. "Here. Now. Or I go back to my engine and you drive back to wherever drifters come from."

Astrid Vane looked at her for a long moment — measuring, Wren realized later, not whether to trust her but whether trusting her would get her killed. Then she drew the bundle out and unwrapped the first fold, no further, showing just enough: a sheet of heavy paper covered edge to edge in tight, angular script, and beneath it, glimpsed, the unmistakable geometry of a drawn map.

Wren didn't need more. She knew that handwriting the way she knew her own reflection — better, maybe, because she'd spent years studying it for clues the way other kids studied stars. Every one of those letters was a small accusation.

"When did he die?" she asked.

"Two nights ago. On the Miller's Ford road, during the flood." Astrid rewrapped the map with careful hands. "He was hurt before I found him. Shot. He made me promise to bring it to you."

"Shot by who?"

"He didn't say. He didn't have time to say much of anything." A muscle worked in the woman's cheek. "Except your name. And mine, apparently, now."

Wren looked out at the river. The Kestrel ran high and brown with snowmelt, dragging branches and foam past the ferry landing, going somewhere with the dumb patience water has. Somewhere upstream, past the gorges and the ghost camps and the escarpment wall, that water began at a source her father had spent his whole life looking for — and apparently died holding directions to.

"Why you?" she asked. "Why would he trust a stranger with it?"

"Because I was there and you weren't." Astrid said it flatly, without self-pity, and Wren grudgingly respected the honesty. "Because I had a truck. Because dying men don't shop around."

"And what do you want out of it? Nobody drives two hundred miles of bad road for free. What's your price?"

For the first time, the stranger smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of somebody who'd already done the arithmetic and didn't like the total. "You ask good questions."

"I live on a river. Questions are how you keep from drowning."

"My price," Astrid said, "is nothing you can pay and nothing I'd take. Let's leave it at that."

*Which means money,* Wren thought. *Or debt. Which means somebody's after her, or after the map, or both.* She filed it away. The ferry town taught you to count everything — passengers, cargo, current, clouds — and to assume anything uncounted was the thing that would sink you.

* * *

They talked terms on the ferry house porch over coffee that Wren made strong enough to strip paint, because she wanted to see if the stranger would complain. She didn't. She drank it black and thanked her, and Wren added a point to the mental ledger marked *not entirely useless.*

"I'm not going for company," Wren said. "Understand that. I'm going for answers. Why he left. What he found. Who shot him. Once I have those, you and I are done, and you can chase whatever it is you chase."

"Storms," Astrid said.

"What?"

"I chase storms. That's the trade. Weather forecasting for hire — mining outfits, ranchers, film crews, whoever pays." She shrugged. "It's honest work, mostly."

"Mostly."

"There's a portion of it that isn't. We'll get there." She set the cup down. "You run this river?"

"Since I was nine. Commercial license at seventeen. I know every rock between here and Split Water Canyon, and most of them know me."

"Good. Because the map follows the river upstream, and I can't row worth a damn."

Wren almost laughed. Almost. "You're telling me a professional drifter can't handle a boat."

"I'm telling you I grew up where water came out of taps. I read sky, not current." Astrid's eyes went, briefly, to the southern horizon, where heat haze shimmered above the escarpment rim. "Though I'll tell you this for free — that haze is stacking wrong. There's a system building over the high country. Three days, maybe four, and this valley gets weather it hasn't seen in ten years."

"You're serious."

"I'm always serious about weather. It's the only thing that's never lied to me." She said it lightly, but Wren heard the weight under it, the same way you hear boulders under calm-looking water. This was a woman with a history, and the history had teeth.

Wren stood and walked to the porch rail. Below, the *Maggie B* knocked gently against her mooring, impatient, the way boats get. Nineteen years of not-going had built up in her like pressure behind a dam. Every letter unanswered, every birthday missed, every winter her mother had spent staring at the door — all of it frozen in her, waiting on a thaw. And now here came a stranger with a dead man's map, offering her the one thing no one in Kestrel's Ford could give her: the actual, physical, upstream truth.

But going meant leaving the ferry. Leaving Mrs. Okafor's bakery and the boys who played cards on her dock and the whole web of small obligations that made a town a town instead of a spot on a map. Going meant becoming, briefly, the kind of person who leaves — which was her father's category, not hers, and she'd sworn on her mother's grave she'd die before joining it.

Going also meant answers. And Wren Thorn had never once in her life left a question alone.

"All right," she said. "Terms. One: we take my boat as far as it can go, then my truck, then our feet. Two: you don't lie to me again. Not even kindly. I'd rather have ugly truth than pretty anything."

"Done."

"Three: if whoever shot him comes for us, you tell me immediately. No protecting me. I'm not cargo."

Astrid Vane studied her with those flat river-ice eyes, and something in them shifted — a fraction of respect, maybe, or just recalibration. "You're not what I expected."

"Nobody ever says what they expected. Say yes to term three."

"Yes." A pause. "Term four's mine. When I say run, we run. Don't argue, don't pack light, don't be brave. Run."

"Who's coming?"

"I don't know yet. But my experience is that when a dead man's map changes hands, the people who wanted it from the living man come looking at the dead man's speed." She glanced south again, at the shimmering rim of the escarpment. "And I've got creditors with excellent instincts."

*Creditors.* There it was, the shape under the shape. Wren nodded slowly. "You're in debt."

"Deep enough that the interest has a personality." Astrid drained her coffee and stood. "How fast can you be ready?"

Wren thought about the engine, half-tuned. The fuel drums. The cash box under the floorboard with four hundred dollars and her mother's ring in it. The note she'd have to leave Mrs. Okafor, who'd worry, who'd tell everyone, who'd by tonight have the story shaped into something the whole town could chew on.

"An hour," she said.

"Make it forty minutes."

* * *

They were twenty minutes into packing when Wren saw the car.

It came down the valley road at a pace that told her everything — not the hurry of an emergency, but the smooth, unhurried glide of a man who believed the world would wait for him. A dark sedan, city clean, rolling past the bakery and the church and the card players' dock like it was taking inventory. It slowed at the ferry landing. Stopped.

The man who stepped out wore a gray suit that had no business surviving the valley roads, and he wore it the way some men wear authority — lightly, like it cost him nothing. He surveyed the landing, the boat, the truck with the antenna mast, and then he smiled, and Wren's skin crawled, because it was the warmest smile she'd ever seen on a colder face.

"That's Voss," Astrid said quietly. She'd gone very still beside the truck, one hand resting on the tailgate as if leaning casually, though Wren noticed her other hand had drifted somewhere near her jacket. "Second collector. The polite one."

"Collector of what?"

"Debts. Mine, mostly." Astrid's voice stayed level, but Wren, who read water for a living, read the current underneath it. Fear. Real fear, banked and controlled. "Listen to me. He doesn't know why I'm here. As far as he knows, I'm chasing a storm contract. Let me talk."

"And if he asks about the map?"

"Then we're both about to learn something." Astrid pushed off the tailgate and started down the ramp, and Wren watched her transform — shoulders loosening, chin lifting, the whole body rearranging itself into a woman with nowhere to be and nothing to hide. It was, Wren admitted, a masterful performance. It was also, she noted grimly, the performance of a natural-born liar, which confirmed everything she'd thought on the ramp that morning.

Voss met her halfway. Their voices carried in fragments on the breeze — *Miss Vane, what a coincidence;* *small world;* *storm work, actually, valley client;* *and who's your friend?*

Wren came down the ramp with a coil of rope over her shoulder and her face arranged into the open, uncomplicated friendliness of a ferry operator greeting a paying customer. "Morning. Ferry runs at eight. Fifty cents."

"Not today, thank you." Voss's eyes were pale and pleasant and missed absolutely nothing. They touched the truck, the packed gear, the rope in Wren's hands. "You two traveling together? How lovely. Whereabouts?"

"Upstream survey," Wren said, and was proud of how bored she made it sound. "Client wants gauge readings above the gorge before runoff peaks."

"Mm. And you'd be Miss Thorn." Something flickered in the pleasantness — a hook finding flesh. "Elias Thorn's girl. Your father was known to some associates of mine. Talented man. We've been hoping to renew the acquaintance."

"He's been gone nineteen years," Wren said. "If you find him, bill him for the ferry rides."

Voss laughed — a real laugh, warm as brandy — and Wren understood suddenly why people trusted men like this right up until they didn't. "Charming. The whole family's charming." He produced a card from his breast pocket and held it out to Astrid, not Wren. "If your valley client falls through, Miss Vane, Meridian's always interested in capable forecasters. Very interested. Do call."

He tipped an imaginary hat, strolled back to the sedan, and drove off north — toward the escarpment road, Wren noted, the road that followed the river upstream.

Nobody spoke until the engine noise faded into wind.

"He knows," Wren said.

"He suspects. There's a difference, and right now it's the only thing keeping us alive." Astrid took the card, looked at it, and tore it into pieces small enough for the river. "He came here for me. He'll figure out the rest within a day. Maybe less, once he talks to the bakery or the gas station or anyone whose business it is to know strangers."

"Then we leave now."

"Now," Astrid agreed.

They loaded the last of it in silence — fuel, rope, the wrapped map riding against Astrid's ribs, Wren's mother's ring zipped into an inner pocket where it rode like a second heartbeat. Wren locked the ferry house, wrote a note for Mrs. Okafor that said GONE UPSTREAM, BACK IN A WEEK, and told no one else, because the fewer threads leading out of Kestrel's Ford, the harder any of them would be to pull.

At the top of the ramp she stopped once and looked back. The *Maggie B* rocked at her mooring. The card players waved, lazy, not knowing. The town sat in its bowl of green fields with the river running through it like a spine, and Wren felt the leaving tear along some seam in her that had never been tested before.

Her father had left this view. Had looked back, maybe, or maybe hadn't. And now she was leaving it too, chasing his ghost upstream, and the symmetry of it made her jaw tighten until it ached.

"For answers," she said aloud, to herself, to him, to the river. "Not for company."

"Did you say something?" Astrid called from the truck.

"No." Wren climbed in and pulled the door shut. "Drive."

The truck rattled onto the north road with the escarpment rising ahead of them, blue and enormous, wearing its first thin crown of storm cloud — and behind them, far back down the valley, a dark sedan turned around.
