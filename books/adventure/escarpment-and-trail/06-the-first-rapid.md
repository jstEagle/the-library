## 6. The First Rapid

The boat was my father's, which meant it was ugly, and I loved it the way you love a scar.

She was twenty feet of riveted aluminum and cedar, patched in six places with whatever Elias Thorn could scrounge before he left, and she'd sat under a tarp behind the ferry shed for eleven years, collecting spiderwebs and my resentment. I'd spent three days getting her river-ready, mostly to give my hands something to do that wasn't shaking. The Kestrel ran shallow and mean below the ford — boulder gardens, strainers, one stretch the old-timers called the Throat because it swallowed a dredge barge in '61 and never coughed up so much as a cleat. Nobody ran it anymore. Nobody had a reason to.

Now I had a stranger with storm-gray eyes and a liar's calm sitting in my bow seat, holding a wrapped map like it might bite her, and I had every reason there was.

"Life jacket," I said.

"I have one."

"Wear it."

Astrid Vane looked at me for a long second — measuring, I'd learn later, the way she measured everything, the way she'd measured the sky over the escarpment while I loaded gear and pretended not to watch her do it — and then she pulled the jacket on without another word. Small mercy. She didn't argue. My father would have argued. He'd have made a joke first, then an argument, then a speech, and by the end you'd have forgotten what you asked him for. This woman just did the thing. It unsettled me more than arguing would have.

I pushed us off the gravel bar at first light, and the river took us the way it takes everything: without comment.

* * *

The first two miles were easy water, and easy water is its own kind of trap. It lets you talk.

"So," I said, not looking at her, watching the current fold around a midstream boulder. "You found him dying on a road."

"Flooded back road, east fork. His truck was off the shoulder, nose-down in the ditch. Water coming through the doors." Her voice was flat, but flat in a deliberate way, like someone smoothing a wrinkle out of a sheet. "He'd been shot. Through the chest, low. He'd walked, or crawled, maybe half a mile from wherever it happened."

"And he gave you a map."

"He offered a trade." A pause. The paddle knocked once against the gunwale. "A promise for a map. Take it to his daughter in Kestrel's Ford. That was the whole price."

"That's not a trade. That's a dying man grabbing at anything warm."

"Yes," she said quietly. "It was."

I dug the paddle in harder than I needed to. The bow swung; I corrected. "And you said yes. To a stranger. On the side of a road. You didn't know him, didn't know me, didn't know if the daughter even existed."

"I know how it sounds."

"Do you? Because here's how it sounds to me. Some drifter finds a wounded man alone and helpless, and walks away with the only thing he had left to give, and now she's sitting in my boat telling me it was charity." The words came out sharper than I'd planned, edged with something older than this trip. Eleven years of sharpening. "What did you get out of it, Astrid? Really."

The silence went on long enough that I finally looked at her. She wasn't angry. She was looking at the water going past, and her face had gone somewhere else entirely — some gas station at night, some road, some version of herself I hadn't met yet.

"You want the honest answer?"

"I've had enough of the other kind."

"The honest answer is I don't know." She turned and met my eyes, and there was no performance in it, and that was somehow worse. "He grabbed my wrist and he was strong, still, stronger than a shot man should be, and he said *promise me*, and I heard myself say yes before I decided to. Like the word was already in my mouth waiting for him." She shook her head slowly. "I don't say yes to people, Wren. It's not — I'm not built for it. And then he died before dawn in the cab of my truck, and I buried his map under my seat and drove toward your town feeling like I was carrying something live."

Something live. I knew exactly what she meant. I'd been carrying it since she walked into the ferry office with his handwriting in her bag.

We came around a bend and the canyon walls closed in, red rock streaked black with seep lines, and the sound of the river changed. Deeper. Hungrier.

"There," I said, glad of the excuse to stop talking. "You hear that?"

"I hear it."

"That's the Throat. When we go in, you do exactly what I say, when I say it. Not before, not after. You lean when I say lean, you paddle when I say paddle, and if I say down, you get flat in the bottom of the boat and you stay there." I cinched my own jacket, checked the throw rope coiled at my knees, checked the bailer. "Questions?"

"One." She'd gone very still, and I realized she wasn't afraid — or rather, she was afraid, and she was doing arithmetic with it. "How many times have you run this?"

"None."

Her eyebrows went up a fraction of an inch.

"But I've run everything else on this river since I was nine, and I've read this stretch from the rim a dozen times. There's a line. Left of the fang rock, right of the hole, then hard left before the sieve. If we hold the line, we're fine." I heard myself and almost laughed. "That's a lie. Nobody's fine in the Throat. But we're probably upright."

"Probably," Astrid repeated. And then she did something I didn't expect. She smiled — small, crooked, real. "I chase storms for money. Probably is the best odds I've ever worked with."

* * *

The Throat announced itself as a horizon line — the river simply ended ahead, dropping into white noise, and the walls narrowed until they funneled the whole Kestrel through a gap you could have thrown a stone across.

I've been scared on water plenty of times. Fear on the water is different from fear anywhere else. It's clean. It has a shape. You can put your hands on it and work.

"Left of the fang," I said, and the fang rose up gray and fanged indeed, and I ferried us left across the current with short hard strokes, Astrid paddling when I called it, matching me better than anyone I'd ever had in that seat except maybe nobody. The drop hit. The world became noise and cold and a wall of standing water taller than the boat, and then we were inside it, inside the roar, the aluminum flexing around us like a drum.

"Right! Right side, pull!"

She pulled. God, she pulled. Whatever else Astrid Vane was — debtor, drifter, keeper of other people's promises — she was strong in a way that had nothing to do with muscle and everything to do with refusal. The boat refused to die. We punched through the trough behind the fang, took green water over the bow, and then the hole came, the big one, the one I'd read from the rim and told myself was survivable, and it was NOT survivable head-on, and I screamed "LEFT, ALL OF IT, LEFT—" and she threw her whole body into the stroke and the bow swung and we skated along the hole's shoulder close enough to see down into it, into that slow green turning eye where the drowned dredge barge still lived, and then—

Then the lateral wave caught us anyway.

I felt the boat go weightless. I heard Astrid say something short and profane, and then the Kestrel picked us up like a toy and swept us sideways into the sieve rocks, and everything after that was instinct and prayer.

"DOWN!"

She went down. Flat in the bilge, arms over her head, no hesitation, no argument — and that obedience saved us both, because the boat pinned against the sieve for one endless second, water stacking up against the upstream side, and then the current found the gap and flushed us through sideways between two boulders with four inches to spare on either side, scraping metal on stone in a shriek I will hear in my sleep for the rest of my life.

And then we were through. Below the Throat the river widened, calmed, went glassy and stunned, as if it were embarrassed by what it had just done.

We floated. We dripped. The boat sat low in the water, taking on slow water through a new seam somewhere aft.

Astrid lifted her head out of the bilge. Her hair was plastered to her face. She spat out river.

"Is it always like that?"

"No," I said. My voice shook. "Usually it's worse."

She laughed. It burst out of her before she could stop it, a real laugh, startled out of her like the yes had been startled out of her on the roadside, and hearing it I started laughing too, both of us laughing like idiots in a leaking boat in the middle of a gorge neither of our families had ever escaped from, and I understood suddenly why people did stupid things together. Because afterward, you're not strangers anymore. The river had done in ninety seconds what a week of talking couldn't.

"Bail," I managed. "Bail now, laugh later."

She bailed. She was good at that too — fast, economical, no wasted motion. I watched her work and revised my ledger of her again. Third revision since the ferry office. I was starting to suspect the ledger would never balance, that she was one of those numbers that kept moving.

* * *

We pulled out at a gravel bench a mile below the rapids to patch the seam. While the epoxy cured, I built a driftwood fire to dry our gear, and Astrid sat across from it turning her boots upside down to drain them, and the quiet between us had changed texture. It wasn't guarded anymore. It was just quiet.

"My father used to run this stretch," I said, before I could decide not to. "Before he left. He ran the Throat twice, that I know of. Once for sport and once hauling assay samples out of the upper canyon." I poked the fire. "Everyone in town says he was the best boatman the valley ever produced. Everyone also says he abandoned us. Both things are true. I've spent eleven years trying to make them fit in the same sentence."

Astrid was silent a moment. Then she reached into her jacket — the inner pocket, the one she'd kept dry through all of it, I noticed, the pocket where the map lived — and instead of the map she brought out a folded page. Ledger paper. Columns of figures in a cramped hand.

"This fell out of the oilcloth. I should have shown you at the office. I didn't, because I didn't understand it yet, and because—" she exhaled "—because I'm not good at handing people things. But you deserve to see it before we go further."

I took it. Numbers, dates, names. Most of it meant nothing to me. Two things did not.

The name *Meridian Syndicate*, appearing eleven times.

And the name *Halloran*, appearing beside entries marked with a symbol I recognized from my father's old survey notes — the mark he'd used for silver.

"What is this?"

"A rigged claim, as far as I can tell. Your father's accounting of it. Money moved, claims shuffled, permits bought." She fed a stick to the fire. "There are men following this river right now who want that page and the map both, and they aren't kind men. One of them already found me once. He smiled the entire time."

I looked at the ledger, then at her. "Why are you telling me this now? On the water, where I can't walk away?"

"Because you almost died next to me an hour ago, and I found I minded." She said it plainly, no drama, eyes on the fire. "And because you asked me what I got out of your father's promise, and the true answer is: nothing yet. Maybe trouble. Maybe worse. But I keep saying yes to this thing, Wren, and each time I do, it costs me more, and I keep doing it anyway, and I wanted you to know that. That's all the honesty I've got today."

The fire cracked. Downstream, the river ran smooth and brown-green over its bed, carrying us toward whatever my father had hidden at its source.

I folded the ledger and handed it back. "Keep it. You're better at numbers than me." Then, because the river had knocked something loose in me that the rapids hadn't: "When we make camp tonight, you're going to tell me exactly how he died. All of it. Every detail. No smoothing it over."

"All of it," she agreed.

"And Astrid?" I stood, brushing gravel off my knees, and looked down at her — this woman who chased storms because stopping scared her more than lightning did, who'd promised a dying stranger something she couldn't afford, who bailed water like she was refusing to drown. "For what it's worth. In the Throat, when I called the stroke — you didn't hesitate. Not once."

She looked up at me, and for a moment the storm-chaser's armor slipped and something younger showed underneath, something surprised to be seen.

"Neither did you," she said.

We launched again into the long green water, and I didn't trust her yet — trust was a bridge my father had burned and I wasn't rebuilding it for any drifter with a soft voice and a hard paddle stroke — but somewhere below the Throat, in the spray and the screaming and the four inches of luck on either side of the hull, something had shifted its weight. A flicker. A pilot light.

We rounded the next bend, and Astrid went rigid in her seat.

"Smoke," she said quietly. "Upstream side, two miles. Somebody's camped on this river. And it's fresh."
