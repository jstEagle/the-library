## 7. Optimists with Ledgers

The trading post had been dead longer than Wren had been alive. Astrid could tell by the way the road had forgotten it — two ruts gone soft with grass, a leaning sign whose paint had surrendered to lichen until only the word POST remained legible, and even that looked like an apology. The building itself was a long, low thing of gray timber, porch sagging in the middle like a tired back, windows staring out at the river with the blank patience of a skull.

They pulled the boat trailer into the weeds behind it just as the light went copper. Upstream, the gorge walls had narrowed to a throat, and the river ran loud enough through them that Astrid felt it in her teeth before she heard it. That sound was a clock. It told her how much water was coming down from the high country, and what it said was: *more than you'd like.*

"Fuel's low," Wren said, climbing out, working a kink out of her shoulder. "We should've topped up at the Ford."

"There's nothing to top up from for sixty miles." Astrid popped the truck's hood anyway, out of habit, and stared at an engine she already knew was fine. "We camp here tonight. Run the narrows in the morning, full light."

Wren squinted at her. The girl read water the way other people read faces — instantly, and without mercy — but she hadn't yet learned to read weather, and Astrid wasn't going to teach her why this particular evening made her want walls on three sides. There was a cell of instability stacking itself over the escarpment behind them. She'd been watching it build since noon: a hard anvil edge, a green cast under the base that meant hail, a wind field organizing itself with the slow confidence of something that intended to be remembered. By tomorrow night it would be over the headwaters. By tomorrow night they needed to be past the narrows or above them entirely.

She didn't say any of that. She said, "There might be canned goods left inside. Places like this always have one shelf nobody looted."

* * *

Inside smelled of dust and mouse and kerosene gone to varnish. The trading post's great room still held its counter, its iron stove, a wall of pegs where fifty years of trappers' packs had hung. A calendar from a year neither of them had been born advertised fertilizer. Wren moved through it with her lantern up, touching things lightly, the way you check whether a place is inhabited by ghosts or just history.

Astrid found the shelf. Canned peaches, six cans, labels bleached to ghosts of themselves. A tin of coffee. A brick of salt. She was loading them into her pack when she saw, through the front window, the headlights.

Not approaching. Parked. Half a mile down the valley road, engine off, lights off now too — which meant whoever it was had been sitting there watching the trading post decide whether to be worth the walk.

Her hands went still on the cans.

She knew the car. You learned cars in her line of work the way you learned storms — by their silhouettes, their tells, the way they moved before they committed. A dark sedan, heavy, city-clean even under valley dust. Meridian Syndicate issued them to collectors the way churches issued hymnals.

Voss.

Her stomach did what it always did, the old familiar drop, the body remembering debt before the mind finished the arithmetic. But underneath the dread, something new had moved in and taken up space, and it took her a second to identify it, because it wasn't about the money at all.

Wren was across the room, holding up a rusted trap to the lantern light, saying something about the springs, about whether the old ferry cable upstream might still hold. And Astrid stood there with stolen peaches in her arms and understood that the cold weight in her chest wasn't fear for herself.

It was the fear of the door opening and the girl walking out of it.

*When did that happen?* Three days on a river. Four nights of bad sleep and burned coffee and Wren calling her *chaser* like it was half insult, half title. Somewhere between the first rapid and last night's fire, Astrid had stopped being alone in her own truck, and she hadn't noticed the exact moment, the way you never notice the moment you stop being hungry.

"Vane." Wren set the trap down. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting cans." Astrid smiled, and hated how easy the smile came, how many thousands of times she'd practiced it on men with ledgers. "Eat. Sleep. I'll take first watch."

"You've taken every watch."

"I sleep fine in moving vehicles. It's my one talent."

Wren gave her a look — the look, the one that stripped paint — then unrolled her bedroll by the stove and lay down facing the wall, because she trusted Astrid exactly as far as the river had forced her to, and not one inch further. Astrid sat in the dark by the window with the lantern off and watched the sedan's darkness half a mile away, and did the arithmetic she always did, and for the first time the numbers wouldn't stay in their columns.

*Debt: eleven thousand four hundred and change. Interest: compounding weekly. Time remaining on Voss's reset deadline: nine days.* Against that: one map, oilcloth-wrapped, riding against her ribs in an inside pocket. One ledger, Thorn's, hidden in the truck's spare-tire well, full of names and numbers that could hang half the Syndicate's claim business if it ever reached the right desk. One girl, asleep by a dead stove, breathing slow.

Voss would offer to trade. Voss always offered; it was cheaper than violence and the Syndicate loved cheap. And Astrid knew, with the sick clarity of someone who has run this calculation before, that she was going to stand in that doorway and stall him, and that stalling was a skill she had, and that the reason she'd need the skill was that some traitor part of her — the part that had spent six years paying interest instead of principal, the part that had signed the original papers with a grin because the man across the desk had seemed so friendly — that part would hear the number he named and feel the pull of it like current.

She watched the sedan. The sedan watched the trading post. Neither blinked.

* * *

He came at first light, walking up the road alone, hands visible, coat open — all the little theater of harmlessness that Voss wore like cologne. He was a compact man with a barbered beard and reading glasses on a chain, and he carried a leather folio the way a priest carries scripture. Behind him, at the treeline by the sedan, two shapes stood with the stillness of men paid to be furniture.

Astrid met him on the porch, boots on, jacket zipped, Wren still inside — she'd checked twice, had left the girl sleeping, had told herself it was mercy and known it was strategy. Keep them apart. Keep the map out of his eyesight. Keep Wren from hearing whatever number he named until Astrid had decided what to do with it herself.

"Astrid Vane." Voss smiled up at her from the bottom step, warm as bank lighting. "You look well. Rivers agree with you."

"Meridian agrees with rivers," Astrid said. "You both take everything downstream eventually."

He laughed, genuinely, and that was the worst thing about him — the laugh was real. "I've missed you. May I?" He gestured at the step beside her and didn't wait for permission, climbing up and settling himself, folio on knees, glasses coming out on their chain and perched on his nose like punctuation. "I'll be brief, because I respect your time and I know you're running a schedule. Elias Thorn is dead. Tragic. The map he sold you is company property — he was carrying it against a debt of his own, did you know that? Old man's obligations die hard. We simply want the asset returned."

"The asset," Astrid said, "is a piece of oilcloth."

"The asset," Voss agreed pleasantly, "is the source of the Kestrel River, and there are parties who value it considerably more than we do, which is why we're motivated to be reasonable." He opened the folio. Inside, she saw, were papers arranged with loving precision, and on top, a single sheet turned toward her with two fingers. "Here's what I'm authorized to offer. Full forgiveness of principal. Forgiveness of accrued interest — all of it, the whole ugly snowball, gone. Your file closed. Your photograph removed from our active ledger. You walk away clean, today, with a signed release I'll draft myself while you watch."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; silence was his compound interest.

"And in exchange," Astrid said.

"The map. And the girl."

The words landed in Astrid's chest like stones dropped down a well — she heard them fall a long way before anything stopped them. She kept her face still, because Voss was watching her face the way she watched cloud bases, reading every flicker for direction.

"The girl isn't mine to hand over."

"She's Thorn's heir. Thorn owed us. Debts pass to blood, Astrid, that's practically scripture where we're from." He tapped the paper. "And she's standing between several very serious people and a great deal of money, which is a dangerous place for anyone to stand, especially someone young and attached to a town full of people who'd grieve her. Whereas you—" he spread his hands, the picture of generosity— "you're not attached to anything. That's always been your finest quality. You travel light. I've always admired it."

*You're not attached to anything.* He said it like a compliment, and it slid into her like a blade finding an old scar, because it was true, it had been true for six years, it was the entire architecture of her life — no lease, no forwarding address, no one waiting anywhere with her name in their mouth. She had built it that way on purpose, after the signing, after the friendly man with the ledger had turned out to have partners with tire irons, after she'd learned that everything you can't carry gets used against you.

And now here was a man pricing her out of the one thing she'd picked up that couldn't be carried.

"How long do I have to answer?"

"Sunset." He folded the folio. "I know you. You'll want to run first — go ahead, look at the truck, think about the north road. But the north road has my colleagues on it, and the river has Halloran's people, and honestly, Astrid, running is just the slower way of arriving where I'm asking you to arrive." He stood, brushed off his coat, and paused at the top step, close enough that she could smell his soap, absurdly, expensively clean. "For what it's worth — and I mean this professionally — I hope you take it. You're one of the optimists, Astrid. You actually believe the numbers will work out. Men like me collect optimists. It's sad, really. They always think the ledger is negotiable and it never is. Only the people are."

He walked back down the road. At the sedan he stopped, turned, and raised one hand — not a wave, a countdown, though she couldn't have said how many hours each finger bought.

Then the car sat back down in the trees to wait, patient as weather.

* * *

Astrid didn't go inside right away. She sat on the porch with the offer folded into her mind like a knife folded into a pocket, and she made herself look at it straight.

Eleven thousand dollars. Six years of her life measured in weekly payments that never touched the principal, in storm-chasing contracts that put her under hail and beside tornado-warned cells because the pay cleared, in gas-station sleeps and county-road dawns and the specific loneliness of a woman whose only permanent address was a notebook column of figures. All of it, erased, for a piece of oilcloth and a girl she'd known four days.

And the honest thing — the thing she made herself hold up to the light, turning it, refusing to look away — was that for about four seconds, when Voss said *full forgiveness*, something in her had leaned toward it. Not decided. Leaned. The way a tree leans in wind. And the shame of that lean was worse than the debt had ever been, because the debt was Voss's construction, but the lean was hers.

She got up and went inside to tell Wren herself. That was the plan. Walk in, wake her, lay it out flat — the offer, the deadline, the sunset — because Astrid had spent six years being lied to by friendly people and she would not become one, not even usefully, not even kindly.

But the bedroll by the stove was empty. Cold. Folded with a precision that was itself a message.

And Wren was standing in the doorway of the back room, where the wall was thin as a hymn book and the morning air carried voices up the valley like a wire carries current. She had her father's compass in one hand and her face was doing something Astrid had never seen it do: nothing at all. Completely still. The river-face. The face Wren wore when the water under the boat went from green to brown and every rock became a decision.

"How much," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"*How much.*"

"Everything. The whole debt. For the map and for you." Astrid kept her voice level and her hands where he could see them, empty. "I was coming to tell you. I was going to tell you before I answered him."

"But you didn't say no." Wren's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about her anger; it went quiet and deep, like water over a hole. "You sat out there and you listened to the whole number. You let him finish."

"Yes. I did. Because I wanted to know exactly what I was refusing, and because I don't lie to you, and because—" Astrid stopped. Started again, slower, hating every word and meaning all of them. "Because some part of me is the person he thinks I am, and I needed to hear the price out loud to know how loud my own no was going to be. It's very loud, Wren. It's the loudest thing I own."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the river ran loud through the narrows, and somewhere down the valley a car door opened and closed, soft as a period at the end of a sentence.

"My father promised me nothing for nineteen years," Wren said finally. "You promised me nothing four days ago. Turns out that's the family trade." She slung her pack over her shoulder and pushed past Astrid onto the porch, toward the boat, toward the river, toward the narrows and the rising water and the day ahead. "Sunset's a long way off. Let's spend it moving."

Astrid followed her down the steps, and behind them, in the trees, the sedan's engine started — not following, not yet. Just idling. Counting.

And high over the western escarpment, where the sky had been stacking its green-gray deck all night, the first thunder rolled down the valley like something clearing its throat.
