## 8. Ghost Camp at Miller's Bend

The river told us before we saw it.

Wren had grown up reading water the way other people read faces — the way it went quiet around a bend, the way driftwood piled in a certain pattern when the current had been pushed sideways by something new. Two miles below the old Miller's Bend landing, the driftwood started stacking wrong. Fresh cottonwood limbs, bark still on them, wedged against the rocks in a line no flood had made.

"Somebody's been dragging boats through here," she said, and Astrid, hunched in the bow with the map spread across her knees under a sheet of plastic, looked up with that expression she wore when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"How recent?"

Wren touched one of the limbs. The wood was still wet at the break. "Yesterday. Maybe this morning."

They ran the rest of the bend in silence, both of them watching the banks now instead of the water, and that was its own kind of wrong. A river you watched the banks of was a river you were afraid of.

Miller's Bend announced itself the way all dead towns did: first the smell — rust and creosote and the sweet rot of timber that had been wet and dried and wet again for eighty years — then the chimneys. They rose out of the willows like fingers, a dozen of them, all that was left of houses whose walls had been hauled away or burned or simply given up. Silver had built Miller's Bend in fourteen months and abandoned it in three, and the river had spent the century since quietly taking back everything else.

Wren backed the boat into an eddy below the old stamp mill, its wheel still hanging from a rotted axle like a broken jaw, and tied off to a piling that groaned but held.

"Camp's on the flat," Astrid said quietly. She'd seen it too. A thread of smoke, thin and gray, rising from behind the assay office.

"Could be campers."

"Campers don't drag their boats over a riffle at dawn." Astrid folded the map with the care of someone handling something live. "That's Halloran's people."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure about smoke, and I'm sure about fresh tracks, and I'm sure that every mile we've gained on them they've somehow gained two." Astrid's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a flat, humorless line. "I'm also sure that if they're ahead of us, they haven't found what they're looking for yet. Because if they had, they wouldn't still be following the river. They'd be gone."

Wren sat with that. The boat rocked against the piling. Somewhere up on the flat, the smoke kept rising, patient as a held breath.

"So we go around them," she said. "Portage high, come back to the water above the town."

"Or," Astrid said, and stopped. Wren had learned that about her in six days on the river: Astrid Vane never finished a sentence she thought would cost her. She made you ask.

"Or what?"

"Or we find out what they know. What Thorn's partners have figured out that we haven't." She was looking at the assay office, not the smoke. "Your father hid something in this town, Wren. He told me enough to know that much. And he told me the map alone wasn't the whole of it. There are samples. Rock. Proof. He said—" Astrid paused, and for once she finished it anyway, like pulling a splinter. "He said the proof was the part worth killing for. The map just tells you where to dig. The samples tell you what you'll find."

The wind came down the gorge and moved the willows, and the smoke bent, and straightened.

"They're between us and it," Wren said.

"Probably."

"Then we go get it anyway."

Astrid looked at her then, really looked, the way she rarely did — most of the time her eyes slid past people like they were weather she'd already decided not to wait out. "You understand what your father was, don't you? What he did for a living before he came home to die?"

"He found things."

"He found things for men who took them." Astrid said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "And the men he found them for are camped up there right now, smoking a fire and waiting for the river to hand them a rich man's retirement. If we let them find those samples first—"

"They won't." Wren heard her own voice come out flat and hard, her mother's voice, the ferrywoman's voice, the one that told drunks to sit down and told the spring flood it could wait. "Because my father didn't trust them either. Whatever he hid, he hid it so nobody could walk in and take it. Not even them. *Especially* not them."

* * *

They came into Miller's Bend from above, through the graveyard.

It seemed fitting, though Wren wasn't sentimental about graveyards; the valley had too many of them, most of them unmarked. This one had thirty-odd stones leaning in the sagebrush, half the names worn to ghosts themselves, and beyond it the town fell away in terraces toward the river — Main Street reduced to a trench of foundations, the hotel a roofless shell with wallpaper still clinging to one interior wall like shed skin, faded roses climbing toward a sky that hadn't been asked to look at them in a hundred years.

Astrid moved like she'd been born in places like this. Low, quiet, always choosing the shadow side of the street even though there was no one to see. She carried the map inside her jacket now, against her body, and Wren had noticed she slept with it too, curled around it like a second spine.

"The assay office is the stone building," Astrid breathed. "Down by the mill. Everything else was wood. Wood burns, stone doesn't. If Elias wanted something to last—"

The voice came from the hotel.

"—no, I'm telling you it's not in the office, I've been through it twice. Floorboards, walls, the safe's empty and rusted through. He wouldn't have hidden it somewhere obvious anyway. You knew Elias. The man hid his tobacco in the coffee tin and his money in the dog's collar."

A second voice, younger, sullen: "Then where?"

"That's what we're being paid to figure out. Start on the boarding house. Take it apart board by board if you have to."

Wren pressed herself into the doorway of the collapsed saloon, dust sifting down over her shoulders, and felt Astrid go very still beside her. Through a gap in the fallen planking she could see them: four men working the far end of Main Street with the unhurried thoroughness of professionals, packs stacked by the hotel steps, rifles leaned against the wall in easy reach. And standing apart from the work, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring architecture, a fifth — tall, silver-haired, wearing a canvas coat too clean for the country.

Halloran himself.

She'd never seen him. She knew him anyway, the way you know a storm by the pressure in your ears. Her father had said the name only twice in the letters, and both times he'd written it slowly, like setting down something heavy.

"Three days," Halloran was saying, conversational, carrying. "We've been on this river three days behind a dead man's ghost, gentlemen, and I confess my patience is becoming a renewable resource. It simply keeps making more of itself." A ripple of careful laughter from the others. "But patience is not infinite, and neither is the season. The Syndicate's permit hearing is in five weeks. If we do not put rock on a table in Denver before then, the lawyers win, and I promise you the lawyers' version of this valley has no river in it at all."

*The Syndicate.* Wren's stomach turned over. She glanced at Astrid and found Astrid already looking at her, and in the dimness of the ruined saloon they exchanged the whole conversation in a single glance: *the same men who own Astrid's debt. The same paper. The same machine.*

Her father hadn't run from the valley because he was weak. He'd run because these men had followed him home.

"We should pull back," Astrid whispered. "Come back after dark."

"No."

"Wren—"

"They're tearing apart buildings. My father's buildings. The town he—" She stopped, jaw tight. "We can't let them be the ones who find it. Whatever it is. If they find it, it's over — permits, hearings, Denver, all of it. We find it first or we lose everything, and I am not losing this town to men who talk about it like acreage."

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. Then she exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh, entirely without humor. "You sound like him."

"Don't."

"It's not an insult." She was quiet a beat. "Elias Thorn, forty years old, standing in a bar in Meridian City, telling me about the valley he came from. Same voice. Like the place was a person he was protecting." She shook it off. "Fine. But we do this smart. While they're hammering boards on Main Street, we take the assay office. In and out. Ten minutes."

"And if they catch us?"

"Then we're two lost boaters looking for shelter," Astrid said, "and I'm very good at lying to dangerous men. It's practically my profession."

* * *

The assay office was stone, all right — squat, windowless on the river side, its door swollen shut with a century of wet and dry. Astrid got a shoulder into it while Wren watched the street, and on the third try the frame cracked and gave with a sound that seemed, in the silence, loud enough to raise the dead of the graveyard behind them.

Inside: dust, a collapsed desk, a rusted stove, shelves of sample trays emptied long ago by scavengers or time. Afternoon light fell through the gap where a roof slate had slipped, lighting the room like a stage.

"Ten minutes," Astrid said, and started on the near wall, tapping stones, prying at the floor.

Wren stood in the middle of the room and tried to think like her father.

Not like the man in the letters — she wasn't ready for that man yet, maybe never — but like the prospector. The man who'd hidden his money in a dog's collar. The man who'd spent his life finding what other people couldn't see.

She looked at the room the way he would have. Where would a man hide something in a building everyone knew was his? Not the floor. Not the walls. Not the safe. Everyone would tear those apart — Halloran's men were doing it right now, fifty yards away.

So he'd hide it where tearing it apart would destroy it. Where greed itself would be the lock.

Her eyes found the stove. Cast iron, squat, its firebox door hanging open. Scavengers would have checked the firebox — and they had; the ash inside had been disturbed years ago. But the stovepipe. The stovepipe went up through the ceiling gap, and where it passed through, the roofing around it was newer than the rest. Not new. *Replaced.* One sheet of tin, different rust, different nail heads.

"Astrid," she whispered. "The pipe."

Astrid crossed the room in three strides, looked where Wren pointed, and something flickered across her face — respect, quick and unhidden. She climbed onto the desk, worked the pipe loose at the seam, and tipped it.

Something slid out, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy, and Astrid caught it against her chest before it hit the floor.

They knelt together in the dust and unwrapped it. Inside the cloth: a canvas sample bag, and inside the bag, a dozen cores of rock, each labeled in her father's tight, slanted hand — *MB-1 through MB-12, 40 ft., vein matter* — and beneath the samples, folded small, a single typed page on letterhead so old the paper had gone the color of tea.

Wren unfolded it. Read it. Read it again.

*Assay report, Miller's Bend Group. Sample MB-4: 312 oz. silver per ton. Sample MB-7: 288 oz. Sample MB-9: 401 oz. Vein structure indicates continuation northeast at shallow depth.*

Northeast. She turned the page around, oriented herself by the light, by the shape of the town she'd studied on the map a hundred times since Kestrel's Ford. Northeast of Miller's Bend. Upstream. Up the gorge.

Toward home.

"Oh," she said. Just that. The word came out of her small and strange, like someone else's voice.

"What?" Astrid took the page, read it fast, professional, and Wren watched the understanding land on her face in stages — the numbers first, then the direction, then the meaning. "Oh," Astrid echoed, softer.

The lode didn't end at Miller's Bend. It had never ended here. The silver ran under the river, under the gorge, under the willow flats and the ferry crossing and the schoolhouse and the church and every foundation in Kestrel's Ford. Under the graves. Under Wren's whole life, all nineteen years of it, she had been walking on a fortune deep enough to buy the valley ten times over — and the only thing standing between that fortune and the men upstairs was a dead man's secret and a piece of paper in a stranger's jacket.

"That's why he left," Wren said. Her hands had started shaking. She watched them shake like they belonged to somebody else. "That's why he really left. Not debt. Not shame. He found this — or he helped find this — and he understood what would happen. Strip-mine. The whole canyon. They'd take the mountain down to the riverbed." She looked up at Astrid, and her eyes were burning. "He hid the proof and he walked away and he let everyone in Kestrel's Ford think he was a coward, because a coward they'd forget. A missing man doesn't draw a claim stake."

Astrid was silent. Outside, faintly, came the crack of Halloran's men pulling apart the boarding house, board by board.

"The map stops being treasure now," Wren said. "You understand that? The minute we carry this out of here, it's not worth money anymore. It's worth a war. It's a weapon, and it's aimed at my home, and the only question is who pulls the trigger."

"Then we make sure it's us." Astrid rewrapped the samples with steady hands, stowed them in her pack beside the map, and stood. "Come on. We've still got eight minutes of their ten, and a river to outrun them on."

They slipped out the way they'd come, through the graveyard, down to the boat. Wren cast off the piling with hands that wouldn't quite stop trembling, and as the current took them and Miller's Bend sank behind the willows, she looked back once at the thin thread of smoke still rising from the enemy's fire.

Behind them, faint across the water, came a shout — one of the men, standing on the bank, pointing downstream.

At them.

"Halloran knows we're here now," Astrid said quietly, taking up her paddle.

"No," Wren said, and dug her own paddle in, hard, feeling the boat leap. "He knows his father's ghost is paddling away with the deed to his fortune. Now he *has* to follow us."

The river bent north, toward home, and they raced it.
