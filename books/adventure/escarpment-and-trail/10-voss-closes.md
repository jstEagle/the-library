## 10. Voss Closes

The portage at Sow's Ear Bend was marked on no map worth trusting — just a scar of pale rock where the river gave up on its canyon walls and slid sideways through a chute of willows. Wren spotted it first, the way she spotted everything, her eyes doing arithmetic on the water before her mouth did anything else.

"Half a mile of carry," she said, backing the boat into an eddy the color of steeped tea. "We run the gear over the gravel bar, line the boat down the left side, and put in below the falls."

Astrid looked up from the notebook. The same notebook that held storm tracks and interest tables, now crowded with river miles and Voss's probable route. "How long?"

"Two hours if we're smart. Four if we argue about it."

"Then let's be smart."

They weren't smart. They were tired — three days of whitewater and cold camps and the particular exhaustion that comes from sleeping next to a stranger who might be lying to you. The gravel bar ate their footing. The boat, heavy with waterlogged spruce, fought the lining ropes like something alive and insulted. By the time they had it half-dragged around the falls, the light had gone the color of old pewter, and Astrid's shoulders were singing.

That was when she saw the truck.

It sat on the rim road above the portage, parked casual as Sunday, engine ticking as it cooled. A man stood beside it with his hands in his pockets, watching them work, and even at two hundred yards Astrid knew the posture. Weight forward. Shoulders loose. The stance of a man who had all day and knew you didn't.

Voss.

"Wren." She said it low, not moving. "Don't look up. Rim road. Our friend found us."

Wren's hands kept working the rope. Only her eyes moved, flicking up and back in half a second, a bird's accounting. "One man visible. Probably two more in the trees. That's how they do it — one where you look, two where you don't."

"You've met his kind before?"

"I grew up in a valley men like that want to own." She tied off the rope with a knot Astrid didn't recognize and finally turned, wiping her hands on her thighs. Her face was calm in a way that frightened Astrid more than the truck did. "So. What's the play, storm-chaser? You know these people better than I do."

And there it was, laid out flat between them like a card turned face-up. *You know these people.* Because Wren had figured it out days ago — had probably known since the gas station, since the way Astrid flinched at white sedans — that the debt chasing Astrid down the river wasn't metaphorical.

"The play," Astrid said slowly, "is that he wants me, not you. He's offered before. My debt walks away if I hand over the map and walk away from you."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him to go to hell."

"That was days ago. People get reasonable when they're wet and tired." Wren's jaw was set. "I'm not going anywhere. But I want to hear you say what we're doing."

Astrid looked at the river. It was coming up — she'd been reading it all morning without quite admitting it to herself. The water against the lining rocks had climbed four inches since dawn. The sky over the western escarpment had that bruised, stacked look she'd spent ten years learning to translate: a supercell building over the high plateau, dragging its rain curtain east, straight toward the headwaters of this drainage. Six hours, maybe eight, and this gentle green river would become a brown animal.

Six hours was also enough time to be gone. There was a stock trail off the right side of the portage, she'd seen it on Thorn's map — climbed to the rim, cut south, lost themselves in the sage. Running. Always running. She'd gotten so good at running that she'd mistaken it for a skill instead of a symptom.

She thought about the gas station, six weeks ago, the smiling man with the ledger who'd reset her deadline like a man resetting a watch. She'd driven all night after that, because stopping meant feeling it. She'd been driving all night for three years.

"I say," Astrid heard herself say, "we stop running."

* * *

They made it look natural. That was the whole trick — keep carrying gear, keep sweating, keep being two women wrestling a boat around a waterfall, while Astrid's mind ran the geometry of the bend.

The portage sat in a horseshoe of rock. The river came in fast on the north side, dropped over the falls, and pooled below before threading out through the willow chute. The rim road overlooked the whole horseshoe from the south. Voss's position was good for watching and bad for approaching — any man coming down off the rim had to funnel through a single gully, forty yards of open scree, before he reached the gravel bar.

Forty yards of open ground. Astrid had spent a decade watching storms choose their corridors. Terrain was terrain. Water was water. Men were just weather that bled.

"There," she murmured, nodding at the gully while pretending to fight a stuck duffel. "That's his door. Anybody comes down, they come down there, single file, exposed."

"And we do what, throw rocks at them?"

"We make the door expensive." She glanced at the sky, measuring. Rain first, maybe ninety minutes. Then the river. "Here's the ugly part. Somebody has to stand in the open and be interesting while the rest happens."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. "You're volunteering."

"I owe money to these people. You don't. And I'm faster than I look."

"You look pretty fast."

"I'm slower than I look. That's why it works. He'll believe he can catch me."

Something shifted in Wren's face — not softness exactly, but a recalculation, the way she'd looked at Astrid the day after the first rapid, when Astrid had admitted the map was bought with a dying man's last breath. Like she was updating a ledger of her own.

"If this goes wrong," Wren said, "I'm coming back for you. I want that understood. I don't leave people."

"Noted." And Astrid found, to her surprise, that she believed it — and that believing it changed the weight of everything, made the plan heavier and lighter at once. Nobody had ever been coming back for her before. She'd built a whole life on the assumption.

* * *

Voss came down the gully at 4:40 in the afternoon, with the rain just starting to speckle the scree, and he brought two men with him, exactly as predicted, single file, exactly as hoped.

He'd changed since the gas station. Or rather, he hadn't changed at all, and that was the horror of him — the same pleasant face, the same voice like a man recommending a restaurant. "Ms. Vane!" he called across the gravel bar, spreading his arms as if greeting her at a wedding. "You are extremely difficult to find, and I want you to know the office respects that. We respect resourcefulness here."

"Voss." She stood by the boat, hands visible, heart going like a drumline. Behind her, at the edge of her vision, Wren slipped into the willows with the rope coil. "You're a long way from paved roads."

"So are you." He walked closer, boots crunching, his two men fanning wide with the unhurried confidence of men who'd done this many times in places with fewer witnesses. "Let's skip the part where we pretend this is a coincidence. Meridian's offer stands. Improved, actually — I have authority now. Full forgiveness. Not restructuring. Forgiveness. The number goes to zero, Ms. Vane. Zero. You know what that word is worth to you."

Astrid did know. She knew the number to the penny, had carried it so long it had worn a groove in her like a river wears a canyon. Eleven thousand four hundred and some dollars, compounding monthly, plus the original sin of having signed with people whose contracts were enforced by baseball bats and cheerfulness.

Zero. He said it like a gift.

"And the girl?" she said.

"Goes home to her valley. Unharmed, I promise you. We're not monsters, Astrid — may I call you Astrid? Halloran gets the map, the claim proceeds legally, everyone gets rich except the dead man, who doesn't care anymore. You drive away free. First time in three years your mirrors are clean." He smiled. It was a genuinely nice smile. That was the worst thing about the Syndicate; they hired people who were good at being liked. "You could stop running today. Isn't that all you've ever wanted?"

And God help her, for three full seconds, it was. The vertigo of it nearly took her knees. A life with no number attached. Sleep without the notebook under her pillow.

Then she heard Wren's voice in her head: *I don't leave people.*

"No," Astrid said.

Voss sighed, theatrical, almost fond. "I was afraid you'd say that. Denny —"

Denny stepped forward, and the rain chose that moment to arrive in earnest, dropping out of the bruised sky in fat, warm sheets. Visibility collapsed to thirty yards. The scree went slick. And Astrid stopped calculating exits and started calculating angles.

She gave them the beating they'd come for. That was the deal she'd struck with herself in the willows, the terms she hadn't spoken aloud: take it, survive it, buy the minutes. Denny caught her first blow on the shoulder and put her down on the gravel twice. Gravel takes skin. She'd forgotten how much. The second man — Reyes, quiet, professional — held her arms while Voss crouched and went through her jacket with apologetic fingers, finding the oilcloth roll inside.

"Thank you," Voss said sincerely, standing, tucking the map into his coat. "Genuinely. This was easier than the paperwork alternative."

"It's not the map," Astrid said, spitting blood into the rising river.

Voss paused. "Excuse me?"

"Check it. Go on. You've got dry hands and I've got nothing but time."

He almost didn't. Professional discipline made him; vanity unmade him. He unrolled the oilcloth, and Astrid watched his face do the arithmetic — the careful surveyor's hand, yes, Thorn's hand unmistakably — but the markings were wrong. Tributaries that didn't exist. Elevations off by hundreds of feet. A source drawn in the wrong canyon entirely, because Astrid had copied it by firelight two nights ago with Wren's knowledge and Wren's grudging, furious assistance, and the real map rode in a waterproof bag against Wren's ribs.

Voss looked up. For the first time, the pleasant face had weather on it.

"Where is it."

"Where's yours?" Astrid smiled through split lips. "Funny how that works."

He hit her then, no theater in it, and she went down again and stayed down longer than she wanted, tasting copper and rain. Above the roar of the falls, beneath it, underneath everything, she heard the sound she'd been listening for all along: the river changing its voice. Deepening. The pool below the falls swallowing its own edges.

"Sir," Reyes said quietly. "The water."

Everyone looked. The river had come up a foot in twenty minutes. The willow chute was gone — not gone, drowned, the willows combing flat in the current. The eddy where they'd landed the boat was a boil. And up-canyon, the rain curtain was still arriving, mile after mile of it, the storm Astrid had been tracking for two days unloading itself onto the headwaters with the patience of a debt collector.

"The trail's flooding," Denny said. "Rim road's a clay chute in this. We go upstream or downstream, and downstream's—"

"Downstream's a class five gorge at flood stage," Astrid said from the ground, and laughed, and hated the sound of it, wild and wet. "But hey. You've got a boat."

They didn't have a boat. Their truck sat on a rim road that was turning to grease. The only boat in the world was fifty yards away, tied in the willows, with Wren Thorn somewhere near it, and Astrid had maybe one move left and no idea if it would work.

"Voss," she said. "Last honest thing I'll tell you. That storm sits on the headwaters for six more hours. Everything above us drains past this bend. You can wait it out on the rim — cold, wet, hungry, but alive — or you can try to take me with you wherever you're going and find out what this river thinks of your plans."

Voss looked at the water. Looked at the map in his coat — the fake, the worthless beautiful fake. Looked at Astrid bleeding on the gravel, and made the mistake she'd built the entire afternoon around:

He hesitated.

In that hesitation, the willows exploded. Wren came out of them with the coiled rope throwing loops like a lasso, and the first loop took Denny across the knees and hauled him off his feet into the shallows, and the river — the rising, furious, grateful river — took him ten feet before he caught a willow root. Reyes turned toward the motion, and Astrid, who had spent ten years reading which way a storm would break, read which way a man would break, and rolled into the backs of his knees.

What followed was short, graceless, and effective. Voss got a boot in the chest from Wren and went down hard on the rocks. Denny clung to his willow, coughing. Reyes, arm twisted behind him in the shallows, decided with visible professionalism that his contract did not cover drowning.

"Get him out of the water," Astrid panted. "All of them. Above the high-water mark."

"Why?" Wren demanded, hauling Reyes by the collar. "Why save them?"

"Because we're not them." She said it and felt it land somewhere in her own chest like a keystone settling. "Tie them. Take their radio. Leave them food — no. Leave them one granola bar each. I'm not a saint."

They trussed the three of them to a strainer log well above the flood line, wrists lashed, comfortable-ish, furious. Voss talked the whole time — offers, threats, a genuinely impressive range of emotional registers — until Wren gagged him with his own sock, gently, almost kindly.

Astrid stood at the water's edge, dripping, bleeding, shaking, and realized her legs weren't shaking from fear. They were shaking from staying. Three years of running, and the first time she planted her feet, the ground held.

The boat came free of the willows. They loaded what was left of the gear. Upstream, invisible behind the rain, the storm kept eating the headwaters, and somewhere ahead of them, Halloran's expedition was about to learn what that meant.

"Below the falls," Wren said, taking the stern. "Flood stage. You sure?"

Astrid looked at the river — brown, huge, alive, the exact thing she'd spent her whole life chasing from the outside. Now she was in it.

"Nowhere else I'd rather be," she said, and meant it, and pushed off.
