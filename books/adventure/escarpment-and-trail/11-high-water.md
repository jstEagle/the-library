## 11. High Water

The rain started an hour before dawn, and Wren knew before Astrid said a word that it was wrong.

She'd grown up on this river. She knew its moods the way she knew her own hands — the lazy brown heat of July, the green glass of September, the mean gray shiver of spring melt. This was none of those. This rain came down in ropes, straight and silent, without wind to carry it, and that silence was the thing that scared her. Storms that shouted were honest. This one was keeping its own counsel.

Astrid stood at the edge of the tarp shelter with her notebook open, letting the water run off the brim of her hat, and she wasn't watching the sky. She was watching the canyon walls.

"It's early," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the same thing in the gauge rocks downstream — the high-water stain, fresh, still dark.

"Two days early." Astrid's voice had that flat calm Wren had learned to distrust and then, grudgingly, to rely on. "The system stalled over the divide last night. Everything it was going to drop over three days is coming down now." She turned a page. Her pencil moved without hesitation. "The gorge will peak by midday. Maybe sooner."

"So we camp here. Wait it out."

"No." Astrid closed the notebook. "Halloran's crew broke camp below us an hour ago. I heard their winch." She looked up, and the rain ran down her face like she didn't notice it. "They'll read the river the way a permit-holder reads it — slow, careful, hugging the banks. Which means if we wait for the flood to pass, they pass us. And if they get ahead of us—"

"They don't need to get ahead of us," Wren finished. "They just need to be between us and the source."

The map sat in its oilcloth inside Wren's jacket, against her sternum, where it had lived since Miller's Bend. She could feel it there now, heavier than paper had any right to be. Her father's handwriting. Her father's cowardice. Her father's stand. All of it pressed against her heart like a second pulse.

"We run it," Astrid said.

"At flood stage." Wren heard her own voice come out flat and hard. "You've never run this gorge at all. I've run it maybe forty times, and never above eight feet on the ferry gauge. It'll be past twelve by noon. There are holes in there that swallow whole trees."

"I know."

"You don't know. You read storms. You don't read *water*."

Astrid took a step closer, close enough that Wren could see the exhaustion under her eyes and something else underneath the exhaustion — a steadiness that hadn't been there two weeks ago, back when every mile felt like a woman fleeing her own shadow. "You're right. I don't know your river. But I know this storm, and I know what it's doing to the headwaters, and I know Halloran has forty years of gear and six men and no reason to hurry except greed." She held out the notebook. "So you read the water, and I'll read the sky, and between us we might be faster than both of them."

Wren looked at the notebook. Columns of numbers, arrows, times. Astrid had been tracking this cell for two days, calling its movement like a stockman calls strays. Somewhere in those numbers was the reason the gorge was about to become a drowning throat.

Somewhere in those numbers was also the only chance they had.

Wren took off her hat, shook it out, and put it back on. "Then we run it," she said. "And if we die, I'm haunting you first."

Something moved across Astrid's face — not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if she'd ever learned how. "Fair enough."

* * *

They launched into a river that no longer deserved the name.

The Kestrel had come up four feet overnight and was rising as Wren watched, the current carrying whole pines sideways, their roots combing the water like drowned men's hair. The put-in eddy she'd used a hundred times was gone — simply gone, folded into the main current, which ran fast and thick and the color of milk chocolate. The boat sat low in the water even unloaded. Wren had stripped everything but the oars, the spare paddle, the bail bucket, and the rope.

Astrid took the bow without being told. That was new too. Two weeks ago she'd have negotiated for the stern, or the shore, or anything that kept her options open. Now she clipped herself in and crouched low, and when Wren glanced back, Astrid was reading the water ahead with her whole body, leaning into the current like she could feel it through the hull.

"First rapid's a half-mile," Wren called over the noise. "It's a read-and-run normally. Today we don't read. We commit center-left and pray."

"Pray to who?"

"The river. It's the only one listening."

The gorge walls closed in around them, black basalt streaked with runoff — a hundred small waterfalls unspooling down the rock, feeding a river already drunk on rain. The sound changed. Wren felt it in her chest before she heard it with her ears: a deep, subterranean boom, like the gorge itself clearing its throat.

"Drop!" she yelled. "Big one! Center! PADDLE!"

The river bent and fell away beneath them.

What followed was not running a rapid. It was being permitted, briefly, to exist inside a catastrophe. The boat went weightless off the crest, hung — Wren's stomach floating free — and then slammed down into a trough full of standing waves taller than the gunwales. Water hit them like a wall. The world became brown chaos and roar and Astrid's shoulder jammed against hers, solid, steering nothing and anchoring everything.

"HOLE LEFT!" Astrid screamed, and Wren saw it — a frowning seam in the flood where the water piled and rolled back on itself, big enough to eat the boat whole. She dug the oars in and pulled until her shoulders burned, and the current, indifferent and enormous, carried them past the seam by less than a boat-length. She felt the eddy line tug at the stern, hungry, trying to flip them back into it.

Then they were through, spinning in the tail-out, both of them bailing with their hands before either thought to grab the bucket.

Astrid spat out water and laughed.

Actually laughed — a short, wild, disbelieving bark of a laugh, the first one Wren had ever heard from her, and it did something strange to Wren's chest, loosening a knot she hadn't known was tied.

"That," Astrid shouted, "was *nothing*!"

"That was the *warm-up*, you lunatic!"

* * *

The middle gorge nearly killed them twice.

The first time was a sweeper — a cottonwood the size of a church steeple, whole and thrashing, riding the current down the throat of the channel exactly where they needed to be. Wren saw it too late to dodge cleanly. There was no clean. There was only left-of-it or through-it, and through-it meant the root ball, which meant death by a thousand tons of wet wood.

"LEFT! HARD LEFT!"

They scraped. God, they scraped — the hull shrieking along the submerged trunk, the current trying to pin them, wrap them, fold the boat around the tree like paper. Wren threw her weight against the oars and felt the frame flex, felt the whole boat groan like a living thing, and then the stern swung free and the river took them again, and behind them the cottonwood ground on toward the sea it would never reach.

Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say. Wren's hands were shaking so badly she could barely hold the oars, and she looked back once and found Astrid looking forward, watching the next bend, one hand raised — *keep coming, keep coming* — and the shaking stopped. Not because the fear left. Because it had somewhere to go.

The second time was the portage rapid, Sow's Ear, which at twelve feet wasn't a rapid at all but a single continuous hydraulic half a mile long, a churning white trench with no eddies, no rest, no lines. No line existed. You entered and the river decided.

Halfway down, the wave train hit them broadside.

The boat stood up. Wren felt it happen — the hull lifting, tilting past the angle where boats come back, the world rotating, Astrid's eyes meeting hers across two feet of air and certainty — and then Astrid did the only thing left. She threw herself onto the high side, all of her, arms locked on the thwart, dead weight where dead weight was the difference, and the boat hung there, teetering on the edge of its own grave—

—and came back down.

Slammed down, shipped a ton of water, wallowing half-sunk, but upright. Wren bailed like a madwoman while Astrid paddled from the bow, and the river, having made its point, let them go. They limped into the first slack water below, both of them gasping, the boat riding so low the current lipped over the gunwales in little greedy tongues.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Astrid reached back without looking, and Wren took her hand, and they held on — hard, wordless, two people gripping the same rail of the same wreck — until their breathing came back down to something like human.

"Your father," Astrid said finally, hoarse, "ran this river his whole life?"

"He grew up on it." Wren wiped her face. Her hand came away shaking. "He never taught me. I taught myself. He was already gone."

Astrid nodded slowly, water dripping off her chin. "Well," she said. "He'd have been proud of what he didn't teach."

It was the kindest thing anyone had said to Wren in years, and it came wrapped in a joke, and she found she couldn't answer it at all. So she didn't. She just squeezed Astrid's hand once, hard, and let go, and took up the oars.

* * *

They made the lower gorge by late afternoon, running on adrenaline and a handful of jerky eaten one-handed while drifting. The rain had thinned to a steady whisper. Behind them, somewhere upstream, the flood was still peaking; ahead, the canyon began its long descent toward the country Halloran wanted to own.

Wren checked the oilcloth package for the hundredth time. Dry. Intact. Her father's tight, patient script safe inside.

"He drew this map knowing what it would cost him," she said. It wasn't a question anymore; the river had beaten the question out of her somewhere in the middle gorge. "Didn't he."

Astrid was quiet for a moment. "I think," she said carefully, "he drew it hoping nobody would ever have to use it. And then, at the end, hoping the right person would."

"The right person being me."

"The right person being whoever wouldn't sell."

Wren looked at her. Astrid Vane, who had sold weather forecasts to pay off leg-breakers, who admitted freely that she'd have burned the world for a clean ledger two months ago — sitting in the bilge water of a beat-up boat, bruised from saving Wren's life with her own body, saying *whoever wouldn't sell* like it was obvious.

Like it was Wren.

Ahead, the canyon opened its final throat, and Wren heard it before she understood it: a new sound under the river's roar. Engines. Winches. Voices, faint and carrying over water, from the far bank of the bend below.

Campfires. A lot of them.

Astrid heard it too. She went very still, then very calm, in the way Wren had learned meant danger.

"How far to the take-out?" Astrid murmured.

"Two miles." Wren eased the boat toward the shadowed bank, oars barely kissing the water. "And they're sitting on it."

Below them, around the bend, the partners' expedition waited at the mouth of the gorge — and the only path to the source ran straight through their firelight.
