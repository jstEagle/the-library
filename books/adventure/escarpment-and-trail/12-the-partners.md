## 12. The Partners

They came at dusk, the way civilized men do their worst work.

Astrid saw them first from the high bank above the portage trail, where she'd gone to scout the carry around the falls. Three figures on the far side of the river, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned whatever ground they stood on. Not Voss's crew — those men moved like hired muscle, all shoulders and hurry. These three walked like landlords.

She counted her options the way she counted storm cells: quickly, honestly, without flattery. Run upstream and lose a day they didn't have. Run downstream and run straight into Voss, who was somewhere behind them with a grudge and a broken nose to avenge. Or stand still and see what courtesy wanted.

The truck was hidden two miles back. The boat was beached below. Wren was at camp, mending the gunwale crack that had been worrying her since Miller's Bend, humming something tuneless and fierce through her teeth.

Astrid stood still.

They crossed at the shallows below the falls, single file, and she got a better look at them as they came. The one in front was sixty, maybe, silver-haired, wearing a canvas jacket that had never once been rained on hard enough to matter. He carried a walking stick he clearly didn't need and used anyway, tapping the stones like a man greeting old friends. Behind him came a heavyset woman with a surveyor's transit slung over her shoulder and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they touched. The third was young, nervous, carrying a leather document case against his chest like a breastplate.

"Astrid Vane." The silver-haired man said her name the way you'd read it off an invoice. "Halloran. Declan Halloran. I believe you've met my associates — or at least their paperwork."

"The Syndicate's collectors," Astrid said. "The ones with the smiles."

"Voss works for the Syndicate. We are not the Syndicate." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it made her skin crawl worse than any of Voss's grins had. "We were Elias Thorn's partners. Thirty years ago. Before he decided to become a ghost."

* * *

They sat her down at her own fire. That was the first thing she noticed — how naturally they did it, how Halloran took the flat stone she'd been using as a seat and settled onto it like he'd been coming to this exact spot for years. The woman with the transit — she introduced herself as Mercer, no first name offered — set up nothing, but looked at everything: the boat, the map case hanging from Astrid's pack, the way Wren's mending needle had been left stuck in the gunwale.

The boy with the document case stayed standing. He looked like he wanted to be anywhere else, which meant he was probably the only honest one among them.

"Elias," Halloran said, warming his hands at a fire he hadn't built, "was the best claim-reader I ever knew. Better than me, and I'll say so out loud. He could taste ore in the gravel. But Elias had a flaw, Ms. Vane, and the flaw was conscience. It came on him late in life, like a fever. Do you know what he did?"

"He hid something," Astrid said.

"He hid *everything*." Halloran laughed, delighted, as if the betrayal were a fine joke between old friends. "We spent eleven years and most of our money proving what he'd found — a lode, Ms. Vane, a genuine mother lode, running from the head of this river down under the valley itself. Silver, and enough of it to buy the state senate twice. And when we went to file, when we went to bring in capital—" he spread his hands, "—Elias had salted our samples, burned our surveys, and vanished. Took thirty years of partnership and folded it into a hat."

Wren had come up from the water. Astrid heard her before she saw her — boots on stone, deliberate, loud enough to announce herself. She stepped into the firelight with the mending needle still in her hand, small and bright as a fang.

"You're talking about my father," Wren said.

"I'm talking about a partner," Halloran said gently. "You have his jaw, did you know that? He made that same face when he lied to us. Righteous. It's a family expression, I think."

Astrid watched Wren take that hit and hold her ground, and something in her chest pulled tight — pride, maybe, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"What do you want?" Astrid asked.

"To make you rich," Halloran said simply. "And to make you free. In that order, if you like."

* * *

He laid it out the way a banker lays out terms, because that's what he'd been before he was a prospector — she could hear it in his cadence, in the way he rounded numbers down for effect and up for truth. Mercer unrolled documents on a flat rock: a strip-mine permit, stamped and countersigned, bearing the Meridian Syndicate's seal. A letter of credit. A schedule of extraction that treated the valley the way a butcher's chart treats a side of beef.

"Here is the machine," Halloran said, tapping the permit. "Eleven years ago, the Syndicate bought our claim — what was left of it after Elias gutted it — for almost nothing, because a claim without a proven source is a lottery ticket. They've been sitting on it since, paying the annual fees, waiting. Because here's what Elias understood and they don't quite: the permit dies without proof of origin. A lode has to be located. Mapped. Assayed at its head. If the source stays hidden past next spring, the claim lapses, the permit lapses, and the valley stays a valley." He looked up at her, and his eyes were kind the way a scalpel is kind. "You're carrying the only document in the world that keeps that machine alive. Elias drew it himself. We've spent three years and four dead prospectors trying to find where he put it."

"Four," Astrid said.

"Prospecting is dangerous work," Halloran said, without a flicker. "Rivers. Rockfall. One gentleman fell off a cliff in broad daylight with no witnesses for fifty miles. Tragic." He let that sit exactly long enough. "And then, three weeks ago, an old man flagged down a storm chaser on a flooded road. Word travels, Ms. Vane. Even upstream."

The fire cracked. Down at the water, the falls kept up their white noise, patient as a debt.

"So here are my numbers," Halloran said, and took out a small notebook of his own — and Astrid felt something twist in her chest at the sight of it, because it was the twin of hers, the one where she wrote storm paths on the left page and interest calculations on the right. "Your debt with the Meridian Syndicate. Principal, accrued interest, penalty schedules — I've seen the ledger, Ms. Vane, I helped write the ledger, back when Meridian was a client of ours. You owe forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars as of last night. It grows at a rate designed so you can never catch it. Am I close?"

She said nothing. He was exact.

"I will retire it. Today. All of it. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars, gone, as though it had never been written down." He turned a page. "In addition: five percent of gross on the first ten million extracted. That is not a rounding error, Ms. Vane. That is half a million dollars before you've lifted a finger, and more every year after, for as long as the mountain gives. You could stop driving through the night. You could buy a house with walls thick enough that you couldn't hear the weather at all."

Mercer slid a pen across the rock. The boy held out the document case.

"And the girl?" Astrid asked. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "What's her number?"

"The girl isn't a line item," Halloran said. "The girl is a complication. Complications resolve themselves once the paperwork is signed. She'll grieve, she'll rage, she'll get over it — people always do. Towns forget. Rivers rise and cover things. In twenty years Kestrel's Ford will be a company town with a school and a clinic and a hockey rink, and nobody will remember there was ever a ferry, because there won't be a river to ferry across. Just a slow brown canal full of tailings." He smiled again, warmly. "Progress is very forgiving, Ms. Vane. It forgives everyone who helps it."

Astrid looked at the pen. It was a good pen, heavy, brass, the kind that signs things that stay signed.

Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars. She knew every digit of that number the way she knew the names of clouds. She had lain awake under a hundred different skies doing arithmetic against it, calculating how many storms she'd have to chase, how many years of sleeping in the truck, how much of her life the interest was eating at six o'clock every morning while she slept badly. The number had a weight. It had sat on her chest for three years. And here was a courteous old man offering to lift it off with one signature, plus half a million dollars of *never having to feel this again* —

— and all it cost was a valley. A ferry. A girl with her father's jaw, sitting across the fire, watching her with eyes that had already read the answer and were bracing for it anyway.

That was the worst part. Wren wasn't pleading. She wasn't even angry. She just sat there with the needle in her fist, watching Astrid look at the pen, and Astrid understood that Wren had done this math too — had done it every day since the gas station, since the boat, since the first rapid — and had decided to watch anyway. Had decided Astrid was worth watching.

Nobody had ever decided that before.

"Ms. Vane," Halloran said softly. "You've been running for three years. I'm offering you the finish line."

"That's the thing," Astrid said. She picked up the pen. Halloran's smile widened by a millimeter. She turned it over in her fingers, feeling its weight, its certainty, its terrible reasonable brassness. "I know finish lines. I chase them for a living. And I'll tell you what I've learned about them." She set the pen down on the rock, pointing away from herself, toward him. "Every one of them moves."

Halloran's face didn't change, but the temperature around the fire did. Mercer stopped writing. The boy with the document case exhaled through his nose, very quietly, like a man watching someone step back from a ledge — or onto one.

"No sale?" Halloran asked.

"No sale."

"Well." He rose, brushing off his trousers, retrieving his stick, and for a moment the courtesy thinned enough that she saw what was underneath it, and it was older and colder than the Syndicate, older than the claim, older than the river. "Then we'll do this the way Elias forced us to do everything. The hard way. Mercer has your position. Voss has your scent. And the spring thaw won't wait for anyone's conscience." He paused at the edge of the firelight and looked back, and for just a second the warmth returned, and that was somehow worse. "For what it's worth, Ms. Vane — Elias would have liked you. He also thought he could outrun consequences. Look how that ended."

They went down toward the water, single file, and the dark took them in pieces — stick, shoulders, silver hair — until there was nothing but the sound of stones under boots, fading.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fire needed feeding. Neither of them fed it.

"He was going to kill us," Wren said finally. "After you signed. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you were going to sign."

Astrid opened her mouth to say no, and found the lie wouldn't clear her throat. She looked at the fire instead. "I picked up the pen."

"You put it down."

"After picking it up."

"Yeah." Wren turned the mending needle over in her fingers. "I counted the seconds. It was nine."

It was eight, Astrid thought, but she didn't correct her. Some mercies you just accept.

"They'll come tonight," Wren said. "Or Voss will come behind them, and they'll squeeze from both ends. My father used to say the river only gets dangerous in two places — where it splits, and where it narrows." She stood, shouldering her pack, and looked downstream into the dark, where the gorge narrowed and the water made its low continuous argument with the rocks. "This is both."

Astrid gathered the map case, the ledger, the notebook with its two columns of numbers, and felt the whole weight of the inheritance settle where it had been settling for weeks now — not on her back but in her chest, in the place where the fear used to live and where something else was slowly being built.

"Wren," she said. "There's something I haven't told you. About the night your father died. About what he said at the end."

Wren stopped. Turned. In the firelight her face was her father's face, righteous, braced.

Above the escarpment, far off, heat lightning flickered inside a cloud that shouldn't have built until Thursday — a storm arriving early, out of season, out of schedule, the kind of cell Astrid had spent her whole life reading and had never once been able to outrun.

"It can keep till morning," Wren said. "Because morning's coming fast, and we need to be on the water before it is."
