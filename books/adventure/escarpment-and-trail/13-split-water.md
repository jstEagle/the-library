## 13. Split Water

Wren woke to the sound of Astrid's voice, low and even, carrying across the water like something that had learned to travel light.

She was still in her bedroll under the overturned hull of the boat, sheltered in the willow tangle where they'd made camp below the last portage before Split Water. The fire had burned down to a red eye. Beyond it, at the edge of the gravel bar, Astrid sat with her back to a cottonwood trunk, the satellite phone pressed to her ear — the one she'd sworn she'd thrown in the river outside Kestrel's Ford, the one that had apparently learned to swim.

"No," Astrid said. "Not the map. Not yet." A pause. The river filled it, talking to itself over the shallows. "Because I'm not finished calculating."

Wren lay very still. Her heart was doing something ugly and slow, like a fish turning over in shallow water.

*Calculating.* That word again. She'd heard it on the first night, when she'd caught Astrid by the fire with the notebook open, columns of figures marching down the page in that cramped, slanted hand — storm tracks on the left, dollars on the right, as if weather and debt were the same animal wearing two coats. She'd told herself then what she'd been telling herself for eleven days on this river: *she's honest about what she is. That's worth more than virtue.*

But honesty was a cold comfort at three in the morning, listening to a woman negotiate.

"I know what Halloran offered," Astrid said. "I was standing there when he offered it." Another pause, longer. "Voss. Listen to me. If your people come up that gorge behind me, I can't stop them, but I can make them wish they hadn't. Tell Meridian that." A dry sound, almost a laugh, with no humor in it anywhere. "No. I don't need more time. I need you to understand that I've stopped running. There's a difference, and it's going to cost somebody."

She ended the call. For a long moment she didn't move. Then she opened the notebook — Wren could see the pale rectangle of its pages catching the starlight — and wrote something, and closed it, and put her head back against the tree, and did not sleep. Wren knew she didn't sleep because Wren didn't either, and the two of them lay thirty feet apart all night, both breathing carefully, both pretending.

The map was in the dry bag under the bow seat. It had been there since Miller's Bend, since the assay samples, since the night Wren had held her father's handwriting under a flashlight beam and understood that the treasure everyone was killing each other over was aimed straight down at her town like a gun barrel.

Wren had one thought, clean and simple as a knife: *If she sells it, they strip-mine the valley. If she doesn't, they break her, and she'll sell it anyway, because broken people always do.*

And underneath that thought, quieter, worse: *I trust her to tell me the truth. I just don't trust her to survive it.*

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the river running low and glassy between the canyon walls. Wren cooked oatmeal without tasting it. Astrid checked the sky twice, frowned at the west, and said, "Pressure's falling faster than it should. Whatever's building up on the escarpment, it's building mean. We should make Split Water by tomorrow noon if we push."

"Then let's push," Wren said, and kept her eyes on the fire.

They ran the flat water in silence. It wasn't their silence — their silence usually had texture to it, comfortable as an old rope, full of things neither needed to say. This silence had a hole in the middle, and Wren kept falling into it. Twice Astrid started to say something and stopped. Once Wren caught her watching, not the river, but Wren herself, with an expression Wren couldn't read and didn't want to try, because reading it might mean finding out she'd been wrong about everything.

At midday they eddied out below a riffle to scout the next stretch, and Astrid climbed the rock spine above the channel to look downstream, and Wren stood alone beside the boat with the dry bag at her feet, and the decision made itself the way a river makes a cutbank — not all at once, but inevitably, grain by grain.

She trusted Astrid's honesty. That was the whole problem. Astrid would tell her exactly what the offer was, exactly what the numbers were, exactly how much of Wren's home could be bought for the price of Astrid's freedom — and then she'd stand there with those gray storm-chaser's eyes and wait for Wren to do the math, because that was who she was. Fair to the point of cruelty. And Wren would do the math, and the math would be right, and the valley would die anyway, because being right about a thing had never once saved anything.

Her father had taught her that. Elias Thorn had been right about everything and it had gotten him shot in a flooded ditch.

So Wren took the dry bag out from under the bow seat, and inside it found the oilcloth roll, and inside that the map — her father's fine surveyor's lines, the cairn symbol at the head of the river, the spring that fed everything — and she rolled it tight against her spine inside her jacket, where it sat like a second heartbeat, and she did not burn it here, though God knew she wanted to. Because burning it here, half a day's run from the source, felt like lying too. Like deciding without seeing. Her father had hidden the lode once; he'd stood at the head of that river and made his choice, cowardly or otherwise, and Wren was done making choices about him secondhand.

She'd go to the spring. She'd see what he'd seen. And then she'd end it — the map, the lode, the whole inheritance — with her own hands, where nobody could buy it out of hers.

Astrid was still up on the rocks. Wren had maybe four minutes.

She took the food bag, the first-aid kit, the lighter, and the spare paddle. She left the tent, the stove, the rope, everything heavy. At the last second she took the notebook off the thwart — Astrid's notebook, the one with storms and debts in the same columns — and then set it back down, because taking it would be theft and leaving it was a message, and the message was: *I know what you are. Go be it somewhere that isn't my river.*

Then she shoved the boat off the gravel bar and let the current take it, and she paddled hard and quiet, and she did not look back at the rock spine until the bend swallowed it.

Astrid was already coming down. Wren saw her small against the sky, saw her stop, saw her stand absolutely still in the way that meant she'd understood — and then the bend took the rocks too, and it was just Wren and the water and her father's map against her spine, and the strange, sick lightness of having finally done the unforgivable thing she'd been walking toward for days.

* * *

She made nine miles before the canyon turned against her.

It narrowed past the old flume ruins, walls closing in until the sky was a ribbon overhead, and the current picked up speed and attitude, shoving the boat toward a boulder garden she remembered from the survey notes but had never run solo. She eddied out behind a house-sized block of granite, breathing hard, arms shaking, and ate half a candy bar and told herself the shaking was exertion.

It wasn't. It was Astrid's face on the rocks, getting smaller.

"Stop it," Wren said aloud, and the canyon threw her voice back at her, mocking. "You did the right thing."

Had she? The question came from nowhere and everywhere. Astrid had taken a beating at the portage — Wren had watched it happen, had been carried downstream screaming while Voss's men worked her over, and Astrid had stayed down and let them, buying Wren's escape with her ribs. Then she'd turned around and trapped those same men above a rising river with nothing but weather sense and nerve. That wasn't a woman who sold things. That was a woman who *paid* for things, in whatever currency the moment demanded.

But she'd also spent eleven days calculating. And tonight, on a phone that shouldn't exist, she'd said *not yet*, which meant *eventually*, which meant the answer to Halloran's offer was a number, not a no.

Unless—

Wren pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Unless *not yet* meant something else entirely. Unless Astrid was playing Voss the way she played storms — holding position, reading the pressure, waiting for the system to show its hand before committing. Unless the woman who'd stopped running had stopped toward something instead of away from it, and Wren had just abandoned her in a canyon with half their gear and every enemy upstream.

"That's her trick," Wren said to the river. "That's exactly her trick. Make you doubt the evidence of your own ears."

The river didn't answer. Rivers never did. That was why she loved them and why, at moments like this, she hated them.

She pushed back into the current.

* * *

Dusk found her at the mouth of the side canyon — the one her father's map called the Stair, a crack in the east wall where the river bent hard north and the source trail began. She hauled the boat onto a shelf of stone above the high-water mark, stashed the paddle beneath the hull, and stood looking up into a defile so narrow the stars didn't reach its floor.

Two days' climb, the map said. One day if she pushed. Above her, through the ribbon of sky, clouds were moving in from the west — fast, organized, the undersides of them lit faintly green-gray by some light the sun had already lost. Astrid would have looked at that sky and named it, given it a number, told her exactly how many hours they had. Wren looked at it and felt only the oldest human knowledge there was: *that's a bad sky.*

She thought about the notebook again. About the left-hand column, the storm tracks, the neat arrows and dates — two weeks of them now, every system charted from Kestrel's Ford to the escarpment. Astrid hadn't been tracking weather to run from it. Somewhere in the last hundred miles, Wren realized, the arrows had changed direction. Every entry for the past four days pointed *upstream*. Toward the source. Toward the place where everything was going to happen.

She'd been reading the woman's own diary as evidence against her, and it had been testimony in her favor the whole time.

"Oh, you absolute idiot," Wren whispered, and it wasn't clear whether she meant Astrid or herself, and it didn't matter, because it was both, it had always been both, two fools in one boat pretending the other one was the fool.

She sat down on the stone shelf with her back against the hull and let herself feel it, all of it, the whole ugly weight: the theft, the lie of the quiet morning, the image of Astrid standing on that rock spine watching her go and not even calling out — because Astrid wouldn't call out, Astrid would respect the choice, that was the cruelest thing about her, she'd let you walk into the dark and write it down in the notebook afterward as *her decision, her risk.*

Wren cried for exactly as long as it took the light to leave the canyon, which was not long, and then she stopped, because crying was a luxury the Stair did not offer.

She should go back. That was the obvious move. Paddle down in the morning, find Astrid, say the words — *I misread you, I stole from you, I'm sorry* — and eat whatever look Astrid gave her, because she'd earned it.

But the sky was wrong, and the climb was two days, and Astrid was half a day behind her at best with a bruised body and no boat, and Halloran's crew was somewhere on the water between them, and Voss — Voss, whose phone call Wren had overheard, Voss who owed Astrid something no one had explained — was a wild card in a deck already short on them.

And underneath all the logistics, honest and unmovable, sat the real reason she couldn't turn around:

If she went back, she'd have to watch Astrid choose. She'd have to stand there while the numbers got read out loud, while the offer hung in the air, while the woman she'd — the woman she *trusted* weighed a valley against a debt. Wren couldn't watch that. Not because she feared the answer, but because she'd finally understood that Astrid's honesty meant she'd never hide the temptation, and love — or whatever this was, this fierce inconvenient thing that had grown in the bottom of a boat like ballast — couldn't survive watching the scales.

Better to be gone. Better to finish her father's work at the spring and leave Astrid free to make whatever deal saved her life. That was mercy. That was the kind thing.

That was a lie, and Wren knew it was a lie, and she built her fire small and deep in the rocks anyway, and chewed the rest of the candy bar, and looked at the bad sky, and did not go back.

* * *

She was on the Stair before dawn, climbing by flashlight through the crack in the wall, the map's oilcloth warm against her spine from her own body heat. The trail her father had cut — she was sure now it was his, the blaze marks too precise for any prospector's habit, surveyor's marks, *his* marks — switchbacked up through scrub pine and loose scree, and twice she crossed water running downhill in places no map showed, small seeps, the mountain bleeding quietly toward the river.

Near sunrise she came out on a ledge and looked back, and the gorge lay below her like a dropped rope, silver-green in the early light, and far downstream — impossibly far, a speck, a suggestion — something moved against the current.

Not a boat. You couldn't row against that water.

A figure, on the portage trail along the east bank. Moving fast, favoring one side, head down, relentless. Even at that distance Wren knew the walk, the economy of it, the way Astrid moved like she was always slightly late for the next county.

Wren stood on the ledge with the wind pulling at her jacket and the map against her spine and the whole impossible distance between them, and she raised one arm — knowing it was useless, knowing Astrid couldn't possibly see her, doing it anyway.

The speck paused. Turned. Looked up at a canyon wall a mile long and a thousand feet high, at a ledge, at a dot, at nothing a human eye could resolve.

And then — Wren would swear this to the end of her life — the speck raised one arm back.

Wren laughed, and it broke in the middle and came out as something else, and she wiped her face with her sleeve and turned uphill, because the sky to the west had gone the color of a bruise and the mountain had maybe a day left in it, and there was a spring at the top of the world with her father's name on it, and a storm coming down the escarpment with Astrid Ishikawa's name on *that*, and only one of them was going to get there first.

She climbed.
