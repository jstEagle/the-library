## 14. Alone Upstream

The rain found her before the light did.

Astrid stood at the bottom of the gorge with the river roaring past her knees and the boat gone — Wren gone — and understood, in the flat way that understanding sometimes arrives, that she was exactly where she had always been: alone, wet, and moving. Except this time moving meant up.

She had woken to an empty bedroll and a missing map case and the particular silence of someone who has decided something without consulting you. Wren's boots were gone from beside the fire. The coffee tin sat rinsed and upside down on a flat stone, which was worse than if it had been left filthy — it meant she'd taken her time. It meant she'd thought about it while she packed.

*She trusts you to be honest,* Astrid thought, wringing out her sleeves. *She just doesn't trust you to be strong.*

And the hell of it was that Wren was right. Not about the honesty — Astrid hadn't lied to her since the first rapid, hadn't even rounded off a number — but about the rest. Halloran's offer sat in Astrid's chest like swallowed ballast: everything she owed, exacted to the dollar, plus interest forgiven, plus a clean ledger, plus the one thing she'd wanted for six years, which was simply to stop owing anyone anything. A number so precise it felt like mercy. She had looked at it for three full seconds before saying no, and those three seconds were going to live in her a long time.

So Wren had taken the map upstream alone, toward the source, toward Halloran's crew, toward a cairn her father built, carrying nothing but a knife and a grudge and whatever she'd inherited of Elias Thorn's stubbornness.

Astrid could follow the river. She could run the flood-stage water solo in a boat she didn't have, or portage around the falls where Voss's people might still be licking their wounds, or —

Or she could climb.

The south wall of the gorge rose four hundred feet above the water, a staircase of broken sandstone and juniper root, and above it lay the escarpment country: open, exposed, checkered with Syndicate checkpoints at every road junction between here and the head of the river. Nobody sane went over the top. That was precisely why she would.

She cinched her pack down tight, took a last look at the cold fire ring, and started climbing.

* * *

The wall took two hours and most of the skin on her palms. Halfway up, wedged into a chimney of rock with the river a brown ribbon far below, she stopped to breathe and caught herself doing arithmetic — not interest this time, but angles. The storm she'd been tracking for two days was still parked over the high plateau to the northwest, fat and slow, dumping its load on the divide. By afternoon its trailing edge would drag across the escarpment like a wet blanket dragged across a table. Rain on the rim roads. Rain in the checkpoint guards' faces. Rain turning every track into soup and every set of footprints into a rumor.

She'd spent six years reading storms the way other people read faces, because a storm was the only thing that had ever told her the truth about where it was going. Now she read it and saw cover.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the rock, to nobody. "You want to be useful? Be useful."

The storm, being a storm, did not answer. But by the time she hauled herself over the lip of the gorge into the gray noon light, the first outriders of it were already combing the plateau — thin veils of rain marching east, the wind smelling like iron and ozone. She turned her back on the river, on Wren, on the whole downstream world, and walked into the weather.

* * *

The first checkpoint was a fold of canvas and a pickup truck at the junction of two ranch roads, forty minutes after she topped out. She saw it from eight hundred yards, glassed it with the battered binoculars she'd carried since her first chase season, and counted two men under the tarp, one radio, no dog. Good. Dogs were the thing she couldn't out-think.

There was a stock trail half a mile north that crossed the road through a low swale. In dry weather it would be suicide — open ground, sightlines forever. But the rain was thickening now, coming in long silver curtains that erased the horizon in waves, and when the next curtain came she moved, walking fast with her collar up and her head down, counting seconds against the rhythm of the gusts. She crossed the road inside a wall of water so dense she couldn't see her own feet, and came out the other side into juniper without either man ever looking up from their thermos.

It should have felt like triumph. It felt like practice. Six years of this — of reading wind shifts and guard rotations and loan amortization schedules, all the same skill really, the skill of seeing what was coming and staying one step ahead of it — and somewhere in the last month the skill had stopped feeling like freedom and started feeling like a cage she'd built herself, bar by bar, out of competence.

She climbed another ridge. The storm closed behind her like a door.

* * *

The second checkpoint nearly ended it.

She came down off a grassy bench at dusk, aiming for a creek crossing she remembered from a fuel-cache map Thorn had sketched in his ledger's margins, and walked almost directly into a Syndicate truck parked in the trees. Engine warm. Two men inside, one asleep against the window, one awake, eating something out of a foil packet. Thirty yards. No cover between them except a deadfall that wouldn't stop a bullet and wouldn't hide a rabbit.

Astrid dropped flat into the wet grass and lay there with her heart slamming and the rain soaking through her jacket, and did the only thing she knew how to do, which was wait and watch and calculate.

The awake man finished eating. Crumpled the foil. Turned up the radio — static, then a voice, then static. He said something to the sleeper. The sleeper didn't move. And then the sky did what the sky had been promising all day: it let go. Not rain anymore but a cataract, a vertical river, the kind of deluge that made the whole world sound like standing inside a drum, and in that noise Astrid crawled. Elbows and knees, twenty-five yards through rising water and mud, around the nose of the truck, down into the creek cut, gone. She heard the men shouting to each other over the storm — not alarm, just complaint, the universal language of people stuck outside in weather — and then she was a quarter mile downstream and the night had swallowed her.

She found a hollow under an overhanging bank, got a fire going small enough to cup in her hands, and sat there shaking — not from cold, or not only from cold, but from the specific tremor of a body that has been running on adrenaline for six years and has just now, in a hole in a creek bank in the middle of nowhere, begun to wonder why.

* * *

Here is what Astrid Vane knew about herself, the knowledge she carried the way she carried the binoculars — always, everywhere, checked often:

She did not stop. She had not stopped in six years. Three states, eleven towns, forty-one storms chased and one marriage ended and a mother buried from a hospital payphone because by then the debt had gotten so big that going home meant leading the collectors to the only person left who loved her, and so she hadn't gone home, and her mother had died with the phone ringing unanswered in an empty apartment, and Astrid had driven through the night afterward because driving through the night was the one prayer she knew.

Everyone assumed it was the money. The Meridian Syndicate with their cheerful letters and their smiling men and their arithmetic that grew while you slept — sure, it was the money. The money was real. The money was the shape of the cage.

But cages have doors, and Astrid had found hers years ago and never once walked through it. There had been chances. A marshal in Casper who'd offered her protection for testimony, back when the ledgers were smaller. A job offer, a real one, forecasting for a grain cooperative — steady salary, a desk, a town. A woman named June in a town whose name Astrid had deliberately forgotten, who had asked her to stay in words so plain there was no room for misunderstanding, and Astrid had lain awake all night next to her listening to her breathe and feeling something in her chest crack open like river ice, and in the morning she had made coffee, kissed her goodbye, and driven until the tank was empty, and then filled it and driven more.

Because here was the other thing she knew, the thing underneath the thing:

People who stayed could be left. People who stopped could be caught. Every place she had ever belonged had eventually become a place she'd been thrown out of, or a place that burned down, or a place where the phone rang in an empty apartment — and somewhere along the line, without ever deciding it, she had concluded that the problem was not bad luck or bad men or bad timing. The problem was her. That there was something in Astrid Vane that only worked in motion, that if she held still long enough people would see it — the emptiness, the arithmetic where a heart should be — and they would leave first, and that would be worse. So she left first. Always. Six years of leaving first. The debt wasn't a chain. It was an excuse. It was the note she handed people instead of herself: *I can't stay, I owe too much.* And everyone had accepted the note, because it was a good note, a sympathetic note, and nobody — not June, not the marshal, not the woman's sister who'd written her twice — nobody had ever called it what it was.

Wren had called it. On the water, in the ghost camp, in the firelight with her father's letters spread out like evidence: *You keep waiting for us to give you a reason to go.*

And Astrid had burned with shame, and denied it, and known it was true.

She fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. The storm hammered the bank above her head. Somewhere upstream, a girl with her father's jaw was walking toward a spring full of armed men because she believed the truth mattered more than safety, and she'd done it alone rather than watch Astrid do the math one more time.

"I'm not selling," Astrid said to the fire. Her voice sounded strange in the hollow, small and certain at once. "I never was going to sell."

But wanting and being weren't the same thing, and Wren couldn't know the difference from the outside. From the outside, Astrid Vane was a woman who had hesitated for three seconds in front of a fortune, and three seconds was a canyon wide.

* * *

She slept maybe two hours, woke before dawn with the storm still grinding over the plateau, and broke camp in the dark. The plan assembled itself as she moved, the way plans did when she stopped fighting them: the checkpoints were watching the roads, so she wouldn't use roads. They were watching the river corridor, so she'd stay high. They were men with radios and foil packets and a payroll, and she was a woman with a storm, and the storm was bigger.

All morning she moved through the worst weather the escarpment had produced all season, using the gusts for crossings and the curtains for concealment, cutting fence lines and following sheep trails, working a great arc northeast toward the head of the river. Twice she spotted riders on distant ridgelines and went to ground; once she watched, through the binoculars, a Syndicate truck bog down to its axles in a flooded crossing and felt a flicker of something that was almost laughter. The third checkpoint she didn't sneak past at all. She walked straight down the middle of the ranch road at it, hood up, gait loose, just one more soaked idiot out in the blow — and the guard waved her past without lowering his cigarette, because nobody walks *toward* a checkpoint in a supercell unless they belong there.

Her own tricks, used against her own hunters. She almost smiled.

By late afternoon the storm's core was passing and the light came strange and gold-green under the anvil cloud, and Astrid crested a final rise and saw the country ahead change. The plateau broke apart into stone gardens and shallow draws, and beyond them, half-hidden in haze, the land dropped away into a canyon so narrow and deep it looked like a wound — and from the wound rose a thread of mist, and from the mist, faintly, the sound of water falling.

The source. Or near enough. Thorn's cairn would be close now, and Wren would be closer, and between her and that girl were Halloran's crew, the last of the daylight, and four hundred feet of broken ground.

Astrid stood on the rim with the wind pulling at her and did one last calculation, and this one had no numbers in it.

Six years ago she had learned to read storms because a storm tells you the truth: where it's been, where it's going, what it intends. People were harder. People lied, mostly to themselves. But Wren Thorn had never lied to her, not once, and every hard thing Wren had said had been true, and the truest thing was this: *you don't get to decide you're unlovable and then act surprised when nobody loves you.*

Astrid wasn't unlovable when still. She was unpracticed. There was a difference, and the difference was the rest of her life.

She shrugged the pack higher on her shoulders, turned her face into the dying wind, and started down into the canyon — running toward, for the first time, and knowing it, and not stopping.

Below her, in the blue shadow of the gorge, a campfire smoke rose thin and straight from the willows near the spring. Too much smoke for one girl.

She began to run.
