## 15. The Source

Wren had always believed the river began somewhere ordinary — a seep in a hillside, a cattle trough, a pipe. Rivers that mattered to people, the way the Kestrel mattered to Kestrel's Ford, seemed like they ought to start small and apologetic, gathering dignity as they went.

She was wrong. The river began in a cathedral.

The canyon narrowed for two hours until the walls pressed so close she could touch both sides with her arms out, and then it opened all at once into an amphitheater of red stone, a hundred feet high and curved like a hand cupped to catch rain. Water came out of the rock itself — not one spring but dozens, threading silver down the face of the amphitheater, braiding and rebraiding until they gathered in a pool so clear she could see every stone on the bottom, every leaf-shadow, her own face looking back at her upside down.

Above the waterline, the rock was veined with something else. Gray-white threads running through the red sandstone like frost on a windowpane. She didn't need the assay samples to know what they were. She'd spent three days carrying them in a waterproof bag against her spine, and she'd spent three nights reading Elias Thorn's cramped handwriting by firelight, and she knew exactly what silver looked like when it ran through a canyon wall above a town's only water source.

*They'd have to take the canyon apart,* he'd written, in the letter she'd read four times now, the one dated eleven years ago. *Not just the lode. The whole headwall. And everything downstream drinks from here.*

Wren stood at the edge of the pool and let the sound of it fill her — that low, constant hush, older than any claim or permit or ledger. Her father had stood here. He'd stood here and looked at this exact water and decided to spend the rest of his life being called a coward rather than let men with ledgers turn it into slurry.

"Okay," she said aloud, to him, to the canyon, to nobody. "I'm finishing it."

The cairn was where the map said it would be: on a shelf of rock at the base of the north wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging garden ferns gone brown with autumn. It was smaller than she expected — knee-high, built of flat river stones stacked with a mason's care, the kind of work a man does when he expects to come back and needs to find his own mark again. Tucked into its heart, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a tin box.

Inside the box: a second map. Not the one Astrid carried — Wren had taken that one, folded it into her jacket before she slipped away from the campfire, and she still felt sick about how easy it had been, about how Astrid hadn't even woken, or worse, had woken and let her go. This one was older, drawn in different ink, and it showed the lode itself: the vein's full run, from the headwall down under the benchlands, under the orchards, under the north end of town. Under the schoolhouse. Under the ferry landing where Wren had worked since she was fourteen.

Under her mother's grave.

There was also a photograph, soft with handling. A woman holding a baby on the ferry dock, squinting into sun. On the back, in Thorn's hand: *Mara and Wren. The reason.*

Wren sat down on the cold stone and held the photograph and did not cry, because crying took time and she had promised herself she wouldn't waste any. But her throat worked for a long while, and the river kept up its hush, patient as anything that has been doing one thing forever.

Then she took out the map from her jacket — the one Astrid had carried across half the territory, the one bought with a dying man's last breath, the one Halloran wanted, the one the Syndicate wanted, the thing everyone upstream and downstream was willing to hurt each other for — and she got out her matches.

Three of them left. She'd been counting.

She struck the first against the box lid and it flared and died. Damp. Everything up here was damp; the air off the springs was thick as breath. She tried the second, sheltering it inside her cupped hands, and the flame caught the head long enough to show her the map's lines one more time — her father's careful contours, the little x at the cairn, the notation in the margin: *If you're reading this, I'm sorry I couldn't be braver* — and then the wind funneled down the canyon, the way wind does in slot canyons, sudden and indifferent, and snuffed it.

She sat there with the dead match and the unburned map and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that she could not do this alone. Fire needed shelter. Fire needed someone to cup their hands around it while yours shook.

That was when she heard the engines.

* * *

They came up the canyon the way she would have, if she'd thought it through — not by water but by the old pack trail on the south rim, the one the miners had cut a century ago, the one marked on her father's map because her father had marked everything. Two machines, small and quiet, the kind of electric rigs that made no noise until they were already on top of you. By the time she registered the sound it was too late to run; the trail switchbacked down into the amphitheater and there was nowhere to go except into the pool, and the pool was a trap with pretty walls.

Six of them came down the switchbacks. Four stayed with the rigs. Two walked ahead, and one of those two was Halloran.

Up close he was smaller than his reputation, a neat gray man in a canvas coat, moving carefully over the wet rock like a man who respected terrain without loving it. His voice, when he spoke, was mild and pleasant and carried perfectly off the stone.

"There she is," he said. "Elias's girl. You have his chin, you know. Stubborn line, jaw to ear."

"You have his ledgers," Wren said. "Give them back."

Halloran smiled as if she'd said something charming. "The ledgers are evidence, Miss Thorn. Evidence belongs to whoever wins." He stopped a respectful ten feet away, hands visible, palms open — a man performing harmlessness, and good at it. "You've had a hard week. Your father's death, the river, my associates' enthusiasm. I'd like to simplify things."

He nodded, and one of the others — a big man with a rope-coiled shoulder holster — stepped forward and held out a leather folio. Halloran opened it against his forearm like a maître d' presenting a menu.

"A purchase agreement," he said. "For the map and the assay samples. Fair market value for the mineral rights they represent, plus a hardship stipend, plus relocation costs. You could rebuild your ferry twice over. You could leave the valley entirely, if the valley's become painful." His eyes flicked, briefly, toward the pool, the springs, the veined headwall. "Or you could stay, on terms we'd negotiate in good faith. We're not monsters, Miss Thorn. We're optimists with capital."

"I know what you are," Wren said. "My father wrote it down."

"Yes," Halloran said softly. "He did. That was his mistake. Writing things down." He closed the folio. "Here is the other version, since you strike me as a girl who prefers plain speech. If you don't sign, we take the map, the samples, and the tin box, and we don't need you alive for any of it. The canyon is remote. The river is high. Accidents happen constantly in country like this — ask anyone downstream." He glanced at the sky, where clouds were stacking over the rim in gray towers. "And the weather's turning, which will make everything harder for everyone. So. Plain speech. Where are the samples?"

Wren's hand was inside her jacket, on the map, and her other hand was in the bag at her spine, on the assays, and she thought about the matches — one left, one damp match between her and everything — and she thought about Astrid Vane asleep by a dead fire, and she thought about her father standing right here, alone, deciding to be brave in a way nobody would ever see.

He'd done it wrong, though. That was the thing she finally understood, standing in his cathedral with wolves on the switchbacks. He'd done it alone because he believed he deserved to be alone. He'd hidden the lode and hidden the truth and hidden himself, and hiding had killed him on a flooded road with a stranger driving him toward the daughter he was too ashamed to face.

Wren Thorn was not going to die hidden. And she was not going to win hidden either.

"The samples," she said, and pulled the waterproof bag from her spine, and held it up where everyone could see it, "are under the water. In the pool. Weighted. You're welcome to dive for them — it's snowmelt-fed, forty degrees, and the outlet channel runs fast this time of year. People drown in it every spring." A lie, mostly; the outlet was gentle. But the cold was real, and fear doesn't check facts.

Halloran studied her for a long moment. Above the rim, the cloud towers had gone bruise-dark, and the light in the amphitheater shifted, greenish, submarine. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled down the escarpment like furniture dragged across an upper floor.

"That storm," Halloran said, to one of his men, not to her. "How far?"

"Two hours. Maybe less. It's coming straight down the drainage."

Halloran turned back to Wren, and for the first time the courtesy thinned enough to show the machinery underneath. "Then here's what happens. You'll stand there while my men retrieve whatever's in that pool. Then you'll give me the map. Then we'll all get high and dry, and you and I will finish our conversation somewhere warm." He sighed, genuinely regretful. "Your father chose this canyon because he thought remoteness was protection. It isn't. Remoteness is just a place where no one hears you."

"He wasn't protecting himself," Wren said. "He was protecting us. There's a difference, and you know it, because it's the only thing about him you never managed to buy."

Something moved behind Halloran's eyes — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing, from an unexpected mouth, the exact argument that ruined him once before.

And that was when the rain started — fat, cold, scattered drops ticking against the red stone — and that was when Wren saw, on the south rim above the switchbacks, headlights. One pair. Bouncing hard, descending too fast, coming down a trail that no sane driver would take in weather like this.

Halloran saw them too. His men saw them. For three full seconds, everyone in the cathedral at the head of the river watched the lights drop toward them through the rain, and Wren found herself smiling before she could stop, because she knew that truck, knew that reckless, precise, storm-drunk line of descent, and she knew exactly who drove like the sky owed them money.

"You brought backup," Halloran said quietly.

"No," Wren said, shrugging the bag strap tight across her chest, backing one step toward the pool, one match left in her pocket and the whole storm coming down. "She brought herself."

The first lightning hit the rim, white and total, and the rain came down like the canyon had been waiting years for permission.
