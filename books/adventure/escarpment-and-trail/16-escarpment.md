## 16. Escarpment

The storm had been building for two days, and Astrid had been counting on it like a debt coming due.

She'd watched it born over the dry flats to the northwest — a smudge of convection on the horizon at dawn yesterday, a bruise by noon, and by evening a tower so tall it made its own weather, its own wind, its own intentions. She'd sat in the truck on a ridge above the gorge with her notebook open, drawing the cell's path in the same cramped handwriting she used for interest calculations, and she'd felt something she hadn't felt in years: not dread. Appetite.

*It will break tomorrow afternoon,* she'd written. *Southwest flow aloft, moisture streaming up the valley, orographic lift when it hits the escarpment. It will come down the river like it owns the river.*

Because it did own the river. Everything up here belonged to the sky first and everyone else second.

Now it was tomorrow, and the storm was a country unto itself, filling half the world from the rim of the plateau to the west, anvil-crowned and green-black at its base, and Astrid Vane was driving toward it.

Not away. Toward.

She kept catching herself on the strangeness of it. For eleven years the rule had been simple: whatever was coming, get gone before it arrives. Storms, collectors, letters with Meridian's smiling letterhead, the slow accumulating weight of other people's expectations — you read the signs, you did the math, you left. Stopping was dying. She'd built a whole life on that arithmetic, and the arithmetic had never once been wrong.

But it had never once been right, either.

The escarpment road was two ruts and a prayer, switchbacking down through scree and juniper, and she took it with the windows down because she wanted to hear the storm. Thunder came up out of the gorge in long rolling volleys, not the sharp cracks of close lightning but the deep artillery of a big cell organizing itself, and between the thunder there was a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: rain falling somewhere ahead, a vast hiss like static, like the world tuning itself to a new frequency.

The air had that taste. Ozone and wet stone and green things torn open. Every chaser learned to read it, the way Wren read water — the way the light went strange and yellow-green, the way the wind died completely ten minutes before everything arrived at once. Astrid had spent half her life running ahead of this exact feeling, and now she was driving down into it on purpose, toward a hidden spring where a girl she'd betrayed might be standing alone against Halloran's crew, holding a map that was worth more than all their lives put together.

*You'll sell it,* Wren had decided, the night she slipped away with the oilcloth bundle. Astrid had seen it in her face — not hatred, worse, something kinder and crueler: understanding. *I believe you want to burn it. I just don't believe you can.* She hadn't said those words. She'd said almost nothing. But Astrid had heard them anyway, in the silence where the map used to be, and they had followed her up out of the gorge like a second collector.

Well. The girl was about to learn something about Astrid Vane.

So was Astrid Vane.

* * *

The checkpoints were the problem. Voss's crew had them strung across the upper valley roads like beads on a wire — two men and a radio at the Miller's Bend junction, another pair at the stock trail head, a third she could see from three miles off because they'd parked their Suburban in the open like idiots, trusting the badge of Meridian's name to do what actual cover would have done better.

Astrid didn't need cover. She needed the storm.

She pulled off a mile out and climbed onto the hood, notebook braced on her knee, and did what she'd done ten thousand times on ten thousand ridgelines: she read the sky like a contract and looked for the clause everyone else missed. The cell was splitting — she could see it in the structure, the updraft base elongating north-south, the flanking line building on the southern flank while the core went rain-wrapped and mean to the north. In forty minutes, maybe less, the heaviest precipitation would drag a cold outflow boundary south and west across the plateau, and that boundary would arrive at the checkpoints as a wall of dust and horizontal rain and fifty-knot gusts, and every man under Meridian's pay would be huddled in his vehicle watching the world end, seeing nothing, hearing nothing, caring about nothing except staying alive.

Forty minutes. The descent to the source canyon was thirty if she drove like she meant it.

She drove like she meant it.

The first drops hit the windshield as fat, warm, isolated bombs, thudding like thrown fruit, and then the sky opened its hand. Rain came down in ropes. The ruts turned to chocolate and the truck wallowed, four-wheel drive groaning, and Astrid leaned into the wheel and grinned — actually grinned, teeth and all, alone in a bouncing cab in a deluge, because this was the part she'd always loved and always fled, the moment when the storm stopped being a forecast and became a fact, when the world got too loud for anything small to survive in it, including fear.

The checkpoint at the stock trail was a blur of brake lights and waving arms in her peripheral vision, and then it was behind her, swallowed. Someone fired a shot that the wind ate whole. She was through.

* * *

Below the last rim, the canyon closed around her and the storm changed character. Up top it had been violence; down here it was religion. Water fell off the walls in silver sheets, and the river — the same river she and Wren had run at flood stage, the one that had nearly taken the boat and everything in it — was rising even as she watched, brown and muscular, chewing at the rocks with a sound like grinding teeth. Debris came downstream: branches, a section of fence, somebody's lost tarp. The gorge was draining the whole sky into itself.

She left the truck in a fold of rock above high water mark, wrapped the notebook in plastic and shoved it inside her jacket, and started down the drainage on foot, following Thorn's directions from memory because the memory was all she had left of the map. *Where the canyon splits, take the dry fork. Where the dry fork ends, climb the slickrock shelf to the cairn. He built it himself, stone by stone, so he'd know the way back to what he couldn't keep.*

The dry fork wasn't dry anymore. It was a quarter-mile of knee-deep torrent funneled between walls, and Astrid waded it with the current shoving at her hips, testing each step with a pole of driftwood, thinking with a strange calm that this was exactly how Elias Thorn must have walked it, twenty years ago, carrying assay samples and a guilty conscience. The thought didn't frighten her. It steadied her. Dead men had shown her the way; the least she could do was finish the walk.

The slickrock shelf was running with water, black and glassy under a sky gone the color of deep water itself. Lightning strobed behind the western rim, silent at first, then arriving seconds later as concussions she felt in her sternum. She climbed with her palms flat on the wet stone, finding friction where friction shouldn't exist, and told herself the truth she'd been circling for days:

She wasn't here to save Wren. Wren didn't need saving; Wren was the most competent person Astrid had ever met, and if anyone was going to walk out of this canyon it would be the ferryman's daughter. She wasn't even here for the map — the map was paper, and paper burned, and burning it was a decision she'd already made somewhere back on the plateau without letting herself notice.

She was here because for the first time in her adult life, someone's opinion of her mattered more than her survival, and she refused to let that opinion be *coward*. Wren believed Astrid would sell. Fair enough. Eleven years of evidence supported the belief. But a person was allowed one act that contradicted her entire record, and Astrid Vane had chosen hers, and it waited for her at the top of this shelf, beside a cairn of stacked stones, in the teeth of a storm she had chased across two hundred miles of escarpment not to escape but to arrive.

Her boot slipped. She fell flat against the rock, cheek to the wet sandstone, heart slamming, and hung there with the rain hammering her back and the drop yawning below, and she laughed — a short, wild, involuntary sound torn out of her by the lightning. Then she found a new hold and kept climbing.

* * *

She smelled woodsmoke before she saw the camp.

It came down the dry fork on the storm's outflow, threading the rain: a thin bitter ribbon of fire in a canyon drowning in water. Someone had a blaze going up at the head of the drainage, which meant shelter, which meant people, which meant either Wren or Halloran, and Astrid's money — what was left of it after eleven years of interest — was on Halloran.

She crested the shelf into a hanging valley she understood instantly, the way she understood weather systems: the spring rose here, a seam of clear water weeping from the cliff face, gathering in pools before it spilled over the lip and began its long fall toward Kestrel's Ford. Above the spring, the hillside was wrong — a faint terrace, a subsidence line, the earth remembering the tunnels dug beneath it. The lode. Thorn's silver, sleeping under the grass, worth enough to kill for and hidden well enough that only a dead man's map could find it.

And there, at the edge of the pools, stood a figure with a rifle, silhouetted against the firelight of a camp that was not Wren's.

Three tents. Four men moving with the unhurried economy of professionals. Pack mules under a tarp. And seated on a rock by the fire with a tin cup in his hands and his coat somehow still dry, Halloran, watching the storm with the expression of a man admiring a rival's workmanship.

No Wren.

Astrid's stomach dropped through the wet rock. Then she saw it — the cairn, up-slope, and beside it fresh-turned earth and a scatter of ash, and her heart did something complicated and painful, because Wren had been here. Wren had reached the source first, alone, and done what she'd set out to do — and then the trail led away south along the rim, footprints filling with rain, heading for higher ground, and Astrid understood: the girl hadn't been caught. She'd run. Which meant she was alive, and loose, and probably watching this exact camp through the rain right now with that ferocious patience of hers.

Halloran turned. Of course he did — a man like that heard the weather change before it broke. His eyes found Astrid standing at the lip of the shelf, soaked through, mud to the knees, and he smiled the courteous smile she'd been promised by everyone who'd ever described him.

"Miss Vane." He raised the tin cup. "You're late. The map isn't."

Lightning hit the western rim close enough to whiten the whole valley, and in the flash Astrid saw what Halloran hadn't yet: the spring pool beginning to swell against its seam, the hanging valley's single outlet already choked with debris, the storm she'd tracked for two days settling over the headwaters like a hand closing.

"You're right," she said, and started walking toward the fire. "About the map."

Thunder rolled down the canyon, long and continuous now, the sound of the sky emptying itself, and somewhere on the rim above, silent as rain, Wren Thorn was watching.
