## 17. The Storm at the Head of the River

**Astrid**

The storm had been building for two days, and Astrid had chased it the whole way — not across open country this time, but down into the throat of the mountains, along goat trails and game paths, with the sky going green-black behind her like a bruise coming up through skin.

She'd left the truck at the last switchback and climbed. The escarpment fell away beneath her in shelves of wet limestone, and she could see the head of the river far below: a narrow canyon where the water came out of the earth in a spring so clear it looked like a wound in the rock. Thorn's cairn stood above it, a stack of stones no bigger than a man, and beside it — small, soaked, cornered — Wren.

Halloran's crew had her boxed against the cliff. Six men in expedition rain gear, satellite phone in one man's hand, Halloran himself standing under an umbrella held by another like some courteous old king surveying a battlefield he already owned. The map was in Wren's fist. Astrid could see it even from three hundred yards up — the oilcloth glinting darkly every time lightning walked the clouds.

Astrid did what she always did with a storm. She read it.

It was worse than anything she'd chased on the flats. A supercell with rotation tightening over the ridge, dragging a wall of rain behind it that would hit the high country like a dropped lake. The spring-fed canyon had no floodplain worth the name. When that water came down, everything in the gorge floor would be inside the river.

Including six men who thought they had time.

She started down the slope, fast and low, using the thunder to cover the sound of her boots on scree. The rain reached her first — fat, cold drops, then a hissing sheet of it — and by the time she was within fifty yards of the cairn, the world had gone white noise and silver light.

Wren saw her and something broke open in the girl's face — relief and fury in the same expression, the way only family can manage it.

"You climbed *down* that?" Wren shouted over the storm.

"I drove most of it." Astrid reached them, breath tearing. "We need their gear."

"What?"

"Look at the river." It was already rising — brown foam climbing the stones where an hour ago there had been dry rock. "They're standing in a flood channel with all their equipment stacked behind them. Ropes. Pulleys. That inflatable raft they hauled up here." She grabbed Wren's arm. "Everything they brought to take the mountain apart, we can use to keep them alive long enough to arrest them."

Wren stared at her, rain streaming off her chin. "You want to *rescue* them?"

"I want the ledger to matter," Astrid said. "Dead men don't testify. And neither do drowned ones — but drowned men make martyrs, and martyrs bring lawyers, and lawyers bring permits. We do this clean or we don't do it at all."

Lightning hit the ridge behind them, close enough that the air tasted like coins. In the flash, Astrid saw Halloran turn toward the sound of their voices. Saw him smile.

"There she is," he called, warm as a host greeting late guests. "Miss Vane. And the daughter. Wonderful. Everything in one place — you've saved my crew a great deal of walking."

**Wren**

Wren had planned to be alone when this moment came. She'd rehearsed it on the climb up: how she'd stand at her father's cairn with the map, tell Halloran exactly what he could do with his strip-mine permit, and burn the thing in front of him if it came to that. Alone, because alone was how you knew what you were made of.

Then Astrid came down the mountain out of the storm like something the weather itself had sent, and every rehearsal went to pieces.

Now the two of them crouched behind the cairn while the river rose around Halloran's crew, and Astrid was laying it out fast — the raft, the ropes, the anchor points, the way the crew had stacked their own gear on a gravel bar that was about to become an island and then not even that.

"He'll offer you the debt again," Wren said. "He offered me nothing. He just looked at me like I was a fence post."

"He'll offer me everything I owe. Exact numbers. He likes exact numbers." Astrid's jaw was tight. "When he does, that's our window. He'll want to talk more than act. Men like Halloran think talking is winning."

"And the map?"

Astrid was quiet for a second. Rain drummed on the oilcloth between them.

"When the marshal gets here," she said, "the map is ash. Before, if we have to. But Ash—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "The ledger goes to the law. The map goes nowhere. Not to the Syndicate, not to Halloran, not into any filing cabinet in any capital in the country. Your father hid that lode to keep this valley whole. I'm not the one who un-hides it."

Wren looked at her — this woman who had lied to her in Kestrel's Ford, bled for her at the portage, climbed down a storm-wracked escarpment on foot because the truck couldn't make it — and felt the last knot of distrust come loose, not with a snap but with a slow, almost embarrassing warmth.

"You said 'Ash,'" Wren said.

"I say a lot of things in storms."

Downstream, the marshal's party was coming up the canyon trail — four figures, rain slickers, one of them carrying a document case like it was a weapon. Voss walked at the front. Even at this distance Wren recognized the Syndicate collector's stride, and the way he kept glancing upstream at the rising water with the expression of a man who had finally done a sum he didn't like.

"Halloran's crew sees them," Astrid said. "Watch. He'll stall now too. Everybody stalls when the numbers change."

She was right. Halloran turned from the marshal's approach back to the cairn, and his voice carried through the rain, still courteous, still unhurried, though Wren could hear the hairline crack in it now.

"Miss Vane! A word before the circus arrives!" He gestured at the flood-swollen river as if presenting it. "You owe the Meridian Syndicate forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars. I am authorized to make that number zero. The map, the girl's cooperation, five minutes of your time. That's the entire transaction."

Astrid stood up from behind the cairn.

And Wren watched her do the bravest thing she'd seen anyone do: she walked out into the open, into the rain, toward the man with the umbrella, and she laughed.

"Forty-one thousand," Astrid called back. "You know what I paid for this view? A dying man's last breath. You know what it's worth? Nothing — because in about ninety seconds it's going to be underwater, and so are you. Move your crew to high ground, Mr. Halloran. I'm not selling, and the river doesn't negotiate."

**Astrid**

Halloran's smile stayed fixed while everything behind it moved. Astrid had seen that look on collectors' faces a hundred times — the arithmetic running hot behind the eyes. But Halloran didn't get to finish recalculating, because the river finished first.

The flood wall came down the canyon with a sound like a freight train derailing sideways. One moment the gravel bar was gravel; the next it was brown chaos, and the crew's gear — raft, crates, rope coils, the satellite phone — went tumbling into the current. Two of the six men went with it, swept against a boulder field, screaming.

What happened next happened fast, the way storms happen.

Astrid was already moving. She'd spent her whole life reading water from above; now she read it from inside it. "Rope!" she shouted at Wren, pointing at a coil snagged on the rocks where the crew's own camp had been. "Anchor on the cairn — wrap it twice, it's solid stone!"

Wren threw her the line end. Astrid waded hip-deep into the flood margin, braced against the pull, and threw the rope in a flat arc downstream. First throw short. Second throw caught one of the swept crewmen across the chest, and the man grabbed on with both hands, and Astrid leaned back with all her weight and screamed for Wren to haul, and together — Astrid's storm-reading, Wren's ferryman's arms — they swung the man out of the current onto the bank like landing a fish.

The second crewman caught the rope on his own, hand over hand, and dragged himself in.

By the time the marshal's party reached them, the river had taken the rest of Halloran's expedition gear, half his dignity, and — when Halloran himself, umbrella abandoned, tried to ford a side channel to reach the trail and escape the paperwork — nearly took Halloran. It was Voss, of all people, who threw him a line, with an expression of profound distaste, like a man returning a borrowed tool.

The marshal — a broad, gray-haired woman named Okonkwo who introduced herself by holding up a badge and shouting over the thunder — took custody of Halloran's remaining crew, accepted the oilcloth ledger from Astrid's jacket with both hands and a long look, and read enough of the first page right there in the rain to go quiet in the way officials go quiet when the numbers are bad enough.

"This implicates Meridian," she said. "All of it?"

"All of it," Astrid said. "Thorn kept records like other people keep grudges."

"And the map? The man mentioned a map. Halloran won't stop mentioning the map."

Astrid looked at Wren. Wren looked back, and nodded once.

Astrid took the oilcloth packet from inside her coat, walked to the cairn, and knelt. Her lighter was soaked; it took three tries. On the third, flame caught the oilcloth, and she fed the map into it page by page — Thorn's careful handwriting, the silver lode's hidden geometry, the source of the river drawn in ink that had waited thirty years for fire.

The rain tried to kill the flame and failed. Lightning lit the burning pages from behind, and for one second the whole canyon could see the lode's shape glowing in Astrid's hands like a coal.

Then it was ash, and the river took the ash, and the source of the river belonged to nobody again.

**Wren**

Afterward, Wren sat on the wet stone beside her father's cairn while the marshal's radio crackled and Halloran sat handcuffed under a tarp, still trying to negotiate, still smiling, smiling at people who were no longer listening.

Voss came and stood near her, hands in pockets, watching the flood recede from its peak.

"The ledgers name me," he said. "I've made arrangements. Testimony for leniency." He glanced at her. "Your father would have hated that I'm the one carrying his paper to the law."

"He'd have hated a lot of things," Wren said. "He got one right, though. He picked the right stranger."

Voss almost smiled. Almost. Then he went back to the marshal, and Wren was alone with the cairn and the rain easing off and Astrid coming to sit beside her, soaked through, split-lipped from the rope burn, looking like a woman who had finally set down something heavy.

"It's gone," Astrid said. "The map. The debt. All of it."

Wren watched the river run clean and high below them, fresh from the earth, going somewhere it had always gone.

"So what do you do now," Wren asked, "when there's nothing chasing you?"

Astrid was quiet a long moment. Downstream, the marshal laughed at something Voss said, and the sound carried strange and human through the dripping canyon.

"I don't know," Astrid said. "I've never gotten this far before."

* * *

They came down off the mountain the next morning, and Kestrel's Ford was waiting — but that, Wren thought, watching Astrid hesitate at the edge of town like a stray deciding whether the porch light meant her, was a different kind of storm entirely.
