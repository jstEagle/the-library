## 19. Ferryman's Daughter

The spring was quieter than Wren remembered it, which was strange, because she had only been there once — three days ago, with a map burning in her hands and Halloran's crew coming up the canyon like a fever. Quiet was what water did when it finally got where it was going. It stopped arguing.

She knelt at the cairn her father had built, forty years of river stones stacked into a shape that only meant something if you already knew. The letters were in her jacket, wrapped in the same oilcloth that had carried the map. Eleven of them. She'd read every one twice now, once in the firelight at Miller's Bend and once alone on the ferry deck after everyone had gone to bed, and she still couldn't say which reading had hurt more.

"You could have just come home," she said to the cairn. "You could have been a coward in person."

The spring didn't answer. Springs never did. But it kept running, cold and clear over the silver-dark rock, down through the hidden canyon, into the gorge, past Kestrel's Ford, all the way to the flat brown water at the mouth of the world. Her father had spent his whole life walking upstream away from this place, and every step he'd taken had been a step taken for her, and neither of those facts made the other one easier to hold.

She dug at the base of the cairn with her hands, working stones loose until she'd made a pocket in the wet earth beneath them. She put the oilcloth bundle in. She put his last letter on top — the one addressed to *my daughter, if you're reading this, then someone kept my promise* — folded once, because some things deserved to be buried readable.

Then she covered it, and stacked the stones back the way they'd been, and sat back on her heels in the thin cold light coming off the snowfields above the canyon rim.

She'd expected to feel finished. That was what the stories promised: you bury the dead, you close the account, you walk away lighter. Instead she felt like a woman holding a rope with nothing tied to the far end. All that anger she'd carried since she was nine years old — anger needed somewhere to go, and the man it belonged to was under a cairn at the head of a river, and the woman who'd helped put him there was half a mile below her, repacking the truck like a person who expected to be leaving.

Wren stood. Her knees cracked. She looked downstream, where the canyon dropped away in green stair-steps toward the valley, and she thought about the ferry sitting idle at its mooring for eleven days, and about Mrs. Calloway hauling her own garden cart across the crossing because nobody else would, and about Astrid Vane loading gear into a truck cab as if belonging anywhere were a disease she'd caught and was trying to sweat out.

"Right," Wren said, to the spring, to her father, to nobody. "Not done yet."

She started down the trail, taking the switchbacks two at a time, the way she'd learned to take everything.

* * *

She found Astrid at the truck with the tailgate down, folding a tarp into a square so precise it looked machined. That was the thing about Astrid — even her grief came out organized. She'd been different since the storm, since the marshal's boat had come up through the flood mist with its blue light turning the rain to neon, since Halloran went into the water and didn't come out. Different how, Wren couldn't have said exactly. Looser at the shoulders. Like a wire that had been strung to snapping point for years and someone had finally backed the tension off a quarter turn.

"You're packing," Wren said.

Astrid didn't look up. "Truck needs to go back down the escarpment road before another system moves in. Road washes out easy on the north face."

"I didn't ask about the road."

That got her a glance. Astrid's eyes were the gray of weather coming, and right now they were doing their best to be nothing at all. "Debt's voided. Testimony's given. Marshal Okafor signed the release yesterday." She smoothed the tarp one more time, needlessly. "Nothing keeping me here."

"No," Wren said. "Nothing keeping anybody anywhere. That's kind of your whole philosophy."

"Wren—"

"The ferry opens tomorrow."

Astrid's hands went still on the tarp. "Your ferry."

"Our ferry, if you want it. Half share. I can't run both lines alone — the morning crossing and the school run and the freight haul when the mine supply trucks come through. I've been doing it with Dad's old partner filling in and he's seventy-three and afraid of the current at the narrows." She crossed her arms. "You read water better than anyone I've ever seen. You ran the gorge at flood stage in a boat older than both of us. I watched you thread the Split Rock chute by feel in rain so hard I couldn't see the bow from the stern."

"That was panic."

"It was skill. Panic just means you were honest about the stakes." Wren unfolded her arms and made herself say the rest plainly, because she'd learned in the last month that Astrid only believed things said plainly. "I'm not asking you to stay forever. I'm asking you to stay for the season. One season. If you hate it, the truck's right there and the escarpment's full of storms and I'll wave you off from the dock and mean it kindly."

Astrid looked at the truck. At the tarp. At the canyon wall, where the light was going long and gold. Wren watched her do the arithmetic — she could almost see the notebook page, the same one where storm paths and interest rates used to live, except now the columns were different. Cost of staying. Cost of leaving. And underneath both, in small handwriting, the question Astrid had never let herself ask: *what am I actually running from?*

"One season," Astrid said.

Wren felt something unclench in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. "One season."

"And I keep the truck."

"Keep the truck. It's ugly enough to fit in around here."

For a second — half a second — Astrid smiled. Not the gas-station smile, the one that had been all teeth and transaction. A real one, crooked and brief, gone almost before it arrived, but real. Then she cleared her throat and became busy with the tarp again, because God forbid anything be allowed to simply happen without being immediately filed.

"Tomorrow," Astrid said. "What time?"

"First light. River doesn't wait and neither do the Calloway children."

* * *

The ferry had been in Elias Thorn's family for sixty years, and for eleven days it had sat at its mooring looking like a held breath. Wren spent the evening before the reopening scrubbing the deck, and Astrid showed up an hour in with two coffees from the diner thermos and started on the winch without being asked, sleeves rolled, forearms streaked with grease, and Wren decided not to comment on it because commenting would have made Astrid stop.

They worked until dark. They worked in the easy silence of people who had run rapids together, which is a specific kind of silence — the kind where you know the other person will call out if something matters, and won't fill the air otherwise. When the last cable was dressed and the ramp boards were stacked, they sat on the dock with their boots hanging over the black water and drank the rest of the coffee while the first stars came out over the escarpment.

"My dad ran this crossing the morning I was born," Wren said. "Mom told me that story a hundred times. Water high, spring melt, and he still made the crossing because the doctor was on the far side and she wasn't waiting for the water to drop."

"He sounds stubborn."

"He was a coward who was stubborn about exactly one thing." Wren turned the empty cup in her hands. "I used to think those were contradictions. Now I think everybody's mostly one or the other depending on the day."

Astrid was quiet a moment. "I ran from a debt for four years," she said. "And the whole time I told myself it was the debt I was running from." She flicked a pebble into the river. "It wasn't."

"I know."

"Yeah." Astrid exhaled. "I'm starting to."

Above them the escarpment stood against the stars, vast and dark, the whole weight of the high country holding back the sky. Somewhere up there the wind was moving, and Wren thought she could smell it — that green metallic edge that meant moisture stacking up over the divide. She'd grown up reading that smell the way other kids read street signs.

"Storm coming?" Astrid asked, because of course Astrid had smelled it too.

"Three days out, maybe four. Big one, if it holds together."

Astrid nodded slowly, and Wren saw her look up at the escarpment not with dread and not with hunger either — with something more level than either. Recognition, maybe. An old adversary acknowledged across a table.

"We'll be here when it hits," Wren said.

"Yeah," Astrid said. "We will."

* * *

Word traveled through Kestrel's Ford the way word always did: not by announcement but by porch. By the time Wren walked up from the dock the next morning, half the town somehow knew the ferry was opening, and by noon the knowledge had become an event, and by two o'clock it had become, improbably, a celebration.

The first crossing was Mrs. Calloway and her garden cart, and Wren took it slow and ceremonial, and Mrs. Calloway paid her fare in tomatoes and gossip. The second was the Herrera boys with a bicycle between them. The third was the school run, fourteen children shrieking with delight at a conveyance most of them had only heard about in stories, and Wren let Astrid take the helm for that one and watched her handle the loaded deck with a care that would have surprised anyone who'd only seen her drive a storm-chasing truck down a flooded back road.

By late afternoon the crowd on the near bank had thickened into something with gravity. People Wren had known her whole life — people who had watched her father leave and watched her grow up sharp-tongued and alone at the crossing — stood around in the golden light eating pie off paper plates, and in the middle of them, holding a plate she clearly didn't know what to do with, stood Astrid Vane.

Wren drifted close enough to hear.

"—the gorge, in that flood? My cousin saw the boat come through the narrows and he says it was standing on end—"

"—and the marshal, they say she's the one brought the ledgers in, the whole Syndicate book—"

"—well, she looks like she'd bite you, but she fixed my gate hinge Tuesday without being asked—"

Astrid bore it the way she bore weather: head slightly down, eyes moving, cataloguing exits. But she stayed. That was the thing Wren kept noticing, the thing that kept catching in her chest like a fishhook. Astrid stayed. Every instinct in that woman pointed at the horizon and she stood in a crowd of strangers eating Mrs. Petraglia's rhubarb pie and answered questions about the flood in a low, reluctant voice, and she stayed.

Then old Sam Okafor — no relation to the marshal, though he told the story differently every time — raised his cup and said, "To the ferryman's daughter," and the town took it up, and Wren felt her ears go hot, and then Sam added, "and to the new partner," and the cheer that came back included Astrid's name, spoken easily, like it had always belonged in this valley's mouth.

Astrid looked at Wren across the crowd with an expression Wren had never seen on her before. It took Wren a moment to identify it, because it was so ordinary. It was the look of a person who has just been set a place at a table and cannot find the trick in it.

Later, when the lanterns were lit and the crowd had thinned to the stubborn core of it, Mrs. Calloway pressed a covered dish into Astrid's hands — "for your supper, there's plenty, don't argue" — and Astrid accepted it with both hands, carefully, the way you accept something breakable.

Nobody had asked Astrid to stay. Nobody had made a speech about second chances or drawn up terms. The town had simply, without discussion, decided she was one of theirs, the way the river decides a stone belongs in its bed, and handed her a plate.

Wren found her at the rail afterward, looking down at the water.

"They gave me pie," Astrid said, as if reporting a structural anomaly.

"They give everybody pie. It's how they vote."

"Vote on what?"

"On whether you exist yet." Wren leaned on the rail beside her. Below them the ferry rocked gently at its mooring, lamps lit, ready for morning. "Congratulations. You exist."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rougher than usual. "Four years, nobody knew my name except people I owed money to."

"Well." Wren bumped her shoulder, deliberately, the way you'd nudge a skiff off a sandbar. "Now you owe us tomatoes. Mrs. Calloway keeps accounts."

That earned the crooked smile again, longer this time. They stood together while the lanterns burned down and the last of the town drifted home, and above the escarpment the stars wheeled, and far to the northwest, beyond the ridge, a faint flicker lit the clouds — heat lightning, or the leading edge of the system Wren had smelled two nights ago, stacking itself up over the divide like a debt coming due.

Astrid saw it too. Of course she did.

"Big one," Wren said.

"Big one," Astrid agreed. She didn't tense. She didn't reach for keys. She just watched the distant flicker with the calm of a woman who knew exactly where she'd be sleeping tomorrow night, and the night after that.

"Three days," Wren said. "Maybe four."

Astrid drained her coffee and set the cup on the rail, and Wren could see it in her face already — that old bright attention kindling behind her eyes, except aimed now at a horizon she intended to come home from.

"Then we'd better get the truck serviced," Astrid said, "because this time, we're chasing it together."
