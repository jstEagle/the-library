## 21. The Next Storm

The storm announced itself the way Astrid always said it would: three days early, in the way the light went strange over the escarpment, and two days early, in the way the river smelled like iron, and one day early, in the way the ferry cable sang.

I'd learned to hear all three voices by then. That was the thing nobody told you about living with a storm chaser — you didn't just learn to read weather, you learned to read *her*, and the two readings were so tangled together you couldn't pull them apart anymore. When the barometer dropped, her shoulders dropped with it. When the wind swung around to the southeast, she started talking faster. And when the sky over the escarpment went that bruised, expectant purple — the color of a held breath — she got this look on her face that I had spent a whole season learning to name.

It wasn't hunger, exactly, though it looked like hunger. It wasn't fear, though there was fear in it somewhere, way down deep, like a stone at the bottom of a clear creek. It was the look of a woman standing in the doorway of a house she'd finally admitted was hers.

"Big one," she said, from the porch, coffee mug forgotten in her hand. "Dry line's pushing up from the south, moisture's stacked to ten thousand feet, and the cap's going to break by two o'clock." She said it the way other people said *good morning*. "Tornadic, probably. Long-track, if it stays on the river axis."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning it'll walk right up the gorge." She turned and looked at me, and there it was — that look, and under it something newer, something that still caught me off guard every time. An invitation. "Meaning it's going to be beautiful."

* * *

We'd been partners on the ferry for eight months by then, long enough that the town had stopped calling her *the drifter* and started calling her *the Vane woman* in that tone Kestrel's Ford used for people it had decided to keep — half suspicion, half pride, like a scar you were fond of. Long enough that she had a mug at my table with her name on it, done in my handwriting, because I'd gotten tired of her drinking out of whatever was closest. Long enough that I knew the truck's every rattle and she knew the ferry's every groan, and we could run a crossing in silence and it wasn't empty silence, it was full silence, the kind you only get with someone you trust.

But she'd never once asked me to come chasing.

I'd never once asked to go.

That was the treaty, unwritten. She went up the escarpment alone when the sky turned, radio crackling, and came back after dark soaked to the skin and lit up like a struck match, talking in numbers — *mesocyclone, RFD, hook echo, core punch* — and I'd listen and feed her and not ask why she needed it, because I knew why. I'd read her notebook once, back in the early days, before I knew better than to snoop. Storm paths on the left page. Loan interest on the right. The same arithmetic, the same desperate running sum. She'd spent years calculating escape routes and calling them forecasts.

But that was over now. The Syndicate was a pile of evidence in a federal file. The debt was a signature on a settlement. And still, every time a storm built, she went alone — and I watched her go, and I understood it the way you understand a river: it wasn't that she was running *from* anything anymore. It was that she didn't know how to be in motion with someone else. Motion had been her solitude. You can't share a getaway.

So when she said *it's going to be beautiful* and then stopped, mug halfway to her mouth, and looked at me like a woman standing on the edge of a diving board — I said it first.

"I'm coming."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Astrid; everyone thought she was all sharp edges and deflections, but when it mattered, when it was real, she was the most direct person I'd ever met. She just set the mug down and nodded, once, like we were signing something.

"Rules," she said. "You stay belted. You don't touch the radio unless I tell you to. If I say *in the truck*, you're in the truck before the sentence is finished. And if it goes bad —" she paused, and I watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the easy one — "if it goes bad, you do what I say, even if it means leaving me. Deal?"

"No."

"Wren."

"I'll leave you when you leave me, and neither of us is going to do it, so let's skip the part where we pretend." I held out my hand. "Deal."

She shook it. Her grip was calloused and warm and it held on a half-second longer than a deal required.

* * *

Loading the truck was a liturgy I'd only ever watched from the porch. The anemometer bolted to the bed rail. The hail cage over the windshield — she'd welded it herself in March, in the ferry yard, and I'd pretended not to be impressed and had been extremely impressed. The weather radio, the backup weather radio, the paper maps in their oilcloth sleeve — and when she pulled those out, my chest did something complicated, because oilcloth meant Elias, meant the map that had started all of this, meant a dead man's handwriting that I could still draw from memory with my eyes closed.

"You okay?" Astrid asked, quiet.

"Yeah." I folded the maps and stowed them under the seat. "Just — he'd have liked this truck."

"He'd have hated this truck. He'd have called it a rolling confession of poor judgment." She smiled, and it was one of the real ones, the ones that reached all the way up. "He'd have loved riding in it."

We pulled out at eleven. Mrs. Calloway was on her porch with her mending, and she watched us pass, and she raised one hand — not a wave, exactly, more like a blessing, or a bet. Old Marta at the landing called out, "Bring her back in one piece!" and I hollered back, "Which one of us?" and got the laugh I was fishing for.

The whole town knew where we were going. That was the part that got me, sitting in the passenger seat with the escarpment rising ahead of us like a blue-gray wall. Nobody in Kestrel's Ford thought it was strange that the ferryman's daughter was driving into a tornado's path. They just expected us back by dark. Expected it the way they expected the six o'clock crossing, the way they expected the river to rise in spring. We'd become a thing the town counted on, the two of us, a fixed point on somebody's map.

Astrid Vane, who had spent six years being nobody's fixed point.

* * *

The storm built while we climbed.

I'd seen a thousand storms from the valley — watched them stack up over the escarpment like armies on a ridge, watched the lightning stitch the dark. But from up here, on the plateau road with the land falling away on both sides, it was a different animal entirely. You could see the whole architecture of it. The anvil cloud spreading out like a black canopy over half the sky, and under it the tower still boiling upward, and under *that* — lowering, rotating, slow as a door swinging — the wall cloud.

"Look at her," Astrid breathed. She had the wheel in both hands and her eyes everywhere at once — mirror, sky, road, sky — and her voice had gone soft and reverent, the voice other people saved for churches. "Look at that inflow. She's drinking the whole valley."

The wind was pouring past us toward the storm, steady and warm, and the grass on the plateau was lying flat in long silver waves, all of it flowing the same direction, everything on the land feeding the thing in the sky. I'd spent my life on moving water. I knew currents, eddies, the pull of a rapid, the way a river could hold a boat and the way it could let go. And what I felt standing in that inflow wind was recognition. The storm was a river. It was the biggest river I'd ever stood beside, and it ran upward.

"Okay," I said. "Okay, I get it now."

Astrid glanced over. "Get what?"

"Why you chase. It's not the destruction. It's —" I gestured at all of it, the turning sky, the silver grass, the impossible scale. "It's the current. You've been reading water your whole life, you just read it in the sky."

She was quiet for a long moment. The truck hummed. Somewhere ahead, thunder rolled down the plateau like a barrel of stones.

"Nobody ever put it that way before," she said finally. "Everybody always asks why I'd *do* something like this. Like it's a symptom." Her jaw worked. "My whole life, people treated the moving like the sickness. The debt, the running, the never staying. And it took me thirty years to figure out the moving was never the sickness. The *alone* was." She looked at me then, quick, before the road took her eyes back. "I used to chase because the storm was the only thing that ever went where I went."

"And now?"

"Now I've got a reason to come back down the hill." She said it plain, no drama, and that was the most Astrid thing about it — she could say the biggest things in the flattest voice, and you'd feel the ground shift under you anyway.

* * *

We core-punched the northern flank at half past two, because Astrid wanted hail data and because she was, in her own words, "a little bit of a maniac, actually, I've been meaning to mention it." The hail came down the size of walnuts and then the size of eggs, hammering the cage, the whole world gone white and roaring, and I sat in my seat with my belt cutting my shoulder and I *laughed*. I couldn't help it. It was terror and delight in the same breath, exactly the feeling of the first drop into a big rapid, and Astrid heard me laughing and started laughing too, and for a full minute the two of us sat in a hailstorm on top of the world howling like idiots while the windshield held and the wipers lost their fight.

Then we dropped south, ahead of it, and the hail quit, and the light went green-black, and the tornado came down.

I will tell the truth about it, because Wren Thorn tells the truth even when it costs: I was scared. Properly scared, the animal kind, the kind that makes your hands stupid. It dropped off the wall cloud maybe three miles west of us, a gray rope at first, then fatter, then a wedge with debris turning at its base, and the sound reached us across the plain like a waterfall the size of God. Astrid parked on a rise with the storm's right side to us — the safe side, the *photographer's* side, she said, though she wasn't shooting pictures — and we watched it walk a fence line two miles out, tearing a farm shed into the air in pieces no bigger than kindling.

"EF-2, maybe three," she murmured. "It'll cross the river north of the Ford. North of the ferry." She checked her map, checked the sky, checked the map again. "Nobody's in its path. I've got the spotter net up — Hollis is relaying to the county." She exhaled. "It's going to miss everything that matters."

"Then why are we here?"

She looked at me like the answer was obvious. Maybe it was.

"Because it's *there*," she said. "Because it's the most alive thing on this continent right now and we're the only two people watching it from the right side." She leaned her forearms on the wheel. "You know what I used to think, out here alone? That this was the only honest thing left. A storm doesn't lie to you. It doesn't smile while it takes your legs out. It just is what it is, at full volume, and you either read it right or it kills you." The tornado crossed the river road a mile out and was gone from our sight behind a ridge, and the rain curtain swallowed it. "And then this woman with a ferry and a grudge taught me that people can be honest too. Took her a whole season. Stubborn as a mule in a hailstorm."

"Watch it."

"Compliment," she said. "Highest one I've got."

* * *

We came down the escarpment at dusk with the storm walking away north, its last lightning flickering inside the cloud like a lantern behind a curtain. The valley was gold and washed-clean, the river running high and bright, and Kestrel's Ford sat in it like a thing that had always been there and always would be — the ferry, the landing, the woodsmoke, the lights coming on one by one because the town knew we'd be hungry and the town had decided, without a vote, without telling us, that we'd eat at Marta's tonight.

"Back by dark," Astrid said, rolling down the main street. "Told you."

"You didn't *tell* me. You bet me a pie."

"And I won a pie."

"You bet *on* us. That's different. That's cheating."

"That's forecasting," she said, and grinned, and pulled the truck up in front of Marta's, where a plate was already set — two plates, because of course, because the town had stopped asking which of us was staying and had simply set the table for two, every time, like gravity.

We went in out of the last of the rain. And that's when Hollis caught us at the door, hat in hand, rain still dripping off him, wearing the particular face he wore when the news was strange.

"Letter came for you, Vane. Courier, this afternoon." He handed it over, apologetic. "Postmarked out of Meridian City."

I felt Astrid go still beside me — that old stillness, the debt-collector stillness, the gas-station-in-the-dark stillness — and then I felt her let it go, deliberately, like setting down a weight. The Syndicate was broken. Its ledgers were in a federal courthouse. Whatever this was, it couldn't reach us anymore, and she knew it, and I watched her know it.

She turned the envelope over once in her fingers. Then she tucked it unopened into her jacket pocket, right over her heart, and put her arm through mine, and walked me into the warm and the noise and the smell of Marta's stew.

"After supper," she said. "Some things you read on a full stomach."

But I saw her hand drift up to that pocket twice before the food came, and I saw the way the firelight caught her face when she thought nobody was looking — and I knew that whatever was in that letter, the next storm was already building.
