## 22. Source and Mouth

**Astrid**

The river ended in a fan of gray water and pale sand, spreading out into the sea like a hand opening after being clenched for a very long time.

Astrid stood at the edge of it with her boots off and her trousers rolled to the knee, the cold pushing up through the wet sand at her ankles. Behind her, two hundred miles inland, the escarpment stood blue against the morning haze — she could just make out its line from here, that long stone shelf where she'd spent so many years of her life running along the top of storms. It looked smaller from this distance. Everything did.

The truck was parked on the shingle above the tideline, still wearing the dust of three days' drive from Kestrel's Ford. Wren had insisted they take the river road the whole way down, following the water from the ferry landing to the sea, as if the river needed an escort to its own ending. Astrid hadn't argued. She was learning not to argue with Wren about rivers.

"You're doing the thing again," Wren said.

She was crouched at the waterline, sleeves pushed up, turning something over in her hands. The tin. Old dented aluminum, the kind prospectors carried tobacco in, the kind Elias Thorn had carried his whole ruined life in.

"What thing?"

"The calculating thing." Wren glanced up. Sunlight caught the scar through her eyebrow — the one she'd gotten at Miller's Bend, ducking under a collapsed beam while Astrid held the lantern. "You're standing there working out distances. Storm paths. Interest rates. Whatever it is you do behind those eyes when you think nobody's watching."

"I don't do interest rates anymore," Astrid said. "That's the whole point."

"Then what are you calculating?"

Astrid looked out at the water. The river came down out of the mountains carrying silt the color of old silver, and where it met the sea the two waters didn't mix right away — you could see the seam of them, the river's ghost holding its shape for a hundred yards before the ocean took it apart.

"How far a man's promise travels," she said. "That's all."

Wren considered that, then nodded once, the way she did — no ceremony, no softness wasted. She stood and walked up the beach to the truck and came back with the tin and a folded square of oilcloth, and together they walked out to where the water ran shallowest and fastest, at the seam between river and sea.

* * *

**Wren**

Her father's ashes weighed almost nothing.

Wren had known they wouldn't — she'd read enough, heard enough, from the women at the Ford who'd buried husbands — but knowing it and feeling it were different animals. The tin sat light in her palms, lighter than a map, lighter than a debt, lighter than any of the things he'd left her. She'd carried heavier loads across the ferry crossing when she was twelve years old.

The letters were gone now. Buried three weeks ago at the spring, under Thorn's cairn at the head of the river, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, with a flat stone on top that Astrid had hauled up from the canyon floor without being asked. Wren had said nothing while she watched her do it. Some things you couldn't say while they were happening. You had to wait until the ground settled.

She'd read every letter twice before burying them. The last one was dated eleven years back, written from a mining camp she'd never heard of, and it ended with a line she could still recite with her eyes closed: *Tell her the river runs one direction only. Tell her I'm sorry I couldn't be the kind of man who stays. Tell her the water remembers everything.*

He'd never sent it. That was the part that broke something open in her chest, even now. He'd written it and folded it and kept it in his coat for eleven years, a coward to the last, too ashamed to mail an apology to a girl who'd stopped waiting for one.

But he'd kept the map honest. He'd kept the lode hidden. He'd died in a stranger's truck with a bullet in him and floodwater rising, and his last breath had gone not to fear but to a promise: *take it to my daughter.*

And the stranger had done it. Through collectors and canyons and a beating at the portage that still showed on Astrid's ribs when the weather turned cold. Through all of it, she'd come.

"Should I say something?" Wren asked. Her voice came out rougher than she meant it to.

"You can say anything you want," Astrid said. "Or nothing. He owed you more words than he ever gave anybody. Doesn't mean you owe him any back."

Wren almost laughed. That was Astrid all over — practical as a pry bar, and somehow, underneath it, exactly the right thing.

She unscrewed the tin.

The wind came off the sea and gusted the ash sideways, gray-white against the gray-blue water, and Wren tipped the tin slowly, letting the river have him at its own pace. The ashes caught the current and fanned out toward the seam where fresh water met salt, and for a moment — she'd swear this later, to herself, in the dark, though she'd never say it aloud — the seam seemed to open around them, the river's shape holding him a moment longer before the ocean took him apart and made him part of everything.

"The water remembers everything," she said.

Astrid didn't answer. But Wren saw her hand move, quick and small, touching the place below her collarbone where she kept things she didn't talk about — the same gesture she'd made at the spring, at the burning, at the moment the map went up in sparks and rain. A habit, maybe. Or a prayer. Wren had stopped trying to figure out which.

They stood there until the tin was empty. Then Wren rinsed it in the shallows, and Astrid took it and stowed it in the truck bed without discussion, because some jobs belonged to both of them now and neither needed to say so.

* * *

**Astrid**

They built a driftwood fire above the tideline because Wren said you didn't leave a graveside without a fire, and Astrid had learned by now that Wren's rules about rivers and funerals and breakfast were not negotiable, and that obeying them cost nothing and paid in ways she was still learning to count.

The fire burned low and orange against the afternoon. Astrid sat with her knees drawn up, watching the smoke lean east, back toward the mountains, back toward the source.

"There's something I haven't told you," she said. "About how he died. All of it."

Wren fed a stick to the flames. "I figured there was."

"He wasn't dead yet when I found him." Astrid kept her eyes on the fire. "You know that part. What you don't know is what he said before the map. Before the promise." She turned the stick in her fingers. "He asked me if I was running from something. And I told him yes. And he laughed — this broken, wet laugh, blood in it — and he said, *good. Runners know how to finish.*"

The fire popped. Down the beach, a gull complained at the tide.

"He knew," Astrid went on. "Somehow he looked at me for thirty seconds on a flooded road and knew exactly what I was. A woman driving away from her own life at ninety miles an hour, telling herself it was work. Telling herself the debt was the reason, when the debt was just the excuse." She paused. "He gave me the map because he thought a runner was the only person who could get it through. And he was right. But he also gave it to me because—" Her voice caught, and she let it catch; she'd learned that much, at least. "Because he wanted someone to finish something. And he wanted it to be someone who understood why finishing was hard."

Wren was quiet for a long time. When Astrid finally risked a glance at her, the younger woman's face was turned toward the water, jaw tight, eyes bright in the firelight.

"He picked well," Wren said at last.

"He picked a stranger with a price on her head and a notebook full of loan math."

"He picked somebody who stayed." Wren turned to face her fully. "That's the thing you still don't get, Astrid. Everybody in Kestrel's Ford has run from something — the mine, the drought, the war, their own names. Staying is the rare thing. Staying is the expensive thing. And you stayed. Through Voss, through Halloran, through the flood, through me being a complete—"

"Don't ruin it," Astrid said.

"—a complete mule about trusting you," Wren finished, unrepentant. "You stayed. So whatever story you've been telling yourself about who you are when you're not moving — it's wrong. It's been wrong since the portage. Maybe since the gas station."

Astrid looked at her hands. They were steady. For years they'd only been steady on a wheel, at speed, with weather on the horizon. Now they were steady sitting still beside a fire on a beach at the end of a river, and the steadiness felt like a room she'd never known her house had.

"I keep waiting for it to cost something," she admitted. "The staying. Like there's an invoice coming."

"There's always an invoice coming," Wren said. "That's just living. The trick is finding people worth splitting it with."

* * *

**Wren**

The tide came in and they moved the fire back, and the light went long and gold across the water, and Wren found herself thinking about the word she'd been carrying since the spring. Since the cairn. Since Astrid climbed down the escarpment ahead of a supercell with hail hammering her shoulders and put herself between Wren and Halloran's crew without a second's arithmetic.

She'd almost said it that night, in the flooded gorge, with the map burning and the marshal's boat cutting through the chop. Almost said it at the ferry landing, the day the town set a third plate at the table without anyone deciding to. Almost said it a dozen mornings since, over coffee, over rope work, over the small accumulating domesticity of two people sharing a dock and a truck and a life.

Words had never been cheap for Wren Thorn. Her father had taught her that by never spending any.

"Astrid."

"Mm."

"When we get back — the ferry season starts in six weeks. There's the north crossing to rebuild, and the town council wants to talk about a second boat, and Mrs. Aldous has already volunteered you for the harvest festival whether you agreed or not." She watched the fire instead of the woman. "It's going to be a lot. It's going to be years, probably. It's going to be staying."

"I know."

"And I want to say something, and I'm going to say it badly, and you're going to have to live with that."

Astrid turned. In the low gold light her face was open in a way it had never been on the escarpment, never been in the truck, never been anywhere except maybe — maybe — the moment at the source when the map went up in flame and she'd smiled through the rain like a woman setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"My father wrote that the water remembers everything," Wren said. "I used to think that was about guilt. About how you can't outrun what you did." She shook her head. "It's not. It's about this. Every mile of that river, from the spring to here — every camp, every rapid, every bad night and good one — it all ends up in the same place. All of it arrives together. None of it gets left behind."

She took a breath.

"So. Family. That's the word. That's what I'm saying. Badly. You and me. Family."

The fire cracked. The tide breathed in and out against the sand, patient as anything.

Astrid didn't cry — Wren would have bet the ferry on that; the woman probably cried once a decade and billed herself for it afterward. But her throat worked, and her eyes went glass-bright, and when she spoke her voice was rough as river gravel.

"I've never been anybody's family," she said. "I've been a client, a target, a storm chaser, a name on a ledger. I didn't know it came in a version without paperwork."

"It doesn't," Wren said. "This is the paperwork. This fire, this beach, this conversation. Congratulations. You're stuck with me."

Astrid laughed — really laughed, the sound startled out of her like a bird — and reached over and shoved Wren's shoulder, and Wren shoved back, and for a minute they were just two people wrestling in the sand by a fire like kids, and the ocean took their laughter apart and scattered it the way it had scattered the ashes, everywhere, part of everything.

* * *

**Astrid**

They drove back up the coast road as the sun went down, the truck windows open, salt air moving through the cab. Wren drove. Astrid rode with her arm out the window and her notebook open on her knee — the same battered notebook, though the pages of loan math were long since torn out and burned, and what filled it now were river stages, ferry schedules, seed prices for the garden Wren had bullied her into planting, and weather.

Always weather. That would never change. Some part of her would always watch the sky the way other people watched the door.

Ahead, far inland, over the escarpment, a thunderhead was building — tall and white-shouldered, catching the last light, the first cell of the season. Astrid watched it the way you watch an old friend come up a road you both know.

"Big one tomorrow," she said. "Maybe the day after. It'll track northeast along the ridge."

Wren glanced at the cloud, then at her. "We'll be on the north crossing by then. New pilings won't survive a flood stage."

"We'll finish early, then."

"That's your answer to everything now, isn't it. We'll finish early."

"It's a good answer." Astrid clicked her pen closed and looked at the storm building over the high country, and then at the girl beside her — the ferryman's daughter, her family, hers — and felt the strange, settled weight of a life with a return address. "Everything worth doing, we finish early. Together."

The road bent north. The river ran beside them, one direction only, home.

*The End*
