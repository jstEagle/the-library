## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The supercell had a name before it had a face, which was how Ulyana Fairweather liked her storms. Names were orderly. Faces were trouble.

The National Weather Service called it a mesocyclone over Custer County. The chasers on the radio called it "the Elk City cell." Ulyana called it *Doris*, because Doris had been her mother's least favorite aunt and this storm had the same energy: enormous, slow to commit, and liable to ruin an afternoon without ever apologizing.

She'd been tracking Doris since dawn from a gas station in Weatherford, Oklahoma, drinking coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during a previous administration, watching the radar paint itself green and then yellow and then that particular bruised magenta that meant *pay attention now*. By eleven she was westbound on Route 66 with her truck loaded, her instruments humming, and her phone buzzing in the cupholder like a hornet trapped under glass.

She didn't look at it. She knew who it was.

The Meridian Syndicate did not call like normal people called. Normal people let a phone ring four times and give up. The Syndicate's calls came in waves — three quick, one long, a pause, then two more — a pattern Ulyana had timed once out of boredom and never managed to un-notice. Three-one-two. Like a knock at a door you couldn't refuse to answer, delivered by someone patient enough to knock all day.

"Go away," she told the phone. "I'm working."

The phone buzzed again. Three-one-two.

*Working* was generous. What Ulyana did for a living occupied a gray zone between science, sport, and vagrancy. She chased storms. She sold data — wind profiles, pressure traces, hail-core imagery — to universities, insurance firms, and once, memorably, a Norwegian film production that wanted realistic weather for a scene involving a lighthouse and, inexplicably, a submarine. She lived in her truck, a battered white F-250 named Persephone, because Persephone spent half the year underground and the other half dragging spring back into the world, and Ulyana felt that was a reasonable professional aspiration.

Persephone was financed, as it happened, by the Meridian Syndicate. So was the radar unit bolted to the bed. So was last season's fuel, last season's lodging, and a regrettable six-week stretch in Kansas when Ulyana had eaten nothing but gas-station taquitos and optimism.

The Syndicate did not think of these things as loans. They thought of them as *investments in opportunity*. This was the phrase they used, always, with the serene confidence of people who had never once been punched for it. The Meridian Syndicate was, formally, a private lending concern registered in Delaware with satellite offices in four countries and no discernible scruples. Informally, they were what happened when a book club got ambitious. They wore good coats. They smiled easily. They believed — genuinely, fervently, in a way that made them far more dangerous than mere greed — that every debt contained a door, and every door opened onto something better for everyone involved, provided you defined "everyone" narrowly enough.

Ulyana owed them forty-three thousand dollars and change. The change mattered to them. She could tell because they kept mentioning it.

Three-one-two went the phone.

"All right," she muttered, and answered on speaker, because holding a phone while driving through increasing hail was how people died stupid. "Fairweather."

"Miss Fairweather." The voice belonged to Mr. Aldous Vane, the Syndicate's senior collections officer, a man who sounded permanently like he was reading aloud from a document he found delightful. "What a pleasure. Are we interrupting anything?"

"You're interrupting a supercell."

"Wonderful. Is it going well?"

"It's going," Ulyana said, and eased Persephone around a drifting RV with Iowa plates and no survival instinct whatsoever. Ahead, the storm had begun assembling its argument. The updraft base stretched across half the sky, green-black and churning, and beneath it hung the smooth gray bulge of a wall cloud rotating with the lazy confidence of something that had all day. Which it did. Storms were the only creditors Ulyana respected. They told you exactly what they wanted, gave you a precise window to pay, and if you failed to comply they took everything without litigation.

"We've received your quarterly statement," Vane said.

"I know. I wrote it."

"And your accompanying note." A pause, delicately weighted. "The one that said, and I'm quoting our transcriptionist, 'the check is in the mail, stop asking.'"

"I stand by it. It's technically true. There is a check in the mail. It's for eleven dollars. It's the eleven dollars I have."

Vane laughed. It was a warm sound, genuinely warm, which was the worst thing about him. "You're funny, Miss Fairweather. That's an asset. We value assets."

"I'm a person, Vane."

"That too. Now — business. The board has reviewed your account, and I'm authorized to offer you a restructuring package. Rather than cash repayment, which we understand is... geographically difficult for someone of your lifestyle—"

"My lifestyle is a truck."

"—the Syndicate would accept repayment in kind. Opportunities. We have a venture requiring your particular skills, and successful completion would clear a substantial portion of the principal. Substantial," he repeated, letting the word sit there like bait in clear water.

Ulyana watched the wall cloud tighten. Somewhere ahead, maybe eight miles, maybe ten, the air had gone that strange bruised yellow-green that old farmers called *hail light* and that Ulyana called *Tuesday*. Her hands were steady on the wheel. Her stomach, meanwhile, had quietly begun doing the thing it did whenever the Syndicate said *opportunity*, which was to remember that opportunities, in their vocabulary, were things other people fell into.

"What kind of venture?" she asked, because not asking would only mean more calls.

"A trip. Overseas. All expenses covered."

"I don't do overseas. Overseas has weather I can't read and paperwork I can't outrun."

"This one involves weather you'll want to see." Vane's voice did something subtle, a half-step down, the salesman becoming the confidant. "Coastal. Anomalous tidal behavior. I'm told there's a stretch of English coastline where the sea does something once a generation that it shouldn't be able to do at all."

Ulyana's hands went still on the wheel for exactly one second. One second was all she allowed herself.

Because here was the thing, the thing she told no one, the thing she kept in the same locked room of herself where she kept her mother's voice and the smell of salt on a cold morning: she had grown up on a stretch of English coastline where the sea did something it shouldn't be able to do. She had left it at nineteen with a duffel bag and a grudge and a rule, and the rule was simple, load-bearing, carved into the foundation of everything since:

*Never stop moving.*

Stopping was where the grief caught up. Stopping was where the debts came due. As long as Persephone was rolling — toward storms, away from storms, it hardly mattered which — Ulyana Fairweather was a woman in motion, and motion, she had learned, was the closest thing to forgiveness the world offered at retail prices.

And now Vane, with his delighted little voice, was dangling the one destination she had spent fourteen years driving away from.

"Cornwall," she said flatly. "You want me to go to Cornwall."

There was a pause on the line. Small, but Ulyana had spent her life reading pauses the way other people read clouds, and this one had structure. Rotation. Something in it was spinning up.

"The location details will follow through proper channels," Vane said, which meant yes. "A colleague will accompany you. He's already booked. Consider him... a co-investor's representative."

"A handler."

"A colleague," Vane repeated pleasantly. "He'll meet you at the airport Thursday. His name is Caspian."

"Of course it is," Ulyana said. "Nobody born to collect debts gets named Gary."

Another laugh, warm as banked coals. "Thursday, Miss Fairweather. And do mind the storm. We've grown rather fond of you. Assets are so hard to replace."

The line went dead. Three-one-two, one last time, out of habit or contempt.

Ulyana dropped the phone into the cupholder and looked up at the sky, because the sky at least never pretended to be her friend.

Doris had committed. The wall cloud had lowered into a tight, furious bowl, and beneath it dust was rising off the fields in a ragged skirt — not a tornado yet, but the atmosphere rehearsing one, running its scales. Rain curtains dragged across the prairie like gauze over a wound. The light had gone the color of old brass. Somewhere in that churning dark was a column of wind waiting to be born, and Ulyana felt the old pull, the clean and simple hunger of it: *get closer, get the data, get the shot, get gone.*

That was the whole religion, really. Get close enough to touch the terrible thing, survive it, and leave before it learned your name.

She swung Persephone south onto a farm road, gravel crackling under the tires, and let the storm fill the windshield. This was the part she was good at. This was the only part. Ahead of her the world turned green and roared; behind her lay eleven dollars, forty-three thousand dollars and change, and a man named Caspian waiting at an airport with a smile like a signed contract.

"Hail!" she announced to the empty cab, cheerfully, as the first stones came down fat as grapes and began drumming the roof like applause. "Good girl, Doris. Show me everything."

For twenty minutes she was entirely happy. That was the honest truth of her and nobody would have believed it: inside the worst weather on earth, threading the margins of a spinning monster with hail hammering down and lightning stitching the clouds together overhead, Ulyana Fairweather was more at peace than she ever was anywhere else. The storm demanded everything and lied about nothing. You always knew where you stood with a supercell. It wanted your attention, possibly your truck, conceivably your life, and it told you so in advance, in plain language, with a barometer.

No creditor had ever been that honest with her.

The tornado dropped at 4:17 p.m., a slender white rope against the rain shafts, dancing along a section line for six minutes and touching nothing but wheat and fence posts. Ulyana got the whole thing on camera, got the pressure trace, got the wind profile, got the money shot that would pay for next month's fuel. Then the rope thinned, dissolved, and Doris moved off northeast to bother somebody else, trailing her anvil of cloud across the sky like a dress hem.

And the silence came in, the way it always did. The post-storm quiet, wide and ringing, with the smell of rain-wet earth and torn wheat and the sun breaking through low and gold over the flooded fields. Anybody else would have called it beautiful. Ulyana did call it beautiful. But underneath the beauty sat the other thing, the familiar passenger, the one that climbed into the cab the moment the engines of the sky shut down: the stillness. The stopping.

In the stillness there was nowhere to drive except inward, and inward was where the unpaid things lived. Her mother's voice on a voicemail she'd saved and never replayed. The cottage on the escarpment she hadn't seen in fourteen years. The letter — and here her stomach dipped, because there *was* a letter, wasn't there, sitting unopened in the glovebox for nine days now, forwarded twice, soft-cornered from handling, with a solicitor's return address and a faint tide-mark stain across the envelope like the sea had licked it on its way to her.

She knew what it was. She'd known since she saw the postmark. Estates required heirs, and she was the only one left, and her mother was dead, and Ulyana had found out about it from a piece of paper instead of a phone call because she had spent fourteen years being unreachable, professionally, deliberately, gloriously unreachable, and now the bill for all that freedom had arrived in an envelope stained with salt water.

*Never stop moving.* But the mail, it turned out, could move faster than she could.

Ulyana pulled Persephone onto the gravel shoulder and sat in the golden wreckage of the afternoon, engine ticking, wipers smearing the last of the hail-melt across the glass. Forty miles east, the storm walked away over the horizon, huge and indifferent and free, owing nothing to anyone.

"Opportunities," she said aloud, tasting the word. Coastal. Anomalous tides. Cornwall.

They knew. Of course they knew. The Syndicate didn't fund chases out of love of meteorology; they funded them the way fishermen chummed water. And somewhere in their ledgers, between her radar unit and her taquito debt, someone had written down the one place on earth she could never drive away from fast enough — and decided to send her there, with a smiling man named after the sea, and call it generosity.

Ulyana rested her forehead briefly against the wheel.

Then she straightened, because that was the rule's corollary: dread was fine, dread was fuel, but you put it in the tank and you drove. She reached into the glovebox, past the registration and the expired parking passes from six states, and took out the letter. Salt-stained. Soft. Patient as the tide.

She didn't open it. Not yet. Opening it was a door, and doors were how the past got in.

But she set it on the dashboard, face-up, where she could see it, which was as close to brave as she could manage before sundown.

"Thursday," she told the empty road, and put Persephone in gear, and drove toward the sunset because it was the only direction that wasn't east, and east was where the ocean waited, and the ocean, she suspected, had been talking to the Meridian Syndicate.

Behind her, the storm faded into the gold. Ahead of her, the phone buzzed once in the cupholder — not three-one-two this time, just a single buzz, a text, from a number she didn't know:

*Flight's booked. Don't be late. — C*

Ulyana looked at it for a long moment.

"Well," she said to the storm-light, "aren't you charming."

She drove on, and the letter rode the dashboard beside her like ballast, and somewhere over the Atlantic a man was packing a suitcase full of other people's obligations, and neither of them knew yet that the sea kept books of its own.
