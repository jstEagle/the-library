## 2. A Letter with Salt Water Stains

The letter arrived in Tulsa on a Tuesday, which was already a bad sign, because Ulyana Fairweather had learned long ago that the universe reserved its worst weather and its worst mail for days that promised nothing.

She found it wedged in the screen door of the motel office, half-soaked from the sprinklers, its envelope bloated and warped like something the sea had tried to swallow and given up on. The postmark read *Penzance, Cornwall*. The return address belonged to a firm of solicitors whose name sounded less like lawyers and more like a firm of undertakers: *Tregose, Penhale & Vellacott*.

Ulyana stood in the parking lot with her boots in a puddle of her own making and did not open it for a full minute. She held it up to the Oklahoma sun instead, tilting it, as if she could see through it the way she saw through cloud decks—by reading the light. The paper had gone translucent in places where the water stains spread, and there were marks along one edge that weren't water at all. They looked like salt. Crystallized, dried, faintly glittering salt, as though the letter had made part of its journey pressed against something that remembered the ocean.

Her mother's handwriting was not on the envelope. That was the first thing she checked, and the relief of it annoyed her. Relief implied she'd been hoping. Hope was a luxury for people who stayed put.

Inside the motel room, with the air conditioner rattling like a loose panel on a chase truck, she finally tore it open.

*Dear Ms. Fairweather,*

*We write with regard to the estate of your late mother, Mrs. Morwenna Fairweather, of Trenarrow Cottage, Escarpment Row, Porthcullis, Cornwall.*

Ulyana sat down on the bed. Then she stood up again, because sitting felt too much like being caught.

*Late.* The word sat there on the page, small and terrible and official. Her mother was dead. Had been dead, according to the dates further down, for eleven months. Eleven months, and Ulyana had been chasing storms across four states while somewhere on a cliff in Cornwall the tide came in and out over a cottage she hadn't seen since she was nineteen.

They hadn't spoken in fourteen years. Not since the argument—the real one, the one with doors slammed so hard the windows buzzed, the one about Ulyana leaving, about chasing weather instead of staying, about how a Fairweather who couldn't be still was no Fairweather at all. Her mother had said something at the end of it that Ulyana had carried ever since, wrapped in cotton wool, taken out only at 3 a.m. in motel rooms exactly like this one: *You'll run until the road runs out, and then you'll have nowhere left to stand.*

Well. The road hadn't run out yet. But apparently the woman standing at the start of it had.

*As sole named beneficiary,* the solicitor continued, in prose so careful it was almost apologetic, *you are requested to attend in person to conclude matters of inheritance. There are documents which we are instructed may only be placed in your hands directly, and certain effects which we believe you will wish to see.*

There followed an address, a date three weeks out, and a line that made her laugh out loud, alone, in a way that startled the ice machine through the wall:

*Please note: the cottage sits upon an unstable escarpment. Sensible footwear is advised.*

Sensible footwear. Fourteen years of silence, an entire childhood folded up and filed away, and the man wanted her to bring sensible footwear.

She set the letter down on the bedspread and looked at it the way she looked at a mesoscale discussion that didn't match what the sky was doing outside. Something underneath it. Something moving.

Because here was the thing—the thing she told no one, the thing she'd buried so deep even she sometimes lost the map to it: she had been meaning to go back. Not soon. Not honestly, not deliberately. But every spring, when the supercells stacked up over the plains like a debt coming due, some animal part of her brain whispered *northwest*, and she always assumed it meant Montana. It had never once occurred to her, until this moment, holding a salt-stained letter, that the animal part of her brain might have been navigating by a different compass entirely.

Homesickness, she decided, was just grief that hadn't found its storm yet.

She was still standing there, one hand flat on the letter as if it might blow away, when her phone rang.

The ringtone was the Syndicate's. Of course it was. Meridian never called on good days; they called the way barometric pressure dropped before a hailstorm—quietly, steadily, and always ahead of impact.

"Fairweather." She said it like a dare.

"Ulyana!" The voice was warm as fresh asphalt. Dez Okafor, Syndicate operations, the man who signed off on her financing and smiled in his voice the way loan sharks smile in doorways. "How's the Plains? We saw your last stream. Gorgeous structure on that cell near Woodward. You've got an eye, girl. It's why we love you."

"It's why you lien me," Ulyana said. "What do you want, Dez?"

"Straight to business. I respect that." A pause, papers shuffling somewhere in whatever glass tower the Syndicate kept its optimism in. "So. Funny story. We hear you've come into an estate."

The room went very still, the way air goes still before it starts to rotate.

"I haven't come into anything," Ulyana said carefully. "I got a letter."

"A letter inviting you to England! To a property! On a coastline!" Dez's delight was genuine; that was the frightening thing about the Meridian Syndicate. They weren't cynics. They believed, sincerely and completely, that everything in the world was an opportunity, and that opportunities were meant to be harvested. "Ulyana, do you know what coastal property in Cornwall is going for? Do you know what a *cliffside* parcel does? And you're the sole beneficiary. Sole! That's beautiful. That's poetry."

"It's my mother's house."

"And it can be your *asset*," Dez said, with the gentle patience of a man explaining rain to someone who'd never been wet. "Look. Here's the thing. Your balance with us isn't small. Radar array, the new truck suspension, that little incident in Kansas with the fence—" He let the fence hang there, unpaid and unburied. "But we're not monsters. We're optimists. So here's our offer: we fund the trip. Flights, lodging, the works. First-class if you want it. You go settle the estate, and we talk about restructuring. Maybe the property itself covers the debt. Everybody walks away dry."

Ulyana sat down on the bed again, and this time she stayed down.

Every instinct she owned was screaming. She had spent ten years learning to read the sky, and the same instrument worked on people: when something looked too smooth, too organized, too *generous*, there was rotation in it somewhere. The Syndicate did not fund trips. The Syndicate funded *returns*. Somewhere in this arrangement was a hook, and she could feel its shape without seeing it.

"What's the catch?" she asked.

"Catch? Ulyana. I'm wounded."

"You're a businessman. Wounded is your brand."

A laugh, warm and unbothered. "One condition. Tiny. Housekeeping, really. We send a man with you. Our guy. He handles the paperwork on-site, makes sure the transfer's clean, keeps the whole thing tidy. You won't even notice him. His name's Caspian. Caspian Ardent. Charming fellow. English, actually—he'll feel right at home."

"Your collector," Ulyana said, "comes to my mother's funeral-adjacent paperwork."

"Our *representative* comes to make sure *you* don't drown in this," Dez said smoothly. "Cornwall's got riptides, Ulyana. Legal ones. You want someone in the water with you who knows the currents."

She looked at the letter on the bedspread, at the salt stains glittering along its edge like tiny accusations. Her mother's cottage. Her mother's cliffs. The sea she'd run from at nineteen with a duffel bag and a grudge, promising herself she'd never stand still long enough to be buried anywhere.

And now the sea was calling her back, and the people who owned her wheels wanted to ride along.

"If I say no?" she asked.

"Then we talk about the Kansas fence," Dez said pleasantly, "and the radar array, and the suspension, and we do it in a courtroom in a jurisdiction that likes us." A beat, and then, softer, almost kind, which was somehow worse: "Take the trip, Ulyana. Grieve a little. See the ocean. Let us help. Helping people is literally our whole business model."

He hung up before she could answer, because he already knew the answer. That was the Syndicate's true genius: they never forced anything. They just arranged the weather and waited for you to walk into it.

* * *

She packed that night, which surprised her. She'd expected to fight it longer—to pace, to draft refusals, to sleep badly and bargain with herself till dawn. Instead she found herself pulling the duffel from under the bed, shaking the red dust out of it, folding shirts with the mechanical efficiency of someone who'd moved forty times in ten years.

It was only when she reached the bottom of the bag, into the pocket she never opened, that she stopped.

Inside, wrapped in a plastic sandwich bag gone brittle with age, was a photograph. Her mother, younger than Ulyana was now, standing on the cliff path above Porthcullis with wind tearing her dark hair sideways, one hand shading her eyes, the other gripping the collar of a squalling toddler—Ulyana herself, furious at the wind, at the sea, at everything. Her mother wasn't looking at the camera. She was looking out at the horizon with an expression Ulyana had spent her whole adult life trying to reproduce in reverse: the look of a person reading weather that hasn't arrived yet.

On the back, in faded ballpoint, her mother had written: *M. F., watching for the Meridian.*

Ulyana had thought, all these years, that it meant the ship. There had been a ship—the *Meridian Star*, wrecked off that coast in '79, before she was born, a story her mother told exactly once and then never again, closing the subject like a door. Watching for the Meridian. Waiting for a wreck to wash ashore. Grief, or guilt, or both.

Now, holding the photograph under the motel's buzzing light, with a solicitor's letter and a loan shark's blessing both pointing her toward the same cliff, the words rearranged themselves into something else. Not watching *for*. Watching *out for*.

She put the photograph back in its bag, and the bag in her jacket's inner pocket, over her heart, where she kept the things she refused to think about.

Three weeks later, at Will Rogers World Airport, she watched a man come toward her through the terminal carrying a leather satchel and wearing the particular smile of someone who has already read your file.

He was maybe thirty-five, tall, fair-haired, with the kind of neat, unhurried handsomeness that suggested he ironed his shirts himself and enjoyed it. Everything about him was orderly—the satchel, the stride, the way he'd clearly timed his approach to arrive precisely as she finished her coffee. But it was the smile that snagged her professional attention. She knew that smile. She'd seen it on the leading edge of every storm she'd ever chased: calm, well-formed, and hiding an enormous amount of energy behind a deceptively smooth face.

"Ms. Fairweather." He extended a hand. His grip was cool and exact. "Caspian Ardent. The Syndicate sent me. I'm told I'm your paperwork."

"You're told right," Ulyana said, shouldering her bag. "Rules of the road, Mr. Ardent. One: you don't touch my instruments. Two: you don't ask about the Kansas fence. Three: when I say we're leaving somewhere, we're leaving."

"Understood." He fell into step beside her, matching her pace without seeming to try, and glanced at the departure board with what looked like genuine pleasure. "Cornwall. I haven't been home in years."

"Home?"

"The southwest of it." Something crossed his face—quick, private, gone before she could name it. "You could say I have family history in the area."

"Lucky you," Ulyana said, and did not tell him that she had the sudden, irrational urge to check whether her mother's photograph was still in her pocket, safe from his neat, knowing hands.

They walked toward the gate together, the collector and the debtor, two strangers bound for the same cliff. Above the terminal windows, the Oklahoma sky was building towers—fat, white, ambitious cumulus stacking themselves toward trouble.

Ulyana watched them for a moment and felt, beneath the suspicion and the dread and the grief arriving sideways, one small treacherous ember of something that felt almost like hope.

The sea was calling her home. She just wished it hadn't sent a bill collector to fetch her.
