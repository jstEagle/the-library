## 3. The Man Who Smiled Like Collateral

Caspian Vane had a rule about airplanes, which was that they were the only place on earth where a man could be honest for six uninterrupted hours, because nobody could walk away from him mid-sentence.

He was not, strictly speaking, being honest right now. He was being pleasant, which was different, and which was the entire architecture of his profession.

"Chicken or pasta?" asked the flight attendant.

"Pasta, thank you," Caspian said, and smiled the smile that had closed four hundred deals and opened exactly no doors he hadn't wanted opened. It was a good smile. He maintained it the way other men maintained their cars: regular servicing, no rust, never let it stall in front of witnesses.

Across the aisle, three rows up and one window over, Ulyana Fairweather slept with her forehead against the glass like a woman who had made her peace with turbulence years ago and considered it a boring acquaintance.

Caspian opened his leather folio — real leather, the Syndicate's expense account had taste even if its morals were negotiable — and reviewed the file for the eleventh time since Reykjavik.

**FAIRWEATHER, ULYANA M., b. 1989. Storm chaser, self-employed, insolvent. Outstanding principal: £212,400. Interest accrued: considerable. Collateral offered: "opportunities." Collateral accepted under protest by the Board. Current assignment: escort to Cornwall estate of E. FAIRWEATHER (deceased). Recover debt or asset. Asset undefined. Define asset.**

The last line was in his own handwriting, from a month ago, when the board had handed him a folder thinner than most of their lunch menus and told him to make something of it. *Define asset.* He'd written it as a joke. Nobody at Meridian laughed at jokes; they wrote them down and priced them later.

He glanced up again. Ulyana's head had slid down the window until she was nearly folded in half, and a man in the next seat was eyeing her elbow with the wariness of someone who suspected it might deploy.

She'd spent the first hour of the flight doing what she'd done every hour since Heathrow: working. Charts on a battered tablet, a barometer app he was fairly sure she didn't trust, a paper notebook filled with handwriting that slanted downhill like weather fronts. She'd eaten the airline meal with the efficiency of someone refueling machinery, thanked the attendant twice, and then gone back to her numbers without once making small talk.

This had offended Caspian professionally. Small talk was his instrument section. He'd tried three openings over the Atlantic — the weather, the state of the coffee, an anecdote about a colleague who'd once tried to smuggle a ferret through customs in a hat — and she'd parried all three with the same polite, distracted *"mm"* you'd give a parking meter.

So he'd done the thing he did when charm failed: he'd watched, and waited, and taken notes.

*Subject sleeps sitting upright but badly. Subject checked the exit row twice without appearing to. Subject's notebook contains tide tables for the Cornish coast dated three weeks ahead — she has already predicted something. Subject said "thank you" to the baggage handler, the ticket agent, and the man whose trolley ran over her foot, in descending order of sincerity.*

He turned a page.

*Subject is my sister.*

There it was. The sentence he kept writing and kept finding again, like a coin in a coat pocket. He'd known for eleven days, since the solicitor's file had crossed his desk and he'd read the genealogy appendix out of professional thoroughness and then had to sit very still in a chair for twenty minutes while a building in London went on existing around him.

Eleanor Fairweather. First marriage, 1978, one son, given up. Second marriage, 1984, one daughter, kept. The son had been adopted by a family named Vane, shipbrokers, respectable, cold. The son had grown up to become very good at pretending things were fine.

Caspian had run the numbers on his own face in the mirror that night — the Fairweather jaw, apparently, though he'd always credited the Vanes — and then he'd packed his bag and told himself the assignment was simple.

Recover the debt or the asset. That was the brief. Clean as a knot.

What the brief did not account for was the fact that his sister — the word still fit strangely, like a shoe bought online — slept with her mouth slightly open and drooled faintly on the window, and that this undid him more thoroughly than any argument the board had ever staged against him.

* * *

They changed planes in Dublin, and Caspian learned something important, which was that Ulyana Fairweather could be talked to if you talked about weather.

It happened by accident. He'd fallen into step beside her in the terminal, running his usual opening gambit — "Terrible spring we're having, isn't it," which was not small talk so much as small artillery — and instead of the parking-meter *mm*, she stopped walking.

"It's not terrible," she said. "It's interesting. There's a blocking high over Scandinavia that's going to funnel everything up the Irish Sea for the next week. If you want terrible, wait until Thursday."

And there it was — the door in her, the one he'd been looking for. Not friendliness. Enthusiasm. She lit up the way other people did when discussing their children or their grievances.

"You track weather," he said, keeping his voice at the register of polite interest rather than the register of *I have read your file cover to cover*, which was a distinction he maintained like a firewall.

"I chase it." She adjusted the strap of a duffel bag that looked old enough to vote. "Or it chases me. We've never settled which."

"And the Syndicate finances this?"

Something closed behind her eyes — quick, total, like a shutter in wind. He noted it. He noted everything; it was the job, and also, increasingly, it was something else, something with less paperwork attached.

"The Syndicate finances lots of things," she said pleasantly. "That's their whole personality."

"They financed mine too, once." This was true, in a way. The Syndicate had paid for his education, his suits, his first apartment with the good light. In exchange he'd signed a piece of paper agreeing to be, essentially, a very well-dressed bloodhound. He'd been nineteen and flattered. It remained the worst deal he'd ever watched anyone sign, including himself.

Ulyana looked at him properly for the first time — a long, assessing look, the kind she probably gave to cloud formations before deciding whether to drive toward them or away. Whatever she concluded, she filed it without comment.

"Caspian, isn't it? Caspian Vane?"

"At your service."

"What kind of name is Vane?"

"A weathervane kind," he said. "My family liked instruments that point somewhere without committing."

The ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. "And what do you commit to, Mr. Vane?"

*Nothing,* said eleven years of training. *Commitment is exposure. Exposure is leverage held by someone else.*

"My expenses," he said, and she actually laughed — short, surprised, quickly suppressed, but a laugh. He logged it in the folder in his chest where he was beginning to keep things that would never go in the official report.

* * *

The second flight was smaller, propellers, the kind of plane where you could feel the sky thinking about you. Ulyana took the window and immediately pressed her palm flat against it, and Caspian realized she wasn't resting — she was reading the air, feeling for vibration the way a doctor palpates.

Below them, Cornwall assembled itself out of the haze: green fields stitched with stone walls, the sea a hammered pewter color, and then — as they banked south — the escarpment, rising sheer and dark from the water like the spine of something buried.

"There," Ulyana said quietly. Not to him. To the glass, maybe. To herself.

Caspian followed her gaze and felt the strange double vision that had plagued him since Dublin: the asset, and the sister, occupying the same body, looking at the same cliff.

His brief sat in the folio on his knees. He knew it by heart now, better than the board did, because he'd spent eleven nights adding to it in his own hand, late, when the hotel rooms got quiet:

*Day 3: Subject distrusts me. Correct instinct.*
*Day 5: Subject's mother's journals are missing pages. Someone redacted them before the solicitor sealed the estate. Not subject. Not me. Question stands: who?*
*Day 7: I have not reported the missing pages. First omission. Filed under: thoroughness.*
*Day 9: The village pub keeper says the cave opens once a generation and charges admission. Truth, price, or life. The board will want this quantified. I find I do not want them to have it.*
*Day 10: I looked up whether Eleanor Fairweather ever tried to contact me. She did. Four letters, returned unopened, per the adoption agency's records. My father intercepted them. I am having trouble filing this under anything.*

He closed the folio.

Below, the escarpment unrolled, and he saw what Ulyana saw: a scatter of roofs clinging to the clifftop like barnacles, a church tower, a lane that ended where the land simply gave up and became sea. And near the edge, alone, a low stone cottage with a slate roof furred in lichen — the Fairweather place, per the file, though the file hadn't mentioned how much it looked like a held breath.

The pilot announced their descent. Ulyana didn't move. Her hand stayed on the glass, and her lips moved slightly, shaping words he couldn't hear, and Caspian — collector, bloodhound, the man who smiled like collateral — looked away and gave her the privacy, because some debts you don't collect.

But underneath the decency, the older machinery was still turning, patient as interest. The Syndicate wanted an asset. His brief said define it. And he was beginning to suspect, with the particular dread of a man who reads documents for a living, that the asset was not gold and not land and not even the debt itself.

The asset was the truth about what had happened here in 1979 — and whoever carried it ashore would be carrying something that weighed more than treasure and cut worse than glass.

The wheels touched down. Ulyana exhaled, finally, like a diver surfacing.

"Welcome to Cornwall," Caspian said, and smiled his best smile, and for the first time in his career wondered who it was for.
