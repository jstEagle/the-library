## 4. Escarpment

The road to Porthcarrow did not so much end as give up.

Ulyana felt it happen through the steering wheel—the tarmac surrendering to gravel, the gravel surrendering to something that was technically a road only because someone had once painted a faded line down the middle of it, out of optimism or spite. The hire car's suspension made a sound like a small animal being asked a difficult question.

"Easy," she murmured to it, the way she'd talk to a truck in crosswinds. "We're almost there."

She was not almost there. She had been "almost there" for forty minutes, which was its own kind of weather system.

The lane climbed through hedgerows grown wild and architectural, arching overhead until she drove through a green tunnel that smelled of salt and cow parsley and something older underneath it—brine soaked into stone for centuries, the way smoke soaks into a coat. Then the hedges fell away all at once, the way scenery does at the top of a climb, and Ulyana pulled over without deciding to, because some things you do not drive past your first time.

The escarpment opened below her like a held breath let go.

It was not a cliff, exactly. Cliffs were walls. This was a collapse in progress, a mile of coastline caught mid-argument with the sea—tilted strata banded rust and slate and bone-white chalk, stepped down in terraces toward water the color of hammered pewter. A village clung to the middle of it, or perhaps merely refused to leave it: Porthcarrow, a scatter of granite houses stacked along ledges like nesting seabirds, chimneys leaning at angles that would have alarmed her if she hadn't spent her life around structures that survived by leaning. Down at the base, where the terraces met the tide line, a stone harbor curled around itself like a cupped hand, holding a dozen boats and a great deal of confidence about the sea's intentions.

Wind came up the face of the escarpment and hit the car with both palms. The whole vehicle rocked on its springs, gently, like a dog being greeted.

"Oh," said Ulyana, to no one, "you're *windy*."

She meant it as a compliment. It was the first thing Cornwall had said to her, and it had said it in her native language.

She sat there longer than she needed to. That was the thing nobody told you about grief arriving late—it didn't come when you expected it, at the funeral you skipped, or the phone call you took standing up because sitting down felt like admitting something. It came sideways, three weeks later, at the top of an unfamiliar road, in the form of wind against a windshield, and it said: *she looked at this every morning.* And you had no defense prepared, because who prepares defenses against a view?

Her mother had lived down there. Had walked those tilted lanes. Had died in a house perched over all that moving water, and Ulyana had found out from a solicitor's letter with saltwater stains on the envelope, as though even the post office here conducted itself according to the weather.

"Right," she said, and put the car in gear, because the alternative was sitting still, and Ulyana Fairweather had one rule older than any of her debts: never stop moving. Movement was how you outran supercells, creditors, and apparently feelings, though the last of these had better acceleration than most.

* * *

Porthcarrow received her the way small villages receive strangers: by watching.

She parked—there was one place to park, a gravel shelf above the harbor where three other cars sat wearing the patient expression of vehicles that had given up going anywhere fast—and got out into air that tasted of kelp and woodsmoke. The village street ran along the contour of the hillside, one lane wide, houses on the seaward side built low and thick-walled, their windows small, their doorsteps worn into shallow smiles by two hundred years of boots.

A woman was scrubbing a step opposite. She looked up, assessed Ulyana in approximately one second—boots, field jacket, camera bag, the particular sun-creased squint of someone who spent her working life looking at the sky—and delivered her verdict to the step rather than to Ulyana:

"You'll be the Fairweather girl, then."

Not a question. News traveled faster than cars here; Ulyana had probably been sighted at the top of the escarpment road before she'd finished having her moment with the view.

"I'm—" She hesitated over the word *daughter*. It had been so long since anyone had used it of her in connection with this place that it came out rusty. "Ulyana. Yes."

"Mm." The woman wrung her cloth with tremendous authority. "Maggie Trenoweth. Your mother bought her bread off me for thirty years and never once let me put butter in the bag unasked." A pause, weighted like ballast. "You've her chin."

"I've been told I have her everything except her habit of staying," Ulyana said, before her brain could stop her mouth.

To her surprise, Maggie Trenoweth laughed—a short, rough bark, genuine as gull noise. "Aye, well. She'd have liked that. She used to say the Fairweathers were a family of weathervanes—all direction, no roots." The laugh dropped away, and what was left underneath was older and more careful. "Cottage is up the terrace path. Second turning past the chapel. Key's under the third stone, same as ever." She went back to her step, then added, without looking up: "Mind the gulls. They've opinions about the roof."

* * *

The cottage stood at the end of the terrace path like a sentence someone had stopped writing halfway through.

It was smaller than Ulyana remembered, though her memory of it was twenty years old and mostly composed of one summer—salt on her lips, her mother's hands steady on a barometer, a kitchen that smelled of tea and solder. Granite walls gone soft with lichen, green-gold and silver. Slate roof sagging in the middle like a tired shoulder. A garden gone entirely to sea pinks and thrift, which at least had the decency to be beautiful about neglect. And beyond the low back wall, nothing but air and the long drop of the escarpment and the sea, enormous and unhurried, doing whatever it had been doing since before there were names for it.

The third stone yielded the key on the first try. Something about that undid her more than the cottage did—that the hiding place had held, that the world had kept her mother's small arrangements intact while Ulyana had been three thousand miles away outrunning storms that couldn't have cared less.

Inside, the air was cold and mineral and utterly still. Dust hung in the light from the one seaward window like a photograph of snowfall. There was a table. Two chairs. A stove, cold. Shelves with jars on them, their contents long since evaporated into residue and mystery. On the wall, a brass barometer, and Ulyana crossed to it before she'd consciously decided to, the way you check a pulse.

It read *Stormy*. Of course it did. Her mother had been dead five weeks; the needle hadn't moved, but somehow it still felt like a message left deliberately, the way you'd leave a light on.

"You dramatic old instrument," Ulyana said softly, and tapped the glass, and the needle shivered but held its ground. Good. She respected that in a barometer and in a person.

The journals were where she knew they'd be—not from memory, exactly, but from inheritance. Third shelf of the dresser in the back room, behind the tin that had once held shortbread and now held rubber bands and keys to doors that no longer existed. Eleven volumes, cloth-bound, their spines hand-labeled in her mother's upright, unsentimental hand: dates. Just dates. *1961–63. 1964–66.* Through to the last one, its cover stained with salt, ending five weeks ago.

Ulyana carried them to the table, and the light, and herself, and sat down in the chair that faced the sea because the other chair faced the empty room and she wasn't ready for that conversation yet.

She opened the earliest volume first. Storm journals. Her mother had been a keeper of them the way other people kept diaries—except where a diary says *I felt*, a storm journal says *the pressure fell*, and the feeling hides in between the lines like fish under ice. Ulyana knew the format intimately, because it was her own format, learned without knowing she was learning it, the way you learn a language from a parent who never formally teaches it.

*March 14th. Glass falling since dawn. Gulls inland by noon—they know before we do, they always know. Barometer 981 and steady fall. Took the shutters in off the north wall.*

And then, in the margin, smaller, in pencil, the part that wasn't meteorology: *Saw her standing at the wall again. Told her about the sea. She listened the way her father listens—with her feet already pointed elsewhere.*

Ulyana's breath did something complicated.

*Her.* That was her. That was the summer. She remembered the wall, the sea, her feet pointed elsewhere—pointed at horizons, always, even at eight years old. Her mother had written it down without judgment, without anything Ulyana could find to be wounded by, and somehow that was worse. Somewhere in Oklahoma, in a motel outside Woodward, she had once told a bartender that her mother never wrote to her. The bartender had nodded sympathetically. Neither of them had considered that maybe the writing had simply never been addressed to anywhere Ulyana was standing.

Grief arrived sideways, then. It came in through the margins.

She turned pages. The handwriting aged with the decades, loosening, the entries growing longer, more technical, more confident—a self-taught meteorologist assembling herself across the years, correlating tide tables with pressure traces, noting the behavior of the escarpment in sou'easters, recording things about the coast that no official station bothered with because no official station was mad enough to sit on a collapsing cliff and take notes.

And then, in the volume covering 1978–80, Ulyana hit the holes.

She noticed it first as rhythm. The journal had a cadence—daily entries, terse, consistent—and suddenly the cadence broke. Pages torn out, close-cropped at the spine, torn deliberately, carefully, by someone who loved these books enough to bind them by hand and then, one season, removed pieces of them. Not randomly. All the excisions clustered around the same stretch of months: late autumn 1979 into spring of 1980.

Where pages remained in that span, they were redacted. Not blacked out—her mother had been neater than that, stranger than that. Whole passages had been scraped away with a blade, leaving thin translucent windows in the paper through which you could see the ghost of the writing on the reverse side, mirrored, illegible, maddening. What survived around the scrapings was worse than nothing, because fragments are the ocean's favorite food:

*...the Meridian Star came in ahead of the...*

*...he said the cargo was insured twice over and laughed...*

*...they will say it was the weather. It is never only the weather...*

*...I saw the ring. God help me, I saw the ring on his...*

Ulyana set the book down. Her hands weren't shaking, which surprised her; she'd have bet on shaking. Outside, the wind had come around and was testing the chimney, and somewhere below, the sea worked at the base of the escarpment with the patience of something that had already won and was just being polite about it.

*Meridian Star.*

She knew the name. Everyone in her line of work knew the name, the way everyone in a family knows the name of the rich uncle nobody invites to Christmas. The Meridian Syndicate—storm-chasing financiers, salvage speculators, lenders of last resort to people like her, whose founder had built an empire on weather data and wreck rights and a reputation for generosity that Ulyana had personally tested and found to be a loan shark wearing a cardigan. The Syndicate had financed her last chase. The Syndicate's man was currently in the village pub downstairs from her lodging, smiling like collateral, waiting to hear what her mother's estate contained.

And her mother's journal, in the autumn of 1979, had been writing about the *Meridian Star*—and then had stopped writing about it, violently, page by page, as though the ship's name were a leak that had to be patched.

*I saw the ring.*

Ulyana looked at the scraped windows in the paper for a long time. The light moved. The dust drifted. The barometer in the other room said *Stormy*, and held.

There were eleven volumes. She had gone through one and a half. Whatever her mother had found—or seen, or been threatened into forgetting—was scattered through the rest like wreckage after a storm: not gone, just redistributed, waiting for someone patient enough to walk the tideline.

Ulyana reached for the next volume, and that was when she heard footsteps on the terrace path—unhurried, deliberate, and stopping, just once, just briefly, at the gate, as though whoever it was wanted her to know they'd arrived before they knocked.

Through the salt-hazed window she could see the shape of him. Caspian. The Syndicate's smile, come calling.

He raised a hand, friendly as a foreclosure notice, and knocked on the door of the cottage her mother had kept, and Ulyana closed the journal on the word *ring* and went to answer it with her heart hammering out a forecast of its own.
