## 5. Legends Charge Admission

The Anchor & Ebb smelled of woodsmoke, spilled stout, and forty years of the same fish frying in the same oil. Caspian noted all three on entry—habit, not sentiment. Smells were data. A room's smell told you how long its secrets had been simmering.

He had been in Porthcarrow for six days and had already catalogued the village the way he catalogued everything: eleven occupied cottages along the cliff road, four derelict, one church with a leaning spire that would not survive another decade of southwesterlies, a post office open Tuesdays and Thursdays only, and a single lane down to a shingle beach that ended abruptly where the escarpment had shrugged off a hundred tons of itself sometime in the nineties. The cottage Ulyana had inherited sat at the seaward edge like a man leaning too far over a railing. He'd watched her walk its rooms yesterday through a gap in the curtains she hadn't drawn, and he'd seen her stop at the bookshelf and go very still, and he had written *asset attached to place* in his notebook and then crossed it out, because it wasn't true, and because for one unprofessional moment he had felt something adjacent to shame.

Tonight he was doing what the Syndicate paid him to do: listening.

The pub was half full—a handful of fishermen, two retirees playing cribbage with the slow ferocity of people who had been beating each other at the same game since Thatcher, and a barman who polished glasses that did not appear to need polishing. Ulyana had claimed the corner table by the window with her back to the wall, which told him she'd done this kind of work before, or at least watched enough films about it. She had her instruments spread across the table like a surgeon's tray: barometer, tide tables annotated in pencil, a laptop whose screen glowed with pressure maps that looked, to his eye, like abstract art with opinions.

"Your round," she said without looking up.

"I bought the last one."

"And I'm buying the next one. That's how rounds work, Caspian. It's a beautiful system. You should let yourself enjoy it."

He went to the bar. This was part of the job too—the buying of drinks, the small purchases of goodwill. He ordered two pints of the local bitter and a packet of salt and vinegar crisps, because Ulyana had declared crisps "a food group" on day two and he had learned to pick his battles.

The old man at the end of the bar had been watching him since he walked in. Caspian had clocked him immediately: weathered to the color of old rope, hands like knotted mooring line, a cardigan that predated the pub's current carpet. He sat alone with a half-pint of sherry—he'd refilled it twice from a bottle the barman kept under the counter—and he had the particular stillness of someone waiting to be asked something.

Caspian asked him something. "Evening. Is the fishing good here?"

It was the wrong question, or possibly exactly the right one, because the old man laughed like a door hinge and said, "Fishing. Aye. That's one word for what goes on out there." He nodded toward the window, toward the darkening sea beyond the escarpment. "Name's Trelawney. Old Trelawney, if you're being formal, which you look like the type."

"Caspian."

"That's not a Cornish name."

"No," Caspian agreed. "It isn't."

Old Trelawney studied him with eyes the pale grey of a winter sky deciding whether to be cruel. Then he looked past Caspian's shoulder, to the corner table, to the woman bent over her instruments with a pencil behind her ear and a frown of pure concentration. Something moved across his face—not recognition exactly. More like the shadow of a shape he remembered.

"Fairweather," he said quietly. Not a question either.

Caspian turned, slowly, and found Ulyana already watching them. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard; pubs carried sound the way caves carried water. She raised an eyebrow at him—an expression that meant, in their brief shared vocabulary, *what have you started?*

"You knew my mother?" she called across the room.

Trelawney didn't answer right away. He picked up his sherry, considered it, drank half of what remained. Then he said, "Bring your glass over here, girl, and your friend can bring mine, and I'll tell you a story that's worth more than the drink."

*Worth more than the drink.* Caspian filed the phrase away. Everything in this village was priced in something other than money. He was beginning to suspect that was the whole point of the place.

* * *

They gathered at the old man's table by the fire. Ulyana brought her pint and her crisps and her instruments, because she was constitutionally incapable of leaving instruments anywhere, and Trelawney eyed the barometer with deep suspicion, as though it might be a device for stealing souls or, worse, predicting weather accurately.

"Merlin Fairweather's girl," he said.

"Ulyana."

"Aye, that was the name. Strange name for a strange woman." He said it without malice, the way you'd describe a storm: *unusual, but not personal.* "She used to sit where you're sitting. Right there. With her books and her brass things." He tapped the barometer. "Always measuring. Never believing."

"Believing what?"

"What the coast tried to tell her." He leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep lines of his face so that he looked suddenly older than the sea itself. "You'll have seen the cave."

It wasn't a question. Everyone saw the cave; it was visible from the cliff path at low water, a black slot in the base of the escarpment like a keyhole with no door. Caspian had photographed it from three angles on his second morning and measured its aperture against the tide charts, and had concluded, provisionally, that it flooded completely at anything above mid-tide and was therefore accessible perhaps ninety minutes a month, during the lowest springs. He had also concluded that the villagers never went near it, which was the most interesting datum of all. Fear was expensive. People didn't maintain fear for free.

"The sea opens once a generation," Trelawney said. "Properly opens. Draws back further than any tide table says it should, further than God intended, and lays bare the mouth of the cave like a wound. My grandfather saw it in 1911. My father saw it in '46. I saw it myself in—" he paused, counting backward through decades the way other men counted money, "—'79. And I'm the last one left who did."

Ulyana's pencil had stopped moving. Caspian noticed that she wasn't writing anymore; she was listening with her whole body, the way she listened to thunder. He made a mental note of that too, and then made a secondary note about how many notes he was making about her lately, and decided not to examine it.

"Nineteen seventy-nine," she said carefully. "What happened in '79?"

Trelawney's gaze slid sideways, toward the fire, and stayed there. "A ship came in," he said. "Or rather, a ship came *out*. The Meridian Star. Brig out of Falmouth, gone thirty years by then, given up as lost with all hands somewhere in the Atlantic. And there she was, sitting inside the cave like she'd sailed in for shelter and forgotten to leave. Dry as a sermon. You could walk her decks." His voice had changed—flatter now, careful, a man walking on ice he knew was thin. "And the sea held the tide. Held it for hours. Long enough."

"Long enough for what?" Caspian asked.

The old man looked at him then, and Caspian understood that he had just asked the wrong question in front of the right person, and that Trelawney was deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

"For the taking," Trelawney said. "That's what the cave does. It doesn't open for nothing. Nothing on this coast is free—you know that if you've ever bought a pasty. The sea charges admission. Three currencies it accepts." He held up fingers, gnarled as driftwood. "A truth. A price. Or a life. One of the three, no substitutions, no refunds. Them that went into the cave in '79—" he paused again, and this pause had weight in it, actual mass, "—paid. Every one of them. Some in gold. Some in words they couldn't take back. And some—" he drained his sherry and set the glass down with a click like a period at the end of a sentence, "—some are still down there, if you take my meaning, and I expect you do."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the pub, the cribbage players argued about the count of a hand with the low-grade fury of long marriage. Outside, the wind had come around to the southwest, and Caspian heard it testing the windows the way a burglar tests a lock.

"That's a lovely story," Ulyana said, and Caspian heard the exact register of her voice—the warm one, the one that meant she'd decided the story was weather. "But tides don't hold. It's hydrodynamics. There are places in the world—Mont Saint-Michel, the Bay of Fundy—where the tidal range creates optical illusions, standing waves, seiches in the cave system that could—"

"Girl." Trelawney cut her off gently, the way you'd stop a child from touching a stove. "I've watched you since you walked in. You measure everything. Your mother was the same. Measured the wind, measured the waves, wrote it all down in them journals of hers, page after page after page." He shook his head slowly. "And she died not knowing the one thing worth knowing, which is that some doors don't care what's written about them. They care what you're carrying when you knock."

Ulyana's jaw tightened. Caspian saw it—the flicker of grief arriving late and sideways, dressed up as irritation. He had learned to read her in six days the way you learn to read a coastline: by what it shows you, and by what it obviously hides.

"And what am I carrying?" she asked.

"That," said Trelawney, "is between you and the water."

* * *

Later, when the old man had gone home up the hill with a lantern and the barman had begun stacking chairs, Caspian stood at the window with the last of his pint and watched Ulyana across the emptying room. She had spread her tide tables out again and was working through them with the pencil, muttering, cross-referencing, entirely in her element. He recognized the posture. It was the same posture she'd had in Oklahoma, riding shotgun while a wall cloud turned green-black over the wheat, calling out wind speeds with something close to joy. She met the world with instruments. She met grief with instruments. She met legends with instruments.

He took out his notebook—black, unlined, the kind that made people assume he was either a poet or a spy, and he encouraged both assumptions—and wrote:

*Cave legend. Sea opens once/generation. Last opening 1979 (corroborates journal fragment, p. 34). Next opening due. Locals treat as literal. Subject treats as meteorological anomaly. Either way: she will go.*

He underlined the last sentence twice.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. It didn't matter whether the legend was true. What mattered was that Ulyana Fairweather would hear *the sea opens once a generation* and think *once a generation means soon, and soon means measurable,* and she would start running numbers instead of running away, and by the time she realized she was running toward something she'd already be committed. The Syndicate wanted the cave opened and its contents assessed. Vespera Meridian wanted her family's lost fortune. But Caspian was beginning to understand that the real currency in Porthcarrow wasn't gold at all. It was attention. Whoever watched most closely—whoever read the text correctly—won.

And the text, he thought, looking at Ulyana bent over her tables, reading the sea as a physics problem while an old fisherman read it as a creditor, could be read two ways. Two readers, one legend, opposite conclusions. She thought the cave was weather. He knew it was leverage.

The trouble—the small, growing, inconvenient trouble—was that he was starting to hope she was right.

His phone buzzed against his ribs. He checked it in the shadow of the window frame. Encrypted message, Syndicate prefix, no salutation, because sentiment was for people who weren't owed money:

*Confirm spring tide window. Founder's records indicate asset recovery priority. Do not disclose findings until assessment complete. —V.M.*

Caspian looked at the message for a long moment. Then he looked at Ulyana, who had gone still over her tide tables in that particular way he'd learned to recognize—pencil frozen, eyes wide, the look of a woman whose instruments had just told her something impossible and wonderful.

"Three days," she said softly, to herself, to the tables, to the sea beyond the glass. "There's a spring tide in three days that shouldn't exist. The low's off the chart. Off every chart." She looked up at him, and her face was lit from inside, alight with the pure animal joy of a storm chaser who has just seen the sky do something new. "Caspian. The sea's going to open."

Across the room, the fire settled. Outside, the wind rose a note, patient as a creditor.

He put the phone away without answering it, and smiled, and said, "Then we'd better decide what we're carrying."
