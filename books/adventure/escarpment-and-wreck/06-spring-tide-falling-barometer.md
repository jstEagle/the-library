## 6. Spring Tide, Falling Barometer

The barometer had been falling for two days, which was normal. What wasn't normal was the way it fell—slowly, steadily, like a held note, like the atmosphere itself had leaned in to whisper something.

Ulyana Fairweather noticed it at four in the morning, sitting on the cottage kitchen counter with her boots off and a mug of tea going cold beside her, because she'd woken up with the specific itch behind her sternum that meant *something is coming* and she'd learned decades ago not to argue with the itch.

She'd strung her instruments through the derelict cottage like Christmas lights nobody sane would want. The anemometer was zip-tied to a chimney that had no business holding it. The rain gauge sat in what might once have been a herb garden and was now mostly nettle ambition. And on the kitchen table, surrounded by her mother's redacted storm journals, her laptop ran tide models she'd written herself—the kind of models that got you politely uninvited from hydrology conferences because they kept being right when the official ones were wrong.

At 4:17 a.m., the model finished its run.

Ulyana looked at it for a long time. Then she refreshed it, as if the numbers might be embarrassed and fix themselves.

They didn't.

"Okay," she said out loud, to the empty kitchen, to the nettles, to the sea muttering below the escarpment. "That's not possible."

The spring tide due in three days was already forecast to be a big one—the almanac promised a low of 0.4 meters, which was respectable, the sort of tide that exposed rocks you normally only met by falling onto them. But her model, fed with the barometric pressure drop, the swell direction, the seiche resonance she'd measured off the cliff face with a borrowed accelerometer, and one variable she couldn't fully justify except that her gut insisted, predicted a low of **negative 2.1 meters**.

Negative two point one. A tide more than two meters below anything the tables said the sea could do here. An impossible low. A tide that would pull the water back from the base of the escarpment like a curtain being drawn—and hold it there, if her timing math was right, for nearly forty minutes.

Forty minutes of seabed where there should be ocean.

Her eyes went, without her permission, to the cliff line visible through the crooked window. Somewhere down there, behind rockfall and sixty years of kelp, the legends said there was a cave mouth that opened once a generation. The old fisherman Tam Berriman had told them about it over pints Caspian had bought with Syndicate money, his voice dropping the way men's voices do when they're about to say something their grandmothers would approve of: *the sea opens, and it charges admission. A truth, or a price, or a life.*

Caspian had written it down in his neat little notebook, the way he wrote everything down, and Ulyana had caught herself thinking: *leverage.* He probably thought she hadn't noticed how he filed things away, this charming man who catalogued the world like an auditor with excellent manners.

She'd thought: *weather.*

Because that was the thing about Ulyana Fairweather—she read everything as weather. A legend was a forecast handed down orally across generations, degraded but data-rich. A curse was a hazard warning. A cave that opened once a generation was a system with a period, and systems with periods could be predicted, and things that could be predicted could be chased.

And now her instruments were telling her the chase was on.

She poured the cold tea down the sink, watched the last of it spiral away, and felt the familiar cocktail settle over her: dread and delight in equal parts, shaken, not stirred, served in a glass she couldn't put down.

Three days.

* * *

By nine she'd done everything a responsible person would do, which is to say she'd checked her model eleven times, argued with it twice, lost both arguments, and eaten toast standing up over the sink like a woman with no future worth sitting down for.

The problem with an impossible tide was that it came with impossible questions attached. Tides didn't just decide to be two meters lower than physics allowed. Something had to move the water. A pressure anomaly that deep would mean a storm system of genuinely apocalyptic proportions parked offshore—and her satellite feeds showed nothing but a fat, lazy high-pressure ridge lounging over the Atlantic like a cat in a sunbeam.

Unless the water wasn't going anywhere. Unless it was being *displaced*—pushed aside, held back, by something local. Some feature of the coast itself. Some hollow in the rock breathing in.

Ulyana stood at the window with her arms crossed and let that thought walk all the way in without knocking.

A cave that inhales.

"That's fine," she told the kettle. "That's completely fine and normal."

The kettle, having no opinion, began to boil.

It was while she was waiting for it—because tea was the load-bearing wall of her entire coping architecture—that her phone rang.

She knew before she looked. There's a particular quality to a call you've been dreading, a weight to the buzz against the table, and this one had the unmistakable heft of Meridian Syndicate money calling in its interest. She picked it up anyway, because the alternative was watching it ring forever, and because some part of her—the part that had signed their contracts, the part still carrying their debt like ballast—always answered.

"Fairweather."

"Ulyana!" The voice was warm, silken, and belonged to Dez Okafor, the Syndicate's operations man, a person who had never once in his life delivered good news without wrapping it in velvet first. "How's Cornwall treating you? Getting any real weather, or is it all just picturesque?"

"Some of each." She carried the phone to the window. Below, the village of Porthallow clung to the cliff shelf like a barnacle colony, chimneys smoking, gulls conducting their eternal grievances. "What do you want, Dez?"

"Straight to business. I respect that. It's why we like you." A pause, the sound of him settling somewhere expensive. "We've been reviewing your expense reports."

"My expense reports are a work of art."

"They're a crime scene," Dez agreed pleasantly. "But that's not why I'm calling. I'm calling because we've heard something interesting about your little corner of the coast. About a cave."

The kettle clicked off. In the sudden quiet, Ulyana could hear her own pulse, steady and slow, the way it went when the adrenaline arrived and decided to be professional about it.

"A cave," she repeated.

"The locals have stories. Old ones. Something about the sea opening up, once a generation." Velvet, everywhere velvet, and underneath it the sound of a trap being assembled with great care. "And our research department—which, between us, is one very obsessive archivist and a subscription to a folklore database—turned up a mention. Nineteen seventy-nine. A wreck. Cargo. And a Fairweather name attached to the salvage claim."

The kitchen seemed to get colder by several degrees, though the thermometer on the wall disagreed.

"I grew up in America, Dez," she said carefully. "I don't know anything about—"

"Of course you don't." Warm as bathwater. "Which is exactly why we're so glad Caspian is there with you. He's thorough. He notices things." Another pause, longer this time, and when Dez spoke again the velvet had thinned enough to show the wire beneath. "Here's the thing, Ulyana. The founder's family has been looking for that wreck for forty years. It matters to them. Personally. Sentimentally. And if it happens to be inside a cave that opens once a generation—well. That would be a remarkable coincidence, wouldn't it? Almost like weather."

Her stomach dropped the way it did on a chase when the storm made an unexpected turn—not panic, exactly, but the recalibration of every plan at once.

"You think I'm chasing a cave," she said.

"I think you're chasing *something*, because you're always chasing something, it's the only mode you come in." Dez laughed softly. "And I think whatever you find in three days belongs to us. Contractually speaking. All opportunities arising from Syndicate-funded expeditions revert to Syndicate ownership. You signed. Page four. Initials in the margin—you have such distinctive initials, like a little bird drawn by a child."

UF. She remembered signing it. She remembered needing the money more than she needed to read page four.

"And if I find nothing?" she asked. "If the tide comes in like a normal tide and goes out like a normal tide?"

"Then you find nothing, and we're all disappointed together, and your debt stands." A sigh, theatrical, regretful. "But Ulyana—our archivist found tide records too. Local anomalies. Nineteen forty-six. Nineteen twelve. Every forty-odd years, the sea does something strange near your escarpment, and the years line up with the stories, and the stories line up with a wreck." His voice dropped to something almost gentle. "You're a scientist. You've seen the pattern. Don't insult us both by pretending you haven't."

She looked at her laptop, at the impossible number glowing on the screen—**−2.1 m**—and understood, with the clarity that always arrived a beat too late, that she'd been beaten to her own discovery. They hadn't followed her to Cornwall. They'd *sent* her, the way you send a bloodhound, and now the bloodhound had treed the quarry and the hunters were walking up the lane.

The trap wasn't snapping shut. It was closing gently, the way weather closes in—barometer falling, light going strange, birds going quiet—so gradually that by the time you noticed, you were already inside it.

"When do you need an answer?" she asked.

"Oh, you misunderstand." Cheerful again, the wire hidden. "There's no answer required. We're simply informing you. As partners." A beat. "Vespera may come see the coast herself. She's been talking about it. She loves the sea, our Vespera. Practically grew up on it—or wishes she had, which amounts to the same thing in her mind."

The line went dead in the way of people who consider goodbye a formality for other people.

Ulyana set the phone face-down on the table, next to her mother's journals, and stood very still.

Outside, the gulls wheeled. The high-pressure ridge sat fat and smug on the satellite image. Everything looked calm, and everything was lying.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing fieldwork, because fieldwork was the only prayer she knew how to say properly.

Down the cliff path, boots slipping on chalk mud, wind pulling at her jacket with the familiarity of an old dog that hadn't learned manners. She planted a temporary tide gauge on a ledge above the foreshore—a battered pressure transducer in a housing that had survived three hailstorms and one divorce—and rigged a second barometer under an overhang where the readings would be clean. Then she sat on a rock with her notebook and did what she actually came for: she looked at the water.

The sea at the base of the escarpment was behaving. Swell out of the west-southwest, a meter maybe, tidy lines marching in and folding over the ledges. But she'd watched water long enough to catch the lies underneath the behavior. Twice in an hour, the surface out beyond the kelp line shivered—a long, low ripple moving *against* the swell, like something underneath had turned over in its sleep.

Seiche. Standing wave. The whole bay was a basin, and basins slosh at their own frequency, and hers was sloshing at a frequency that matched nothing in the literature.

She wrote it down. Her handwriting had gone small and careful, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to admit it.

*Something under the escarpment is breathing,* she wrote. *In on a period of roughly 14 hours. Out—*

She stopped. Looked at the water. Looked at the cliff, at the jumbled fall of rock at its base where the old path used to run before the sea ate it, at a section of the cliff face where the strata bent inward instead of down, a fold in the limestone like a swallowed word.

*Out in three days.*

Her phone buzzed. Not Dez this time—a text from Caspian, punctual as a train:

*Caspian: Found more of your mother's papers behind the wardrobe panel in the back room. You'll want to see this. Also I bought eggs. Also the pub landlady says the tide Thursday will be "wrong" and we should stay home. I told her you never stay home. She gave me a free scone out of what I can only assume is pity.*

Despite everything—the Syndicate's velvet wire around her ankle, the impossible number on her screen, the sea breathing under her feet—Ulyana laughed. Just once, short, surprised out of her.

*Tell her thank you,* she typed. *And tell her she's right. I never do.*

She pocketed the phone and stayed on the rock a while longer, watching the water lie to her beautifully, and let herself feel the whole shape of it at last: the debt, the cave, the wreck somebody else had claimed before she even knew it existed, the half-brother she didn't know she had sorting through her mother's secrets behind a wardrobe.

They wanted what was inside. They'd known before she did. They'd paid for her ticket, her instruments, her room and board, and page four said everything she touched belonged to them.

But page four didn't say anything about tides that broke the rules. Page four didn't know her model existed. And page four, whatever else it owned, did not own the forty minutes on Thursday morning when the sea would pull back its curtain and show them all what it had been keeping.

Ulyana closed her notebook, stood, and brushed the chalk dust off her jeans.

Three days. Then the water would go out farther than water should go, and she intended to be standing on the seabed when it did—with or without the Syndicate's blessing, and preferably before they finished packing their boats.

Behind her, far offshore, the surface shivered again. Against the swell. Like something exhaling.

Like something counting down.
