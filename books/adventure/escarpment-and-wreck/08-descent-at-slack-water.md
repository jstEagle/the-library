## 8. Descent at Slack Water

The tide went out like a rumor leaving a room.

Ulyana stood at the cliff's edge at four in the morning with her anemometer ticking in her pocket and watched the Atlantic perform the single most unnatural thing she had ever asked it to do: it withdrew. Not the polite two-hundred-meter retreat of a normal spring tide, but something geological—a peeling back of the sea itself, exposing ledges and weed-slick rock and, far below, a black archway in the escarpment's base that had no business existing above water.

"There it is," Caspian said beside her. He had his hands in his coat pockets and his voice pitched low, as though the sea might overhear them and change its mind.

"Three days early," Ulyana said. "My model said Thursday."

"Your model said Thursday. The sea said now." He shrugged. "I've found that's usually how negotiations go."

She shot him a look. Three weeks ago she'd have called that smile smug. Now—now she wasn't sure what she'd call it, which was its own kind of problem. The man had been sent by the Meridian Syndicate to collect on her debts, and instead he'd spent three days helping her calibrate barometers and arguing about kettle etiquette in the derelict Fairweather cottage, and somewhere in there he'd stopped being collateral and started being company, and Ulyana did not trust things that happened without her noticing.

"Ropes," she said, because thinking was dangerous before dawn.

* * *

They rigged the descent from the old coastguard path, where the escarpment leaned out over the exposed seabed like a drunk leaning over a fence. Ulyana worked the anchors with hands that remembered Oklahoma grain silos and Kansas water towers and every other vertical thing she'd ever cheated. Caspian coiled rope with a precision that suggested he'd been taught by someone who charged by the knot.

"You've done this before," she said.

"I've done many things before."

"That's not an answer."

"It's not, is it." He tested the anchor, nodded. "Climbing gym in Geneva. My employer believed physical confidence inspired client confidence. I believe my employer mostly enjoyed watching people sweat."

The Syndicate. Right. She let that word sit between them for a moment, cold as the offshore air. They were going down into a hole in the world together, and one of them was still technically employed by the people who owned the other one's future. Ulyana turned back to the cliff face so he couldn't see her do the arithmetic.

Below them, the cave mouth waited. It was larger than the fisherman's description had promised—old Tam Berriman had held his hands apart in the pub like a man describing a trout—and it breathed. That was the only word for it. A slow exhalation of cold, mineral-smelling air rolled out of the dark and up the cliff face, carrying a sound underneath it, almost too low to hear. Like a building settling. Like a ship groaning at its moorings.

"Tam says it takes a truth, a price, or a life," Caspian said.

"Tam also says the pub cat is his business partner, so I adjust his testimony accordingly."

"And yet you checked the tide tables twice last night."

"Because I'm a professional, not because I believe in admission-charging geology." She clipped her harness. "It's a sea cave. Sea caves have acoustics. Acoustics have moods. That's not magic, that's physics with opinions."

"Physics with opinions," Caspian repeated, and she could hear him writing it down somewhere behind his eyes, in whatever ledger he kept. "You know, most people would be terrified right now."

"Most people haven't spent eleven years driving toward the worst weather on the continent." She stepped to the lip. Below, the exposed seabed gleamed under a sky just beginning to consider the color blue, all bladderwrack and tide pools and the skeletons of boats that had lost arguments. "Fear's just data you haven't organized yet."

"And what does your data say about him?" Caspian asked quietly.

She looked at him. He met it, unflinching, pleasant, absolutely opaque—the man who smiled like collateral, except lately the smile kept arriving a half-second before she expected it, which meant either sincerity or very good practice.

"My data," she said, "says we're roped together, so if you're lying about anything, I'll find out at approximately forty meters."

"Efficient," he said. "I respect that."

* * *

The descent took ninety minutes and cost Ulyana the skin off both palms and most of her certainty.

The rock was worse than it looked from above—which was saying something, because from above it looked like a divorce. Ledges crumbled at a touch; the kelp was treacherous as wet ribbon; twice the whole escarpment seemed to shudder, though she knew it was only the sea working at foundations it had been working at since before anyone had written down the word *Fairweather*. At the halfway mark they passed a gull colony that objected to their presence with the full-throated fury of shareholders at a bad meeting, and Ulyana laughed—actually laughed, hanging off a cliff at five in the morning—and heard Caspian laugh too, startled out of him, and hated how much she liked the sound.

Forty meters up from the bottom, her boot found a ledge that wasn't.

The world tilted. For one long, glittering second she was weightless, and then the rope caught, and she swung against the rock face with her heart doing 180 through her chest and her palms screaming, and Caspian's voice came down from above, tight and stripped of all its polish:

"Ulyana. Talk to me."

"I'm fine." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Ledge lied to me. I'm reorganizing my data."

"Hang on. I've got you." And he did—she could feel it through the line, the steady take-up of slack, someone else's strength holding her whole weight over open air, and there was a moment, swinging there against the ancient stone with the sea breathing below, when she thought: *this is what trust feels like, and I am doing it with a debt collector.*

Then her boots found rock again, and the moment passed, but it didn't entirely pass. Some moments leave residue.

They reached the seabed as the light came up gold and merciless, and the cave mouth stood before them, twenty feet of black arch fringed with dead barnacles and the pale scars of creatures that had never needed sunlight. The exhalation was stronger here. Cold air poured out in slow rhythm, and beneath it, unmistakable now, that deep settling sound.

"Listen," Caspian said.

"I hear it."

"What is it?"

Ulyana cocked her head. Eleven years of listening to skies had tuned her ear to systems—to pressure and rotation and the particular grammar of things about to change. This sound was structural. Rhythmic. Water, moving somewhere inside the rock, filling and emptying some hidden chamber like lungs.

"The cave breathes with the tide," she said slowly. "There's a chamber behind us that floods and drains. Which means—" She stopped. Her skin prickled.

"Means?"

"Means the sea isn't done with this place. Slack water isn't peace. It's an inhale." She pulled her headlamp from her pack and clicked it on. "We have maybe four hours before the exhale. Whatever's in there, we see it fast and we get out faster."

Caspian had gone quiet in a way she was learning to read. Not relaxed-quiet. Calculating-quiet.

"You knew the window was short," she said. "That's why the Syndicate wanted me now. That's why Vespera's people called twice this week." She watched his face. "What aren't you telling me, Caspian?"

He turned to look at her, headlamp light catching the planes of his face, and for a moment something moved behind the pleasantness—something that looked almost like regret, or its well-dressed cousin.

"I'm telling you everything that keeps us alive today," he said. "The rest is bookkeeping."

"Bookkeeping." She laughed, short and sharp. "Right. Of course. Everything's bookkeeping with you people."

"With me," he said, and there was an odd emphasis on the *me*, as though he were drawing a border on a map she couldn't see. "Not always with me."

She wanted to push. Every instinct she had—the same instinct that made her drive toward rotating black skies while sensible people drove away—wanted to grab this man by the lapels and shake the truth out of him like coins from a coat pocket. But the cave was breathing behind her, and the tide tables didn't care about her suspicions, and four hours was four hours.

"After you," she said, gesturing at the dark with a courtesy so pointed it could have drawn blood.

"Ladies first."

"I'm armed with a headlamp and unresolved anger."

"Then definitely after you."

* * *

Inside, the cave was a cathedral that had been built by accident and then abandoned by God.

Her headlamp beam climbed walls ribbed like whalebone, forty feet high, streaked with mineral green and the white crust of centuries of salt. The floor sloped gently downward, scattered with boulders and driftwood and things she chose not to examine closely. The breathing sound was everywhere now, coming through the rock itself, and beneath it—she stopped walking.

Beneath it, wood.

"Caspian."

He came up beside her. His lamp joined hers, and the twin beams reached down the slope together and found what the legends had been charging admission for all these generations.

A ship.

Or the spine of one—a brig, by the lines of her, driven half into a side chamber of the cave and broken along her length, masts snapped, hull opened like a walnut. Timbers black with age but stubbornly, impossibly whole, preserved in the cold and the salt and the dark. Rigging hung in fossilized ropes. And set into the sternpost, catching their light, letters carved deep and painted once in gold:

**MERIDIAN STAR**

Ulyana felt the name go through her like current through wire. *Meridian.* The Syndicate's name. The family name. The name on every threatening letter and generous offer of the last three years.

"That's not possible," Caspian said. His voice had changed completely—all the charm gone out of it, leaving something younger and much more frightened underneath. "That ship was lost in 'seventy-nine. Off the Azores. It's in every registry. My—" He stopped himself. Swallowed. "*They* have records. Official ones."

"Official records are just stories with better funding," Ulyana said, but she barely heard herself, because her lamp had swept across the deck and found the strongboxes—three of them, iron-banded, lashed to the frame with rope gone hard as rebar. And beside them, half-tumbled into the shadow of the collapsed mast, something small and white and curved.

She walked toward it. Her legs felt borrowed.

It was a journal. Leather-bound, swollen with salt, a brass clasp green with age. And embossed on the cover, in lettering she knew better than her own signature—in lettering she'd seen on the flyleaf of every storm journal her mother had ever kept, the ones missing pages, the ones with redactions—

*E. Fairweather.*

The cave exhaled around them, long and slow, and somewhere high above, faint as memory, the sea began to turn.

"Ulyana," Caspian said quietly. "The water."

She raised her lamp toward the cave mouth. Far off across the exposed seabed, at the very edge of the light, the Atlantic had begun, very gently, to come home.
