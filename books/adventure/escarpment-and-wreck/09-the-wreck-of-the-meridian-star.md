## 9. The Wreck of the Meridian Star

**Ulyana**

The cave breathed.

That was the only word for it. They'd come down the last forty feet of rope into a cathedral of black stone, and the whole space inhaled around them—a slow, tidal sigh that lifted the salt smell off the water pooled at their boots and pushed it up into the dark overhead, where their headlamps couldn't reach.

"Tell me you felt that," Caspian said behind her. His voice had gone carefully casual, which was how she knew he was rattled.

"The cave has lungs," Ulyana said. "Everything does. Even debt."

"You're not going to let that go, are you."

"Not while it's still funny."

She swept her light across the floor of the chamber and stopped breathing for a different reason entirely.

The tide had gone out further than any tide had a right to go—her instruments had promised this, an impossible low, a once-a-generation retreat—and what it left behind was not rock. It was timber. A keel, barnacled and green with three centuries, running the length of the chamber like the spine of something enormous that had lain down here to die and never gotten back up.

"Oh," Ulyana said. It wasn't a word so much as a small collapse.

"That's a ship," Caspian said.

"That's *my* ship."

"It's nobody's ship. That's rather the point of shipwrecks."

"Caspian." She turned to look at him, and her headlamp caught his face, and she saw that he wasn't being contrary. He was awed too. He just wore awe the way he wore everything—buttoned up, pressed flat, like a man determined not to let the weather show. "The Fairweather journals. My mother's pages. She wrote about a brig. I thought it was a story."

"I know," he said quietly. "I read them too."

There was something odd in the way he said it—*I read them too*—a little too smooth, a little too fast, but the wreck was right there, filling her vision, and suspicion would have to wait its turn in line.

They walked toward the keel together, boots splashing through ankle-deep pools, and the cave breathed again, and somewhere far above them the sea waited offshore like a creditor who knew exactly when the payment was due.

* * *

**Caspian**

Caspian Meridian—though no one in this cave knew him by that name, and God willing no one ever would—had been trained his whole life to inventory things.

His father had taught him. *A room is a ledger, boy. Walk in, price everything, walk out.* He'd done it in boardrooms and auction houses and, once memorably, in a monastery in Portugal where the abbot had caught him appraising a reliquary and laughed until he cried. He did it automatically now, walking the length of the dead brig: hull timbers, oak, wormed but sound; ribs splayed upward like a whale's; a mast fallen diagonally across the chamber, crusted white; and there, half-buried against the starboard side—

Strongboxes. Four of them. Iron, banded, sealed with wax that had gone the color of old teeth.

He priced them out of habit. Then he made himself stop, because the number kept climbing past the point where numbers meant anything, and because Ulyana had gone very still beside the nearest box, her light fixed on the seal.

"That's a stamp," she said. "On the wax. Look."

He looked. An M, entwined with a five-pointed star. The Meridian mark. His family's mark, pressed into wax by hands two hundred years dead, sitting in a sea cave off the Cornish coast on a wreck that should have been lost to every record.

His brief from Vespera had been one sentence: *Recover the asset or confirm its loss.* He had imagined, if he imagined anything, salvage. Divers. A legal apparatus. Not this—not standing in the actual dark with the actual thing, feeling it hum under his skin like a live wire.

"Ulyana," he said, and heard his own voice do the careful-casual thing again. "I think we should document before we touch anything."

"Since when do collectors document?"

"Since always. Documentation is how collectors get paid." He crouched by the strongbox, photographing the seal with the methodical patience his father had beaten into him, and while the shutter clicked he let himself think the thought he'd been refusing since the pub, since the fisherman's legend, since the moment he'd first seen her mother's handwriting in those journals and recognized, absurdly, the same stubborn leftward slant that lived in his own signature.

*She's your sister.*

Not legally. Not in any way that paper acknowledged. But blood didn't care about paper. Blood had brought him here—Vespera's orders were merely the excuse he'd told himself—and blood was going to make what came next very, very complicated.

Because if those boxes held what he suspected they held, then his family hadn't merely profited from some ancient misfortune. His family had *caused* it. And the woman crouched beside him, laughing shakily at the cave's breathing, was owed everything his name had spent two centuries enjoying.

"Three more," Ulyana said, moving down the line. "Sealed. All of them. You know what sealed means?"

"Nobody got here first."

"Nobody got here *ever*." She grinned at him, wild-eyed in the lamplight, and for one unguarded second she looked about nineteen years old, the way she must have looked chasing her first storm across some Oklahoma wheat field. "Two hundred years and we're the first people to see these. Do you understand how—"

Her light moved past the last strongbox and stopped.

"—oh," she said, in a completely different voice.

* * *

**Ulyana**

The skeleton sat where the man had died, which appeared to be the captain's cabin, or what remained of it—a low space beneath the quarterdeck where the timbers had folded inward like cupped hands.

He was seated. That was the worst part. Not sprawled, not scattered by currents, but arranged—propped against the fallen mast with his back straight, as though someone had set him there deliberately, or as though he had chosen, in his last hour, to die sitting up like a man keeping watch.

His clothes had gone to lace. His boots had lasted better. And on the bones of his right hand, gleaming dully under two centuries of silt, was a ring.

Ulyana knelt. She didn't remember deciding to kneel.

"Don't," Caspian said sharply.

"I'm not touching. I'm looking. Looking is free." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears—flat, careful, the voice she used reading barometers when the numbers were going bad. "It's a signet. Gold. There's a crest."

She knew the crest. That was the thing. She'd never seen it before in her life and she knew it instantly, the way you know your own reflection in a dark window—the M and the star, the same mark on the strongbox seals, the same mark that had been stamped on the letterhead of every threatening, courteous, patient letter the Meridian Syndicate had ever sent her.

The letters that wanted repayment. The letters that offered opportunities. The letters signed, all of them, in the flowing antique hand of the founder himself, Elias Meridian, whose portrait hung in the Syndicate's offices above a motto about *weathering every storm together.*

"Elias Meridian drowned in 1979," she said. "That's the story. That's the founding tragedy. He went down with his ship, the Meridian Star, trying to save his crew, and the Syndicate was built on his memory."

"Yes," Caspian said. Very evenly.

"This isn't 1979." She gestured at the wreck—at the worms in the wood, the depth of the silt, the sheer geological patience of the place. "This is older. Much older. This ship has been here since—" She did the math on the marine growth and didn't like the answer. "Eighteenth century. Maybe earlier."

"So the founder didn't die here."

"No." She stood up. Her knees cracked. Somewhere in the walls of the cave, water dripped with the regularity of a metronome. "So the founder never died at all. Or—" and here the fury arrived, not all at once but in stages, like a front rolling in, "—or this is a different Meridian. Another one. A whole dynasty of men wearing that ring, and every single one of them knew this ship was down here."

Caspian was quiet for long enough that she turned to look at him.

He was staring at the skeleton with an expression she couldn't read—something between recognition and nausea—and when he spoke, his voice had lost all its polish. "Ulyana. The manifest. Your mother's journal mentioned a cargo manifest. If there was a manifest, there's a record of what this ship carried and who owned it. We need to find the cabin. The papers might have survived in wax cloth, in a chest—"

"*Who owned it*, Caspian? We're looking at him." She pointed at the ring. "We're kneeling next to him. He's been down here holding the answer on his finger for two hundred years while his descendants sent me letters about my debts."

"Ulyana—"

"And my mother knew." The fury crested, clean and cold. "Her journals. The missing pages, the redacted ones—I thought it was grief, I thought she'd torn them out because she couldn't bear to write about Dad, but she wasn't hiding grief. She was hiding *this*. She found it, or she figured it out, and someone made her stop writing about it, and then she died alone in that cottage on the cliff while the Syndicate sent me polite letters asking after my health."

The cave breathed. In the silence between breaths, she heard Caspian say, very softly:

"There. Behind the captain's chair. A chest."

* * *

**Caspian**

The chest was small, lead-lined, and locked—but the lock had rusted through sometime during the reign of some king or another, and it gave way to Caspian's knife with a crumble that felt almost apologetic.

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff as bark: paper.

He unwrapped it with the care of a man defusing something. Ulyana crowded close, her shoulder against his, her breath quick and shallow, and he was acutely aware of her warmth in the cold of the cave, of the fact that this was the closest they had ever stood, and that he was about to hand her the evidence that would burn his own house down.

The top document was a bill of lading. He read it twice to be sure.

*"Cargo of the brig Meridian Star, out of Falmouth: specie, plate, and deeds of conveyance, being the estate and inheritance of the Fairweather family, consigned to Elias Meridian for safekeeping and transfer."*

"Safekeeping," Ulyana repeated. "For transfer."

"Keep reading."

Below the lading, in a different hand—a hurried, blotted, frightened hand—was a letter, unfinished, addressed to *Mrs. Fairweather, of the escarpment.*

*"Madam—I write to confess what I have done. The inheritance was never lost at sea. I scuttled her myself, in the cave as instructed by my employer, and the crew—"*

The letter stopped. The ink was smeared, as if by a wet thumb, or a wave.

"He was the captain," Ulyana whispered. "The skeleton. He wasn't Elias Meridian. He was the captain, and Elias made him sink his own ship with his own crew aboard, and then—" She swallowed. "Then Elias sealed him in here with the evidence. Left him sitting up. As a message. Or a punishment. Or both."

Caspian said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie, and he had already lied to her once today—in the small way, the *I read them too* way—and the lies were stacking up inside him like ballast, weighing him down toward the truth whether he steered there or not.

Because he knew the rest. He'd grown up on the sanitized version: the noble founder, the tragic loss, the fortune rebuilt from nothing. But he'd also grown up hearing the servants' version, the version told after brandy, the version where old Elias had come back from the sea suspiciously solvent and never once spoken of the crew. Two hundred years of comfortable lies, and here they all were, rotting in a chest at the bottom of a hole in Cornwall.

"Ulyana," he said. "I need to tell you something."

Above them—far above, up the rope, through the chimney of stone—the cave exhaled, long and slow.

And then, instead of inhaling, it began to gurgle.

* * *

**Ulyana**

The water came back the way weather comes back: gradually, then all at once.

One moment the pools at their feet were still. The next, ripples crossed them from the direction of the cave mouth, and the drip-drip-drip in the walls became a hiss, and Ulyana's internal barometer—which had never once lied to her—screamed a single word: *turning.*

"Slack water's over," she said. "The tide's coming back. Caspian—we have maybe twenty minutes to the mouth, less if the surge funnels—"

"The documents—"

"We take the letter and the lading. The boxes stay."

"The boxes are worth—"

"They're worth nothing if we drown holding them!" She was already moving, stuffing the papers inside her jacket, waterproofed against her skin. "Come on. Move. And Caspian—whatever you were about to tell me—it keeps. Everything keeps. Except us."

They ran—or waded, then ran—back along the spine of the dead brig, past the strongboxes with their pretty seals, past the captain sitting his eternal watch with his ring full of proof. At the mouth of the captain's cabin Ulyana paused, one second, no more, and aimed her light back at the bones.

"I'm sorry," she told him. "I'll finish the letter for you."

The cave roared softly, like a man clearing his throat before speaking.

And from somewhere above—thin, distorted, impossible—came the sound of an engine. A boat engine. Out on the water, in the dark, approaching the coast where no boat had any business being.

Caspian heard it too. She saw it hit his face like a slap.

"That'll be the Syndicate," he said. "They followed us."

The water rose around their ankles, black and patient, collecting its admission fee.

"Then run faster," Ulyana said, "because I am *not* explaining this to them soaked."
