## 10. Admission

The water came for us the way debt always does: quietly at first, then all at once.

I heard it before I saw it—a change in the cave's breathing, a wet slap against stone that hadn't been there when we'd entered. We were forty minutes into the wreck of the *Meridian Star*, and I was elbow-deep in a strongbox that had surrendered its lid to Caspian's pry bar, when the sound reached us down the passage like a rumor traveling through a small town.

"Ulyana." Caspian's voice had lost its charm. What remained underneath was better—sharp, quick, honest. "The tide's turned."

"I know." I did know. That was the humiliating part. My own instruments, sitting in the truck a hundred feet above us on the escarpment, had told me slack water would last ninety minutes. I'd budgeted the sea like a line item and come up short, because I'd wanted more time with the wreck, because wanting things had always been my most expensive habit.

"How much time do we have?"

I pulled my hand out of the box. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone to leather-soft rot, were coins that caught even our dying torchlight and made something of it. Gold. Her gold. My family's inheritance, sitting in a dead man's hold for forty years while the Meridian Syndicate built an empire on the interest.

"Twenty minutes," I said. "Maybe fifteen if the swell's lying about its size."

* * *

We'd found the wreck an hour earlier, and I want to get this right, because some part of me understood even then that I would spend the rest of my life telling it wrong.

She lay in a chamber the size of a village church, keel up, masts snapped and furred with white anemones so that she looked less like a ship than a sleeping animal grown into her bed. The *Meridian Star*. My mother's journals had described her in careful, furious handwriting—the brig that was supposed to carry the Fairweather fortune to safety in '79 and instead carried it to the bottom of a cave only the sea could open. Except she hadn't carried it anywhere. She'd been scuttled. Deliberately, lovingly scuttled, by the man whose name was stamped on the manifest we'd found lashed inside a lead tube: **E. Meridian**. The founder himself. The legend's first customer, paying his admission in other people's lives.

And near the stern, where the chart table had been, a skeleton in what remained of a coat, one hand resting on the table as if he'd died mid-calculation. On the finger bones, a signet ring. A meridian line through a star.

Caspian had gone very still when he saw it. Stillness looks different on him than it does on other people—he wears it like a tailored coat, everything tucked in, nothing showing. But I'd spent three days learning his weather by then, and I knew a front when I saw one.

"That's him," he said quietly. "That's the ring."

"Yes."

"He scuttled his own ship." He said it like a man testing whether ice would hold. "He killed his crew and stole—" He stopped. Recalculated. I watched him decide not to finish the sentence, and I filed that away too, in the drawer where I kept everything I wasn't saying to him.

"Stole what?" I asked, sweet as tide.

"The cargo," he said.

The lie sat between us, small and warm as a coal. Because we both knew what the Fairweather manifests said, what my mother's redacted pages had screamed in their careful way: half that cargo was ours. And Caspian Meridian—or whatever name the Syndicate had raised him under—had known it since before we ever roped down into this hole together.

But the water was rising, and there is a strange mercy in emergencies: they let you postpone the conversations that matter.

* * *

The side chamber took us because we let it.

We were retreating along the passage toward the entrance shaft, Caspian hauling the recovery equipment—the Syndicate's kit, waterproof cases and a winch line and two sealed dive bags meant for exactly this cargo—and I carrying my mother's original journal in its oilskin wrap against my chest, when the sea sent its first scout. A surge came through the lower gallery, waist-high, cold enough to make my teeth sing, and shoved us both against the wall. When it withdrew, it took the light from the entrance with it. Not all of it. Just enough to tell us the arithmetic had changed.

"The chamber," Caspian shouted over the boom of water somewhere below. "There was a ledge—we passed it—"

"There's no way out of a ledge."

"There's no way out of *this*," he said, and he was right, which I was beginning to understand was a thing he frequently was.

We scrambled up into the side chamber—a pocket in the limestone, ten feet across, its floor already slick with the first tongue of the returning tide. From it we could see the entrance shaft, thirty feet away across a passage that was filling like a bathtub with opinions. The rope still hung there, swaying. Thirty feet of black, fast water between us and the sky.

I checked my watch against my gut and didn't like either answer. "The surge period's shortening. In ten minutes that passage is a river. In twenty it's a pipe."

"So we wait for the next low?" Caspian was rigging the winch line as he talked, hands moving with that infuriating competence. "High tide's six hours. Air in here—"

I did the math and watched his face do it too. The chamber's ceiling was maybe four feet above the current waterline, and the tide tables in my mother's journals—for she had charted this cave the way other people charted their grudges—said tonight's spring high would put the waterline a foot above our ledge. Six hours underwater, waiting for a low that might not open the cave again for another generation.

"We're not waiting," I said. "We're going through on the next trough. Between surges there's maybe forty seconds when the passage runs knee-deep. We move on the ebb of each wave, we shelter behind that spur of rock halfway, and we take two waves to cross."

"Two waves," Caspian repeated. "Carrying how much?"

And there it was. The question the cave had been waiting all day to ask.

Because here's the thing about the equipment: it was heavy. Two dive bags, the cases, the winch spool—eighty pounds of Syndicate money, engineered in some clean office by people who had never heard the sea clear its throat. And the gold—I'd filled a satchel, because I am not a saint, I want that on the record. My family's gold, taken back after forty years, and I had stuffed every pocket I had.

And the journal. My mother's journal, the original, salt-stained and furious and the only true copy of anything in my whole life.

I looked at the water climbing the passage and I looked at my arms, which are strong but not miraculous, and I understood that the cave was running a till, and we were at the counter, and the legend had never once been wrong about its prices.

A truth, a price, or a life.

We'd already paid truths today—his silence, my pretense. Maybe the cave counted those. But the water didn't seem interested in metaphysics. It wanted mass. It wanted us light.

* * *

"You go first," Caspian said. "You're faster. You take the journal and the satchel. I'll bring what I can."

"No." I said it before I'd decided to. "No, that's backwards and you know it. If you cross first with the gear, you'll try to come back for a second load, and the third wave will take you. I've seen water do this. It doesn't negotiate."

"And you'll leave the gold?" He almost smiled. "Ulyana Fairweather, leaving money on a table. Vespera would sooner believe the moon fell."

"Don't say her name in here."

"It's her gold too, technically—"

"It was never her gold. It was bought with drowned men." My voice cracked off the limestone and came back at me, and I hated the echo for making it sound bigger than I felt. "Forty years ago a man tied his crew to this ship and opened her seams and rowed away in the dark, and every coin down there has a fingerprint on it. I'm not leaving the gold because I'm noble. I'm leaving it because I finally read the price tag."

The next surge hit the passage and threw spray to the ceiling of the chamber, and in the white scatter of it Caspian looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere—not collector, not brother, not liar. Something older than all three.

"The equipment stays," he said quietly. "All of it. The cases, the bags, the winch. Vespera sent me to recover assets, and I'm going to tell her the sea recovered them first." He set the dive bags down on the ledge with the care of a man laying down a rifle. "That's my admission fee. Let's see yours."

And oh, he thought he was being generous. He thought he was paying double so I wouldn't have to pay at all. I could see the arithmetic behind his eyes, the same tidy ledger the Syndicate had raised him on: sacrifice the company's property, protect the girl, balance the books.

But the cave doesn't take the Syndicate's property. The cave takes what you can't replace.

I unwrapped the oilskin from my mother's journal and held it a moment, feeling the weight of it—the weight of every storm she'd ever chased alone, every page she'd cut out to hide from whoever she'd feared would read them, every night she'd sat in that crooked cottage writing *the sea opens, the sea remembers* in handwriting that got shakier each year.

"Caspian," I said. "Before we cross. There's something you should know about what we found."

"Not now—"

"The manifest. The one in the lead tube. I photographed it. Every page. And the ring." I tapped my chest, where the camera rode in its waterproof case next to my heart like a second heartbeat. "The originals belong to the sea now. The truth doesn't."

He stared at me. Somewhere below, the water boomed against the gallery wall like a landlord knocking.

"You've had those photos since the wreck," he said slowly. "You knew, and you didn't say—"

"I was waiting to see what you'd do with your face. You just did it." I allowed myself one breath of warmth, because the moment deserved it and because we might both die in the next three minutes. "Now we're even. Now we both owe the same person the same secret. That's called trust, little brother. Look it up."

His face did several things at once, none of them tidy, and I confess I enjoyed every one.

Then I knelt and laid my mother's journal on the highest dry fold of rock, weighted it with a stone from the chamber floor, and opened it to the last written page so that whatever found it next would find her words facing upward. *The sea opens,* she'd written. *Pay it honestly.*

"I'm sorry," I told her. "I'll keep the rest."

The water took the words' echo before it took anything else.

* * *

We crossed on the fourth trough.

First wave: me, journal-less, gold-less except for the single coin I'd kept in my boot—call it a souvenir, call it evidence, call it the last stubborn inch of Fairweather pride—and Caspian a body-length behind with nothing but the clothes he stood in and the secrets we shared. The passage ran thigh-deep and pulled like a crowd leaving a stadium, and I made the mid-channel spur with my fingers bleeding and my lungs full of salt spray, and I turned to haul him in.

Second wave: the long one. Forty seconds of knee-deep retreat, and we went through it bent double, hand over hand along the guide rope, the sea sucking at our ankles like it was reconsidering the deal. At the shaft, Caspian boosted me to the first knot and I climbed like the water was already telling my story without me.

We came out of the cave mouth into evening air so clean it felt stolen, and collapsed on the shingle below the escarpment, two drowned rats laughing in the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming. Above us the cliff glowed rust-gold in the last light. Below us the sea had closed its shop, smooth and smug as a banker.

"It took the equipment," Caspian said at last, staring at the water. "Vespera's going to ask me why."

"Tell her the truth."

"She'll never believe it."

"She believes the sea owes her money," I said. "Let her learn otherwise."

He laughed—really laughed, no collateral in it—and then went quiet, and I watched him watch the horizon where, far off, a light was riding at anchor that hadn't been there that morning. White, steady, patient. A vessel standing off the point like a creditor outside a door.

"They're early," Caspian said, and his voice had gone flat and professional, the coat back on. "They weren't due until Thursday."

I sat up, wringing salt out of my hair, and followed his gaze to that patient white light, and felt the old familiar dread settle over me like weather.

"Well," I said. "Then they can watch us walk up the hill with empty hands and full pockets, and wonder what the sea kept."

The light blinked once, as if it had heard.
