## 12. The Syndicate Arrives

The ships came in on a Tuesday morning, which felt rude of them. Tuesdays were for laundry and second chances, not for the arrival of three black-hulled vessels that sat on the horizon like punctuation marks at the end of a sentence you hadn't finished writing.

Ulyana saw them first from the cottage window, mug of tea halfway to her mouth, and for one merciful moment she thought they might be fishing boats. Then she counted. Fishing boats came in odd numbers, sure—but fishing boats didn't anchor in perfect formation, spaced like soldiers on parade, and fishing boats didn't have helipads.

"Tea's going cold," she said to Caspian, who was at the table with his laptop open and his face arranged into something she'd come to recognize as *I already know but I'm deciding how much to tell you.*

"How many?" he asked.

"Three."

He closed the laptop with the gentle click of a man shutting a door on a room he'd rather not be in. "Then it's not fishermen."

"No," Ulyana said. "Fishermen don't fly flags." She could see them even from here—three pennants snapping in the offshore breeze, each bearing the same device: a stylized meridian line running through a compass rose, gold on black. The Meridian Syndicate had never been shy about branding. That was the thing about dangerous optimists, she'd learned; they believed so hard in their own eventual success that they invested early in the stationery.

She drank her tea anyway. It had gone lukewarm, which matched her mood exactly.

* * *

By noon the whole village knew, because Mrs. Tregose at the post office had a telescope older than the church and opinions newer than the tide tables.

"Three of them," she announced to the pub at large, where Ulyana and Caspian had gone ostensibly for lunch and actually for intelligence. "Big ones. The kind that don't rock. You know what I mean by don't rock? Boats that are too rich to be moved by weather. My Harold used to say you can tell everything about a vessel by how she sits at anchor, and those three sit like they own the water under them."

"Do they now," said the landlord, polishing a glass that did not need it.

"And there's a launch coming ashore," Mrs. Tregose continued, with the relish of a woman who had waited her whole life for something to happen in Porthcarrow and was determined to enjoy every minute of it. "White cushions. A woman in a coat that costs more than my house. Two men carrying cases, and I'll wager my pension those cases aren't full of sandwiches."

"They're full of lawyers," said old Tam Budge from his corner by the fire, without opening his eyes. He'd been telling the cave legend for sixty years and had apparently decided to branch out into prophecy. "Cases like that are always lawyers. Or diving gear. In this weather, likely both."

Ulyana set down her pasty. "Tam. The legend. Say it again—the part about what the cave takes."

Tam opened one eye. "Truth, price, or life. Always has, always will. Why?"

"Because I think someone's about to pay all three at once," she said, "and I want to know if the cave gives change."

* * *

The launch grounded on the shingle at half past two, and Vespera Meridian stepped out of it the way other people step out of cars that have been driven to meet them.

Ulyana watched from the sea wall, arms crossed against the wind, and made herself study the woman properly, because you studied storms before they arrived and this was the same discipline wearing better shoes. Vespera was perhaps fifty, tall, silver-blond hair cut with architectural precision, and she wore a wool coat the color of wet slate that probably had its own insurance policy. Behind her came the two men with the cases, a third man who looked like he ironed his face every morning, and four figures in dive suits who moved with the loose-shouldered ease of people who spent more time underwater than their mothers would have liked.

What struck Ulyana most—not the money, not the muscle, not even the divers—was the smile. Vespera smiled at the village, at the shingle, at the gulls wheeling overhead with their usual contempt, and it was a genuinely lovely smile. Warm. Receptive. The smile of someone delighted to be here.

It was also, Ulyana realized with a small cold drop in her stomach, the exact smile Caspian used when he was assessing collateral.

"Ms. Fairweather." Vespera crossed the shingle without a single misstep—heels on stone, the audacity—and extended a hand. Her grip was cool and brief, a handshake shaped like a receipt. "Vespera Meridian. I believe we're finally meeting. Caspian has told me so much about your work."

"All good, I hope."

"He says you're the best storm reader he's ever seen." The smile widened a degree. "He also says you owe us rather a lot of money, but we needn't discuss that today. Today is about family history, isn't it? Yours and mine. It turns out our families have been entangled longer than either of us knew. I find that rather moving."

"You find it profitable," Ulyana said.

"I find," Vespera said pleasantly, "that the two are rarely in conflict." She turned a gloved hand toward the escarpment, toward the stretch of cliff below the cottage where the sea had shown its secret floor three nights ago. "My grandfather lost a ship somewhere along this coast in 1979. The *Meridian Star*. Crew of eleven, cargo manifest worth more than this county's annual budget, and no wreckage ever recovered. No bodies, either—which my grandfather always called a mercy, though he never once visited the memorial the village built. Did you know there was a memorial? Charming little thing. Moss all over it."

"We've seen it," Caspian said quietly, at Ulyana's shoulder.

"Of course you have." Vespera's eyes flicked to him—there and gone, a look Ulyana couldn't parse and didn't trust herself to try—and then back to Ulyana. "Here is my position, stated plainly, because I respect plainness: I believe my grandfather's ship went down in or near that cave. I believe the cargo is recoverable. And I believe that between your instruments, your access, and your—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel— "*familiarity* with the site, you two are the most valuable consultants currently unemployed on this coastline."

"We're not for hire," Ulyana said.

"Everyone's for hire. It's just a question of currency." Vespera gestured at the divers, who had begun unpacking the cases with the crisp choreography of people paid very well indeed. "Rebreathers, side-scan sonar, a submersible on the middle vessel. I've brought the finest salvage team in Europe to a village with one pub and a post office. Do you know why?"

"Because you're certain the cave holds your fortune," Ulyana said.

"Because I'm certain," Vespera agreed, "and certainty is the only luxury I've never learned to live without." She smiled again—that warm, terrible smile—and pulled a card from her coat pocket. Heavy stock. Embossed compass rose. It probably weighed more than Tam Budge's pension. "Dinner, tomorrow evening, aboard the *Perpetual Return*. Just conversation. No obligations. I'm told the chef worked in Lyon before he got tired of being poor."

She walked back across the shingle, heels clicking, divers trailing, lawyers following like well-dressed gulls, and Ulyana stood at the sea wall watching the launch pull away and feeling the particular dread of a storm chaser who has just realized the supercell isn't coming toward her.

It's already here. It parked offshore. It sent a card.

* * *

"She knows," Caspian said that night.

They were in the cottage kitchen, the lamp low, the wind worrying the window frames the way it always did, patient and familiar as an old dog scratching at a door. Ulyana had her mother's surviving journal open on the table, the redacted pages, the gaps where pages had been cut away entirely. She'd been staring at the same paragraph for twenty minutes.

"Knows what, exactly?" she asked. "Say it plainly. I'm tired of everyone in my life saying things sideways. You, her, the solicitor, my dead mother. Somebody in this story should try sentences."

Caspian turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table. He did that when he was choosing words, and she hated that she'd noticed, and she noticed anyway.

"The cave opens on spring tides. She has tide tables. She has money. She may have known about this site for years—decades, maybe. Her grandfather's records, whatever he kept, whatever he destroyed." He looked up. "What she doesn't know is what's inside. Whether the wreck survived. Whether anything's recoverable. That's why she wants us at dinner. We're not consultants, Ulyana. We're reconnaissance."

"And if we tell her nothing?"

"Then she sends the divers down blind, and they find it themselves, and we lose any say in what happens next." He paused. "And if we tell her too much—"

"She gets the fortune, and we get a receipt." Ulyana pushed back from the table and stood at the window. Below, in the dark, the three ships rode at anchor with lights strung along their decks like carnival rides, beautiful and wrong against the black water. "You know what my mother's journal says about the wreck? What's left of it says?"

"No."

"Nothing. Because the pages are gone. Someone cut them out—her, or the Syndicate, or time itself. But the margins survived. Little notes. Tide times. A sketch of a hull. And one line, over and over, in the margin of nearly every surviving page." Ulyana touched the paper without looking at it. "*The sea keeps honest books.*"

"That sounds like her."

"You didn't know her."

"No," Caspian said, and there was something in his voice then, some small fracture under the smooth surface, and Ulyana filed it away the way she filed all anomalies—for later, when the pressure dropped. "But I've read enough of her writing to wish I had."

Outside, far off across the water, a boat engine started up—one of the Syndicate launches, running a slow circuit along the base of the escarpment, its searchlight sweeping the cliff face in long white strokes. Reading the rock. Measuring the cave mouth from outside, maybe. Or just reminding everyone ashore exactly who was watching.

Ulyana watched the light slide across the cottage wall and thought about the legend. Truth, price, or life. Three ships. Three currencies. She didn't believe in omens—she believed in barometers—but even she had to admit the arithmetic was getting suggestive.

"Caspian," she said. "Tomorrow, at dinner. Whose side will you be sitting on?"

He was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence twice over.

"Yours," he said finally. "I keep telling you that."

"I know," Ulyana said. "It's the *why* I haven't heard yet."

The searchlight swept past the window one last time and slid away down the coast, and somewhere beneath the dark water, in a cave that charged admission, the sea kept its books and waited for someone to settle up.
