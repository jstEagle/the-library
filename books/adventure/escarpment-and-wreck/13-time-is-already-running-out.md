## 13. Time Is Already Running Out

Caspian had attended enough Syndicate meetings to know that the furniture always arrived before the menace did.

It was a company habit. Wherever Vespera Meridian intended to conduct business, a small army of contractors preceded her by forty-eight hours: white linen on the tables of whatever pub or parish hall she'd commandeered, fresh flowers in cut-glass vases, a coffee service that would have looked at home in a Zurich boardroom. The effect was meant to be reassuring. *We are civilized people*, the room said. *We do not break legs; we break even.*

The Fisherman's Arms had never seen linen in its hundred and sixty years, and the landlord—Trembath, who Caspian suspected would have charged the sea itself for mooring rights—was being paid handsomely for the indignity. He kept refilling the good china teapot with an expression suggesting he was watching them all drown slowly and taking notes.

Vespera sat at the head of the long table like weather deciding where to make landfall. She was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, dressed in a coat the color of wet slate that probably cost more than the cottage Ulyana had inherited. Her lawyers flanked her—two of them, identical in their grey suits and their posture of patient appetite—and behind her stood a man Caspian hadn't been introduced to but had already catalogued: broad hands, salt-cracked knuckles, the stillness of someone whose job involved water and didn't involve asking permission from it. Head of dive operations, if Caspian's read was right. The kind of man Vespera brought along the way other people brought umbrellas.

"Mr. Vale," Vespera said, and smiled. It was a good smile. Caspian knew good smiles professionally; he owned several himself. Hers had the particular quality of a smile that had never once been surprised by anything. "And Miss Fairweather. Thank you both for coming."

"We were told it was optional," Ulyana said.

She'd taken the chair farthest from Vespera, angled so she could see both the door and the window—the window because it faced the sea, and Caspian had learned over the past weeks that Ulyana Fairweather checked the sea the way other people checked their phones. She was wearing her storm jacket indoors, unzipped, as though the meeting might turn meteorological without warning. Knowing her, it might.

"It was entirely optional," Vespera agreed pleasantly. "As is cooperation. I find the two words describe rather similar behaviors." She lifted the teapot herself, unbidden, and poured three cups with the ease of a woman who had never once been told no by a piece of crockery. "Sugar?"

"I'm fine," Ulyana said.

"She'll take sugar," Caspian said, before he could stop himself. "Two. She pretends she doesn't, then steals it off my saucer when she thinks I'm reading the charts."

Ulyana shot him a look that could have stripped paint. But she took the cup.

*Careful*, Caspian told himself. That was the trouble with these weeks on the escarpment—the trouble with her. He'd spent eleven years building himself into a man who noticed everything and revealed nothing, a ledger with a pulse, and somewhere between Oklahoma and Cornwall his sister had begun reading him back. Not knowing what she was reading yet. He felt the fact of it approaching like pressure before a front.

He filed the thought away and turned to business, which was safer ground. Business was always safe ground. Business had rules.

"You said you had news about the cave," he said.

"I said I had news," Vespera corrected gently. "Whether it concerns the cave depends on how you receive it." She set down the pot and folded her hands. Behind her, the diver shifted his weight, once, like a ship adjusting anchor. "But yes. To business. My geologists have completed their survey of the escarpment."

"Your geologists," Ulyana said. "You've had geologists on my cliff."

"On the *cliff*, Miss Fairweather. Legally speaking, the cliff belongs to whoever owns the land above the high-water mark, and portions of it belong to the National Trust, none of whom we needed permission from to look at rocks. Looking is free. It's one of my favorite things about looking." Vespera produced a slim folder—not a tablet, Caspian noted; paper, because paper couldn't be subpoenaed out of a cloud—and opened it with two fingers, the way a dealer turns a card. "The winter storms have done more damage than anyone realized. The strata beneath the point has been hollowing out for decades. Sea caves are temporary things, you understand. Beautiful, profitable, occasionally treasure-filled—but temporary. They're the sea's way of making withdrawals."

"And?" Ulyana said.

"And the withdrawal is nearly complete." Vespera slid a photograph across the linen. A cross-section diagram, all clean lines and shaded strata, with a red overlay marking the cave they'd descended into—the cave Caspian had reported as empty, because reporting otherwise would have set the Syndicate's machinery turning six days sooner than it had. "Structurally, the mouth is held up by perhaps four meters of compromised limestone. One significant storm surge—a spring tide combined with anything above a Force nine—and the entire section fails. Not erodes. *Fails.* Comes down all at once, takes fifty meters of cliff face with it, and closes the cave forever under several thousand tons of rock."

The room went quiet. Somewhere below the window, the sea worked patiently at the base of the escarpment, the sound of it coming up through the floorboards like breathing through a wall.

"When?" Ulyana asked. Her voice had changed. Caspian heard it—the flattening, the professional calm sliding over something rawer. He'd heard that voice in Oklahoma, in the truck, with a wall cloud rotating black a mile away and her hands perfectly steady on the wheel while her knuckles went white around it.

"That," Vespera said, "is the interesting part."

*

Here is what Caspian knew, and what he did not say:

He knew the survey was real. He'd read the preliminary data three days ago, intercepted through channels Vespera believed were hers alone, and the numbers were ugly and honest. The cave was dying. Whatever the legends said about generations and admissions, the sea was calling in its final loan this season, and there would be no next time.

He also knew—he was nearly certain—that Vespera had known the timeline before she'd ever come ashore. The dive equipment had arrived on the second boat. The lawyers on the third. You did not bring a salvage crew to a negotiation unless you already knew the asset had an expiry date, and you did not learn an expiry date like that overnight.

Which meant every hour since the boats anchored had been theater. Courteous, well-lit, flower-arranged theater.

He kept his face pleasant and his hands still and waited for the card to turn.

"The final deep-water storm system of the season," Vespera continued, tapping the photograph, "is forming west of Biscay. Our meteorologists give it a seventy percent probability of tracking northeast toward Cornwall within twelve to fourteen days. When it arrives, it will bring exactly the conditions required to close the cave permanently." She looked up, and her eyes found Ulyana's with the precision of a woman who had studied her file and knew precisely which buttons existed and which ones were load-bearing. "A storm chaser would appreciate the irony, I think. The same weather that made your reputation will bury your inheritance."

"My inheritance," Ulyana said slowly, "is a leaking cottage and a dead woman's journals."

"Your inheritance," Vespera said, "is whatever came off the *Meridian Star* in 1979. Let's not be coy; coy wastes everyone's time, and we haven't much of it." She let that sit for a moment, watching them both, and Caspian gave her nothing—gave her the mild, faintly bored expression of a man doing sums in his head, which was even true. "My grandfather lost a fortune in that wreck. I intend to recover it. And I am given to understand—" the smile again, wider by a degree, "—that the two of you have already been inside."

The silence stretched.

"We looked," Ulyana said. "There's a lot of rock and some old timbers. Nothing worth the rope."

"Mm," said Vespera, in the tone of a woman filing a statement she fully intended to disprove later. "Well. Whether or not you saw anything—and I'm sure if you had, you'd tell me, because you're both reasonable people—I'm here to offer you the courtesy of a deadline. I dislike surprises. I find that most people do, once they've experienced a few." She closed the folder. "You have until the storm arrives. Call it two weeks. In that time, I'm prepared to be generous—generous beyond anything the Meridian Syndicate has offered either of you, and I include, Mr. Vale, the considerable generosity already extended to your debts."

Caspian felt that one land exactly where she'd aimed it. His debts. The number Vespera carried in her head—the precise figure of everything he owed the organization that had raised him, employed him, and owned him—sat between them now like a third person at the table.

"In exchange for what?" Ulyana asked.

"Cooperation." Vespera spread her hands, the picture of reasonableness. "Access. Information. If you've surveyed the interior, share your notes. If you know the tides, share your timings. If you found anything of interest—" the pause was surgical, "—share that too. And when my divers go in, you'll stand on that cliff and watch, and afterward we'll all shake hands, and I will tear up certain papers, and everyone will walk away richer or at least unburdened. It's a good offer. It's the only offer. Because in two weeks, Miss Fairweather, Mr. Vale, the sea is going to take the cave whether I cooperate with it or not—and the only question left worth asking is who stands beside it when it does."

She rose. The lawyers rose. The diver rose, last, slow, like something surfacing.

"Think it over," Vespera said. "Though I'd think quickly. Weather doesn't wait, and neither, unfortunately, do I." At the door she paused, half-turned, and added—lightly, almost kindly, which was somehow worse: "Oh, and Miss Fairweather? Your mother wrote to my grandfather, you know. Near the end. Asking questions about 1979. She never got answers. I do wonder sometimes whether that's what killed her—wondering." A beat. "I'd hate to see wondering run in the family."

Then she was gone, taking her linen and her flowers and her courtesy with her, and the pub exhaled.

*

Ulyana didn't speak until they were outside, on the cliff path, wind coming off the water hard enough to lean against. Then she stopped walking and stared out at the three anchored vessels—white hulls, grey superstructures, cranes folded like sleeping insects—and said, very quietly:

"Two weeks."

"Twelve days, if the model holds."

"You've seen the model."

"I've seen *a* model." He watched her profile. "Hers tracks with mine. Within margin."

"So the cave really is going down." She laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "Of course it is. Of course the one thing my mother left me is on a timer." She pulled her jacket tighter. "She knew, Caspian. Before she ever came ashore. All of it—the collapse, the timeline, the whole polite little ambush. That wasn't a negotiation in there. That was a countdown with tea."

"Yes," Caspian said. "It was."

Ulyana turned to look at him, and the wind pushed her hair across her face, and for a moment she just studied him with those storm-grey eyes that missed less than anyone he'd ever met.

"You're not surprised," she said. "Any of it. The geologists, the deadline, the 'generosity.' You sat there like a man rereading a book." Her head tilted. "What aren't you telling me, Caspian Vale?"

And there it was—the question he'd been dodging since Oklahoma, wearing different clothes each time. *What aren't you telling me.* He had answers stacked and ready: the intercepted survey, the dive team's manifest, the fact that Vespera's "cooperation" came with a contingency clause he'd glimpsed in the legal brief, one that named him personally as the Syndicate's man on site, accountable for any assets recovered or concealed. Any of those would satisfy her. Any of those would keep her looking at the Syndicate instead of at him.

Instead he heard himself say, "Ask me again in a day or two. There's something I need to—"

"To what?" she pressed. Wind snapped the word off short.

"To work out how to say it."

Something moved behind her eyes—not suspicion exactly, or not only. Something older and warier, the look of a woman who had learned young that the people who loved you were also the people positioned to hurt you. She looked at him for a long moment, out there on the crumbling edge of the world, with the sea counting down beneath them both.

"Fine," she said finally. "Two weeks, right? Apparently we've got time."

She walked on toward the cottage. Caspian stayed where he was a moment longer, watching the waves detonate against the escarpment's base, four meters of limestone holding back the whole gray weight of the Atlantic.

In his breast pocket, folded twice, was the page from their mother's hidden journal—the one with the manifest, the name, the ring. He'd carried it for nine days. He'd lied to the Syndicate for nine days. And now Vespera had put a clock on the lie, and clocks had a way of forcing hands.

He touched the pocket once, the way you'd touch a wound to check it was still yours, and followed his sister home.
