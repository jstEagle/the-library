## 14. Half-Brother

The solicitor's office smelled of dust and old decisions.

Mr. Tregose—third of that name, judging by the brass plate, and possibly the last, judging by the state of the brass—had gone to fetch tea, which meant he'd gone to escape me, which meant I had roughly four minutes alone with his filing cabinets. I am not proud of how quickly I found them. I am not ashamed either. Storm chasers are, at heart, people who believe that anything worth knowing is worth knowing before it kills you.

The cabinet marked FAIRWEATHER was thinner than I expected. A dead woman's life, reduced to a hand's width of manila. Estate inventory. Deed to the cottage. A letter from a bank in Falmouth, 1994, about an overdraft that would have made me feel right at home.

And then, near the bottom, a folder with no label at all, just a coffee ring on the cover like a small brown moon.

I opened it because unlabeled things are where the weather hides.

Inside: correspondence from 1971. My mother's handwriting—young handwriting, looser than the storm journals, the letters still optimistic—and addressed to a Mrs. Penhale of St. Ives. Adoption arrangements. And beneath those, a birth record that stopped my breath the way a hailstone stops a windshield.

*Caspian Fairweather Merrow. Born 14 March 1968. Mother: Maren Fairweather.*

My mother. Born eight years before me. Given up when she was nineteen, before the cottage, before the journals, before me.

Caspian. *Caspian.* The name went through me like a squall line through a wheat field, flattening everything I'd built in three weeks of careful not-thinking. The way he'd stood in the cottage doorway and known where the light switch was. The way he'd read our mother's handwriting upside down without asking what it said. The way he'd looked at the cliff path and said, "She used to walk this every morning," and I'd assumed he was quoting the solicitor.

He hadn't been quoting anyone. He'd been remembering.

I sat down hard on Mr. Tregose's visitor chair, which complained under me like a gull, and read the file twice more while the tea went cold somewhere in the building behind me. There were photographs too—one, anyway, grainy as fog: a young woman on these very cliffs, hair whipping sideways, holding a baby wrapped against the wind. My mother's face. My mother's jaw, which I shave around every morning.

The baby had her chin too.

*Oh,* I thought, with the terrible clarity of someone who has just watched a funnel cloud touch down. *Oh, you absolute liar.*

* * *

I didn't confront him immediately, because I am a professional, and professionals check their instruments before they trust their gut. Also because I needed two hours and half a pasty to figure out what I actually felt, and the answer kept changing like a radar loop.

Anger, obviously. Anger first, anger loudest. He'd known since the flight over. He'd let me call him "the Syndicate's man" to his face for weeks, let me make jokes about collateral and cufflinks, let me tell him things about my mother—the only person I'd ever told things to about my mother—and all the while he'd been sitting on a fact the size of a house.

But underneath the anger, quieter and worse: relief. Because some part of me, the part that had noticed the light switch and filed it away as coincidence, had wanted him to be family. Had been so starved for it that a stranger with our mother's chin had felt like coming home.

And underneath *that*, the deepest layer, the one I'd have to dig for later with proper equipment: fear. Because if Caspian was a Fairweather, then the Syndicate hadn't sent a random collector. They'd sent family. Which meant they knew. Which meant everything he'd told them—

No. One thing at a time. That was chase protocol. You don't try to outdrive the whole storm; you drive the road in front of you.

The road in front of me was the cliff path, where Caspian had gone an hour ago with his coat collar up, walking toward the point where the escarpment dropped away into gray water. The barometer in the truck had been falling all afternoon—not storm-falling yet, but thinking about it, the way a dog thinks about barking.

I found him standing at the edge with his hands in his pockets, looking out at a sea the color of hammered pewter. Wind came up the cliff face in long pulls, tugging at his coat, and he didn't lean back from it. Fairweathers never did. I'd thought that was bravado. It turned out to be inheritance.

"Caspian," I said.

He turned. He smiled—that collector's smile, the one like a signed contract—and something in me snapped clean through.

"Fourteen March 1968," I said. "St. Ives. Mrs. Penhale."

The smile died by degrees, like a sunset you could watch happen. "Ulyana."

"You knew where the light switch was." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You knew her handwriting. You said 'she used to walk this path' like you'd walked it beside her. And I stood there being grateful that the Syndicate had finally sent me one human being, and the whole time—" The wind took my words and scattered them over the water, which was fine. They'd said enough. "You're my brother."

"I'm your half-brother," he said, and God help me, even now, even here, there was a flicker of that meticulous pedantry, the same instinct that made him correct tide tables and wine vintages. "Technically. She gave me up before—"

"I know the dates, Caspian. I read the file. What I don't know is why you've spent three weeks letting me think you were nothing."

"Because I wasn't nothing," he said quietly. "I was exactly what I appeared to be. A debt collector. That was the job. That was the only thing I've ever been allowed to be."

The wind rose another notch. Somewhere below us, waves were working at the base of the escarpment with the patience of a locksmith.

"So talk," I said. "All of it. Starting with whether they know."

He looked out at the sea for a long moment. When he spoke again, the charm was gone entirely, and what was left underneath was younger and more tired than any version of him I'd met.

"They know I'm hers. Vespera found the adoption records when she bought the debt—she buys everything, it's the only hobby she has. She thought it was elegant. Send the estranged son to collect from the estranged daughter. She said it had *narrative symmetry*." His mouth twisted. "Vespera believes she's living in a novel. It's her worst quality, and she has genuinely impressive competition."

"And you agreed? To come shake down your own sister?"

"I didn't know you existed until six months ago!" It burst out of him, and the wind snatched it and flung it back at us both. "I knew her name—Maren Fairweather, the woman who gave me up, the woman whose face I got secondhand from a social worker in 1973. I came here to find *her*. Nobody told me she was dead. Nobody told me there was a—" He gestured at me, at all of me, the truck and the instruments and the debts, and his hand wasn't steady. "There was a whole other life. A whole other child. She kept you and she gave me away, and I needed to know why, and the only way to get close to any of it was through the Syndicate's errand. So I ran the errand."

"And collected on it."

"And collected on it," he admitted. "Because that's what I do. That's what they made me. You want the truth, Ulyana? Here's mine: I have spent fifty years being somebody's instrument. First the care system, then the Syndicate. I am very good at it. I am so good at it that I can stand next to my own sister and log her weaknesses in a report." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard since the last tornado took a barn. "Entry one: she trusts too fast. Entry two: she'll forgive almost anything if you bring her tea."

"I don't forgive almost anything," I said.

"No," he agreed. "You forgive specific things. I'm counting on it."

The wind was properly up now, moaning along the cliff top, and the grass bent flat in long silver strokes. Out at sea, the horizon had gone soft and wrong, the way it does when the atmosphere is rearranging its furniture. My instruments were twenty meters away in the truck and I could feel the pressure dropping through my teeth.

"Why tell me now?" I asked. "Why not keep lying? You're good at it. You said so yourself."

"Because the cave changed the arithmetic." He stepped closer, and up close I could see our mother in him again, unmistakable now, the way a face is unmistakable once you've seen it in a photograph. "In that chamber, when the water was rising, you gave up her journal. Her actual handwriting. The thing you'd crossed an ocean for. You fed it to the sea to save both of us." His voice caught on something. "And I stood there thinking: the Syndicate sent me to take from this woman. From *this* woman. And I realized I'd rather drown than do it."

"That's dramatic."

"We're Fairweathers. Drama is genetic. Look at the weather we attract."

Despite everything—despite the fury still banked in my chest like coals—I laughed. Just once, just a bark the wind stole immediately, but he heard it, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, like a man setting down luggage he'd carried across an ocean.

"I'm furious with you," I told him.

"I know."

"You lied to me for weeks. You let me grieve in front of you like you were a stranger, and strangers are safe, and you weren't safe, you were the single most dangerous thing on this cliff, and I *told you about her*—"

"I know." He said it simply, without defense. "And I will spend whatever's left of my life making it right, starting with the fact that I never reported what's really in that cave. Vespera thinks it's empty. As far as the Syndicate knows, the sea took everything decades ago."

I stared at him. "You lied to Vespera."

"I lied to Vespera." He said it the way other men say *I jumped*. "First lie I've ever told them. It felt terrible. Then it felt wonderful. I understand now why you do it constantly."

"I don't do it *constantly*—"

"Ulyana. The rental car is registered to a fictional meteorological institute."

"That's *administrative fiction*, it's different—"

The ground shivered.

Not much. Not an earthquake—something smaller and more local, a deep subsonic shudder rolling up through the turf and into our boots, the sound of tons of old rock reconsidering its position. We both went still. Down at the base of the escarpment, a shelf of cliff face calved off and slid into the surf with a boom we felt more than heard, and a white scar opened on the black rock like a wound.

"The cave," Caspian said.

"Yeah." My mouth had gone dry. "That wasn't thunder."

We stood at the edge together—brother and sister, half or otherwise, liars both, heirs to a stolen fortune and a drowned brig—and watched the sea chew at the place where our mother's secrets were buried. The wind pushed at our backs like a hand between the shoulder blades, hurrying us toward a decision we hadn't made yet.

"How long?" I asked.

Caspian's jaw tightened. He looked at the fresh scar on the cliff, then at the bruised sky, doing the same math I was doing, arriving at the same number neither of us wanted to say aloud.

"Less time than Vespera thinks," he said. "And Vespera's already booked her divers for Thursday."

* * *

Back at the cottage, we spread everything on the kitchen table like surgeons: the photographs I'd taken in the wreck, printed blurry and precious from the camera's little screen; the manifest numbers I'd memorized twice over; Caspian's knowledge of the Syndicate's legal machinery, laid out alongside my knowledge of its temper.

"If we go to Vespera with the photos," he said, "she buries us. If we go public without leverage, her lawyers bury us slower, with better stationery."

"Then we need something she wants more than silence." I tapped the photograph of the manifest, the founder's name sharp even through the water-blur. "And I think I know how to price it."

Outside, the wind found the loose slate on the cottage roof and worried it, click and rattle, click and rattle—a sound I'd fallen asleep to as a child and hadn't remembered until this exact moment. Caspian glanced up at it, and I saw him hear it too, and neither of us said anything, because some inheritances don't need words.

Thursday was three days away. The cliff had started counting down.
