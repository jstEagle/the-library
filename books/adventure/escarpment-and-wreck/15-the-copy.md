## 15. The Copy

The solicitor's office smelled of dust and old decisions, the way all solicitors' offices do, as though paper itself had a mortality rate and everyone inside was quietly grieving it. Caspian Fairweather—no, that wasn't right either, it never quite had been—Caspian Merrow sat in a chair too small for his shoulders and watched Ulyana read.

She'd been reading for eleven minutes. He knew because he counted things when he was nervous, and he had been nervous since the cliff edge, since the wind had torn her voice apart and handed him the word *brother* like something fragile she wasn't sure he deserved to hold.

The file lay open between them on the solicitor's desk: their mother's estate papers, the adoption records, the whole sad architecture of a family split down its middle in 1981 and told to pretend it hadn't happened. Ulyana's finger moved down the page. Stopped. Moved back up.

"Merrow," she said. "That's not a Cornish name."

"It's a made-up one," Caspian said. "The Syndicate gave it to my father when they took him in. Orphans get rebranded. It's very efficient." He tried for lightness and heard it land wrong, tinny, like a coin dropped in an empty church. "I kept the name. It seemed rude not to."

Ulyana closed the file with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal. Then she looked at him, and he braced himself for the fury—he'd earned fury, he'd budgeted for fury—but what came instead was worse. She looked tired. She looked like someone doing arithmetic.

"Fourteen days," she said. "That's how long Vespera gave us. And you've known who I was since the plane."

"Since before the plane," he admitted. "Since the brief. They showed me your file. Photographs, chase footage, debt schedule." He hesitated. "A school report from when you were nine. You'd written an essay about tornadoes and your teacher said you were 'alarmingly certain about weather.'"

"I got a B," Ulyana said. "Because I refused to hedge."

"That tracks."

Silence filled the office like water filling a hull. Outside, the village went about its business—a gull screaming at something, the pub's sign creaking, the sea doing its endless patient work against the escarpment. Fourteen days, and somewhere under all that grey water, a cave was getting ready to die and take its secrets with it.

"You were going to collect," Ulyana said finally. Not a question. "Recover the debt or the asset. That was the brief."

"Yes."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a solicitor's office in a village where nobody knows my real name, having just learned my real name is the wrong one anyway." He spread his hands. "Now I don't know what I am. That's a new experience for me. I'm told it builds character."

"It doesn't," Ulyana said. "It just builds."

* * *

They walked up to the cottage because neither of them could stand the office anymore, and because the cottage was theirs now—hers, legally, but he'd begun to suspect that some inheritances didn't check the paperwork. The wind had dropped to that post-storm hush where the whole coast seemed to be listening to itself.

Inside, Ulyana went straight to her kit bag—the battered green duffel she'd carried through eleven states and two continents—and knelt over it with her back to him. He heard zippers. A long pause. When she turned around, she was holding her phone in both hands, the way you hold a bird you've just caught and aren't sure is injured.

"Before the chamber flooded," she said, "before we left the journal—I took photographs."

Caspian went very still. Stillness was his professional skill; he'd been trained by people who considered a raised heartbeat a security breach. But this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a man realizing the ground beneath him has been load-bearing all along.

"The manifest," she said. "Every page. The ring—macro shots, the crest, the engraving on the band. The skeleton's position relative to the strongboxes. Timestamps, GPS lock, everything. I photograph everything, Caspian. Storms, damage, insurance claims, menus I want to remember. It's not paranoia if the world actually is out to get you, and mine has been out to get me since 1979 and I only found out last week."

"Why didn't you say anything?" His voice came out strange. "On the cliff. In the office. You let me stand there feeling like the only one holding cards."

"Because I needed to know what you'd do first." She said it without apology, which he was learning was simply how she did everything. "You walked into that solicitor's office knowing who I was. I wanted to see what you'd walk out of it as."

"And what did I walk out as?"

"A man who looks terrible," she said, "and hasn't slept, and keeps counting things when he's nervous—you count the gulls, by the way, it's very obvious—which means either you're a better liar than the Syndicate trained you to be, or you're not lying at all anymore and it's making you physically ill." She set the phone on the table between them, screen up, gallery open. Ninety-one images. "So. Which is it?"

Caspian looked at the photographs. The manifest pages glowed on the screen, their mother's careful transcription of the cargo list, the founder's name signed in ink that had waited forty years underwater for exactly this. He thought about the Syndicate's methods, which he knew intimately: leverage first, litigation second, and if those failed, weather of a more human kind. He thought about Vespera's polite gloves and her fourteen-day courtesy.

He thought about the fact that he had already lied once for this woman—told the Syndicate there was no wreck—and discovered the lie fit him better than any truth he'd ever reported.

"I'm not lying anymore," he said. "It's making me physically ill. Also I'm hungry. Those may be related."

Something happened to Ulyana's face then—not quite a smile, but the geological event that precedes one. She sat down across from him, and for the first time since Oklahoma, since the plane, since the cave, they were two people at one table with nothing hidden between them except, possibly, how to proceed.

"All right," she said. "Then here's my thinking. We have ninety-one photographs. Vespera has lawyers, divers, three boats, and a deadline. If we sell her the photos, she buries them, pays us off, and the Syndicate goes on being the Syndicate with better PR. If we publish them, we torch her family name, and she torches us back—she'll claim forgery, she'll sue, she'll make our lives expensive and short. Neither option breaks the debt. Both options enrich somebody." She tapped the table. "I'm done enriching people. I want to break something."

"The debt," Caspian said slowly, following her.

"The debt. Specifically. Here's the shape of it: the Syndicate financed my chasing because they wanted access to this coast—they've wanted it for years, ever since the old manifest rumors. My debt was never really money, it was bait. I was the lure they dangled over the water." Her jaw tightened. "But if the founder scuttled his own ship, murdered his crew, and stole the inheritance my mother's family was owed—then every penny the Syndicate has ever spent is built on stolen capital. Their founding fortune is a crime scene. And a debt collected on behalf of a crime isn't a debt. It's an accessory arrangement."

Caspian felt something shift in his chest, some load redistributing. "You want to negotiate with the evidence, not surrender it. Silence in exchange for cancellation."

"I want to trade the truth for my freedom," Ulyana said. "Not for gold. Not for the wreck. For the ability to stop running. That's the whole transaction. And—" she paused, and he watched her decide to say the next part, watched her choose it the way she chose to drive toward storms other people fled, "—and I want a partner who knows both sides of the table. Who knows their playbook because he wrote half of it."

"You're hiring me," Caspian said. "The Syndicate's collector. As your negotiator. Against the Syndicate."

"I'm asking my brother," Ulyana corrected, and the word landed in the room like a struck bell, ringing through the cottage, through forty years of separate rooms and separate names, "to help me. The collector can bill me later."

He laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound that surprised them both—and then, because trust is built in small transactions, not grand ones, he did something he had never done in nine years of service. He took out his own phone, opened it, and laid it on the table beside hers.

"My reports," he said. "Everything I've sent them since the flight over. Including last night's: 'cave located, no wreck present, site unstable.' My first lie, timestamped and delivered. If they come after you, they come after me. If they audit me, these messages prove I misled them deliberately." He met her eyes. "Shared leverage. That's what you're describing. So let's share it properly. Your photos, my lies. Yours are worth more, but mine are more dangerous to me. I believe that makes us even."

Ulyana studied the two phones side by side on the table—her evidence, his confession—and Caspian watched her do the thing she did with storms: assess, respect, commit.

"Not even," she said at last. "Close enough. Family math."

"What's family math?"

"Rounding errors you forgive." She stood, went to the kettle—it was the one domestic gesture he'd seen her make in the entire time he'd known her, and somehow it undid him more than the cliff-edge confrontation had—and put it on. "Terms, then. One: we use the photos to kill the debt, full stop. No sale, no ransom, no enrichment. Two: we go public only if Vespera forces it—we give her the chance to do the decent thing quietly, because I've met her kind of desperate and it gets people drowned. Three—" she glanced at him over her shoulder, and the gentleness in it nearly finished him, "—three, nobody rebrands anybody. Merrow, Fairweather, whatever we call ourselves, we decide together."

"Fairweather-Merrow," Caspian said. "Alphabetical. It's the principle of the thing."

"Fairweather-Merrow," she agreed, and handed him a chipped mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, which had apparently belonged to their mother, which meant, he realized with a lurch, that it had belonged to both of them all along.

The kettle began its low climb toward a whistle. Outside, the light was going long and gold over the water, and fourteen days suddenly felt less like a countdown and more like a runway.

"One problem," Caspian said, cradling the mug. "Vespera doesn't negotiate. She acquires. When I tell her the wreck is gone and the evidence is ash, she'll believe me for about a day. Then her divers will find something, or her lawyers will pull my financials, or she'll simply look at my face, which has apparently become unreliable." He turned the mug slowly. "We need to move first. Tomorrow, before she tightens the screws. And Ulyana—when we sit down with her, she'll offer you everything. Money, freedom, the cottage clear-titled, my debts forgiven too. She'll make it generous, because generosity is cheaper than guilt. You'll have to refuse the version of this that ends with everyone paid and nothing changed."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I've been refusing things my whole life. It's the one sport I'm ranked in."

The kettle whistled. She poured, and steam rose between them, and for a moment the cottage held the two of them in something that was almost peace.

Then Caspian's phone buzzed against the wood.

They both looked at it. The screen lit with a message from a number he knew by heart, the one that had directed his whole adult life:

**VESPERA MERIDIAN:** *Change of plans. Divers report structural movement at the cave mouth. We're moving the operation up. Boat leaves the harbor at dawn — both of you are expected aboard.*

Below it, a second message arrived before he could breathe:

*I know about the photographs, Caspian.*
