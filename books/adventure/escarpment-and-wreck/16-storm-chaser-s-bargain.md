## 16. Storm Chaser's Bargain

The Meridian Syndicate's lead vessel was called the *Optimist's Return*, which told you everything you needed to know about people who named their boats after other people's losses.

Ulyana watched it from the cottage window while she buttered toast she had no appetite for. Three ships sat offshore in the grey morning light, riding at anchor with the patient menace of chess pieces that hadn't been moved yet. The largest flew a pennant Vespera Meridian probably believed was tasteful: deep blue, a silver star, no gilding. Tasteful menace. That was the house style.

Behind her, Caspian was cleaning instruments that didn't need cleaning. He'd been doing it for an hour. Barometer, hygrometer, the little brass compass their mother's journals said had survived two wrecks and one marriage. He polished it like it might confess something.

"You're going to wear the plating off," Ulyana said.

"I'm going to make it shine."

"It's already shining."

"Then I'm nearly done." He set it down and immediately picked up the barometer instead.

She let him have another minute. Then: "She'll come ashore today. Barometer's falling faster than the forecast says it should, and women like Vespera don't waste good weather on lawyers who get seasick."

"And your offer?"

"Same as last night. Silence for freedom." She turned from the window. "I walk in, I smile, I tell her I know what her grandfather did to that crew, and I offer to keep it between us if she tears up my debt and lets us both walk away. Clean trade. Nobody bleeds."

Caspian looked at her over the barometer. "You've negotiated with debt collectors before?"

"I *am* a debt collector. I collect storms. Same skill set—you find out what the thing wants, and you give it something cheaper than what it's asking for."

"And what does Vespera want?"

Ulyana looked out at the ships again. "Her grandfather back," she said quietly. "That's the trap of it. You can't buy off someone who's grieving a hundred years late."

* * *

Vespera Meridian came ashore at eleven in a rigid inflatable operated by two crewmen who moved like they'd been hired for their cheekbones. She stepped onto the shingle below the village slipway in a coat that cost more than Ulyana's truck, and she did not stumble once on the wet stones, which Ulyana respected even as she resented it.

They met on the sea wall, because Vespera had suggested the village hall and Ulyana had refused—too many witnesses she didn't control—and Vespera had suggested the pub and Ulyana had refused again, too many witnesses she *did*. The sea wall it was: public enough to be safe, empty enough to be honest.

"Miss Fairweather." Vespera's handshake was brief and dry. "You look better than your file photographs."

"My file photographs?"

"We photograph all our investments." She said it the way other people said *we water our plants*. "You were thinner in Oklahoma. Chasing is hard on the body."

"So is owing money." Ulyana leaned against the wall, arms crossed, going for relaxed and landing somewhere near armed. "Let's skip the weather talk."

"If you insist." Vespera folded her hands. Up close her face was older than her voice, lines around the eyes that suggested she'd done her own waiting over the years, in boardrooms instead of trucks. "My divers report the cave mouth is unstable but navigable at spring tides. They also report that someone has been inside before them. Rope scuffs. Boot prints in the silt. A chamber where the sediment's been disturbed around—" she paused, delicately, "—a strongbox that is now empty."

"Fancy that," Ulyana said. "The sea moves things about."

"The sea," Vespera agreed, "moves things about. My grandfather's manifest lists cargo worth, conservatively, nine million pounds in today's terms. I would very much like to know where it went."

"Would you?" Ulyana pushed off the wall and faced her square. Here it was, then. No more circling. "Because here's what I'd want to know, if I were you. I'd want to know why the *Meridian Star* went down with its own captain's signet ring on a skeleton in the hold. I'd want to know why the lifeboats were still stowed. And I'd want to know why my family's inheritance—the inheritance your grandfather 'salvaged' in 1979, according to paperwork your company filed—was sitting in a wreck he told the world never existed."

The wind came up the escarpment and pushed between them, carrying salt and the smell of the falling tide.

Vespera didn't flinch. But something behind her eyes recalculated, the way a compass needle settles after a shock. "You've been inside."

"I've been inside."

"And you took photographs."

"I took everything that mattered."

For a long moment the only sound was the water worrying the shingle below. Then Vespera smiled, and it was a genuinely fine smile, warm at the edges, and Ulyana understood suddenly how a man like Aldous Meridian had talked crews onto his ships.

"Good," Vespera said. "I hoped you'd be sensible. Sensible people can do business. So let me propose mine." She drew a slim envelope from her coat—not a folder, not a contract, just an envelope, because the truly dangerous paperwork always traveled light. "Inside is a release. Full forgiveness of your debt to the Syndicate. Every pound, every 'opportunity' you owe us, gone. Signed this morning, witnessed by my solicitor aboard the *Return*. It becomes yours the moment those photographs become mine."

"And the ring? The manifest? The fact that your grandfather murdered six sailors and stole from mine?"

"Stays exactly where it is," Vespera said pleasantly. "In a collapsed cave under forty feet of Cornish sea, along with everyone who was there when it happened. Which, conveniently, is two people. One of whom owes me a great deal of money." Her eyes flicked toward the cottage above the lane, where a figure stood at the window pretending not to watch. "And one of whom works for me."

"There it is," Ulyana said softly.

"There what is?"

"The part where it stops being a negotiation." She kept her voice level, kept her hands loose. Storm-chaser trick: the calmer you sounded, the worse the cell actually was. "You're not offering to forgive a debt. You're offering to buy silence with a hostage."

"I'm offering *employment continuity*," Vespera said, "to a man whose contract contains clauses you would find quite creative. Salvage law is a strange country, Miss Fairweather. Everything recovered from a wreck belongs, in the first instance, to the wreck's registered owner. Guess who's been the registered owner of the *Meridian Star* since 1979." She tilted her head. "If certain items left that wreck before my salvage operation began—and if certain parties failed to declare them—that isn't treasure hunting. That's theft. From my family. With evidence. My solicitors are very good at evidence; we keep it in climate-controlled rooms."

"He didn't take anything."

"He was inside the wreck. He signed nothing. He reported nothing. In a courtroom, those three facts will do all the work while I supply the adjectives." Vespera smoothed her coat, unhurried, a woman checking the time against a train she knew wouldn't leave without her. "So. The photographs, or Mr.—" she used his surname, his false one, deliberately, letting Ulyana hear the hinge of it, "—discovers what our legal department does with thieves. I'm told the food in prison is improving."

* * *

Ulyana walked up the lane slowly, because her legs wanted to run and running would have meant Vespera won something.

Caspian met her at the gate. He'd heard enough through the window—or all of it, knowing him; the man could read lips through weatherboard—and his face had gone carefully blank, which was how he looked when he was feeling the most.

"She offered you the debt," he said.

"She offered me the debt *for the photos*, and you as the down payment if I say no." Ulyana leaned on the gate. Below, Vespera was walking back to the inflatable, unhurried, coat snapping in the wind like a flag claiming territory. "Charming woman. Really. If she weren't threatening to bury you in legal paperwork I'd ask her for skincare tips."

"Ulyana."

"I know."

"She'll do it. The theft charge. It doesn't even need to stick—it needs to exist. An arrest, a trial date, a headline. The Syndicate doesn't need me convicted. It needs me *costly*." He laughed, short and without any humor in it. "That's the first thing they teach you. Assets aren't retrieved by force. They're retrieved by making every alternative more expensive than surrender."

"Then we make surrender expensive for *her*." Ulyana pulled the phone from her jacket and held it up between them, screen dark, holding the copies of the manifest, the journal pages, the photograph of the ring on bone-white finger bones. "This goes nowhere unless we both agree. That's the deal now. Not my leverage, not yours. Ours."

He looked at the phone the way sailors look at weather coming over the horizon—measuring, respectful, a little afraid. "And if she calls it? If she files tomorrow morning?"

"Then we publish tonight, and her family name burns with ours in the same fire." Ulyana pocketed the phone. "Mutually assured disclosure. Cold War rules. Nobody wins, everybody survives."

"That's not a bargain, that's a standoff."

"Caspian." She waited until he looked at her. "Every standoff ends. The only question is whether it ends in a handshake or a crater. We're trying for the handshake."

He exhaled slowly. "You sound like her."

"I know," Ulyana said, and hated it. "That's the worst part. She's not wrong about how these games work. She's only wrong about what's worth winning."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing for both futures, which mostly meant arguing.

Ulyana drafted the publication packet—the manifest scans, the journal pages, a timeline she built the way she built chase forecasts, clean and undeniable. Caspian drafted a letter to a solicitor in Falmouth whose name he produced from a memory Ulyana was beginning to suspect contained entire libraries. By four o'clock they had a plan for peace and a plan for war, filed side by side like storm shelters and sun loungers.

At five, the barometer fell so fast the needle audibly clicked.

Ulyana stood over it, frowning. She checked it against her laptop, against the buoy feeds, against the sky itself—which had gone a strange bruised green-grey in the southwest, the color of weather that hadn't made up its mind yet but was leaning toward violence.

"That's wrong," she said.

"What?"

"This low. It's deepening twice as fast as the models had it. Look at the pressure trace." She turned the laptop toward him. The line fell away steeply, a cliff of its own. "This isn't the season's last window closing politely. This is something else. This is a system that wasn't supposed to exist until Thursday arriving early and angry."

Caspian came to stand beside her. Outside, the gulls had gone quiet, which was never a good sign; gulls complain about everything except the things that matter.

"Vespera's divers," he said slowly. "Her schedule. She planned the salvage window around the forecast."

"Everyone planned around the forecast." Ulyana stared at the green horizon, and the old familiar cold walked up her spine—the one that arrived whenever the atmosphere broke its promises. "And the forecast just lied to all of us."

Her phone buzzed. The village harbor master, a man who communicated exclusively in fragments. *Barometer dropping. Boats coming in. Tell the Meridian woman.*

Below, out on the water, the *Optimist's Return* rode at anchor with its lights beginning to show, and Ulyana thought about Vespera's divers, and the unstable cave mouth, and a legend that charged admission in truths and prices and lives, and she felt the day tilt beneath her like a deck in a swell.

The storm was coming early.

And nobody ashore or afloat seemed to know it yet except her.
