## 17. The Night the Sea Came Early

### Ulyana

The barometer began lying at four in the afternoon.

Ulyana knew it was lying because she'd built the thing herself from a salvaged aircraft altimeter and a stubbornness she'd inherited from a mother she'd only just begun to understand. It read 1002 millibars and falling gently, a nice ordinary evening's decline. But her knees said otherwise. Her knees had been saying otherwise since lunch, and her knees had never once lied to her about a storm.

"Knees again?" Caspian asked. He was at the cottage table with the solicitor's files spread out like a card game he was losing on purpose, and he didn't look up when he said it, which after three weeks of sharing air with him meant he'd already looked up twice and decided to pretend he hadn't.

"Knees again."

"How bad?"

"Bad in a way that doesn't match the numbers." She stood at the window, watching the sea. The escarpment dropped away below the cottage in a long green-brown shoulder of rock, and beyond it the Channel lay flat and pewter-colored, almost polite. "The numbers say we've got until Thursday. My knees say the sea didn't get the memo."

Caspian did look up then. Three weeks ago she would have read his expression as the collector's — calculating, weighing, filing. Now she could see the other thing underneath it, the thing that had been there all along apparently, the brother-shaped worry. "Vespera's divers went down an hour ago," he said quietly. "Two boats, six divers, the whole production. She told them the window closes Thursday."

"The window is closing *now*."

"Then we should probably tell someone who can still do something about it." He was already reaching for his coat, and Ulyana caught his sleeve.

"Wait." The word came out smaller than she intended. She made herself stand in it anyway. "If we warn them — if we run down that cliff in the dark shouting about rogue waves — we're warning the people who came to strip-mine my family's grave. Vespera Meridian sat across a table from me and threatened to feed Caspian to her lawyers. Those divers are down there pulling out whatever's left of the *Meridian Star* with dredges and cameras, and every piece they take is a piece of the truth going into a private vault in Geneva."

"I know what they are, Ulyana."

"Do you? Because I've been rehearsing this argument since chapter one, so to speak, and I want to be honest about where I landed first." She let go of his sleeve. "My first instinct — my honest, ugly, human instinct — was to make tea. To sit here and watch the weather do what the courts can't. Let the sea collect. That's the legend, isn't it? A truth, a price, or a life. They came for the treasure without paying admission. Maybe the sea does invoicing."

Caspian set down his coat very carefully, the way he did everything, as though objects might be collateral too. "And your second instinct?"

"My second instinct is standing in front of me wearing a coat he bought with Syndicate money, and I would very much like him not to be on that cliff when it comes down." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it and kept going. "Six divers, Caspian. Six people who signed on for a salvage job, not a burial. Whatever Vespera deserves — and believe me, I have a list, and it's annotated — the kid on the second boat who's diving for hazard pay because the Syndicate owns his boat loan too? He doesn't deserve the escarpment coming down on him while I sip tea and call it justice."

The wind arrived then, properly, the way it does on that coast — not a gust but a decision. The windows shivered. Down in the village, Ulyana could see lights coming on early, and old Tam Berriman's dog starting up its end-of-the-world bark, which had been right more often than the Met Office.

"That's the whole argument, isn't it," Caspian said. Not a question. "You can't pick which people the storm is allowed to take. You tried that in Oklahoma. The F3 that took the Kiefer farm while you were filming the one that missed the school."

Ulyana stared at him. "I never told you that."

"You talk in your sleep. You narrate, actually. With commentary." And there it was, the smile like collateral, except she knew now it wasn't a collector's smile at all; it was the smile of a man who had been collecting something else entirely this whole time. "Come on. If we're going to commit treason against our own grudges, we should do it quickly, before I come to my senses."

She grabbed the radio, the big handheld with the marine band, and her headlamp, and the flare gun she'd bought in Penzance mostly because it was red and dramatic. At the door she stopped once, looking back at the cottage — at her mother's journals stacked on the shelf, the photographed pages hidden in the truck, the whole fragile archive of a truth they were supposed to be protecting.

"If the sea takes the cave tonight," she said, "it takes the rest of the evidence with it."

"Then we'd better save some divers," Caspian said. "We'll need witnesses."

* * *

### Caspian

The path down the escarpment had been treacherous in daylight. In the dark, with the barometer doing whatever it was doing instead of falling, it was a dare written in loose shale.

Caspian went first, because he was heavier and because Ulyana had spent twenty years learning exactly how much risk her body could carry and he trusted her arithmetic over his own pride. Behind and above them the cottage light shrank to a match-head. Below, the sea had changed its voice. He'd grown up landlocked — a fact he'd mentioned exactly once, and Ulyana had looked at him with such naked pity that he'd resolved never to mention it again — but even he could hear it: the water wasn't crashing anymore. It was *breathing*. Long, deep inhalations, dragging back from the rocks further than the tide had any business going, and then that silence between breaths that was worse than any crash.

"That's a drawdown," Ulyana shouted over her shoulder. "Sea's pulling back before it pushes. Classic tsunami signature, or—" she paused, and he heard her swallow, "—or the cave system below us just opened something it shouldn't have."

"The divers."

"Yeah. The divers."

His phone buzzed against his chest. He nearly ignored it — he'd been ignoring Syndicate calls for two weeks with increasing creativity — but the caller ID made his stomach drop. Not a handler. Not accounting. Vespera herself.

He answered. "Meridian. This is a bad time."

"It's a bad time for everyone, darling." Her voice was glass over moving water, calm in the specific way that meant she was reading instruments. "Our dive master has gone quiet. Comms went to static eleven minutes ago. The *Corvina*'s skipper says the swell period is wrong — eight seconds and shortening. I'm told you and my debtor are the nearest people with weather sense. So tell me, Caspian, as a professional courtesy between people who both work for the same firm: what's happening to my divers?"

He stopped on the path. Below him, in the dying light, he could see the dive boat *Corvina* bucking at anchor off the cave mouth, its running lights smearing in the spray. Two figures on deck, hauling at something. A third running for the winch.

"They're under a collapsing sky," he said. "Get them out. Get everyone off that water and away from the cliff base. Now, Vespera. Not in ten minutes. Now."

A pause. On the wind he could hear, faintly, the sound of her own ship behind her — orders being given in that clipped Syndicate cadence, engines changing pitch. Whatever else Vespera Meridian was, she moved fast when the numbers turned.

"And the cave?" she said. "The wreck?"

"Is about to become gravel. Your fortune included."

Another pause, longer. When she spoke again the glass had cracked and he could hear something almost human underneath. "My grandfather drowned building that fortune, Mr. Fairweather. Or so the story goes." A breath. "Get clear of the escarpment. Both of you. That's not an order, it's — please."

She hung up. Caspian stood in the rising wind for a moment, holding the phone like a small dead bird. *Please.* In eleven years of service he had never once heard the Meridian family say please.

"Ulyana!" he bellowed upward. "They're recalling the divers!"

"Good!" Her voice came down through the dark, ragged. "Because the cliff isn't waiting for them to finish!"

The ground agreed with her. It moved — not dramatically, not yet, just a deep structural shrug, like a giant shifting in sleep, and somewhere above them a section of the path they'd descended five minutes earlier slid away into the dark with a sound like a drawer full of stones.

* * *

### Ulyana

They reached the shelf above the cave mouth as the sea gave its first real shout.

Ulyana had seen water do terrible things. She'd stood in the inflow sector of an EF4 and felt the rain turn horizontal and personal. But she had never seen the ocean inhale the way it inhaled now — a single vast withdrawal that laid bare thirty meters of seabed below the cave mouth, kelp and stone and the ribs of old boats gleaming wet in the strobe of the *Corvina*'s searchlight, and held it, held it, a held breath the legends had promised her weeks ago —

— and then exhaled.

The wave that came back wasn't a wave. It was the sea arriving all at once, a wall of gray muscle that hit the cliff base and went *up*, forty feet of spray and shattered foam, and when the searchlight found the water again the *Corvina*'s tender was gone and two divers were in the surge clinging to a skiff that was trying to become driftwood.

"There!" Caspian's arm shot past her shoulder. "On the rocks — left of the mouth —"

She saw them. Two figures in the churning shallows at the lip of the cave entrance, half in the water, one dragging the other. The cave mouth itself was a black arch that had been, three weeks ago, a careful secret, and was now a throat with the tide pouring into it.

The radio crackled. "*Any station, any station, this is Corvina actual — we have two divers unaccounted, repeat two divers unaccounted, they entered the cavern on the recall order and the surge has them pinned inside —*"

Ulyana lowered the radio slowly.

Here it was, then. The moment she'd been rehearsing in the ugliest corners of herself since Vespera's lawyers had slid the first threat across the table. The sea was collecting. The legend was working exactly as advertised, and all she had to do was nothing — stand on this shelf in the rain and let the Meridian name pay its admission in full, and tomorrow the debt would be settled by drowning, and no one could ever say Ulyana Fairweather hadn't been paid back fairly.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed, distantly, that they weren't shaking with fear.

"*Corvina actual,*" she keyed, "this is Fairweather on the east shelf above the cave mouth. I see your divers. Two, at the entrance, one ambulatory. The surge cycle is roughly ninety seconds — drawdown, then push. You'll get maybe forty seconds of slack at the mouth between sets. Do not send a boat in; the drawdown will beach you on the ledge and the next set will roll you. Send lines from the cliff top if you have anyone who can climb. We're going down to them."

Static. Then Vespera's voice, stripped of all its glass: "*Why.*"

It wasn't *why are you helping*. Ulyana understood that instantly. It was the other question, the one underneath — *why would you, after everything.*

"Because the sea doesn't get to finish your family's work," Ulyana said. "That's mine. And I'm choosing not to."

She clipped the radio to her harness, checked Caspian's rope twice, and looked at him. Rain ran down his face and he was grinning — actually grinning, the mad bright grin of a man who had finally, after a lifetime of meticulous collateral, found something worth risking.

"Ninety-second cycles," he said. "Forty seconds of grace. That's your professional assessment?"

"That's my knees' professional assessment."

"Then let's go meet the deadline."

They went down into the roar together, and behind them, high on the failing escarpment, a crack opened in the night like the sky remembering it had teeth.

* * *

### Caspian

The water took him to the chest at the mouth of the cave and tried, with enormous indifferent patience, to take the rest of him.

He had the diver by the jacket — the young one, the boat-loan kid, whose mask was gone and whose eyes were white circles in the searchlight spill — and the boy weighed nothing and everything, the way drowning people do. Behind him Ulyana had the second diver by the harness, hauling her toward the inner ledge where the surge couldn't quite reach, and Caspian was counting. He was always counting; it was the one inheritance the Syndicate had given him worth keeping.

Thirty seconds since the crest. Forty-five. The water around his waist began, impossibly, to fall.

"Move! Drawdown! MOVE!"

They moved. The four of them scrambled up the slick inner slope as the sea inhaled again behind them, and the sound it made sucking back out of the cavern was the sound of the world clearing its throat, and above them — above all of them — the roof of the world groaned.

Not the cave roof. The cliff. The escarpment itself, the great ancient shoulder that had held this coast for ten thousand years, deciding, in the manner of all old structures, that tonight was the night.

Dust and pebbles rained through the black arch of the entrance. Through it, framed like a painting in a burning gallery, Caspian could see the *Corvina*'s searchlight swinging wildly, and beyond it the shapes of the Meridian ships turning their bows seaward, and beyond *that* — high on the cliff road — headlights. Village vehicles. Tam Berriman's tractor, God help them all, chains flashing, coming down toward the cottages to evacuate whoever hadn't left yet.

The diver — the boy — grabbed Caspian's arm. "Sir — sir, there were four of us in the water, there were FOUR—"

Caspian looked at Ulyana. Ulyana was already looking at the cave mouth, at the black arch, at the sea stacking itself beyond it in the lightning like a wall being built by something with opinions.

Above them, the escarpment shifted again, deeper this time, a sound like a door closing in the earth.

"One more cycle," Ulyana said. Her voice was steady in the way that Caspian had learned, over three weeks and one lifetime, meant she was more afraid than she would ever admit. "Maybe two. Then the mouth goes." She pulled the flare gun from her harness, checked it, and looked at the darkness deeper in the cavern — the direction of the side chamber, the direction of the wreck, the direction where a fourth diver might or might not be clinging to a hundred-year-old rib of the *Meridian Star*.

"Caspian," she said. "Tell me I'm not about to do this."

He took the rope off his shoulder and paid it out.

"You're not about to do this alone," he said.
