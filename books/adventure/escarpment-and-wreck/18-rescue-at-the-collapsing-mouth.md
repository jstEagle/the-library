## 18. Rescue at the Collapsing Mouth

**ULYANA**

The storm came early, the way storms do when you've made plans around their absence.

Ulyana felt it in her teeth first—she always did, a low ache like a tooth complaining about weather it hadn't been invited to—and then in the barometer, which fell so fast she laughed out loud, alone in the cottage kitchen at four in the morning, because what else was there to do? The instruments had promised her nine days of grace. The sea had looked at that promise and torn it up like a bad contract.

By six, the escarpment was groaning.

She'd heard it before, that sound—the deep, structural complaint of a cliff deciding whether to remain a cliff—but never this close, never this personal. It came up through the floorboards and into her boots and settled somewhere behind her sternum, where dread kept its office hours.

Caspian stood at the window with a mug of tea he wasn't drinking. He'd slept on the sofa for three nights now, ever since the confrontation on the cliff edge, ever since *half-brother* had stopped being a word and become a fact with a face. They were still learning each other's silences. His silences, she was discovering, were load-bearing.

"Vespera's boats went out an hour ago," he said.

"I saw."

"She was warned. We both told her. In writing, Caspian. I made you put it in writing so I could watch you write it."

"You did." He turned the mug a quarter-turn on the sill, a habit she was beginning to recognize as his version of pacing. "Her divers went in anyway. Two of them. The rest refused."

Ulyana closed her eyes. Behind them: the cave mouth, black as a keyhole; the water rising through it in the dark; two strangers in diving gear who had been sent down by a woman in a good coat to fetch gold from a grave.

"They'll be trapped," she said. "When the surge comes through, the chamber floods top to bottom. There's no high ground left. I've done the math three times hoping I was wrong."

"Are you wrong?"

"I'm never wrong about water. It's the one thing I've never lied to myself about."

Caspian set the mug down. She watched him arrive at the decision the way she watched fronts arrive on radar—a smudge at the edge of the screen, then suddenly everywhere.

"We can't reach them from the water," he said. "Not in this swell. But the eye's coming through around eight. Twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five, of wind that forgets itself."

"And the descent rope's still anchored."

"It is."

They looked at each other across the kitchen, two people who shared a mother they'd both lost differently, and Ulyana felt the strange calm descend that she only ever felt in the worst weather—the calm that was probably, some doctor would say, a symptom.

"Get the truck," she said. "I'll get the ropes."

* * *

**CASPIAN**

Caspian had spent his entire career making cold calculations on behalf of other people, and he knew the arithmetic of risk the way some men knew scripture. What he was calculating now, in the passenger seat of Ulyana's mud-crusted truck as it bucked along the coast road through horizontal rain, was not risk.

It was the fact that his sister drove like a woman being chased by her own past, and that he had never once, in thirty-four years, felt this much like laughing and praying at the same time.

"Left," he said.

"I see the left."

"You're going to argue with the left."

"I'm going to *negotiate* with the left—" The truck took the corner on two wheels and a prayer, and Caspian gripped the handle above the door and decided, in the interest of family harmony, to stop narrating.

The eye found them at the cliff top, exactly as she'd said it would. The rain stopped all at once, like someone lifting a wet blanket off the world, and the wind died to nothing, and the light went strange and yellow and holy. Below them the sea lay flattened, stunned, holding its breath. And there, at the base of the escarpment, half-drowned already, the cave mouth gaped with water churning inside it.

He could see the dive line. He could see, snagged against the inner rocks maybe forty feet in, a strobe flashing its small desperate argument.

"Twenty minutes," Ulyana said, already out of the truck, hauling the rope bag over her shoulder like it weighed nothing, like the last twelve hours hadn't happened, like her hands weren't shaking. "You anchor topside. You don't come down unless I call."

"No," Caspian said.

She stopped. Turned. Rain was starting again at the far edge of the world—he could see it coming, a grey curtain walking toward them across the water—and in the yellow light her face was fierce and exhausted and so much like their mother's photographs that it hurt somewhere structural.

"Caspian—"

"You spent ten years telling me—telling *everyone*—that you work alone, and then you spent three days teaching me the anchor system anyway. You didn't teach me by accident. Fairweather women don't do anything by accident." He shouldered the second rope. "We go together or we don't go, and if we don't go, those divers die, and you'll carry it forever, and I'll have to watch you carry it, and frankly I've just met you and I'm not willing to start our acquaintance that way."

A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"God," she said. "You even argue like Mum."

"I'll take that as a yes."

* * *

**ULYANA**

The descent was worse than the first time, because the first time the sea had been polite. Now the cave breathed. Water surged in and dragged back out in long irregular gulps, and every time it exhaled, the whole passage shuddered, and grit rained down from the roof like the sky shedding dandruff.

Ulyana went first, because she was better on rope and because she needed her hands busy or her mind would start doing interviews with itself.

*You're going back into the place that took your mother's journal,* said the mind. *For people working for the woman who tried to blackmail you.*

"Shut up," she said aloud, and the cave echoed it back in a voice much bigger than hers, which was humbling.

The first diver was wedged in the narrows where the passage bent toward the old chamber, regulator out, mask fogged, clinging to rock with the blind strength of the nearly drowned. Young, maybe. Hard to tell with the blue lips. His partner was nowhere visible, but the second air line ran taut into the dark, which meant alive, which meant hurry.

She got her arm under him and shouted over the water: "MY BROTHER'S COMING BEHIND ME. HOLD ON TO HIM AND CLIMB."

The word *brother* came out without permission. It sat in the air between them, absurd and enormous, and the diver grabbed her wrist and squeezed once—*understood, Godspeed, madwoman*—and then Caspian's boots hit the water beside her and everything became motion.

* * *

**CASPIAN**

Later, Caspian would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory gave him fragments instead of film: the weight of a drowning man against his shoulder; Ulyana's voice cutting through the roar with the clean authority of someone who had talked to weather her whole life and expected it to listen; the second diver surfacing in the inner pool, suit torn, tank empty, eyes white-rimmed with terror, saying something that might have been *please* or might have been a name.

What he remembered most clearly, in the way you remember the single sharp detail from a car crash, was the sound.

The escarpment had stopped groaning. It had moved on to something worse—a deep, grinding, deliberate noise, like a giant clearing its throat before speaking, and every instinct he owned said *that is the sound of a structure revising its opinion of gravity.*

"GO!" Ulyana screamed. "UP! NOW!"

They climbed. Four bodies on two ropes in a throat of stone filling with sea. The rescued divers went first, half-hauled, half-climbing, running on whatever fuel terror refines from panic. Caspian climbed behind them with Ulyana below, calling the rhythm—*move, move, breathe, move*—and the water rose past his waist, his chest, chasing them up the shaft with the patience of something that had already won a hundred thousand years of arguments with cliffs.

Above, daylight. A circle of it, wild and grey, widening.

The lead diver reached the lip and was dragged out by hands that must have belonged to villagers—Caspian would learn later that half the pub had followed them down the coast path, because Cornish fishermen apparently treated a rescue as a spectator sport with optional participation. The second diver came up. Then the first of the ropes went slack in his grip—not cut, *unanchored*, the whole cliffside anchor point simply ceasing to exist—and Caspian swung free over forty feet of boiling water and thought, with remarkable clarity: *so this is how the Syndicate collects after all.*

Then Ulyana's hand closed on his collar.

She had anchored herself to the last living bolt with her harness and leaned out over the void like a figurehead, and she hauled him against the wall, screaming "CLIMB ON ME, CLIMB, USE ME AS THE ROPE," and he did, boots finding her shoulders, her thighs, her harness, her fury, until his fingers closed over rock at the lip and the village pulled him out into the rain.

Behind him, the cave mouth began to die.

* * *

**ULYANA**

It didn't collapse all at once. That was the terrible, beautiful thing. It collapsed the way a sentence collapses when the speaker loses faith in it—piece by piece, a clause at a time, the arch of the mouth letting go of itself in slabs the size of buses. Each one hit the sea with a boom she felt in her molars. The spray went up white and enormous, higher than the cliff top, and the crowd on the path fell backward from it as one body.

Ulyana lay on her back on the grass, soaked to the soul, lungs full of salt and victory, and started to laugh.

It wasn't heroic laughter. It was the other kind—the unhinged, grateful, oxygen-drunk kind—and it doubled her over while Caspian knelt beside her checking her pupils like she was a piece of equipment he was reluctant to write off.

"You're laughing," he said.

"There's a version of me," she managed, "who stayed in bed this morning. I'd like to buy her a drink sometime. Explain the concept of luck."

Below, the sea finished its demolition. Where the cave mouth had been there was now a raw wound in the escarpment, wepping rubble, and beyond it—through the settling spray, briefly, unmistakably—the exposed ribs of something dark and wooden, thrust up out of the broken rock like a ship's skeleton rising from its own grave.

The wreck. Or what the wreck had become, once the sea cracked open its last hiding place and let the daylight in.

Around them, the rescued divers coughed seawater onto the turf. One of them, the young one, caught Ulyana's eye and said, hoarse: "You came back for us. Vespera wouldn't send anyone. She said the window was too narrow."

"The window," Ulyana said, "was exactly wide enough."

Down on the water, riding at anchor in the dying storm, Vespera Meridian's launch turned slowly toward shore. Through the rain-blurred lens of exhaustion, Ulyana watched it come, and saw the figure at the rail—still, coatless, watching the wreck's bones rise and fall in the swell—and understood, with the flat certainty of a barometer falling, that the cave had taken its fee at last.

It had given the divers back.

But it had given up the wreck. And everything sleeping inside it was about to belong to everyone.

Caspian followed her gaze, and she felt him go very still beside her.

"That hold's open," he said quietly. "Whatever's in it—cameras will see it by nightfall. Vespera's cameras. Everyone's." He swallowed. "Ulyana. Whatever we were going to control—it's out of our hands now."

The launch dropped its tender. The truth was coming ashore, and it was wearing a good coat.
