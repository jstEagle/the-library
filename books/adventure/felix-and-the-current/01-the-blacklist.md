## 1. The Blacklist

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Casper Galloway would later decide was the mountain's idea of a joke. Tuesdays were for small disasters. Tuesdays were when the ferry ran late, when the propane tank sputtered out mid-shower, when clients remembered at the last possible moment that they'd forgotten to mention their heart condition, their fear of water, or their second wife's restraining order.

Fifteen years of Tuesdays had taught him that much.

He was re-caulking the seams of his oldest boat when the mail skiff puttered up to his dock — if you could call three pilings and a plank of gray driftwood a dock — and tossed a bundle wrapped in oilcloth onto the boards. Bills, mostly. A flyer for a chainsaw sharpening service he'd never use. And beneath them, an envelope heavy as a headstone, cream-colored, with a wax seal pressed into the flap like a wound that had been dressed too neatly.

Casper set down his caulking iron and looked at it for a long time.

He knew the seal before he knew anything else about it. You didn't forget a seal like that — a stylized V with a mountain peak threaded through it, the whole thing stamped in deep blue wax that some stationery company in the lowlands probably sold by the thousand to people who wanted to look important. But Mara Voss hadn't bought hers from any stationery company. She'd had it cut custom, years ago, back when they were both young enough and stupid enough to think that a personal crest was a reasonable thing for a river guide to own. He'd teased her about it for a season. *What are you, royalty?* She'd said, without looking up from her maps: *No. I'm thorough.*

That was Mara in one word. Thorough. Thorough enough to build the most respected outfitting company on two rivers. Thorough enough, eventually, to stand in front of a tribunal and say exactly what needed saying to end another guide's career.

His.

Casper wiped his hands on his thighs, leaving two pale streaks of caulk across the canvas, and picked up the envelope the way you'd pick up something that might be sleeping. It was heavier than it looked. Good paper. The kind that costs more per sheet than he charged per hour.

"You're not going to open it," he told himself aloud, because fifteen years alone on the lower Serrin had made a habit of him talking to himself, and because saying it out loud was the only way to make it true.

He opened it anyway. Of course he did. Pride is a strange animal — it will keep a man from apologizing for fifteen years but won't stop him from tearing open a letter from the woman who ruined him, just to see what she wants.

*Mr. Galloway,* the letter began, and even the formality stung. Fifteen years ago she'd called him Casper. Called him worse things, too, usually during rapids, usually with great creativity.

*I'll be brief, because I know brevity is not your natural habitat.*

He huffed. Despite everything — despite the tribunal, the testimony, the two countries that had struck his name from their registries with the tidy finality of a pencil line erased — despite all of it, he huffed. That was the worst part about knowing someone that long. They knew where you lived, figuratively speaking, even after you'd moved.

*I have a client. A significant client, with a significant objective, requiring a significant guide. I am aware of the irony of my writing to you. I am aware of many things. I would prefer to discuss this in person rather than in ink, ink being permanent and my patience being not. I will be at the Ferryman's Rest in Corben's Landing on Thursday evening. If you do not come, I will assume your answer is no, and I will not write again. This is not a threat. It is a courtesy, which I understand we are both short on.*

There was no signature. Just the seal again, smaller, at the bottom — the V with its mountain threaded through it, stamped in blue.

Casper read it three times. Then he folded it along its original creases, walked to the woodstove in the corner of his boathouse, and held it over the fire door for a long moment.

The stove was cold. It was July. He'd have to light it first, and lighting the stove felt like too much commitment to the destruction of the letter, so instead he did what he always did with things he couldn't face: he put it in the tin box under the floorboard, next to the other letters he'd never answered, the photographs he never looked at, and the coil of rope he'd cut off his harness fifteen years ago and never thrown away.

Then he went back to caulking the boat, and thought about nothing at all, very loudly, for four hours.

* * *

The lower Serrin, in the stretch where Casper worked, was a river that had been passed over. Upstream, past the confluence, it turned wild and famous and forbidden — the headwaters locked inside protected land that no licensed guide could touch, guarded by rangers and regulations and the kind of scenery that made grown surveyors weep into their clipboards. Downstream, below his reach, it flattened into barge traffic and mill towns. What remained for Casper Galloway was the middle child of rivers: pretty enough, mean enough to be interesting, and legally accessible, which was the only reason anyone let him near it.

He ran trips for people who couldn't afford better guides or wouldn't hire one with his history. Fishing parties who wanted stories. Corporate retreats whose organizers hadn't done their research. Once, memorably, a wedding party, all of them drunk by the second rapid, the bride paddling in her dress, screaming with joy, and Casper had thought — briefly, dangerously — that maybe this was enough. Maybe marginal was fine. Maybe a man who'd lost everything should be grateful for a river that asked nothing of him except competence.

But the Serrin's lower reaches had a way of reminding him what he wasn't allowed anymore. On clear days, standing on the high bank above the confluence, you could see the upper gorge in the distance — a blue-gray seam in the mountains, the water running silver through it. Every guide on every registry in the country could run that water. Casper could look at it. That was the arrangement.

He'd stopped looking, mostly. Mostly.

Thursday came faster than he wanted it to. He spent Wednesday telling himself he wasn't going, and Wednesday night packing his good jacket, and Thursday morning standing in front of the tin box under the floorboard, deciding whether to bring the letter. He left it. Letters were evidence. Evidence was how careers ended.

Corben's Landing was forty minutes downstream by road and a hundred years behind the rest of the world, which suited everyone who lived there. The Ferryman's Rest sat at the bend where the road met the water, a low building of dark timber with a porch that sagged in the middle like a tired back. Casper had drunk there exactly twice in fifteen years, both times in the far corner with his back to the wall, both times leaving before anyone could ask questions.

He arrived early, because arriving late to Mara Voss would hand her a victory she hadn't earned yet today, and took the corner seat with his back to the wall. Then he realized the corner seat with his back to the wall was exactly where she'd expect him, and moved to a table by the window, and then felt foolish about that too, and finally settled on a stool at the bar like a man with no strategy at all, which was, he admitted, the truth.

She came in at seven o'clock precisely, because of course she did.

Mara Voss had not softened. He'd half expected her to — time was supposed to sand people down, round their edges, teach them mercy. Instead the years had seemed to plan her. Her hair was cut short and practical, silver coming in at the temples like frost claiming high ground. She wore field clothes that cost more than his boat, and she carried herself the way she always had: like a woman who had already measured the room, found its load-bearing walls, and decided which ones to remove.

She saw him at the bar and crossed to him without hurrying. That was its own message. Hurrying would have meant the meeting mattered.

"Casper." She said it carefully, testing the weight of it.

"Mara." He turned on his stool but didn't get up. "You're looking thorough."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, haunting the premises. "You're looking old."

"I'm looking experienced. There's a difference. One of us can still carry a boat."

"One of us pays other people to carry boats."

"There it is," Casper said. "Fifteen years and you haven't changed a bit."

"Fifteen years," she said, "and you haven't answered a single letter until now."

"This is the first one."

"The others were returned unopened. I kept the envelopes. There are eleven." She sat down on the stool beside him, laid her hands flat on the bar, and looked straight ahead at the bottles like a woman praying to a god she didn't believe in. "I counted."

"Why count?"

"Because numbers are honest." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes were the same as he remembered — quick, assessing, already three moves ahead. "You want to know why I'm here."

"I want a drink first."

"Get two," she said. "You'll need the second one."

He got two. The bartender, an old man named Dov who had served Casper exactly twice and pretended to remember neither time, poured without comment and retreated to the far end of the bar with the practiced diplomacy of a man who had seen conversations like this end furniture.

Mara waited until Dov was out of earshot. Then she said, quietly, "Aldous Crane."

The name landed in Casper's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. It took a while.

"Should that mean something to me?" he said.

"It shouldn't," Mara said. "It does. I watched your face."

"My face is old and unreliable."

"Your face hasn't lied to me once in twenty-two years, Casper. It's the only part of you that hasn't." She lifted her glass but didn't drink. "Crane is a mining magnate. He owns more of the northern range than the government does. He's dying — the doctors won't say it plainly, but the doctors don't need to; men like Crane buy second opinions until one of them tells him what he wants to hear, and the ones worth buying all tell him the same thing. Months, not years."

"That's unfortunate," Casper said, in the tone of a man for whom it was entirely fortunate because it meant nothing to him whatsoever.

"He believes there's a cure in the Serrin's source. The glacial spring at the headwaters. There are stories — mineral content, some old survey notes, a chemist in the lowlands who tested a sample thirty years ago and wrote something enthusiastic before he died. Crane believes it. And when a man like Aldous Crane believes something, it stops being a belief and starts being a budget."

Casper turned his glass slowly on the bar. "The headwaters are closed. Protected. No guide with a license can take a client up there, and no guide without a license can take anyone anywhere."

"Yes," Mara said. "Which brings us to the awkward part."

"The awkward part," Casper said, "is that you're asking me."

"The awkward part," Mara agreed, "is that I'm asking you."

He laughed — one short, hard syllable with no humor in it. "You testified against me, Mara. You stood in that room with the flags on the wall and you told them I ignored protocol. You told them I pushed past safe margins. Your testimony is the reason two countries won't let me guide a scout troop across a parking lot. And now you want me to run an illegal expedition into the one place on this river I'm forbidden to go, for a dying man I've never met, for money I don't need, because—"

"Because you're the only guide alive who knows the upper gorge," Mara said, "and you know it because of what happened there, and we both know it, and I am not going to pretend otherwise because pretending is a luxury neither of us has ever been able to afford."

The bar noise went on around them — glasses, laughter, someone feeding coins into a music machine — and none of it touched the space between the two stools where they sat.

"Who else knows?" Casper asked finally. "About Crane. About the trip."

"A crew. Six, maybe seven people. A geologist. Cooks. And Crane is sending his daughter."

"His daughter." Casper's glass stopped turning.

"Wren Crane. Twenty-six. Sharp as a filleting knife, from everything I've heard. She's going in her father's place — his health won't survive the approach, so she goes as his eyes." Mara paused, and here, for the first time, Casper saw something move behind her composure, something that might have been caution or might have been shame wearing caution's coat. "Casper. Before you answer — and I know you're going to say no, you've been saying no since the envelope hit your dock — I need you to hear one thing."

"Say it fast."

"The route we'd take runs through the gorge."

The bar noise receded. The bottles behind the counter blurred. Somewhere very far away, a coin dropped into the music machine and began a song with no melody in it.

"You know I don't go there," Casper said.

"I know you've gone there," Mara said. "Every year. Alone. Don't bother denying it — I've seen the survey markers, Casper. Fresh ones. Your handwriting on the tags. You've been walking through that canyon every autumn for fifteen years like a man visiting a grave, and I let you think nobody noticed, because noticing felt like a cruelty. But now I need what you know, and you need—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, "—maybe you need nothing. Maybe you're fine. Maybe marginal trips and a cold stove and a tin box full of unanswered letters is the life you chose. But I watched you not burn that letter, and I watched you show up tonight anyway, and I'm telling you: triple pay, full crew, my name on the line beside yours. And one more thing."

"What."

"Crane's daughter doesn't know why you were blacklisted. She knows the official version — negligence, hubris, the usual words tribunals like. She may figure out the rest. Or she may not." Mara stood, dropped bills on the bar that covered both drinks and then some, and pulled her collar up against the river damp. "Thursday's nearly over, Casper. Think about it however you like. But think about whether the story those two countries believe is the story you want to die inside."

She walked out. The door swung shut behind her, and the song on the music machine finally found its melody, tinny and cheerful and absurd.

Casper sat for a long time with his untouched drink, watching the window where the river ran dark past the lights of the Landing, running down from places no map invited him, carrying snowmelt from a spring he'd sworn off and a canyon he visited like a penitent every year when the leaves turned.

Triple pay. His name beside hers. A daughter with her father's eyes going up a river that ate her brother's father's son—

He stopped. Sat very still. Turned the thought over slowly, the way you'd turn a stone to see what lived underneath.

*Aldous Crane.* The name had landed like a stone down a well, and he'd waited for the splash, and it had taken a long time, and now he understood why. Because somewhere in the back of his mind, in the dusty archive where he kept the tribunal transcripts he'd never reread, a file had opened on its own. A witness list. Family members of the deceased. And among the names, listed but never called to testify, too young then, too grief-struck to attend:

*Aldous Crane. Father of the deceased.*

Casper put money on the bar for a drink he hadn't finished, walked out into the wet dark, and stood on the sagging porch of the Ferryman's Rest until the cold worked through his good jacket.

He was going to say no. He was already composing the refusal — he'd tell her at the dock, he'd tell her in writing, he'd return her envelope unopened, all twelve of them, he'd—

He was going to say yes. He knew it the way you know weather coming over a ridge: pressure dropping, light changing, no point pretending.

Because the dead man's father was sending the dead man's sister up a river that killed him, and Casper Galloway — blacklisted, disgraced, fifteen years a ghost in his own valley — was apparently the only person alive who could keep her from drowning in the same water.

He walked back to his boat in the dark, took up the floorboard, and lifted the cream-colored letter out of the tin box.

He still didn't burn it. But he read it one more time, by lamplight, and this time he noticed what he'd missed before — a single line at the bottom, beneath the seal, in handwriting too hurried to be Mara's, added after the letter was sealed:

*Ask him about the North Col. Ask him what really happened. — W.C.*

Casper set the letter down very carefully, the way you'd set down something that might be sleeping.

Then he started packing.
