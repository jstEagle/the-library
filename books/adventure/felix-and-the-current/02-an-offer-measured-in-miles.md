## 2. An Offer Measured in Miles

The letter had sat on Casper's table for three days before he worked up the nerve to open it, and it took another two days of staring at the seal — a stylized mountain peak pressed in dark green wax, the kind of wax that cost more per stick than his entire winter grocery budget — before he answered it.

He answered it the way he answered most things: by fixing the porch.

There was a board out front that had been warping since October, curling up at one corner like a page someone had dog-eared and abandoned. He pried it loose, planed it flat, set it back down, and stood there with his hands on his hips as if he'd accomplished something the valley would remember for generations. The truth was that Casper Galloway did his best thinking with a tool in his hand and sawdust in his beard, and the worst possible thing that could happen to a man like him was an empty morning and a letter bearing the seal of Mara Voss.

Because Mara Voss did not write letters. Mara Voss sent invoices, contracts, the occasional postcard from some summit or other with a single line scrawled across the bottom — *You'd have hated it* — designed to lodge under a man's skin like a splinter too deep to dig out. A formal letter, sealed in green wax, meant business. And business, between the two of them, had historically ended with somebody's career in a shallow grave.

So he fixed the porch. Then he re-hung the gate that didn't need re-hanging. Then he split wood nobody needed until the pile threatened to annex the yard, and only when there was nothing left to fix did he sit down at his table with the letter and a cup of coffee gone cold twice over, and read it.

*Galloway,* it began, which was how she'd addressed him even in the years when they'd been something adjacent to friends. *I'm assembling an expedition. Upstream. All the way upstream. I need a guide who knows the Serrin above the confluence better than any map does, because no map does. Triple your going rate. I'll be at the Ferryman on Thursday. Wear something that isn't flannel.*

Casper read it four times. On the fourth reading he noticed what he'd missed the first three: *I'll be at the Ferryman.* She was coming to him. In fifteen years, Mara Voss had come to him exactly never. She summoned; she did not travel. If she was driving two days upriver to sit in a tavern with sticky tables and a bartender who watered the whiskey, then whatever she wanted, she wanted badly.

That should have been reassuring. It was not. It was the feeling of watching a storm cell build over a ridge you were planning to cross — beautiful, in its way, and absolutely a warning.

* * *

The Ferryman occupied the ground floor of a building that had been a trading post, a brothel, a church, and briefly — according to Penn's uncle, who claimed to have been present — a very unsuccessful taxidermy shop. It retained elements of all five vocations: a long bar, a stuffed pike on the wall that appeared to have been mounted mid-scream, and a general atmosphere of forgiveness for whatever a person had come in to forget.

Casper arrived early, wearing flannel, out of principle. He took the corner table with its back to the wall and a view of both doors, because old habits are the last things to drown, and ordered tea he had no intention of drinking.

Mara came through the river door at seven o'clock exactly, shaking rain off a coat that probably cost more than his truck. Fifteen years had done to her what fifteen years do to everyone, but they'd done it politely: a few silver threads at her temples, lines around her eyes that might have been laughter or squinting into wind, and the same way of walking into a room like she was already halfway to the next one.

"Galloway." She sat down without being asked, which was also traditional. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"Both true." She signaled the bartender with two fingers, and when the man brought whiskey she pushed one glass across the table toward Casper with the particular firmness of a woman who had watched him nurse tea through enough difficult conversations to know the ritual. "Drink. You'll want it."

"I'll want it more if I don't."

"Fine." She drank hers instead, all of it, and set the glass down with a click that sounded like a period at the end of a sentence. "I'm putting together an expedition to the source of the Serrin."

Casper felt the words go through him like cold water through a boot. "The source is closed. Protected headwaters. No permits issued past the confluence, not since the Kestrel Act. I've run the lower river for fifteen years and I've never once been tempted—" he paused, honest enough to wince at himself, "—all right, tempted daily, but never once *gone*. Because it's illegal, Mara. Not gray-area illegal. Fines, confiscation, registry-ban illegal. For me specifically, it's *they will finally get to make it permanent* illegal."

"I know what it costs. I priced it in."

"That's not a price. That's a cliff."

"Triple pay," she said. "Plus hazard. Plus a retainer large enough that you could stop running those sad little fishing trips for tourists from the city who call rapids 'the whitewater experience' and tip in gift cards."

"They tipped in a canoe once," Casper said, with the faint defensiveness of a man defending the only dignity available to him. "A good canoe."

"Casper." Her voice dropped, and something in it made the room feel smaller. "I wouldn't ask if I had anyone else. You know that. There are maybe four people alive who've seen the upper gorge and remember it, and two of them are dead, and the third won't return my calls because I ruined his life."

"The third has excellent judgment," Casper said.

"He does," Mara agreed, and for a moment — just a moment — something moved behind her eyes that wasn't ambition. Something older and tireder. "I do. But I'm asking anyway."

"Why?" He leaned forward. "Not why me. Why *this*. Who pays triple rate plus hazard plus retainer to break the law on a river nobody's mapped? What's up there, Mara? What's worth a felony?"

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the roof, and the screaming pike stared at them both with its glass eye, and Casper understood that whatever came next was the actual reason she'd driven two days through weather to say it to his face.

"A client," she said finally. "Wealthy. Dying. He believes there's a spring at the source of the Serrin — glacial melt filtered through mineral seams, some story about waters that restore vitality. His doctors laugh at him. His money doesn't care. He wants to drink from it before the season closes, and he wants it done quietly, and he is paying enough to fund this entire valley's retirement."

Casper sat back. "People die chasing miracle water every year. Usually closer to home."

"This one can afford to die somewhere spectacular."

"No."

It came out flat and fast, faster than he'd planned, and he watched it land on her like a stone thrown at a window. She didn't flinch. She'd taken harder stones than him.

"No?"

"No. I'm sorry you drove all this way. I'm sorry about the whiskey. But I spent fifteen years learning where my line is, Mara, and it runs right along the border of that protected zone. I guide people down rivers. I don't smuggle dying men up them. Find yourself another ghost."

"There is no other ghost." She said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "And I haven't told you the client's name yet."

"You think the name changes anything? Some mining magnate with more money than sense—"

"Aldous Crane."

The name went into Casper like a hook going into a fish — clean, quick, and deep below anything he could reach with his own hands.

He knew the name. Everyone in guiding knew the name. Aldous Crane owned half the claims in the northern ranges and leased the other half, a man whose expeditions were famous less for their summits than for their schedules. But that wasn't why the name landed the way it did.

The reason was younger than Aldous Crane. Twenty-six years old at the end. Fair hair frozen stiff with rime. A man named Tomas Crane, who had died on the North Col fifteen years ago, tied into a rope, and the rope had been Casper's.

The Ferryman went very quiet around him, or perhaps it only seemed to; perhaps it was just his own blood making noise. Mara watched him with the careful stillness of a climber who has just heard the snowpack settle and is waiting to learn whether it settles into place or lets go.

"Tomas's father," Casper said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. He'd have bet on a crack. "The client is Tomas's father."

"Yes."

"And he knows I'd be on the crew?"

"He requested discretion about the crew. Which means either he doesn't know, or he knows and doesn't care, and I genuinely don't know which of those is worse." Mara turned her empty glass slowly on the scarred wood, around and around, a small orbit. "I found out yesterday. I nearly didn't tell you. I want you to understand that. I sat in my truck outside your house for forty minutes deciding whether to drive away."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you'd have found out eventually. You always find out eventually. You're insufferable that way." She stopped turning the glass. "And because I thought — I don't know what I thought. Maybe that you deserved to choose. Maybe that some part of me wanted to see your face when you heard it. Maybe both. I'm not always sure which of my motives are which, Galloway. Never have been."

Casper looked at his hands. They were steady, which felt like a betrayal; hands like his ought to shake when that name came up. Fifteen years ago they hadn't shaken either, not while he cut the rope free, not while he carried what was left of Tomas down the col, not during the inquiry, not during Mara's testimony, delivered in a clear voice that never wavered once. Steady hands. The whole disaster had been conducted by a man with steady hands, and he had sometimes thought that was the cruelest part — that grief and ruin could happen to a body without ever disturbing it.

"He was twenty-six," Casper said. It wasn't an argument. It was just a fact he kept in his chest the way other men kept coins, and it fell out now because the table was full of whiskey and rain and Mara Voss and there was nowhere else for it to go.

"I know."

"He trusted the route. I told him it was good. I'd tested it — I thought I'd tested it. The mountain disagreed, and the mountain got the final word, and I've spent fifteen years listening to that word echo." He rubbed his jaw. Sawdust was still in his beard from the porch. Of course it was. "And now his father wants to hire the man who killed him to haul him up a forbidden river after fairy-tale water."

"Do you believe in the water?"

"No."

"Do you believe in second chances?"

"That's not a real question. That's a question rich men ask so they don't have to ask the real one."

"Then here's the real one." Mara leaned in, and the lamplight caught her face, and Casper saw — really saw, for the first time in fifteen years — how much the North Col had cost her too, however differently she'd paid. "Aldous Crane is dying, Casper. Months, not years. And his daughter is coming on the expedition in his place for the approach — his eyes, his representative, his—" she hesitated, choosing, "—his heir. She's the one who insisted. She's the one who found me. And whether or not there's anything in that spring but pretty rocks and poison, that girl is going up that river with or without a guide who knows the gorge. With or without you."

Casper said nothing. The rain filled the silence for him, patient as ever.

"You can refuse," Mara said. "I'll respect it. I'll even understand it, which will be new for both of us. But understand what you're refusing. You're not refusing a payday. You're refusing to be on that river when she finds out who you are — and she will find out, Casper, because names travel upstream faster than boats. You're choosing whether she hears it from a stranger at ten thousand feet, or from you, at a table, with time to breathe."

"That's manipulation."

"It's arithmetic." She stood, pulled on her expensive coat, and left a fold of bills on the table that covered both whiskeys and then some. At the door she paused, half-turned, rain-wet light behind her. "Thursday. The crew musters at the lower landing. I need your answer by then."

"Mara."

She waited.

"His daughter," Casper said slowly. "What's her name?"

"Wren," Mara said. "Wren Crane."

And then she was gone into the rain, and Casper sat alone at the corner table with his untouched tea and the screaming pike and the sound of a name settling into him like a stone into deep water.

Wren. A small bird that lives close to the ground and sings anyway.

He thought about the porch board he'd planed flat, and how some things can be straightened and some things can only be replaced, and how he'd never learned which was which until it was too late to matter. He thought about Tomas's father, dying by inches in some glass tower, reaching for a spring that Casper would have bet his remaining reputation did not exist — and if it did exist, was almost certainly worse than nothing. He thought about a daughter who had insisted on coming, and what she would do, standing on the lower landing Thursday morning, when she shook the hand of the guide her father's money had bought and read the name off his jacket.

*Casper Galloway.*

He finished the whiskey Mara had poured him. It was, he admitted, good whiskey. Watered-down town, his foot.

Then he went home in the rain, took down the old survey maps he swore every year he'd burn, and spread them across the table under lamplight — the upper gorge, the confluence, the long forbidden miles marked in his own younger handwriting, neat and confident and written by a man who did not yet know what his routes cost.

He told himself he was only looking.

He was already packing.
