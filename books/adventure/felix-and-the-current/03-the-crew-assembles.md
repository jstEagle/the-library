## 3. The Crew Assembles

The thing nobody tells you about dying slowly is how much of it is logistics.

Wren Crane had spent the last four months learning this. Blood draws on Tuesdays. Infusions on Thursdays. A pill organizer the size of a tackle box. Her father's doctors spoke in the gentle, rounded tones of people delivering weather reports for a hurricane: *manageable*, they said. *We can buy you time.* Nobody said the word *cure* anymore, because Wren had asked once, directly, and Dr. Amara had looked at her hands instead of her face, and that was answer enough.

So when her father announced he was sending her upriver as his representative — his eyes, he called it, though she suspected *his alibi* was closer to the truth — she packed a duffel bag and a syringe case and told herself it would be an adventure. Something to do with the time she was buying. A story to tell in whatever waiting room came next.

She had not expected the crew.

---

She met them at a gravel bar forty miles past the last paved road, where two rubber rafts lay deflated like beached whales and a man in a chef's apron was arguing with a propane stove as though it owed him money.

"You're the daughter," said the man with the stove, without turning around. He was broad, sunburned at the neck, and had the air of someone who had been awake since four a.m. out of pure spite toward breakfast.

"Wren," she said.

"Penn." He gestured upstream with a spatula. "That's Rill. My brother. Try not to encourage him."

Rill was crouched at the water's edge, skipping stones across the current with startling accuracy. He was younger than Penn by three or four years, leaner, with the kind of grin that suggested he'd already done something he shouldn't have today and was planning to do it again tomorrow. "You skipped school for this?" he called.

"I finished school."

"Then you skipped *life*. Respect." He threw another stone. It skipped six times. "Penn only got four."

"That's because I have responsibilities," Penn said darkly, to the stove.

Wren decided, provisionally, that she liked them. Provisionally, because her father had warned her about expedition crews the way other fathers warned about strangers with candy — everyone smiles until the money runs out — and because she had learned, these past months, to hold every new thing loosely. Nothing stayed. That was rather the point of her condition.

The geologist arrived next, or rather, Wren realized the geologist had been there all along, sitting so still on a supply crate that she'd taken him for part of the cargo. He was a tall, narrow man with silver-shot hair cropped close and reading glasses pushed up into it, annotating something in a waterproof notebook with a pencil worn down to a stub.

"Dr. Okafor," he said, when he noticed her looking. He did not stand. Standing, she gathered, was for emergencies. "You may call me Okafor. Everyone does. The 'Doctor' is mostly habit."

"What are you writing?"

"The river." He said it the way other people said *the gospel*. "Sediment load, flow rate, bank composition. This stretch has never been properly surveyed. Do you know what that means?"

"That no one's bothered?"

"It means," Okafor said, with the mild delight of a man correcting a child who has just said something charmingly wrong, "that no one has been *allowed* to bother. There is a difference between ignorance and prohibition. One is a gap in knowledge. The other is knowledge of a gap." He tapped his pencil against the notebook. "I intend to close it."

"And you don't mind that it's illegal?"

Okafor considered this over the top of his glasses. "Miss Crane, I am sixty-one years old. I have been arrested twice, both times for being right too early. At my age, legality is a question of pacing, not principle."

Wren laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, unguarded, the first one in weeks that didn't feel like it cost her anything. Okafor looked quietly pleased, as though he'd collected a specimen.

---

Mara Voss arrived by raft from downstream, standing at the stern like a figurehead, and Wren understood immediately why her father paid this woman whatever he paid her. Mara was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, built like rigging — all tension and function — and she surveyed the gravel bar with the expression of an auditor finding a discrepancy.

"You're late," Penn told her.

"I'm exactly on time. You're early. It's different." She stepped off the raft onto dry stone without getting her boots wet, which Wren suspected was a skill you either had or spent your whole life acquiring. Her eyes found Wren and did a fast, thorough inventory — the syringe case at her hip, the pallor under her tan, the way she stood slightly favoring her left side. "Crane's daughter. You'll ride second boat. If I say get down, you get down. If I say paddle, you paddle. Questions?"

"Just one," Wren said. "Where's Galloway?"

Something moved through Mara's face — quick, controlled, gone — like a fish under ice. "He's coming."

"When?"

"When he comes." Mara turned away, which was apparently the Voss method of ending conversations, and began inspecting the rafts' rigging with sharp little tugs.

Rill drifted over to Wren, hands in pockets, wearing the expression of a boy about to share forbidden fruit. "Nobody knows if he'll actually show," he murmured. "Penn says he won't. Okafor says fifty-fifty. I've got twenty bucks on yes."

"On him showing?"

"On him showing *and* being terrifying." Rill grinned. "Mara won't tell us anything about him except his name, which, honestly, tells us plenty. Casper Galloway. The blacklist guy. Two countries. You know the story?"

"I know some of it," Wren said carefully.

"Everyone knows some of it. Nobody knows the same parts." He shrugged. "Fifteen years ago, an expedition went up the North Col — big commercial outfit, famous client, lots of money. Came down one man short. Galloway was lead guide. The story is he pushed on past turnaround time with weather closing in. Client's son died. Galloway walked away and every registry on the continent struck his name. He hasn't led a legal trip since."

"And you'd follow him anyway?"

Rill looked at her with genuine surprise, as though she'd asked whether he'd eat food. "Mate, I'd follow him *because* of that. Think about it. The man made the worst mistake in the history of mountain guiding, in public, and survived it. That's not a coward. That's a textbook with legs." He paused. "Or a monster. Twenty bucks says we find out."

Wren didn't take the bet. She had come up this river, in part, to look a monster in the face herself. Her brother Thomas had died on that expedition. She had been fourteen. Her father had never spoken of it — not once, not ever — and the silence around Thomas had grown so dense over fifteen years that Wren sometimes felt she remembered him wrong, the way you misremember a room after the furniture's been moved.

Aldous Crane had hired Casper Galloway to lead him up a forbidden river. Wren had not yet decided whether that was grief, madness, or strategy. Possibly her father no longer knew the difference. Possibly none of those were different things, at his age, with his diagnosis, staring down the same narrowing corridor his daughter was staring down from the other end.

*Casper Galloway,* she thought. *Let's see what you are.*

---

He came at dusk, walking down the shingle bank out of the tree line with a pack that looked heavier than courtesy allowed and a coil of rope slung across his chest like a bandolier.

Wren knew him instantly, though she'd never seen a current photograph. Fifteen years had put frost in his beard and carved the lines around his eyes deep enough to hold shadow, but he moved like the stories said — economical, silent, each step placed rather than taken. He was not a large man. He didn't need to be. Some people filled space by taking it up; Casper Galloway filled it by making everything around him seem to hold its breath.

The camp went quiet in stages. Penn stopped fighting the stove. Rill stopped mid-throw, a stone still cocked in his hand. Even the river seemed briefly to lower its voice.

"Galloway," Mara said.

"Voss." His voice was low and roughened, a voice used to wind. "Your line's rigged wrong on the bow of the green boat. Cross-loading the carabiner."

"It's rigged fine."

"It's rigged fine until it isn't." He set down his pack, crossed to the boat, and fixed it in eleven seconds without another word. Then he straightened, and his gaze swept the camp — Okafor, the brothers, Mara — and stopped.

On Wren.

It was not the look she was used to. Men looked at Aldous Crane's daughter and saw money, or saw access, or saw a pretty face attached to a powerful name. Doctors looked at her and saw a chart. Her father looked at her and saw, she feared, a clock ticking somewhere behind his own eyes.

Casper Galloway looked at her and saw a ghost.

There was no other word for it. The color left his face in a slow tide. His hand, resting on the gunwale of the green boat, tightened until the knuckles paled, and for a moment — three seconds, maybe four, long enough to count — he simply stared at her as though the river had carried her there from somewhere else entirely. From some other year. From some other life.

Then he blinked, and it was gone, shuttered away behind the same competence that had fixed Mara's rigging, and he said, in a voice gone carefully flat:

"You'll be Crane's daughter."

"Wren."

"I know." And then, as if hearing himself, he added, "Mara briefed me," which was obviously a lie, because Mara had said *he's coming* and nothing else, and Wren watched him know that she knew, and watched him decide not to care.

"Welcome aboard," she said.

He nodded once, turned away, and began checking the second raft's load distribution with the focus of a man defusing something.

---

Dinner was rice and beans with dried chilies, served by Penn with the gravity of a state occasion and eaten by Rill at roughly twice the legal speed. Okafor lectured between bites about glacial flour and river braiding, using a ladle and three tin cups as visual aids. Mara ate alone, apart, studying a map by headlamp.

Galloway sat at the edge of the firelight, eating slowly, saying nothing, and watching.

Not the fire. Not the river. Wren.

She caught him at it three times. Each time he looked away a half-second too late, and each time his expression was the same — not curiosity, not the appraisal of a guide sizing up a client's fitness, though she supposed that was the cover story. It was recognition. It was the look of a man seeing something he had buried come walking out of the ground, dusted off and breathing.

It should have frightened her. Instead, to her own surprise, it made her furious.

Because she knew that look. She'd seen it in mirrors lately, in hospital windows, in her father's face when he thought she wasn't watching. It was the look of a person carrying a body they couldn't put down. And whatever Galloway thought he saw when he looked at her — whoever he thought she was, whose ghost she wore — he had no right to it. He had no right to grieve at her, silently, across a fire, while she sat there alive and annoyed and hungry for more beans.

She carried her bowl over and sat down across from him, deliberately, the way you'd sit down across from a locked door you intended to open.

"Rill says you're a textbook with legs," she said.

Galloway's spoon paused. "Rill talks a lot."

"He also says you might be a monster. He's got twenty dollars riding on the distinction."

"Which way is he leaning?"

"Toward monster. He thinks it pays better."

Something flickered at the corner of Galloway's mouth — not quite a smile, but the archaeological remains of one. "Smart kid."

"You knew my brother."

It came out of her before she'd decided to spend it. Across the fire, Rill's chatter stumbled and died. Penn looked up. Even Okafor's ladle went still.

Galloway set down his bowl with great care, as though it might go off. When he raised his eyes to hers, the ghost-look was back, but closer now, nearer the surface, and beneath it was something raw and old and terribly tired.

"Yes," he said.

"Thomas Crane. You were his guide."

"I was."

"He was twenty-six."

"Yes."

"You've never contacted our family. Never written. Never—"

"No." The word came out quiet and absolute, a door closing on a room she couldn't see into. "No, I haven't."

"Why not?"

For a long moment the fire cracked and spat and the Serrin ran loud and indifferent in the dark, and Casper Galloway looked at her the way men look at water they're deciding whether to cross. Whatever he found in her face — the fury, or the illness she hadn't mentioned, or just the plain unbearable fact of her sitting there where Thomas wasn't — he seemed to arrive at a decision.

"Not here," he said finally. "Not tonight. But you'll get your answers, Wren. All of them. Before this river's done with us." He picked up his bowl again. "I give you my word on that."

It was, she would think later, the first honest thing anyone had said to her in months. It was also, she suspected, a lie of omission wearing honesty's coat — because *before this river's done with us* was not a promise. It was a countdown.

She went to her tent unsatisfied and lay listening to the water, cataloguing what she knew. A guide who broke every rule of decency by never apologizing. A rival who hired him anyway and lied about briefing him. A father who sent his dying daughter upriver with both of them and called it trust.

And a man who had looked at her like a ghost, which meant he was carrying a ghost of his own, which meant —

Wren sat up in the dark.

Her father had never told her the name of the client whose son had died on the North Col. She'd assumed she didn't want to know. She'd assumed the silence was kindness.

But Aldous Crane had hired Casper Galloway. Had sought him out, through Mara, across fifteen years of blacklisting, paying triple rates for a man no one else would touch. And Casper Galloway had looked at Wren Crane and gone white as glacier ice.

*He recognized me,* she thought. *Not my name. My face.*

Somewhere downstream, her father's satellite phone waited in a padded case, and first light was eight hours away, and Wren Crane lay in a tent on the banks of a forbidden river understanding, with cold and perfect clarity, that she had boarded this expedition asking the wrong question.

She hadn't come to learn what Casper Galloway was.

She'd come to learn what her father had done.
