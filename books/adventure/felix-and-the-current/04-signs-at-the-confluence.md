## 4. Signs at the Confluence

Adaeze Okafor had a theory about rivers, which she shared with anyone who made the mistake of sitting next to her at breakfast: a river was just geology learning to gossip. It told you everything — what the mountains were made of, what they'd survived, what they were planning to do next. You only had to listen.

Nobody at camp had made that mistake in three days. She took it as a compliment.

The first week on the Serrin had gone suspiciously well. That was the thing she kept circling back to, turning over like a stone with something underneath it. *Suspiciously.* In her experience — two decades of fieldwork across four continents and one memorable evacuation from a volcano that had lied to her about being dormant — smooth was not a condition nature offered for free. Smooth was a down payment. Something always came due.

But so far: six days of glassy water, cooperative weather, and a crew that had settled into rhythm faster than most. Penn cooked like a man who'd been personally wronged by blandness. Rill hauled gear and quoted terrible poetry. Mara Voss ran the expedition with the crisp efficiency of a woman who alphabetized her anxieties. And Casper Galloway —

Casper Galloway read water the way she read strata. She'd watched him stand at the bow of the lead boat on the second morning, tilt his head slightly, and say, "Sweeping bend past the bluff, shallow on the left, run it right of center," without once looking at the map strapped open on his knee. The map, she'd noticed, was more decoration than tool. He consulted it the way a man consults an old photograph of someone he used to know.

She'd started keeping notes. Not because she suspected anything, exactly. Because she was a scientist, and scientists are professionally incapable of letting an anomaly walk around unobserved.

*Day 3:* Galloway navigated the Sow's Teeth rapids before our maps showed them. Knew the eddy behind the second tooth "from somewhere." Would not elaborate. Rill says he heard him mutter "still there" to a rock.

*Day 4:* Voss asked him about water levels above the confluence. He answered in numbers precise to the inch. Either he has a phenomenal memory or he was here recently. Neither answer is comfortable.

*Day 5:* He hums when he thinks no one is listening. Old mountain songs, the kind guides use to keep a rope team's pace. Wren Crane pretends not to notice. She is bad at pretending.

That last one wasn't an anomaly. That one was just sad, and Okafor found herself filing it under a different heading entirely.

* * *

The confluence announced itself a full kilometer before they reached it — the gray-green water of the Serrin proper meeting the milk-blue melt of a tributary that came down off the high ice, the two currents running side by side like strangers sharing a bench, refusing to mix. It was, Okafor thought, the single most honest thing she'd seen all week. Two histories, clearly visible, not pretending to be one story yet.

Mara called for an early camp on the gravel bar below the junction. "Long day tomorrow," she said, in the tone of a woman who knew precisely how long and had already priced it into everyone's contracts. "Okafor — if you want samples, take your hour now. Light's good."

Light was good. Light was, in fact, the kind of golden, forgiving light that made even scree slopes look photogenic, and Okafor took her kit and went upriver along the tributary's edge with the particular happiness of a woman released from human company into the company of rocks.

The tributary cut through old metamorphic country here — schist mostly, glittering where the bank had slumped, bands of it folded like taffy pulled by hands that had all the time in the world. She knelt at the waterline, filled vials, labeled them, tested pH and conductivity with the small contented rituals of her trade. The water ran cold enough to ache. Upstream, the glacier that fed it hung in its valley like a held breath.

It was on her way back, cutting inland to avoid a stretch of soft bank, that she found the first marker.

She almost didn't register it as anything. A length of rebar driven into the ground, rusted to the color of dried blood, with a faded band of surveyor's tape still knotted at its top — the tape bleached nearly white, frayed to threads. Fifteen years of weather, her eye said automatically, doing the arithmetic of decay the way other people did tips. Maybe twenty. Driven deep, though. Whoever had set it had meant it to stay.

Survey markers weren't strange. Survey markers were her bread and butter; half the reason Mara's client was paying triple rates was the possibility of mineral claims in the upper drainage. But this marker was *here*, nine kilometers above the last legal put-in, well inside the buffer zone where permits ended and the protected headwaters began. No licensed survey had been filed for this stretch in her lifetime. She'd checked. Checking was what she did instead of hobbies.

She crouched beside it and brushed the silt from its base with her thumb. Stamped into the cap, half-eaten by rust but legible:

**C.G. — SURVEY 11 — UPPER SERRIN**

She sat back on her heels.

*C.G.*

Well. Lots of people had those initials. Casper Galloway. Carl Gustavsson, a hydrologist she'd known in Tromsø. Probably a dozen others. The rational part of her brain — the part that had spent thirty years insisting on boring explanations — laid these out neatly like specimens on a tray.

The rest of her brain looked at the tape, at the fifteen years of bleach and rot, and said: *He told us he'd never been above the confluence.*

He hadn't said it in those words, exactly. But on the first night, over Penn's stew, Rill had asked him directly — the way Rill asked everything, with the tactless innocence of a large friendly dog — whether he'd ever run the upper river. And Galloway had looked into the fire for a long moment and said, "Not the way you mean."

Which was either a riddle or a lie, and Okafor had learned that the difference between those two things was usually just how much trouble you were standing in.

She found the second marker forty meters on, half-swallowed by a slide alder thicket. Same stamp. **SURVEY 12.** Then a third, on a low bluff overlooking the braided shallows, and this one came with something extra: a flat stone balanced atop the rebar cap. Deliberately placed. A cairn of one.

People didn't stack stones on working markers. People stacked stones on graves.

Okafor stood there in the gold light with her sample kit heavy on her shoulder and felt the week's smoothness finally show its invoice. The mountain remembered more than its people did. That was the whole trouble with mountains. They kept every receipt.

* * *

She might have said nothing. Honestly, she considered it. It wasn't her business; she was here for the geology, and the geology was magnificent and asked nothing of her except attention. There was a version of the evening where she washed her vials, ate Penn's excellent dumplings, and let the markers rust in peace.

But she was also, by training and temperament, a terrible keeper of other people's secrets, and besides — she wanted to see his face.

She waited until after dinner, until the fire had drawn the crew into its usual orbit: Penn arguing with Rill about whether a dumpling could be overcooked (it could; Penn had done it twice), Mara reviewing the next day's portage with a pencil stub, Wren Crane sitting slightly outside the circle the way she always did, close enough to listen, far enough to leave. Galloway was mending a torn spray skirt by firelight, his big hands moving with a patience that looked practiced rather than natural.

"Casper," Okafor said, settling onto the log beside him. "Question. Purely academic."

His needle paused. "Those are usually the worst kind."

"When you said you'd never been above the confluence — 'not the way I meant' — what way did you mean?"

The fire popped. Down the bar, Rill had begun reciting something about albatrosses, badly.

"I meant," Galloway said carefully, "that the upper river and I have a history. Different from guiding."

"Mm." Okafor nodded slowly, as though this were a reasonable sentence. "And does that history include survey work? Eleven, twelve stations? Rebar caps stamped C.G.?"

She watched it land. She'd expected denial — the quick, easy flinch of a man reaching for cover. What she got instead was worse. He went very still, the way water goes still over something deep, and then he looked at her, and his face did something complicated: not guilt exactly, and not fear. Recognition. Like a man hearing, from a great distance, a song he'd hoped was forgotten.

"Where," he said quietly.

"Along the tributary. Above the confluence, east bank." She kept her voice level, conversational, pitched under the albatross poem. "Fifteen years stale, give or take. Which would put them right around the time you stopped being welcome on this river."

Wren had gone motionless in her seat outside the firelight. Okafor noted it without turning her head. The girl missed nothing and pretended to miss everything; between the two of them, Okafor figured, the crew's collective observation skills were adequate.

"That's impossible," Mara said from across the fire, sharp and quick. She'd been listening too, apparently. Everyone had been listening; that was the trouble with fires. "There's been no permitted survey up there since—"

"Since before my career started, yes," Okafor agreed pleasantly. "And yet."

Galloway set down the spray skirt. For a moment she thought he might actually answer — might say whatever lived behind those markers, the history he'd alluded to with his careful non-words. His mouth opened.

"There's a storm system coming down the valley," he said instead. "We should double-lash the boats tonight."

And that, Okafor thought, was the most damning thing he could possibly have done. A man with nothing to hide talks. A man with something to hide checks the weather.

* * *

She woke once in the night — old habit, the body of a field scientist keeping its own survey schedule — and lay in her tent listening to the river. It was talking, as always. *Cold, fast, patient,* it said. *I was here before your rebar. I'll be here after.*

Through the tent wall she saw a shape move along the gravel bar. Broad shoulders, unhurried gait, heading upstream toward the confluence. Toward the markers.

She should have gone back to sleep. Instead she slipped out into the cold, boots unlaced, and followed at a distance, because she was a scientist and sleep was for people whose anomalies stayed buried.

At the third marker — the one with the balanced stone — Galloway stood for a long time without moving. Then he reached out and touched the cap of the rebar, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder at a funeral. He said something. One word, maybe two, lost under the river's voice. Then he straightened the stone that didn't need straightening, turned, and walked back toward camp, passing within ten meters of her hiding place without seeing her, his face in the starlight looking less like a guide's face than like a man visiting a debt.

Okafor stayed out a while longer, cold soaking through her jacket, thinking.

Here was what troubled her, and it wasn't the illegality. Half this expedition was illegal; that was practically the job description Mara had handed out. It was the *tenderness*. Markers fifteen years old, maintained. A stone kept balanced. A man who came back — she was suddenly certain of it, certain the way she was certain about fold axes — again and again, alone, to drive stakes into ground he was forbidden to walk and then keep them company.

You didn't do that for a crime scene. You did that for a grave.

She got back into her tent, wrote three lines in her notebook by penlight — *markers confirmed C.G.; he knows; he grieves; ask why later* — and was nearly asleep when she heard the other sound.

A zipper. Soft footsteps crossing the gravel, light-footed, deliberate. Then, from the direction of the boats, the faint blue glow of a screen through nylon, held low, cupped against spillage. A camera shutter's whisper of a click. Once. Twice. Three times.

Then silence, and after a while, very faint, the particular chirp of a satellite messenger negotiating with the sky — sending something downstream, where the river couldn't follow.

Okafor lay in the dark with her eyes open.

Six people in this crew, and one of them had photographed a dead man's markers in the middle of the night and mailed the mountain's memory to somebody below. She didn't know who. She didn't know why. She only knew that the smooth first week was officially over, and that come morning, she was going to start watching her fellow humans as closely as she watched the rocks.

The rocks, at least, never lied. They just waited.

Upstream, the glacier held its breath. Somewhere below, a message crawled south through the dark at the speed of satellites, carrying fifteen-year-old initials toward eyes that had paid a great deal of money to learn exactly where Casper Galloway had been — and, perhaps, exactly what he'd left behind at Survey Eleven.

The river, Okafor thought drowsily, had tried to warn them. Rivers always do. Nobody listens.
