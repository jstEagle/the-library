## 5. Mara's Ledger

Mara Voss kept her ledgers the way other people kept diaries. Not the financial ones — those lived in a locked drawer and were boring as gravel — but the private ones, the ones with columns that had nothing to do with money. Debts owed. Debts paid. Debts that could never be settled by any currency she trusted.

She was adding to the third column when the letter came back.

*Accepted,* Casper Galloway had written, in handwriting like a rockslide — all downhill momentum and no punctuation to speak of. *Triple pay. But I pick the route, I pick the pace, and if I say turn around, we turn around.*

Mara read it twice, then set it on her desk beneath the brass lamp she'd bought with her first real guiding fee, fifteen years ago, on a night when she'd still believed ambition was a clean thing.

*He said yes.* She should have felt triumphant. Instead she felt the particular nausea of a gambler who has just realized the pot is bigger than the game.

She opened the ledger to the page she visited perhaps twice a year, the one with the date written at the top in ink gone brown: *North Col. October 14.*

The column headings were simple. *What happened. What I said happened. What actually happened.*

Under *what happened*, she had written, years ago: *Rope team caught in serac fall. One dead. Guide error.*

Under *what I said happened*, in testimony before the inquiry board, voice steady as a level: *Galloway pushed the schedule. He ignored conditions. I warned him.*

And under the third heading — the one that mattered, the one she'd only ever written once, in a hotel room in Kathmandu at three in the morning with her hands still shaking — she had written: *I was fifty meters below him when the call came down. He asked me to confirm the ice. I told him it looked good from where I stood. It did not look good from where I stood. I wanted the summit more than I wanted to be sure.*

That was the sentence. That was the whole of it, the thing she had never told anyone, not the board, not the journalists who'd circled the disaster like gulls, not the priest she'd spoken to exactly once afterward and never again. Casper had taken the blame for pushing the schedule — and he *had* pushed it, that part was true, he'd been hungry and certain and twenty-nine years old — but Mara had fed his certainty with a lie of omission, a two-second answer on a radio that would have cost her nothing to give honestly and cost everything given otherwise.

Tomas Crane had died because two ambitious people had each made one small selfish decision, and only one of them had been standing close enough to the wreckage to be blamed.

Casper had never once defended himself. That was the detail that had haunted her longest, the splinter under the skin of her career. At the inquiry he'd sat there with his big ruined hands folded and said, *My rope, my call, my responsibility*, and nothing else. No mention of the radio exchange. No mention of her. She'd waited for him to say her name the way you wait for a tooth to finally crack, and he never had, and some part of her had hated him for it — for making her the sole keeper of a secret she'd have gladly shared into halves, into quarters, into dust.

She'd testified against him anyway. Cleanly, professionally, with the calm of a woman who understood that the truth she possessed was not the truth the board needed, and that her own survival required Casper Galloway to remain the villain of record. His registry status revoked in one country. Blacklisted in the second when he'd tried to keep working under a partner's license. And Mara Voss had risen, year by year, expedition by expedition, until her name opened doors and her signature moved money and clients said things like *she gets people up mountains that shouldn't be climbable* — always leaving off the end of the sentence, which was *because she knows exactly what she's willing to trade.*

Her assistant buzzed. "Ms. Voss? Aldous Crane's office on line two."

"Tell them I'll call back."

"He says it's about the daughter."

Mara closed the ledger. Some debts you paid in installments; this one she was about to pay all at once, with interest, upstream, in a gorge she'd sworn off the way you swear off anything that has already taken a piece of you.

* * *

Wren Crane arrived at the staging warehouse unannounced, which Mara respected, and carrying a duffel she'd clearly packed herself, which surprised her. The photographs hadn't done her justice — or rather, they'd done her too much justice, all polished and posed beside her father's mining interests, when the actual young woman standing in the doorway was thin in the way that illness thins people, sharp-eyed, with her brother's exact jaw.

That jaw stopped Mara for half a second. Fifteen years, and the dead man's face kept reassembling itself out of living relatives like a ghost with excellent bone structure.

"You didn't tell my father you were hiring Galloway," Wren said. Not a question.

"I told him I was hiring the best guide available for the upper Serrin." Mara gestured her inside, past crates of dehydrated rations and Okafor's rock samples already labeled in three colors of tape. "Galloway is the best guide available for the upper Serrin. These facts are adjacent but distinct."

"My father hates him."

"Your father blames him. Hating requires engagement." Mara poured coffee from the industrial urn, offered a cup, wasn't surprised when it was refused. "Your father also wants to live, and wanting to live makes men flexible about their grudges. You'll find that's true of most of us."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Mara had been assessed by bankers, generals, and one very memorable customs official in Ushguli, and this sick, stubborn girl in her mid-twenties assessed her faster than any of them.

"Why do you want him on this trip?" Wren asked. "Really. You ended his career. Everyone in the industry knows the story. So either you need something only he knows, or—"

"Or," Mara agreed pleasantly, "I'm engineering an elaborate reconciliation over four hundred kilometers of Class IV whitewater. Yes. That's exactly what this is. Pack a warmer jacket."

Something flickered across the girl's face — not amusement, quite, but its cousin. Then it hardened again. "He won't talk to me. About Tomas. I've tried."

"Has it occurred to you," Mara said, and heard her own voice go careful, "that there may be things he can't tell you because they aren't his to tell?"

Whose were they, then? Hers. The thought arrived with its usual taste, copper and cold. She drank her coffee to drown it.

"The crew assembles Thursday," she said instead. "Okafor you'll like — she thinks rocks gossip. Penn and Rill cook better than anyone has a right to expect on river trips, though Rill talks the entire time and Penn communicates exclusively through bread. Galloway will arrive late, alone, and stand at the edge of the light like something the river coughed up. Be kind to him or don't, but don't lie to him. He can smell it. Occupational hazard of having been lied about."

"And you?" Wren said. "Should I lie to you?"

Mara smiled with every muscle she owned. "You can try."

* * *

Thursday night, after the others had bedded down among the crates, Mara sat in the warehouse office with the ledger open and added a new column to the North Col page. She titled it *What I'm doing about it now*, which was optimistic phrasing for what was essentially a confession with logistics attached.

Item one: hire Galloway. Done, against every instinct that said keeping your ghosts employed was how they got strong enough to make demands.

Item two: get to the source. The client wanted water; the syndicate behind the client wanted claims; Mara wanted — and here her pen had hovered for a long time — Mara wanted the first legal-free descent of the upper gorge, the one route no guide had run since the protections went up, the feather that would crown a career already heavy with them. She knew what that hunger was. She'd watched it kill a man once, wearing Casper's face and her own silence.

Item three, written smaller: *tell him.*

She looked at item three for a long time. Fifteen years of looking at versions of it. There was a version of her life where she'd told him at the inquiry, and lost everything, and deserved to. There was a version where she'd told him at some reunion, drunk, and been forgiven cheaply, which would have been worse. And there was this version, where she told him on a river, with witnesses, at altitude, where neither of them could walk away cleanly.

It was cowardice dressed as strategy. She wrote it down that way, because the ledger only worked if she refused to flatter herself in it.

A sound at the door. Rill, the younger cook brother, hair full of packing straw, blinking at her.

"Ms. Voss? Sorry — saw the light. Is it true we're going past the protected line? Like, actually past it? Because Penn says if we get arrested he's blaming you specifically and I said that seems fair but I'd personally blame you slightly less—"

"Rill."

"—also is it true Galloway got blacklisted for killing someone, because Okafor says the story's more complicated and Okafor's usually right about complicated—"

"Rill." Mara closed the ledger. "Yes, yes, and ask Okafor. Go to sleep."

He went, cheerfully, and Mara sat alone with the lamp and the ledger and the honest arithmetic of her life. Somewhere north of here, a river ran through a gorge she had mapped in another life, back when she and Casper had been rivals in the friendly sense — racing each other down the lower Serrin for bragging rights, splitting a bottle of terrible wine, arguing about rope anchors until dawn. Back when the worst thing between them had been competition.

She had needed his knowledge of the upper gorge. That was the true reason, the one beneath the syndicate's money and the client's desperation and even beneath her own ambition, if you dug far enough: no one alive knew that canyon like Casper Galloway, because no one else had ever come back from it twice — once with a body, and once, in the years after, alone, though he'd never admitted that to anyone. Mara had seen the survey markers. Stale ones, fifteen years old, in places no licensed guide had ever legally stood. He'd gone back. Again and again, by the look of it. She understood that kind of returning. She'd done it herself, every time she opened this ledger.

The difference was that he'd carried his guilt up the gorge alone, and she'd built a career on top of hers and called it a life.

Mara uncapped the pen. Under item three, she crossed out *tell him* and wrote, in smaller letters: *tell him when it can't be avoided. Not before. Not unless the river forces it.*

Then, because the ledger demanded honesty even when she couldn't provide it: *coward.*

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the warehouse smelled of rope and diesel and the green cold smell of the sample bottles Okafor had sealed, water from the lower Serrin, harmless, ordinary, waiting for its famous cousin upstream.

Three days later, at first light, the crew assembled at the put-in, and Casper Galloway came down the bank exactly as predicted — late, alone, a shape resolving out of the mist with a boat on his shoulder — and stopped when he saw Wren Crane, and went very still, in the way of a man seeing a face he'd spent fifteen years trying not to remember.

Mara watched him watch her, and felt the ledger open itself in her chest, and thought: *not yet. Not yet. But soon, whether I choose it or not.*

Upstream, the river waited, patient as arithmetic, ready to collect.
