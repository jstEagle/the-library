## 6. The First Portage

Penn had cooked for oil rigs, research stations, and one memorable summer aboard a crab boat where the head chef had been a seagull with opinions. He knew how to feed people in bad conditions. What he did not know — what nobody had put in the brochure — was that he'd be doing it at the top of a rockslide while the sky came apart like wet paper.

"Portage starts here," Casper said, standing at the base of the chute with his hands on his hips, looking up at three hundred meters of tumbled granite the way a man looks at an old dog that has bitten him before. "We haul the boats over the slide, run the flat water above, and camp below the narrows."

"That's not a portage," said Rill, Penn's younger brother by four years and several pounds of misplaced confidence. "That's a pilgrimage."

"It's both," Casper said. "The difference is whether you complain."

Rill opened his mouth to complain, thought better of it, and closed it again. This was becoming a pattern with everyone around Galloway. The man didn't talk much, but when he did, it landed like a dropped anchor — and then everybody stood there in the silence after, adjusting to the new depth.

Penn shouldered his pack, which contained, among other things, four days of food for seven people, a cast-iron skillet he refused to apologize for, and a jar of pickled onions that had already saved morale twice. He was the cook. He was also, as far as he could tell, the only member of this expedition whose secrets were limited to the location of the good chocolate.

* * *

The weather turned an hour into the haul.

It didn't turn dramatically, at first. It turned the way rivers do — a darkening upstream, a smell like coins and cold iron, a wind that arrived without any of the usual courtesy of warning. Okafor looked at the clouds and said something in Yoruba that Penn suspected was not a compliment, then started double-checking the lashings on the gear sleds.

The boats were the problem. Two catarafts and a supply raft, all of them heavier than sin and roughly as cooperative. The crew formed a chain — Mara at the front calling moves, Wren and Okafor on the lines, the brothers pushing from behind, Casper wherever the work was worst, which seemed to be everywhere at once, moving between positions with a quiet economy that made Penn think of water finding its level.

By midday they had gained maybe half the slide, and the first rain hit like gravel thrown by an angry neighbor.

"Hold!" Mara shouted, and the line froze, boats groaning against their rollers, everyone leaning into the slope with their boots skidding on wet stone.

Above them — well above them, but not so far above that Penn's imagination couldn't close the gap — a section of the slide sat stacked like a badly built chimney. Loose blocks, frost-cracked, sitting at the angle that geologists call "unstable" and cooks call "nope." Water was running through it now, threading down through the gaps, and every minute the rain got more confident.

"We make the shelf," Casper called up to Mara. His voice carried without effort, the way some voices do, like they've been trained by canyons. "Twenty minutes. Then we wait out the worst of it under the overhang."

"The boats don't fit under the overhang," Mara said.

"The boats stay lashed. We go."

For a moment the two guides looked at each other across the slope, rain streaming off them, and Penn saw something pass between them that wasn't about boats at all. It was old and complicated and had edges. Then Mara nodded once, sharp, and the line moved.

They made the shelf with ten minutes to spare, which is the kind of margin that feels generous until you learn what it was margin against.

* * *

The overhang was a slab of granite leaning out over the river like a bored giant's brow, and beneath it the crew huddled in a row, steaming, passing around Penn's thermos of ginger tea because he'd shoved it into Rill's hands at the last second with the instruction *drink this or become unbearable*. Rill had chosen tea. Barely.

The storm committed itself fully now. Rain went sideways. The river below swelled from green to brown in the space of twenty minutes, and somewhere upstream there was a long, deep report — not thunder, exactly. More geological. A mountain clearing its throat.

Okafor had her notebook out, writing in it with the focused calm of a woman who found comfort in data. Wren Crane sat closest to the edge, watching the water with an expression Penn couldn't read — hungry, almost. Like the flood was telling her something she needed to hear.

Casper wasn't under the overhang.

He was out in it, thirty meters along the shelf, checking the boat lashings himself despite having assigned that job twice already. He moved through the downpour like it was a thing he'd negotiated with personally, some private treaty the rest of them weren't party to. He checked each line, tested each knot, and when he found one he didn't trust, he re-tied it in the rain with fingers that didn't seem to notice the cold.

"He does that," Mara said quietly, to no one in particular. "Trusts his own hands over everyone else's."

"You let him?" Wren asked.

"I hired him," Mara said, which was not an answer, and everyone heard it not be one.

Rill, being Rill, decided the lull required filling. "So what's the story with this guy? I mean — we all know the shape of it. Blacklisted twice. Won't say why. Hired by the woman who—" He caught himself, glanced at Mara, and finished with the grace of a man falling downstairs slowly, "—who knows him professionally."

"Rill," Penn warned.

"What? Everyone's thinking it. I'm just the one who says things out loud. Somebody has to. It's a public service."

"It's a public menace," Penn said, but he was curious too, and he wasn't going to pretend otherwise. Fifteen years was a long time for a man to carry a silence that heavy. You could see the weight of it in how Casper stood — slightly forward on his feet always, like a man perpetually about to catch something falling.

Mara didn't answer. She drank her tea and watched the rain, and her face did something careful and closed, like a ledger being shut.

Wren stood up. "I'm going to help with the lines."

Nobody pointed out that Casper had already checked them twice. Nobody except Rill, who opened his mouth, received a look from his brother, and converted whatever he'd been about to say into a loud appreciation of pickled onions.

* * *

It happened forty minutes later, when the storm paused for breath and Mara called the move to finish the haul.

Later, Penn would replay it frame by frame in his head, the way you do with moments that rearrange your understanding of a person. The way the line went taut. The way Rill — pushing the supply raft from behind, boots on wet rock, taking the slope too casually because Rill took everything too casually — stepped onto a block that had been waiting all day for exactly this moment.

The block moved. Not much. A hand's width, a grinding shrug of granite settling into a new opinion about its position. But Rill's foot went with it, and then Rill went with it, sliding toward the chute — the gap in the slide where everything loose funneled down toward the river, a stone throat maybe four meters wide, polished smooth by decades of floods.

He didn't even have time to yell properly. It came out as half a shout and half a question, like he was asking the mountain if it was serious.

Casper was fifteen meters away. Penn would swear to that later — fifteen meters, uphill, in the rain, with a rope in his hands. And then Casper wasn't fifteen meters away. He was simply *there*, the way certain things are simply true, dropping the rope, crossing the wet rock in a sprint that shouldn't have been possible, and diving flat onto his stomach at the lip of the chute with one arm buried in it to the shoulder.

The sound Rill made when the arm caught him — a grunt, a jolt, the whole chain of it jerking tight — would live in Penn's ears for a while. So would the image: Casper anchored by nothing but friction and will, boots braced against a boulder, holding two hundred pounds of little brother above a drop that ended in brown water and worse rocks.

"Don't move," Casper said. Not loudly. Almost gently. "Don't try to help me. Just stop being heavy for a second."

"I'm trying," Rill wheezed, "to be *light*-hearted—"

"*Rill.*"

"Stopping. Stopping now."

Okafor got a rope to them in seconds — she was fast, faster than anyone credited — and they hauled Rill out together, Casper climbing up last with the unhurried movements of a man getting off a bus. He was breathing hard. That was the only evidence the effort had cost him anything. No shaking hands. No wide eyes. Just a man who had done a thing and filed it away.

"Everyone accounted for?" he asked Mara.

"Everyone."

"Then we finish the haul. Storm window's closing."

And that was it. That was the whole ceremony. No speech, no trembling heroism, no joke to cut the tension. He picked up the rope he'd dropped and walked back to his position in the line, and Penn stared after him with the skillet of his mind thoroughly scrambled.

Because here was the thing Penn kept turning over that night, and the next morning, and while stirring dinner: honest guides are good. Great ones are great. But nobody moves like that. Nobody crosses fifteen meters of wet, sloping, murderous rock in under three seconds unless they have done it before — many times, in worse places, for reasons that didn't end well. Reflexes like that aren't trained. They're *survived into*.

"No honest guide should be able to do that," Penn said to Okafor, quietly, by the fire.

Okafor stirred her tea. "No," she agreed. "Which suggests our guide is either dishonest or extraordinary."

"Can't he be both?"

"Most interesting people are." She looked across the fire at Casper, who sat slightly outside the circle of light, mending a strap, always slightly outside it. "The question is which one we're traveling with. And I suspect," she added, tapping her pen against her notebook, "that Miss Crane intends to find out."

* * *

Wren did intend to find out. Penn could see it happening over dinner, the way you watch weather build across a valley.

She waited until Rill was fed and bandaged and retelling his own rescue with escalating heroism ("and then Galloway says, *stop being heavy* — which, honestly, rude"), until the fire had settled into its comfortable middle age, until Casper rose to check the boats a third time even though the storm had passed and the stars were out and there was nothing left to check.

She followed him. Of course she followed him. Penn pretended to wash pots and listened with the shameless attention of a man who had raised a younger brother and therefore possessed no remaining illusions about his own discretion.

"You could have told us to rig a belay," Wren said. Her voice carried in the cold air, low and even. "Before the haul. Through that chute. Any competent lead would have."

"It's a Class 2 scramble."

"It's a meat grinder with a river at the bottom, and you knew it. You looked at it this morning like you knew it personally." A pause. "You moved like someone who's pulled bodies out of places like that."

Silence. Long enough that Penn, elbow-deep in dishwater, winced.

"I moved like someone who didn't want to fill out paperwork," Casper said finally.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you get."

Wren's voice changed — softer, and somehow more dangerous for it. "My brother used to guide. Did Mara tell you that?"

Penn heard it then: the small sound of a man's whole body going still. Not a flinch. Stillness. The stillness of a held breath, of a rope coming taut, of a block deciding whether to move.

"No," Casper said. "She didn't tell me that."

"Well. He did. River country, mostly. He loved it the way you love it — I could see it today, when you caught Rill. That wasn't a job. That was—" She stopped herself, and when she continued, her voice had gone carefully neutral, which in Penn's experience was what grief sounded like when it wore a uniform. "Anyway. He died on a guided trip. Bad call by the lead. So I notice these things. I notice when a guide makes calls nobody else would make. I notice when he won't explain them."

"Wren."

"Why won't you just *say* it? Whatever it is. Whatever happened to you. Because I've been watching you for six days, Mr. Galloway, and everything you do is a confession wearing a locked door."

Another silence, longer than the first. Down at the boats, the river ran loud and indifferent, carrying snowmelt from a place none of them had ever seen.

"Get some sleep," Casper said. "Big day tomorrow."

Penn heard Wren walk back toward the fire, slow, unsatisfied, coiling. And he heard Casper stay where he was a long time afterward, alone in the dark with the boats and the river, standing guard over nothing anyone could see.

Penn dried the last pot, looked up at the stars, and thought: *well, that's not going anywhere good.*

He was right. By breakfast, Wren had stopped asking questions out loud — which was how Penn knew they'd gotten serious. And when Casper gave the morning briefing, pointing upstream toward the narrows and the canyon beyond, Wren watched him with an expression Penn recognized from his brother's face whenever Rill was about to do something spectacularly unwise:

the look of someone who had decided the truth was worth any price the river charged for it.
