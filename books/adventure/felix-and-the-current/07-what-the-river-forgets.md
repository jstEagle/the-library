## 7. What the River Forgets

The fire was Penn's idea, which surprised no one, because everything warm in camp was Penn's idea.

"We're a week upstream of nowhere," he announced, stacking driftwood with the ceremony of a man building an altar. "My grandmother had a rule: past the third night without a roof, you owe the fire a story. Otherwise it gets bored. And you do not want a bored fire."

"It's a fire," said Rill, who was Penn's younger brother by four years and his opposite in every measurable way, including enthusiasm.

"It's *our* fire," Penn said, "and it's listening."

Wren pulled her sleeping bag around her shoulders like a cloak and took the spot Okafor had cleared for her on a flat stone — the good stone, the one without the sneaky rock underneath that had been ambushing people all evening. She'd noticed that about the crew already. They watched out for each other in small, unglamorous ways. Okafor saving seats. Rill quietly refilling water bottles that weren't his. Penn arranging the whole evening so nobody sat alone at the edge of the light.

It made lying to them harder. Not that she'd told any lies yet. She'd just declined to mention a few load-bearing facts, like the tumor in her skull and the reason her father had bought this entire expedition like a man buying a lottery ticket shaped like a river.

Casper Galloway sat farthest from the flames, as he did every night, at the exact distance where the warmth still reached but the light didn't quite claim him. He was cleaning a stove part with a rag gone gray from use, turning it over and over, not because it needed cleaning but because his hands wanted employment while his face pretended to be elsewhere.

"So," Penn said, settling cross-legged with the satisfaction of a man who has successfully trapped everyone within earshot. "Stories. I'll start, since I'm generous." He pointed the poker at Rill. "Rill once set fire to a hotel kitchen making toast."

"That is slander."

"There were flames, Rill."

"There was a *controlled* flame situation—"

"The fire department came."

"They came because they were *invited*—"

"Invited," Penn repeated, delighted, turning to the group. "He invited them. With a phone call. While holding a pan."

Okafor laughed into her tea. Even Mara, who had spent the day doing ledger arithmetic in her head and treating the rest of them like line items, allowed one corner of her mouth to move. Wren found herself laughing too — really laughing, the kind that hurt a little afterward, in the place behind her eyes where the headaches lived. She stopped quickly, before anyone could notice her hand drifting up.

Then Penn aimed the poker across the fire like a compass needle finding north.

"Galloway. Your turn."

The rag went still.

"Pass," Casper said.

"You can't pass. House rules. I just invented them, and they're binding."

"I've heard your rules are more like suggestions," Okafor offered mildly.

"My rules are iron law," said Penn, "except when they're inconvenient."

Casper put the stove part down. For a moment Wren thought he might actually get up and walk into the dark, which she suspected was his preferred method of ending conversations. But then something shifted — maybe the firelight, maybe tiredness, maybe just the strange democracy of a circle — and he stayed.

"What do you want to know," he said. Not a question. A door held open exactly one inch.

Penn blinked, clearly having prepared for resistance rather than compliance. "Uh. The blacklist. Everyone knows the shape of it — two countries, fifteen years, the whole legend. Nobody knows the middle."

"There isn't much middle."

"There's always a middle."

Casper looked into the fire. Wren watched him the way she'd been watching him all week — the way you watch a river eddy, trying to read what's underneath. He gave them the version, and she could hear how rehearsed it was, smooth as river stone from being turned over in his mouth for fifteen years.

"There was an expedition," he said. "High mountain. A client who wanted the summit on a schedule, because schedules sell. Weather window closed early. I called the route. My call was overruled." A pause, measured out like rope. "A man died. The inquiry needed somewhere to put the weight. It landed on me. Two registries, two bans. End of story."

Silence. Even the fire seemed quieter.

"And the client?" Okafor asked softly. "The one with the schedule?"

"He paid his fines," Casper said. "Sent flowers to the funeral. That's what money does. It converts tragedy into paperwork."

Something in the way he said it — flat, finished, a lid screwed down tight — made Wren's chest ache in a way she didn't examine. Because she knew that voice. She used it herself, every time someone asked why a woman her age moved through the world so carefully, why she drank her coffee decaf ("heart thing," she'd say lightly, "runs in the family"), why she'd left her last job, her last apartment, a boy named Tomas who'd deserved better than a note on the counter.

*There isn't much middle.* God, she knew that sentence. She'd written it in a dozen dialects.

"Your turn," Penn said, swinging the poker toward her, mercifully oblivious. "New blood pays double. House rules."

"Oh, I'm sure they're iron law," Wren said, and got a snort out of Rill.

She should have deflected. She was good at deflecting; she'd had practice, years of it, deflecting doctors and relatives and her father's hopeful, hungry glances when some new treatment appeared in his inbox. She had a whole toolkit: the joke, the shrug, the swift pivot to someone else's drama.

But the fire was warm, and the river was talking to itself in the dark below the bank, and Casper Galloway was looking at her across the flames with those gray eyes that missed nothing — eyes that had been watching her all week like she was a ghost, though she couldn't imagine whose.

And maybe it was the altitude. Maybe it was Penn's stupid beautiful rule. Maybe it was just that she was tired of carrying the thing alone, even if she could only set it down for a minute.

"My father sent me here to be his eyes," she said. "That part's true. What I haven't mentioned—" Her hands found the hem of her sleeve, worried it. "Is why he needs eyes. Why he needs a miracle spring in the first place."

The circle went very still.

"I'm sick," Wren said. It sounded strange out loud, in the open air, instead of in a beige room with a poster of the lymphatic system. "Slow sick. The kind with a timeline the doctors draw as a curve going the wrong direction. Surgery got most of it two years ago. What's left doesn't respond to anything with a price tag, and my father owns enough price tags to buy a small country, so you can imagine how that's going for him." She managed a smile. "Hence the river. Hence the source. Hence me, playing scout for a cure."

Nobody spoke. Penn had lowered the poker. Rill had stopped pretending not to listen.

Okafor reached over and squeezed her wrist — brief, professional, exactly right. "Thank you for telling us."

"Don't make it weird," Wren said, because if she didn't say it, she'd cry, and she had a rule about crying in front of people she'd known less than a month. "I'm still faster than both of you on the portage lines. I checked."

"She is," Rill confirmed solemnly. "She lapped Penn."

"I was pacing myself—"

"You were *lapping*—"

The laughter came back and broke the tension like ice going out of a river in spring, and Wren breathed again. This was the trick, she thought. You told the truth and then immediately buried it under humor so everyone could pretend it hadn't happened. She'd been doing it for two years. It worked fine.

Except.

Across the fire, Casper hadn't laughed. He was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name at first, and then she could, because she'd seen it in mirrors: recognition. The specific, terrible relief of one liar meeting another and realizing the disguise is identical.

When the crew began to drift toward their tents — Okafor first, ever disciplined; the brothers bickering their way up the bank; Mara vanishing without goodnight, as was her custom — Wren stayed. She told herself it was for the warmth.

Casper stayed too. Of course he did.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, an animal — probably a marmot, possibly a demon — made its opinions known.

"Curve going the wrong direction," Casper said finally.

"Mm."

"How long does the curve give you?"

"Longer than I used to think. Shorter than I'd like." She poked the coals. "You?"

He looked at her.

"The blacklist," she said. "Fifteen years. That wasn't the whole story either. I've gotten good at hearing the difference between a story and a container for one."

For a moment she thought he'd shut the door. His jaw did the thing it did — the tightening, the retreat behind the gray. Then:

"The client with the schedule," he said slowly, "had a son on the rope team."

The fire popped. Wren went cold despite the flames.

"He died on the route I'd condemned," Casper said. "And the father — the man who overruled me, who pressured the lead guide, who built the schedule — stood at the inquiry with lawyers and grief, and everyone found it easier to blame the guide than the money." He turned the stove part over in his fingers, though he'd stopped pretending to clean it minutes ago. "So yes. There's a middle. I just don't tell it, because telling it changes nothing except who hates whom."

"Who was he?" Wren asked. "The client."

"Why?"

"Because I know that look you give me. All week. Like I'm someone you buried." She kept her voice level, though her heart had begun to run. "I know half the wealthy men in three provinces through my father. If he's who I think he is—"

"Don't," Casper said. Quiet, but with an edge that stopped her cold. Not anger. Something rawer. "Not tonight. Tonight the fire got its stories. Let it be satisfied."

They sat there, two liars by a dying fire, each holding a truth like a hot coal passed back and forth, neither willing to drop it and neither able to hold it long.

"You should sleep," he said at last. "We make the confluence tomorrow. Long day."

Wren rose, wrapped her bag tighter, and took three steps toward her tent before stopping. She didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the version you told them — the smooth one, the river-stone one? I believed it for about four seconds. Then I remembered I'm a professional."

Behind her, almost too quiet for the river to cover, she heard him exhale. It might have been a laugh. It might have been surrender.

"Goodnight, Wren."

She crawled into her tent and lay awake a long time, listening to the Serrin talk to itself in the dark, wondering what her father would say if he knew she'd shown her cards to a blacklisted guide — and wondering, with a colder curiosity, what Casper Galloway knew about Aldous Crane that he refused to say.

Upstream, the river forgot nothing. She was starting to suspect neither did he.

* * *

She woke near dawn to voices at the bank — low, urgent, Mara's clipped tones and Casper's lower ones. Through the tent's thin wall she caught only fragments.

"...markers," Mara was saying. "Old ones. *His* old ones. Okafor found them above the legal line."

A pause. Then Casper, flat as slate: "How far above?"

"Far enough that someone photographed them before I could stop it. Someone on this crew sent word downstream."

Wren lay perfectly still in her sleeping bag, heart tapping against her ribs, and understood that whatever honesty the fire had bought them last night, the river had already come to collect.
