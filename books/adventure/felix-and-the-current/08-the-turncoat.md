## 8. The Turncoat

Rill Penn had three rules for life on a river, and he'd learned all of them from his brother.

Rule one: never trust a campfire that lights on the first match. Rule two: always cook more rice than you think you need, because hunger makes liars of everyone. And rule three — the one Penn repeated so often it had worn grooves in Rill's brain like water over stone — *when people stop talking to each other, start counting the food.*

It was the rice that tipped him off.

They were nine days up the Serrin, camped on a gravel bar below the second gorge, and Rill had noticed over the past three dinners that the pot was coming up light. Not dramatically light. Not steal-your-grandmother's-silverware light. Just a cup here, a cup there — the kind of shortfall you could blame on a miscalculated ration, except Rill did not miscalculate rations. Rationing was the one mathematics he was good at, and he was very good at it. He'd been feeding two mouths since he was eleven years old, when their mother's restaurant went under and Penn had decided they'd eat fine or die trying, preferably fine.

So somebody was taking extra. And whoever it was wasn't eating it.

He mentioned this to Penn while scrubbing the pots, keeping his voice low the way you do around a fire that has opinions.

"Maybe Okafor's samples are hungry," Penn said, not looking up from the knife he was sharpening. Penn sharpened knives the way other men prayed.

"It's not the samples. The samples get weighed out separate. I weigh everything separate." Rill set the last pot upside down on a rock to dry. "Somebody's caching food, Penn."

Penn stopped sharpening. That alone was alarming; Penn only stopped sharpening when the mountain itself interrupted him.

"Caching," Penn repeated.

"For a walk-out. Or—" Rill hesitated, then said the thing he'd been chewing for two days like gristle he couldn't spit out. "Or for somebody coming to meet them."

The fire popped. Downriver, Casper Galloway stood at the edge of the gravel bar looking at the water the way he always looked at the water — like it owed him money, or an apology, Rill could never tell which. Wren Crane sat near him with her notebook, close but not too close, the way you orbit someone you're suspicious of. Mara Voss was in her tent with the ledger she consulted more often than scripture.

"You're saying we've got a rat," Penn said quietly.

"I'm saying I count cups, and cups don't lie." Rill wiped his hands on his apron — yes, he'd brought an apron on an illegal expedition, and no, he would not be taking questions. "People lie constantly. Cups never have."

* * *

By morning, everyone knew something was wrong, because Penn told Okafor, who told Wren, who apparently told her notebook, which told everybody else by way of her face. This is how news travels on expeditions: badly, and at the speed of suspicion.

Rill watched it happen over breakfast porridge. Watched Okafor start weighing his sample bags with little glances at the others. Watched Wren move her pack closer to her body. Watched Mara smile her contract-negotiation smile at everyone equally, which was somehow worse than frowning at one person specifically.

And watched Casper Galloway do nothing at all, which frightened Rill more than anything, because a guide who does nothing when the crew starts circling each other is either a fool or a man who already knows.

"So," Penn said, loud enough for the whole circle, in the tone of a man who believed problems should be filleted open on the table like fish. "Someone want to explain why we're short four cups of rice?"

The silence had texture. Rill could have spread it on bread.

"Portage losses," Okafor offered. "The rockslide section. Bags get scuffed, spilled—"

"I seal every bag double," Rill said. "I've sealed bags through two monsoons and a divorce."

"Whose divorce?" Wren asked.

"Mine. It's why I'm here." He let that sit. It was true, mostly, and mostly-true was the expedition currency anyway. "Four cups didn't evaporate. Somebody's holding back rations, and I want to know who before we hit the protected line, because if we're going to get arrested I'd at least like to finish the oatmeal first."

That landed. He saw it land — the flinch that ran around the circle like wind through barley. Because that was the other thing, the thing nobody had said aloud until now: somewhere above the third gorge, the Serrin crossed into the headwaters reserve, and everything they were doing became not just ill-advised but criminal. Rangers patrolled that line. There were fines, confiscations, names in reports that ended careers — Rill had heard Mara say as much on the first night, laying out the route like a woman dealing cards she'd already counted.

"Nobody's holding back rations," Mara said smoothly. "We're carrying buffer stock against delay. Standard practice."

"We're carrying what I packed," Rill said, "and I packed no buffer stock, because buffer stock is heavy and I hate heavy."

Mara looked at him for a long moment, and Rill had the distinct sensation of being a small fish examining a large hook and finding it familiar.

"Then we'll recount tonight," she said pleasantly, "and find your missing cups."

* * *

They recounted that night, and found nothing, which was worse than finding something, because it meant whoever was doing it had moved their cache after Rill started asking questions. Which meant they were listening. Which meant they were smart. Rill lay in his tent listening to the river grind its stones and thought: *I am cooking for six people and one of them is a stranger wearing a friend's face.*

Paranoia, he discovered, is contagious in exactly the way Penn always said hunger was. By the third day of the quiet, nobody shared gear anymore. Rope partners got reassigned twice. Okafor caught Wren photographing his sample kit and demanded to know why, and Wren said "documentation," and Okafor said "documentation of *what*," and the argument lasted forty minutes and settled nothing and cost them an hour of river time.

Even Penn and Rill sniped at each other, which had never once happened in thirty-one years of shared existence.

"You're being paranoid," Penn said.

"I'm being *observant*. There's a difference. Paranoid is when you're wrong."

"And if you're right?"

Rill didn't answer, because the answer was the thing coiling in his stomach: if he was right, then one of these five people — the geologist who hummed show tunes off-key, the girl who ate like a bird and read like a scholar, the legend who carried more than his share without complaint, the boss whose smile never reached her ledger — one of them was selling the rest of them down the river. Literally. To people with badges.

That evening Casper finally did something. He called a halt early, on a wide bench above the water, and built a fire bigger than fuel discipline allowed, and said, "We're burning wood and talking tonight. Both mandatory."

They talked. Or rather, Casper talked, in his flat gravel way, about nothing — about a marmot he'd once known that stole his tobacco for three straight seasons, about the correct way to insult a river (politely, and never within earshot of a rapid), about the year he'd guided a honeymoon couple who turned out to be spies for rival diamond companies. It was nonsense. It was warm nonsense. And by the end of it, Rill realized, everyone was sitting closer to the fire than they had been, and Wren was laughing — actually laughing, the sound surprising her own face — and the knot in the camp had loosened by maybe half a turn.

Which lasted until morning, when Rill found the tin.

* * *

It was buried under the fire ring, of all places — the one spot nobody would dig, because digging in old fire ash is a job for nobody. A battered tea tin, wrapped in a plastic bag, containing: three energy bars, a fistful of dried fruit, a folded map, and a note in careful block letters.

Rill read the note standing very still, the way you stand still when you've stepped on something that might be a stick or might be a snake.

*POSITION CONFIRMED. CROSSING RESERVE LINE DAY 12, AM. PARTY OF SIX. REQUEST INTERCEPTION AT MARKER 44. WILL COOPERATE.*

He didn't show anyone. Not at first. He walked to the river's edge and stood there doing arithmetic with his heart hammering. Party of six. Six. The crew was five plus Mara made six, wait, no — five total, he counted on his fingers twice, five, unless the writer was counting themselves separately, which meant—

Which meant the writer knew they were writing. Knew they were part of the party. Was reporting on themselves along with everyone else, cool as you please, *party of six*, like a restaurant reservation.

He showed Penn. Penn read it twice, and Rill watched his brother's face go through weather — shock, anger, and then a terrible kind of grief, because Penn loved people easily and being betrayed sat on him like a landslide.

"Marker 44," Penn said finally. "That's the old survey cairn above the gorge. We pass it day twelve." His jaw worked. "Two days."

"Could be a plant," Rill said, without believing it. "Could be Casper's, some old—"

"Casper doesn't write like that. Nobody writes like that. That's somebody being careful." Penn crushed the energy bar wrapper in his fist. "Somebody being *paid* to be careful."

They took it to Mara, because that's where problems went on this trip — Mara decided things, that was the architecture of the world. They expected the storm. The shouting, the accusations, the tearing-apart of the camp to find the traitor.

Instead Mara read the note in silence, folded it along its existing creases, put it in her breast pocket, and said, "Thank you both. Say nothing to the others."

"Mara—" Penn started.

"*Nothing.*" Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it dropped, the way water drops just before it goes over the edge. "You want to tear this camp apart looking for a ghost? You want five scared people watching each other instead of the river, two days out from the worst water on the run? No. I'll handle it. Cook me dinner and keep your mouths shut. That's the job."

She walked away toward Casper's tent, and Rill watched her go, and felt the strangest thing: relief and dread in equal measure, like drinking cold water with a fever.

"She's going to tell Casper," he said.

"She's going to *handle* it," Penn said. "There's a difference."

"There's always a difference with her," Rill muttered. "The difference is usually money."

* * *

He was right, as it happened, though it took him a day to learn how right.

Because here is what Rill did next, and he wasn't proud of it, but a man who counts cups counts everything: he kept watch on the cache site. If the informant came back to retrieve the tin — add new supplies, update the note — Rill wanted to see who. He told himself it was for the crew's safety. He told himself a lot of things that night, lying in the cold with his breath making little ghosts above his face.

Near midnight, a figure moved through camp. Quiet. Careful. Went to the fire ring, knelt, worked at the ash with fingers rather than tools — practiced, this, done before.

Rill's whole body went tight as a winch line.

The figure lifted the tin, held it a moment, and then — did not take it away. Set it back. Reburied it. Patted the ash smooth. And stood there a moment in the starlight, shoulders rounded, and Rill saw the profile clear as a struck match.

Wren Crane.

His mind refused it. *No. She's dying — she told us at the fire, she's sick, why would she—* But the note hadn't said anything about why. Notes never did. People sold each other for money, for fear, for promises, and rich girls were people, and dying people were desperate people, and desperation wore every face eventually.

He must have shifted, or breathed, or the river changed its mind about covering him — because her head came up, sharp, animal, and she looked directly at where he lay among the rocks.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Wren said, quietly, into the dark: "How much did you see?"

"Enough," Rill said, sitting up, because hiding was over. "Party of six, marker 44, day twelve. That's in two days, Wren."

She didn't run. He'd half expected her to. Instead she came and sat on a rock near him, arms around her knees, and in the starlight her face was younger than it ever looked by day, and more tired.

"You're going to tell Mara," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I found the note. Penn found the note. Mara has the note." He rubbed his face. "What I want to know is why. Your father's paying for this whole circus. Why sell it to the rangers?"

Wren laughed — a small, awful sound with no floor under it. "My father isn't paying for this trip. My father's *money* is paying for this trip, and my father's money belongs to people who aren't my father, not really. And those people—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "If the rangers intercept us at the line, the expedition ends legally. Cleanly. Everyone goes home. Nobody goes further upstream. That was the deal I made."

"The deal you made with *who*?"

"With the people who are paying for my treatment." Her voice cracked on *treatment*, just barely, a hairline fracture in good masonry. "Rill, I signed papers. There are conditions. I'm not — I can't just—"

Above them, a tent flap opened. Mara Voss stepped out into the starlight with the unhurried air of a woman who had been waiting for exactly this moment, possibly for days, and Rill understood with a lurch that the fire-ring cache had probably never been hidden from her at all.

"Wren," Mara said pleasantly. "Walk with me. Bring the tin."

They walked to the far end of the gravel bar, and Rill — because nobody told him to stay behind, and because he was Rill — followed at a distance close enough to hear and far enough to pretend he hadn't.

What he heard was Mara Voss, in a voice soft as silt, lay out the situation with the precision of a woman reading terms aloud: the syndicate's conditions, the ranger station at Marker 44, the helicopter that would come, the arrest, the end of everything — and then, the counteroffer.

"Here's what happens instead," Mara said. "You burn the note. You send one final message tomorrow — *route changed, crossing delayed, party scattered* — whatever buys us three days past the line. And when we're through the reserve boundary, there's an account with your name on it. Enough to make the syndicate's conditions irrelevant. Enough that you never have to trade your father's expedition for your own medicine again."

"That's bribery," Wren said.

"That's *payment*," Mara corrected. "Bribery is when you pay someone to betray their people. I'm paying you to stop betraying them. The distinction matters to me professionally."

A long silence. The river ground its stones.

"Why?" Wren asked, and her voice was very young. "Why not just expose me? Turn me out? Let them take me at the line and be rid of—"

"Because a crew that catches its traitor stops trusting each other," Mara said, "and a crew that stops trusting each other dies in the gorge above us. I need you whole, Wren. All of you. Even the parts that lie." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "Besides. I know what it costs, signing papers you hate. I've signed a few."

Rill backed away into the dark before he heard any more, heart pounding, and lay awake until dawn doing the only math that mattered: *one traitor, bought back. One camp, still whole. Three days of grace.*

At breakfast the next morning, Wren passed him the rice bag for seconds without being asked, and met his eyes for half a heartbeat, and he nodded once, and that was the whole treaty.

But when Casper shouldered his pack and pointed upriver toward the narrow dark line of the gorge ahead — the one he'd gone silent about whenever anyone asked, the one Mara's maps marked with a single word, *CAUTION*, in handwriting fifteen years old — Rill noticed that Casper was looking at Wren differently now. Not angry. Not forgiving, either.

Looking at her the way you look at water you're about to cross, knowing exactly how deep it goes.

"Day twelve comes early," Casper said to no one in particular, and started walking.
