## 9. Gorge of the Sworn-Off

The gorge announced itself the way bad news does: quietly first, then all at once.

We'd been on the water since dawn, and for six hours the Serrin had behaved like a river with a good temperament — broad, braided, chatty over gravel bars. Then the walls began. First foothills pretending to be scenery, then shoulders, then cliffs that leaned in from both sides until the sky was a ribbon of gray silk overhead and the river's voice changed pitch, dropped an octave, started sounding less like water and more like intent.

"Sworn-Off," Rill said from the second raft, testing the name out loud. "Who swears off a whole canyon? That seems dramatic."

"People who have reasons," Penn said.

"What kind of reasons?"

"The kind you don't ask about at volume."

I let them talk. There is a particular skill in guiding, which nobody puts on the certification exam, and it is knowing when to be stone. I'd been stone for most of nine days now. It was getting heavy.

Mara pulled her raft alongside mine at the mouth of the narrows and didn't look at me when she spoke, which was her way of being kind. "You've been quiet since breakfast."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *quiet* quiet today. There's a difference. One is professional. The other one I remember from fifteen years ago, right before you did something stupid."

"Then enjoy the nostalgia."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked up at the walls going amber in the late light, and whatever she was feeling she folded away into that ledger of hers, the one I'd never been allowed to read. "How far to the campsite?"

"Six kilometers. Maybe seven. The river doesn't believe in straight lines here."

"And after that?"

"After that we walk." I checked the gauge on my wrist — a habit, not a necessity; I could read this water by taste if it came to that. "Flow's holding. We run the narrows tomorrow morning, early, while it's cold and slow. Then the portage trail on river left. Two days through, if the weather holds."

"If it doesn't?"

"Then we sit and get to know each other better."

"God forbid," Mara said, and pushed off.

* * *

I want to say something honest about what it is to return to a place you swore off, because I know the phrase sounds like poetry and the reality is more like dentistry.

It isn't dread, exactly. Dread is a wave; it crests and passes. What I felt at the mouth of the Sworn-Off was closer to recognition — the unpleasant kind, like hearing your own voice on a recording. The canyon knew me. Every bend of it knew me. And I knew it back, in the worst way: not as geography but as sequence. *Here* the walls pinch to forty meters. *Here* the current stacks against the outside of the turn and you ferry hard or you donate your raft to the rock garden. *Here*, on the right, thirty meters up, there is a ledge where a helicopter hovered for four hours while men shouted things none of us could hear over the water.

I had not stood under that ledge in fifteen years. I had stood under it many times before that, and once since — no. Not once. I corrected myself the way Okafor corrects his field notes, ruthlessly, in pencil. Many times since. Alone, mostly, in seasons when even the rangers didn't come up here. I used to tell myself it was reconnaissance, that I was keeping my knowledge of the upper gorge sharp in case anyone ever needed it. That was a lie with good posture. I came because grief is a navigation problem, and I had never solved this route, and some part of me believed that if I ran it enough times — walked it, waded it, sat in it through whole nights — I would find the line I'd missed. The move that would have brought Theo Crane home instead of leaving him in the eddy below the falls, folded against a boulder, patient as luggage.

There is no such line. I know that now the way you know a language you learned too young. But knowing a thing and believing it are different rivers, and only one of them freezes in winter.

"Casper." Wren, from the bow of my raft. She'd claimed that seat on day two and defended it with the quiet ferocity of someone who had grown up around people who took things from her. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at the canyon like it owes you money."

"That's just how I look at canyons."

"No." She turned around, kneeling, facing upstream so her hair whipped. She had her father's jaw and nothing else of him that I could see, thank God. "It's how you look at *this* canyon. You've been counting bends since lunch. You haven't counted a single bend on any other day of this trip. I watch you. It's basically my job."

"It's everyone's job," Penn called from behind us. "He counts everything. He counted the spoons at dinner. He told Rill he was short a spoon."

"I *was* short a spoon," Rill said, wounded.

"You ate it with the freeze-dried lasagna, you animal."

I let the noise wash over me, grateful for it. Noise is ballast. In the old days, on trips gone wrong, I'd learned that silence is what kills people — silence lets them think, and thinking at altitude or in whitewater is how you end up making a decision with your fear instead of your training. So I kept my crews loud. Loud crews live.

Wren wasn't fooled. Wren had spent nine days learning to read me the way I read water, and she was better at it than she knew. She faced forward again but kept talking, low, under the wind. "This is where it happened. Isn't it. The original thing. The reason they banned you."

"Not exactly here."

"But close."

"Close enough that the rocks don't care about the difference."

She was quiet for a stretch. The walls closed another degree. Somewhere above us a raven made its opinion known, that dry wooden knocking call they do, like a stick breaking in slow motion.

"My brother died in the mountains," she said.

I put one hand flat on the thwart and held on to the boat instead of anything else. "I know."

"Do you?" She didn't say it cruelly. That was the terrible part. She said it the way Okafor might test a core sample — gently, precisely, wanting to know what was inside. "Everyone says *I know*. My father says it. He says it in a voice like he's reading it off a card. But you—" she gestured at the walls, at the water, at all of it, "—you talk to this place like it's a person you're apologizing to."

"I don't apologize to rocks."

"You mutter at them. Day three, in the shallows, you said *not this way, not anymore* to a boulder. I heard you. Rill thought you were praying. Penn thought you were swearing. I think you were negotiating."

The current picked up as the walls pinched, and I had work to do — a real excuse, finally. I ferried us left around a submerged fin of granite that I had known about for fifteen years and greeted now like an old colleague. *Still there. Good. Terrible, but good.*

"Wren," I said, when the water gave me back my voice, "some places you go once. Some places go through you once, and then you spend years walking back through the wound to make sure it's still shaped the same. It's not noble. It's not interesting. It's just what I do instead of sleeping well."

She absorbed that. I watched her absorb it — saw her decide not to push, which cost her something visible, a small muscular effort around the eyes. Her father's daughter in discipline if nothing else.

"When we're through," she said, "you'll tell me. The real version. Not the campfire version."

"When we're through," I said, and hated myself for the half-promise, and meant it anyway.

* * *

Camp that night went into a hollow I remembered — a gravel bench above an eddy, sheltered from the downdrafts that funneled down the walls after dark. I'd slept here alone twice. Once in rain. Once in snow, in October, stupidly, beautifully, listening to the river freeze at the margins with a sound like someone slowly unzipping the world.

Okafor got the stove going and produced, from somewhere in his kit, a bag of dried mango that he guarded with the territorial instinct of a wolverine. Penn made flatbread. Rill, wrist still splinted from the shelf, had appointed himself director of morale, a role consisting mainly of commentary.

"So," Rill said, around flatbread, "the Sworn-Off. Genuinely asking. Who named it? Was it official? Is there a committee?"

"Trappers named it," I said. "Hundred-odd years ago. They lost people in here — the narrows, the falls. After a while they stopped running it. Drew their maps with a gap in the middle and wrote *sworn off* across it."

"They just... gave up?"

"They just decided some water isn't worth the price of admission." I fed a stick into the fire. "Rangers formalized it later. Closed to commercial traffic. Closed to most everything."

"And yet," Okafor said mildly, not looking up from his notebook, "here we are."

"Here we are."

"Your markers," he said. Still mild. Okafor's mildness was a scalpel with a comfortable grip. "The survey cairns I found last week, above the confluence. They were yours. Fifteen years old, give or take. But there were newer ones too. Fresher. Same hand, same stacking pattern. Within the last five years, I'd estimate. Possibly more recent."

The fire popped. Downriver, the narrows muttered to itself in the dark, rehearsing.

Penn and Rill exchanged a look. Mara, sitting slightly apart with her boots off and her ledger open on her knees though she hadn't written in it for ten minutes, went very still.

"Newer ones," Wren said.

"Same builder," Okafor confirmed. "I'd stake my degree on it. Which is worth increasingly little, but still."

All of them looked at me. I considered my options, which were: lie, deflect, or stand in the open like a man caught in his own headlights.

"I hiked in," I said. "A few times. Over the years. Alone. Marked the safe lines so I wouldn't have to relearn them every time."

"Why?" Rill asked. Not accusing. Honestly puzzled, the way he was honestly puzzled by most adult behavior. "If you were banned, why keep coming back?"

Because my hands needed somewhere to go. Because the alternative was a chair and a ceiling and the sound of water in pipes. Because a man who has killed someone — no. A man who has *lost* someone, I corrected myself, though the correction never fully took — needs a room big enough to hold it, and no room built by people is big enough, but a canyon will do.

"Because I'm good at this," I said, "and it seemed a waste to stop entirely."

Nobody believed that. Bless them, none of them believed it, and none of them pressed, either. That's the thing about a crew that's been cold and wet and scared together for nine days: they develop a courtesy deeper than interrogation. They file things. They wait.

Except Wren. Wren didn't press either, but she didn't file it. She held it out in front of her, turning it, and I could see her doing arithmetic — the fresh cairns, the muttered negotiations with boulders, the way I'd led today without touching the map once. I watched her arrive at the answer and watched her decide, visibly, not to say it in front of everyone.

*He's been coming back. For years. Alone. To the place where his life ended.*

She looked at me across the fire with something that wasn't pity. Pity I could have handled. This was worse. This was recognition — the specific, awful tenderness of one damaged person identifying another's damage with perfect accuracy.

I poked the fire harder than it deserved.

* * *

Morning came gray and low, the clouds snagged on the rim a thousand feet up like laundry on a fence. Narrows weather. Cold air holds the water down, keeps the flow steady, keeps the light flat so you can read the currents. It was, objectively, perfect conditions.

Subjectively, my chest felt like someone had packed it with river gravel overnight.

We ran it clean. Of course we ran it clean — I called every move before it was needed, ferried left here, punch the seam there, hold center through the haystack waves where the river dropped into its throat. Penn rowed like he cooked, with aggressive competence and occasional profanity. Okafor took notes between rapids, which should have been fatal and somehow wasn't. Wren stayed in my bow and did not speak the entire hour, and her silence was louder than Rill's entire personality.

At the bottom of the last drop, where the walls fell back and the river widened and sighed, there was a beach. Gray sand, driftwood, a view back up the canyon to where the falls showed themselves as a thread of white between black rock.

That was where Theo had washed ashore. That beach. That exact curve of sand, which the river had rebuilt a hundred times since and which was therefore both the same beach and not the same beach at all, depending on how cruel you wanted to be about time.

I beached the raft. I stepped out. My legs worked, which felt like a technicality.

Wren stepped out beside me and stood looking up the canyon at the falls, and then at the beach, and then at me, and I saw her do the geography the way I'd done it — the sequence, the terrible arithmetic of *here to here*.

"This is it," she said. Not a question.

"This is it."

"How long did they search?"

"Four days. Then the water took the rest." I looked at the falls. From here they were almost pretty. Almost. "They found his pack two miles down. His father had it shipped home. I'm told Aldous kept it in his study. Unopened."

Wren wrapped her arms around herself. The wind came down the canyon, cold and mineral, smelling of stone and old ice. "You've stood here before," she said. "Alone. Many times. Haven't you."

It wasn't a question either. There were no questions left between us, only confirmations.

"Yes," I said.

"Why?"

And because she had earned it, and because the canyon had knocked every other wall out of me years ago and this one was only standing out of habit, I told her a piece of the truth. Not the whole architecture. A window.

"Because the first fifty times I came back to figure out what I did wrong," I said. "And then somewhere around the hundredth time I understood there was no wrong to find — just water, and weight, and a schedule somebody else set. And I kept coming anyway. Because it's the only church I've got, and the sermons are terrible, but at least the acoustics are honest."

Wren laughed — a small, cracked sound, surprised out of her — and wiped her face with the back of her glove, quick and furious, daring anyone to notice.

Behind us, Mara's satellite phone chirped. Once. Twice. The third chirp was longer — the incoming-call tone, insistent, the ringtone she'd assigned to exactly one contact and complained about publicly and never changed.

She looked at the screen. Then at me. Then at Wren.

"It's your father," she said.
