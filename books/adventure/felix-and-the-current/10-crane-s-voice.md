## 10. Crane's Voice

The phone rang a fourth time, and I understood something about my father in that moment: he had never once in his life waited for someone to answer. He simply called until the world rearranged itself around his voice.

Mara held the phone out to me like it was something that might bite. Maybe she thought it would. Maybe she was right.

"He's been trying for two days," she said. "The window at this latitude is short. Take it now or he'll keep calling until the battery dies, and then I'll have to listen to him complain about it through Okafor."

I took the phone. It was warm from her pocket, which felt strangely intimate — my father's voice about to arrive through the heat of Mara Voss's thigh. The screen said ALDOUS CRANE in block letters, because of course it did. He had entered his own name into every device he'd ever touched. Once, when I was nine, I'd found our home answering machine and discovered he'd recorded the outgoing greeting himself: *You've reached the Crane residence. Aldous Crane speaking.* As if a machine needed his byline.

I walked away from the fire. Not far — you don't go far from a fire in country like this — but far enough that the others became shapes and the river became a sound instead of a thing.

"Dad."

"Wren." His voice came through compressed and flattened, stripped of everything except the words themselves, and still somehow enormous. "Thank God. Do you know how difficult it is to reach you people? Voss's operator kept putting me into a queue. A queue, Wren. I'm paying for this expedition."

"Hello to you too."

"I'm sorry. Hello. Are you well? You sound thin."

"I'm on a river at four thousand meters. Everyone sounds thin."

A pause. In the pause I could hear the room behind him — the particular hush of his study, the carpeted silence where money went to be comfortable. Fifteen hundred miles away and I could smell the leather chairs.

"The crew is performing?" he asked.

"The crew is *performing*," I agreed, because it was easier than saying: Casper Galloway carried me across a scree field yesterday without complaint, and Penn has started making bannock shaped like my face, and Rill calls me *Crane Junior* in a way that stopped stinging around day six and started feeling almost like a name I'd chosen.

"Good. Good." Papers rustled on his end. I knew that sound too — the sound of my father consulting notes before feelings. He'd done it my whole life. My mother used to say he loved us best when we were on the agenda. She'd said it laughing, mostly. "Wren, I need you to listen carefully, because the connection may not hold and I won't have time to say this twice."

The river filled the silence while he arranged himself. Behind me, faintly, I heard Okafor say something that made Penn laugh, and the laugh carried up the gorge the way sound does at altitude — clean, unobstructed, honest. I envied it.

"There are people invested in this expedition," my father said. "Serious people. The Meridian Consortium — you met two of them at the birthday dinner, the tall one and the one who talked about sailing—"

"Harrow."

"Harrow. They've been patient, Wren. Extraordinarily patient. But patience has terms, and the terms have arrived." Another rustle of paper. "They want samples. First, before anything else. Water, mineral strata, flow rates — Okafor will know what to collect, there's a protocol, it's already been sent to Voss. The therapeutic application comes second. The cure is the *goal*, obviously, but the science has to come out first. That's how these things work."

"That's not how these things work," I said. "That's how *ownership* works."

My father sighed, and the sigh came through the satellite link as a small dying sound, a bird falling out of the sky between us. "Don't be dramatic."

"You taught me the word 'expropriate' when I was eleven. I get to use it."

"Wren." And here his voice changed — dropped its boardroom register and found the other one, the low private one, the one he'd used at my mother's bedside and at mine, in hospitals, in the years of tests and waiting rooms and doctors who spoke in probabilities. This was the voice I could never argue with, and I hated that I could never argue with it, and I loved him for using it, and both of those things were true at once, which is the whole problem with fathers. "I am sixty-eight years old. The specialists give me — they give me numbers. You know what the numbers are. You have your own numbers; God knows you've collected enough of them yourself."

I looked down at my hands. My gloves were fraying at the left thumb. Casper had noticed it two days ago and re-waxed them without asking, and I hadn't thanked him, and he hadn't expected me to, and I had thought about it every day since.

"The spring is real," my father said. "You've seen it. You're weeks from it. All I'm asking — all *they're* asking — is that when you reach it, you document properly. Samples first. That's all. Samples first, and then everyone drinks."

"And if it can't be owned?"

The question came out of me before I'd decided to ask it. That happens sometimes on a river. The current pulls things out of you.

Silence. Long enough that I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

"What do you mean," my father said finally, very evenly, "if it can't be owned?"

"If the source can't be owned. If it's on protected land, if the headwaters can't be leased or licensed or bought, if the syndicate can't put a fence around it and a price on it — what happens then? What did Harrow tell you happens then?"

"Wren—"

"Because I've been doing arithmetic, Dad. Out here there's not much else to do after dark. And the arithmetic goes like this: Meridian doesn't fund expeditions. Meridian funds *assets*. So either the source is an asset, or it's an obstacle, and obstacles—" I stopped. My breath was doing something unhelpful. "Obstacles get removed. Or contained. Or dammed."

The word landed in the satellite silence and just sat there.

"Who told you that word?" my father asked quietly.

"Nobody. It's a big word. It's in the air out here. Ask your surveyors."

Another silence, but a different quality of it — the silence of a man deciding how much his daughter already knows. I had heard it my whole life and misread it my whole life, and I was not going to misread it tonight.

"The headwaters fall inside the Serrin Protected Corridor," I said. "Every guide on this river knows it. Every map says so. Which means either Meridian believes they can get the protection lifted — bought, lobbied, litigated, whatever they do — or they believe they don't *need* it lifted, because a dam doesn't require permission so much as it requires *facts on the ground*. Either way, Dad, the cure was never the point. The cure was the story. The story gets me up here. The daughter legitimizes the expedition. And the expedition maps the asset."

"Wren, sit down."

"I am sitting down. I'm always sitting down. There's nowhere else to go."

"*Listen* to me." And now the private voice cracked open and something older was under it, something frightened, and that frightened thing undid me more than any boardroom register ever had. "I am dying. You are dying. We are the same disease wearing two different bodies, and I have spent forty years buying things, and the one thing I cannot buy is time — except maybe, *maybe*, there is water in a hole in a mountain that buys it. And yes. Yes, there are men involved who see further than I do and want more than I want. I know that. I have known it since Harrow first sat down across from me with his little diagrams. But I told myself: the water is real, the science is real, and whatever else those men take, my daughter comes home alive. That was the trade. That was always the trade. I am not a good man, Wren, but I am not going to apologize for trading everything else in the world for you."

The river ran. Somewhere above us, ice calved off something ancient with a sound like a door closing in a very large house.

"You should have asked me," I said.

"Would you have come?"

And there it was. The trap he'd built without building it, the question with no honest exit. Would I have come? If he'd laid it all out — the syndicate, the samples, the dam contingency, the daughter-as-cover — would I have boarded the plane?

I thought about the fire behind me. About Penn's terrible bannock and Rill's worse singing. About Okafor crouched over her sample kits like a woman reading scripture. About Mara, who watched me with an expression I couldn't name and now, suddenly, could. About Casper Galloway, who looked at me like a ghost and waxed my gloves and wouldn't answer a single question about the gorge we were walking into, and who had, three nights ago, sat beside me in the dark and said nothing at all for an hour, and it had been the best hour of my last two years.

"Yes," I said. "That's what makes it unforgivable."

He made a sound I'd never heard him make. Small. Almost human.

"The samples, Wren. Just — collect the samples. Buy me the time to fix the rest. I can manage Harrow. I've managed worse men than Harrow. Give me until the thaw."

"You're asking me to help them dam a river so you can drink from it."

"I'm asking you to help your father live."

There it was. Love against duty, except the duty was love, and the love was a lever, and I stood there at four thousand meters holding a satellite phone and understood that my father had weaponized the only thing he'd ever had that couldn't be purchased — and that it had worked, that some traitorous part of me was already calculating how many vials Okafor's kit could hold.

"I have to go," I said. "The window."

"The window—"

"Mara said the window closes. I have to go, Dad."

"Wren. *Wren.*"

I ended the call. Then I stood there holding the dead phone against my chest like it was the last warm thing in the world, which, at that moment, it was.

* * *

I didn't go straight back to the fire. I walked upstream along the bank instead, boots crunching on gravel gone stiff with evening cold, and let the river make its argument. Rivers are good for that. They don't care what you're feeling; they just keep stating their position.

Okafor found me there. Of course she did. She has a geologist's gift for locating the exact spot where a person has gone to be alone and standing next to them anyway.

"Your father?" she asked.

"My father."

She nodded and crouched at the waterline, ostensibly checking something in the sediment, actually giving me the courtesy of a face that wasn't looking at me. "In my experience," she said, "the people who fund science are rarely interested in science. They're interested in the *products* of science. It's an important distinction. It took me twenty years and one very bad contract to learn it."

"Did you sign the bad contract?"

"I did. And then I spent five years watching a wetland in Botswana become a parking structure, and I testified, and I lost, and now I'm here." She picked up a stone, turned it over, set it down exactly as she'd found it. "So when Voss offered me this trip, I read the fine print. All of it. Twice."

"And?"

"And there's a lot of fine print, Miss Crane. More than a scientific survey requires. Less than a land grab requires, if I'm being fair — but land grabs don't usually announce themselves in the contract. They arrive later, wearing the contract as a disguise." She stood, brushed off her knees. "What did he ask you for?"

"Samples first. Cure second."

Okafor was quiet a moment. "And third?"

I didn't answer. I didn't have to. She looked at me the way she looks at rock strata — reading the layers, finding the fault line.

"I'll need to redesign the sampling protocol," she said at last, mildly, as though discussing dinner. "If we're collecting samples, we'll collect *proper* ones. Documented, witnessed, chain-of-custody immaculate. Enough to hang a consortium with, if it comes to that." She began walking back toward the fire, then paused. "For what it's worth — the water in this river doesn't belong to anyone. That's not sentiment. That's hydrology. You can't own a source. You can only poison it and call it yours."

* * *

By the time I got back to camp, the fire had been fed and the mood had shifted into its evening gear — Penn arguing with Rill about whether a marmot they'd seen that morning had been "judging" them, Okafor logging readings, Mara sitting slightly apart with her ledger open, writing in that tight backward-slanted hand of hers.

Casper was repairing a boat seam by firelight, and he glanced up when I came into the circle — that look again, the ghost-look, except tonight I recognized something new in it. Recognition runs both ways. He wasn't looking at a ghost. He was looking at someone standing where he had once stood: young, loyal, holding a rope someone else had tied.

"Cranie," Rill said — my name had evolved, over nine days, from *Crane Junior* to *Cranie*, and I had stopped fighting it somewhere around the portage — "your old man okay? You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face Penn makes when the bannock burns."

"It's a good face," Penn said. "It's an honest face."

I sat down on the pack next to Casper, closer than I needed to. He didn't move away. Under the cover of the fire's noise, I said, quietly: "My father wants samples. Before anything. There's a protocol coming to Mara. And if the source can't be owned—"

I stopped. He waited. He's good at waiting. It might be the only thing he's better at than me.

"—there's a plan B," I finished. "There's always a plan B with money like his."

Casper set down the seam-patch and looked into the fire for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, the guide-voice, the one that means the weather is changing.

"Fifteen years ago," he said, "a man paid for an expedition, and the expedition served the payment instead of the mountain, and someone died who didn't have to." He picked the patch back up. Pressed it flat. "I swore I'd never be on that kind of trip again. Turns out I'm bad at swearing."

"So what do we do?"

"We do what guides do." He glanced at me, and for the first time since I'd met him, the ghost-look was gone, replaced by something plainer and much more frightening: trust. "We watch the weather. We watch each other. And when the moment comes — and it comes on every trip, Cranie, it always comes — we decide fast, and we decide right, and we live with it."

Across the fire, Mara closed her ledger with a snap. She was looking at me. She'd been looking at me since the call, and now I finally named the expression I'd been seeing on her face for nine days.

Guilt. Old guilt, worn smooth as river stone, the kind that stops hurting and starts *shaping*.

"Get some sleep," she said to all of us, briskly. "Pressure's dropping. Barometer's been falling all afternoon, and the wind's backing around to the north." She stood, rolled the ledger into her jacket. "Tomorrow we make the confluence below the gorge. After that, the weather owns us."

I lay in my bag that night listening to the river and thinking about my father's voice saying *give me until the thaw*, and about Okafor's stone placed back exactly as she'd found it, and about the wind rising out of the north like something with an opinion.

Sometime past midnight, the first snowflakes came down through the firelight — fat, slow, almost polite, the way snow behaves before it stops being polite at all.

Penn looked up from the coals and said, cheerfully, to no one in particular:

"Well. That's not great."
