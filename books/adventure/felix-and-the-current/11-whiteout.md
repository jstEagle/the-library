## 11. Whiteout

Ngozi Okafor had spent eleven years reading rocks for a living, and she could tell you with reasonable confidence that the Serrin gorge hated people. Not in the dramatic way of avalanches or flash floods — those were just the mountain doing its arithmetic. No, the gorge hated people the way a landlord hates tenants: patiently, bureaucratically, waiting for you to make one small mistake on the paperwork.

She was crouched over a seam of graywacke near the shelf camp, chipping a sample into her notebook, when she noticed the barometer.

It was a brass thing Casper carried, older than most of the crew, and it hung from a carabiner on the cook tarp like a talisman nobody talked about. All morning it had been falling. Not dropping — *falling*, the needle moving with the slow, deliberate certainty of something that had made up its mind days ago and was only now getting around to telling them.

"Hey, Galloway," she called. "Your weather rock is lying to us again."

Casper came over, looked at it, and said nothing. That was the thing about Casper Okafor had learned over three weeks of river: he said nothing at exactly the moments other men would fill with noise. Penn would have joked. Mara would have recalculated. Rill would have started cooking something as a preemptive apology to the universe.

Casper just looked at the needle, then up at the sky, which had gone the color of old dishwater, and then at the walls of the gorge rising around them like the inside of a throat.

"Cache first," he said. "Then boats lashed high. Then we talk."

* * *

The cache was their insurance policy — two hundred pounds of food and fuel sealed in barrels and tarped behind a granite fin forty meters above the waterline, hauled up there on the second day of the portage because Casper believed in redundancy the way some men believe in saints. "The river takes what's low," he'd said. "So we put things where the river can't reach."

Okafor had nodded along, thinking it was superstition dressed as logistics. She was a scientist; she kept her skepticism in a labeled jar.

By mid-afternoon the wind arrived ahead of the snow, funneling down the gorge with a sound like a freight train being dragged across gravel. The temperature dropped ten degrees in an hour. Wren Crane stood at the edge of the shelf watching the clouds come down the canyon wall — not over it, *down* it, pouring like smoke — and Okafor watched her watch, and thought: *she knows. Whatever else that girl is carrying, she knows what this is.*

They got the boats lashed. They got the tarps doubled. What they did not get — what nobody got, because the storm moved faster than arithmetic — was back up to the cache.

Casper made the call at four o'clock, standing in wind that leaned on you like a drunk uncle. "Nobody goes up there tonight. It's eighty meters of exposed slab with ice forming on it. We wait for the window."

"The window might be three days," Mara said. "We have food down here for two."

"Then we eat less," Casper said.

That was the whole conversation. Okafor wrote it in her notebook afterward, because it seemed important: *Day 19. Galloway chooses the crew over the supplies. Note whether this matters later.* She underlined it twice, though she couldn't have told you why.

* * *

The storm hit properly at dusk, and by full dark the world had been deleted.

Okafor had been in whiteouts before — she'd done fieldwork in Svalbard, where the weather treated human plans as a personal insult — but this was different. This wasn't snow falling through air. This was air becoming snow, every direction erased, the headlamp beam bouncing back at you like the light itself had given up. You could stand six feet from another person and be alone.

They huddled in the lee of the overturned boats, seven bodies in a space meant for four, sharing heat and the last of Penn's ginger tea, which he'd stretched with melted snow until it was more of a rumor than a beverage.

"If I die out here," Rill announced, "I want it noted that my brother's tea killed me first."

"It's rehydration," Penn said with dignity. "It's medicine."

"It's punishment. Somewhere upstream there's a glacier making this stuff and even *it* is embarrassed."

Wren laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound, and Okafor saw Casper glance over at her the way you glance at a fire you thought had gone out. Three weeks ago the girl had boarded at the confluence with her father's money and her father's silence and a look on her face like a drawn bow. Now she was passing around a cup of terrible tea in a whiteout, laughing at the cook brothers' ancient routine, and Okafor noted that too. *Day 19, cont. The crew is becoming a crew. This is either very good or very bad.*

Sleep came in shifts. Okafor took the midnight-to-three watch, wedged against a boat hull with the barometer in her lap like a pet she didn't trust. The needle had stopped falling. It had simply given up, lying flat against the pin at the bottom of its range, and she found herself talking to it quietly.

"I know," she told it. "I know. Just tell me when."

* * *

Morning was a theoretical concept. The storm hadn't ended; it had merely paused to reload. But there was a gray suggestion of light, and in that light Casper stood up, looked at the slab above them, and said the words Okafor would think about for the rest of her life:

"We go up for the cache."

Mara was on her feet instantly. "In this?"

"There's a gap. Mid-morning, maybe ninety minutes, maybe two hours, before the next band comes through. After that band, we're buried here for real, and buried here means eating snow and burning boat hulls." He was already coiling rope. "Two climbers. Fast and light. Everyone else stays tied in."

"I'll go," Wren said.

"No," said Casper and Mara, in unison, which was such a rare event that Penn dropped a pot.

"I'll go," Okafor said, because someone had to, and because she'd spent three weeks watching this man and she wanted — needed, professionally, scientifically — to see how he moved on stone. There were stories about Casper Galloway's footwork. Stories from before. She wanted primary data.

He looked at her for a long moment. "You sure?"

"I'm the lightest person here who isn't currently arguing about it."

Rill raised his hand from inside his sleeping bag. "For the record, I would argue, but I pulled a hamstring yesterday being heroic about firewood."

"You tripped over the firewood," Penn said.

"Being heroic *about* it."

* * *

The slab was worse than it looked, which Okafor had not believed possible. Ice had grown in the cracks overnight, thin and black and treacherous, and the wind came off the glacier above in gusts that felt like being slapped by something enormous and annoyed. Casper led. He climbed the way he did everything — without flourish, without wasted motion, placing protection with a speed that suggested his hands knew routes his mind had never seen.

Halfway up, Okafor understood the stories.

There was a moment — a traverse across a sloping ledge glazed with verglas, thirty meters of nothing below — where her world narrowed to her boots and her breath and the rope humming faintly between them. And Casper simply flowed across it. No hesitation, no testing, no prayer. Like walking across a kitchen floor he'd lived on for twenty years.

"You've done this exact pitch," she said when they reached the fin and its sheltered pocket. It wasn't a question.

He was rigging the cache tarp, not looking at her. "Once or twice."

"In a storm?"

He didn't answer. The wind filled the silence for him, screaming down off the icefield above, and Okafor filed it away with everything else she'd filed away about this man: the survey markers fifteen years stale, the reflexes at the rockslide, the way he avoided the upper gorge on the maps and stared at it in person. *This man has been here alone,* she thought. *Many times. In weather like this. On purpose.* She didn't know why yet. But she was a geologist; she knew that the strangest formations always had an explanation, and the explanation was always older than it looked.

They loaded themselves with as much as they could carry — food, the stove fuel, the medical kit — and left the rest barrel-sealed for a second trip. The descent went fine right up until it didn't.

* * *

It happened at the bottom, on the last easy pitch, which was of course how these things worked. The universe had a sense of humor; it was just a bad one.

A gust came off the glacier — not a gust really, a fist, a whole collapsed pocket of cold air sliding down the icefield and hitting the shelf like a door slamming — and Rill, who had come out to anchor the lower belay despite his hamstring and everyone's objections, was standing on a patch of ice-slicked scree when it hit.

Okafor saw it all in the flat gray light: Rill's boots going out from under him, his arm shooting back to catch himself on a boulder, the angle of the fall wrong in the specific way that makes trained people wince. The crack of it carried even through the wind.

He screamed. Then, being Rill, he screamed again but funnier: "MY ARM IS BROKEN AND MY BROTHER'S TEA DID THIS."

They got him into the shelter of the boats. The wrist was bad — a clean break, both bones, the kind of thing that in a hospital was an inconvenience and on a river shelf in a storm was a crisis wearing a crisis costume. Penn held his brother's head and said nothing at all, which from Penn was the loudest sound Okafor had ever heard. Wren splinted it with a boat strut and a tarp strap, her hands steady, her face gone pale and professional. Mara ran numbers out loud — evacuation times, retreat times, the ranger station nine days downstream, the headwaters five days up.

And Casper stood apart, looking upriver, looking at the gorge narrowing ahead into the Sworn-Off, and Okafor watched him do the arithmetic she already knew he was doing.

Retreat meant nine days downriver with a broken-wristed cook, through gorges they'd portaged once already, into weather that had eaten their margin. Push on meant five days to the source, lighter now, faster, with Rill's good arm and everyone else's stubbornness.

"Fifteen years ago," Wren said quietly. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, like a stone.

Everyone looked at Casper.

Okafor watched his face do something complicated and brief, like weather crossing a valley. She had read enough of him by now to know what was underneath: that there had been a storm once before, and a call to push on instead of turn back, and a man who died under his rope because of it. That the whole blacklisted ruin of his life sat downstream of a decision exactly like this one.

He could turn back. God knew every ghost he had was voting for it.

"We push on," Casper said.

"Casper—" Mara started.

"Listen." His voice was low and even, and Okafor noticed his hands were shaking, just slightly, and that he was looking at them with something like disgust. "Five days up. Nine days down. Rill can't paddle a rapid with one hand, but he can ride one lashed into a boat, and the upper gorge has fewer rapids than the lower — that's the whole reason it's never been run commercially. Downstream we fight the current twice. Upstream we fight it once and then it carries us home." He turned and looked at each of them, one at a time, and Okafor felt the weight of it land on her personally. "I'm not asking anyone to agree. I'm telling you the math. If anyone wants to say this is the same call, say it now, to my face, and we'll talk about it honestly."

Silence. Wind. Rill, from inside the boat-shelter, slightly delirious with pain and tea: "For what it's worth, I vote upstream. My brother's cooking is downstream of us too."

A ragged, shocked laugh went around the shelter — even Penn, wet-eyed, punched his brother's good shoulder. And Okafor understood, suddenly, what she was watching. It wasn't bravado. It wasn't the old Casper repeating the old sin. It was a man holding a live grenade of a decision and refusing to throw it blind, laying out the numbers, offering his own history up as evidence for the prosecution. *The same call,* she wrote later in her notebook, *and not the same call at all. Fifteen years ago he pushed on for a schedule. Today he pushes on because turning back kills more slowly.*

Underneath it, she added, and underlined three times: *But the mountain doesn't care about his reasons. Watch him.*

* * *

They broke camp at noon, into the teeth of the storm's second band, because the gap Casper had promised closed exactly when he said it would and not a minute after. They hauled the boats up the shelf trail, Rill strapped into the lead boat with his splinted wrist cradled against his chest and a look of profound betrayal directed at the sky. Penn took point on the tow line. Wren and Mara worked the middle. Okafor brought up the rear with the medical kit and the barometer, which she had confiscated.

"Why do you get the barometer?" Penn asked.

"Because I'm the only one who talks to it nicely."

The gorge swallowed them. Ahead, past the last of the mapped water, the walls drew together into the canyon Casper had sworn off and clearly never left — the Sworn-Off, the others called it now, half joke and half not — and Okafor saw him stop once at its mouth, just for a moment, one hand flat against the rock as if greeting something, or apologizing to it.

Then the snow thickened, and the river roared, and they went in.

Behind them, far below and growing fainter, Okafor caught one last glimpse of the cache fin through the driving white — the remaining barrels still lashed there, still waiting, patient as geology. She thought about mentioning it. She decided against it. Some things you carry forward; some things you leave for the mountain to keep.

The barometer, in her pocket, began to fall again.
