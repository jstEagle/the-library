## 12. The Confession at High Camp

High camp was a lie we told ourselves about shelter.

We'd cut a shelf into the windward ice below the last headwall of the gorge, three tents pinned to the mountain like postage stamps on an envelope nobody would ever mail. The wind came off the glacier in long gray fists and hammered the nylon all night, and by morning it hadn't decided to stop, so we stayed put — the kind of enforced idleness that is more dangerous to an expedition than any crevasse, because idle people talk.

I'd spent fifteen years making sure I was never idle enough to talk.

By midmorning the storm had settled into its working rhythm — not trying to kill us anymore, just reminding us it could — and the crew arranged itself around the stove in Penn's tent with the practiced economy of people who had learned each other's elbows. Rill sat cradling his splinted wrist against his chest like a small angry animal he'd agreed to foster. Okafor was logging samples, pencil scratching in the pauses between gusts. Penn stirred something that claimed to be oatmeal and mostly was. Mara sat apart, as she always did now, reading the sky through the tent door like it owed her money.

And Wren Crane sat across from me, watching me the way she'd been watching me for eleven days.

She'd gotten better at hiding it. That was the trouble with her — she learned fast. In the first week her stares had been naked, almost rude, the stare of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Now she'd wrapped it in politeness. She asked me questions about route-finding, about snow bridges, about how I'd known the chute at the first portage was going to let go before it let go. Innocent questions. Guide questions.

Except every one of them landed in the same place, and we both knew it.

"Casper." She said my name the way you'd set a cup down too hard. "Can I borrow you for a minute?"

"Pretty sure I'm the only thing out here that's yours to borrow," Penn said cheerfully. "Take him."

I followed her out. Of course I did. There are doors you can feel opening behind you for days, and when they finally open, the only dignity left to you is walking through under your own power.

The wind took us immediately. She led me along the shelf to the far edge, past the cache line, to a shoulder of blue ice where the rock wall broke the worst of it. A pocket of relative quiet, maybe ten feet square. She'd scouted it. She'd planned this.

Below us the Serrin ran white and furious through the gorge, a sound like a crowd that never stops applauding something terrible.

"You've been here before," Wren said.

Not a question. I gave it the respect of silence anyway.

"The gorge," she said. "Chapter nine, if you want to call it that. You led us through a canyon in a whiteout with no map, no GPS fix, no beta from anyone alive. You knew where every eddy was. You knew which bank the portage trail favored before we could see either bank." Her breath came out in silver flags. "Nobody knows a river like that from a guidebook, Casper."

"No," I said. "They don't."

"So when."

I looked at the water. It seemed only fair to tell it to the water first; it had heard most of it already, over the years, shouted or whispered or said aloud in the particular flat voice a man uses when there's no one there to hear him.

"After," I said. "For about six years after, I came back every season. Sometimes twice. Alone."

"Why?"

"Because grief is a route-finding problem," I said, "and I kept thinking if I ran it enough times I'd find the line where it stopped hurting."

"And did you?"

"There isn't one," I said. "But you get faster."

She didn't laugh. I'd meant it as almost a joke, the kind of joke that keeps a man upright, and she just looked at me with those eyes that were her brother's eyes — I'd known it since day one, since the crew assembled and she'd stuck out her hand and introduced herself and I'd felt the ground tilt under me like a boat hitting a wave wrong. Theo Crane's eyes. Theo's stubborn jaw, too, though she'd have hated hearing that, because everything else about her was her own: sharper, hungrier, less willing to be liked.

"Theo died here," she said. "In this gorge."

"Not this gorge." I shook my head. "Upstream. The upper narrows. But the same river. The same expedition." I pulled my glove off, flexed my bare hand in the cold until it burned, because some part of me has always believed I should pay these things in installments. "You've read the report."

"I've read your report. The official one. The one where the route shifted overnight and the anchor failed and it was nobody's fault except somehow entirely yours." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Wren — she never rose, she descended. "My father read it two hundred times. He hired a lawyer who read it four hundred. And then he spent fifteen years and most of his fortune getting this trip together, and do you know why?"

"I can guess."

"Guess out loud."

The wind filled the pause for me, generous as ever. Downriver, faintly, Penn was singing to his oatmeal. Some things deserve their soundtrack.

"Because Aldous Crane doesn't accept unfinished sentences," I said. "Your brother died on day nineteen of a thirty-day expedition, and the sentence never got finished, and it's been sitting on his desk for fifteen years like a bill he refuses to pay. So he bought himself a second expedition. Same river. Same guide, if he could get me — and he knew he could get me, because he knew what Mara had on me." I looked at her. "He didn't hire me despite what happened to Theo. He hired me *because* of it. I'm not a guide to him, Wren. I'm a loose end with excellent rope skills."

Something moved across her face — not surprise. Confirmation. She'd carried the suspicion up the whole river like ballast, and now she could finally set it down, and setting it down hurt.

"That's not the truth, though," she said quietly. "That's the shape around the truth. My father's version has you as a villain or a victim, depending on the hour. I want the actual night. I want what happened on day nineteen."

"Wren—"

"I'm dying, Casper."

The words went through me like a picket through old snow. I'd known — she'd told the fire circle three nights ago, half-truths traded like poker chips, her illness laid out calm as a weather forecast. But knowing a thing and having it aimed at you are different animals.

"I don't have the luxury of another fifteen years of you coming back alone to shout at the water," she said. "So you're going to give it to me straight, right now, in this wind, or I'm going to spend whatever time I have left building my own version, and I promise you mine will be worse than anything you could say. I inherited that from my father."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, awful bark of a laugh that the wind stole immediately. God, she was Theo all over. Theo used to say the same thing about deadlines: *give me the bad news now, Galloway, I'll build my own worse version by dinner.*

"All right," I said.

* * *

Here is what I told her, standing on that shelf of ice while the Serrin applauded below:

Day seventeen, we'd camped below the upper narrows, and I'd gone out at dawn to look at the entrance rapid, and I had stood there for forty minutes doing the arithmetic every guide does — water level, gradient, the sieve of boulders mid-channel, the way the current folded over itself like a bedsheet with something trapped underneath — and the arithmetic came back the same way it always comes back when the answer is no.

"It was runnable," I told her. "That's the devil of it. On paper. At that flow, with perfect execution, with a crew that had been running together for six weeks instead of six days. I'd run harder water. But the margin was tissue-paper thin, and the consequence side of the ledger was a strainer that ate everything it caught, and I looked at it and I said no. Out loud. To the whole team. I said we portage the narrows, lose a day and a half, and finish the descent on schedule anyway, because I'd built slack into the calendar for exactly this."

"Aldous called that night," Wren said. Not guessing. Knowing. She'd done her own forty minutes of arithmetic, somewhere, years ago.

"He called on the satellite phone. I remember because the wind was doing that thing where it sounds like someone breathing into the receiver, and I kept asking him to repeat himself." My bare hand had gone from burning to numb, which felt correct. "Theo's summit window — the final gorge run, the crown of the whole expedition — was scheduled for day twenty-two. Aldous had investors flying in. Helicopter footage booked. A press release already written, which I know because I later read it, dated two days before the accident. *Crane Expedition Conquers the Serrin.* Past tense. He'd written the ending before we got there."

"My portage cost him a day and a half," Wren said. "It wouldn't have fit the release."

"It wouldn't have fit the helicopter window," I said. "So he told me to run the narrows on schedule. And when I refused — and I want to be very clear, Wren, I refused, I said the words *no, sir* down a satellite phone to the richest man I'd ever spoken to — he didn't argue with me. He went around me. He made it Theo's decision."

The wind dropped, the way wind does at the worst moments, as if the mountain wants to hear too.

"Your brother was twenty-six years old and the strongest boater on that crew, and Aldous called him — I watched Theo take the call, watched his face change — and afterward Theo came to my tent and he wasn't angry, that's the thing I need you to understand, he wasn't angry at me at all. He was *embarrassed*. His father had told him the portage was cowardice dressed up as caution. Had told him the other guides were watching to see whether the Crane name ran hard water or walked around it. And Theo said—" My voice did something I hated. I pushed through it. "Theo said, *Casp, I'll scout it at first light. If it looks like your line holds, I'll run your line. If it doesn't, I'll walk. Deal?* And I said deal, because saying anything else would have been telling a grown man his father was poisoning him, and I didn't have the right, and I was a coward, and I have had fifteen years to decide which of those reasons was the real one and I still don't know."

"What did the light show?" Wren asked. Her voice had gone very small. Not soft — small, the way voices get when they're carrying something heavy.

"The light showed exactly what dusk had shown. Tissue paper. I told him. I stood on the bank and I told him the margin wasn't there and he should walk, and he looked at the water, and he looked at me, and he said—" I stopped.

"Say it," she whispered. "I want all of it. Even the parts that gut you. *Especially* the parts that gut you."

"He said, *Dad's watching from the ridge.*" The words came out flat and gray, rocks worn smooth by fifteen years of handling. "And I could have grabbed his throw rope. I could have sat on his boat. I could have radioed Aldous and told him his son was dead to me before he was dead at all. Instead I did the respectable thing. I logged my objection. Verbally. With a witness. And then I stood on the bank like a man following procedure while procedure killed my friend."

"He flipped in the strainer."

"He flipped in the strainer. The line held — his line was good, his line was *my* line, executed perfectly for eleven seconds — and then the eddy fence at the bottom did what eddy fences do, and the river took him sideways into the sieve, and I was in my boat before he finished going under, and I found him forty minutes later." I finally looked at her. "Forty minutes, Wren. I know the exact number because I checked my watch, because some animal part of my brain thought the number would matter later. It doesn't matter. Nothing about the number matters. He was gone in ninety seconds and I spent forty minutes proving it."

She was crying. She cried the way she did everything — silently, efficiently, without asking permission or forgiveness — and I stood there and let her, because the least I owed her was the sight of what my face did when it wasn't managing itself.

"And the report," she said finally. "The report says the route shifted. The anchor failed."

"Because that's what Mara wrote," I said. "Mara was expedition coordinator. She wrote the incident report, and she wrote it the way Aldous needed it written — equipment failure, act of God, tragic misjudgment in the field — and then the inquiry needed someone to hold it, and I raised my hand." I swallowed. "Because if I'd fought it, the real story comes out. Aldous pressured the schedule. Theo ran a condemned line to please his father. And Theo — dead Theo, heroic Theo, the son Aldous was already canonizing — becomes the man who killed himself for a press release. I couldn't do that to him. He'd carried my ropes for six years. So I signed the version where it was my misjudgment, and Mara looked at me across that table, and she knew — she *knew*, Wren, she'd read Theo's scouting notes, she knew I'd condemned the narrows in writing two days prior — and she said nothing. She let me burn. Because the contract for the next decade of her career came through Aldous Crane's office, and I was already ash, and ash doesn't testify."

The wind came back, all at once, like a held breath released.

Wren stood very still for a long time. Below us the river kept up its terrible applause. When she finally spoke, her voice had come back up out of that small heavy place, and it was steady, and it was worse than the crying.

"My father sent me on this trip," she said, "to be his eyes. And I spent eleven days thinking I was here to catch *you* in a lie." She wiped her face with the back of her glove, a rough impatient gesture. "I wasn't catching you. I was catching him. And I think some part of me has known since the confluence, since I saw those old survey markers of yours past the legal line — you weren't sneaking around, Casper. You were visiting a grave. Over and over. For fifteen years."

"Visiting and arguing with it," I said. "Mostly arguing."

"And Mara let you hang." She turned toward the tents, toward the thin thread of smoke from Penn's stove, toward the woman reading the sky through the door flap. Something in her posture had changed — settled, hardened, the way snow changes when the temperature drops and it stops being powder and starts being something that can break an ankle. "She stood in that inquiry and watched them take your license, your registry standing, both countries, everything — and she said nothing, because she was buying her future with your name."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up her hand, and it was Theo's gesture, exactly, the stop-palm he used when he'd heard enough. "Don't manage me. Don't soften it. You've carried this alone long enough that you've forgotten how heavy it is, and I'm telling you it's not yours alone anymore. It has three sets of fingerprints on it, and I intend to look at all of them."

She started back along the shelf toward camp, boots crunching, small and straight-backed against the wind. Then she stopped, and half-turned, and for one moment the fury in her face cracked and I saw underneath it — not forgiveness, nothing so cheap, but something rawer and more durable.

"She's going to deny it," Wren said. "And when she does, I'm going to be standing there with the truth, and you're going to be standing there with me. Both of us. Understood?"

"Understood," I said.

Because I'd seen Mara's face at supper, and I'd seen the way she'd gone quiet when the informant business split the camp, and I knew — the way you know weather, the way you know a river — that the storm coming off the headwaters wasn't meteorological at all.

It was already in camp.
