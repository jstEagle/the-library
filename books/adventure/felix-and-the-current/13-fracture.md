## 13. Fracture

Mara Voss had built her career on the principle that a fire should never be bigger than the story told around it. A small flame kept people close. It made them lean in. It made them listen to whoever held the floor.

Tonight she was holding the floor, and the fire was barely wider than her two spread hands, and nobody was leaning in at all.

They had made high camp on a shelf of gray stone above the gorge, tents pitched in the lee of an old moraine, and the wind came off the icefield in long, patient shoves, as if the mountain were trying to move them along like a shopkeeper nudging loiterers toward the door. The crew sat in a ragged crescent around the stove: Okafor with his notebook closed for once, Penn and Rill shoulder to shoulder — Rill's splinted wrist cradled against his chest like something borrowed and breakable — Casper at the edge of the light where he always sat, and Wren Crane standing, because she had been standing for ten minutes now, and no one had found it in themselves to ask her to sit.

Mara knew what was coming. She had known since sundown, when Wren had walked out of Casper's tent with her face doing that terrible young thing where grief and fury occupy the same expression and neither will yield. Fifteen years ago, Mara had worn that same face. She recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting.

"So," Wren said. "Let's talk about the testimony."

"Wren—" Casper started.

"Not you." She didn't look at him. That was the remarkable part, Mara thought. The whole expedition had assumed the reckoning would land on Galloway, the blacklisted man, the ghost with the rope in his past. But Wren had done the arithmetic faster than any of them. Her brother had died under Casper's rope, yes — but ropes don't schedule summits. Ropes don't pressure expeditions to move on time. And someone had let Casper carry all of it, alone, for fifteen years, while building a career on the ashes.

Someone like her.

"Mara." Wren turned, and the firelight caught her face, and Mara thought: *there she is. Aldous Crane's daughter after all.* "You testified. You stood in front of the inquiry board and you told them Casper Galloway ignored protocol, pushed past the turnaround window, and got Theo killed. Those were your words. I've read the transcript eleven times."

"I know," Mara said quietly. "I wrote them twice before I said them once."

"Then tell me why."

The wind shoved again. Somewhere up-valley, the ice boomed — that deep tectonic groan of a glacier settling its accounts — and everyone flinched except Casper, who had gone very still, the way he did when he was listening to the mountain decide something.

"Because I wanted the contract," Mara said.

It came out flat. She had rehearsed a hundred versions of this confession on the climb up from the confluence — versions with context, with mitigation, with the weather and the radio failure and the pressure from the outfitter — and now that the moment had arrived, every one of those versions felt like what it was: a lie wearing a nicer coat.

"The Crane expedition," she said. "The first one. Theo's expedition. It was the contract of a lifetime. Aldous Crane wanted the Serrin headwaters surveyed, and he wanted the best, and there were two names on every shortlist in the country. Mine and Casper's." She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "We'd been rivals since guide school. He beat me on the ice exam by four points. I beat him on rescue scenarios. We hated each other in the way only two twenty-five-year-olds with identical ambitions can hate each other — completely, and with enormous energy."

Penn made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled by politeness. Mara appreciated it. Somebody had to breathe.

"Aldous interviewed us both. Then he did something clever — he hired Casper to lead and me as second. Told me, privately, that if anything went wrong, the lead takes the fall, but the second inherits the reputation. That's how these things work. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it." She looked up. "I'm saying it."

"And then things went wrong," Okafor said. Not unkindly. Okafor had the gift of asking questions like a man handing you a heavy box rather than throwing it at you.

"Then things went wrong." Mara's voice caught on the ridge of it. "Aldous wanted the summit survey done on schedule because his syndicate investors were watching the dates. Casper called for a turnaround. I agreed with him — we argued about it in the tent, actually, both of us saying the same thing in different words, which is its own kind of argument. But Theo wanted the top. And Aldous leaned on him. And Theo was twenty-two and his father was watching the dates, and—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "Casper ordered the turn. Theo kept climbing. Casper went up after him. The rest is in the transcript, minus everything that matters."

"You could have told them," Wren said. "At the inquiry. You were there. You heard Aldous lean on him. You heard Casper call the turn."

"Yes."

"You could have said one sentence. One sentence and Casper keeps his certifications and my brother's death has an honest shape."

"One sentence," Mara agreed. "And I'd have spent the next fifteen years as the woman who torched the richest client in three provinces. Instead I spent them as the woman who inherited the reputation." She smiled, and it hurt her face to do it. "Do you know what I learned? Power doesn't corrupt you all at once. It corrupts you the way silt corrupts a river — grain by grain, until one day the water's a different color and you can't find the moment it changed."

* * *

For a while nobody spoke, and the fire did its small work, and Mara catalogued the crew the way she catalogued everything — by fault lines.

Okafor sat with his hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from, watching her with the patient, appalled attention of a scientist watching a reaction he'd predicted but hoped to be wrong about. Penn had put his arm around Rill. Rill stared into the flames with his jaw set, and Mara remembered — with the particular nausea of a leader remembering too late — that Rill was twenty-three. Theo's age. Everything in this boy's face said *I would not have climbed past the turn.*

Wren hadn't moved. Standing, arms crossed, wind pulling at her hood.

"You let him carry it," she said. "Fifteen years. Two blacklists. Do you know what they say about him downstream? They say he's careless. They say he gets people killed. There are bars in Ferrowdale where they won't serve him."

"I know. I've drunk in some of them."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It was inventory." Mara set down the stick she'd been worrying. "You want me to say I'm sorry, and I am, but sorry is a currency and I've been spending it in advance my whole life, so let me give you something better instead. Let me tell you exactly how it works, so you can watch for it in yourself, because you're going to have power someday — Crane money sees to that — and right now you think power is something other people abuse."

"Get to the point."

"The point is this: silence is never free. Every time you keep quiet to protect your position, you take out a loan. And the interest compounds in the dark where you can't see it, until one day the debt comes due and it's due in full, in public, around a fire exactly this size." She gestured at their miserable little blaze. "I took out that loan fifteen years ago. Tonight the bank called."

* * *

It was Rill who broke the shape of the thing, which surprised her; she'd had Penn pegged as the loyal one.

"So the whole trip," Rill said slowly. "Triple pay. The illegal route. All of it. Was that the loan coming due, or was it more borrowing?"

Mara looked at him. The kid had a splinted wrist and frost-nipped cheeks and the directness of someone who hadn't yet learned to soften questions, and she thought: *this is what I traded away. People who ask clean questions.*

"Honest answer?" she said. "Both. I needed Casper's knowledge of the upper gorge — nobody else alive knows it. And I told myself bringing him along was a kind of repayment. Which is the classic move, isn't it? Dressing up a debt as a gift."

"And the syndicate money?" Okafor asked. "The client. The samples-first arrangement?"

The fire popped. Above them the moraine shed a trickle of scree, ticking down the slope like a slow clock.

"I don't ask Aldous Crane about his investors," Mara said. "That's not innocence. That's strategy. There's a difference and I've spent fifteen years pretending there isn't."

"There it is," Wren said softly.

And the camp split — Mara watched it happen the way you watch a berg calve, the crack running fast and then the whole face going.

Wren stepped away from the fire first, physically away, taking her sleeping bag and moving to the far side of the moraine with the finality of a border being drawn. Okafor hesitated, looked at Mara, looked at Wren, and gathered his kit with an apologetic shrug that somehow managed to convey both *I don't condone this* and *I'm going anyway*. He settled near Wren. Two against the dark.

Penn stayed put, arm still around his brother. "We're paid through the source," he said. "Whatever's true about fifteen years ago, we finish the route. That's not a side. That's a job."

Rill pulled free of his brother's arm. For a second the brothers just looked at each other, and Mara saw the whole history of sibling negotiation pass between them — every shared bunk and borrowed jacket and divided inheritance of chores — compressed into three seconds of eye contact.

"I'm with her," Rill said, nodding toward Wren. "Sorry, Penn."

"Don't be sorry. Be warm. You're useless with one hand." But Penn's voice had gone rough, and he busied himself with the stove until it passed.

Which left Casper, who had not moved, who had said nothing through all of it, sitting at the edge of the light with his forearms on his knees like a man waiting out weather.

"Well?" Mara said. "Say something. You've earned the floor more than anyone."

Casper considered the fire for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was the low even voice he used on sketchy ground, the one that made people stop talking and start placing their feet.

"Fifteen years I thought I hated you," he said. "Turns out I just envied you. You got to feel guilty in private. I had to do it in public." He stood, joints cracking, and picked up his bedroll. "For what it's worth, Mara — I knew. About the inquiry. About what you left out. I've known since year three, when I found your field notes from the North Col in a secondhand gear shop in Ferrowdale. Your handwriting. Your version of events. You wrote the truth down and then you buried it in a donation bin."

Mara felt the ground tilt, the way it does when the slope you've trusted turns out to be corniced. "You knew? And you said nothing?"

"I figured you'd earned the right to bury it your own way." He paused at the edge of the firelight. "But Wren deserved to dig it up. So thank her tomorrow. Not me."

He walked into the dark toward the tents, and the wind took his shape and folded it away.

* * *

Later — much later, when the camp had gone to sleep in its two hostile clusters and the fire had burned down to a red eye — Mara sat awake and did what she always did with shame: she organized it.

That was the corruption, she understood now, watching the embers. Not the original sin. The filing system afterward. Fifteen years of telling herself the silence had been tactical, then necessary, then ancient history, then — most poisonous of all — *a favor to Casper*, because surely a man with nothing left had less to lose than a woman with everything ahead. She had committed the oldest crime in leadership: she had mistaken her own convenience for the greater good, and then promoted herself to the office of deciding what the greater good was.

And the crew had seen it. Grain by grain, the water changing color, and now here they were on a shelf of stone above a forbidden gorge, split down the middle, with five days of the worst terrain on the river between them and the source.

She should sleep. She wouldn't. Instead she pulled out her ledger — the actual paper notebook, the one Okafor teased her about, the one where she tracked rations and rope counts and debts — and by the ember-light she turned to a fresh page and wrote, in her careful surveyor's hand:

*Owed: C. Galloway, fifteen years, compounding.*
*Owed: T. Crane, posthumous, noncollectable.*
*Owed: W. Crane, explanation, partially rendered.*
*Method of payment: TBD.*

She was closing the book when she saw it — a flicker of light down in the gorge, far below, wrong for stars, wrong for reflections. A steady white point, moving in short deliberate strokes along the canyon wall. Not a lantern. Lanterns swung.

She counted the interval between flashes. Three seconds. Deliberate. Surveyed.

Somebody was staking the gorge below them in the dark, and they were using lights patterned exactly like the ones Aldous Crane's advance teams used on every project Mara had ever run for him.

She reached over and shook Casper's tent pole, gently, the way you wake a man you owe fifteen years to.

"Galloway," she whispered. "You need to see what's downstream."
