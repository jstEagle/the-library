## 14. The Dam Surveyors

Penn had always believed that a man could tell the character of a crew by how they cooked in bad weather. His brother Rill, wrist splinted and useless since the whiteout, had taken to sitting by the fire like a disgraced king, issuing instructions — *more salt, less salt, why are you looking at me like that* — and Penn had learned to tune him out the way you learn to tune out wind.

It was on one of those gray mornings, hauling the breakfast pot up from the riverbank, that Penn saw the smoke.

Not their smoke. Their fire sat low and screened behind a shelf of granite, the way Casper insisted, the way Penn had grumbled about for three weeks without ever once disobeying. This smoke rose thin and blue and confident from the far bank, half a mile upstream where the Serrin bent around a shoulder of scree, and it came with something no expedition this far into forbidden country should have: the smell of frying bacon.

Penn stood very still with the pot in his hands.

"Rill," he said quietly.

"I said *less* salt—"

"Rill."

His brother followed his gaze. For once, Rill said nothing at all.

They woke Casper first, because that was simply what you did — the way you'd wake the captain of a ship before you woke the passengers. Casper was already sitting up when Penn reached him, which was its own small horror; the man slept like a heron, one eye apparently always open on the world.

"Smoke," Penn said. "Far bank. Bacon."

Casper was on his feet before the word finished. "Bacon."

"Somebody's eating better than us."

"Everybody eats better than us." But there was nothing joking in Casper's face as he pulled on his boots. He crossed to Mara's tent and stood outside it for a long moment, and Penn watched something pass over him — not hesitation exactly, but the look of a man reading a letter he already knows is bad news.

"Mara," he said. "Up. We've got company."

The camp came awake in stages after that: Okafor buttoning his jacket over his field kit with hands that were already reaching for his instruments, Wren emerging pale and sharp-eyed from her tent, the altitude having chewed on her all night. Nobody spoke above a murmur. They glassed the far bend from behind the rock shelf, passing the binoculars in silence.

Six figures. Orange survey markers staked in lines along the gravel bar. A tripod. And half-buried in the willows, catching the gray light like a beetle's back —

"A helicopter pad," Okafor said softly. "They're grading a helicopter pad."

"They're staking the headwaters." Wren's voice had gone flat and strange. She lowered the binoculars slowly, the way you'd lower a weapon. "Those are Crane Consolidated stakes. I know the marking scheme. I grew up looking at it on my father's job sites."

Nobody said anything. The river said it for them, running cold and constant under the silence.

"Surveyors don't hike six days up the Sworn-Off Gorge," Okafor went on, in the tone of a man laying out evidence he wishes were wrong. "There's an access cut on the east ridge. Dozed, recently. That's heavy equipment trail. That means roads are planned. Roads mean—"

"Dams," Penn said. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. It just fell out of him, the way truth does when it's been sitting too long in a man's chest. "Series of them, probably. Step the whole valley down like stairs. My uncle worked a project like that in the Kettle Range. First thing they do is send in the surveyors. Last thing they do is send in everyone else."

Wren turned to Mara.

She didn't shout. That was the thing Penn would remember afterward, telling it to himself in the years that came after — she didn't shout, didn't cry, didn't do any of the things a person might have been forgiven for doing. She just turned, very slowly, and looked at Mara Voss the way you'd look at a map that has finally, unmistakably, shown you where you actually are.

"You knew," Wren said.

Mara stood with her arms folded against the cold, facing the far bank, facing the orange stakes, facing everything except the girl beside her. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way ice is level — flat, load-bearing, and liable to crack under you.

"I knew the client wanted samples first. I knew the syndicate had interests beyond one old man's health. I did not know about dams."

"But you suspected."

A pause. The wind moved through the willows across the water.

"I suspected," Mara said, "that Aldous Crane does not spend triple rates on anything he can't own."

*

The camp fractured the way ice fractures — not all at once, but along lines that had been waiting all along.

Rill, splinted wrist and all, was the first to say aloud what several of them were thinking: that triple pay was triple pay, that a man with a broken wing couldn't afford principles, that maybe the syndicate would pay a bonus for discretion. He said it defensively, preemptively, daring anyone to judge him, and Penn felt something in his chest fold closed around his brother like a hand.

Okafor said nothing at all. He packed his sample kit with great care, and then he unpacked it, and then he repacked it, and Penn understood that the geologist was deciding something enormous inside that methodical little ritual.

And Casper — Casper walked down to the river's edge alone and stood looking upstream toward the source none of them could see yet, and Penn, who had cooked for guides and poachers and one memorable season of fugitive botanists, recognized the posture. It was the posture of a man standing at a fork he'd stood at before, and knowing which path he'd taken last time, and knowing what it had cost.

Penn went and stood next to him. He didn't say anything. That was mostly his contribution to the expedition generally — showing up and not saying anything — but Casper seemed to accept it.

"My brother thinks we should take the money," Penn said eventually.

"Your brother broke his wrist falling off a shelf in a whiteout because I made the call to push on instead of turning back." Casper's voice was rough. "He's earned the right to think whatever he wants."

"That's not an answer."

"No." Casper watched the water. "It isn't."

Downstream, voices rose and dropped again. Wren and Mara, going at each other in low, hard tones, the argument of two people who have both realized the fight stopped being about facts some time ago. Penn caught fragments — *you used me*, from Wren; *I kept you alive*, from Mara; *that's not the same thing and you know it* — and then the wind took the rest.

"What happens now?" Penn asked. "Practically speaking. If the syndicate's already got surveyors up here, they've got a schedule. Equipment coming in. Men."

"Then we're standing on a construction site that doesn't know it's been built yet." Casper turned from the river. His face had settled into something Penn hadn't seen before — not the haunted watchfulness that had ridden him the whole trip, but something older and simpler underneath it. Decision, maybe. Or the space right before decision, which looks almost identical and weighs ten times as much. "We go up. Today. Faster than we've gone anywhere. Because if the source is real, and if it's poison like Okafor suspects, then the only thing worse than Crane's people finding it is Crane's people finding it and nobody left up here to say otherwise."

"And if Mara's still working for them?"

Casper looked back at the camp, at Mara standing apart from the others with her jaw set like a woman holding a door shut against weather.

"Then we'll find out," he said, "at the worst possible moment. Same as always."

*

They broke camp fast and moved faster, single file along the east bank, putting miles between themselves and the orange stakes. By early afternoon the surveyors were somewhere behind them, and the valley narrowed, and the river began to make sounds it hadn't made lower down — deeper sounds, older ones, water talking to itself in the dark under the ice.

They made high camp in a hollow below a moraine, and it was there, with the light failing and the temperature dropping through freezing like a stone through open water, that Mara called the crew together.

She stood with her back to the fire so her face was in shadow, and Penn thought later that this was deliberate — that Mara Voss had spent her whole life making sure people saw exactly as much of her as served her purposes, and that tonight, for the first time he'd witnessed, she wanted to be seen entirely and couldn't manage it.

"The radio," she said.

She held it up. It was a syndicate unit — Penn knew the make, heavy-bodied, encrypted, the kind of thing that cost more than his truck. It had lived in the bottom of her gear pack since the confluence. He'd noticed it weeks ago and told himself it was standard satellite comms, because the alternative was a thought he hadn't been willing to have.

"It's mine," Mara said. "Issued to me at the start. Check-in schedule every forty-eight hours. Position reports. Condition reports." She turned the radio over in her hands. "The informant we caught at the border — the one I paid off — wasn't reporting to any conservation authority. There never was a conservation authority. He was reporting to me, and I was reporting through this, and every mile this crew has climbed, someone downstream has known exactly where we were."

The silence that followed had weight. Rill's mouth opened and closed. Okafor set down his cup with exaggerated care, the way you do when throwing it would feel too good.

"Why tell us now?" Wren asked. Her voice was quiet and terrible. "Why not keep the lie until the summit?"

"Because I watched you turn away from your father's money today," Mara said, "and I realized I've been standing on the wrong side of that line my entire career, telling myself it was the winning side." She looked around the circle, at each of them, missing no one. "I testified against Casper Galloway fifteen years ago for a contract. I told myself it was justice. It was ambition wearing justice's coat. And I have spent this entire expedition telling myself that a syndicate payday was different from that testimony, and it is not different. It is the same sin with better gear."

She crouched, and fed the radio into the fire.

It didn't go easily. The casing buckled and smoked and spat, and the battery vented with a sound like a small angry animal, and Mara held it down in the coals with a stick until the thing was unmistakably dead, and then she stayed crouched there a moment longer, looking at what she'd done, as if checking her work.

When she stood, her face was streaked with firelight and something that might have been relief or might have been grief, and Penn supposed that at a certain altitude the two were indistinguishable.

"There's no check-in tonight," she said. "By morning, the syndicate will assume we've gone dark. They'll assume the worst, or they'll assume betrayal, and either way they'll come up that access road with more men than we have and better gear than we deserve." She straightened. "So we reach the source first. Tonight if we have to. Tomorrow at the latest. And when Aldous Crane arrives — and he will arrive, because he's my client and he's Wren's father and men like him always arrive — he'll find a full crew standing between him and that spring, and every one of us will testify to what it really is."

"And if it's not poison?" Rill asked. "If it's real? If it actually works?"

Mara looked at Wren. Something passed between them that Penn couldn't read — an apology, maybe, or its skeleton.

"Then we'll burn that bridge when we come to it," she said. "But we'll burn it ourselves. Not for them."

*

They moved through the last hours of light in a silence that was somehow lighter than the silence before. Penn cooked a hasty meal of oats and the last of the dried apples, and Rill salted it, badly, with his good hand, and nobody complained, and that small ordinary act — a broken crew eating bad food together — did more to mend the fracture than any speech could have.

Casper took first watch. Penn brought him a cup of tea around midnight, because that was his way of saying things, and found the guide standing at the edge of the hollow, looking up-valley to where the glacier's shoulder glowed faintly against the stars.

"She bought herself back," Penn said. "That's not nothing."

"No," Casper agreed. "It's not nothing." He drank his tea. "It's also not enough. Redemption's a debt, not a purchase. You pay it in installments, and the interest runs whether you show up or not."

"You sound like a man who knows."

"I sound like a man holding the ledger." He handed the cup back. "Get some sleep, Penn. Tomorrow we walk past the end of the maps."

Penn was halfway back to his bedroll when Casper spoke again, so quietly the wind nearly took it.

"And Penn? Whatever we find up there — however bad it gets — thank you for the tea. And the salt arguments. A crew is a strange thing to be grateful for. But I am."

Penn grinned in the dark, where nobody could see it, and went to sleep.

He woke before dawn to Okafor shaking his shoulder, and the geologist's face in the firelight told him everything before a word was spoken.

"They're moving," Okafor said. "Headlamps on the east ridge. A dozen of them, maybe more. And Penn—" he paused, and the pause was worse than the words, "—there's machinery noise. Coming up the access cut. They're not waiting for the source anymore."

Above them, against the last of the stars, the glacier's shoulder glowed — and somewhere beyond it, faint as a heartbeat, the sound of the helicopter.
