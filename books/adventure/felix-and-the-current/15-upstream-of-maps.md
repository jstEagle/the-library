## 15. Upstream of Maps

The last marker was a rusted iron pin driven into a granite shoulder sometime before I was born, and when we passed it, Okafor touched it the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old man at a funeral. Then the river stopped being the Serrin.

That's not quite true. It was still the Serrin on every map my father owned — and my father owned maps the way other men own racehorses, obsessively, expensively, framed behind glass in his study. But up here the river forgot its manners. It split into eight channels, braided itself back into four, abandoned a channel mid-curve like a sentence it had lost interest in finishing. It ran milky with rock flour, the color of old bone, of milk poured over slate, of something that hadn't decided yet what it wanted to be. There was no bank, exactly. There were banks, dozens of them, migrating nightly while we slept, so that every morning Penn would stand at the edge of camp with his hands on his hips and announce, with the weary authority of a man reading his own obituary, "Well. The river moved again."

"Where to?" Rill asked, wrist splinted and useless in its sling, which did not stop him from being the first one to ask questions.

"Everywhere," said Penn. "It's everywhere now. We're not following a river anymore. We're following a rumor."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, at altitude where laughing costs you something — and Casper looked over at me with that expression he'd been wearing more and more lately, the one that wasn't quite surprise and wasn't quite suspicion but lived somewhere in the neighborhood between them, paying rent to both.

"You think this is funny," he said.

"I think if I don't find it funny I'll find it terrifying, and terror is very expensive at nine thousand feet."

He considered that. He was getting better at considering things I said instead of just enduring them, which I counted as progress, though progress between me and Casper Galloway moved at roughly the speed of glacial melt, which is to say: visibly, if you squinted, over years.

"Fair," he said finally, and shouldered his pack, and we walked upstream of maps.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about dying slowly: you get very good at arithmetic.

Not the dramatic kind — not the *how many months* arithmetic, though I've done that too, in waiting rooms with posters about sun safety, doing sums that came back wrong no matter how carefully I carried the ones. I mean the small kind. The constant kind. How many steps to the latrine trench and back before my legs filed a formal complaint. How many calories a day of pretending I felt fine before the pretending collapsed under its own weight. How long I could carry my pack before someone noticed I'd been quietly transferring weight out of it for two weeks, a few pounds at a time, into Rill's boat bag because Rill was too proud to notice and too injured to care.

Altitude ruined all of it. Altitude took my careful arithmetic and threw it in the river.

It started on the third day past the last marker, as a headache with ambitions. By the fourth day the headache had achieved its goals and expanded its platform. By the fifth morning I woke inside my own body the way you wake inside a coat that belongs to someone much larger — everything heavy, everything padded wrong, my hands distant employees who required written instructions. My lips had gone a color I didn't have a name for, a blue-adjacent shade that Okafor clocked in approximately four seconds because Okafor missed nothing, least of all colors.

"Your oxygen saturation is garbage," he told me that evening, crouched beside me with the pulse oximeter he'd brought along for exactly this reason and had been, I suspected, checking me with secretly for days.

"It's fine."

"It is objectively not fine. It is the opposite of fine. It is a number that would make a nurse sit down next to you and start talking in a calm voice."

"I've heard your calm voice, Okafor. It's worse than the number."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, and went and stood by the fire talking to Mara in a low voice, and I watched Casper watch them, and I understood that the arithmetic had changed for everyone now, not just me.

Here is the thing I had not planned for — and I had planned for a great deal, sitting in hospital chairs, composing contingency plans the way other people compose letters: I had not planned for what it would do to the crew. Illness, I had learned, is a private country. You cross its border alone; you conduct your business there alone; you come back and lie about where you've been. But there is no privacy at altitude. There is only canvas and wind and six people who can hear you breathing.

Mara proposed turning back on day five. Casper said no.

I want to be fair to both of them, because fairness turned out to matter more than I expected, up here where nothing else was available in quantity. Mara's no was the no of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning what her ambition cost other people and could not bear to spend more of mine. She said it quietly, without leverage, which from Mara was practically a confession of love.

Casper's no was different. Casper crouched down next to my bedroll, out of earshot of the others, and looked at me with those gray eyes that had been watching me since the day we met like I was a ghost he owed money to, and he said:

"How bad?"

And because he asked it that way — not *are you okay*, which is a question people ask so they can be reassured, but *how bad*, which is a question people ask so they can do math — I told him the truth. All of it. The diagnosis, the timeline, the reason my father believed a river could succeed where eleven specialists and two experimental trials had failed. The whole ugly ledger.

He listened without moving. When I finished, he sat back on his heels and looked at the fire for a long time.

"My father thinks I'm here to legitimize his claim," I said. "You know that. I'm not sure I'm even here for the water anymore. I think maybe I'm here because it's the last trip." I made myself say it plainly. "The last one I'll ever be strong enough for."

Casper nodded slowly, the way he did when he was absorbing something rather than agreeing with it.

"Then we go on," he said. "But we go on differently. You don't carry a pack anymore."

"That's ridiculous. I'm—"

"You don't carry a pack," he said again, in the voice I'd heard him use exactly twice before, once at the portage and once at high camp, the voice that wasn't asking anyone's permission, including his own. "You carry yourself. That's the job now. It's a real job. It's harder than carrying a pack. Most people can't do it."

And that — God help me — that was the kindest thing anyone had said to me in three years, and it was delivered like a work order, and I lay there in my bedroll blinking at the stars and understanding that I was going to cry, and that if I cried he would pretend very hard not to notice, and that this too was a kindness.

* * *

He carried me for the first time on the sixth day, across a stretch of braid where the channels had merged into something deep and fast and mean, and the crossing route he'd scouted involved forty yards of slick, tilted stone above water that would kill you in ninety seconds flat.

"I can walk it," I said.

"No," he said.

"Casper—"

"The stone's got a film of ice on it. You slip, you slide, you're gone before anyone finishes saying your name. This isn't a negotiation. It's a briefing." He crouched, back to me, hands ready. "Arms around my neck. Don't strangle me. Well — strangle me a little. It helps with balance."

So I climbed onto the legend of the Serrin gorge like a child, and I felt him take my weight — all of it, easily, insultingly easily, this man in his fifties with a face like a topographic map of himself — and he walked out onto the ice-filmed stone with me on his back and the river roaring below, and he walked like the stone was a hallway in his own house.

Which, I realized somewhere in the middle of those forty yards, it was.

He talked while he walked. Not to me, exactly. To the stone, maybe, or to whoever he talked to when he was alone up here, which I already knew was often, which I already knew was always. Little things. "Loose bit coming up, left side." "Don't look down, you'll hate it." And once, so quiet I nearly missed it under the water: "Fifteen years, and you're still trying to take somebody off this rock. Give it a rest."

I pressed my forehead against the back of his shoulder and said nothing, and he pretended not to notice, and we were both lying, and it was fine.

On the far side he set me down with exaggerated care, like cargo, and announced to the crew: "She rides from here on the bad crossings. Anybody has a problem with that, take it up with the river."

Nobody took it up with the river.

Rill, from his sling, raised his good hand and said, "For the record, I would also accept being carried," and Penn said, "You weigh as much as a boat anchor, brother, the offer expired years ago," and the laughter that went around the circle was thin and oxygen-starved and completely genuine, and I sat on a rock in the middle of nowhere, upstream of every map my father owned, and felt something in my chest that had nothing to do with my lungs.

Tenderness, growing in ground that shouldn't hold anything. That's the only way I can put it. Up here nothing grows — no trees past the last krummholz, no grass worth the name, just lichen winning its slow arguments with stone. And yet there it was, taking root anyway, in the worst soil imaginable: Casper carrying me without complaint and without ceremony, Penn saving me the soft portions of everything, Okafor adjusting our pace downward each morning and never once making it sound like pity, framing it always as geology — *"the talus needs a slower line today"* — Mara walking beside me in silence for hours because she understood, better than any of them, what it cost to be carried by your own history.

Even the river softened, some evenings. In the golden hour the braids would run pink and gold, eight threads of light converging and diverging, and it looked less violent then, less like a rumor and more like a promise. I started photographing it. Not for my father. For me. I had begun, without announcing it, keeping a private ledger of beautiful things, the way a person keeps receipts they intend to argue with later.

* * *

On the eighth night past the marker, I couldn't sleep, and I climbed out of my tent — slowly, in installments, the way I did everything now — and found Casper sitting alone at the fire's last coals, mending a strap.

"Couldn't sleep either?" I asked.

"I sleep fine," he said. "I just like this hour. It's the only honest one. Everything's too tired to lie."

I lowered myself onto a rock near him, and we sat in the silence that had become, without either of us signing anything, a shared language. Above us the sky did its obscene mountain thing, more stars than darkness, the Milky Way spilled across the gorge like flour across a black table.

"Do you ever think about him?" I asked. I hadn't meant to ask it. It came out the way questions do at altitude, unescorted. "My brother."

The mending stilled. For a moment I thought I'd broken something.

"Every day," he said. "Some days it's a chore. Some days it's the only thing I do."

"What was he like? Out here. On the mountain."

Casper set the strap down. He looked into the coals, and when he spoke, his voice had changed registers — lower, rougher, the voice of a man opening a drawer he kept locked in daylight.

"He sang. Badly. Constantly. He had maybe six songs and he knew four lines of each, and he'd stitch them together into these... Frankenstein hymns." A breath that might have been a laugh. "Drove me insane. I told him once if he sang on the rope I'd cut him loose myself, and he looked at me dead serious and said, 'Then I guess you'll have to climb fast.' And he grinned, and I grinned, and I have hated myself for loving that grin ever since, because loving it is what made me trust him past the point where trusting was safe."

"He never mentioned any of you," I said. "In his letters home. Just 'the team,' 'the guide.' I used to imagine the guide as — I don't know. Older. Kinder."

"I'm older now," Casper said. "Working on kinder. It's slow going. The material resists."

I laughed, and it hurt, and it helped.

"Why didn't you tell me sooner?" I asked. "At the start. You knew who I was from day one. You looked at me like—" 

"Like a ghost," he said. "Yeah. I know how I looked at you." He picked the strap back up, worked the needle through, bought himself a few seconds. "Because telling you would have been for me, not for you. You needed a guide. You didn't need my guilt dumped on you at the trailhead like extra ballast. And because—" he stopped, restarted. "Because I figured once you knew, you'd look at me the way everyone else does, and I wanted — I wanted a few more weeks of being just a man who carries packs."

"And now?"

"Now you're stuck with the whole inventory." He tied off the mend, tested it, handed me the strap — my pack strap, I realized, the one I'd worn soft over weeks of pretending to carry weight. "How's that."

"Strong," I said, and meant several things.

We sat until the coals went to ash-gray, and the cold came up out of the stones like a tide, and eventually he said, "Get some sleep. Tomorrow's the big push — we clear the headwall, and after that, if Okafor's survey is right, we're a day from the source."

"Are you scared?" I asked, as I rose. "Of what we'll find?"

He considered it honestly, which was the thing about Casper — he wasted nothing, including questions.

"I'm scared it'll be nothing," he said. "And I'm scared it won't be."

* * *

We cleared the headwall at noon the next day, the whole crew strung out along the switchbacks like beads on a fraying cord, and from the top the land opened into a valley no map had ever bothered to insult with a guess. The river ran through it young and furious, all white water and conviction, and beyond it, maybe ten miles upstream, the glacier stood against the sky like a held breath.

Okafor raised his binoculars, swept the valley once, and went very still.

"Wren," he said. His voice had a strange, flattened quality, the voice of a man reporting numbers he doesn't believe. "You need to see this."

I took the glasses. Followed his line. Found the glacier, found the pale smear of the spring at its toe —

— and found, beside it, catching the sun, a cluster of structures. Tents. A mast. A helicopter pad, freshly graded, its surface still scarred dark where the machines had landed.

And figures. Small with distance, unmistakable with intent. Men moving with purpose among stakes and survey flags, red pennants snapping in the wind, planted in the earth of the source like claims, like teeth.

"They're already here," Okafor said quietly.

Behind me, Casper had gone the color of the rock he stood on. Mara lifted her own binoculars, and I heard her breathe one word, barely aloud, and it was not a swear and it was not a prayer, though it may have been both:

"Aldous."
