## 16. The Source

The river stopped being a river two days before we found where it was born.

Upstream of the last survey marker — my marker, the one I'd driven into frozen ground fifteen years ago and never told a living soul about — the Serrin came apart into a hundred threads, braiding and rebraiding across a valley of gray gravel like an old man trying to remember a story he'd only heard once. There was no channel anymore. There were channels. We stopped paddling and started walking, hauling the boats on sleds Okafor had insisted we bring and Penn had mocked him for, and I will admit here, in the privacy of my own skull, that watching Penn eat that particular crow was one of the small warm pleasures of an otherwise brutal week.

"Say it," Okafor said, falling into step beside me on the third morning of the portage.

"Say what?"

"You want to say it."

"I don't know what you're talking about, Doctor."

"Casper." He adjusted his glasses, which fogged the moment he exhaled, which happened roughly every nine seconds at this altitude. He'd taken to wiping them with a cloth he kept tucked in his sleeve like a magician. "I have spent eleven days in a boat with Penn. I have heard every joke the man owns, some of them twice. I have earned one moment of smugness. Say it."

"The sleds were a good call," I said.

He beamed behind his fogged lenses and nearly walked into a boulder. I let him. Fair's fair.

Wren was ahead of us, and she was not well. She'd stopped pretending otherwise around the time the valley opened up. The altitude sat on her chest like a cat that had decided to stay; you could see her counting her breaths, rationing them out, spending them only on steps that mattered. She refused help carrying her pack. She accepted half of it into Rill's boat-sled when she thought nobody was looking, and Rill, wrist splinted and strapped against his chest, pretended not to notice her pretending. There was a whole economy of mercy running through this crew, invisible, unspoken, everyone quietly overpaying everyone else.

Mara brought up the rear. She hadn't said more than forty words a day since the dam surveyors' stakes. Something in her had settled — or hardened, hard to tell the difference from the outside — and she moved through the braided valley like a woman who had finally paid off a debt and was waiting to find out if the creditor believed her.

We camped that night under a sky so full of stars it looked like something had spilled. Penn made soup out of almost nothing, which was his particular genius, and declared it "reconstructed leek and potato," which was his particular delusion.

"It's hot water with ambition," Rill said.

"It's *terroir*," Penn said.

"It's the same packet as yesterday."

"Yesterday's terroir was different terroir."

I lay back on my pad and listened to them argue and thought: this is the last night. Tomorrow we reach it. And whatever we find there, this — the arguing, the steam off the mugs, Rill laughing with his arm in a sling — this is the thing worth carrying back down.

* * *

We smelled it before we saw it.

Not a bad smell. That was the first wrong note, though none of us knew it yet. Sulfur would have been honest. This was mineral and cold and faintly sweet, like the air over a struck match that never quite catches, and it came threading down the valley on a wind that had traveled over ice no map had ever named.

Okafor felt it first. He stopped mid-step, nose up, glasses forgotten, and said, quietly, "Oh," in the voice of a man meeting someone he'd only read about.

The valley narrowed after that, walls rising on either side, and then the walls fell away all at once and we stood at the head of everything.

I have guided people to summits. I have watched grown men cry at sunrise on ridgelines they'd sold their marriages to reach. None of it prepared me for the spring.

It came out of the glacier's root like light forced through a keyhole. The water pooled in terraces of stone the color of old bone, and each terrace held a different shade of blue-green I don't have words for — luminous, glowing from underneath, as if the mountain itself had a lantern lit somewhere in its chest. Steam rose off the pools in slow ribbons. Minerals had built up over centuries into shelves and curtains and delicate spires, all of it glazed and gleaming, all of it beautiful in a way that felt less like scenery and more like invitation.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Even Penn.

Then Wren stepped forward, and I saw her face, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She looked healed. That's the only way to put it. Two days of altitude sickness and rationed breath, and standing at the edge of that glowing water she looked like the healthy version of herself — color in her cheeks, eyes bright, standing straighter, as if the spring were already working on her from twenty feet away. She turned to me and smiled and said, "Casper. It's real. It's actually real," and her voice had a shine to it too.

"Nobody drinks anything," I said.

"Casper—"

"*Nobody drinks anything.* Not a drop. Not on your skin if we can help it. Okafor samples first, and we wait for his word." I kept my voice level, guide-voice, the one that doesn't invite discussion. Inside, something was clawing. Because I knew that look. I had worn that look once, on a different mountain, looking at a summit that glowed just as convincingly.

Wren's smile faltered, but the brightness stayed in her eyes, and that frightened me more than the water did.

* * *

Okafor worked for four hours.

He took samples from three terraces, labeled everything twice, ran his field kit's spectrometer until its battery died and then swapped in the backup he'd also been mocked for bringing. We built a windbreak around him out of boat sleds. Mara stood watch at the valley mouth, though watch against what, none of us asked. Wren sat near the largest pool with her boots off and her feet nowhere near the water, just looking at it, drinking it in with her eyes, and I understood with terrible clarity that for her this pool had already become a person — a hope with a shoreline — and that facts were going to have to fight a very well-loved opponent.

Penn tried to keep spirits up by narrating Okafor's work like a cooking show. "And now the doctor reduces the sample — oh, careful, careful — he wouldn't want to lose any of that precious glacial essence—"

"Penn," Rill said.

"I'm helping."

"You're narrating."

"Somebody has to. The doctor's got the emotional range of a thermometer."

But even Penn went quiet when Okafor finally looked up, took off his glasses, wiped them with the sleeve-cloth, put them back on, and looked down again, as if hoping the numbers would apologize and rearrange themselves.

"Well?" Mara called from the mouth of the valley.

Okafor didn't answer her. He looked at me instead, and I have seen Okafor deliver bad news about rockfall and bad news about weather and bad news about the structural integrity of a rope bridge, and he does it plainly, like a man handing you a receipt. What I saw on his face was worse than plain. It was grief.

"Come here," he said. "All of you. You should hear this standing up."

We gathered. Wren came last, reluctantly, like someone being called away from a bedside.

"The water is extraordinary," Okafor began. "That part is true. The mineral load is unlike anything in the literature. Dissolved solids off every chart I own. The luminescence is real chemistry — suspended metal salts catching light, essentially the spring glowing with dissolved ore." He paused. "Iron. Manganese. Arsenic in concentrations I initially assumed were instrument error. I recalibrated twice. Then cadmium, and lead, and a mercury signature that—" He stopped himself, the way a man stops himself from describing a wound in detail at dinner.

"Say it plainly, Doctor," Mara said.

"All right." He folded his hands, a lecturer steadying himself. "This water tastes of vitality because heavy metals often do. Iron gives a sweetness. Some of these compounds produce flushing, warmth, a sense of clarity, even euphoria. A man who drank this would feel *wonderful*. For weeks, possibly months. His blood work might even improve in superficial ways — hemoglobin up, color in his cheeks. And all the while the arsenic and mercury would be doing their quiet work in his organs, irreversible, cumulative, elegant. By the time the symptoms arrived they would look like the disease he was trying to cure, only faster." He looked around at us, and his voice dropped. "Whoever spread the stories about this place — whether they knew it or not — they weren't describing a cure. They were describing a poison that flatters you on the way down."

Silence. The steam rose off the terraces. Somewhere in the glacier, deep down, something cracked and settled, the mountain clearing its throat.

"Aldous Crane would drink this," Okafor said gently, to Wren, "and feel saved. And it would kill him slowly enough that he'd die grateful."

* * *

Wren didn't cry. I want that recorded somewhere, because she'll never tell it that way herself. She stood very still, looking at the glowing pools, and I watched her do the arithmetic — her father's hope, the syndicate's money, the helicopter that was surely coming, her own illness sitting in her chest like a tenant who'd stopped paying rent — and I watched her arrive at the sum.

"He sent me to find this," she said. Her voice was steady. That was somehow the worst part. "He sent me to legitimize taking it. And it would have—" She laughed, one short, awful note. "It would have worked. He'd have drunk it every day and felt better every day and written checks the whole way down. They'd have bottled it. *Crane Vitalis.* Sold it to dying men."

"They'd have needed the land," Mara said from the valley mouth. "The dam survey stakes. If the source can't be owned—"

"—it gets drowned and piped," Wren finished. "Dad said that on the call. I thought he was repeating the syndicate's line. He wasn't. He was reciting their business plan." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for exactly three seconds — I counted, because watching her hold herself together was the hardest work I'd done on this entire river — and then she lowered them and she was a guide's daughter again, a Voss-and-Crane expedition member, someone with a job. "Okay. Samples sealed? Everything documented?"

"Every terrace, every reading, chain of custody intact," Okafor said.

"Then we have what we came for. The truth." She turned to me. "Casper. How long until a helicopter can reach this valley?"

I looked at the sky. It was clean and blue and horribly accommodating. "Weather like this? From the lower staging camp? Half a day. Less, if they've been waiting."

"They've been waiting," Mara said. "They've been waiting since the informant's last message."

Rill shifted, wincing his splinted arm. "So what do we do? We can't exactly stand around the murder-pool holding a sign."

"We can deny them the landing zone," I said, and everyone turned to look at me, and I realized I'd said it out loud, the thing that had been forming in the back of my skull since the moment I saw the terraces — since before that, honestly, since the gorge, since the whiteout, since the confession at high camp. The glacier above the spring hung in a serac wall I'd been reading all morning the way you reread a letter from someone you used to love. I knew its fracture lines. I knew its loading. I knew, with the ugly intimacy of old experience, exactly how to bring a piece of it down.

"You're thinking something," Wren said, watching my face. "I can tell. You get a line right here—" she tapped between her brows, "—when you're thinking something you hate."

"Do I?"

"Like a plumb line."

I looked up at the seracs, blue-white and patient, and then down at the bone-colored terraces, glowing their lovely lying glow, and I thought about a man on a North Col rope fifteen years ago, and a choice, and how skills don't care what you use them for.

"Eat something," I said. "All of you. Sleep in shifts. Because if a helicopter comes into this valley tomorrow, I intend to make very sure it has nowhere to land."

Above us, the glacier ticked and groaned, and far downstream — so far it was barely a sound, more a feeling in the teeth than a noise in the ears — came the first faint thump-thump-thump of rotors, hours early, and getting louder.
