## 17. Aldous Arrives

The sound came first — a wasp's drone threading up the gorge, thin and wrong against all that stone.

Wren was crouched at the edge of the spring, watching Okafor's test vials change color, when Penn shouted from the ridge. "Company!" He said it the way you'd announce a bear at the food cache, and for one absurd second Wren thought: *good*, maybe they brought sandwiches.

Then she saw the helicopter.

It came up the valley low and deliberate, skimming the braided river like a heron that had learned to hate the ground, and it settled on the gravel flat below the spring with a rotorslap that sent every bird in the headwaters into exile. The downdraft flattened the grass, scattered their carefully stacked gear, and knocked Rill's hat into the water, where it began a dignified journey downstream that Rill watched with genuine grief.

"That hat has been to three continents," he said.

"It's going to four," said Penn.

Wren didn't laugh. She couldn't. Because the door was opening, and the man climbing out of it had her father's walk — that particular Crane gait, shoulders back, chin up, as if the world were a staircase he'd been assured led somewhere worth going.

"Dad," she said, though he couldn't hear her over the rotor wash. "Oh, Dad."

* * *

He looked worse than the last time she'd seen him, which had been eleven days ago through a satellite phone's crackle, and before that six weeks ago in his study, where the light had been kind and the whiskey had been generous and neither of them had mentioned the word *hospice*. Up here in honest daylight, at eight thousand feet, there was no kind light. His skin had gone the gray of river stones. He moved like a man carrying furniture only he could see.

But his eyes — his eyes found her across the gravel flat and lit up like a boy's, and that was somehow the worst part.

"Wren!" He spread his arms. Behind him, four syndicate men climbed out in matching technical jackets, new enough to still have their creases, looking around at the wilderness the way accountants look at a spreadsheet full of surprises.

She let him hug her. His arms were thinner than she remembered. That was the thing about dying fathers — they shrank by degrees, so gradually that every visit reset your baseline, and you only noticed the loss in retrospect, like erosion.

"You shouldn't be here," she said into his shoulder.

"Neither should you, sweetheart." He pulled back and held her at arm's length, beaming. "And yet here we both are. Look at this place." He turned a slow circle, taking in the cirque, the glacier hanging above them like a held breath, the spring itself — that impossible pool of blue-green light at the base of the ice. "It's even better than the photographs."

"What photographs?"

His smile flickered, just slightly. "The informant's reports were thorough."

So that was confirmed, then. Rill's face went carefully blank beside her; Mara's jaw set in a way Wren was learning to read like weather. The informant's final message hadn't just summoned the authorities. It had summoned *this*.

"There were supposed to be rangers," Wren said quietly.

"There were supposed to be rangers," her father agreed pleasantly, "and then there was a conversation, and then there was a delay, and then there was a charter flight. Money is very good at rearranging schedules, Wren. You'd know that if you'd ever paid attention to the business."

"I paid attention to what the business does to people."

"And yet." He gestured at the crew, at Casper standing apart with his arms crossed, at the whole illegal enterprise she'd been embedded in since the confluence. "Here you are."

* * *

Okafor tried to warn him.

Wren would give him that forever — Okafor stood in front of Aldous Crane with his sample kit open like a doctor delivering news, and he did not flinch, and he did not soften it more than science required.

"The water is toxic," Okafor said. "Iron, arsenic, cadmium — concentrations I've only seen near abandoned mines. The mineral content creates an optical effect, that luminescence, and some of these compounds produce a temporary stimulant response. Elevated energy, euphoria, a sense of clarity. It will feel like a cure for approximately forty-eight hours. Then the heavy metals begin accumulating in tissue. Kidneys first. Then liver. Then—" He stopped himself, which was its own kindness.

Aldous listened to all of it with the patient attention of a man waiting for a speech to end so the cake could come out.

"You tested it," he said.

"With three independent methods."

"And your instruments were calibrated properly."

"They were."

"And you're certain the samples weren't contaminated by the vials, or the cold, or—"

"Mr. Crane." Okafor's voice stayed level, but something underneath it was vibrating. "I have spent thirty years reading water. This water is poison. Beautifully packaged, extraordinarily poison. If you drink it, you will feel wonderful, and then you will die faster than you are already dying, and you will die badly."

There was a silence. The glacier creaked somewhere above them, the way it did in the evenings, settling its ancient weight.

Wren stepped forward. "Dad. Please."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw something in his face that broke her heart in a new place — because it wasn't defiance. It wasn't greed. It was exhaustion, deep as the gorge, and underneath it a terror so old it had fossilized into hope.

"Do you know what they told me last month?" he said. "The doctors. They sat me down — both of them, side by side, like an intervention in a movie — and they told me to get my affairs in order. My affairs, Wren. As if I'm a filing cabinet." He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "Sixty-one years building something, and the summary of my life is *get your affairs in order*."

"So we get them in order," she said. "Together. We have time. We—"

"We have *symptoms*," he said. "We have a calendar with fewer pages than yours, sweetheart, don't pretend otherwise — yes, I know about your bloodwork, I've known for two years, do you think I funded a wild goose chase to a legendary spring because I enjoy helicopters?" His voice rose, and the syndicate men shifted uncomfortably, and somewhere behind Wren she heard Casper take one quiet step closer without seeming to move at all. "I did it because there is a pool right there"—he pointed, hand trembling—"that glows like it's lit from inside, and men have written poems about it for two hundred years, and every single person who ever drank from it reported feeling *reborn*, and you want me to believe that's all coincidence? All chemistry? That the universe put a miracle at the top of a river and filled it with fine print?"

"Yes," Wren said. "That's exactly what happened. That's most of what happens, Dad. Miracles are usually minerals."

"Spoken like someone who still has options."

The words landed between them and lay there, ugly and true.

* * *

Here is what Wren understood, standing on the gravel flat while her father argued with reality: he wasn't choosing the water over her. Not exactly. That would have been simpler, and she could have hated him cleanly for it.

What he was choosing was a story. The story where the world rewarded effort. Where sixty years of building meant something, where the universe kept accounts, where there existed — at the top of a river no map bothered with — a payout for all of it. Her father had never been able to tolerate a losing position. He'd rather double down on a bad hand than fold, because folding meant admitting the game itself had cheated him, and Aldous Crane had built an empire on the unshakeable belief that the game was fair.

The spring was his last bet. And he needed it so badly that truth, arriving in triplicate with calibration certificates, simply bounced off.

"Dad," she tried once more, softer. "Come home. Come down the mountain with us. We'll figure out the rest together. Both of us. Two patients instead of one — we can compare notes, complain about the food, terrify the nurses." She smiled, and her voice wobbled, and she let it. "Please. I didn't come up here for the water either. I came up here for you."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment, with the glacier ticking overhead and the spring glowing its gorgeous lie — she thought she'd reached him. His eyes filled. His hand came up toward her cheek.

And then one of the syndicate men said, quietly, "Sir, the window for the flight back closes in ninety minutes," and the spell broke, because Aldous Crane had never in his life made a decision without a deadline attached.

"No," he said. "No, I— I came too far." He turned to the spring. He actually straightened up, squared those shrinking shoulders, and Wren watched her father assemble himself into the man from the annual reports. "I've read the surveys. I've read the legends. I've read the assays your geologist ran — and note, Wren, that he ran them on *samples*, in *vials*, through *instruments*, and I am telling you that some things don't survive the trip to a laboratory. Some things only work at the source."

"That's not how anything works," Okafor said despairingly.

"Everything I built worked exactly how I decided it worked," Aldous said. "That's how I know."

He walked toward the spring.

* * *

Casper moved first.

Not dramatically — Wren was learning that Casper Galloway did almost nothing dramatically, that his entire legend was built out of small precise motions that only looked inevitable afterward. He simply got between Aldous and the water, unhurried, hands loose at his sides, and said, "You'll want to hear this from me, because I'm the only one here who's killed a man by trusting the wrong data."

Aldous stopped. "Galloway. Yes. My son's guide."

"Your son's rope partner." Something crossed Casper's face, quick as a fish under ice. "I condemned the route he died testing. I told the expedition lead it wouldn't hold. I was overruled by a schedule. And I have spent fifteen years learning the difference between wanting something to be true and knowing it is." He nodded at the glowing pool. "That is wanting. I can see it from here. It looks exactly like grief wearing a nice coat."

"You don't know what I—"

"I know precisely what you feel. I drink from it every day." Casper's voice didn't rise at all, and that was the terrible thing about it. "It tells me that if I'd shouted louder, Tomás would be alive. It tastes wonderful. It's killing me anyway. Sir — step away from the water."

The syndicate men had gone very still. Even the helicopter pilot had shut down to idle, the better to listen.

And Aldous Crane — gray, diminished, terrified, magnificent in the way that only doomed stubbornness is magnificent — looked at Casper Galloway for a long moment and said:

"Get out of my way."

"Cas." It was Mara who spoke, from behind him, and her voice had a strange gentleness in it. "Cas, he's a grown man. He owns his choices. We warned him — all of us, on record, witnessed. There's nothing left we're permitted to do."

"He's her father," Casper said, not turning around.

"And she's standing right there watching you make this worse," Mara said. "Sometimes the kindest thing is to lose gracefully."

Wren wanted to scream at all of them. Instead she did the only thing left, the thing she'd been dreading since the helicopter landed, the card she'd sworn she'd never play because playing it meant admitting everything.

"Dad," she said. "If you drink that, I'll drink it too."

Everyone turned.

"I'm dying anyway, right? That's the whole point of this expedition. Your money, my diagnosis, one magic pool." She walked forward until she was beside Casper, facing her father across three feet of gravel and fifteen years of mutual protection-through-silence. "So here's your choice. Either the water works — in which case mine works too, and we're both cured, and you can stop spending your last months chasing a legend for my sake, which is what this has always been about, isn't it? Or the water kills — in which case you get to watch your daughter die of heavy metal poisoning *faster*, with your own eyes, because you needed a story more than you needed me."

"Don't," Aldous whispered.

"Choose," Wren said. "You love deadlines. Here's one."

The silence stretched. The spring glowed. Somewhere above, the glacier calved a small piece of itself into the meltwater with a sound like a door closing far away.

Her father's hand shook. His eyes went from the pool to her face and back, and she watched the arithmetic happen behind them — the terrible, human arithmetic of a man trying to calculate odds on his own child's life, and finding that no column added up to what he wanted.

"You wouldn't," he said, but it wasn't an accusation anymore. It was a question. A plea. *Tell me you wouldn't, so I can keep believing in the story where you're safe.*

"Try me," Wren said. "I learned from the best negotiator I know."

And that was when the syndicate men moved — not toward the spring, but toward the gear pile, toward the radios, toward the sample kit in Okafor's hands — and Mara Voss said, in a voice Wren had never heard her use, low and absolutely final:

"Touch that equipment and this expedition ends differently than any of you planned."

One of the men smiled. It was not a nice smile. "Ma'am," he said, "the expedition ended the moment our client landed. Now it's just cleanup."

Behind them, unnoticed by everyone except Casper Galloway, the wind on the glacier above the spring changed direction — and the old guide looked up at the hanging ice with the expression of a man greeting an old colleague he'd hoped never to need again.
