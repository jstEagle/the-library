## 18. The Standoff at the Headwaters

Mara Voss had spent thirty years in the mountains learning one lesson above all others: the moment you think you've accounted for everything is the moment the mountain hands you something you haven't.

She'd accounted for weather, for mutiny, for a dying man's stubbornness, for her own conscience arriving twenty years late like a guest who'd missed every other dinner party. She had not accounted for the helicopter.

It came up the gorge at midmorning on their second day at the source, a black insect buzzing against all that white and blue immensity, and Mara watched it land on the flat shelf of ice two hundred meters below the spring with a feeling she could only describe as professional offense. That was *her* landing zone. She'd picked it. She'd spent an hour of her life picking it, testing snow bridges, pacing out the approach. And now six men in matching expedition parkas were spilling out of it as if they owned the view.

Well. In the way that money owns things, they did.

"That's Aldous," Wren said beside her. Her voice was flat in a way Mara recognized from mirrors — the voice you make when feeling would be fatal.

"I see him."

"He brought friends."

"He brought a payroll." Mara counted: six men, one of them carrying a hard case that no one carried cameras in anymore. "Wren, go stand near your father. Not to argue. Just — be visible. It's harder to do ugly things in front of your daughter."

"And if he does them anyway?"

"Then we'll know exactly what we're dealing with." Which was a lie, because Mara already knew exactly what they were dealing with. She'd been dealing with it for months, in ledgers and wire transfers and phone calls she'd made from hotel lobbies with her back turned to her own crew. The syndicate didn't send six men across three hundred kilometers of protected wilderness to take photographs.

She watched Wren pick her way down the slope toward the helicopter, small and straight-backed against all that ice, and felt something in her chest turn over — the same organ, she suspected, that Casper Galloway had been living with a knife in for fifteen years.

*So this is what it feels like,* she thought. *To care about someone more than the route.*

Late. Everything with her was late.

* * *

Aldous Crane came up to the spring on foot, which cost him visibly. He was thinner than the photographs, gray at the lips, leaning on a walking staff and on the arm of the man with the hard case — a compact, pleasant-faced person named Ferro who smiled the way accountants smile when the numbers finally work.

"Mara." Aldous's voice was papery but warm; he'd always had warmth the way furnaces have heat, generated for a purpose. "You got her here. I knew you would."

"You paid me to," Mara said. "Let's not confuse the transaction with devotion."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and Wren steadied his elbow without seeming to decide to. Father and daughter stood together at the lip of the spring — that impossible pool of glacial melt, so blue it looked lit from underneath, steaming faintly where the mineral water met the cold air — and for one moment Mara almost pitied him. He looked at it the way believers look at relics. He looked at it like a man reading his own reprieve.

"It's beautiful," he whispered.

"It's toxic," Okafor said, from behind them, holding up her field kit like a traffic signal. "Arsenic, cadmium, and something nickel-based I'm still running down. Drink enough of this and you'll feel wonderful for about three weeks. Your cells will love it right up until they don't. Mr. Crane, I am begging you, professionally and personally—"

"Ferro has a counter-analysis," Aldous said mildly.

"Ferro has a wish," Okafor snapped. "I have a spectrometer."

Ferro smiled wider. "Ms. Okafor's equipment may have been affected by altitude." He said it kindly, the way you'd mention a colleague's illness. Then he turned to the spring and gestured, and two of his men began unrolling survey tape along its banks, driving little orange stakes into the ice with rubber mallets, and the sound of it — *tuk, tuk, tuk*, patient as a clock — was the most violent sound Mara had ever heard.

"What are they doing?" Wren asked.

"Staking," Mara said. "If the source can't be bottled, it gets dammed. Reservoir, processing plant, access road." She'd read the feasibility study eleven months ago, in a Zurich office, before she'd told herself the story about how she could steer the project toward conservation once she was inside it. "Your father's syndicate filed the preliminary claim last spring. This trip was the site verification."

Aldous didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He just looked at the water with his gray lips and his hungry eyes and said, "The claims expire in sixty days. If we can't prove extraction viability—" He stopped. He looked at Wren. Something moved through his face, brief as a fish under ice. "You were supposed to bring me samples, sweetheart. Not sermons."

"Dad." Wren's voice cracked down the middle. "I'm sick too. You know that. I came up here because you asked, and because some part of me needed to believe it too. But it's poison. Okafor tested it twice. If you drink this, you're not choosing a cure. You're choosing a prettier way to die than the one you already have."

For a moment — one long, teetering moment — Mara thought he'd hear her.

Then Ferro leaned in and murmured something, and Aldous's jaw set in the particular way of rich men being reminded of sunk costs, and he cupped his hand and reached toward the spring.

"No," Mara said, and grabbed his wrist.

Three of Ferro's men moved at once. It happened fast after that — shouting, shoving, Okafor's sample vials knocked spinning across the ice, Penn planting himself in front of Rill like a door slamming shut. Mara held Aldous's wrist and he was stronger than he looked, desperate men are, and somewhere in the scrum someone's boot went through a thin patch and there was screaming and flailing and then, cutting through all of it, a whistle.

One note. Clean, unhurried, absolutely certain.

Everyone froze. That was the thing about Casper Galloway's whistle — fifteen years out of the game and it still worked like a hand on the collar. He stood above them on the rock spine between the spring and the helicopter pad, and he wasn't even looking at the fight. He was looking at the sky.

"Everybody off the pad," he said. "Now. All of you. Walk, don't run, and nobody touches the aircraft."

"Casper," Mara started—

"The wind shifted ten minutes ago." His voice was level, almost conversational, the voice of a man reading a river. "Warm front off the southern ridge. You can't feel it down here, but the cornice above the east couloir can. That whole slope—" he nodded upward, at the vast white shoulder hanging over the landing zone, "—is loaded. It's been loading since yesterday. Every hour that helicopter idles its engines, it's hammering the snowpack with vibration. Another twenty minutes of that, or one hard rotor wash cycle, and the east face comes down on that pad with everyone standing on it."

Ferro laughed. Actually laughed. "This is the famous Galloway? The blacklist boy? Now he's a meteorologist?"

"I'm a guide," Casper said. "It's the same job. You watch things until they tell you what they're going to do." He looked at Aldous, and Mara saw him choose his words with the care of a man defusing something. "Mr. Crane. I've stood where you're standing. Not here — but here. Wanting the mountain to give me one thing it doesn't give anybody. Fifteen years ago I made a call like the one you're making now, and a man died, and I've been paying for it ever since. The mountain will give you nothing. It doesn't trade. Get your people off that pad."

And Aldous Crane — dying, brilliant, cornered by his own hope — looked at the spring instead, and said, "Get the samples. We're leaving when we leave."

That was when Mara understood that talking was over. She'd built her whole career on talking — contracts, testimony, negotiations, the slow verbal carpentry of getting people to move where she wanted. She watched Ferro's men keep staking, watched Aldous reach again for the water, watched Wren put her body between her father and the pool with her arms spread like a scarecrow, and she understood that the only language left on this mountain was going to be spoken in snow.

"Casper," she called up to the rock spine. "How long?"

He looked down at her. Fifteen years of silence between them, one testimony, one ruined career, one disaster they'd each carried half of and blamed each other for the other half — and in that look, right then, none of it was there. There was just the old shorthand, guide to guide, the language you learn on rope.

"How long till what?" he said quietly.

"Till you can bring it down. On purpose. Where you want it."

Penn made a small horrified noise. Okafor said, "Mara—"

"Avalanche control," Mara said, loud enough for everyone. "Charges, triggers, timed release. It's legal on every highway in the Alps. They shell mountains every winter to keep roads open. It's a skill, Ferro. A licensed, regulated, boring skill — unless you're Casper Galloway, in which case it's the skill that got a man killed on the North Col, because he triggered a release to save four lives and the slide took a fifth anyway." She heard her own voice shake and let it. "You want to know why he's blacklisted? Because he did the arithmetic with real numbers while everyone else did it with comfortable ones. So ask him. Ask him what he can do."

The pad went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Casper looked at her for a long moment, and Mara braced for the hatred — she'd earned it, she'd banked it, she'd testified with steady eyes while knowing what she knew. Instead he said, "You remember the release points on the east face?"

"Better than my own birthday," she said. Which was true, and they both knew why it was true: she'd spent fifteen years memorizing that other mountain, that other day, the way penitents memorize prayers.

"There's a natural release point at the couloir mouth," he said. "Slope angle's right, the load's there, the wind's doing half the work. I don't need charges. I need to start the crack and get out of the way." He glanced at the helicopter, sitting fat and smug on the ice. "Problem is, the only approach to the release point goes past that aircraft. And these gentlemen are between me and it."

Ferro's smile had gone carefully neutral. "You're threatening us."

"I'm informing you," Casper said. "Threats are about what I want. This is about what the mountain wants, and I'm just the translator. You have maybe forty minutes before that front arrives whether anyone does anything or not. When it comes, it takes the pad, the helicopter, and everyone standing on it. Or—" and here his voice did something Mara had never heard in it, something almost gentle, "—we clear the pad, I walk up there alone, and I bring the slope down into the empty couloir where it hurts nobody, and your pilot lifts off ahead of it, and everybody goes home. Those are the options. I'd take the second one. I've lived with the first one for fifteen years, and the rent is unbelievable."

* * *

They cleared the pad.

Not all at once, and not gracefully — Ferro argued, two of his men refused until Aldous, swaying on his staff, waved them down with the weary gesture of a king granting a retreat he couldn't afford — but they cleared it. Mara herded them uphill toward the spring camp with Okafor and Penn flanking, and Rill, one-armed and furious, kept up a running commentary of insults that Penn finally muffled by handing him a thermos.

Wren stayed at the edge of the pad, watching her father being steered away from the water he still stared at over his shoulder, and Mara stopped beside her.

"You did the right thing," Mara said. "Standing in front of the spring."

"He's still going to drink it someday," Wren said. "Maybe not today. But someday, when I'm not there." Her chin trembled and she locked it. "You knew. About the syndicate. You knew the whole time."

"Yes."

"Why should I believe anything you say ever again?"

"You shouldn't," Mara said. "Believe the mountain instead. It's the only one of us that never lies." She nodded up at the rock spine, where a lone figure was moving, small and deliberate, along a line only he could see. "Watch him. Whatever else is true about Casper Galloway — whatever I helped bury him under — watch what he does in the next ten minutes. That part's true. That part was always true."

Above them, Casper reached the release point.

Mara had seen avalanches started on purpose before — resort control work, artillery cracking across a dawn valley, the whole industrial ritual of it. This was nothing like that. This was one man, alone, on a loaded slope, with a probe pole and fifteen years of guilt, reading the snow the way you'd read a sleeping animal before deciding where to touch it. He knelt. He dug a quick column, sheared it, studied the fracture line. He looked down at the pad once — empty now, the helicopter lifting off in a roar of rotor wash that made Mara's heart stop until she saw it climbing *away*, up-gorge, the pilot having done his own arithmetic — and then he placed his hands on the slope the way you'd place them on a patient.

And pushed.

For one full second, nothing. The mountain held its breath. Mara found she was holding hers, and that Wren's hand had found her sleeve without either of them deciding it.

Then the slope exhaled.

It started as a sound — a deep, structural groan, like a ship tearing loose from a dock — and then the whole white shoulder of the east face let go, tens of thousands of tons of it, pouring into the couloir in a single fluid, thundering motion that filled the air with powder and erased the landing zone, the survey stakes' approach route, the road in, the whole idea of a road in, burying it under forty feet of settled snow that would not be crossed by machine this season or possibly ever. The shockwave rolled over them like weather. Snow dusted down out of a blue sky, soft as ash, soft as forgiveness.

When the air cleared, the pad was gone. The headwaters were sealed. And Casper Galloway stood on the rock spine above it all, breathing hard, looking down at the burial he had chosen — and Mara watched something leave his shoulders that had been sitting there for fifteen years. Not relief. Something older. Something like a man setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing.

He caught her eye across the distance. He didn't smile. He didn't need to.

But then he raised one hand — not in triumph. In warning. Pointing, past them, down-gorge, where the helicopter had gone.

Where it was turning around.

Coming back.

And on the far side of the new wall of snow, cut off from retreat, with a dying man who still believed in miracles, six hired men with nothing left to lose, and night coming on fast — Mara Voss reached for her radio and found, with a calm that surprised her, that she already knew exactly what she was willing to burn next.
