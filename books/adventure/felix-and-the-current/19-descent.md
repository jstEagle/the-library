## 19. Descent

The storm found us an hour below the headwaters, as if it had been waiting politely for us to leave before it lost its temper.

It came down the gorge sideways, a gray wall of sleet and wind that turned the river from something we traveled beside into something that seemed to be chasing us. I'd been in weather like this maybe six times in thirty years of guiding, and three of those times someone had died. The other three times, someone had made the right call early enough.

I was trying to make it now.

"We camp," I shouted to Mara over the wind. "Here. Now."

She looked at the sky, then at me, and for once — for the first time since she'd put her seal on that letter — she didn't argue. That frightened me more than the storm did. Mara Voss arguing with me was a constant of the universe, like gravity or Penn's complaints about his own cooking. Mara Voss agreeing meant things were bad.

We got the tents up in twenty minutes that felt like twenty years, Okafor anchoring them with survey stakes because we'd left half our hardware at the icefall. Rill's wrist was splinted and useless; Penn had stopped talking entirely, which from Penn was its own kind of emergency signal. And Aldous Crane sat on a rock at the edge of camp, sleet beading in his silver hair, looking out at the white chaos like a man watching television.

He'd drunk from the spring two days ago. A full cupped handful, then another, while Wren screamed at him and Okafor waved test results and the syndicate men stood by with their radios dead in their hands. He'd drunk it knowing what was in it. Maybe especially knowing. A man who has spent eighty thousand dollars on a miracle does not want to hear the word *arsenic*, even when it comes attached to a geologist he likes.

Now his lips were gray, and he kept pressing a hand flat against his stomach as if holding a door shut.

* * *

I checked on him first, before my own frostbitten fingers, which felt like the right order of operations even if my hands disagreed.

"Mr. Crane."

"Aldous." He smiled at me, and the smile was wrong — loose at the edges, a smile belonging to a man whose face had been borrowed by someone less careful. "You know, Galloway, I met your file once. Years ago. Before everything." He tapped his temple. "Mara's dossier. I read every page. I wanted to know what kind of man takes people up mountains."

"And what kind did you find?"

"The kind who counts heads twice." He laughed, then winced, hand going back to his belly. "That's why I hired you. Not despite the blacklist. Because of it. A man who counts heads twice after losing one — that's the only kind of guide worth the money."

The words landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there, glowing uncomfortably, like a coal dropped down a shirt.

"You need to drink water," I said. "Actual water. Filtered. And you need to eat, and then you need to let us get you down this river fast."

"I'm dying anyway, Casper. May I call you Casper? You call everyone by their names like it costs you nothing." He waved vaguely at the storm. "The doctors gave me a year. The spring was supposed to give me—" He stopped. His eyes went glassy and distant, and for a moment I thought he was going to cry, but instead he said, very quietly: "Wren has the same thing I have. Did she tell you? Same disease, fifteen years younger. It skips nothing. It waits."

"She told me."

"Then you understand why I drank it." He looked at me with terrible clarity, the poison momentarily burned away by honesty. "If there was even a chance. You'd have drunk too. Don't tell me you wouldn't. I've read your file."

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was yes, and I was tired of being a man who lied by omission. Fifteen years of practice, and it turns out all it took to break the habit was a dying old man and a cup of beautiful poison.

"Wren's not leaving you," I said instead. "So we're getting you both down. That's the whole plan. There is no version where I choose."

Aldous studied me for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a man signing something.

* * *

Wren refused the stretcher.

We'd built one that night from paddles and the tarp off Okafor's gear bag, a proper litter, and in the morning I presented it to her the way you present a verdict. She looked at it the way you receive one.

"I can walk."

"You walked yesterday. Yesterday was downhill and dry. Today is neither." I kept my voice level, guide-voice, the voice that doesn't negotiate. "Your father goes in the litter. You go beside him. That's the arrangement."

"He weighs more than me."

"He's also dying faster right now, and if you burn everything you have walking, you'll be no use to him when he needs carrying. This isn't charity, Wren. It's logistics." I paused. "Also I'm bigger than both of you combined, so physically speaking, this was never really a debate."

She glared at me. She'd been glaring at me for eleven days now, and somewhere in there the glare had changed languages — it had started as accusation and become something more like argument, the kind you have with family. I'd stopped minding it around day four, which should have worried me.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When he can't walk anymore — really can't — you tell me. No protecting me. No counting heads quietly. You say it out loud."

I thought about the North Col. About the head-count I'd done at 3 a.m. in a white room in Kathmandu, and the number coming up one short, and how I'd known, somehow, before anyone told me. How I'd carried that arithmetic ever since.

"Deal," I said.

* * *

The river had risen overnight. Of course it had. The Serrin in storm season doesn't rise so much as change its mind about where it lives, swallowing banks we'd camped on, turning yesterday's easy gravel bars into brown rapids. We portaged the worst of it along a game trail Okafor found — the man could read a hillside like a menu — and by noon we were soaked through, cold to the bone, and making worse time than I'd promised Mara.

Aldous lasted until midafternoon.

He tried, I'll give him that. He walked when we lifted him out of the litter, gripping Penn's shoulder, putting one foot in front of the other with the grim discipline of a man who had built a fortune out of exactly this kind of stubbornness. But the poison was working through him now, and around three o'clock his legs simply informed him the arrangement was over. He went down gently, almost politely, into Wren's arms.

"Casper." Her voice. Out loud, as agreed.

I set down my pack and picked him up.

Here is a thing nobody tells you about carrying a grown man: it is mostly a conversation between your spine and your pride, and your spine wins if you feed it properly. I'd carried men off mountains before. I'd never carried one down a river gorge in a storm with his daughter walking three feet away, watching every step, cataloguing every stumble.

But Aldous Crane weighed less than he should have. Months of illness before the spring, and days of it after. He weighed like a man already halfway to being a memory.

"Put me down," he murmured against my shoulder. "This is undignified."

"Sir," said Penn, falling in beside me with Rill's share of the load stacked on his own pack, "you paid for this expedition. Technically this is a premium service."

Aldous laughed, and then groaned, and then laughed again, weaker. "The cook is funny. Nobody told me the cook was funny."

"Nobody asked," said Penn.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of a rockslide as the light failed, and I learned what Wren Crane looks like when she stops fighting.

She didn't stop loudly. That would have been easier. She just went quiet, sitting next to the litter with her father's hand in both of hers, feeding him filtered water in small sips while the storm hammered the tarp above them. She'd used up whatever she had at the headwaters — the screaming, the begging, the fury. What was left was a daughter doing arithmetic of her own, and I recognized the expression because I'd worn it myself, once, over a different body.

I crouched down across the fire from her later, when Aldous finally slept.

"He asked me to carry him," she said, without looking up. "Before he passed out. He said, 'Tell Galloway I'm sorry about the weight.' Like it was luggage. Like he was an inconvenience."

"He's not heavy."

"He's dying, Casper."

"Yes," I said. "And he's still not heavy. Those are two different facts and I need you to hold both of them, because tomorrow I'm going to carry him again, and you're going to walk beside us, and neither of us gets to fall apart until the ranger station."

She looked up at me then. Firelight and exhaustion and something underneath both that I'd been carefully not naming for eleven days.

"Why?" she asked. "Why are you doing this? He's the reason you—" She stopped herself, recalculated. "His money ruined you. His pressure killed Tomas. You owe this family nothing. Less than nothing."

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"Fifteen years ago," I said, "I made a call on a mountain because a schedule mattered more than a person. I've spent every day since learning the difference. This—" I nodded at the litter, at the sleeping old man, at her. "This is the difference. That's all. It's not noble. It's late."

Wren was quiet for a long time. Then she reached across the fire and put her hand over mine, briefly, before pulling it back like the fire had bitten her.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I take a shift carrying him."

"You absolutely do not—"

"My father, my shift. Guide." And there was the glare again, the family kind, and I heard myself laugh for the first time in days.

* * *

We came down out of the worst of the storm on the third morning, into a world scoured and glittering, the gorge walls hung with fresh meltwater like the whole canyon had been rinsed and hung out to dry. The river ran high and loud but honest again. Behind us, far upstream, the source sat in its bowl of ice, luminous and lethal, waiting for the syndicate to decide whether beauty was a commodity.

Okafor caught up to me at the midday halt, walking with that tireless geologist's shuffle that covers ground the way water covers rock.

"Galloway. Two things." He held up one finger. "Crane's color is better. Still poisoned, still needs a hospital, but the acute phase is passing. Whatever's in that water, it hits hard and then it eases. He'll live to face consequences." A second finger. "There's a ranger station at the lower boundary. Twelve miles. And there's a radio tower on its roof."

I looked at him.

"You're going to walk in there and give testimony," Okafor said. "All of us are. The dam survey, the informant, the syndicate, the spring. Everything. And you're going to surrender your license — such as it is — and ask for amnesty for the crew." He wasn't asking me. He'd clearly rehearsed this speech on the game trail while I was busy playing furniture for a mining magnate. "Because if we don't, Crane's lawyers will be here in a week with permits and bulldozers, and that spring will be a bottling plant inside two years, and everything we carried each other through will have been for nothing."

Twelve miles. One radio tower. One small concrete building full of people whose job description included arresting me.

I looked back up the gorge, where the storm was still chewing on the high country. Then downstream, where the Serrin widened and gentled toward country with maps and laws and consequences.

Fifteen years ago I'd have run. I knew the terrain; I could vanish up any tributary and be a rumor by nightfall. Some part of me — the part that had counted heads twice in a hotel room in Kathmandu — was still offering, quietly, the old way out.

"Tomas Crane was my rope partner," I said to Okafor. "Did I ever tell you that?"

"You mentioned."

"He taught me that you don't get to choose what happens on a mountain. Only what you carry down." I shouldered my pack. "Let's go give our statement."

Behind us, Wren was lifting her father's arm across her shoulders, taking the first steps of her shift, and Aldous was protesting, weakly, that he could manage, and Penn was telling Rill that if he dropped the stove one more time he was cooking his own meals forever, and the river ran beside us all the way down, patient as anything, carrying everything toward the sea.

Twelve miles to the truth. For once in my life, I was walking toward it.
