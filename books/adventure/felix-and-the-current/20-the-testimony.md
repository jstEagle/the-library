## 20. The Testimony

The ranger station at Kettle Falls smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and forty years of instant coffee boiled twice. Okafor had spent enough time in field stations to know the species on sight: a building built by optimists, maintained by pessimists, and staffed by exactly one person who cared about both.

In this case that person was Ranger Deputy Ilsa Brandt, a broad-shouldered woman in her fifties who had met their ragged procession at the boundary cairn with her hand resting — not threateningly, Okafor noted, but *available* — on the radio clipped to her shoulder. She'd counted them twice. She'd looked longest at Casper Galloway, whose name she clearly recognized from a laminated notice taped inside the station door, and then she'd said, "Coffee's on. Somebody start talking."

So somebody did. Everybody did, actually, which was the problem.

* * *

They gave their statements in the order they arrived at the table, which was not the order anyone would have chosen. Penn went first because Penn always went first when there was a chance of food afterward; he'd already charmed two granola bars out of Brandt before Okafor sat down. Rill sat beside him with his wrist splinted in a sling made from a cut-off shirt sleeve, narrating his own account with the dramatic timing of a man who had broken a bone and intended to milk it through at least one winter.

"And then," Rill said, "the cache rope snapped, and all our food went into the river, and my brother said — tell her what you said, Penn."

"I said we were going to have a very educational week."

"You said it *cheerfully.*"

"Someone has to be cheerful. It's clearly not going to be the geologist."

Okafor looked up from his notebook. "I'm being thorough."

"He hasn't smiled since the confluence," Rill told Brandt solemnly. "We've been monitoring it."

Brandt, to her credit, wrote *cache lost to river, subject broke wrist, brother cheerful* in her log without comment. She was, Okafor was beginning to appreciate, the rarest kind of official: one who understood that testimony came out sideways, in jokes and arguments and people finishing each other's sentences, and that the truth lived in the seams between them.

Mara Voss went last. Or rather, she tried to go last, and Casper stopped her.

"No," he said. It was the first thing he'd said in an hour. "Me first."

Okafor watched him do it. He'd watched Casper make hard calls for three weeks — the portage, the whiteout, the icefall — and he had catalogued, with a scientist's detachment that fooled no one including himself, the particular quality of those decisions. Casper decided the way water decided: no wasted motion, no visible struggle, and a result that looked inevitable only after the fact.

This was different. This time Okafor could see the cost of it in his face.

"My name is Casper Galloway," he said. "You'll find me on your wall there. Fifteen years ago I was struck from the guiding registries of two countries after an accident on the North Col. I've never contested it." He paused. "I'm going to contest part of it now. Not for me. For them."

He laid it out plainly: the syndicate, the survey stakes at the headwaters, the satellite phone hidden in Mara's gear duffel that had been reporting their position downstream until she burned it, the helicopter, Aldous Crane drinking from a spring laced with enough cadmium and arsenic to kill him slowly and beautifully. He named the informant — a quiet man named Teodor whom Okafor had liked, damn it, had genuinely liked — and described how Mara had paid him off rather than turn him in, and how Teodor's final message had summoned the helicopter anyway.

Then he got to the part Okafor hadn't known. None of them had, except Wren and Mara.

"The accident fifteen years ago," Casper said. "Aldous Crane's son died on a route I had condemned as unclimbable in that season. We went anyway because Crane senior pressured the expedition lead to summit on schedule. The lead was Mara Voss. When the inquiry came, I took the blame. She let me."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a raven said something rude.

"Why?" Brandt asked. Not unkindly. Just the question.

"Because someone had to carry it," Casper said, "and I was already carrying something. It fit."

Okafor put down his pen. Fifteen years of professional habit told him to record everything; something older and better told him that some sentences should be allowed to sit in the air a moment before being pinned down like specimens.

* * *

They broke for food, which in a ranger station meant rehydrated stew and bread Brandt produced from a tin as if she'd been expecting a hungry crowd for years. Maybe she had. Border stations saw every kind of arrival.

Penn ate standing up, which Okafor had learned was how Penn processed things. "You knew," Penn said to him quietly, nodding toward Casper, who stood out on the porch looking at the river. "At the whiteout. When he made the call to push on. You knew about the North Col."

"I suspected. There were survey markers above the legal line. His markers. Stale ones." Okafor stirred his stew. "I photographed them. I sent word downstream. That's why the conservation authority was waiting at the boundary."

Penn stared at him. "You're the reason we almost got arrested?"

"I'm the reason the headwaters might stay protected. Both things can be true." Okafor tasted the stew. It was terrible. He ate it anyway. "I spent three weeks deciding whether Casper Galloway was a danger to this crew or the only thing keeping it alive. The evidence kept contradicting itself. Eventually I realized the contradiction *was* the answer. A man can be both. Most honest men are."

"Huh," said Penn. "Rill owes me ten dollars. He had money on you being a spy for the mining company."

"There's a pool?"

"There's been a pool since day four. You were the favorite."

Okafor considered objecting to this and decided the data spoke for itself. "Who won?"

"Wren, technically. She bet 'all of the above.'"

* * *

The formal session resumed at dusk, and this was the part Okafor would think about for years afterward: the moment the crew stopped giving separate statements and started giving one.

It happened without discussion. Wren began describing the dam surveyors' camp — the stakes, the flagged lines, the concrete test cylinders — and when she faltered on a detail, Rill filled it in. When Rill misremembered the date, Okafor corrected him from his notes. When Okafor's notes ran dry on what happened inside the syndicate tent, Penn picked up the thread. Mara testified last among the crew, flat-voiced and unsparing, about the contract, the payments, the radio, her own silence fifteen years ago and her own ambition now. She did not soften any of it. Okafor noticed that Casper watched her do it and that neither of them looked away.

Brandt took it all down in longhand, then typed it, then read it back aloud, which took an hour and would have been tedious except that hearing the whole story in one voice — their collective voice, stitched together — did something to the room. Okafor found himself thinking about strata. About how a core sample tells you nothing from a single layer, but taken whole, in sequence, it tells you everything about what the mountain has survived.

"That's the statement," Brandt said finally. "Signatures at the bottom. All of you are witnesses, not suspects — the protected-zone violation is a civil matter and frankly, given what you've handed me, nobody upstream is going to press it." Her eyes went to Casper. "Except you. The registry issue isn't mine. But surrendering voluntarily counts. It always counts."

"I know," Casper said. "I want it on the record that I'm surrendering. And I want one condition."

"Conditions aren't usually how surrenders work."

"This one comes with a dam survey, a poisoned spring, and a federal land-grab case. I think I've earned a condition." He didn't smile, but something in his voice nearly did. "Amnesty for the crew. Full amnesty. Whatever they carried, whatever they crossed, whatever they knew and didn't report — none of it touches them. They followed a blacklisted guide into bad country because a dying man paid them to, and most of them needed the money more than they needed sense. Punish me. I'm the one who led them there."

"Casper—" Wren started.

"It's done," he said, in the water-deciding voice, and Okafor understood that this, too, was a route Casper had surveyed long ago and condemned, and was climbing anyway, on schedule, because someone had to carry it and it fit.

Brandt wrote it down. "I'll forward it with my recommendation. For what it's worth, my recommendation will use the word 'unanimously.'"

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to their bunks and the stove had burned down to a red heart, Okafor stepped onto the porch. Casper was still there, wrapped in a blanket, watching the Serrin run dark and quick below the station.

"May I?" Okafor asked, and sat without waiting for an answer, which after three weeks on a river felt like the correct formality.

For a while neither of them spoke. The river did the talking. Rivers were good at it.

"I have a professional observation," Okafor said eventually.

"Those are usually insults wearing a lab coat."

"Yes, well." He turned his mug in his hands. "Fifteen years ago you took blame that wasn't wholly yours, and it destroyed your life, and you called it fitting. Today you surrendered to a punishment that may no longer even apply, to protect people who don't need protecting anymore. Also fitting. Do you see the pattern? You keep volunteering for sentences. It's a kind of dishonesty, Casper. The most respectable kind, but still."

Casper was quiet a long time. "What would you call it instead?"

"Accepting help," Okafor said. "It's an experimental technique. Very little field data. But the preliminary results suggest the subjects survive."

That got the closest thing to a laugh he'd ever heard from the man — short, rusty, real. Downriver somewhere, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, as usual.

"They'll review the record," Casper said. "Maybe. Probably not. Fifteen years is a long time to be a name on a laminated page."

"Fifteen years is nothing," Okafor said. "Geologically speaking, you haven't even finished being deposited yet."

* * *

Morning came gray and clean, smelling of snowmelt. Brandt drove them down to the crossroads in her battered truck, all six of them plus gear, which meant Wren rode in the bed holding the cooler with the water samples — evidence now, chain-of-custody tags and all — and refused all offers to trade places.

At the crossroads, where the road split toward the valley towns and the rail line, Brandt handed Casper a folded paper. "Your surrender receipt. And a copy of the joint statement, certified. You'll be contacted by the commission within thirty days." She paused, then added, off the record, in the tone of a woman who had read a great many statements and believed very few: "Yours was the first one in nine years where everybody signed the same story. Whatever else happens — that's not nothing."

They stood in the road a moment, unsure, suddenly, of the mechanics of parting. Then Rill hugged Casper, which surprised everyone including Rill. Penn shook his hand like closing a business deal and slipped something into it — Okafor craned and saw it was a granola bar, the last one, the sacred one. Mara embraced him briefly, fiercely, saying something too quiet for the rest of them, and Casper nodded once.

Wren went last. She didn't hug him. She just stood in front of him and said, "Thirty days. I'll be counting."

"I know," Casper said. "You count everything."

"Learned from watching you measure miles."

And then the truck pulled away with four of them in it, and Okafor watched in the side mirror as Casper Galloway stood alone at the crossroads with a granola bar in one hand and his future in the other, getting smaller, not leaving — waiting, Okafor realized, for the truck to disappear first, because some men needed to see a thing safely home before they let themselves begin.

Beside him, Wren watched the mirror too, her jaw set in a way Okafor had learned to recognize as a woman preparing paperwork.

"Thirty days," she said again, to no one, to the road, to the commission that didn't know yet what was coming for it.

Okafor opened his notebook to a fresh page and, for once, wrote nothing down. Some things deserved to travel unrecorded.

But he did note, privately, that the road ahead bent toward the city, toward hearings and lawyers and a girl with a petition to file — and that for the first time since the confluence, the whole crew was pointed the same direction.
