## 21. Amnesty

The letter from the Registry Board arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day the world did its paperwork.

Wren read it standing in her father's kitchen, because she had learned over the winter that bad news and good news both went down easier if you didn't sit for them. The kitchen still smelled faintly of Aldous's tea — he drank it now with honey, a concession to the doctors, a small surrender he performed loudly so everyone would know it cost him something.

*The petition of Ms. Wren Crane having been reviewed... the case of C. Galloway is hereby referred for formal reconsideration...*

She read it three times. The words didn't get any less bureaucratic, but they got more beautiful, the way a mountain gets more beautiful the longer you look at it even though it hasn't moved an inch.

"Is it yes?" Aldous asked from his chair by the window. He was thinner than the man who'd once commanded a helicopter into the headwaters like a king claiming territory. His hands had a tremor now that the specialists called *residual* and he called *the tax*. But his eyes were clear — clearer than they'd been in years, maybe decades, since before the spring water, since before everything. Poison, Okafor's tests had said. Beautiful poison. Her father had drunk a glass of it believing it was forever, and what it had actually given him was a long, slow lesson.

"It's a referral," Wren said. "It means they'll look at it. Really look."

"That's not a yes."

"It's not a no." She set the letter on the counter where he could see it. "Dad, you have to understand how these things work. Nobody says yes right away. Yes has to be earned in committee."

Aldous snorted, which was progress; six months ago he would have offered to buy the committee. "You sound like him."

"I sound like someone who spent two months on a river learning how things actually happen." She poured herself coffee. "Casper would say the river doesn't care about your schedule. The board doesn't either."

"The board," Aldous said gravely, "has never carried anyone out of a gorge."

"No," Wren agreed. "But I'm going to make them wish they had."

* * *

Her illness was in remission, which was a word she distrusted. Remission sounded like a truce negotiated by cowards. Her hematologist, Dr. Ferro — a small, brisk woman with the bedside manner of a well-loved drill sergeant — preferred the word *responding*, and Wren had come to prefer it too, because responding implied you were doing something, participating, holding up your end.

"You almost refused the treatment," Dr. Ferro reminded her at every appointment, as though it were a hobby of hers. "I want that noted for posterity. You sat in that chair and told me you'd rather die on a mountain than in a hospital."

"I said I'd rather die somewhere honest."

"A hospital is extremely honest. It's the most honest building humans have ever constructed. Everything in it admits exactly what it is." Ferro tapped the chart. "And yet here you are, responding beautifully, and you're welcome."

Here she was. That was the strange part. In August she had been carried downriver on Casper Galloway's back, too weak to walk the last twelve miles, watching the world through a haze of fever and thinking: *this is what the end looks like, and it smells like woodsmoke and wet rope.* In September she had signed the consent forms. In October she had started the treatment she'd sworn off — not because she'd stopped wanting the mountains, but because Casper had said one sentence to her in the hospital parking lot, and it had rearranged something fundamental.

She'd been arguing, of course. Refusing with flair. And he'd stood there with his hands in his pockets, this man who had lost everything twice, and said:

"Wren. I watched your brother die because somebody decided the summit mattered more than the person. Don't you dare hand me a second version of that story with you in it."

Then he'd gotten back in his truck and driven away before she could answer, which was very him. Casper Galloway had never once in his life stuck around for gratitude. Gratitude made him itch, Mara said. Like wool.

So: treatment. Six months of it. Nausea, needles, a wig she named Bartholomew and then retired with honors when her hair came back in stubborn, unruly curls. And now — responding. Responding beautifully, per the drill sergeant.

The petition had been Mara's idea first, actually. Wren liked to remind her of that, because Mara hated being credited with kindness.

* * *

Mara Voss arrived on Thursdays now, ostensibly to review testimony documents, actually to eat Aldous's cook's food and argue about route maps spread across the dining table like battle plans for a war nobody was fighting anymore.

"You wrote 'grace earned, not granted' in your cover letter," Mara said, reading over Wren's shoulder one Thursday in early spring. "To a government board."

"Yes."

"You can't write that. They'll think you're mocking them."

"I am a little mocking them."

"They have no sense of humor, Wren. These are people who think a comma splice is a moral failing."

"And yet it worked, so perhaps they have more sense of humor than you credit."

Mara made a noise like a kettle deciding against boiling. She was different these days — or rather, she was the same, but the load-bearing walls had shifted. She'd turned over every scrap of syndicate correspondence to the rangers. She'd testified for eleven hours. She'd stood in front of the conservation authority and said, in a voice that didn't waver, "I knew what the money was buying and I took it anyway, and a man nearly died of my ambition, and a girl nearly died of my silence." Wren had watched her do it. It had looked like it cost more than the North Col ever had.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Mara said, rolling up the maps. "That night at high camp. When you found out. I keep waiting for you to hate me properly. You've done it in installments, like a subscription. When does the full year arrive?"

Wren considered the question honestly, because Mara deserved honesty even when it was inconvenient. "I don't think it's coming. I was furious. I'm still — I don't know, disappointed? But Casper let you carry it alone for fifteen years, and you let him carry it, and you were both so busy carrying it that neither of you ever put it down and looked at it. Hating you feels like adding another bag to the porter line."

"That's disgustingly mature."

"I've been told."

"My testimony ended his career. My silence kept it ended. And you just—" Mara gestured vaguely, the closest she came to emotional language. "Forgave me in installments."

"Not forgave. Understood. Forgiveness might still be upstream." Wren smiled. "Ask me again after the hearing."

* * *

The hearing was held in a conference room in the regional registry office, which occupied the ground floor of a building that had clearly been designed by someone who hated windows. There were four board members. Three of them looked like they'd been issued from the same warehouse as the furniture. The fourth was a small older woman named Chair Okonkwo who asked the only questions that mattered.

"Ms. Crane. Your petition states that Mr. Galloway's conduct during the Serrin expedition demonstrated, quote, 'exactly the judgment the blacklist exists to find and preserve.' Explain."

Wren had prepared forty minutes of material. She abandoned all of it.

"Fifteen years ago," she said, "a man died on a mountain because a client demanded a schedule and nobody said no loudly enough. Casper Galloway said no quietly, afterward, in the only way he could — he took the blame, and he disappeared, and he spent fifteen years guiding the same hundred miles of river over and over because those miles were safe and he couldn't forgive himself for anything beyond them. Then last summer he led an illegal expedition, saved my life twice, destroyed a helicopter landing zone with a controlled icefall to stop armed men from seizing protected land, and then walked into a ranger station and surrendered himself before anyone asked. He didn't run. Running was the one thing left that he was good at, and he didn't do it."

Chair Okonkwo made a note. "You say he saved your life twice."

"He carried me eleven miles downriver when I couldn't walk. And before that—" Wren paused. This part she'd never said aloud, not even to Casper. "Before that, at the source, I wanted to drink the water. I was sick, and tired, and it glowed, and I wanted to believe. He physically stopped me. He'd already tested it. He knew. And instead of lecturing me he just stood between me and it until I came back to myself. That's the judgment. That's the whole thing. Fifteen years ago he failed to stop a man from walking into danger. Last summer he succeeded. Same man. Same instinct. The difference is he finally got to finish the sentence."

Silence. One of the warehouse-issued board members cleared his throat and asked about the icefall, whether triggering it constituted reckless use of avalanche expertise in a protected zone, and Wren laughed before she could help it.

"Sir, he dropped several tons of ice on a helicopter pad to prevent a felony. If that's a crime, I'd like to see the statute."

* * *

They called Casper in for the final session, which meant Wren got to watch him sit in a folding chair under fluorescent lights looking like a heron that had wandered into a bank. He wore a collared shirt. Someone — Penn, she suspected, based on the smugness radiating from the brothers in the hallway — had ironed it for him, mostly correctly.

"Casper Galloway," Chair Okonkwo said. "Fifteen years ago you accepted blame for a death that, per new testimony, involved substantial pressure from the expedition's funding party. Why did you accept it?"

The room went very quiet. Wren realized she was gripping the armrest.

"Because it was partly true," Casper said. "I tied the knots. I chose the route. I let a schedule matter more than my own doubt. A man paid for that with his life, and the fact that other people pushed doesn't vacuum-seal my hands clean." He paused. "What wasn't true was that I was the only one responsible. And what I did wrong afterward was let the lie stand because the truth would have cost me nothing and cost others something, and I was a coward about other people's pain. So — blacklist me for the failure. I earned it. Just don't blacklist me for the cover-up anymore, because I returned that too."

Chair Okonkwo looked at him for a long moment. "Mr. Galloway, do you want reinstatement?"

Everyone leaned forward. Wren realized she was holding her breath.

"Honestly?" Casper rubbed the back of his neck. "Six months ago, no. I wanted the record fixed, not the job. But I've been running trips on the lower Serrin all winter — legal ones, small ones, teaching kids to read water. And I caught myself planning routes upstream. Past the old markers. Wanting to see what's up there now that it's protected and will stay protected." He glanced at Wren, quick, then away. "So yes. I want it. Turns out I always did. I just thought wanting things was how people got hurt."

* * *

The decision came eight weeks later. On a Tuesday.

Wren opened the envelope in the kitchen again, because traditions mattered, and Aldous hovered behind her pretending to read the weather report, and Mara pretended not to hover while hovering with tremendous skill.

*Having reviewed the matter in full... the provisional suspension is lifted... Mr. Galloway's certification is restored effective immediately...*

"Well?" Aldous demanded.

Wren handed him the letter without a word. He read it, and his tremor picked that moment to still itself, and he read it again, and then he sat down heavily in his chair by the window and said, very quietly, "Good. Good." As if a debt thirty years old had finally cleared.

Mara read it third and said nothing at all, which from Mara was a standing ovation.

* * *

Casper took the news the way he took most good things: suspiciously, then gratefully, then by immediately finding work to do.

He was re-certifying his swiftwater credentials when Wren found him at the training center, dripping, half-frozen, grinning like an idiot in a way he would deny under oath.

"So," she said. "Certified guide again. Legal in two countries. How does it feel?"

"Like I owe the board a fruit basket." He wrung out his sleeve. "How does it feel? You did the digging."

"Like grace earned, not granted." She bumped his shoulder. "Told you the mocking worked."

"It wasn't mocking. It was true, which reads as mockery to committees." He looked out at the river — the lower Serrin, brown and cheerful in the spring melt, the safest hundred miles in the world, the miles he'd paced like a monk for fifteen years. "I keep thinking about the headwaters. Okafor sent photos. The protection order went through — permanent, fenced, the works. The spring's still glowing up there, poisoning nobody."

"Somebody should check on it," Wren said. "Professionally. Periodically. For science."

"Somebody should." He squinted at her. "Somebody with medical clearance."

"Responding beautifully, per my doctor. She said the word *beautifully*, I have witnesses."

"And your father?"

"Coming as far as the confluence. He wants to see the river he tried to own. Mara's driving him. She says if he complains about the road she's leaving him with the geologists." Wren grinned. "Penn and Rill are catering. Rill's wrist healed crooked and he claims it improves his pastry technique."

Casper laughed — really laughed, the unguarded kind she'd heard only a handful of times, usually around fires, usually after confessions. Then he sobered, looking at the water moving past, going somewhere none of them had mapped yet.

"You know what I figured out this winter?" he said. "I used to measure everything in miles. Distance from the disaster. Distance from forgiveness. Miles to the source, miles back out." He shook his head slowly. "It's a terrible unit. Miles don't tell you anything about whether you're getting closer to the right thing."

"What's the better unit?"

He considered it, the way he considered rapids — respectfully, with his whole attention.

"Years," he said finally. "Maybe people. Ask me again downstream."

Downstream. Wren looked at the river, at the man beside her who had once been a ghost and was now, improbably, a colleague, a friend, a guide with a laminated card and everything. The season was opening. The permits were real. Somewhere upstream, a forbidden spring glowed in the dark of a protected valley, beautiful and poisonous and finally, permanently, left alone.

"Race you to the put-in," she said, and ran.
