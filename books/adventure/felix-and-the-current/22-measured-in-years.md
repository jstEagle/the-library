## 22. Measured in Years

The Serrin ran high that spring, fat with snowmelt, and it sang the whole way down.

Casper had forgotten — or maybe he'd only ever pretended to forget — how much noise a river made when you weren't afraid of it. Fifteen years of running the lower reaches under other men's permits, with other men's names on the paperwork, he had learned to hear the water as an adversary: something to be read, outguessed, survived. Now, standing at the put-in above Miller's Bend with a permit folded in his breast pocket bearing his own name — *C. Galloway, Lead Guide, License Renewed* — he heard it differently. It sounded like a conversation he was finally allowed to join.

"You're smiling," Mara said beside him, hauling the last dry bag onto her raft with the efficiency of a woman who could pack a kitchen into a shoebox. "Stop it. It's unsettling."

"I'm allowed to smile. It's in the license."

"It's not in the license. I read the license. There's a clause about excessive cheerfulness near Class IV rapids." She cinched a strap, then glanced up at him, and for a moment the old armor slipped and she just looked at him — looked, the way she hadn't been able to for fifteen years, without a ledger running behind her eyes. "It suits you," she said quietly. "Don't let it go to your head."

"Too late."

Wren came down the bank carrying her own pack, which she refused to let anyone else touch, and which was still, after everything, too big for her. The winter had thinned her further before the treatments caught hold; she moved carefully now, like someone relearning the weight of her own body. But there was color in her face again, and when she reached the rafts she dropped her pack with a theatrical groan that was pure performance, and Casper loved her a little for the performance.

"Lead guide," she said, squinting at him. "They actually gave it back to you. There's no accounting for bureaucratic taste."

"There's accounting for testimony. Yours counted."

"Mine was mostly swearing."

"It was very effective swearing." He checked her PFD buckle without asking, the way he'd done a hundred times now, and she let him, which was its own kind of treaty. "Okafor sends his regards from the survey office, by the way. Says the headwaters protection order is signed, sealed, and laminated. Laminated, Wren. That's permanent."

"He laminated it? God. That man has never been happier than he is right now."

"He framed a copy for his office and a copy for his mother."

* * *

They ran Miller's Bend clean, all three rafts threading the tongue like beads on a string, and the crew they'd hired for the season — two kids out of the ranger program, green as April — whooped like it was the ocean itself. Casper let them whoop. There was a time he would have barked them quiet. There was a longer time when he wouldn't have been on the water at all.

At lunch on the gravel bar, Penn unpacked the food he'd insisted on making himself even though he technically owned a restaurant now — a small one, upriver, with a hand-painted sign reading PENN & RILL'S, RILL PRESIDING OVER THE DESSERTS AND THE OPINIONS. He handed out sandwiches with the gravity of a man distributing sacraments.

"My brother wanted to come," Penn said, settling onto a rock. "Rill. He says his wrist is fully healed and he owes the river an apology."

"The river doesn't want his apology," Mara said. "The river wants him to stop trying to stand up in moving boats."

"He says the same about you."

They laughed, all of them, easily, and Casper sat with his sandwich half-eaten and let the sound wash over him. This was the thing nobody told you about surviving: that joy came back ordinary. Not triumphant, not earned in some grand ledger sense — just ordinary, arriving unannounced on a gravel bar in the form of bad jokes and good bread. For fifteen years he had believed redemption would feel like being forgiven. It turned out it felt like lunch.

Wren sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders touched. She didn't say anything for a while. She'd gotten good at that this winter — at silence that wasn't avoidance. Her illness was in remission, the doctors said, though she hated the word. *"Remission,"* she'd told him once, *"sounds like the disease is just out getting coffee."* But she was here, on the river, eating a sandwich in the sun, and the coffee-drinking disease could stay away as long as it liked.

"I talked to my father yesterday," she said finally.

"How is he?"

"Smaller." She turned a stone over in her fingers. "The metals did what Okafor said they would. The doctors flushed most of it, but there's damage. His hands shake. He forgets words sometimes. He sold the syndicate's mineral rights back to the conservation trust for basically nothing — penance, or maybe just because he can't run it anymore and couldn't stand to see anyone else profit." She paused. "He asked if you'd take him fishing someday. On the lower river. He said — and I quote — 'if Galloway can stand the sight of me, I'd like to learn how to sit in a boat without commanding it.'"

Casper looked out at the water. The current slid past, patient, indifferent, carrying everything downstream eventually.

"Tell him June," he said. "Water'll be gentler then."

Wren exhaled, and he heard in it fifteen years of her family's weight shifting, just slightly, toward something bearable. She bumped her shoulder against his. "You're a better man than the blacklist ever made you look."

"The blacklist never made me look like anything. I did that part myself."

"Yeah, well." She stood, brushing sand off her legs. "You're fired from doing that part yourself. New rule. Effective immediately."

* * *

The afternoon run took them through the Narrows, and Casper called the lines from the lead raft while the two young guides echoed them back, nervous and precise. Mara rode sweep behind them, watching everything, missing nothing, the way she always had. Somewhere in the second drop, one of the kids froze on a stroke call, and Casper felt the old cold start to climb his spine — *the same call, the wrong call, history rhyming* — but the kid recovered, dug in, and came through clean, and the cold receded, and Casper realized his hands were steady.

That was new. That was worth noticing.

At the take-out, golden light already leaning long across the water, they derigged in the easy rhythm of people who had done hard things together and come out the other side still liking each other. The kids drove off with the trailer. Mara sat on the tailgate with a thermos of terrible coffee, and Wren stretched out on the warm planks of the boat ramp like a cat, and Casper stood at the edge of the current with his boots in the wet gravel.

"Fifteen years," Mara said, not looking at him. "Do you know what I measured, all that time? Miles. Contracts. Elevation gained. I had a number for everything. It's how I kept from having to have a feeling about anything." She sipped her coffee. "And then one winter I sat down with my ledger and realized every number in it added up to nothing I'd want written on my stone."

"What do you measure now?"

She considered. "Whether Rill's desserts are improving. Whether the headwaters stay wild. Whether you smile more than you did last month." A pause. "Whether Wren laughs. That one's the big one. That one's the whole ledger, honestly."

Up the ramp, Wren snorted without opening her eyes. "I can hear you."

"Good," Mara said. "Then hear this: you're both insufferable and I'm glad I burned that radio."

Casper laughed — really laughed, from somewhere below the ribs, somewhere he'd thought had silted shut years ago. The sound carried off across the water, and the water carried it away, and neither of those things felt like loss.

Later, when the light went amber and then rose, they walked the bank together, the three of them, no destination. Wren walked between them, and at some point her hand found Casper's sleeve and stayed there, casual as breathing.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" she said. "The source. Everyone always asks me about the source — the spring, the poison, the helicopter, all of it. And I tell them, and they get that hungry look, like they're disappointed it wasn't magic." She kicked a stone into the shallows. "But that's not the part I remember. I remember you carrying me through the braids. Four days. You never once complained, and you never once treated me like I was dying. You treated me like I was heavy and alive and worth the carrying." She looked up at him. "That was the miracle. It just wasn't for sale."

Casper stopped walking. The river went on without them, silvering in the dusk, and he found that his throat had gone tight in the specific way it did when something true arrived and there was nothing useful to do with it except receive it.

"Wren," he managed.

"Don't make it weird, Galloway."

"Too late. It's weird. It's deeply weird."

"Good," she said, and grinned, and the grin was her brother's grin, and for the first time in fifteen years that thought landed in Casper like sunlight instead of a knife.

* * *

They camped that night on the bluff above the confluence — the same confluence where Okafor had once found Casper's stale survey markers, the ones that had started all the unraveling. Someone had pried the old markers out; the trust had done it in the autumn, Okafor reported, with ceremonial solemnity. In their place stood a small brass plaque, bolted to rock:

*UPPER SERRIN HEADWATERS — PROTECTED IN PERPETUITY. MEASURED IN YEARS, NOT MILES.*

Okafor had chosen the inscription. He was very proud of it. He had, in fact, sent Casper a photograph of himself standing next to it, thumbs up, grinning like a man who had personally defeated entropy.

Mara turned in early. Wren sat by the fire turning her father's old compass over in her hands — Aldous had given it to her at Christmas, shaky-handed, wordless, the closest he could come to saying what needed saying. Casper sat across the flames and watched sparks climb toward a sky rinsed clean of everything but stars.

"Casper," Wren said.

"Hm?"

"When we ran the gorge — the Sworn-Off. You led us through by memory. Forty-some rapids, in the dark, in a storm, and you never hesitated once." She fed a stick to the fire. "How many times had you been back there? Before us. Alone."

He didn't lie. He was out of practice at lying, and besides, she'd know.

"Every year," he said. "On the anniversary. Sometimes twice, if the first time didn't take."

"Did it help?"

"No." He considered. "Yes. Neither. It was like — visiting a grave that kept moving. I couldn't fix it. I couldn't leave it. So I learned it by heart instead, until the grief had a route map, and then at least I knew where the drops were." He looked at her across the fire. "That's what I'd tell your father, if he ever asks. You don't get over the ones you lose. You just learn the water. And then one day you're running it with someone living beside you, and you realize the river got wider while you weren't paying attention."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then she crossed the fire circle, sat down beside him, and put her head on his shoulder, the way she had on the portage shelves and in the whiteout and all the places where tenderness had grown in ground that shouldn't hold anything.

"June," she said. "For the fishing trip. I'm coming too."

"I assumed you'd be coming."

"And I'm bringing the terrible sandwiches."

"Penn's bringing the sandwiches."

"Penn's sandwiches are a cry for help. I'm bringing mine."

"God help us all," Casper said, and meant: *thank you, thank you, thank you.*

* * *

Dawn came up rose-gold over the eastern ridgeline, and they broke camp and ran the last twelve miles of the season's first legal trip with the river high and bright and loud. At the take-out, Mara shook Casper's hand formally, then ruined the formality by hugging him hard enough to crack a rib, then stepped back and pointed at him with two fingers, eyes narrowed.

"Same time next season. Both runs. And you're teaching the new guides eddy turns, since apparently you're a legend now."

"Apparently."

"And Casper—" She hesitated, and he saw her reach for the ledger and find it empty, and choose to speak anyway. "I'm glad it was you. All of it. Even the parts I caused."

"Me too," he said. "Ask me again in June."

She laughed and walked to her truck, and Wren loaded gear, humming, and Casper stood a moment alone at the water's edge with his permit in his pocket and his name on the paperwork and the whole long river unwinding ahead of him toward the sea.

Fifteen years he had measured in miles. In elevation lost. In distance from a disaster he couldn't outrun and couldn't explain. He had kept accounts the way drowning men keep air — desperately, uselessly, one breath at a time.

But standing there in the spring light, watching Wren laugh at something on the ramp, hearing Mara's truck grind away down the valley road, he understood at last that the river had never been measuring anything. It just moved. It carried what it carried — silt, sorrow, salmon, the occasional broken guide — and it didn't stop to count, and it didn't look back upstream, and somehow everything it touched ended up farther along than it started.

Casper Galloway took off his hat, let the wind have his hair, and stopped measuring altogether.

Behind him, Wren called out — something about sandwiches, something about June, something about a man named Aldous Crane learning to sit in a boat without commanding it — and Casper turned toward the sound of her voice, and the river sang, and he went to meet it.
