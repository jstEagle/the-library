## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Vell spoke to Jonah Brightwater before she ever opened her eyes.

She woke in the narrow bunk behind the wheelhouse with her ear pressed to the hull, as she had every morning for nine years, and listened. The river's voice came up through the timbers of the *Provision* like a murmur through a wall — a low, ceaseless conversation between water and wood that most people slept straight through and Jonah had never once been able to ignore. This morning it said: *slow water, silt-heavy, the current running lazy two fingers below seasonal mark.* It said: *rain somewhere upstream, days ago, nothing close.* It said, in the creak of the cargo strakes and the tick of the cooling boiler lines: *all well.*

She lay a moment longer anyway, cataloguing the small wrongnesses out of habit. A captain who stopped listening to her boat was a captain already drowned; she'd known men who died of exactly that, comfortable right up until the sandbar took them under. The *Provision* was ninety feet of patched iron and stubbornness, older than Jonah by thirty years, and she complained the way an old woman complains — constantly, specifically, and only when something actually needed doing. This morning she had nothing to say beyond the ordinary litany of wear. Good.

Jonah swung out of the bunk, pulled on the same grey coat she'd worn for three seasons, and went out into the blue hour before dawn.

The lower Vell at first light was a country made of silver and fog. Mist stood on the water in long white columns where the warm current met the cool air, and the paddle-wheel's wake unrolled behind them like a seam being stitched through cloth. Ahead, the banks rose soft and green — willow and alder, the occasional fisherman's hut on stilts, a heron standing in the shallows with the patience of a saint. Somewhere off to port, hidden by fog, the town of Marrow Bend would be waking to its market day, and by noon the *Provision* would be tied up at its quay with forty tons of grain and dyewood to unload.

Jonah climbed to the wheelhouse roof and read the river the way other people read a letter from home.

There — the smooth tongue of water sliding off the point below Cutter's Landing, unbroken, which meant the snag that had plagued that bend since spring had finally shifted or sunk. There — the faint oily dimple downstream of the second island, marking a boil where the current fought itself; she'd give that a wide berth out of respect rather than necessity. And there, half a mile on, the brown stain in the green where the Otterlick came in off the hills, carrying its usual burden of clay. All of it familiar. All of it, after nine years, as legible to her as her own handwriting.

She loved it. That was the thing nobody who knew her casually ever guessed — that Jonah Brightwater, who talked about the river like a debt collector talks about debtors, who called the Vell "her" the way you'd speak of a creditor you couldn't shake, loved the water with a devotion so total it had curdled into something closer to fear. You could only love something that much if it had already taken everything you were willing to lose. She kept the love buried under the expertise, the way you keep a coin under the floorboards: not spent, not admitted, but there.

"Morning, Captain."

Felix came up the wheelhouse ladder with two mugs of Odile's chicory sludge, moving with the easy roll of a boy who'd grown up on a deck. He was sixteen now, though he still had the hollow-cheeked look of the year she'd found him — fourteen, alone, sleeping in a coal skiff at Tarnmouth with his dead parents' name sewn into his collar where some well-meaning soul had written it so no one would forget him. Nobody had. Except her, eventually, which was how he'd come to be handing her chicory on the roof of her own boat at dawn.

"You're up early," she said, taking the mug.

"So are you."

"I'm always up early. You're up early. That's the alarming part." She drank. It was terrible. It was, in its way, perfect. "Something bothering you?"

Felix leaned on the rail beside her and looked out at the fog. He'd gotten tall in the last year without quite filling out, all wrists and collarbones, and he had a way of going quiet that wasn't sullenness — it was more like a door closing gently while he decided whether to open it again.

"It's nothing," he said, which meant it was something. "I just — I dreamed about the upper river last night. Again."

Jonah's hand did not tighten on the mug. She was proud of that.

"Dreams are free," she said. "Everything else costs."

"That's what Bramm says too. You two should start charging each other." He smiled a little, then let it fade. "I didn't mean anything by it. I know we don't go up there. I just wonder sometimes why. Everyone says the oath, everyone says the water remembers — but nobody says why, and you've been up there, haven't you? Before me. Before the Provision even."

The fog was thinning. Below them the wake curled away, and for one absurd moment Jonah had the sensation that the river was listening — that the whole length of the Vell, from the sea gates to the source nine hundred miles upstream, had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when your name is spoken in it.

She put that away with the rest of the things she kept under floorboards.

"Felix," she said, "the upper river is a story people tell to keep captains honest. There's nothing up there but cold water and old towns that don't want visitors. We haul cargo down here, we get paid down here, and we sleep sound because of it. That's the whole of it."

"You didn't answer my question."

"I answered every question worth answering." She said it lightly, and watched him decide — visibly, the way he always did — to let it go. That was the bargain they'd never spoken aloud: she gave him a home and a trade and a place at the rail, and he gave her the courtesy of not asking about the nine years before him. Some mornings she wondered which of them was getting the worse end of it.

They drank their chicory and watched Marrow Bend resolve out of the fog, chimney smoke first, then rooftops, then the long timber quay with its cranes and its crowd of waiting stevedores, and the moment passed the way such moments always passed between them — set down carefully, neither broken nor resolved.

* * *

Marrow Bend smelled of tar, frying onions, and the particular sourness of a grain market in high summer. Jonah ran the docking herself, as she always did — no mate of hers would bring the *Provision* alongside while she had hands and eyes — and she brought her in sweet, a single bell of reverse killing their way at the exact right instant so that the hull kissed the fenders like a lover rather than a brawler. The stevedores, who had seen a hundred boats come in that morning alone, still gave the little approving grunt that dockmen gave good seamanship anywhere on the Vell. It was the only currency Jonah had ever cared to earn.

By midmorning the boat was a hive. Bramm had the engine hatch open and was swearing at the starboard thrust bearing in the meditative, almost tender way he swore at everything mechanical; Odile had established her market circuit with the efficiency of a general taking ground, and came back aboard with salt pork, onions, a sack of apples, and — for reasons she declined to explain — a live goose, which immediately declared war on the deck crew. Felix got sent to settle the manifest with the harbormaster's clerk, which he did faster than any boy his age had a right to, because Felix could do sums in his head the way other boys threw stones.

Which left Jonah to deal with Dockmaster Hesk.

Hesk had held the Marrow Bend berth for twenty years and looked like it: a broad, rumpled man the color of his own pilings, with a beard full of sawdust and a memory for cargoes that bordered on the supernatural. He found her at the gangway checking lashings and handed her the signing papers along with a look she recognized and distrusted — the look of a man with news.

"Brightwater," he said. "You hear anything from upriver lately?"

"Upriver's a big country, Hesk. I hear about prices from Tarnmouth twice a week."

"Not Tarnmouth." He lowered his voice, which was unlike him, and glanced back toward his office as though the paperwork might be eavesdropping. "Above the Last Lock. Upper water."

The lashing in Jonah's hand went still.

"There is no traffic above the Last Lock," she said. "There hasn't been in nine years. That's rather the point of the oath."

"Well." Hesk scratched under his beard, dislodging sawdust. "That's what I'd have said too, this time last month. But barges have been coming down. Three that I know of, maybe four. Big ones, deep-drafted, riding light — coming down empty, mind, which is backwards of how money works. And they're not stopping at the middle towns at all. They run straight through to Corvane landings, flying Corvane colors, and the lock crews up there have all been paid off or bought off, depending who you ask. My cousin keeps the toll book at Wether's Gate, and he says—" Hesk paused, savoring the possession of information the way other men savored brandy. "He says the new traffic doesn't carry manifests. Just sealed orders. And that the men on those barges don't talk, don't drink, and don't blink."

Jonah signed the papers without looking at them. Her signature came out steady, which cost her something.

"Sable Corvane," she said, "hauls whatever she likes wherever she likes. It's no affair of mine."

"No," Hesk agreed. "No, I suppose it isn't." He hesitated, and then — because he was, beneath the sawdust and the gossip, genuinely fond of her in the way of men who had watched her dock a hundred times — he added, quietly: "Only I remembered you used to run up there. Before. And I thought, if anyone would know what Corvane wants with the upper reaches, hauling empty and paying double—"

"Hesk."

It wasn't loud. That was the thing people misread about her temper; they expected volume, and what they got instead was a drop in temperature, a flatness, the voice of a woman standing on ice and hearing it crack. Two stevedores nearby found urgent business elsewhere.

"The upper river is closed," Jonah said. "Closed means closed. It's not a mystery, it's not an invitation, and it's certainly not gossip for a dockmaster with a cousin at Wether's Gate. If Corvane wants to waste her fortune running empty barges through fog, she's welcome to drown doing it. I haul cargo. I don't haul stories. Are my papers in order?"

Hesk looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made him take a half step back, and something in his expression shifted from friendly to careful, the way a man's does around a dog he's suddenly unsure of.

"They're in order, Captain," he said. "Fair currents, then."

"Always," said Jonah, who did not believe in fair currents, and turned back to her lashings.

* * *

She worked the rest of the day harder than the day required.

Anyone who knew her would have known what that meant — that the words *before* and *upper* and *nine years* had gotten under her coat like river damp, and the only remedy she trusted was labor. She re-ran rigging that didn't need re-running. She inspected the cargo hold twice, counting bales she'd already counted. She went down into the bilge with a lantern, ostensibly to check the limbers, actually to lie against the frames and listen to the water slide past six inches of iron from her skin, and to tell herself — as she told herself every time — that the past was upstream, and she was going the other way, and distance was a kind of safety, and nine years of distance was practically a fortress.

The river murmured under the hull. It sounded, today, almost amused.

Toward evening Felix found her at the stern rail. The loading was done; the *Provision* sat heavy and satisfied in the water, and the lamps of Marrow Bend were coming alight along the quay, gold smudges in the blue dusk. He came to stand beside her the way he always did, without asking, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation — the kind they were best at.

"Odile's making the goose fight Bramm for kitchen rights," Felix offered. "Bramm's losing."

"He usually does."

"Also the harbormaster's clerk says there's a woman asking after boats at the landings downstream. Asking after us, specifically. Somebody named Corvane keeps sending letters."

Jonah felt the day's labor drain out of her arms all at once, replaced by something colder and older.

"What kind of letters?"

"Business letters, the clerk thinks. Contracts. But he says the woman asking isn't a clerk's sort of person. He says she has a way of looking at a boat like she's already bought it." Felix turned, and in the lamplight his face was open and young and trusting, and Jonah thought, with a sudden sick clarity: *whatever comes up that river is going to come for him first, because I taught him to answer questions honestly.* "Captain? Is something wrong?"

The Vell slid past below them, black and patient, carrying its secrets down toward the sea. Nine years, Jonah thought. Nine years of keeping her word and her distance and her mouth shut, and the world had apparently decided the interest had compounded long enough.

"Nothing's wrong," she lied, and put her hand briefly on the boy's shoulder, and wished — for the first time in a very long time — that she believed in prayer. "Get some sleep. We make the Tarnmouth run at dawn."

Felix went. Jonah stayed at the rail until the lamps burned low, watching the dark water, listening to it remember.
