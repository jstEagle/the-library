## 2. A Debt with Teeth

The town was called Marrow's Bend, and it smelled of tar, frying onions, and the particular sweetness of river mud left to bake in the sun — a smell Felix had come to think of as the smell of stopping, because they so rarely did.

"Two hours," Jonah had said at the gangplank, not looking at him, her eyes already on the manifest Bramm had thrust at her. "Cargo's grain and salt pork. Odile wants pepper if you can find it that isn't ground mouse. Don't buy anything from anyone who smiles too fast."

"I never do," Felix said.

"You always do." But there was the ghost of something at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the place where a smile had once lived and paid rent — and she turned up the plank toward the wheelhouse before he could catch it properly.

That was how it went between them. Nine years she'd kept him, since the night she'd found him folded into a coil of rope on her aft deck like cargo nobody claimed, seven years old and hollow-eyed, and she'd never once asked him where he came from. He'd decided long ago this was kindness. Lately he'd begun to wonder if it was something else — some old wound of hers that made questions dangerous, so that she extended to him the same mercy she wanted for herself: no past required.

He didn't hold it against her. Mostly.

Marrow's Bend clung to the lower Vell the way barnacles cling to a hull — stubbornly, lopsidedly, half-submerged in its own commerce. The main street ran parallel to the water and was really just the dock grown ambitious: chandleries, a cooper's yard ringing with hammer-song, three taverns of descending reputation, and a fish market where catfish longer than Felix's arm lay stacked in blue ice, their whiskers still twitching with indignation. Above the street, houses climbed the bluff in crooked tiers, their laundry lines strung between chimneys like bunting for a festival nobody had announced.

Felix walked it all with his hands in his pockets and the Provision's key ring jingling against his thigh — Jonah trusted him with the keys now, had for two years, and he carried them the way other boys carried knives: as proof of what he was worth to somebody.

He bought the pepper first, haggling with a spice merchant whose smile was exactly as fast as Jonah had warned, and got the price down by a third by claiming — falsely — that the cook aboard the Provision poisoned slow learners. The merchant laughed, Felix smiled back, and both of them understood the transaction completely.

Then he had an hour, and an hour in a strange town is a kind of wealth, and Felix had learned to spend it the way Jonah spent everything: carefully, but on himself when no one was watching.

He went up. Away from the water, past the last warehouse, where the street gave way to a lane and the lane to stairs cut into the bluff, and the town opened beneath him like a map of itself — the Vell running wide and brown-green and patient, the barges small as toys, and among them, moored at the deep-water berth, the *Provision*, her black hull gleaming, her single mast bare, unmistakable. His chest did the thing it always did when he saw her from outside: a tightening, half pride, half fear, as if seeing her small from a distance reminded him how small everything was, how easily a boat becomes a speck, a speck becomes a story, a story becomes nothing.

There was a notice board at the top of the stairs. Towns like this always had one — a great weathered slab of planking nailed over with layers of paper, proclamations atop proclamations, so that peeling one loose revealed the ghost of another underneath, decades of official language composting into mulch. Ferry schedules. A lost dog, white, one ear. A harvest festival. A tax decree so old the seal had faded to a bruise-colored smudge.

And posters.

Felix had seen wanted posters before, the way you see weather. They were part of the river's furniture: smugglers, debt-fleers, a man who'd burned a lockhouse up near Grayfall. He read them idly, the way you'd skim a stranger's mail, feeling nothing.

The first one stopped him because the face was wrong and right at once.

It was a woodcut, and woodcuts flatten everyone into the same hard geometry — heavy jaw, flat stare — but the jaw was hers. The set of the shoulders in the portrait, even cropped to the head, was hers: that forward-leaning readiness, like a woman perpetually about to step onto unstable deck. And under the face, in letters that had been printed fresh, ink still sharp-edged:

**JONAH BRIGHTWATER. WANTED FOR QUESTIONING. CHARGES WITHHELD BY ORDER OF THE RIVER COMMISSION. REWARD: 200 CROWNS. INFORMATION ONLY. DO NOT DETAIN.**

*Do not detain.* Felix read it three times. Every other poster on the board said *detain*, said *bind and bring*, said *dead or alive* with cheerful barbarity. This one said the opposite, which meant whoever wanted her didn't want her caught. They wanted her *found*. Watched. Tracked.

Charges withheld. By order of the River Commission. Felix knew the Commission the way every boat kid knew it: as a seal on documents, a toll collector's badge, a rumor of men in gray who arrived by gunboat and left with ledgers. It had never, in nine years, had anything to do with Jonah.

He looked over his shoulder. The lane was empty. A woman beat a rug two balconies down; the thump of it kept time with his pulse.

Nine years, he thought. She took me in nine years ago. Nine years ago she started running the lower river only. He had never put those two facts side by side before. They fit together with a click he felt in his teeth.

His hand went to the poster's corner before he'd decided anything. The paper came away from the nail with a soft tearing whisper, and then it was folded in his pocket, against his hip, warm already, like something alive.

He told himself he'd give it to her. Of course he would. He just needed to understand it first, because if he handed Jonah Brightwater a piece of paper with her own face on it and asked *what is this?* without knowing anything, she would do what she always did when a question touched the hull of her — she'd go quiet, and steer harder, and whatever door the question had knocked on would be barred for another year.

He needed leverage. Not against her — God, not against her — but against the silence. Something to trade. An answer for an answer.

So he kept reading the board.

The second poster was smaller, older, its corners curled and sun-bleached to the color of weak tea, pinned so long it had grown into the wood, and he almost missed it behind a ferry schedule. The paper stock was finer than any of the others. Expensive. Out of place among the dog notices and tax decrees like a silk glove dropped in a fish market.

No picture. Just text, in a typeface so clean it looked machine-stamped:

**BY PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY OF THE RIVER COMMISSION: NOTICE OF INQUIRY.**

**PERSONS OR DESCENDANTS OF PERSONS HERETOFORE DESIGNATED KEEPERS OF THE SOURCE ARE REQUESTED TO PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION.**

**COOPERATION WILL BE COMPENSATED. NON-COOPERATION WILL BE ADJUDICATED.**

**REWARD FOR VERIFIED IDENTIFICATION OF ANY KEEPER LINE: 1,000 CROWNS.**

Felix stood very still.

The rug-beating woman had stopped. Somewhere below, a barge horn sounded, long and low, and the sound seemed to pass through the board, through him, through the whole sunlit afternoon.

Keepers of the source. He turned the words over. There were stories — deckhand stories, the kind told at night when Odile had gone below and Bramm was drunk enough to talk — about the upper Vell, the water above the Last Lock that no captain would run. The water that remembered what it was given. In the stories there was always something at the top of the river. A stone. A spring. A heart, one version said, though Bramm had laughed at that one until his cough doubled him over. And keepers — families sworn to it, generations deep, until —

Until what? The stories never said. The stories trailed off the way stories do when the teller realizes he's talking about things that got people hurt.

*Descendants of persons.* Felix's mouth had gone dry. He thought of the rope coil, the night sky, the deck of the Provision under his cheek. He thought of how Jonah had never asked. He thought — suddenly, horribly, with the clarity of a man falling who has just noticed the ground — of his own face. Of the face people sometimes stopped and looked at a half-second too long in river towns, the face an old woman at a fish stall in Corley's Landing had gripped his wrist over, murmuring a word he hadn't caught, before shuffling away. He'd forgotten it. He'd filed it under strangeness and moved on, because moving on was the first thing the river taught you.

He reached out and peeled the second poster free. It fought him — the old paste held like a grudge — and half the backing tore away, leaving a pale rectangle on the board like a scar. He folded it with the first one, both papers together against his ribs now, and they felt heavier than paper had any right to feel.

Below him, the *Provision* sat at her berth, patient and black and beautiful, and for the first time in nine years Felix looked at her and saw a hiding place instead of a home.

* * *

He took the long way back down, telling himself it was to find Odile's second errand — lamp oil, actually, though the pepper had been the real one — and mostly to delay. The stairs, the lane, the chandlery where he bought oil and didn't hear a word the chandler said. His body walked and bargained while his mind sat on the notice board turning the two posters over and over like river stones.

Two hundred crowns for information. A thousand for a keeper line. And *do not detain* — someone wanted Jonah watched, not caught, which meant someone wanted her to lead them somewhere. You don't follow a woman for nine years unless she knows a road you don't.

The upper river. The thought arrived whole, unbidden, cold as well-water. Someone thinks Jonah knows the way up.

But she didn't. Did she? In nine years he'd never heard her speak of it except to shut it down — and she shut it down hard, doors slamming, conversation dead, once making a dockhand at Pell repeat himself just so she could refuse him twice. The oath, the other captains called it. Every sworn captain keeps off the upper water. Jonah wasn't sworn to anything that Felix knew of. She mocked the oath. And yet she kept it more faithfully than any of them.

Unless keeping off it wasn't obedience. Unless it was penance. Unless there was something up there she'd left, or done, or buried, and the poster with her face on it was a hook someone had baited with her own history.

He was seventeen years old and standing in a chandlery holding lamp oil, and he understood suddenly that the woman who'd fished him out of the dark nine years ago was herself swimming in something darker, and had been the whole time, and had never once let it touch him. That was the shape of it. That was what the silence was. Not coldness. Construction. She'd built him a boat inside her boat, and sealed it, and kept it dry, and he'd lived in it thinking the whole vessel was watertight.

He paid the chandler. He didn't smile.

* * *

The dock at Marrow's Bend was louder than he'd left it — a grain barge had come in astern of them, and stevedores swayed along the planks with sacks on their shoulders, chanting some work-song older than the town. The *Provision*'s deck was organized chaos: Bramm in the engine hatch to his waist, swearing at the starboard shaft bearing with real affection; Odile on the galley roof sorting peppers and rejecting half of Felix's purchase on sight ("Mouse-ground," she said, "I can smell the rodent ambition"); and Jonah up on the foredeck checking lashings on the new cargo, moving with that economy of hers, no motion wasted, no motion soft.

Felix stood at the foot of the gangplank with the oil in one hand and the posters against his ribs, and looked up at her, and felt the papers burn.

Tell her. Now, before the fear sets in and teaches you to wait. Tell her, and watch her face do the thing it does—

Jonah looked up. Their eyes met across forty feet of deck, and she raised one hand — not a wave, exactly, more an acknowledgment of existence, captain to crew, *you're back, good* — and went back to her lashings.

And Felix, standing in the noise and the light with two pieces of paper folded over his heart, found that he couldn't move.

Because here was the thing he saw, in that half-second of eye contact, plain as the woodcut on the poster: she was happy. Not visibly — Jonah Brightwater's happiness was a private currency, rarely minted — but he knew its denominations. The set of her shoulders as she worked. The way she'd checked the lashings twice, thorough rather than anxious. Two hours in port, cargo loaded, river ahead, and for once nothing chasing them. He could read her the way he could read water, and the water today said *easy, says easy, says run easy.*

If he handed her that poster, the easy water ended. Whatever was coming — and something was coming, he could feel it now the way sailors feel weather, in the ache of old scars — it would start the moment she saw her own face on that board. He could keep it one day. One day to understand, to ask around, to learn what *keeper* meant and why a thousand crowns hung on the word. One day, and then he'd tell her everything, and she'd be angry, and then she'd get over it, because she always got over it, because whatever else Jonah Brightwater was, she was fair.

That was the arithmetic he did at the foot of the gangplank, seventeen years old, and it was almost honest, and it was wrong in every way an arithmetic can be wrong.

"Felix!" Odile bellowed, hurling a rejected pepper at his head with unerring aim. "You sleeping down there? Oil!"

He caught the pepper. He climbed the plank. He stowed the oil, ate Odile's stew, endured Bramm's third retelling of the shaft-bearing saga, and answered Jonah's evening question — "Everything squared?" — with "Squared," which was true of the errands and false of everything else.

The posters stayed folded in the inner pocket of his jacket, where he'd moved them, where he could feel them when he crossed his arms, two thin rectangles of dread riding over his heartbeat.

They cast off at dusk. Marrow's Bend slid away astern, its lamps coming on one by one along the bluff like a string of low stars, and the Vell took them south, broad and slow and brown-green, smelling of mud and onions and the sweet rot of summer. Felix stood his watch at the bow while Jonah had the wheel, and the river murmured against the hull, and everything was ordinary, and everything was over.

Because it was during that watch, in the last gold light, that he noticed the boat.

It had been two miles back since Marrow's Bend — he'd taken it for a fisherman, the way you take any lone craft for a fisherman, because fishermen are the river's alibi for everything. But fishermen work the water. This one held station. Two miles back, hour after hour, matching their speed through the dusk with the patience of something that had done this before, that was *paid* to do this before, that had a poster of Jonah Brightwater somewhere aboard and instructions that said *do not detain.*

Felix didn't turn around. He'd learned that much from her — never look directly at the thing following you. He watched it in the corner of his eye, a dark fleck on darkening water, and he pressed his hand flat against his jacket, over the papers, over his heart, and he thought: *one day. I said one day.*

Behind him, faint through the engine thrum, he heard Jonah begin to hum — tuneless, absent, the sound she made when she believed herself alone and unobserved, the sound of a woman who thought, tonight, that the river was finally letting her rest.

The fleck on the water held its distance.

Felix stood his watch, and said nothing, and the river carried them all — the guilty, the hunted, and the boy carrying two pieces of paper that weighed as much as the world — steadily, patiently, upstream of everything that was coming.
