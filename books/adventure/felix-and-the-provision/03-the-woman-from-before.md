## 3. The Woman from Before

The Provision came into Harrow's Bend with the last of the light, and Jonah Brightwater let her drift the final hundred yards rather than fight the current, because there was no hurry in the world that was worth hurrying for at the end of a long run downriver. The town's lamps were waking one by one along the levee — oil-yellow, wavering in the dusk like a string of low stars someone had hung carelessly and never bothered to straighten. Somewhere behind the fish-smoke houses a fiddle was tuning up, one note repeated until it found its neighbors.

Jonah stood at the wheel with her hands loose on the spokes and felt the boat settle under her the way an old dog settles by a fire: a sigh through the hull, a softening of everything that had been taut all day. Nine years she'd been running this water, and the lower Vell still told her things it told no one else. Where the channel shifted after spring flood. Which sandbars were honest and which ones lied. Where the current ran cold and quick over some drowned thing below, so that the surface went glassy and wrong for thirty feet and then forgot itself again.

She knew the river the way you know a house in the dark. That was the trouble with knowing anything that well. You stopped being able to leave it.

"Felix!" she called aft. "Lines!"

He was already moving — she'd seen him before she'd called, which was the way of them now, two years of shared work worn smooth as river stone. The boy took the bow line in three strides and turned it twice around the bollard on the Harrow's Bend dock without being told which bollard, because he'd learned her habits better than she'd ever taught them. Fourteen years old and already he read water like a man three times his age. Already he stood the night watch alone without complaint, and sang the old working songs under his breath in a voice that cracked only when he forgot to be embarrassed about it.

"Easy," Jonah said, backing the paddlewheel down. The Provision kissed the pilings gentle as a moth landing. "Good."

Felix grinned up at her from the dock, rope still in his hands, hair full of wind. He had that look he got at every stop — the look of a boy cataloguing a town, filing away its alleys and its markets and its rumors for later exploration. She'd have to keep half an eye on him tonight. The last time they'd laid over at Harrow's Bend he'd come back at midnight with a pocketful of river pearls he swore he'd found fair and a black eye he refused to explain.

"Cargo's light tomorrow," Odile said, appearing at Jonah's elbow with the eternal cloth she used to wipe down surfaces that were already clean. The cook's forearms were bare, flour to the elbow, and she smelled of onions and woodsmoke as always. "Grain and salt, then we're empty as a Sunday church."

"Good. I want a fast turn."

"You want to outrun something." Odile said it mildly, wiping the rail, not looking at her. "You always want a fast turn when you're outrunning something."

"I want triple freight rates and a tailwind, and neither of us is getting either." Jonah stepped off the wheel and rolled her shoulders until something in her back cracked agreeably. "Bramm has the engine?"

"Bramm has the engine, the engine has Bramm, and the two of them are having a religious experience down there. Go eat. You've been steering since dawn and you steer worse when you're hungry, which I'd have said was impossible."

Jonah ate standing up in the galley doorway, bread and beans out of the pot, watching the lamplight come up on the levee. It was a good town, Harrow's Bend. Honest traffic, honest tolls, a dockmaster who didn't ask questions because he'd been asking questions back when Jonah first ran the Vell and had retired from the practice on the sensible grounds that answers cost more than curiosity paid.

Which was why it surprised her, an hour later, when Dockmaster Pell asked one anyway.

* * *

Pell caught her at the foot of the gangplank, where she'd gone to check the mooring lines against the evening breeze. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray-bearded, with hands like cured hams, and he came up close the way men do when they don't want to be overheard — too close, apologetic, breathing mint over whatever he'd had at supper.

"Captain Brightwater." His eyes went past her shoulder, upriver, toward the dark line where the valley narrowed and the hills closed in. "You heard anything out of the upper reaches lately?"

Jonah felt the question go into her like a fishhook going into a thumb — clean entry, no warning, all the damage on the inside.

"There's nothing up there but water and ghosts," she said. "And the water's not worth talking about."

"That's what I thought too." Pell rubbed his jaw. "But I've had three manifests through here this month marked for Ashport, and Ashport's the last manned lock before the forbidden reach. Nobody ships grain to Ashport. There's nothing at Ashport to feed. And yesterday a Corvane barge went through flying no colors at all, which around here means somebody paid enough that colors weren't necessary." He lowered his voice further, though there was no one near. "Traffic on the upper river, Captain. First in nine years. First since—"

"Pell." She kept her voice level and pleasant, the voice she used for negotiating wharfage. Inside, something old and cold turned over. "I haul cargo. I don't haul gossip. If the Corvane interests want to drown themselves above the last lock, that's their affair and their funeral, and I'll thank you to keep my boat out of the sentence."

"I wasn't—" He spread those ham-hands. "I just figured if anyone would want to know—"

"Well, I don't." She said it harder than she meant to, hard enough that a couple of deckhands loading salt down the dock looked over, and she saw Pell take a small step back, and she hated herself a little for it, and did it anyway. "Enjoy your evening, Dockmaster."

She walked back up the gangplank with her spine straight and her heart going like a drum in a parade, and she stood at the rail in the dark and made herself breathe.

Nine years. Nine years since she'd been above the Last Lock, nine years since the source, nine years since the thing she'd done that she told no one about, not Bramm, not Odile, not Felix, not God, if God was taking inquiries. The captains' oath kept everyone off the upper river — sworn or unsworn, the whole brotherhood of the Vell treated the water above Ashport like a grave, and Jonah had spent nine years being grateful for the company of their superstition, because it meant no one else went looking either.

And now Corvane barges, running dark, headed up.

She told herself it was coincidence. She told herself Sable Corvane was a toll-keeper and a freight baron and a woman who bought things, and buying things was all she'd ever wanted, and none of the things she bought had anything to do with Jonah Brightwater or the year the fish died.

She almost believed it. Then she saw the launch coming across the water.

* * *

It came without lights, which on the Vell at night meant one of two things: smugglers, or people who owned enough of the river that the rules bent around them like current around a piling. The launch cut the black water in a clean white seam and came alongside the Provision's starboard quarter with the confidence of a cat stepping onto a familiar fence, and the man at the tiller threw a line without being asked, and Jonah was already at the rail when the woman stood up in the stern.

Sable Corvane had not changed, which was somehow worse than if she had. Nine years, and she was still narrow and straight as a pike pole, still dressed in gray so dark it read as shadow, still wearing her silver hair cropped short as a man's and her face arranged into an expression of courteous attention that made you feel like a ledger entry being reviewed. Her eyes found Jonah in the dark the way a lamp finds a mark.

"Captain Brightwater." Her voice carried across the water without effort, pitched like a bell struck softly. "You're a difficult woman to find. Or perhaps just a difficult woman to approach. I've tried letters."

"I burn letters."

"So my clerks surmised." A faint movement at the corner of her mouth, not quite a smile; Sable Corvane did not smile, she permitted her mouth to suggest it. "May I come aboard?"

"No."

"Captain—"

"This is my boat, madam, and my rail, and you're hanging off both without invitation. State your business to the water. It's more patient than I am."

Below, Jonah heard Felix stir on the forward deck, heard Bramm's hatch scrape, felt her crew assembling behind her the way a body assembles a fever. Good. Let them see. Let them learn what she looked like when she was frightened, so they'd know it later, though she'd have given a good deal for them never to find out.

Sable regarded her for a long moment. Then she put one gray-gloved hand on the Provision's rail — Jonah watched the glove close on wood she had varnished herself, and disliked how much it mattered to her — and said, pleasantly, "Triple freight. Paid in advance. One sealed cargo, one trip, to the source of the Vell."

The words dropped into the night and sat there.

Around them the river went on doing what rivers do — slapping the hull, worrying the pilings, carrying the fiddle music from the levee out across the water in frayed threads. But Jonah heard none of it clearly. She heard the word *source*, spoken aloud, in the open air, above the Last Lock, by a woman who had no business knowing the word meant anything at all.

"You're mistaken about your geography," Jonah said. "There's no such destination. The charts end at Ashport. Everything above is blank paper."

"The charts end at Ashport because the captains' guild pays to keep them blank." Sable's tone didn't change; it never changed; that was the horror of her, that voice like still water. "I've read the older surveys, Captain. The ones from before the oath. The source of the Vell is a spring-fed amphitheater in the high stone, four days' hard steaming above the Last Lock for a boat of this class. Your boat can make it in three and a half, if her captain doesn't dawdle." She paused. "I'm told she never dawdles."

"Whoever told you that was selling you something."

"You did. Nine years ago." For the first time, something moved behind the gray eyes — not warmth, nothing so human as warmth, but a kind of appetite sharpening. "You sold me passage once before, Captain Brightwater. Upstream. You were younger, and cheaper, and considerably less rude."

The bottom dropped out of Jonah's stomach so smoothly, so completely, that for a moment she kept her face perfectly still and her voice perfectly bored while everything beneath it fell.

"I don't remember you."

"No," Sable agreed. "You were careful not to. It was a different name on the manifest, and a different flag on the mast, and you took payment in coin that couldn't be traced, because the man who hired you insisted, and you didn't ask why, because the pay was triple." She let that sit. "I've done my reading, Captain. I know about the boat that went up the Vell nine years ago with a hold full of machinery and came down empty. I know about the season the fish died. I know about the floods that followed, and the towns that drowned, and the oath the captains swore afterward — an oath, incidentally, that I notice you've honored with remarkable devotion for a woman who breaks every other rule on this river."

"Get off my boat."

"I haven't boarded it."

"Get off my rail, then. Get off my night. Whatever you think you know, you're wrong, and even if you're right, there isn't money enough in Corvane's coffers to buy what you're asking for."

"Triple freight," Sable said again, mild as milk. "Paid in advance. Five hundred crowns deposited with the Harrow's Bend assay office this afternoon, against your signature. I'm told you're carrying debts, Captain. I'm told the Provision needs a new boiler plate and her starboard paddle bearings are worn to lace. I'm told you've been running thin margins for two years and paying your crew late." Her head tilted, very slightly, a bird considering. "I don't need your answer tonight. I need it by morning, when the assay office opens. Refuse, and the deposit returns to me, and I'll find another boat — there are always other boats, other captains, men who ask fewer questions than you do. Accept, and you'll be rich enough to stop counting."

"And if I say the answer's no right now, tonight, from this rail?"

"Then I'll say good evening, Captain, and mean it." Sable straightened, and the launch shifted beneath her, ready. "I am not in the habit of forcing people. Forcing leaves marks. I prefer to arrange circumstances so that people force themselves." She gathered her skirts — gray wool, plain, expensive the way a knife is plain — and paused, one hand on the rail, and looked at Jonah with those ledger-keeping eyes, and delivered the rest in the same soft bell-tone she'd used for everything, which was how Jonah knew it was the point of the whole visit:

"One more item for your consideration. The cargo I wish to ship is sealed, and heavy, and it must reach the source intact. What it is doesn't concern you. But you should know that I chose your boat for reasons beyond her speed. I chose her because her captain has been to the source before, and knows the channels, and — most valuable of all — knows exactly where to dig." The pause stretched, delicate as wire. "Whatever you buried up there nine years ago, Captain Brightwater, I will find it. With your help or without it. The only question the morning holds is whether you'd rather be paid for the shovel work."

The launch pulled away without another word, oars dipping silent, the white seam of its wake closing behind it like a wound healing. Jonah stood at the rail until the sound of it was gone, until there was nothing on the black water but the lamps of Harrow's Bend shivering upside-down, and the fiddle music, and her own breath going in and out of her like a bellows with a tear in it.

Behind her, quietly, Felix said, "Captain? Who was that?"

Jonah looked at him. The boy stood in the lamplight spilling from the cabin, rope calluses on his hands, worry on his young face, and she thought: *not yet*. Not yet, not ever, if she could manage it. Some cargoes you didn't let a fourteen-year-old carry.

"A bill collector," she said. "Go to bed, Felix. We sail early."

"For where?"

She looked upriver, at the dark narrowing between the hills, where the water ran cold over everything she'd done.

"Nowhere," Jonah said. "Nowhere at all."

But she didn't sleep, and neither, though he pretended otherwise, did the boy — and in the morning, when the assay office opened, there would be five hundred crowns waiting against her signature, and a sealed crate waiting on the Corvane dock, and a hook set so deep in her that no amount of running downriver would ever pull it free.
