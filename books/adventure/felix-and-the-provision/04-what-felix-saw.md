## 4. What Felix Saw

Felix dreamed of the poster.

In the dream it was bigger than a barn door, Jonah's face printed across it in ink that ran like oil, and beneath her likeness the words kept rearranging themselves — *WANTED*, then *KEEPER OF THE SOURCE*, then something he couldn't read because the letters were swimming upstream against the grain of the paper. He woke with his heart going like a paddlewheel and the cabin dark around him, the Provision rocking gently at her moorings in Harrow Bend, and for a moment he couldn't remember which fear was real and which was only sleep.

Then he heard the shouting.

It came from above — from the main deck — and it was not Jonah's voice. Jonah's anger had a shape he knew well: low, controlled, dangerous as deep water. This was different. This was several voices at once, hard and clipped, the voices of men who gave orders rather than asked questions, and underneath them he could hear Bramm's engine-room boots hammering up the ladder and Odile's door bang open two cabins down.

Felix was out of his bunk before he'd finished deciding to move. He grabbed the knife off his shelf — not the gutting knife, the short one he kept for lines and knots — and then stood there in the dark feeling foolish, because what was he going to do with a knife against whatever was happening up there? He took it anyway. His hands wanted to hold something.

The corridor smelled wrong. That was the first thing that truly frightened him, though he didn't understand why until later: the Provision always smelled of bilgewater and tar and Odile's onions, a smell so constant Felix had stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Tonight there was lamp-oil in the air too, sharp and fresh, and boot-mud that wasn't theirs, and under all of it a faint sweetness like the perfume rich women wore at the landings. Sable Corvane's perfume. He'd caught it once on the dock at Cattermill when the woman herself had swept past, and his body remembered it the way a dog remembers a whip.

He pushed open the deck door and the cold hit him first — river fog, thick as wool, beading on his face — and then the lamplight, and then the men.

There were four of them. They stood in a loose line along the starboard rail with the ease of people who had already decided they belonged wherever they were standing, and they were dressed like working boatmen, canvas jackets and rubber boots, but nothing about them was work-worn. Their jackets were too clean. Their boots had never hauled a line. And each of them wore a long gun slung across his back with the casualness of a man carrying an umbrella, which on the lower Vell was a thing no honest deckhand ever did, because the river towns had laws about weapons and the river itself had opinions.

Jonah stood amidships with her back to the wheelhouse, arms crossed, and even now — even with four guns pointed generally in her direction — she managed to look less like a woman being threatened than a woman being inconvenienced. Her jaw was set. Her eyes moved constantly, cataloguing: the men, the gangplank, the fog, the dark water beyond the rail where anything might be waiting.

Bramm stood by the cargo hatch, huge and gray-faced, his wrench still in one fist as if he'd come up swinging and been talked out of it. Odile had planted herself at the top of the galley stairs with a cast-iron skillet held like a shield, and one of the agents — the tall one, the one who seemed to be in charge — kept glancing at her with the wary respect of a man who'd heard about women like this.

And on the deck between Jonah and the hatch, papers lay scattered like dead birds.

"Manifest," the tall man said, when he saw Felix's eyes go to them. "Your captain's being difficult about it."

"I'm being *thorough*," Jonah said. "You boarded my boat without papers, without a harbor warrant, in the middle of the night—"

"We had papers." The tall man smiled. It was a professional smile, practiced, the kind that never reached anywhere near the eyes. "You just weren't shown them."

Felix stayed in the shadow of the deck door. Nobody had seen him yet, or nobody cared, and some animal instinct told him that staying unseen was worth more right now than courage. He watched the tall man the way he'd learned to watch eddies — reading the surface for what moved underneath.

"You can search the hold till spring," Jonah was saying. "There's no shipment aboard. I haven't signed any contract. I don't haul sealed cargo, I don't run the upper river, and I don't take orders from women who send hired guns instead of calling at my office like a civilized person."

"No contract yet," the tall man agreed pleasantly. "That's the difficulty, isn't it? Madam Corvane is a patient woman, Captain Brightwater. She buys things slowly. Land, locks, loyalties." He tilted his head. "But patience has limits, and tonight we've reached hers."

"We?" Odile said from her stairs. "You got a mouse in your pocket?"

The tall man ignored her, which took a certain kind of discipline, and reached inside his jacket. Felix's grip tightened on his knife — but what came out was not a weapon. It was a folded paper, cream-colored, heavy stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than Felix made in a week. The tall man unfolded it with two fingers, delicately, like a surgeon opening a patient.

"Madam Corvane asked me to convey a message," he said. "She asked me to convey it *clearly*, because in her experience, Captain, you are a woman who hears what she wants to hear and no more."

"Get off my boat."

"In a moment." He smoothed the paper against his thigh. "First — the boy."

Every hair on Felix's arms stood up.

"He sleeps aft," the tall man continued, conversationally, as if discussing cargo weights. "Small cabin, port side, below the galley. Keeps a knife by his bunk — the short one, good for lines. Keeps a folded newspaper clipping in a tin under his floorboard, though I doubt that's relevant. Wakes easily. Dreams loudly, one imagines." He looked up, and his gaze went past Jonah, past Bramm, past Odile's skillet, and found Felix in the doorway as precisely as a lantern finding a mark in fog.

"There you are," he said warmly.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fog had burned off and the sun came up mean and white over Harrow Bend — Felix would try to reconstruct those next minutes and find that memory had been at the brandy. What he retained were fragments, sharp and disconnected as broken glass.

Odile coming down the stairs like weather, skillet raised, and Bramm catching her around the waist with both arms, the skillet ringing off a stanchion. Jonah's voice cracking across the deck like a whip — *"Touch him and I will put you through the wheel of this boat"* — and the strange, calm certainty in Felix's own chest that she meant it absolutely, and the stranger, colder certainty underneath it that it wouldn't matter. Two of the agents moving toward him with the unhurried confidence of men doing a job they'd done many times. The tall man raising one finger, and everything stopping.

"No need," he said. "No need at all. We're leaving." He looked at Felix almost kindly, which was worse. "You see how it is, lad. Your captain needs convincing, and you're the argument."

He crossed the deck, stepped over the scattered manifest pages without looking down, and pressed the cream-colored paper into Jonah's hands, folding her fingers over it.

"The manifest is copied. We have what we came for. Deliver the shipment to the source, Captain — on Madam Corvane's schedule, by Madam Corvane's route. Do that, and the boy grows old and gray and tiresome." He smiled again, and this time it did reach his eyes, and what lived there made Felix's stomach drop through the deck plates. "Don't do it, and—" He spread his hands, leaving the rest to imagination, which was the cruelest thing he could have done, because Felix's imagination was very good.

"And if I go to the magistrates," Jonah said. Her voice was level. Only Felix, who knew her, could hear the wire pulled tight beneath it. "If I report armed men boarding a licensed vessel—"

"Report to whom, Captain?" The tall man was already walking toward the gangplank, his three shadows falling in behind him. "Harrow Bend's harbormaster banks with Corvane. The magistrate at Cattermill leases his courthouse from her. You've spent nine years keeping your name off paper, and now you want to file one?" He paused at the head of the plank, and the fog curled around him like something summoned. "Oh — and Captain. The oath. The one all you river captains swore, the one that keeps every hull off the upper water." He tapped his temple. "We know you've already decided to break it. We counted on it. Sleep well."

Then they were gone, down the plank into the fog, four sets of clean boots fading into the dark, and the Provision rocked gently at her moorings as if nothing had happened at all.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

The fog closed over the gangplank. Somewhere across the bend a night bird called, absurdly peaceful. Odile lowered her skillet by degrees, like a flag coming down after a battle, and Bramm let out a breath that seemed to empty half his enormous chest.

Felix became aware that his legs were shaking. He sat down on the deck, right there, right where he stood, because standing had stopped being a thing his body would agree to.

Jonah hadn't moved. She stood exactly where the tall man had left her, the cream paper in her fist, staring at the fog where the men had gone. In the lamplight her face had gone the color of old rope, and Felix saw something in it he had never seen in nine years of watching her — nine years of storms and lock-fees and debt collectors and once, memorably, a floating casino full of men with knives. He had seen Jonah afraid exactly twice, and both times she'd hidden it inside anger so fast you'd miss it if you blinked.

She wasn't hiding it now. There was no anger left to hide it behind.

"Jonah," Odile said quietly.

"They knew his cabin." Jonah's voice came out flat and strange. "They knew which side. They knew about the knife. They knew about—" She stopped. Her hand, holding the paper, had begun very slightly to shake. "They've been watching us. Watching *him*. How long?"

"Long enough," Bramm rumbled. He said it gently, for Bramm, but the word landed like a dropped anchor.

Jonah looked down at the paper in her hand as if she'd forgotten it. Then she unfolded it, and read it, and Felix watched her face while she did, and what crossed it was not surprise. That was the thing he couldn't stop turning over afterward, lying awake in his small cabin listening to the river: not surprise. Recognition. Like a woman reading a letter she'd been dreading for years and had finally, finally arrived.

"What does it say?" Felix asked. His voice came out younger than he wanted it to.

Jonah didn't answer. She refolded the paper along its creases, precise as a card sharp, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, which was either a gesture of care or of containment — Felix genuinely couldn't tell which.

"It says," she said at last, "that I'm to deliver a shipment I never agreed to carry, to a place I've sworn never to go, on behalf of a woman I threw off this boat two nights ago." She turned and looked at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious and terribly tired. "And it says that if I don't, you pay for it."

"I'm not scared of her," Felix said, which was a lie, and everyone on the deck knew it was a lie, and everyone was kind enough not to say so.

"I know you're not." Jonah came and crouched in front of him, close enough that he could see the fog-beads in her gray hair, and for a moment her hand hovered near his shoulder — he felt the warmth of it, almost-touching — and then withdrew, the way it always did, the way it had for nine years, Jonah Brightwater keeping even her comforts at oar's length. "That's not the point, Felix. The point is—" She stopped. Started again. "I built this life so that nobody could ever be taken from me as leverage. Nine years. No debts I couldn't walk away from, no family anyone could find, no port I loved enough to die in. That was the whole design." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Felix had ever heard from her. "And she walked aboard and took the one thing I forgot to design against."

"Which was what?" Odile asked softly.

"A crew," Jonah said. "People." She stood up, and when she turned away Felix saw her shoulders square themselves, muscle by muscle, the way they did before a hard lock or a hard lie. "This was never about money. Triple pay, sealed cargo, all of it — that was the bait for some other captain. Some greedy, stupid captain who'd sign anything." Her voice dropped low, and the river seemed to lean in to listen. "Sable Corvane doesn't want a hauler. She wants me. Specifically me. And she wants me at the source, and she's known for nine years exactly what it would take to get me there."

The fog was thinning. Through the thinning, far downstream, Felix could see the first gray suggestion of dawn, and the black line of the upper river beyond the last lock, the forbidden water, the place the captains' oath forbade and the maps simply stopped.

"So what do we do?" he asked.

Jonah was quiet for a long time. When she answered, she wasn't looking at any of them. She was looking upstream.

"Now," she said, "we do the one thing she's certain we'll never do."

She turned back to the wheelhouse, and her hand found the bell-cord, and before anyone could ask what the one thing was, Jonah Brightwater rang the Provision awake — three hard strokes, the signal for all hands, the signal Felix had heard perhaps five times in nine years, always before disaster.

"Get your kit," she said. "We're taking on cargo at first light. And God forgive me — we're going upriver."
