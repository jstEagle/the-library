## 5. Crew of the Willing

The Provision sat low in the water that evening, riding heavy with the silence of a boat that had been searched. Jonah felt it in the way she moved through her own decks — every plank familiar, and yet nothing hers anymore. Sable's agents had gone over her boat like appraisers at an estate sale. They had touched Bramm's tools. They had opened Odile's flour bins and run their fingers along the rims. One of them had put his boot on Felix's bunk while he read the message aloud, slow, savoring it, like a man tasting wine he intended to spit out.

*Deliver the shipment or the boy answers for it.*

She had burned the note in the galley stove after everyone had seen it, but the words were still there, written in ash on the inside of her skull.

Now she stood in the saloon with the lamp turned low and the shutters drawn, waiting for her crew. Three people in the world she could call hers, and one of them was a boy asleep below with a knife under his pillow because she'd told him to keep it there. The other two were coming up the gangway now — she heard Bramm's uneven tread, the clank of his bad knee against the iron plate, and behind him Odile's lighter step, quick as water over stones.

They came in and took their places without being asked. That was the thing about a crew that had run with you for years: they knew when a captain wanted chairs and when she wanted them standing. Bramm folded himself onto the bench by the bulkhead, arms crossed over a chest like a boiler, gray stubble on a jaw that looked carved for scowling. Odile perched on the table's edge, which she was never supposed to do, which she did every time, wiping her hands on an apron that had fed Jonah longer than Felix had been alive.

"Someone talk," Bramm said. "Or I'll start guessing, and my guesses are ugly."

Jonah poured three glasses of the plum brandy she kept for funerals and contracts and didn't offer any explanation for it. She set one in front of each of them and kept the third herself, turning it, watching the lamplight swim in it.

"You've both been on this river long enough to know what the Vell remembers," she said. "You know why no captain runs the upper water. You know the oath."

"I know you don't talk about it," Bramm said. "Which is louder than any oath."

"The oath is simple. Every licensed captain swears it the day they take their papers. *I will not pass the Last Lock. I will not carry cargo above Drowned Bellhaven. What the river gave back, I will not go looking for.* We swear it because nine years ago something went wrong up there, and the men who wrote the oath decided forgetting was safer than fixing." She drank. The brandy bit down her throat like a hook going in. "Tonight I'm asking you to break it with me."

Odile stopped wiping her hands.

"There's a cargo," Jonah went on, before courage could fail her. "Sealed. It came aboard two nights ago in Corvane crates, paid for in Corvane gold, and it sits in my hold right now behind a lock I don't have the key to. Sable Corvane wants it taken to the source of the Vell. Not near it. To it. And she has taken Felix to make sure I say yes."

"Took him," Bramm repeated flatly. "Took him where?"

"He's aboard. Asleep in his bunk with a knife under his pillow. Her agents left him breathing as collateral." Jonah heard her own voice go strange and calm, the voice she used in rapids, and hated it. "If we turn the boat around, if we run, if we so much as look like we're running — they answer for it. There are men watching us from the bank tonight. There will be men watching us every mile upstream."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the old girl creaked the way she always did when the temperature dropped, settling into her bones.

"So that's the shape of it," Jonah said. "I've told you the crime. Now I'll tell you the truth, because you've earned it and because I'm done lying to people who bleed on my decks."

She set down the glass. Her hands were not steady. She let them be seen not steady.

"Nine years ago I ran cargo for a man named Halveric, and the last job he gave me was a run to the source. I told myself it was salvage. I told myself a lot of things. What came down from that water I helped carry, and what happened after — the floods, the fish dying belly-up from Ashport to the delta, the towns that went quiet — began that same season. I didn't understand what I'd helped steal until it was too late to give it back. And then I spent nine years running downstream as fast as this boat would carry me, telling myself the oath was someone else's promise." She looked at each of them in turn. "It wasn't. It was mine. And now the woman who owns my debt owns my boy, and the only road to either is straight up the throat of the river I swore off."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the deck plates.

Bramm reached for the brandy and drank half of it in one motion. "You're asking," he said slowly, "whether we sail."

"I'm not asking anything. I'm telling you what I have to do, and then I'm telling you that you're free. Any man or woman who walks off this boat tonight walks clean. No wages lost, no grudge carried, and if Corvane comes asking who helped me, they can say they quit a madwoman's crew before she broke her oath. I'll swear to it myself, publicly, at the harbormaster's office tomorrow morning if it keeps Corvane's lawyers off you." She made herself smile, and it hurt her face. "That's the offer. It's a good one. Take it."

Odile hadn't moved. She was staring into her glass like it held the whole river. "And Felix?"

"Felix stays with me. Wherever this ends, he ends it under my protection or not at all."

"And you?" Odile looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once, the way only Odile's eyes could be. "Where do *you* end, Jonah Brightwater? Because I've cooked for you eleven years, and I've watched you outrun everything — creditors, sheriffs, your own name — and I never once saw you outrun yourself. This isn't a cargo run. This is you walking back into the one place you can't run from."

"Yes," Jonah said simply.

"*Yes?*" Odile stood up off the table, apron snapping. "That's all? Nine years of lies and one word?"

"What would you have me say? That I'm sorry? I am. That I was a fool? Worse than a fool. Say it plain, Odile — what do you want from me that I haven't already lost?"

"I want you to have asked us!" Odile's voice cracked and she pushed it back down. "Eleven years, and tonight's the first time you've told us the truth, and it comes wrapped in a dismissal — *here's the door, walk through it, save yourselves.* You don't get to decide our courage for us, Captain. That's not loyalty. That's just pride wearing loyalty's coat."

The words landed harder than any blow Jonah had taken in nine years, because they were true, and because they came from a woman who had earned the right to throw them.

Bramm cleared his throat. "Sit down, Odile."

"Don't you tell me to—"

"Sit down and let me talk, woman, before my knee gives out and I have to do it standing." He waited until she dropped back onto the bench, glaring. Then he looked at Jonah, and his face, which usually showed nothing but grease and grievance, showed something older. "I ran the upper water once. Before the oath, before the trouble. Hauling timber, when I was young enough to think rivers were roads." He tapped his knee. "Got this on the shallows above Bellhaven, back when Bellhaven still had a name that wasn't an insult. The water up there is different, Jonah. It doesn't push like lower water. It *decides*. I've felt it. So I know exactly what you're asking us to sail into, and I'm going to tell you my answer, and then I'm going to bed, because I'm old and this conversation has cost me enough brandy."

He stood. His knee cracked like a mooring line parting.

"My wife's buried in Marrow Bend, downstream. My whole life is downstream. Everything sensible in me says walk." He picked up his glass, drained it, set it down with a click. "But I built half this engine with my own hands, and I'll be damned if I let some Corvane hired hand with soft palms run her upriver. She's mine same as yours. So I stay. Not for the oath. Not for your guilt. For the engine, and for the boy, who eats like a stoker and reads like a scholar and deserves better than to be currency." He paused at the door. "Fix the number-three bearing before we run the narrows. It's been singing for a week and I'm tired of hearing it."

Then he was gone, boots fading down the companionway, leaving Jonah holding a breath she hadn't known she'd taken.

Odile remained. The lamp guttered and steadied. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone quiet, scraped clean of anger.

"When I was nineteen I worked a crossing ferry on the Sorrel, and it went down in an ice flood with my brother aboard. Sixteen people, and the river kept every one of them. I swore I'd never love anything that floated again." She laughed, short and bitter. "Then a skinny captain with more debts than sense hired me to cook on a rust-bucket called the Provision, and here I am, twenty-six years later, about to break a sacred oath for her."

"Odile—"

"I'm not finished." She held up a hand. "You told us the truth tonight, and it was ugly, and it was late, and it was yours. Fine. But hear mine. If we go up that river and the water decides against us — if it takes the boat, takes you, takes that boy — I want you to know that I chose it. Nobody dragged me. No debt, no threat, no loyalty I couldn't help. I choose it the way I chose this boat, knowing what floating things cost." She stood, and for a moment her hand rested on Jonah's shoulder, brief and hard as a rivet driven home. "Two stay. That's your answer. Now go check on Felix, because I put honey in his tea and he pretends he doesn't notice, and pretending is a habit this family needs to break."

She left. Jonah stood alone in the saloon with three empty glasses and the full weight of what she'd asked for and received.

* * *

She found Felix awake, of course. He was sitting up in his bunk with the knife across his knees and the honeyed tea untouched on the shelf, and when she came in he didn't pretend to sleep or smile or be brave. He just looked at her with Odile's question already in his eyes: *where does this end?*

"They're staying," she said. "Both of them."

"I heard." He turned the knife over. "Through the floor. Bramm's voice carries like a foghorn." A pause. "Captain — the men on the bank. How many?"

"Enough."

"And if we run anyway? Fast, at night, dark water — this boat can outrun anything Corvane hires."

"She'd catch us in a week, and then it wouldn't be a negotiation." Jonah sat on the edge of the opposite bunk, the springs complaining beneath her. "Felix, I need to ask you something and I need the truth, even if it's ugly. When those men came aboard — did they say anything to you? Anything at all?"

He was quiet a long moment. "One of them looked at me funny. Like he was checking me against something. A list, maybe." He shrugged, too casual by half. "Why?"

Because I've seen that look before, she almost said. Because Sable Corvane doesn't spend gold on insurance she hasn't verified. Because there is something about you, boy, that I've never asked about, and I'm afraid I already know why. But the lamp was low and the hour was late and she was a coward in exactly one direction, so she said only:

"No reason. Get some sleep. We make way at first light."

She rose to go, and his voice caught her at the door, small and steady at once.

"Captain? Whatever we're carrying — whatever she wants with it — I'm glad it's us taking it there. Anyone else would just deliver it." He lay back, pulling the blanket up. "We might actually understand what we're doing."

Jonah stood in the doorway a moment longer, watching the boy who was not her son settle into sleep on a boat sailing toward judgment, and understood that she had just been given something more frightening than any threat Sable Corvane could issue.

Trust.

Upstream, the river waited, and somewhere past the Last Lock, an old friend of hers was keeping watch over the gate she had sworn never to pass — and come morning, she would have to look him in the eye and ask him to open it.
