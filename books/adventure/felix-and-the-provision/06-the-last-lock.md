## 6. The Last Lock

Felix felt the change in the river before he saw it.

For three days the Vell had been climbing — not steeply, but with a patience that unnerved him more than any rapid would have. The water moved like something walking uphill in its sleep, slow and heavy and deliberate, pooling behind every bend as if it were reluctant to go where it was going. The banks had narrowed. The hills had closed in on both sides, dark with pine, and the air had taken on a cold mineral smell that Felix had never smelled on the lower river, where the water tasted of silt and towns and people.

This water tasted of nothing. That was the strangest part. It was clean the way an empty room is clean.

"You keep looking over the rail like you dropped your supper in it," Odile said, coming up beside him with two mugs of chicory. She pressed one into his hands. Her knuckles were still bandaged from the winch accident at Grayford Landing, and she held her mug awkwardly, but she'd refused to stay below decks, refused to be cargo on her own boat.

"Something's wrong with it," Felix said.

"It's cold, is what's wrong with it." She drank, grimaced. "Cold water always looks wrong to me. Like it's pretending."

Behind them, the Provision's engine turned over with its familiar uneven rhythm — Bramm called it a heartbeat, Jonah called it a complaint, and both of them were right. They'd been running since dawn without speaking much. The silence aboard had changed too, Felix thought. Before, when they'd all agreed to go upstream, there had been a kind of grim festival feeling, the crew of the willing against the world. Now the willingness had worn down to something quieter and harder, like rope gone stiff with salt.

Jonah stood at the wheel, as she almost always did, one hand resting on the spokes without gripping them. She hadn't slept much. Felix knew because he hadn't either, and twice in the night he'd heard her boots cross the deck above his bunk, slow, out and back, out and back, like a woman pacing a cell she'd built herself.

Ahead, around a shoulder of granite, Felix saw the towers.

* * *

The lock at Harrowgate was the last one. Everyone on the river knew that, even boys who'd grown up on the lower reaches and never come this far. Below Harrowgate, the Vell was a working river: locks and tolls and dockmasters, barges and pleasure boats, the whole busy commerce of the valley. Above it, the river climbed another hundred miles through country that got wilder and emptier until it reached the source — and no licensed captain went there. Not because it was illegal, exactly. Because of the oath. Every captain on the Vell swore it when they took their papers, the same words in the same old formula, and Felix had heard Jonah recite it once, drunk enough to be honest, her voice flat as slate: *I will not take my vessel above the last lock, for the upper water remembers what it is given.*

He'd asked her once what it meant. She'd looked at him for a long moment and said, "It means captains are superstitious," and poured herself another drink, and he'd known better than to ask again.

Harrowgate itself was older than anything else on the river. The lock sat in a gorge so narrow the boat seemed to fill it wall to wall, and the stonework was black with centuries of waterline moss, cut in great blocks by hands that had no business having been this good at it. Two towers flanked the gates, and from the taller one hung the chain that would be raised to bar their passage if the lockkeeper decided they shouldn't pass.

A man was already standing on the lower gate when they came alongside, and Felix watched Jonah see him, and watched something happen in her face that he had never seen there before — not fear exactly, and not joy, but some old thing between them, surfacing like a body coming up out of deep water.

"Hallo, Tam," she called down.

The man on the gate was sixty if he was a day, lean as a fence rail, with white hair cropped close and a lockkeeper's long coat buttoned to the throat despite the mild morning. He looked at Jonah the way men look at a storm front.

"Jonah Brightwater," he said. His voice carried easily across the water; it was a voice built for shouting over machinery. "Nine years I've kept this gate, and nine years I've watched you turn around right about where you're standing now, every season, regular as migration. And now here you come, pointed upstream, with a sealed hold and a face like a funeral."

"Business," Jonah said.

"Business." Tam said the word like he was tasting something spoiled. He came down the gate ladder with a spryness that belied his age and stepped aboard without asking, which told Felix everything about how long these two had known each other. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and wet stone. His eyes went over the boat — the patched hull, the new scuffing along the starboard side from the narrows, Bramm's head poking curiously out of the engine hatch — and then they found Felix, and stayed on him a moment too long, and something in them tightened.

"And who's this?"

"Felix. My deckhand."

"Your deckhand." Tam said that strangely too, and Felix felt the hair rise on his arms, though he couldn't have said why. "Well. Come into the wheelhouse, the pair of you. What I've got to say isn't for open decks."

* * *

The wheelhouse was small and smelled of Odile's coffee and Bramm's pipe, though Bramm had the sense to put the pipe away when Tam's eyes flicked to it. Odile stayed at the door with her arms crossed, radiating the particular hospitality of a cook guarding her galley's border. Jonah leaned against the chart table. Tam stood in the middle of the room like a man delivering a verdict, and for a while nobody spoke, and the only sound was the engine turning over below and the water slapping the hull with that patient, uphill patience Felix had come to distrust.

"I'll say it plain," Tam said finally, "because plain is all I've got left. Turn back, Jonah."

"No."

"You don't even know what I'm going to say yet."

"I know why you're saying it." Jonah's voice was level, but Felix, standing near her, could see the small tell he'd learned to read over four years aboard — the muscle jumping once in her jaw, the hand at her side curling and uncurling. "You're going to tell me the upper river is death. You're going to tell me about the oath. You might even tell me about the drowned towns, if you're feeling dramatic. And then I'm going to say no again, and we're going to waste the morning."

Tam didn't flinch. If anything he grew calmer, the way old men do when they've decided grief can wait its turn behind duty. "Corvane's bought the locks," he said.

The word landed in the wheelhouse and sat there.

"All of them?" Jonah asked. Her voice had changed. Not louder. Quieter, which was worse.

"Every manned lock above Harrowgate. Six of them. Bought outright, crews dismissed or hired over, papers signed before the frost. I know because Corvane's agents came to me first, three weeks back. Offered me a number I'll not repeat in company." He smiled without any humor in it. "I told them the gate wasn't mine to sell. It belongs to the river commission, and the commission belongs to the charter, and the charter says this lock stays free passage or no passage at all. So they stopped arguing with me and started buying everyone else. By the time I understood what was happening, I was the only keeper left on the upper Vell who hadn't signed."

"So you're telling me," Jonah said slowly, "that if we pass your gate, there's nothing ahead but Corvane's people. Every gate opens for her. Every lock is hers. We'd be running a hundred miles of river inside her fist."

"I'm telling you worse than that." Tam's eyes went to Felix again, and this time he didn't look away quickly enough, and Jonah caught it.

"What?" she said. "What is it about the boy?"

Tam took his time. Felix realized he was holding his breath and made himself let it out.

"There was a second man," Tam said. "Came with Corvane's agents. Didn't talk, didn't sign anything. Just walked the gate, top to bottom, and then stood looking at you—" he nodded at Felix, and the bottom dropped out of Felix's stomach— "and asked me questions. Whether I knew the family that used to keep the source. Whether any of them survived. Whether any of them had fair hair and grey eyes and a scar over the left eyebrow."

Felix's hand went to his eyebrow before he could stop it. The scar was old; he'd had it since before he could remember, since before the fever took his mother and the river took everything else, and he'd never once thought of it as anything but an accident of a childhood he couldn't recall.

"He described me," Felix said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted.

"He described you like a man reading off a bill of lading," Tam said gently. "Like cargo."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Odile had gone still at the door. Bramm had come up the ladder at some point without anyone noticing and now filled the hatchway, big hands wrapped around the frame, his weathered face unreadable.

"They're not hunting us," Jonah said. The realization moved across her face like weather. "They're not even hunting Wren, not primarily. They're collecting. The heart, the keeper, and the heir. All three pieces, and they already know where two of them are sleeping tonight."

"That's the shape of it," Tam said. "Which is why I'm begging you, Jonah, and I'll beg as long as you'll stand there and let me, and longer after. Turn this boat around. Take whatever's in that hold and drop it in the deepest hole in the lower Vell and let Corvane chase her fortune downcurrent. There's no version of what's ahead that ends well. I've kept this gate thirty years. I've seen what comes down from the upper water when it comes down at all. Boats without crews. Crews without sense. Once — only once, and I've never told a living soul — a skiff came down with a man in it who'd gone white as paper and wouldn't say a word, just kept making a motion like this—" Tam raised one hand, fingers spread, and drew it slowly down through the air in front of his chest, over and over, the gesture of a man pulling something heavy out of deep water. "Whatever's up there doesn't want visitors. Your oath exists for a reason, girl. Nine years ago you helped write the reason."

Jonah went very still.

Felix watched it happen — watched the sentence land somewhere beneath her ribs, watched her decide, in the space of half a breath, whether to pretend she hadn't heard it. He'd spent four years learning her silences, and this one was the deepest he'd ever seen.

"You don't know what I did nine years ago," she said at last.

"I know what came down the river that autumn," Tam said. "Dead fish from Harrowgate to the delta, floating belly-up for forty miles. Water that ran brown in high summer. The first of the drowned fields. I keep a gate, Jonah. Gates feel things. This one's been aching for nine years, and it started aching the season you stopped coming upstream." He shook his head slowly. "I'm not your judge. I'm just the last man standing between you and the water, and I'm asking you to hear me. Whatever Corvane promised you, whatever she's holding over you—" his eyes flicked to Felix, and this time he didn't disguise it— "it will cost more than she said. It always costs more than she said."

* * *

They stood on the foredeck afterward, Jonah and Tam, while the crew waited and the engine idled and the black water pooled patiently against the closed lower gates. Felix kept within earshot, coiling a line that didn't need coiling, and neither of them told him to move.

"Who was the quiet one?" Jonah asked. "The one who asked about Felix."

"Didn't give a name. Grey coat, grey eyes, hands like a clerk. But he wore his signet on the wrong finger, and I've seen that trick before — men who don't want their house recognized." Tam paused. "There's an old mark on the seal, though. I caught it when he signed the refusal receipt. A circle with a line through it, and a dot above."

Felix's coil of rope slipped out of his hands.

He didn't know why. He didn't know the mark, had never seen it, could not have drawn it if you asked him to. But his body knew it the way his body knew the river's mood before his eyes did — some inheritance deeper than memory, some thread running back through the fever and the orphanage and the four years on the Provision to a family and a duty that had died before he learned to walk. A circle with a line through it, and a dot above. He mouthed the shape to himself and felt it fit somewhere inside him like a key fitting a lock that had been rusted shut his whole life.

On the foredeck, Jonah had gone pale.

"You know it," Tam said. It wasn't a question.

"I know it." Jonah looked out at the water, at the towers, at the chain hanging slack above the gates, and Felix saw her make the decision before she spoke it, saw it settle over her shoulders like a coat. "Open the gate, Tam."

"Jonah—"

"You heard me. Open it. And then get yourself off this lock and downriver, because if Corvane finds out you refused her money and helped us anyway, she'll bury you in paperwork until you drown in it. Go stay with your sister in Millbrook. I mean it."

Tam studied her for a long moment. Then he did something Felix didn't expect: he laughed, short and dry, like a single knock on a door.

"Thirty years keeping this gate," he said, "and the Brightwater girl asks me to abandon my post. Fine. I'll go to Millbrook. But I'm leaving the gate open behind you, Jonah, and I'm leaving it open coming back, because someone ought to leave a door open in case the river decides to give anything back." He turned to climb down to the gate controls, then paused, one hand on the rail. "One more thing. Free advice, worth what you paid. That water ahead of you — it's been strange this past month. Clearer than it's ever been. And the current's wrong. Runs stronger than the fall should make it, and always, always upstream of the wind. Like it's pulling." He met her eyes. "Like it wants something brought to it."

He went down. Chains began to move. Somewhere inside the ancient stonework, gears that had turned for three hundred years groaned awake, and the lower gates swung inward, and the black patient water of the upper Vell slid into the lock chamber to meet them.

Felix stood at the rail with the mark burning behind his eyes — circle, line, dot — and watched the last door on the river open in front of them, and understood that whatever waited on the other side already knew his name.

The Provision eased forward into the lock, and the gates closed behind them like a mouth.
