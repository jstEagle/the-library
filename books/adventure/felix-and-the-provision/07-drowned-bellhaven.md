## 7. Drowned Bellhaven

The river changed its mind about them two miles below the drowned town.

Jonah felt it first in the wheel, a softness in the current that hadn't been there at dawn — as though the Vell had been pushing them along all morning and now, abruptly, had stopped caring whether they arrived. The water went glassy and slow, and the color of it deepened from the brown-green of the lower river to something darker, colder, like tea steeped too long in iron. She'd been running the Vell for nine years and she had never seen it do this. Rivers didn't hesitate. Rivers were the most honest things she knew.

"Slow her down," she said, though Bramm was already easing the throttle back, the engine's note dropping to a mutter.

Felix came up from the foredeck with the sounding pole still in his hands, his sleeves rolled past his elbows, his hair stuck to his forehead in the heat off the boiler. He'd grown into the work over the years — he read the water now without being told, watched for snags and sandbars the way other boys watched for trouble. But even he had gone quiet, standing at the rail and staring ahead at what waited on the river.

Bellhaven.

It rose out of the water like something surfacing from a long sleep. The rooftops came first — slate and shingle, dark with decades of silt, some of them collapsed into their own attics, others strangely whole, chimneys still standing straight as fence posts. Then the upper stories of the taller houses, windows just breaking the surface, glass long since gone, so that each window was an open black eye looking up through the water at nothing. A church spire leaned at a drunken angle off to the north side, its bell still hanging in the belfry, green with age, silent. The town sat in maybe eight feet of clear, cold water, and the clarity was the worst part. Jonah could see the streets below. Cobbled lanes running between drowned doorways. A wagon bed half-buried in silt. A child's rocking horse, impossibly, still rocking faintly in some deep current, its runners whispering against the stones.

Nobody on deck said anything for a long time.

Charts called it the Bellhaven Reach. The lockkeepers called it the Shallows, if they called it anything at all, and they never explained why. Jonah had passed it twice before in nine years — twice, because there was no reason to come this far up the Vell unless you were hauling timber or running from something, and she had done both — and both times she had hugged the eastern channel where the old floodplain dropped away and the drowned town stayed to port, a thing to be steered around and not looked at.

There was no avoiding it today. Sable Corvane's charts, delivered with the sealed cargo manifest, put the channel straight through the heart of Bellhaven.

"She flooded it," Felix said quietly. It wasn't a question, but Jonah answered it anyway, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be fought.

"No," she said. "Not her. This was before her time." She turned the wheel a few degrees, lining up on the steeple — the only landmark left worth trusting. "But somebody did. And they did it on purpose."

"How can you know that?"

"Because towns don't drown themselves, boy." She heard how hard her own voice came out and softened it. "Look at the cut banks. See how clean they are? That's not erosion. That's excavation. Somebody dug those channels. Somebody built that dam upstream and let the water rise slow, house by house, street by street, until everybody who lived here gave up and walked away. That takes years. That takes patience. That takes money."

Bramm had come up from the engine room, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being gray sometime last year. He was a broad, slow-moving man with a beard like rusted wire and eyes that missed very little. He stood at the rail beside Felix and studied the drowned town with the particular expression he wore when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"My grandfather hauled freight out of Bellhaven," he said. "Before my time. Grain, mostly. He used to say it was the fairest town on the upper Vell. Good people. Good wells." He paused. "He never said what happened to it. Nobody ever says what happened to it. You ask, and they change the subject like you'd asked after a dead wife."

"Maybe they did," Odile called from the galley doorway, where she'd appeared with a dish towel over her shoulder and an expression that said she'd been listening through the bulkhead, which of course she had. "Towns die like wives. Quietly, and then everyone pretends it was always going to happen."

"Odile," Jonah said.

"I'm cooking. I'm allowed opinions." She went back inside, but not before Jonah caught the look she traded with Bramm — the look of two people who had signed on for a cargo run and were beginning to suspect they'd signed on for something else entirely.

* * *

They took the channel through the town at quarter speed, and it was like sailing through a graveyard with the graves open.

The Provision drew four feet light, six loaded, and the flooded streets gave them seven at the deepest, according to Sable's charts — charts drawn by someone who knew this water intimately, someone who had measured every lane and alley before the flooding or sounded them after. That thought kept working at Jonah as she conned the boat between the rooftops: whoever made these charts had wanted this route navigable. Had wanted a boat to be able to come here, heavy-laden, quiet, unremarked. Which meant the route itself was part of whatever Sable was doing, planned down to the fathom.

"Steeple bears two points off the port bow," Felix called from the bow, pole ready. "Water looks deep enough. There's a lane marker — look, on that chimney —"

Jonah saw it. Painted on the brick of a chimney rising from the water, faded almost to nothing but still legible if you knew to look: a white diamond, and beneath it a number. Seven. Someone had marked the channel through a drowned town, decades ago, and the marks were still true.

"That's keeper's work," said a voice behind her, and Jonah's blood went cold before her brain caught up and reminded her it was only Wren.

The old man had barely left the hold since they'd found him in the crate three nights ago — gaunt as winter, hands like knotted rope, speaking rarely and then only when the silence got worse than the words. He came to the rail now and stood beside her, looking down at the white diamond on the chimney with an expression Jonah couldn't read. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. With Wren it was hard to tell where one ended and the other began.

"Keepers marked the safe channels," he said. "All the way from the source to the delta. Every hazard, every shoal, every crossing. So that anyone could travel the river safely, and no one could claim it." His voice was dry as the paper of old maps. "My mother painted that mark. Or one like it. Before I was born."

"You're telling me your family charted a town they knew was going to drown?"

"I'm telling you," Wren said, "that the keepers opposed the dam for thirty years. Petitions, surveys, lawsuits. They lost. Money lost. It usually does." He turned his pale eyes on her. "You asked once why the fish started dying nine years ago. Ask instead why this town died fifty years ago, and nobody will say its name."

Jonah gripped the wheel and said nothing, because the answer was already forming in her chest, cold and complete, and she wasn't ready to say it. The dam upstream. The source. Something up there worth drowning a town full of good people and good wells — worth buying every lock, worth hiring Sable Corvane, worth stealing the heart of a river. You didn't go to that much trouble to hide water.

* * *

They anchored for the night in the wide water east of the church, where the charts showed an old millpond, deep and sheltered. Jonah didn't like stopping inside the reach — dead towns made her skin crawl, and the silence was wrong, thick, listening — but the light was failing and the channel narrowed ahead into the shallows the charts warned about, and pride had sunk better boats than hers.

Supper was beans and salt pork and nobody's appetite. Odile set the bowls out and watched everyone pretend to eat. Felix went up top for the first watch, and Jonah heard his boots on the deck overhead, pacing, pausing, pacing — the rhythm of a boy turning a question over and over like a stone in his pocket. He'd been carrying questions ever since Ashport, ever since the poster, ever since she'd told him the truth about what she'd done nine years ago, and she knew the weight of them. She carried her own matching set.

She was below, checking the cargo lashings for the third time that day, when Bramm's boots came down the ladder fast — faster than Bramm moved for anything short of fire.

"Captain. You need to see the hull."

The lantern in his hand threw jumping shadows as he led her aft along the bilge stringers, crouched double under the deck beams. He stopped at the turn of the starboard bow, where the planking curved down toward the waterline, and held the lantern close.

"There," he said. "And there. And — give me your knife."

Jonah handed it over. Bramm scraped gently at a patch of the hull below a seam, and the river grime came away in thin curls, revealing what lay beneath: a row of small, precise holes drilled into the outer planking, plugged with wax, wax painted over to match the bottom paint. Three inches apart. Perfectly spaced. Below the waterline, where no deckhand scrubbing the sides would ever see them, where no casual eye would ever catch them.

"They're bored clean through in two spots," Bramm said quietly. "Wax plugs holding the rest. Those two have been weeping slow since before Ashport — that's why the pumps have been running more than they should. I thought it was a bad packing gland. It wasn't a gland." He looked up at her, lantern light carving his face into planes and hollows. "Somebody sabotaged us, Captain. In port. Before we ever cast off. Somebody who wanted us taking on water slow, so we'd be bailing and tired and grateful by the time we got wherever they want us."

Jonah crouched and touched the holes herself. Small. Neat. Professional. Done by someone with access to the Provision's hull in a friendly harbor, someone whose presence on the dock wouldn't have raised a single eyebrow.

Sable's people had been aboard her boat before Sable ever boarded it. Maybe before Sable ever sent the contract. The threat hadn't begun with Felix. It had begun here, in the dark, with a drill and a pot of wax, while Jonah slept ten feet above.

"We plug them proper?" she asked.

"With what? We're a hundred miles from a marine railway. I can drive oakum and pitch into them from inside, slow the seepage to nothing, but if one of those plugs lets go in rough water—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to.

"Do the oakum tonight. All of them."

"Aye." Bramm hesitated, and Jonah knew him well enough to know what was coming. "Captain — whoever drilled these knew our berth in Ashport. Knew our schedule. Knew we'd take this contract even after you threw Corvane off the boat." He met her eyes. "This was never a job we were hired for. It's a job we were steered into. Like a boat in a current."

Jonah stood in the dimness of her own bilge, in the belly of the only home she had left, and felt the truth of it settle over her like cold water rising. Every mile upstream, she'd told herself she was choosing this — choosing it for Felix, choosing it to keep her word, choosing it because a Brightwater didn't leave a debt unpaid. But the holes in her hull said otherwise. Somebody had decided her course before she had. Somebody had drilled it into her, plank by plank, and let her believe she was steering.

Above her, faint through the deck beams, she heard Felix's boots stop pacing.

Then she heard him shout — not alarm exactly, but close, the sharp note of a boy who has seen something he cannot explain — and she was moving, up the ladder, out onto the deck, into the night air where the drowned town lay black and silver under a high moon.

Felix stood at the bow rail, pointing. Not at the rooftops. At the water alongside.

The river around the Provision was glowing. Faintly, fitfully, a green-blue shimmer rising from somewhere far below the keel — from the drowned streets, the drowned wells, the drowned church — pulsing slow, like breath, like a heartbeat, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and dreaming of the surface. As Jonah watched, the glow brightened once, twice, and in its light she could see straight down through forty feet of impossible clarity: the cobbled square, the leaning bell tower, and the bell itself, hanging silent in its frame, trembling.

Ringing without a sound. Or waiting to.

"Wren," Jonah called, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Get up here. Tell me what my river is trying to say."

Behind her, below her feet, she heard Bramm driving oakum into the wounds in her hull, and the river glowed, and glowed, and glowed.
