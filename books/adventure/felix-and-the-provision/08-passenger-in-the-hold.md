## 8. Passenger in the Hold

The crate had been bothering Felix for two days.

Not the way a loose plank bothers you, or a fouled line, or any of the hundred small complaints a riverboat makes to whoever will listen. The Provision complained constantly, and Bramm answered her in grunts and grease, and that was the natural order of things. This was different. This was the kind of bother that lived in the back of the skull and turned over when you tried to sleep, like a stone in a riverbed shifting under current.

It was the silence.

Felix had spent nine years learning what cargo sounded like. Grain shifted and sighed. Timber creaked as it took the damp. Salt meat wept brine into its barrels with a soft, wet whisper he could hear through two decks if the wind was right. Even stone — and they'd hauled stone once, downriver from the quarries at Marrow Bend — even stone had a voice, a low grinding mutter when the boat rolled.

This crate said nothing. It sat amidships in the hold, lashed to the keelson with new rope that smelled of tar and money, six feet long and four wide and four deep, wrapped in oilcloth and bound in iron bands, and it made no sound at all. Not when the Provision pitched in the chop below Drowned Bellhaven. Not when Bramm's engine shook the whole hull like a dog shaking off water. Nothing. It was less like cargo than like a held breath.

And Jonah wouldn't look at it.

That was the other thing. Jonah Brightwater looked at everything — read water the way Odile read weather, read locks and ledgers and men's faces, missed nothing on her own boat except, apparently, the one thing she'd been paid a fortune to carry. She walked past it twice a day on her rounds and her eyes went over it the way a tongue goes over a missing tooth: around, never into.

So on the third night out from Bellhaven, while Jonah stood her watch and Bramm slept in his engine-room hammock and Odile dozed over her cards in the galley, Felix took the lantern down into the hold to have a look at what nobody wanted to see.

* * *

The hold of the Provision was a country Felix knew better than most countries. He'd learned its geography by feel before he'd learned it by light — the low beams that caught a tall man's crown, the bilge boards that sang hollow near the stern, the corner behind the fresh-water tank where he'd hidden during his first week aboard, nine years old and certain he was about to be put ashore again. He knew where the shadows pooled thickest and where the lantern's reach ended. He went down the ladder quiet, though there was no one to hear him, because some habits are just respect for a place.

The air below was cool and close, smelling of tar, old wood, river damp, and — faintly, underneath it all — something green. That was wrong too. The lower Vell smelled of mud and fish and engine oil. Green was an upstream smell. Green was the smell of water that hadn't been argued with yet.

He knelt by the crate and set the lantern on the deckboards.

Up close, the thing was worse. The oilcloth was good stuff, waxed heavy, but it had been cut and re-sewn rather than fitted — someone had built the wrapping around whatever was inside, the way you'd dress a body too large for its coffin. The iron bands were stamped with a mark Felix didn't know: a circle bisected by a wavy line, struck through with a second, deeper stamp so that the first mark had been deliberately ruined. Defaced. Someone had gone to trouble to unmake a symbol.

He put his palm flat against the oilcloth.

Cold. Not cold like metal or stone. Cold like the deep part of the swimming hole at Ashport in high summer, the cold that has weight to it, that pulls at your ankles. And beneath the cold — he pressed harder, laid his ear against the cloth, feeling foolish and doing it anyway — beneath the cold, something moved. Not a shifting of contents. Not settling. A rhythm. Slow, vast, patient, like the pulse of something sleeping at the bottom of a lake.

Felix sat back on his heels, heart going hard.

"Okay," he whispered, to the crate, to himself, to the dark. "Okay."

There were tools in the chock by the ladder — there always were; Bramm believed a tool should be within arm's reach of every point on the boat, and mostly he was right. Felix took the pry bar, weighed it, and felt suddenly like a burglar in his own home. But Jonah had lied to him about this. Or hidden it, which was a cousin of lying, and cousins ran in families. He set the bar's edge under the nearest band.

"You'll want to stop that."

Felix came off the deckboards so fast he cracked his head on a beam and stood there seeing sparks, pry bar up like a knife, lantern swinging wild shadows across the hold.

In the shadow between the crate and the hull, where no one had been a moment ago, sat an old man.

He was gaunt the way winter fields are gaunt, all edges and absence, with a beard gone grey-white and hair to match hanging past his shoulders. He wore a coat that might once have been fine and was now only old, patched at the elbows with cloth that didn't match, and his eyes — this was the thing Felix would remember longest — his eyes caught the lantern light and gave none of it back. They were dark the way the crate was silent. Completely. Deliberately.

"Don't shout," the man said. His voice was dry and quiet, like paper turning. "Your captain sleeps light, and I've no wish to explain myself to her yet. Nor you to her."

"There's—" Felix's voice cracked and he hated it. "You're a stowaway."

"I'm a passenger." The old man tilted his head. "The distinction matters to me. Stowaways hide from the crew. Passengers hide from the people hunting them. There's a difference in the hiding." He extended one hand, slow, showing it empty. "My name is Wren. And you must be the boy. Felix."

Every hair on Felix's arms stood up. "I didn't tell you my name."

"No. But I've seen your face before tonight, boy, and not on this boat." Wren's gaze went, deliberate and terrible, to the space above Felix's breast pocket — where, folded into eight creases and carried against his heart since Marrow Bend, the second poster sat. *Reward for information concerning keepers of the source.* "You carry their mark. You always did. It's in the shape of your face. Your grandmother had the same look. I knew her."

The pry bar sagged in Felix's grip. The hold seemed to tilt, though the river was flat calm. "You're lying."

"I'm old, not dishonest. There's rarely profit in dishonesty at my age; you haven't time left to spend it." Wren leaned forward, and the lantern light found the planes of his face, hollowed and hungry. "Listen to me now, because I've been waiting three days for the boat to be still and the crew to be asleep, and I don't know how many more nights I have. Look at the crate. Truly look."

"I looked."

"You listened. Now look." He nodded at the bands. "That mark — the circle and the wave? That is the sigil of the keepers of the Vell. Struck through, because Corvane's people defaced every one they could find. And the box itself—" he tapped his own chest, once, "—is not a box. It is a cage. Built to my order, forty years ago, to hold a thing that should never have needed holding. They stole my design and my cage both, and they've filled it with—"

"With what?" Felix heard his own voice go thin. "What's in it?"

Wren looked at him for a long moment, and in that moment Felix saw the old man decide something — saw it happen behind those lightless eyes like weather changing.

"The heart of the river," Wren said simply. "The source-stone. The thing that made the Vell the Vell, and not merely water running downhill. Nine years ago men took it from the spring where it had slept since before there were names for anything here. They were told they were freeing the valley. Some of them may even have believed it." His eyes rested on Felix, gentle and awful. "Since then: the floods that drowned Bellhaven. The fish dying belly-up from the bends to the delta. The water going strange and forgetful. You've noticed the water going strange, boy. Don't tell me you haven't. Keepers' blood notices."

"I'm not—" Felix started, and stopped, because the poster was in his pocket and his grandmother's face was in his memory and the word *keeper* had been sitting in him for days like a swallowed hook. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because Sable Corvane is not carrying this cage to the source to give the heart back." Wren rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and even stooped he seemed somehow taller than the hold allowed. "She's carrying it there to install it. To plant it like a flag. Whoever holds the heart holds claim to every mile of the Vell — its towns, its tolls, its locks, its dead. She means to own the river, Felix. All of it. Forever. And she needs this boat because your captain is the only fool alive who'll run the upper water, and she needs—" he paused, and something almost tender crossed the ruined landscape of his face, "—she needs you. Keeper's blood opens what gold cannot. The last lock at the source answers to your family's line and no other. She's known whose boy you were since before she ever offered your captain triple pay."

The lantern flame guttered, though there was no draft.

Felix thought of Sable Corvane on the dock at nightfall, all silk and patience, looking at Jonah. He thought of the agents boarding in the dark, quick and professional, taking nothing but paper and leaving a message. He thought: *we were never crew to her. We were tools in a kit.* And underneath that, colder: *she chose me before she chose the boat.*

"They're hunting you too," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen it in the way the old man flinched, three nights back, at footsteps overhead that weren't threatening anything.

"At Ashport they nearly had me. Two of Corvane's men, working the crowd with my likeness on paper." Wren smiled without warmth. "I hid in a grain shipment and rode one lock downstream, then swam for your boat at night. Old trick. The river helped me — it does that, still, a little, when it remembers how. Which is why I know it can be helped back." He laid his hand on the crate, flat, the same gesture Felix had made, and the cold coming off it seemed to bend toward him like a plant toward light. "But I am the last keeper, boy, and I am old, and my hands shake. Blood alone won't finish this. It will take all of us — your captain's recklessness, the engineer's hands, the cook's nerve, and yours. Especially yours."

Above them, boots crossed the deck. Jonah's stride — Felix knew it anywhere, the slight drag on the left foot from an old rope-burn scar. Passing over the hatch. Pausing.

Coming back.

Wren melted backward into the shadow between crate and hull, and it was like watching ink return to a bottle. "Not yet," came the whisper, barely breath. "When you're ready. When she's ready. But soon, boy — the river runs faster now, and so does she."

The hatch opened and lamplight spilled down the ladder, and Jonah's voice came with it, rough with sleeplessness: "Felix? What in the hell are you doing down here at this hour?"

He turned, pry bar still in his hand, lantern at his feet, standing between his captain and the shadow where the last keeper of the Vell waited — and understood, all at once, the true shape of the voyage they were making. Not a cargo run. Not a ransom. A funeral procession going the wrong direction, hauling a stolen heart home in a cage, with hunters ahead and behind and a stowaway prophet in the dark.

"Just checking the lashings," Felix said.

Jonah's eyes went to the crate — around it, never into it — and then to him, and stayed there a beat too long, reading his face the way she read water.

"Felix," she said quietly. "What did you find?"
