## 9. The Oathbreakers' Reach

The narrows came at dawn, the way bad news always did — quietly first, then all at once.

Jonah had been awake since the third bell, standing at the wheel with her hands bare against the cold wood, watching the Vell gather itself between two walls of granite. The river here was older than anything else in the valley. It had cut this gorge before there were towns to drown or captains to swear oaths, and it moved through the stone with the patience of something that knew it would outlast them all. The water ran green-black and deep, and the cliffs on either side rose so sheer that the morning sun wouldn't touch the surface for another two hours. Mist hung on the current like breath on a mirror.

Behind her, the Provision's engine turned over slow and steady, Bramm keeping her down to a crawl. He'd been running her quiet since Drowned Bellhaven, since he'd found those gouges in the hull that no rock had made. Nobody had said much since. Odile still cooked, because Odile always cooked, but the galley smelled of nothing ambitious — just coffee and fried bread, the food of people who weren't sure they'd live long enough to taste anything better.

Felix came up the companionway with two mugs, handed her one without asking, and leaned on the rail beside her. Nine years old when she'd found him stealing bread off her deck; seventeen now, and taller than her by half a head, though he still stood beside her like he was small.

"Water's wrong," he said.

Jonah had already seen it. "I know."

Downstream of them — behind them, now — the mist was moving against the current. Not drifting. Moving. A long gray smear coming up the gorge at speed, and beneath it, low and dark, the blunt silhouette of a boat built for one purpose.

"Gunboat," Felix said. "Corvane's?"

"Who else buys gunboats." Jonah drank her coffee. It was terrible. Odile had started making it weak to stretch the tin, and Jonah loved her for it and hated it all the same. "How far to the rapids?"

Felix didn't answer right away, which meant he'd already looked at the chart and already wished he hadn't. "Four miles. Maybe five."

"And after the rapids?"

"Open water to Ashport. If we make the rapids."

*If.* The boy had learned to say *if* like a man. That was hers too, Jonah supposed. Everything hard in him was something she'd taught, on purpose or not.

She set down the mug. "Wake Bramm properly. Tell him I'll want everything the engine has in three minutes, and tell him not to ask why."

"He'll ask why."

"Then tell him fast."

* * *

The gunboat gained on them the way certainty gains — unhurried, unbothered, sure of its arithmetic. Jonah could read it in the way the wake spread behind the thing: a wide, flat bow-wake, a hull designed to pin rather than chase. Sable Corvane hadn't bought a fast boat. She'd bought a door. Somewhere ahead, in the next mile or the mile after, the gorge would narrow again, or shallow, or bend, and the gunboat would simply be there, waiting, and every mile of river behind them would become a corridor with walls.

Unless the corridor ended somewhere the door couldn't follow.

"The Hallowing," Bramm said, when Jonah told him. He said it the way other men said the names of dead friends. "You're talking about the Hallowing Reach."

"I'm talking about living through the morning."

"Nobody runs the Hallowing. Not in forty years. Not in a steamboat. Not—" He stopped, wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth, and looked at her with the particular exhaustion of an engineer who has spent nine years learning exactly what his captain is capable of. "Not even you."

"Not even me," Jonah agreed. "That's the point. She'll have charts. She'll have pilots who've run every legal stretch of this river twice over. What she won't have is anyone stupid enough to try the Reach, because everyone smart enough to matter already knows better." She tapped the wheel spoke with one knuckle. "Being known as a fool is worth more than gold today."

Bramm looked past her, down the gorge, where the mist was peeling back from the water and the gunboat's running lights showed red through the last of the fog. Then he looked aft, toward the galley, where Odile was humming something old and tuneless over her pans.

"You tell her," he said. "Not me."

* * *

Odile took it better than Bramm, which was its own kind of wound. She stood in the galley doorway with flour to her elbows and listened to Jonah lay it out — the narrows, the gunboat, the Reach, the odds — and when Jonah finished, Odile only nodded once, the way she nodded when Jonah asked for double portions on a hard day.

"You want me below decks," Odile said. "Lashed in. Where the boat can hold me."

"If she rolls, I don't want you going over the rail."

"Captain." Odile smiled, and there was flour in the creases of her face and something older than flour in her eyes. "I have been thrown out of kitchens in four river towns and one monastery. I do not go over rails." She went back to her pans. The humming resumed. Jonah stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to, memorizing the sound, and then went forward to find Felix already at the bow line, coiling rope that didn't need coiling.

"You heard," she said.

"I heard."

"It's not too late to put you ashore. There's a fisherman's stair a mile up. You could walk to Ashport, wait for us—"

"On the day Sable Corvane learns my name?" Felix's jaw was set, and Jonah saw the keeper's bones in his face for the first time — not softness, but structure, the kind of face that had been built to stand in front of something. "No. Whatever's coming, I'm on this deck for it."

Jonah wanted to argue. She was good at arguing; it was most of how she'd survived. But the river made a sound then, far ahead — a long, low mutter under the gorge walls, like something turning over in its sleep — and both of them looked upstream together, and neither of them argued about anything after that.

* * *

The Hallowing Reach announced itself a half-mile early, the way all honest dangers do. First the sound: not white-water roar yet, but a rising hum, a pressure in the ears, the whole gorge beginning to resonate like a struck bell. Then the light changed, the cliffs falling away into a bowl of open sky, and then the water itself — visible now in long silver ridges, folding over ledges Jonah could not see the bottoms of.

The chart called it a quarter-mile of descent. Charts lie by omission. There were no soundings for the Reach because no surveyor had ever lived long enough to take them. What the old pilots' lore said instead was simpler: *the Hallowing drops thirty feet in three hundred yards, and it does not care how you feel about it.*

Jonah brought the Provision's head around and gave Bramm the throttle.

The engine answered like a living thing — a full-throated bellow, the paddlewheel biting deep, the whole hull shuddering as the old boat surged toward the drop. Behind them the gunboat's whistle sounded, one long arrogant note, and Jonah understood it perfectly: *we see you, we know where you're going, we will be at the bottom when you arrive.* Sable Corvane had done the same arithmetic any sane pilot would do. No one ran the Reach. Therefore the Reach was a wall, and walls only needed guarding at the ends.

The arithmetic was correct. It was also wrong, because it assumed the Provision was captained by someone who valued her own life at market rates.

"Hold on!" Jonah shouted, to no one in particular, to everyone, to the river itself.

The bow lifted. The world tilted. And the Vell took them.

* * *

There was no piloting the Hallowing, not really. There was only choosing, moment to moment, which violence to accept. The first ledge hit them like a fist under the keel — a grinding, staggering blow that threw Odile's coffee pot the length of the galley and put every soul aboard on their knees — and then the river had them wholly, and Jonah stopped being a captain and became something smaller: a woman reading water faster than thought, calling corrections that Bramm executed on faith alone.

"Starboard! Hard starboard—"

The gorge wall rushed close enough to spit on. The wheel fought her like a panicked animal. Green water came over the foredeck in a single solid sheet and took Felix off his feet, and Jonah saw him go down sliding toward the rail with the river's whole weight behind him, and she screamed his name into the roar, and his hand — his hand found the forward bitt, closed around it, held. Seventeen years old and holding against the Vell itself.

Then the second drop, worse, a chute of churning foam between two black teeth of stone, and Jonah laid the boat into it with her heart in her throat because there was nowhere else to lay it, and the Provision went through sideways, half-broached, the paddlewheel lifting clear of the water and screaming free-spinning before it slammed home again—

—and amidships, something gave.

Jonah felt it through the wheel before she heard it: a change in the boat's voice, a note gone flat. And she heard Odile cry out — not a scream, nothing so simple — a short, hard sound of a body striking iron, and then Bramm's voice roaring up the speaking tube, wordless with horror.

The third ledge. The last one. Jonah drove the dying boat at it straight-on because crooked was death, and the Vell rose up and swallowed them whole.

* * *

They came out the other side.

That was the whole miracle of it, and Jonah never found better words for it, then or ever: *they came out the other side.* The Reach spat them into the long calm pool below, listing badly, taking water somewhere forward, the paddlewheel turning with a lurching stagger like a man walking on a ruined ankle — but floating. Moving. Alive.

The gunboat sat at the mouth of the pool, exactly where Sable's arithmetic had placed it, and Jonah watched its crew watch the Provision come down out of the Hallowing trailing spray and smoke and pieces of her own railing. She watched the certainty drain out of them at a distance. She watched the gunboat's wheel come over, watched it turn, watched it run — because whatever Sable Corvane paid her men, they hadn't signed on to chase a captain willing to feed herself to a river to stay free of them.

Felix got the pump working. Bramm got the engine settled to a wounded but honest rhythm. And Jonah went below, because nobody would tell her what had happened in the galley, and because some things a captain has to see with her own eyes.

* * *

Odile lay on the galley floor where Felix and Bramm had eased her down, one arm cradled against her chest at an angle arms don't go, blood on the deck plates thinned pink by river water that had come through the seams during the worst of it. Her eyes were open. Of course they were. Even now, especially now.

"The pot," she said, when Jonah knelt. "Did the pot survive."

"Damn the pot."

"Mm." Odile's breath came shallow and careful. "Broke it on the second drop. Knew I should have lashed it." She tried to smile and it cost her visibly. "Shoulder's out. Arm's broke above the elbow, I think. Heard it. Sounded like the mast in the storm at Carrow's Bend, that year."

"We'll splint it. Bramm knows setting—"

"Jonah." Odile's good hand caught her wrist, grip surprisingly strong. "Listen to your cook. We are four days from Ashport, if the boat holds, and I cannot cook with one arm, and you cannot run this river without eating, and that boy cannot grow without feeding. So you will splint me, and you will lash me upright in a chair, and tomorrow I will teach Felix the galley, and none of us will speak of this again." Her eyes glittered, fierce and wet. "We came down the Hallowing. Do you understand? Forty years, and we came down it alive. Let me have my triumph without your face doing *that* at me."

Jonah laughed, or something near it, and it hurt her chest in a place that had nothing to do with ribs. "Yes, ma'am."

"That's better." Odile let her wrist go. Above them, faint through the deck, the river murmured against the hull — patient, unhurried, the sound of something that had tested them and found them, for now, sufficient.

* * *

Evening found them anchored in a willow bend, ten miles past the Reach, past anything Sable's bought lockkeepers could shut against them. Bramm had the leak down to a manageable seep. Felix had burned the rice — his first attempt, under Odile's one-armed instruction, shouted up the companionway like a drill sergeant — and Odile declared the burnt rice *characterful* and ate two bowls of it to prove the kitchen's honor intact.

Later, Jonah stood at the rail while Felix pumped the bilge, and looked back downriver into the dark.

The Vell ran black and smooth behind them, and somewhere in that darkness was the Hallowing, and somewhere beyond it the gunboat, and somewhere beyond that all the locked and bought miles they'd left behind. They were committed now in a way that oaths and cargo and threats had only begun to suggest. There was no running the Reach twice. Whatever waited upstream — Ashport, the source, Sable Corvane, the truth about the sealed hold and the boy beside her — they would meet it going forward, or not at all.

The river saved us, she thought. Or it tested us, and we haven't seen the bill.

As if in answer, the current against the hull shifted — a slow, deliberate pull, gentle as a hand on a shoulder, drawing the anchor chain taut upstream. Toward the source.

Jonah watched the chain come tight and did not give the order to pay out more line.

Upstream, faint and far, a bell began to ring — one note, repeated, patient. From Ashport. Or from something older than Ashport.

Felix stopped pumping. "Captain," he said quietly. "Do you hear that?"

"I hear it."

Neither of them said what they were both thinking: that bells meant churches, and churches meant towns, and Ashport was still two days away — and that the ringing was getting louder.
