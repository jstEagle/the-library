## 10. What Jonah Buried

Felix heard the argument before he saw it — low voices through the bulkhead, the kind of talking that people think is private because they keep it quiet. He had learned young that quiet was where the true things lived. Loud things were for show. Quiet things were for keeping.

He stood in the companionway with a lantern in one hand and the second poster folded against his chest, the one Wren had asked him about that morning with eyes like a man reading his own death warrant. *Keepers of the source. Reward for information.* Felix's family name had been on it, small and faded, beneath a woodcut of a face he barely recognized as his father's.

The Provision lay moored in the lee of a drowned orchard, her lines humming in the night wind off the water. Through the gap in the bulkhead door he could see the galley: Odile's stove banked to embers, Bramm's oilskins dripping on their hook, and Jonah sitting at the table with her hands wrapped around a mug she wasn't drinking from. Wren sat across from her, gaunt as a winter branch, his stolen coat hanging off him like bark off a dead tree.

"You've been asking me questions since Ashport," Jonah said. "I've answered none of them."

"And I haven't pressed," Wren said. "But we're past the last lock now, Captain Brightwater. Past Bellhaven. Past every place a body could turn this boat around without Sable's guns finding her. There's no more *later* left on this river. Only what's ahead."

Felix should have gone back to the fo'c'sle. He knew that. But his feet wouldn't take him, and the lantern trembled slightly in his grip, and so he stayed, half-hidden in the dark of the passage, listening to the woman who had raised him prepare to confess something to a stranger that she had never confessed to him.

"Nine years ago," Wren said gently, "the fish began dying in the lower Vell. The floods came out of season. The water went sour in the wells two hundred miles from the source. And everyone looked at the keepers and said, *what have you done?* And the keepers looked at each other and said nothing, because they didn't know either." He paused. "Because it wasn't them. It was whoever took the stone out of the spring."

Jonah's mug clicked against the table. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"I know exactly what I'm talking about," Wren said. "I was there when it went missing. I carried the last message the keepers ever sent downriver, warning the valley towns that something had been taken. Nobody believed an old man and a boy with a message tube. They believed the floods instead."

Felix's heart was hammering. His father's face swam up in his memory — not the woodcut face, but the real one, tired and laughing, teaching him the names of reeds on the banks of some river he'd been too young to name. *Keeper's blood,* the poster had said. He hadn't understood until this moment how much those words were going to cost him.

"I helped steal it," Jonah said.

The words came out flat, like coins dropped on wood. Felix felt them land in his own chest.

Wren didn't move. "Say it again."

"I helped steal it." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she covered it by taking a drink she clearly didn't want. "There. Is that what you wanted? Nine years I've carried it, and you sit there looking at me like a priest, waiting for me to bleed on your schedule."

"I'm not a priest," Wren said. "I'm just the last man alive who remembers what was lost. So tell me how."

For a long time Jonah said nothing. The lamp above the table hissed. Somewhere forward, the hull ticked against its mooring lines, patient as a clock. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — smaller, younger, the voice of a woman Felix had never met.

"There was a man called Doss. He came down from the high country with maps nobody else had and money nobody could count, and he bought drinks in every tavern from Greywater Landing to the falls, and everywhere he bought drinks he told the same story. That the keepers weren't stewards. That they were jailers. That the source-stone wasn't the river's heart at all — it was a lock, and the keepers held the only key, and every toll, every flood-season sacrifice, every drought-year ration the valley ever suffered was because a handful of families in stone houses upstream decided the river belonged to them."

"That was a lie," Wren said quietly.

"It was a beautiful lie." Jonah laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "You want to know how good a lie can be? I was twenty-three years old and I'd spent my whole life watching my father pay the keepers' tolls with coin we didn't have. Doss stood up in the Salt & Anchor with a map of the upper Vell spread across three tables and told a room full of drowning men that the water was theirs, that it had always been theirs, that a few days' hard rowing would set it free forever. And every hand in that room went up. Including mine."

* * *

Felix eased himself down onto the companionway step, the lantern dark now between his knees. He didn't dare move. This was the story under every other story — the one that explained why Jonah never slept whole nights, why she ran cargo on the dangerous runs, why she'd gone white as bone when the dockmaster mentioned traffic on the upper river. Why she'd thrown Sable Corvane off her boat and then, hours later, agreed to carry her sealed cargo anyway.

"Doss needed boats," Jonah was saying. "Boats that could run the shallows above Bellhaven, crews that wouldn't ask questions, captains with grudges. He found four of us. I was the youngest and the angriest and the best with the poling irons, and I hated the keepers the way you hate a landlord — personally, specifically, down to the name. My father died the year before with the toll-collector's receipt still pinned to his shirt, like the river itself had sent him a bill for drowning."

"The stone was guarded," Wren said. Not accusing. Just setting the stones of the path for her to walk.

"By six old men and a boy." Jonah's voice caught. "Six old men and a boy, Wren. We told ourselves they'd be soldiers. We told ourselves anything rather than look at it straight. I went in first, through the spring channel, because I was the smallest and the fastest and because Doss had promised me double share if I drew the guards off. And I did draw them off. I fired the boathouse. And while the whole amphitheater was lit orange and everybody was running and shouting, Doss's men went into the spring itself and took the heart out of the water where it had sat for — how long? How long had it sat there?"

"A thousand years," Wren said. "Maybe more. Longer than names."

"A thousand years." She repeated it like she was weighing it. "And we had it out in a canvas sling inside of twenty minutes. The old keeper who tried to stop us — he didn't fight like a soldier. He fought like a man trying to hold a door shut against a flood. And when we broke past him, he wasn't shouting curses. He was saying, *you don't understand what you're carrying, you don't understand what you're carrying*, over and over, all the way down the bank, until one of Doss's men put him in the water to shut him up." Jonah's hands had gone white around the mug. "I heard him go under. I was already in the boat. I heard it, and I rowed."

Felix closed his eyes. The boy in the boathouse. Six old men and a boy. His grandfather had been a keeper; his father had been born on the source-water. Somewhere in that burning light and that cold spring, his family and hers had passed each other in the dark, nine years ago, and neither of them had known they'd spend the rest of their lives tied together by it.

"What happened to the stone?" Wren asked.

"Doss took it north. Said he'd negotiate with the valley towns — sell the rain back to them, essentially, season by season, the way the keepers supposedly had. Except the floods started before he ever got a buyer. The river went mad inside a month. Fish floating belly-up from the falls to the delta. Wells going bitter. The spring-fed mills choking on silt that had never come down before. And Doss—" Jonah stopped. Started again. "Doss drowned. Officially. Crossing a ford with the stone in a lead-lined chest, and the ford was knee-deep, and it took him anyway. Took him and the chest and the horse and never gave any of it back."

"And you?"

"I ran." Simple as that. Terrible as that. "I took my share, such as it was, and I ran downriver and kept running. Bought the Provision with blood money and told myself I'd earn it honest. Told myself the floods weren't mine. Told myself the keepers had it coming, whatever came for them." She looked up, finally, and her eyes found the dark companionway where Felix sat — or seemed to; he couldn't tell in the black whether she saw him. "And then the posters started appearing. *Keepers of the source.* As if the river were calling its own home. As if it knew their faces."

Wren leaned forward. "Captain. Do you know what the stone is?"

"I know what Doss said it was. A lock. A key. A leash."

"Doss was a thief who dressed his theft in politics," Wren said. "The stone is not a lock on the river. The stone is the river's *remembering*. Every drop that falls on the high country passes through the spring, and the spring holds what the water has given and what it has been given. Floods, harvests, drownings, mercies — all of it, held in one pale stone at the bottom of one cold pool. Take it out, and the river doesn't die. Rivers don't die. It *forgets how to be itself*. It thrashes. It grieves. It takes and takes and cannot stop taking, because nothing tells it when enough is enough." He spread his ruined hands. "That is what you rowed away from, Captain Brightwater. Not a jailbreak. A grief."

The silence that followed was the longest Felix had ever heard aboard the Provision. Even the hull seemed to stop ticking.

"My father used to say the river remembers," Felix said.

His voice came out of the dark before he could stop it, and every head in the galley turned. He stood, feeling caught and past caring, and stepped into the lamplight with the folded poster still in his fist. Jonah stared at him like a woman seeing a ghost wear her own guilt.

"Felix—"

"You said six old men and a boy." His voice shook and he let it. "My grandfather was a keeper. My father grew up on the source-water. There's a poster in my pocket with my family's name on it and a reward underneath, and Sable Corvane's agents took our manifest and threatened *me* to make you haul this cargo, and I have been walking around this boat for two days feeling like a stranger, and now I find out that the woman who fed me and taught me knots and never once asked about my family—" his voice broke, and he hated it, and he kept going, "—that woman burned my family's house down."

"I didn't know," Jonah said. "Felix, I swear on the Provision herself, I did not know who you were when I took you in."

"Would it have mattered?"

"Yes." She stood up too, and the table rocked between them. "No. I don't know. Nine years ago I'd have said no. Tonight I don't know what I'd have done, and that frightens me more than Sable Corvane and all her guns." She dragged a hand through her hair. "You want a confession? Here's the rest of it. When I pulled you out of that flood in Greywater Landing, half-drowned, ten years old, clutching a piece of driftwood with a keeper's mark burned into it — I *saw* the mark. I knew what it meant. And I took you aboard anyway, because the river had taken enough from both of us, and because—" her voice failed, steadied, "—because I thought maybe if I kept one thing safe, one thing, after everything I'd broken, the ledger might balance."

"So I'm a payment," Felix said. "A tithe. You've been doing penance with me for nine years."

"No." It came out of her like a slap. "No, boy, you listen to me. You are the only unbroken thing I ever—"

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river made a sound against the hull that Felix had never heard it make — long, and deep, and deliberate, like a great animal turning over in sleep. The mooring lines groaned. Down in the hold, muffled through two decks of timber, something shifted — the sealed cargo, the cage, whatever slept in Sable's crates — shifting as if it had heard its own name spoken.

Wren rose slowly, and his eyes were not on Jonah or Felix but on the deck beneath their feet.

"She knows," the old keeper whispered. "All this way, and the water under us knows whose boat this is, and whose blood, and what was buried here tonight." He looked up at Jonah, and his face was terrible with pity. "Captain, the river isn't pushing you upstream anymore. It's pulling. And it hasn't yet decided what it wants when it gets you there."

* * *

They stood watch together after that, though no one suggested it aloud. Felix at the rail, Jonah beside him, the drowned orchard black against the stars and the current sliding past below like a held breath.

"I'm not sorry I told you," Jonah said at last. "I should have told you years ago. I was a coward with a boat."

"I'm not ready to forgive you," Felix said.

"I know."

"But I'm not getting off this river either."

Jonah nodded slowly, and for the first time since Felix had known her, she looked her age — every mile of the Vell carved into her face, every drowned town, every dead fish, every year of running. "Then we'd better see what's in those crates," she said. "Because come morning, Sable's gunboat will be behind us, the source will be ahead of us, and whatever's sleeping in my hold has been listening to every word we've said."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, the cargo shifted again — and this time, Felix would have sworn on his father's grave that it knocked.
