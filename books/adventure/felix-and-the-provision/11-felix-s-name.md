## 11. Felix's Name

The river had gone quiet in the way Jonah hated most — not calm, but listening. For two days since the narrows, since Odile's bandaged shoulder and Bramm's white-knuckled silence at the wheel, the Vell had run smooth as poured glass, and every mile of that smoothness felt like a held breath. Jonah stood at the stern rail past midnight, watching the wake unspool behind them, and turned the folded paper over in her hands for the hundredth time.

Wren had given it to her that evening, pressing it into her palm with fingers like dry reeds. "You should hear it from yourself," he'd said, "before you hear it from her." Then he'd shuffled back into the shadow of the hold, where he spent his hours now, gaunt and patient as a heron, saying things Jonah didn't want to be true.

She'd known since Ashport. She'd known since she saw Felix walk into Sable's camp wearing that face — that open, guileless, keeper's face — and come back out again with intelligence that had saved them all. She'd told herself then what she'd been telling herself for nine years: that some truths could wait until the water was safe. That there would be time.

But Wren had looked at her with those pale eyes and said, *Sable didn't take the boy because he was yours. She took him because of whose he was.* And the paper in Jonah's hand had stopped being something she could sit on.

She unfolded it again. The lamplight from the deckhouse caught the ink, faded but legible, the paper soft as cloth from years of handling. A reward notice, older than Felix himself by a decade or more. And beneath the blocky printed text, a woodcut portrait: a woman with high cheekbones and a stubborn jaw, holding an infant wrapped against her chest.

*SEEKING SURVIVORS OF THE SOURCE LINE,* the poster read. *KEEPERS OF THE VELL. INFORMATION LEADING TO THE RECOVERY OF—*

Jonah didn't need to read the rest. She knew the rest. She'd helped write the rest, nine years ago, when she'd believed she was tearing down a tyranny instead of pulling the keystone out of a living thing.

The infant in the woodcut had a smudge of dark hair and a fist curled under its chin. Jonah had seen that same fist, curled the same way, a thousand times since — around a rope, around a mug of Odile's chicory, around the rail when the water ran rough. She'd never once wondered why the sight of it had made her stop on a dock in Lowmarsh six years ago, why she'd heard herself say *you can work for your passage* to a scrawny orphan who couldn't have weighed more than a sack of flour.

She'd told herself it was charity. It had never once felt like charity. It had felt like recognition.

* * *

Felix was awake. Of course he was awake. The boy slept like a cat aboard a moving boat — lightly, in snatches, one ear always on the engine's rhythm. When Jonah knocked on the frame of his bunk nook below decks, she heard the rustle of him sitting up before her knuckles finished falling.

"Trouble?" His voice was sleep-roughened, but his eyes were already sharp. He'd learned that from her too, God help him.

"No trouble." Her mouth was dry. "Can't a captain check on her crew?"

"At midnight? With a face like that?" He swung his legs out of the bunk, bare feet finding the cold deck planks, and studied her the way he did the water ahead of a shoal — patiently, thoroughly, looking for what wasn't visible yet. "What is it, Captain?"

*Captain.* Six years of that word, worn smooth as river stone. She'd let him call her that because it kept other words at bay. Not *mother*, never that, though Odile teased them both about it. Not *family*. Just the clean hierarchy of a working boat, where everything had its place and nothing had to be named.

"I found something," Jonah said. "In Ashport. Before you went into the camp." She held out the folded paper. "I should have shown you then."

He took it the way he took everything — carefully, with those long clever fingers, turning it toward the little brass lamp beside his bunk. Jonah watched his face as he read, watched the puzzlement give way to stillness, watched the stillness crack.

"That's—" His thumb moved over the woodcut, over the woman's face, over the bundle in her arms. "That's my mother."

The words came out flat, stripped of inflection, the way he spoke when the current was too strong to fight. "This is my mother. Where did you get this?"

"Ashport. Tacked up in the ferry office, under newer notices. Somebody had pasted bills over it for years — toll schedules, grain prices. It was underneath all of them." Jonah's voice sounded far away to her own ears, like someone else confessing. "It's a reward notice, Felix. For keepers of the source. Survivors of the source line. It's been circulating since before you were born."

Felix looked down at the paper. His jaw worked. "Keepers," he said. "Like Wren."

"Like Wren."

"And this infant." His finger hovered over the woodcut without touching it. "This is—"

"You," Jonah said. "It's you."

The lamp flame guttered in a draft from the engine room, and in its stutter the boy's face seemed to change — not older, exactly, but deeper, as if a layer Jonah had always taken for surface had suddenly revealed itself as depth. He sat back down on the bunk edge, slowly, the way a man sits when the deck has tilted under him.

"My mother was a keeper," he said. "My father too?"

"I don't know. Wren might. Wren says the Brightwater line ended with me, but the source line — the true keepers — they scattered when the stone was stolen. Some were killed. Some just... vanished." Jonah heard herself reciting it like a manifest, itemized and bloodless, and hated the sound. "Sable Corvane has been looking for survivors for years. Long before she ever came to my deck with that contract."

"Why?" Felix asked, and then, before she could answer, answered himself, his voice rising: "Because whoever holds the heart needs someone who can carry it back. Needs a keeper's hand. That's what Wren said — the stone returns to the source only through a keeper. So she's been hunting us. Hunting—" He stopped. Looked at the paper. Looked at Jonah.

And Jonah watched him arrive at it, watch the arithmetic complete itself behind his eyes, and knew there was no mercy left in the world that could spare either of them the sum.

"She didn't take me because I was your deckhand," Felix said quietly. "She took me because of what I am."

"Felix—"

"Six years." He was standing now, the poster crushed slightly in his fist, though Jonah didn't think he knew it. "Six years on this boat. You fished me off a dock in Lowmarsh and never once asked — never once — why a kid alone on the docks doesn't ask questions. Why I already knew knots. Why I could read current like I was born to it." His laugh was terrible, small, and cracked in the middle. "Born to it. I was *born* to it. And you knew. You must have suspected. You looked at me that first day like you'd seen a ghost, Odile told me, she said you went white as sailcloth and then you fed me stew and put me to work and never said another word about it for six years."

"I didn't know," Jonah said. "Not for certain. I suspected—"

"You *hoped* I wasn't. Because if I was, then you'd have to think about what you did. About the floods and the fish and the drowned town we sailed through last week, the one with the bell tower under the water." He wasn't shouting. That was the worst of it. He was speaking with the precise, controlled clarity of someone handling something fragile and furious. "Every mile upstream, I kept thinking — why does she look at me like that sometimes? Like I'm a debt she's paying? And now I know. I'm not your crew, Captain. I'm your penance."

The word landed like a gaff hook. Jonah felt it go in under the ribs and set.

"That's not fair," she managed.

"No," Felix agreed. "It isn't." He held up the poster. "Is this fair? Some man in Ashport tacking up my mother's face for thirty years, waiting for her child to grow into a bounty? Is Sable buying every lock on the river fair? Is Odile's shoulder fair? You want to talk about fair, Captain, you can start with why a woman who's spent nine years running from the upper river took a contract to haul a sealed cage to the source the week after her deckhand turned out to be the last keeper alive."

"Because they had *you*," Jonah said, and her voice finally broke its banks, rough and loud in the little nook. "Because they had you, and I would have hauled Satan's own luggage to hell's front door to get you back. Don't you dare stand there and tell me you're penance. You are the one thing on this river I have never once thought about running from."

Felix stared at her. In the lamplight his eyes were wet, and he blinked hard, furious at it, thirteen years old and six years seasoned and still, underneath everything, a boy standing in a room with the only person he had.

"Then why didn't you tell me?" he whispered. "Before tonight. Before Wren made you. If I'm — if I'm the one thing — why did I have to learn my own name from a piece of paper?"

And there it was. The question under all the others, the one Jonah had been dodging since Lowmarsh, since the first night she'd given a starving kid soup and a bunk and a lie of omission so comfortable she'd stopped noticing it was a lie.

"Because I was afraid," Jonah said. It cost her more than the rapids had. More than the oath. "Because the day I say it out loud, it stops being something that happened to the river and becomes something I did. To you. To your mother, maybe, wherever she is. Every year I didn't tell you was a year I got to keep being just your captain instead of the woman who broke the world you came from."

Felix was quiet a long moment. The engine thrummed beneath them, steady, indifferent, carrying them all upstream toward judgment.

"My mother used to sing," he said at last. "I don't remember much else. But I remember that. River songs. Old ones — Odile says they're keeper songs, she heard them growing up near Bellhaven before the flood. I thought everyone's mother sang them." He smoothed the crumpled poster against his knee, once, twice, a gesture so like tenderness that Jonah's throat closed. "You let me forget her, Captain. Six years, and I never even tried to remember, because this boat was enough. Because you were enough. And now I find out you knew what I was before I did, and you decided for me what I got to keep."

"Felix—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, not cruel, just final, a lock-gate closing. "I need — I can't right now. I need to not be in this room with you."

Jonah stood there a moment longer, in the doorway of the nook she'd built for him with her own hands the first winter, and understood that she had just lost something she hadn't known she was holding until it slipped. She nodded once, captain to crew, because it was the only language left between them that hadn't broken, and climbed back up into the night.

* * *

On deck, the river lay black and silver under a half moon, and the air smelled of coming rain. Bramm had the wheel, humming his tuneless hymn; Odile was asleep with her bad shoulder propped on pillows; Wren sat wrapped in sacking by the cargo hatch, watching Jonah come toward him with the expression of a man who has planted a field and is waiting to see whether anything grows.

"Well?" the old keeper asked.

"He hates me," Jonah said. She leaned on the rail beside him. Below, faintly, she could hear water moving against the hull, and beneath the water, if she let herself believe it, something else — a pulse, slow and vast, like a heart beating in the deep channel. They were close now. Two days, maybe three, to the source. "He has every right."

"Wren hates no one," the old man said mildly. "And neither, I think, does the boy. Grief wears hate's clothes, Captain, but it's grief underneath. You handed him a dead mother and a stolen name in the same breath. Let him curse you awhile. Cursing is how keepers pray."

Jonah almost smiled. Almost. Then her eye caught movement aft — a lantern signal, far downstream, winking green-white-green through the dark. Then another, farther left. Then a third.

Bramm saw it too. His humming stopped. "Captain," he said quietly. "Those aren't fishing boats."

Jonah counted the lights. Four. Five. Spread across the river in a line, closing slow, patient as a net — and at their center, larger than the rest, a familiar silhouette with a familiar rake to its stacks. She knew that boat. She'd thrown its owner off her deck a lifetime ago, in another life, in the low warm water of the world they'd both left behind.

"Sable's found us," Wren said, and there was no fear in it, only a kind of grim satisfaction, like a man watching weather he'd predicted. "Of course she has. The heart's waking. She can feel it pulling, same as the river does. Everything that wants the source is being pulled toward it now." He looked up at Jonah, pale eyes catching the moonlight. "How fast can this old girl run?"

Jonah watched the lanterns close the distance, and inside her chest, beneath the hurt and the guilt and the exhaustion, something older and harder rose up and bared its teeth. She'd lost the boy's trust tonight. She would not lose him.

"Bramm," she said, "wake Odile. Full steam. Whatever's left in her boilers, we spend it tonight."
