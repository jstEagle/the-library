## 12. The Corvane Ledger

The rain had followed them upriver for two days, a thin gray insistence that beaded on the Provision's deck and ran in threads down the cabin windows, and Felix had learned to read the boat's moods the way other boys read weather. Tonight she was restless. The hull spoke in low groans against the current, and Bramm's engine turned over with a cough he kept promising to fix, and somewhere below, in the hold where the sealed cargo sat lashed like a sleeping animal, Wren was asking for paper.

Not food. Not water. Paper.

"He says if we want to know what Corvane's buying," Odile had reported, wiping her hands on her apron with the air of a woman delivering a verdict, "he wants something to write it down on. And a lamp that doesn't flicker. He says the numbers won't hold still otherwise."

"The numbers," Jonah had repeated flatly.

"His words." Odile shrugged. "Old man talks like a ledger got religion."

So now Felix sat cross-legged on the cold boards of the hold, a storm lantern burning steady between them, watching Wren work. The old man had spread Sable's stolen manifest across a crate — pages Felix had lifted from her agents' satchel back at the drowned town, pages Jonah had nearly thrown overboard until Wren saw them and went still as a heron. Now Wren bent over them with a stub of pencil and the terrible patience of a man who had waited nine years to do arithmetic.

Felix had never seen anyone read like that. Not reading words — reading *structure*. Wren's finger traveled the columns of figures, the shipping codes, the toll schedules printed in fine gray ink along the margins, and his lips moved without sound, and every so often he would stop, close his eyes, and press two fingers against his eyelids as though pressing the numbers deeper into his skull.

"You're counting," Felix said.

"I'm *auditing*." Wren didn't look up. "There's a difference. Counting is what a merchant does with his own money. Auditing is what you do with someone else's sins."

He turned a page. The lantern light caught the scarred topography of his face — the deep lines, the eyes gone pale as river ice, hands that trembled unless they were holding something. Holding things steadied him. Felix had noticed that the first night: give Wren a rope, a cup, a page, and the shaking stopped. It was as if the old man needed proof, constantly, that the world could be gripped.

"What kind of sins?" Felix asked.

Wren finally looked up. In the lantern light his pale eyes had an odd luminance, and for a moment Felix felt the way he always felt when Wren looked directly at him — seen through, all the way down, to some layer of himself he hadn't met yet.

"Sit properly, boy. This will take telling."

* * *

Wren laid the pages out in a fan across the crate, weighted at the corners with bolts from Bramm's tin. "Corvane's people are careful. They use cipher on anything that matters — but ciphers are for cowards, and cowards repeat themselves. Every code she uses has to pass through ordinary hands eventually. Dockmasters. Toll clerks. Lockkeepers." His mouth twisted around the word. "She bought those too, I expect. But bought men are lazy men, and lazy men file their papers twice."

"So you've read her mail."

"I've read her *spine*." Wren tapped the manifest's central column. "Look here. These aren't cargo weights. Cargo weights don't need to balance against toll assessments for six towns. These are valuations. She hasn't been pricing what the cage costs to move. She's been pricing what the Vell is worth."

Felix leaned over the pages. He was quick with figures — Jonah had drilled him on manifests since he was ten, said a deckhand who couldn't audit a load would end up hauling someone else's trouble — and even he could see the shape of it now that Wren pointed. Columns of towns. Bellhaven. Ashport. The lock villages. Each with a number beside it, each number enormous, each number annotated in Sable's tight, slanted hand.

"They're not prices," Felix said slowly. "They're... incomes. What each town pays in tolls, fees, dock rights—"

"—and what each town *would* pay, once the rates were set by someone with the right to set them." Wren's voice dropped into something dry and terrible. "Your captain thinks Corvane is a thief. Thieves take things. Corvane is not taking the river, boy. She is *foreclosing* on it."

The word landed in Felix's chest like a dropped anchor.

"Explain."

Wren gathered the pages, squared them, and held one up between two fingers as though it were a dead fish he'd pulled from bad water. "When your keepers — when *my* order — held the source-stone, everyone thought we were wizards. Priests. River-witches. Even the keepers mostly believed it. We fasted, we prayed, we touched the stone and the floods obeyed, and the valley called it power." He set the page down. "It was never power. Power is what you have over a thing. What the stone grants is older and uglier than power. It grants *claim*. Whoever holds the heart of the Vell holds title to the Vell. Every mile of water. Every bank it touches. Every millrace, every ferry crossing, every fish taken from its current. Not stewardship — ownership. The old keepers understood this, and it frightened us more than any flood ever did, which is why we swore never to use the claim. Why we kept the stone hidden and let the valley believe in magic instead of deeds."

Felix thought of the poster folded in his shirt, the one he'd taken from the river town — REWARD FOR PERSONS KNOWN AS KEEPERS OF THE SOURCE — and the word *persons*, so legal, so bland, sitting there like a trap already sprung. "And Sable knows this how?"

"Because nine years ago, when your captain helped steal the stone out from under us, the theft left records. Deeds leave records. That's the nature of claim — it wants to be written down. Somewhere in the transfer, in whatever bargain the thieves struck with the men who moved the stone, the truth of what the stone *is* leaked. Corvane found the leak and followed it like blood in water." Wren's hands had begun to shake again; he gripped the edge of the crate until his knuckles whitened. "She doesn't want to free the river from the keepers. She doesn't even particularly want the floods to stop, though stopping them makes good theater. She wants to stand at the source with the heart in her hands and own every soul who ever drank from the Vell."

* * *

Felix carried the weight of it up onto the deck, because the hold had grown too small to breathe in, and found the rain had softened to mist and Jonah was standing at the rail doing the thing she did when she thought no one was watching — holding herself very still, staring upstream, jaw set like a woman arm-wrestling the horizon.

"We need to talk," Felix said.

"That's my line."

"Wren cracked the manifest."

That got her. She turned, and in the lamplight spilling from the cabin her face did the complicated thing it always did when the cargo came up — guilt and dread and stubbornness braided together so tightly Felix couldn't tell which strand was holding the others up.

"Tell me."

So he told her. He told it badly at first, because the shape of it kept sliding — *claim, not power, title, not magic* — and Jonah interrupted twice, once to say "that's superstition," and once to say nothing at all, which was worse. But Wren had come up behind them, silent as driftwood, and it was Wren who finished it, standing at the rail with the mist beading in his white hair, laying it out in the voice of a man reading a sentence that had already been passed.

"Ask yourself, Captain, why a woman like Corvane needs a boat at all. She has gunboats. She has bought locks. She could hire forty men to carry a box upriver. Instead she takes your boat, your boy, your oath — why?" He let the question sit in the wet air. "Because the claim must be *installed*. The stone has to return to the source to take full title — that's the law under the law, the clause beneath the clause. And it must be returned by river, by boat, by hands that the water knows. Your hands, Captain Brightwater. You're not her hauler. You're her signature."

Jonah's knuckles went white on the rail. Felix watched her absorb it — watched her understand, in real time, that every mile they'd fought upstream hadn't been resistance to Sable's plan but *participation* in it, that the whole brutal journey was the pen stroke, and that the only way to stop signing was to stop moving, and the only way to stop moving was to abandon Felix to the people holding his name on a poster.

"There has to be another way to break the claim," Jonah said. It wasn't a question, but it wanted to be answered like one.

"There is exactly one," Wren said. "The stone goes back to the source freely, by a keeper's hand, and the claim dies unexercised. Or it goes back in Corvane's grip, and the valley belongs to her until the river itself ends." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Felix had ever seen. "I spent thirty years guarding a deed I prayed would never be read aloud. Now I'm racing the only woman greedy enough to read it."

Below them, the engine coughed. Above them, the mist thickened toward evening, and somewhere far upstream — Felix could almost swear he heard it, or imagined it, or the river put it in his ear the way it seemed to put everything in his ear lately — came the sound of water moving faster than water should, as if the Vell itself had heard the word *claim* and begun, quietly, to object.

Jonah pushed off the rail. "Wake Bramm. Double watches tonight." Her eyes found Felix's, and for one unguarded second she looked less like a captain than like a woman standing in a courthouse waiting for a verdict she'd earned. "Nobody signs anything."

But past her shoulder, downstream, a light was climbing the dark water toward them — running lamps, three of them, riding low and fast, in the unmistakable formation of a gunboat coming to collect.
