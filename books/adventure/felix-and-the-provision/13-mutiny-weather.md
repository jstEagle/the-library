## 13. Mutiny Weather

The storm came down out of the western hills like something with a grudge.

Jonah felt it before she saw it — a pressure in her back teeth, a wrongness in the way the water lay against the hull. The Vell had been running strange for two days now, ever since they'd passed the drowned rooftops of Bellhaven: too clear, too quiet, as if the river were holding its breath and watching them go by. Now the breath came out all at once.

"Cloud's over the ridge," Bramm said, appearing at her elbow on the pilothouse deck. He said it the way other men said *fire*. His hands were already moving, checking the lashings on the fuel drums, though his eyes never left the sky. "That's not weather, Captain. That's a wall."

It was. The clouds had stacked themselves above the ridgeline in gray-black shelves, and beneath them the air had gone the color of old tin. The wind that reached them first was cold and carried the smell of stone dust and lightning-not-yet-fallen.

"How far to shelter?" Jonah asked, though she was already reading the banks herself — the steep clay cut on the left, the gravel shoals spreading wide on the right, the narrow channel threading between.

"There's an oxbow maybe three miles up," Bramm said. "Old cutoff. Deep water, high banks. If we can make it."

"*If* is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"*If* always does." Bramm wiped his palms on his trousers. Nine years he'd stood beside her, and she could count on one hand the times she'd seen him frightened. This was one of them, and it wasn't only the storm. She knew that. So did he.

"Felix!" she called down. "Get Odile below and everything lashed that isn't already! Anything loose becomes a weapon when this hits!"

Felix moved along the deck with the quick sureness she'd taught him himself, and something in her chest pulled tight watching him — the boy who'd been taken once already by Sable Corvane's men, who'd walked into their camp wearing his keeper's face like a mask, who'd come back to her with intelligence bought at a price she didn't want to calculate. He didn't look afraid now. That scared her more than anything.

* * *

They made the oxbow with minutes to spare.

Getting in was the worst of it — the channel had narrowed since the charts were drawn, and the current, running backward against the storm wind, fought the wheel every foot of the way. Jonah took her personally, both hands on the spokes, feeling the Provision's rudder answer her through the steel like a pulse through a wrist. The old boat groaned but obeyed. She always did. That was the thing about the Provision that people who'd never worked a river couldn't understand: she wasn't equipment. She was a crew member, and the oldest one aboard.

Then the bow swung into the cutoff's still water, and Jonah ran her nose into the bank hard enough to bite, and Bramm dropped the spuds — the great iron pins — through the guard rails and into the mud, and they were tied to the earth itself just as the sky came apart.

The rain didn't fall so much as arrive sideways, in sheets, in tons. Thunder rolled down the valley like barrels going downstairs. Lightning walked the ridgeline on white legs. In the pilothouse, with the windows streaming, the world beyond the glass became a rumor.

Odile brought up coffee that was more chicory than coffee and less apology than usual, which meant she was rattled too. They drank it standing, four of them in the small warm room, listening to the storm try to tear the roof off and mostly failing.

It was Bramm who finally said it.

"We should talk about the cargo."

Jonah set her cup down. "Should we."

"Don't do that." His voice was steady, but his hands weren't; he folded them under his arms to hide it. "Don't captain me, Jonah. Not about this. I've hauled grain, timber, gunpowder once, and a circus menagerie, and I never asked what was in a crate in my life. But I've heard it. Down in the hold, at night. It hums."

Nobody spoke. Rain hammered the deck overhead like a crowd demanding entry.

"It hums," Bramm repeated, quieter. "And the river's been wrong since we took it aboard. You know it. The fish floating belly-up below Bellhaven. The water running clear as glass where it ought to be brown. That's not a storm coming, Captain. That's a *symptom*."

"Bramm—"

"So here's what I'm saying." He turned to face her fully, and Jonah saw that he'd rehearsed this, probably all night, probably against the sound of the rain. "We ground here. We wait out the blow. And then we open that cage — because it *is* a cage, Wren says so, and Wren would know — and we put the thing inside it on the bank, in the mud, wherever we are, and we walk away. We take the skiff downstream. We let Sable Corvane chase a box full of nothing. Let her buy her empty river. And we go home."

"Home," Jonah said.

"The Provision," Bramm said. "Our run. Our towns. Odile's kitchen. Felix's—" He stopped, hearing himself, and looked at the boy.

Felix sat on the pilot's bench, soaked to the skin still, saying nothing. Watching her.

"You'd have me break contract with Sable Corvane," Jonah said carefully, "with her guns on the river behind us and her name on every lock ahead of us. You'd have me strand us in forbidden water with no cargo, no leverage, and no boat worth stealing."

"I'd have you keep us *alive*."

"And I'm telling you it doesn't work that way." Jonah heard her own voice flatten into the register she used for dock negotiations and funerals. "You don't walk away from Sable Corvane. You don't hand her a reason. Right now the only reason she hasn't burned us on the water is that she needs us and the cargo in the same place. Split them, and we're worth nothing alive."

"Then we're worth nothing alive anyway!" Bramm's composure cracked clean through. "Jonah — nine years. Nine years I've followed you up and down this river and never once asked why we never go past Harrow Lock, never once asked what you buried up there that makes you flinch when dockmasters talk about the source. I earned the right to ask, and I'm asking now: what is in that hold? What did you *do*?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped anchor.

Wren answered it.

The old keeper had kept to the shadows of the lower deck since Ashport, moving like a man made of paper and secrets, but now he climbed the pilothouse stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, rain-wind pulling at his thin gray hair. He looked at Bramm with eyes that had seen too many years of the same river.

"She stole the heart of the Vell," Wren said simply. "Nine years ago. With my help, and against her better nature, and for reasons that seemed holy at the time. Everything dying in this valley — the floods, the fish, the towns gone under — began the day we carried it away in a crate smaller than a coffin." He gestured vaguely downward, toward the hold, toward the humming. "And now it wants to go home."

Bramm stared at him. Then at Jonah. Then at Felix, who didn't look away.

"That's not possible," Bramm said, without conviction.

"No," Jonah agreed quietly. "It isn't. And it's true."

* * *

The argument lasted through the worst of the storm and into its long ragged tail, and by the end of it they were all hoarse and none of them had moved from the pilothouse, as if leaving the room would mean surrendering the field.

Bramm wanted the cargo gone. Dump it, sink it, bury it in the oxbow mud — anywhere but upstream, anywhere but the source. Run south while Sable's gunboat was pinned somewhere by the same weather. Live.

Odile, to Jonah's surprise, wanted to go on — but not for honor. "That woman owns half the towns on my river already," she said, chopping the air with a paring knife she'd apparently brought to the argument as a visual aid. "You think she stops at tolls? You think bread and promises is where it ends? I fed men like her in another country, before yours. It ends with lists. It ends with names." Her knife pointed at Felix. "His family's names are already on one."

"I know what's at stake," Bramm snapped.

"Do you? Because it sounds to me like you want your river back and you're dressing it up as sense."

"And it sounds like you want a martyr's ending because you're too proud to admit we bit off more than any boat can chew!"

"Enough." Jonah's voice cracked across the room like the spud-iron hitting mud.

Silence, except the rain, gentler now, ticking on the roof instead of hammering.

She stood at the window a long moment, looking out at the oxbow — the black water, the thrashing willows, the far bank lost in murk. And she found, to her genuine astonishment, that she knew what she believed. For the first time in nine years, the oath rose in her clear and whole, not as fear dressed in ceremony but as simple truth: *the water remembers what it's given.* The heart belonged to the source. Carrying it there wasn't Sable's errand anymore — hadn't been since Wren told them what the cage held, since Felix learned whose blood ran in him. It was hers. It had always been hers. Every mile she'd run downstream for nine years had been this errand, deferred.

"You're right," she said.

Bramm blinked. "I—"

"About the cargo. About the river being wrong. About all of it." She turned to face her crew, and it cost her more than any rapids ever had to say the next part plainly. "Dumping it won't save us. Burying the heart in the mud won't quiet it — Wren, tell them."

Wren nodded slowly. "It would make it worse. It's been caged nine years. Abandon it in the dark and the river takes its grievance out on whatever's nearest. That would be this boat. This bend. Maybe this valley." He spread his thin hands. "There is only one place it can be put down, and it isn't here."

"So we go on," Jonah said. "Not for Sable. Not for triple pay. Because it's the only right thing left on the table, and I've spent nine years learning what wrong things cost." She looked at Bramm, and let her voice soften, because the next part needed softening. "But you're my crew, and I don't spend you without your say. So we vote. All of us. And whatever we decide, we decide together, and nobody carries it alone. That's the offer."

Bramm laughed — a short, bitter, exhausted sound. "A vote. On a riverboat. Saints preserve me." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, his eyes were red-rimmed and resolute. "Fine. Vote."

They voted.

Odile for going on. Wren for going on — "though my vote weighs nothing, I am cargo myself." Felix raised his hand without hesitation, and Jonah caught Bramm watching the boy with an expression she couldn't read and didn't have time to.

Two to two, counting herself. She'd voted for the source. Which meant—

"No," Bramm said.

Everyone looked at him.

"I voted to dump it," he said. "So it's two and two, and the captain breaks ties, and—" He stopped. Looked at Jonah. Looked at the rain. And Jonah watched, with a kind of grief she knew would stay with her, as nine years of loyalty argued with nine years of trust and neither quite won. "Captain breaks ties," he said again, flatly. "That's the rule. Your boat."

"It is," Jonah said. "And I'm telling you we go on."

"Then we go on." He picked up his oilskin off the hook, roughly, and shrugged into it. "But hear me, Jonah Brightwater, because I'll only say this once. I've steamed behind you through ice, fire, and one war scare, and I never once checked your course. Today I checked it. Today I looked ahead and I don't see a channel — I see a woman steering toward the thing that scares her most because she thinks she owes it. That's not navigation. That's penance." He jammed his cap on. "I'll keep the engines lit. God help us all, I'll keep them lit. But don't ask me to believe."

He went down the stairs into the dark, and the engine room door closed behind him with a sound like a verdict.

* * *

Jonah stood the middle watch alone.

The storm had spent itself by midnight, wandering off eastward to bully some other valley, and what remained was a fine cold drizzle and a moon fighting through torn cloud. The oxbow lay silver and still. Somewhere on the bank a night bird called, got no answer, called again.

She thought about Bramm's words until they wore grooves in her. *Penance.* Was that what this was? She'd asked herself a hundred ways since Bellhaven and never gotten an answer she trusted. She'd stolen the heart believing she was freeing the valley, and the valley had drowned instead, and now she was hauling it home with a tyrant's guns at her back and a boy's life as the price of her caution. If that wasn't penance, it was certainly its close cousin.

Behind her, the pilothouse door opened without a knock. Felix.

He didn't speak at first. Just came and stood at the window beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, the way he had since he was twelve years old and afraid of thunder. He wasn't afraid of thunder anymore. She wondered what he was afraid of now, and suspected the list had gotten shorter and worse.

"Bramm will come around," Felix said finally.

"He shouldn't have to come around. He's right about half of it."

"He's right about all of it except the ending." Felix turned to look at her, and in the moonlight his face had a steadiness that made her feel suddenly, sharply older. "You taught me that rivers don't give you the crossing you want. They give you the one that's there. We go on because that's the crossing that's there."

"When did you get wise?"

"About the time you started needing it." He almost smiled. Then it faded. "Jonah. There's something else. I've been meaning to tell you, and the storm kept eating it."

"Tell me now."

He hesitated — and Jonah had learned, over nine years and one kidnapping, to dread Felix's hesitations more than most men's confessions.

"At Ashport," he said, "when I was in Sable's camp. I found her ledger tent. I couldn't read most of it — coded, or just her handwriting, which amounts to the same thing — but Wren's been working on the pages I copied. Tonight, while you and Bramm were shouting, he finished." Felix pulled a folded scrap of paper from inside his shirt, damp at the creases, and handed it over. "It's not a shipping manifest, Jonah. It's a claim. A legal instrument. Whoever holds the heart holds title to the whole Vell — every mile, every town, every lock and toll and fishery. Sable doesn't want to free the river. She wants to *own* it. And the last page says—" His voice caught, steadied. "The last page says the transfer has to happen at the source. With a keeper's blood present. She's not hauling us there despite the danger, Jonah. The danger is the point. She needs the river awake to take delivery."

Jonah read the page twice. The drizzle ticked on the roof. Down in the hold, faint through the deck plates, the heart hummed — and she could have sworn, in that moment, that the note had changed. Grown warmer. Closer to some pitch it remembered.

Ahead of them, three days' steam through country where every lock answered to Sable Corvane, the upper Vell waited in the dark — clearer than any river had a right to be, and listening.

"They'll be waiting at Harrow Narrows," Jonah said at last, folding the page into her coat. "Gunboat across the channel, if she's smart. And she's smart."

"Then we'd better be smarter," Felix said.

Jonah looked down at the boy — no, not the boy, she corrected herself, not anymore — and rang the engine bell twice, slow, the old signal: *make ready to run.*

Below, after a moment, Bramm answered.
