## 14. The Toll of Ashport

The river changed before Ashport did.

Felix noticed it from the bow, where he'd taken to standing in the mornings with his hands on the rail, learning the water the way Jonah had taught him — not by charts or soundings but by the feel of it through the wood. The Vell ran colder here. Clearer. The brown-green murk of the lower river had given way over the last two days to something almost glassy, so that when Felix looked down he saw not water but a suggestion of water, stones and drowned branches passing beneath him like things seen through a window at night. And the current — the current had opinions now. It tugged at the Provision's hull in long, deliberate pulls, as if a hand beneath the surface were testing their weight, deciding whether they were worth carrying.

"You keep looking at it like that, it'll look back," Odile said, coming up beside him with two mugs of chicory. She moved carefully still; her ribs were bound where the rapids had punished her, and she'd stopped arguing about resting. She handed him a mug and drank hers in silence for a moment. "Bramm says we're a day out. Maybe less."

Felix nodded. He hadn't slept well. None of them had, since Wren had spread Sable's ledger across the galley table and shown them what the heart really was — not power, but *claim*. Every mile of the Vell, every lock and toll and town, written down in Corvane ink if she reached the source first. Which meant she would reach it first. Of course she would. She'd bought the locks months ago; her gunboat knew these waters; and somewhere ahead, in the last town before the source, she would already be waiting.

Ashport appeared around a bend just past midday, and Felix understood immediately why it had been named for what it was not. There was no ash here. The town sat on a shelf of pale stone above the river, its houses built of timber gone silver with age, its chimneys cold, its single street climbing away from the water like a spine. It was smaller than Bellhaven had been, poorer too — but it was *alive*, and after days of drowned rooftops and sabotage marks and the memory of Odile's blood in the bilge, alive was a word that made something loosen in Felix's chest.

Then he saw the banners.

They hung from the eaves of the riverhouse at the foot of the landing — long strips of grey cloth bearing a device Felix had last seen stamped in wax on Sable's contract: a closed hand over a wave. Below them, on the dock itself, stood a line of men in Corvane grey, and beyond them, stacked in neat rows like ammunition, crates of bread.

"Bread," Odile said flatly.

"Flour, probably," Bramm said from the wheelhouse door. "Salt pork. Lamp oil." He spat over the rail. "That's how you buy a town that's hungry. You don't threaten it. You feed it."

Jonah came aft from the engine room, wiping her hands on a rag, and looked at the landing for a long time without saying anything. Felix watched her face do the thing it did — go still and unreadable, the river-smooth mask she wore when the water under her was deeper than she wanted anyone to know.

"We don't have to stop," Bramm said. "There's a shingle beach half a mile up. We could take on water there."

"And then what?" Jonah said quietly. "Run the last reach without knowing where she is, how many guns she has, what she's done to the source approach? No." She folded the rag into a tight square, once, twice. "We stop. We're a provisioning boat taking on supplies, same as any other. Felix—" and here her eyes found his, and Felix felt the old warmth in them, complicated now, threaded through with everything the poster had torn open between them, "—you stay aboard."

"Ashport's keeper blood," Felix said. "Wren said—"

"Wren says a lot of things." But her voice wasn't hard; it was tired, and afraid, and Felix recognized the fear because he'd been living inside it for days himself. "I know what I'm asking you to sit still for. Do it anyway."

* * *

They moored at the downstream end of the landing, as far from the grey banners as the dock allowed, and Jonah went ashore alone to bargain for flour while Bramm nursed the boiler and Odile, against doctor's orders that did not exist, supervised nothing in particular from a deck chair with a rifle across her knees. Wren stayed below, in the hold, with the cage — because that was what it was, whatever the manifest called it, an iron-latticed box the size of a coffin, and the old man sat beside it some hours each day with his palm flat against the bars, murmuring, as though the thing inside could hear him. Maybe it could. Felix had stopped disbelieving Wren somewhere back in the drowned town, when the river had pulled them through the narrows like it knew their names.

Which left Felix with nothing to do but obey, and obedience, he was discovering, sat in him like a swallowed stone.

He tried working. He coiled lines that didn't need coiling. He went below and looked at the cage until Wren opened one eye and said, gently, "It knows you're angry. It makes the water restless. Go be angry somewhere the current can't hear you." He climbed to the wheelhouse roof and lay back and watched gulls wheel over Ashport's silver roofs, and all the while his thoughts kept circling back to the same place: the poster in his pocket. *Keepers of the source. Reward.* His family's faces rendered in bad ink, three generations of them, printed for a bounty he hadn't known existed until a week ago. Sable Corvane had known who he was before Jonah had. Had known it when her agents boarded the Provision in the night. Had known it when she took him — not as insurance, not even only as bait, but as a key she intended to use.

And now she was feeding bread to a town of keeper descent, and Jonah thought he should sit on a boat and coil rope.

By late afternoon the light had gone long and gold, and Jonah still hadn't come back, and Felix made his decision the way he did most things — quickly, and with his stomach leading. He borrowed clothes: Odile's oldest shirt, a fisherman's coat off a drying line near the stern, a hat that smelled of tar. He left a note wedged under the deck chair where Odile couldn't miss it — *Gone to look. Back by dark. Don't kill Bramm waiting for me* — and slipped over the side onto the dock pilings, dropping the last few feet to the planks, and walked into Ashport with his collar up and his heart going like a paddlewheel.

* * *

The town smelled of woodsmoke and fresh bread, and the smell drew him uphill along the single street toward the square, where the whole population of Ashport seemed to have gathered in a loose, wary ring. At the center of the ring stood a wagon heaped with loaves, and beside the wagon, handing them out herself, stood Sable Corvane.

Felix had met her exactly once, at nightfall, on the Provision's deck, and had mostly been trying to disappear into the rigging at the time. Up close she was smaller than he remembered, and more precise — a narrow woman in a plain dark coat, hair bound back, moving down the line of townsfolk with the unhurried economy of someone dealing cards. She spoke to each person briefly. She touched children on the head. She remembered names — Felix heard her use them, *Marta, old Teo, the Halloway girl* — and he understood, watching, that this was the most dangerous thing about her. Not the gunboat. Not the grey-coated men at the edges of the square with their carbines slung casual as fishing rods. It was that she had learned a town's names, and a town that has been named by a stranger begins to belong to her.

"The upper mills are dead," she was saying to a stooped man whose hands were black with old forge-grime. "Have been for years, because the water's wrong, because the fish are gone, because nobody upstream will tell you why. I'm going upstream, Teo. I'm going to fix it. And when the Vell runs clean again, Ashport will hold the first toll-house on the new river. Your toll-house." She pressed a loaf into his hands. "No more watching other towns eat."

Felix drifted along the edge of the crowd, close enough to hear, far enough not to be counted. The bread was doing its work. He could see it in the shoulders of the people — the loosening, the leaning-in. Hunger makes honest arithmetic of loyalty, Wren had said once, and here it was, performed in flour and promises.

But it was the third thing in the square that made Felix's skin prickle.

Off to one side, apart from the wagon and the guards, stood a table under an awning, and at the table sat a thin clerkish man with ink-stained fingers and a ledger open before him, and one by one, between loaves, the people of Ashport were brought to him — sometimes led, sometimes walking on their own — and made to press a thumb into an inkpad and then onto the page. Felix worked his way closer, pretending interest in a shuttered stall, and read the ledger's heading upside down across the desk:

*Register of Assent — Township of Ashport — Grant of River-Rights, Corvane Charter.*

Thumbprints. Whole families of them, page after page, each one a small dark signature selling something the signers plainly didn't understand they owned. Wren's voice in his head: *the heart doesn't grant power, it grants claim.* Sable wasn't buying bread-loyalty. She was collecting deeds. Every thumbprint was another mile of the Vell signed away by the people who'd never known it was theirs.

"There you are."

Felix froze.

A woman had come up beside him — grey coat, carbine, the bored competence of a soldier on crowd duty — and for one lurching second he was certain he'd been caught. Then he saw where she was looking: past him, at the forge-handed man, Teo, who had finished his loaf and was hovering at the edge of things like a man who wants to ask a question and doesn't dare.

"You're with the old quarter?" the guard said to Felix. "Teo's kin?"

The lie assembled itself in Felix's mouth before he could examine it. "His grandnephew," he said. "Up from Caster's Bend. Just got in."

"Then get him to eat something and take him home before dark. Curfew's at dusk bell, and Corvane doesn't pay us enough to drag grandfathers to jail." She moved off, and Felix stood very still, feeling the shape of what had just happened settle over him like the borrowed coat. They hadn't caught him. They had *filed* him. To Sable's people he was no stranger to be questioned but a local to be managed — because he had keeper's features, the dark eyes and the river-broad face that half of Ashport shared, and because Sable's operation here ran on the assumption that everyone with that face was either bought or broken.

*Use it*, said a voice in him that sounded uncomfortably like Jonah at the wheel in bad water. *You're inside. Use it.*

* * *

Dusk bell rang from a chapel tower with no chapel attached to it anymore — just the tower, and a boy paid to pull the rope. After it, the square emptied fast, and the grey coats tightened into patrols, and Felix learned the second rule of being filed instead of caught: the file expects you to behave. He walked Teo home, as instructed, enduring the old man's rambling account of the years the mills died, and left him at his door with a promise to come back tomorrow that he had no intention of keeping, and then, instead of going anywhere, he went where the files wouldn't look — toward the lights.

Because the lights were wrong. Ashport was a poor town; its windows held candles and the occasional lamp. But at the top of the street, in the shell of the old toll-house — a broad stone building squatting above the river like a toad — every window burned bright, and carts came and went in the yard, and armed men checked papers at the gate. That was where Sable would sleep tonight. That was where the plans would be.

Getting in was easier than it should have been, which frightened Felix more than difficulty would have. A cart loaded with empty bread crates stood idling at the gate; Felix simply took hold of its tailboard and walked with it, head down, one of four laborers, and the guard at the gate glanced at the keeper's face under the tar-smelling hat and waved the lot of them through. Inside, the yard was organized chaos — crates, lanterns, a portable forge, and, under an oilcloth tarp near the stable arch, a long shape that made Felix's breath catch: the iron cage's twin, empty, its lattice door hanging open. A spare. Or a decoy. Or a thing meant for something else entirely, and he filed that away with his thumbs and his grandnephew story and kept walking.

He found what he needed in the toll-house itself, in the room that had clearly become Sable's map room. A clerk was supposed to be minding it; the clerk was asleep over his ledger, and Felix forgave him completely. On the great table lay a chart of the upper Vell drawn in fresh ink, and it was not a surveyor's chart. It was a claim-map. Every reach of the river was ruled off in lots, numbered, priced, and annotated in Sable's own hand — Felix recognized the sharp forward slant from the contract Jonah had thrown back at her. Lots for the water. Lots for the banks. Lots marked *toll* and lots marked *fishery* and one entire valley wall marked, in letters small and neat, *timber rights in perpetuity.*

And at the top of the chart, where the river began, the ink lines converged on a single circled feature labeled in the same precise hand:

*THE SOURCE — installation site. Cage to be set in the spring-pool, keyed at low water, dawn following arrival. Witness required: a keeper of the old line, thumb to the stone, to perfect the claim.*

*Witness required.*

Felix read it three times. The room seemed to tilt and right itself. She didn't just need the heart and the cage and the guns. A claim perfected by force might be contested — Wren had said so, that first night in the hold, muttering about old law older than any charter. But a claim *witnessed* by a keeper of the old line, sworn thumb to the source-stone, would be unbreakable. Clean title. Forever. And the last keeper of the old line within a hundred miles of that spring-pool was currently sitting in a hold on the Provision, eating Odile's stew and trusting Jonah Brightwater with the future of the river.

Except the last keeper wasn't Wren. Felix's hand went, without his willing it, to the poster in his pocket. Three generations of faces. His mother's jaw. His grandmother's eyes. *Thin keeper's blood,* Wren had called it, dismissive, pitying — but the law in Sable's ink didn't say *pure*. It said *of the old line.* And Sable had known his name before her agents ever boarded the boat. Insurance, Jonah had called it. Bait. But it had been neither, or not only. It had been recruitment. She had stolen Felix the way she meant to steal the river — as property, as an instrument, as a signature.

The sleeping clerk snorted. Felix was already moving, out the door, across the yard, past the tarped twin-cage, through the gate in the wake of another cart, his mind running faster than his feet. Dawn following arrival. Low water. Thumb to the stone. If he could get back to the Provision — if Jonah would listen, really listen, the way she used to before the poster came between them —

The lane down to the docks was dark, and the dark in it was wrong.

Three shapes detached from the shadow of the net-sheds and closed on him with the unhurried confidence of men who had been waiting, and the tallest of them lifted a lantern so the light fell full on Felix's face, and smiled.

"There you are," the man said, and his voice was pleasant, and terrible. "Master Keeper. Madam Corvane's been asking after you since noon. She'll be glad indeed to know you came to her." He turned, gesturing up the hill toward the lit toll-house, toward the woman buying a river one thumbprint at a time. "Walk with us, lad. And don't run. The water's cold this time of year, and Madam wants you *dry*."
