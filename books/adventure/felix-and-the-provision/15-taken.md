## 15. Taken

The note was pinned to the wheelhouse door with a fishhook, and Jonah knew before she touched it that the day had already gone wrong.

She had woken to silence — the wrong kind of silence, the kind that has weight to it, that presses on the ears like deep water. Ashport's harbor lay gray under a sky the color of old tin, and the Provision rocked at her moorings with a lightness Jonah didn't trust. She had counted heads in her sleep, the way she always did in strange ports: Bramm below in the engine room, Odile snoring like a landslide in the galley, Wren hidden somewhere in the hold with his secrets, Felix —

Felix.

She had told herself he'd gone up to the market for bread. She had told herself it while her boots were still cold from the deck planks, while her hands shook so badly she tore the note twice getting it free of the hook. She told herself it right up until she read the single line of Sable Corvane's handwriting, neat as an invoice:

*The boy is safe. The stone will decide whether he stays that way.*

Jonah stood very still at the wheelhouse door. Downriver, gulls argued over something dead. The river moved past the hull with its low, patient murmur, and for one mad moment she wanted to lean over the rail and ask it what it knew, because lately it seemed to know everything before she did.

"Bramm," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Get up here."

* * *

They found the place easily enough, because Sable wanted them to find it. A warehouse on the east quay, salt-stained timber, doors thrown open like a mouth. Two of Sable's hired guns flanked the entrance with the bored posture of men being paid by the hour, and between them, seated on an overturned crate with his wrists bound in front of him and his chin up like he was waiting for a play to start, sat Felix.

He looked small. That was the thing that went through Jonah like a harpoon — not the ropes, not the bruise darkening along his jaw, but how *small* he looked, this boy who could work a deck in a gale and read water like scripture. Nine years she'd been building him taller in her mind without noticing, and now the true size of him stood there between two armed men, and her chest felt caved in.

"Captain Brightwater." Sable Corvane emerged from the warehouse shadows the way she did everything — unhurried, certain the world would arrange itself around her. She wore traveling gray today, practical as a ledger, and her eyes went past Jonah immediately to catalog the others: Bramm glowering, Odile with a cleaver she made no effort to hide, Wren half-hidden behind them all, gaunt and burning. "And you brought the whole family. How economical."

"Let him go." Jonah kept her voice flat. Flat was armor. "You've got your cargo. You've got me. He's nothing to you but ballast."

"He's everything to me," Sable said pleasantly. "That's rather the point. Come inside, Captain. All of you — no, leave the cleaver on the dock, Odile, thank you. I've spent enough on this venture without paying for funerals I don't need."

Odile set the cleaver down with the exaggerated care of someone imagining other uses for it.

Inside, the warehouse smelled of tar and rope and old fish. Someone had cleared a space in the middle of the floor, and there — God help her — there was the cage. It sat on a pallet like a piece of machinery, which was the lie of it: iron bands, brass fittings, a lock worked in patterns Jonah recognized from nowhere and everywhere, from nine years ago, from a night on the upper river she had spent the rest of her life trying to drown. Through the bars she could see nothing at all. Just darkness that wasn't empty. Just a weight in the air, the way the air above a deep well is heavy.

Her mouth went dry. Her hands remembered things her mind had refused for nearly a decade — the smoothness of the source-stone, warm as a sleeping animal, heavier than its size should allow.

"You've seen it before," Sable observed, watching her face with the attention of a woman reading a tide table. "Don't bother denying it. Wren's been chattering, I expect. He does love his revelations." She circled the cage slowly, fingertips trailing the iron. "Here is where we stop pretending, Captain. You were never going to refuse me. Not really. The moment my people took the boy, every argument you had was already dead. I simply needed you to arrive at it honestly, because I need your hands, and hands do poor work when they're forced."

"They're doing fine work right now," Jonah said, "reaching for your throat."

"I'm sure they are." Sable smiled, and it was almost warm, which was worse. "But consider. The heart must be carried to the source. Not shipped, not hauled — carried, by hand, through water that has opinions about thieves. Wren can't do it; the river knows him too well and hates him too much for what he let happen. My people can't do it; the last man who touched the stone without leave came back with white hair and no memory of his own name. But you—" she stopped circling, faced Jonah fully, "—you carried it out once. The river let you. Whatever bargain it struck with you nine years ago, it hasn't forgotten, and neither have I. You are the only person alive who can walk that stone home without dying of it. And I am the only person standing between Felix Brightwater and a very long stay at the bottom of the Vell."

The words landed one by one, each with its own small detonation. Jonah heard her own breathing. Behind her, Wren made a sound — low, wounded, furious — and she was glad of it, because it meant someone else in the room understood exactly what Sable had just confessed: that this had never been about hiring a boat. It had been about finding the one thief the river had spared.

"You want me to carry it," Jonah said slowly. "Not haul it. Carry it. Into the source."

"I want you to install me," Sable said, and here her voice changed — dropped its pleasantness like a coat shrugged off, and underneath was something older and hungrier, something that had probably been waiting in her since before Jonah ever met her across a card table in Bellhaven, all those years ago. "The heart grants claim, Captain. Claim on every mile of the Vell. Every town, every toll, every fish and ferry and floodplain — mine, lawfully, irrevocably, once the stone acknowledges its holder at the source. All I require is that it be *returned properly*. Set down in the water by a hand the river trusts. Yours. And then the valley belongs to me, and you and your boy sail away rich, and everyone lives."

"And if I say no?"

Sable sighed, genuinely regretful, an actress to the bone. "Then we'll wait here together while the river decides what Felix is worth. He's a keeper's son, after all. Thin blood, but blood. The river might find a use for him." She glanced at Felix, and her expression softened into something almost tender, which turned Jonah's stomach over. "He's been very brave, by the way. Barely cried at all."

"I didn't cry at all," Felix said. His voice cracked on the second word, and he lifted his chin higher to cover it, and Jonah's heart broke cleanly down the middle.

* * *

They gave her an hour to decide, which was an insult dressed as mercy, and Jonah spent it on the dock with the river talking under the pilings and her crew arranged around her like a jury that had already deliberated.

"She's lying about the rich part," Bramm said. "Obviously."

"Obviously," Jonah agreed.

"But she's not lying about the rest." Bramm rubbed his jaw, grease still black in the creases of his knuckles. "I've been thinking on what Wren said. About claim. If that stone goes back in the water with Sable standing over it, calling the tune — every lock from here to the sea answers to her. Every town. Your boat, my engine, Odile's galley. We'd be tenants on our own river."

"So we take the stone and run," Odile said. "Ground the cage somewhere she'll never find it. Sink it in the marshes."

"The river won't allow it," Wren said quietly. They all turned. The old man sat on a bollard with his ruined hands folded, looking smaller than the morning had left him. "You've felt it pulling since the narrows. It wants what's in that cage back where it was taken from. Sink it in the marshes and the floods come back — worse. Drown three towns instead of one. There is no running from this. There is only the source, and who stands over it when the stone comes home." His eyes found Jonah's, and there was no accusation in them anymore, only a terrible tired understanding. "She chose you because of what you did. And she's right, Jonah. It has to be your hands. The question isn't whether you carry it. The question is what you do when you're standing at the water with her behind you and the boy beside you and the whole river watching."

Jonah looked out at the Vell. Gray water, gray sky, the far bank dissolving into mist. Somewhere upstream, past the drowned rooftops of Bellhaven, past the rapids that had taken Odile's blood as toll, past all the miles she had sworn never to travel again, the river waited with its memory open like a book.

Nine years ago she had stood at that source with the stone in her arms and believed she was setting the valley free. She had been young and certain and wrong, and the valley had paid her debt in drowned houses and dying fish, and she had run downstream ever since, telling herself distance was the same as penance.

It wasn't. She knew that now. Distance had just been another word for cowardice with better manners.

"If I do this," she said, "she wins. She owns the river."

"Not yet," Wren said. Something flickered behind his eyes — not hope exactly, but the ground hope grows in. "A claim is granted at the source, yes. But claims can be *answered* there too. The river is not a purse, Jonah. It's a court. Go. Carry the stone. Keep Felix close and keep your eyes open, and when the moment comes — and it will come — remember that the river remembers more than any of us. It remembers what she's done to get here. It remembers what you've done. And it weighs."

"That's a lot of faith to put in dirty water," Bramm muttered.

"It's not water," Wren said. "That's the first lesson of the keepers, and the last. It never was."

* * *

They cast off at dusk, the Provision and the gunboat behind her like a shadow with engines, the cage lashed amidships under tarpaulin that fooled no one, least of all the river. Sable stood on her own deck with binoculars and patience, trusting Jonah exactly as far as the boy's life required, which was precisely far enough.

Jonah took the wheel herself. Felix stood beside her, freed, unbound, rubbing his wrists, silent in the way he got when something had cut him deep — and she knew, God help her, that some of the cut was hers. He had walked into Sable's camp wearing his keeper's face to spy for her, and she had let him go, and now the face had nearly cost him everything.

"Felix." She kept her eyes on the channel markers, the last red light of Ashport sliding astern. "What I'm about to do—"

"Don't." His voice was rough. "Don't apologize. Don't explain. Just — don't hand me over again. That's all. Whatever happens up there. Don't trade me twice."

The words went in under her ribs and stayed there. She gripped the wheel and steered into the gathering dark, and the river rose to meet them, clear and strange and awake, pulling the hull forward like a hand closing around a wrist — gently, for now, the way you hold someone you intend to judge.

Ahead, faint as a memory, the water began to glow.
