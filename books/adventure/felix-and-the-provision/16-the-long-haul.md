## 16. The Long Haul

Felix had never known water could be quiet like this.

Not still — the Provision was moving, and moving fast, her paddle wheel biting into a current that should have fought them every fathom of the way. But the noise of it, the ordinary argument between hull and river that Felix had slept inside for three years, had gone strangely hushed. The water ran glass-clear over stones he could count from the deck. He could see fish hanging in the deep pools below, motionless, all of them facing upstream. All of them facing the same direction the boat was being pulled.

Toward the source.

He stood at the rail with his wrists raw where the ropes had been, watching the banks slide past. The guards Sable had put aboard — four of them, hard-faced men with carbines slung across their backs and a fifth at the wheel who never blinked — kept to the edges of things. They didn't touch the cargo. They wouldn't even look down the hatch that led to the hold, where the cage sat lashed in its cradle of iron and chain. One of them, the young one with the split lip, had crossed himself when he thought no one was watching.

They were afraid of it too. That was the only comfort Felix had found since Ashport, and it wasn't much.

"You shouldn't stand so close to the edge."

Wren's voice came soft behind him, dry as old paper. The old man lowered himself onto the coil of rope beside the rail with the care of someone whose joints had been broken once and set wrong. His eyes went to the water, and something moved through his face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The way a man looks at a house he grew up in and hasn't seen in fifty years.

"Neither should you," Felix said.

"I've been standing at this river's edge my whole life." Wren folded his hands in his lap. "It knows me."

"Does it?" Felix heard how sharp his own voice came out, and couldn't help it. Three days under guard, three days of Jonah kept apart from him except at meals, watched, measured, and every hour the water growing clearer, stranger, more awake. "Then maybe it can tell us what it wants."

"It's telling you now." Wren nodded at the bow. "Listen."

Felix listened. And beneath the hush of the river he heard it — or felt it, rather, low in his chest and the soles of his feet, a pulse like a second heartbeat that wasn't quite his own. The Provision wasn't fighting the current. She was riding it, the way a horse rides under a rider it has decided to carry. The wheel turned smooth. The engine, down in Bramm's domain, ran without its usual cough and grumble, almost eager, as if the fire in her belly had caught some of whatever was in the water.

"She's pulling us," Felix said. "The river's pulling us."

"The river is going home," Wren said. "And we are in its way. Or in its keeping. I haven't decided which word I like better."

* * *

Jonah stood at the stern rail with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and Felix watched her from across the deck because the guards wouldn't let them stand together. She hadn't slept — he could see it in the gray under her eyes, in the way her knuckles whitened on the rail whenever the boat shuddered. Nine years she'd run this river, and Felix had never once seen her look at it the way she looked at it now: like a creditor.

Sable Corvane's gunboat hung back half a mile astern, black smoke against the pale sky, patient as a debt. It didn't need to press them. There was nowhere to go. Ahead the valley narrowed into the final reach, walls of granite rising on either side, and the channel threaded between shoals that no chart marked because no captain had ever been fool enough or forced enough to come this far and live to draw one.

Except one. Nine years ago. Felix glanced at Jonah again and wondered — not for the first time, but for the first time with the poster's faces burning in his memory — what she had seen up here. What she'd carried out. What she'd left.

At midday they passed the first of the drowned towns.

Felix had seen Drowned Bellhaven, back in the early days of the voyage, and that had been bad enough — rooftops just under the surface, chimneys like drowned men's fingers. But Bellhaven had been flooded long ago, its ruin softened by decades of silt and weed. This place was different. The steeple of a church broke the surface ahead of them, tilted, and around it the roofs of houses lay barely a hand's depth under the clear water, so fresh that Felix could see the shutters still hanging at their windows, a washline strung between two chimneys with a bedsheet on it, white as bone, waving gently in the current.

Nobody said anything. Even the guards had gone silent. The young one with the split lip took off his hat.

The bedsheet waved as they passed, slow and courteous, like a hand letting go.

"They were moved out," Wren said quietly, when the town slid astern. "Mostly. The damming raised the water faster than the wagons could carry them off. Some chose to stay. Keepers' families, mostly. They said if the river was going to be caged, someone should stay near the bars."

Felix's throat was tight. "Your family?"

"Some of them." Wren looked at his hands. "I was away. Buying lamp oil, if you can believe it. I came back to a lake."

The pulse in the water beat on. The Provision climbed.

* * *

In the afternoon the light changed.

It came down through the granite walls at a slant, and where it touched the river it didn't scatter the way light ought to. It went down into the water and stayed there, glowing along the stones of the riverbed like veins of gold in quartz, and the whole channel ahead of them lit up section by section as though something beneath the surface were turning pages. Felix had grown up on rivers. He knew every trick water could play — refraction, reflection, the green lies of depth. This was none of them. This was the river showing itself, deliberately, the way a person turns their face toward a lamp.

Bramm came up from the engine room wiping his hands, took one look at the glowing channel, and went back down again. Odile, her arm bound and slingless because slings slowed her down, leaned in the galley doorway and muttered something in her river-French that Felix was fairly sure was a prayer dressed up as a curse.

Even the guards felt it. The one at the wheel — a broad, silent man called Harrow whom Sable had installed there because Jonah was not to be trusted with her own boat — kept glancing over the side, and each time he did, his grip on the spokes shifted, like a man holding a railing in a high wind.

Only Jonah stood unmoved. Or stood as if unmoved, which Felix was beginning to understand was a thing she did the way other people bled — automatically, constantly, at cost.

When the guards changed watch at dusk, Wren shuffled across the deck to where Felix stood, and for a while they watched the last light go down the granite walls together.

"You asked what the river wants," Wren said.

"I asked if it could tell us."

"It told your captain nine years ago. She didn't listen." The old man's voice held no accusation, only a terrible gentleness. "It's been telling the whole valley ever since. The floods. The fish dying belly-up in the shallows. The wells going bitter in Ashport. Every bad season since then is the river saying the same sentence over and over, in the only voice it has left." He tapped the deck planks with one knuckle. "We stole its voice, boy. Its heart. What's left is a whisper, and whispers carry a long way when everything else has gone quiet."

Felix looked down into the luminous water. A fish hung below him, facing upstream, and as he watched it turned — slowly, deliberately — and looked up at him. He knew that was impossible. Fish didn't look. But it looked, with an eye like a coin at the bottom of a well, and then it turned back and resumed its patient climb toward the source.

"Wren." He made himself say it. "What happens when we get there? To us. To Jonah. To—" He stopped, because the next word was *me*, and saying it aloud would make it a thing that could happen.

"To Jonah," Wren said, "the river will show what it shows everyone who comes to the source honestly. Everything. Every consequence, every cost, laid out plain. Most people can't bear it. Your captain has spent nine years practicing, whether she knows it or not." He paused. "To you — that depends on choices not yet made. The river is patient about choices. It's the one thing it never rushes."

"And Sable?"

Something hardened in the old man's face, and for a moment Felix saw what he must have looked like forty years ago, standing on these same waters before they were taken from him.

"Sable thinks she's bringing a crown to the source," Wren said. "She'll find out she's bringing a thief to a magistrate." He looked astern, at the gunboat's smoke smudging the first stars. "The river isn't dragging us to doom, Felix. Doom is easy. Doom is what any strong current can do to any weak boat." He laid his hand flat on the rail, palm down, as if taking a pulse. "It's dragging us to judgment. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Doom doesn't weigh anything. Judgment weighs everything — and then it decides."

* * *

Night came up the valley like a tide.

There was no moon, but they didn't need one; the river lit its own way now. The glow beneath the surface had strengthened until the whole channel ran silver-white, and the granite walls caught it and threw it back, so the Provision steamed through a corridor of soft light with the stars above and the drowned world below. Felix stood his watch at the rail because sleep wouldn't have him. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the bedsheet waving. He saw the fish turn. He saw the second poster, the one he'd pocketed in that river town a lifetime ago — *REWARD: KEEPERS OF THE SOURCE* — and underneath it, in his mind's eye, he added the faces he'd never gotten to know: his mother's, his father's, gone before he was old enough to hold their faces steady in memory.

Keepers. The word fit differently now. Not a title. A weight.

Near midnight the channel narrowed to its last throat, and the current quickened, and Felix felt the change in his teeth before he heard it in the water — a rising note, a hum, the sound of a held breath about to be released after nine long years. The Provision surged. Harrow wrestled the wheel and the wheel answered like a living thing, gentle and irresistible, and ahead of them the gorge opened into darkness that the river-light could not reach — a vast black mouth in the glowing world, breathing cold air down at them.

Beyond it, faint as a memory, Felix heard water falling. A great deal of water, falling a great distance, with a sound like applause.

Wren rose stiffly from his rope coil and stood beside him, and the old man's eyes were wet, and he was smiling, and it was the most frightening thing Felix had seen in three weeks.

"We're nearly there," Wren whispered.

Astern, the gunboat's whistle blew — one long blast, Sable's signal, *keep pace, keep schedule* — and its sound fell dead on the water, swallowed whole, as if the river had reached up and taken the noise for itself.

Jonah crossed the deck to them, past the guards, past caring, and stood at the rail with her son-of-the-river's instinct and her nine years of guilt written plainly on her face in the strange light. She looked at the black mouth of the gorge, and then at Felix, and her voice came out rough.

"When we go through," she said, "whatever happens — you get behind me. You hear me? Whatever it shows, whatever it asks—"

"What if it asks for me?" Felix said.

The question hung there, and the river pulsed once beneath the hull, slow and deep, like a heart considering.

Jonah didn't answer. She couldn't, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them heavier than the cage in the hold.

The Provision gathered herself, trembled from stem to stern — and ran for the dark.
