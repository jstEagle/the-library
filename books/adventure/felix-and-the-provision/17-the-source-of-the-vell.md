## 17. The Source of the Vell

The water changed before the land did.

Jonah had been watching it for hours — the way the current beneath the Provision's hull had gone from brown to green to a clarity that made her stomach tighten, as though she were looking down through glass at nothing at all. No silt. No shadow. The river this far up was so clean it seemed unfinished, like something still being thought into existence.

"Depth's holding," Bramm called from the engine hatch, his voice rough with three days of climbing water. "But she's pulling. You feel that?"

Jonah felt it. Everyone felt it. The Provision was being drawn forward now, gently but insistently, the way a hand closes around a wrist — not dragging, not quite. *Guiding.* Nine years on the Vell and she had never once felt the river want anything. Now it wanted them, and the wanting ran through the deck plates and up through her boots and settled somewhere behind her sternum like a second heartbeat.

Ahead, the gorge narrowed to a throat of gray stone, and beyond it —

She cut the engine to half and stood very still at the wheel.

Beyond the throat, the walls fell open like hands unclenching. An amphitheater of stone rose in tiers on every side, pale as bone, veined with channels where water had run for ten thousand years before anyone thought to name it. And at its center, filling the floor of that vast stone bowl, a spring boiled up out of the earth itself — wide as a millpond, impossibly deep, the water rising from some darkness below darkness and spilling over a lip of rock to become, in a single silver thread, the beginning of the Vell.

The source.

Jonah had seen it once, nine years ago, by torchlight and by lies, and she had spent nine years telling herself she'd dreamed it. She hadn't dreamed it. It was worse than memory. The amphitheater made a sound — a low, continuous resonance, like a struck bell that never finished ringing — and the sound came up through the water and into the boat and into her teeth.

Behind her, chains rattled. Guards. Sable's men had ridden the Provision up the last reach with their rifles loose in their slings and their boots on Felix's shoulders, figuratively if not literally; the boy sat against the wheelhouse wall under the eye of a man named Garrick, who hadn't stopped smiling since Ashport. Wren was lashed to the rail near him, old wrists raw, his eyes closed. Jonah couldn't tell if he was praying or listening.

Sable Corvane came up the steps from the main deck slowly, the way she did everything, as if the world were a staircase built for her descent. Her coat was dry. Her boots were dry. Three days on a riverboat in rising water and the woman managed to stay *dry*, and Jonah hated her a little more for it.

"There it is," Sable said softly. Not to Jonah. To the spring. To all of it.

"You've seen it before," Jonah said. Her voice came out flat. "You didn't need me to find it."

"I needed you to bring it." Sable turned, and there was something in her face Jonah had never seen there before — not triumph, exactly. Something hungrier and more frightened than triumph. Awe, maybe, in a woman who had never let anything awe her. "Nine years ago you carried a piece of it out of here in a crate and told yourself you were a liberator. Now you'll carry it back and tell yourself whatever you need to. That's what you are, Jonah Brightwater. A carrier. The river's own mule."

"And you're what? Its owner?"

"I'm going to be."

* * *

They brought the Provision alongside the southern shelf of the amphitheater, where the stone dipped low enough to form a natural quay — and Jonah understood, with a cold settling in her gut, that it *was* a quay. Cut deliberately. Chiseled edges worn smooth by centuries of keeper boats. This place had been a harbor once, a working harbor at the birth of the river, and the people who worked it had carved their mooring rings into living rock, and those rings were still there, waiting, patient as everything else about this water.

They tied off. There was no point refusing. Six rifles said there was no point.

Felix was brought down the gangplank first, and Jonah watched him go with her hands curled so tight around the rail that her knuckles cracked. He looked small on that ancient stone — sixteen years old, keeper-blood thin as creek water, walking across ground his family had kept for longer than any record held. He didn't look afraid. That was somehow worse. He looked like he *recognized* it.

Wren shuffled past her next, and as the guards pushed him by, the old man's shoulder brushed hers and he murmured, barely a breath: "When it opens, don't touch it. Whatever happens. Don't touch it unless you mean to carry it all the way."

"Carry it where?" Jonah whispered back.

But he was already gone, herded onto the stone with the others, and Sable's voice cut across the amphitheater like a blade across silk.

"Open the cage, Captain."

* * *

The cargo had been moved to the shelf's edge, forty feet from the spring, and the crate sat there in the white light coming off the water — iron-banded, seal-wrapped, humming. Jonah had felt the hum through the deck for two hundred miles. Up close it wasn't a hum anymore. It was a pulse. Slow and enormous, slower than any heart should beat, and each pulse sent a ring of shimmer across the surface of the spring, perfectly concentric, perfectly timed, as though the water below were answering.

Or calling.

"The key," Sable said, and one of her men put a black iron bar into Jonah's hands. It was warm. Everything about this cargo was warm, blood-warm, and Jonah had spent the whole journey pretending not to know why.

She looked at Felix. Felix looked at her. Between them passed everything they hadn't said since the night she'd told him about the posters, about his family, about the theft — the fracture between them that had never healed because neither of them had tried, because trying would have meant saying things there weren't words for yet. His jaw was set. His eyes were wet and furious and steady.

He nodded. Once.

*Do it,* the nod said. *And then we finish this.*

Jonah set the bar against the first seal.

The iron went through the wax like wax itself, and the sound the crate made when the band parted was not metal. It was a note — low, aching, the same note as the amphitheater's endless bell-tone, but closer now, unbearably close, and the water in the spring shivered from shore to shore. The guards shifted. One of them swore. Even Sable's composure flickered, just at the corners.

Second band. Third. Jonah's hands were shaking and she let them shake. There was no dignity left to protect. There was only the doing.

The lid came free.

Light came out of the crate.

Not firelight, not lamplight — no color Jonah had a name for, a pale gold-white like sun through the skin of a closed eye, and it poured upward and outward and touched the tiered stone walls and made them glow like the inside of a shell. And in the center of the crate, cradled in nine years of packing straw gone brown and brittle, lay the heart of the Vell.

It was smaller than she remembered. A stone, roughly the size of a loaf, smooth as a riverbed pebble, and it *beat*. She could see it beat — a slow swell of light, dark, light — and with every beat the spring answered, and with every answer the whole amphitheater rang, and Jonah stood above it with the lid in her hands and wept without deciding to, tears running hot down her face in the cold air, because it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen and she was the reason it had been in a box.

"Well," Sable breathed.

She stepped forward. Jonah stepped sideways, into her path, before she knew she'd moved.

"It doesn't work like that," Jonah said. "Whatever you think it does. Wren told you —"

"Wren told me stories." Sable's voice had gone soft again, which from Sable was the most dangerous register there was. "I read the keepers' ledgers myself, Captain. I bought them town by town, out from under dying families who signed anything for bread. I know exactly how it works. Claim runs through it like current through wire. Touch it, swear to it, and the river is yours — every mile, every lock, every toll gate from here to the sea." She smiled, and it was almost gentle. "Move."

"Jonah." Felix's voice, tight. "The water."

Jonah looked.

The spring was rising.

Not flooding — *rising*, the way a chest rises, drawing breath. The level had come up a hand's width along the stone lip while they stood there, and the thread of outflow had thickened to a rope, and the rope was quickening, and down in the depths of the spring something vast turned over, slow as a whale, and the light from the heart pulsed faster now, faster, matching something.

Matching the water.

"Get it out of the crate," Sable snapped at her men. "Now."

Garrick moved first, reaching past Jonah with both hands — and the moment his fingers broke the plane of the crate's rim, the light *stopped*.

Everything stopped. The pulse. The ring-tone. The shimmer on the spring. A silence dropped over the amphitheater so complete Jonah could hear her own blood, and in that silence the heart lay dark and still as an ordinary stone, and Garrick's hands hovered above it, suddenly ridiculous.

Then the spring exhaled.

The wave came off the center of the pool in a perfect ring, waist-high, silent, impossibly fast, and it took Garrick off his feet and slammed him against the tiered stone thirty feet away and left him crumpled and groaning in six inches of retreating water. The other guards stumbled back, rifles swinging wildly toward the spring, which was like aiming a rifle at the sky.

"No," Jonah heard herself say. Not to the guards. To the water. To the whole shining bowl of it. "No — listen — we brought it back. Do you hear me? We *brought it back—*"

The heart flared.

And Sable Corvane walked into the light.

Later, Jonah would try to describe it and fail, because what happened next happened in a grammar the ordinary world didn't speak. Sable crossed the stone with her coat streaming behind her, reached into the crate, and lifted the heart of the Vell in both bare hands, and she held it up against the white sky and said, clear and carrying, in the voice of a woman reading her own coronation:

"I claim the Vell. Every mile. Every mouth. Mine."

The heart burned.

Light poured through her fingers like she was holding a piece of the sun, and for one heartbeat — one long, ringing heartbeat — it seemed to answer her. The spring swelled. The stone tiers blazed. Somewhere far downstream, Jonah would swear forever after, she felt every lock and weir and drowned rooftop between here and the delta tremble at once, like a chain pulled taut.

Then the river said no.

There was no other way to hold it in her mind afterward. The water rose — all of it, everywhere, at once — the spring surging up out of its own depths, the outflow thread becoming a torrent, spray lifting off the whole surface in a fine bright mist that hung in the air and did not fall. And the light in Sable's hands changed. The gold-white curdled. Darkened. Went the color of deep water over deep stone, and Jonah saw Sable's face above it, and what she saw there broke something in her that never quite mended:

Terror. Pure, undiluted terror, on the face of the most feared woman on the Vell, as the thing she had murdered and bribed and starved a valley to possess looked back at her from between her own fingers and found her wanting.

"This is mine," Sable said, and her voice cracked in the middle of *mine*. "I paid for it. I *paid—*"

The river took its interest.

The wave that took Sable Corvane did not knock her down. It lifted her — heart and all, hands and all, crown-claim and all — up off the stone in a column of water that stood for a moment in the white light like a glass tower, and inside it Sable thrashed and screamed and did not drown, because the river was not done with her, because the river wanted her to *see*. Jonah could see her through the water, distorted, refracted, the way you see a face through ice, and the heart was burning between her palms like a brand, and the claim she had spoken was being unwritten letter by letter, toll by toll, mile by mile, all the way down the valley.

The guards broke. All of them, at once, rifles thrown down, running for the high tiers, and Jonah let them run. Felix grabbed her arm — when had he gotten to his feet, when had he gotten so *close* — and shouted over the roar:

"She can't hold it! Nobody can hold it — it has to go *back*, Jonah, it has to go in the water, someone has to carry it—"

The column of water bent. Tilted. Began, with terrible deliberation, to lower Sable toward the stone — not gently, not cruelly, the way a hand sets down something it has finished examining — and the heart tore free of her scorched fingers and hung in the air above the crate, blazing, beating, waiting.

Waiting for a hand.

Wren was shouting something from the rail of the boat, his old voice cracking across the roar: "*A keeper! It wants a keeper's hand!*"

And the heart turned in the air, slow as a compass needle finding north, and its light swung across the amphitheater — past Jonah, past the cowering guards, past Sable crumpled and steaming on the wet stone —

— and settled, soft and gold and absolute, on Felix.
