## 18. The Keeper's Choice

The light found Felix the way a hand finds a shoulder in the dark — gently, and without any possibility of mistake.

He felt it before he understood it. A warmth in his chest, in his wrists, in the thin places of him he had never thought of as anything but ordinary: the soft skin inside his elbows, the backs of his knees, the hollow of his throat. The heart hung in the air above the amphitheater's flooded floor, turning slow, shedding gold across the water like a lamp behind silk, and every eye in that ring of stone turned with it — and landed on him.

"No," Felix said.

It came out small. He said it again, louder, and the word broke apart in the roar of the rising river. The water was climbing the stone steps where Sable's guards crouched, climbing the terraces where Jonah stood ankle-deep and swaying, climbing toward all of them with the patience of something that had waited nine years and could wait nine more. And through it all the heart held its position above the flood, patient too, golden, waiting on him the way a door waits on a key.

Wren's voice cut through, cracked and urgent. "Boy! Come to it — *come to it now!*"

Felix looked at Wren, at the old man clinging to the Provision's rail with his white hair plastered to his skull, and what Felix saw in his face was not triumph. It was grief. It was the face of a man watching a debt come due to the wrong account.

"I'm not—" Felix started, and his voice failed him, because the lie was already dying in his mouth. He *was*. He had known since Ashport, since he'd walked into Sable's camp wearing his own face like a borrowed coat and felt the old women look at him and cross themselves. He had known since the poster, since the word *keepers* written in faded ink over a reward, since Jonah had told him the truth about his family on the deck of the only home he'd ever had. Thin blood, she'd called it. A thread of it, running down through drowned generations to land, of all the unlikely places, in an orphan boy hauling rope on a riverboat.

Thin blood. But the heart did not measure by the cupful. It measured by the having.

"Felix." Jonah's voice reached him across the water, hoarse from shouting, raw as a scraped knee. She was wading toward him now, fighting the current that poured down the terraces, and her face was the face he knew better than any in the world — stubborn, furious, terrified. "Don't you dare move."

The guards were moving though. Two of them had found their feet on the high steps and were coming for him along the dry ridge of stone, and Felix understood why with a cold drop of his stomach: if the heart needed a keeper's hand, then a keeper's hand was suddenly the most valuable thing in the valley. Sable's men would take him the way they'd taken everything else — as cargo.

Jonah saw them too. Felix watched her see them, watched her do the arithmetic she always did, fast and ruthless and usually right — and then watched her throw the answer away. She didn't turn to fight them. She turned to the water.

* * *

There is a moment, Felix would think later, when a person chooses, and the choosing takes no time at all. It happens in the space between one heartbeat and the next, in the gap where a life has been running on habit and suddenly runs on something else.

Jonah Brightwater reached the heart first.

She had to climb for it — up the wet steps of the inner terrace while the river pulled at her legs like hands, while the guards shouted behind her, while Wren screamed something that might have been her name or might have been a prayer. The heart hung low enough now that she could have touched it with an outstretched arm, and it turned as she approached, slow, questioning, its light sliding off her like rain off oilcloth.

"You're not mine," she told it. Her voice was nothing against the roar, but Felix read her lips, read the set of her jaw, read nine years of guilt standing up at last to be counted. "I know. I stole you anyway. So I'll carry you back."

And she took it.

Felix heard the sound she made — a sound he would never fully leave behind, the sound of a person being unmade and remade in the same breath. The light that had slid off her sank *in*, flooding her veins visible through the skin of her arms and throat, gold running under her skin like sunrise under ice. Her whole body arched. Her hand closed around the stone and did not open, could not open, welded to it by something older than muscle.

The river went quiet.

Not calm — quiet. The way a room goes quiet when someone says the true thing. All around the amphitheater the flood stood still, trembling, every surface of it lit from beneath by the woman standing hip-deep in the center holding the heart of the Vell in her bare hand, and her hair streaming loose, and her eyes wide and wet and burning with the reflected gold.

"Jonah!" Felix was moving before he decided to, sliding down the terrace stones on his heels, arms out, the current shoving him sideways. "Jonah, let go—"

"I can't," she said, and almost smiled. It was the worst thing Felix had ever seen, that almost-smile — crooked and brave and full of everything she'd never said to him in three years of sharing a boat. "That's rather the point, lad."

Wren's voice, ragged: "It will burn her out! Keeper's blood carries it — hers is *none* — it will take everything she has and keep taking—"

"Then it can stand in line," Jonah said. "Behind everyone else I owe."

The guards had stopped coming. They stood on the high steps, guns forgotten in their hands, staring down at the lit woman in the water like men staring at a fire they'd been hired to start and couldn't put out. Somewhere behind them Sable Corvane stirred on the wet stone, groaning, her claim dissolving off her like frost off a window, and no one moved to help her, and no one needed to.

Felix reached her. He caught her free arm and the current tried to peel him away and he wrapped himself around her instead, both arms locked, his boots finding a seam in the stone, and up close he could feel it — a vibration in her body like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, heat coming off her skin in waves, her pulse hammering somewhere far too fast and far too slow at once.

"You don't get to do this," he shouted into her ear. "You don't get to pay my debts!"

"Not your debt," she said. Her lips were gray. Her eyes found his and they were clear, clearer than he'd ever seen them, and there was no fear in them, which frightened him more than anything else on the river. "Mine. Nine years of mine. You were the best thing I ever did, Felix, and I did it running away. Let me do one thing standing still."

The heart pulsed. The light in her arms brightened, and she gasped, and her knees buckled, and Felix took her weight without letting go, and the water around them began — slowly, with the deliberation of something enormous turning over in its sleep — to move.

Downstream. All of it. The flood drawing back off the terraces in long silver sheets, the river gathering itself toward the center of the amphitheater, toward the spring-fed throat of stone where the Vell was born, opening there like a road.

"It's showing her the way," Wren whispered from the rail, and his old eyes were streaming, whether from spray or something else he didn't say. "Nine years I've prayed for a keeper to carry it home. Nine years, and the river sends me a thief with a good heart." He laughed, wet and broken. "It always did have a sense of humor."

"Jonah." Felix's voice cracked. He shook her, gently, the way you shake someone waking from a dream. "Walk. I'll walk with you. You don't have to—"

"I know," she said. "That's what makes it worth doing."

She stepped forward, and the water parted around her ankles, and the heart blazed, and Felix held onto her arm and walked with her into the throat of the source, one step, two, the gold light climbing them both until he couldn't tell where her glow ended and his began —

— and the heart turned in her grip, gentle as a compass needle, and its light swung once more across the water, past the guards, past Sable, past Wren's weeping face —

— and settled, soft and absolute, on Felix again.

Because thin blood was still blood. Because the stone knew what Jonah refused to say: that it would not survive her hands alone. That it wanted both of them — the sinner and the son of keepers — walking together, or not at all.

Jonah looked down at the light on the boy beside her, and for the first time in nine years, Jonah Brightwater wept.

"Well," she said. "I suppose you're coming after all."

The river opened before them, all the way home.
