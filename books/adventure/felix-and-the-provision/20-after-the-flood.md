## 20. After the Flood

The water came down the valley like a held breath finally let go.

Felix stood on the bank above the source with Jonah's hand still wrapped in his, and watched the Vell run clean for the first time in his life. He hadn't known it was dirty. That was the terrible part — nine years of grey-green water, silt-choked and tired, and he had thought that was simply what rivers were. Now the current below the amphitheater ran so clear he could see the stones on the bottom turning over, each one bright as a coin, and the light came up through the water instead of dying in it.

The roar had passed. What remained was a sound like the river breathing — long, slow, unhurried, as if something enormous had finished a task it had waited a very long time to finish.

Sable Corvane's men were gone. Felix kept checking, half expecting them to come back up the trail with their guns and their ledgers, but they had melted away the way hired men do when the money stops meaning anything. He'd seen one of them — the tall one with the scarred knuckles who had guarded him at Ashport — pause at the edge of the camp, look back once at the rising water, and take off his hat. Not to anyone. To the river. Then he'd walked into the trees and didn't come out again.

Whatever claim Sable had carried in her ledger books, her seals, her bought locks and her bread-and-promises camps — the river had taken it the way it took everything else. Felix had watched her from across the water when the heart rose free of its cage. She had stood on the stone shelf with her arms open like she could catch it, like ownership was a thing you could hold against your chest, and the river had risen around her boots, then her knees, then her waist, patient as arithmetic, until she understood. She'd waded out backwards, soaked to the collarbone, her ledger floating away from her in pages, and no one had touched her. They hadn't needed to. The river had read her accounts aloud, and every man who worked for her had heard them.

Now she was gone too — downstream somewhere, or gone entirely; Felix found he couldn't make himself care which.

What he cared about was standing behind him.

* * *

They came down from the amphitheater slowly, because two of them were slow. Wren walked leaning on Bramm's arm, his old keeper's robes soaked and clinging, his breath whistling in a way it hadn't before the heart went home. Odile leaned on nobody, which was its own kind of stubbornness — her burned hands wrapped in river-soaked linen, held out from her body like a woman carrying two small animals she refused to put down.

And Jonah —

Felix looked at her sideways, the way he'd been doing since the bank, since she'd come up out of the source alive when every part of him had been certain she wouldn't be. Her hair was down, plastered to her neck. Her coat was gone; the river had taken it, along with the Provision's wheel-line she'd wound around her wrist. She moved like a woman whose bones had been replaced with someone else's, heavier ones. But she was walking. She was walking, and every few steps her eyes went past the bend in the gorge, downstream, to where the Provision was moored.

Except it wasn't moored anymore.

Felix saw the moment she noticed. Her step hitched — barely, a stutter no one else would have caught, but Felix had spent three years learning to read her the way she read water. He took her elbow without asking. She let him.

"She's still there," Felix said. "You can see her mast."

He wasn't sure that was mercy or cruelty. The Provision lay in the wide pool below the last rapids, where they'd left her before the final climb on foot. From this height you could see all of her — the low black hull, the deck he'd swabbed ten thousand mornings, the wheelhouse with its cracked glass pane Bramm had never fixed, and the mast, tall and bare, throwing its thin shadow across water that was now too clear to hide anything.

Too clear to hide what was coming.

Because the pool was draining. Not fast — nothing the river did now was fast — but steadily, deliberately, the way a man rolls up his sleeves. The level had dropped a hand's width since they'd crested the ridge. The Provision sat lower than she should have, not from taking water but from losing it beneath her keel, settling by degrees toward a riverbed that was coming up to meet her like an opened palm.

"The payment," Wren said quietly. He had stopped at the lip of the slope, and the wind off the water moved his white hair. "I told you. The river weighs what it's given against what's returned. It has weighed. This is the balance."

"There has to be another way," Bramm said. His voice cracked on *another*. "She's sound. Hull's sound, engine's sound — I rebuilt that engine with my own—"

"Bramm." Jonah's voice was rough as dredged gravel. She hadn't stopped looking at the boat. "Hush a minute."

Bramm hushed. He was a big man, engineer's forearms and a beard full of grease and grey, and Felix had never seen him look small before. He looked small now, standing with his cap wrung in both fists, watching the only home any of them had left settle inch by inch onto the riverbed.

"We can get aboard," Odile said. "Cut her loose, walk her down on the freshet — the whole valley's got high water, we could ride it all the way to Bellhaven, patch her at the yard—"

"Odile." Jonah said it gently, and gentleness from Jonah Brightwater was rarer than clean water in the Vell. "Look at the current."

Felix looked. Below the pool, through the narrows where Sable's gunboat had once cornered them, the river ran white and total — not violent, exactly. Purposeful. Every eddy that had ever sheltered them was gone. Every slack pocket, every seam of slow water a captain could thread a hull through. The river had closed all its doors except one.

"It's not angry," Wren murmured. "Understand that. If it were angry, none of us would be standing here. It's settling accounts. And the account is hers." He nodded at the boat. "Nine years she carried the stolen thing's weight in her hold without knowing it. Every mile she ran, she ran on the river's grief. The river knows her name, Captain. It's only asking for it back."

Jonah stood very still.

Felix had seen her afraid — on the upper river, in the narrows, the night Sable's agents boarded and left their message nailed to the wheelhouse door. He had seen her furious, and he had seen her guilty, dragged out of her like a splinter over those long dark miles. But he had never seen her like this: stripped of even the guilt, standing in the plain daylight of consequence, a woman being asked to hand over the last thing she owned.

"Nine years," Jonah said, mostly to herself. "She was older than me when I won her at cards. Half-won. The other half I paid off over four seasons." A ghost of a laugh, no humor in it. "I ran her hard. I ran her everywhere I could think of, and some places I shouldn't have. And every time the water felt wrong under the hull — every time she handled heavy, like something riding in the hold — I told myself it was cargo shift. I told myself a lot of things."

"You didn't know," Felix said.

"I suspected." She turned and looked at him then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "That's worse, and you know it is. But thank you for saying it anyway."

* * *

They went down to the pool because Jonah asked them to, and because none of them would have let her go alone.

The path was wet rock and new grass — grass already growing, impossibly green, in the flood-scoured margins, as if the river had decided to redecorate on its way through. The air smelled different. Felix kept breathing it in like a man testing whether he was dreaming: cold mineral water, crushed green things, and underneath it something faintly sweet he had no name for. The smell of the source, carried downstream. The smell the keepers must have lived inside for generations, before his family's line thinned to a boy with a wanted poster in his pocket.

The Provision sat in the shallowing pool with her decks awash to the scuppers. The wheelhouse window caught the afternoon light and threw it, one bright bar, across the water.

"I'll go out," Bramm said hoarsely. "Waist-deep, maybe less. I can get her logbook. Charts. Your father's compass, Captain, it's in the drawer under the—"

"No." Jonah said it softly. "Leave her fitted. Leave her as she is."

"Jonah—"

"She's not wreckage, Bramm. She's payment. You don't strip a payment." Jonah rolled her trousers to the knee, and Felix realized she meant to wade out herself, and something in his chest clenched hard enough to hurt.

"I'll carry you," he said. "Your legs are done, you know they are. Don't argue."

For a moment he thought she would. Three years of arguing — about his watch rotations, his card debts, the time he'd painted the dinghy *corvane-red* as a joke and she'd made him sand it off with a brick. But she just looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod, once, and turn her back, and climb on.

She weighed almost nothing. That frightened him more than the water did.

He waded out while the others picked their way along the shore. The water came up his shins, his knees, cold enough to steal his breath, and clear — so clear he could see the Provision's shadow lying on the riverbed below him, and the riverbed itself, stones and old rope and one of Odile's lost galley spoons, all of it lit from above like something in a dream. The boat rocked gently as he approached, though there was no wind. Greeting him, he thought. Or saying goodbye.

At the gunwale he set Jonah down on the deck, and she stood a moment with her bare feet on the boards she'd owned for nine years, and Felix climbed up beside her and did not say anything, because there was nothing in him equal to the moment and he knew it.

The wheelhouse door was open. Inside, everything was exactly as they'd left it that morning — the chart spread and weighted with a tin cup, Bramm's wrenches racked, Odile's dried herbs hanging from the beam in bundles, filling the little space with thyme and river-sage. The logbook lay open on the shelf. Jonah's handwriting marched across the last page in entries that had stopped yesterday:

*Upper reach. Water clear. Crew well. Boy at the wheel and proud of it.*

Felix read it upside down and had to look away fast at the water.

Jonah touched the page once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. Then she took the pen from its holder, and wrote, in the same steady hand:

*Paid in full. She was a good boat. J.B.*

She set the pen down, squared the book on the shelf, and closed the wheelhouse door.

"Well," she said. Her voice almost held. "That's that."

"That's not that," Odile called from the shore, and there was something wild in her voice, and Felix turned to see the cook standing hip-deep at the margin with her bandaged hands raised, laughing.

Actually laughing. Bent forward, wheezing, tears cutting through the grime on her face, laughing so hard Bramm had to grab her elbow to keep her upright.

"What—" Felix started.

"Her mast!" Odile gasped. "Look at her mast, the vain old girl — going down with her nose in the air!"

And she was right. As the pool gave way beneath her — slowly, with a long groan of timber and a rush of water across the deck that sent them both scrambling to the rail and then, at Jonah's quiet word, wading back to shore — the Provision settled stern-first, the way a tired woman sits down. But her mast stayed up. Stayed up long after the deck went under, long after the wheelhouse windows filled with green light, standing straight out of the river like a finger pointed at the sky, like a flagpole with no flag, like — Odile howled — *like the last word in an argument.*

"She always did hate losing," Jonah said. Her voice broke on *losing*, and she laughed too, suddenly and helplessly, and then she was crying, and Felix put his arm around her waist and she let him, and they stood there on the bank, the four of them plus Wren, watching the mast sink by inches.

It took most of an hour. Nobody left.

The sun crossed the gorge wall. The water rose over the crosstrees, over the truck, and the last thing visible was the very tip of the mast, a dark point in bright water, trembling slightly with the current beneath. Then a ring spread outward from it, smooth and round, as if the river had accepted something and signed for it.

Then that faded too, and there was only the pool, deep and clear and lit from within, and if you knew where to look — Felix knew where to look — you could just make out the pale shape of the hull far below, resting on the stones, at rest.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The river breathed. Somewhere upstream, the source sang its one high note.

It was Bramm who finally sat down. Just sat, right there in the wet grass, cap in his lap, and put his face in his huge hands, and his shoulders shook. Odile lowered herself beside him, awkward with her bandages, and leaned her head against his arm. Wren stood apart, facing the water, his lips moving silently — Felix caught fragments of it, an old cadence, words in the keepers' tongue that sounded like thanks and sounded like farewell and might have been both.

And Jonah stood at the waterline with her feet in the shallows, dry-eyed now, watching the place where her boat had been, and Felix watched her watch it, and understood that she was not saying goodbye. Goodbye was for things leaving. The Provision wasn't leaving. She was staying — here, in this pool, in this valley, part of the river she'd served and stolen for and finally paid for. Jonah would pass this water for the rest of her life.

"Captain," Felix said.

"Don't," she said. "Not today. Today I'm just Jonah, and she was just a boat, and I'm just a woman standing in a river with nothing but the clothes the water left me."

"You've got more than that."

"Oh?"

Felix counted on his fingers, because it was easier than counting on his heart. "An engineer who rebuilt your engine twice and cried into his hat just now, though he'll deny it. A cook who can feed eight men off a stove the size of a coffin and laughed at your funeral before it was even over. A hundred-year-old keeper who says the river likes you, and he'd know. And me." He shrugged. "You've got us. That's not nothing. That's — actually, that's most of everything."

Jonah looked at him for a long moment. The evening light came up the gorge and gilded the water behind her, and for the first time since he'd known her — three years of watching her scan every horizon for the next thing to outrun — she wasn't looking anywhere else. She was looking right here.

"No," she agreed quietly. "It's not nothing."

She waded back to the bank. She wrung out her trousers, sat down in the grass between Bramm and Odile, and accepted the strip of dried fish Odile produced from somewhere inside her shirt with the solemnity of a sacrament. They ate what little they had. The stars came out over the gorge rim, thick and close, reflected so perfectly in the clear new water that Felix couldn't tell where the sky ended and the river began, and decided it didn't matter.

It was nearly full dark when Wren spoke from his place apart, and his voice had changed — steadier, younger somehow, a man setting down a load he'd carried sixty years.

"There will be people coming," he said. "Soon. Word travels faster than boats on this river, and cleaner water travels fastest of all. The towns will want to see the source. The tolls are dead — you understand that? Every lock Sable bought, every charter she held, all of it dissolved with her claim. The valley belongs to itself for the first time in a century, and it doesn't know what to do with that." He turned. In the starlight his eyes found Felix first, then Jonah. "They'll need keepers again. Not owners. Stewards. And they'll need someone to tell them how a river is kept — and someone the towns already trust, someone who ran these waters when no one else dared."

Felix felt Jonah go still beside him.

"They'll ask," Wren said simply. "Both of you. And when they do—" the old man smiled, tired and sly as the river itself, "—you should decide tonight what you're going to say. Because tomorrow morning, I promise you, there's a boy on the far bank with a message from Bellhaven, and he's been waiting since sundown for the water to calm enough to cross."
