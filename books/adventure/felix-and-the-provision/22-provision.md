## 22. Provision

The keel went into the water on a morning so still that the river held the sky like a bowl holds water — perfectly, without spilling a drop.

Felix stood at the edge of the slipway with his palms pressed flat against the new timber, feeling the shudder run up through the wood as the Provision took her first taste of the Vell. She was smaller than the old boat. Everyone said so, in the careful voices people used around Jonah that first season, as if grief were a cargo that might shift and crush them all. But she was sounder, too — Bramm had overseen every joint, Odile had argued about the galley until it was bigger than anyone's sense of proportion allowed, and Wren had walked her length three times with his hand trailing along the rail like a man greeting an old friend wearing a new coat.

She slid, settled, rose. The river accepted her.

"There," Odile said beside him, wiping flour from her hands onto an apron that would never again be clean of anything else. "Now she's real."

Felix didn't answer right away. He was watching the water lap against the hull, watching how it moved — not pushing, not pulling, just meeting the boat the way a hand meets a hand. Nine months since the source. Nine months since he'd stood on the bank with nothing but wet clothes and a view of the mast going down, and Odile laughing through her tears because someone had to laugh or they'd all have drowned standing there in the sun.

He touched two fingers to the rail, the way his mother used to touch the doorframe when they left the house. *For luck,* she'd say. *For keeping.* He hadn't known then what the words meant. He knew now.

"Captain wants you at the wheel," Odile said, and there was something in her voice — a warmth with an edge of mischief under it, the way she always delivered news she thought would land harder than it looked.

"I'm not captain."

"No." She patted his shoulder once, briskly, and turned back toward the galley. "But you're at the wheel."

* * *

The new Provision had been built in Ashport, in the yard where Sable Corvane's hired guns had once bought loyalty with bread. That was over now — had been over almost before the floodwater finished running clean. When the heart went home to the source, everything Sable had built on her claim dissolved like sugar in current. The tolls vanished overnight. The lockkeepers woke to find their contracts void, their chains unlocked, their rivers their own again. Some towns threw festivals. Some just stood very quietly by the water for a long time, the way you do when a pain you've carried so long you forgot its name finally stops.

Sable herself had melted away the way hired things melt away. Felix heard stories sometimes — that she'd gone downriver, gone coastal, gone wherever money could buy a fresh start and no questions. Once, in a market in Bellhaven New, he'd seen a woman with her posture, her particular way of holding her chin, and his whole body had gone tight as rope before the woman turned and proved to be nobody at all. He wondered if Jonah ever saw ghosts like that. He suspected she did. He suspected she'd stopped flinching at them somewhere around the time she waded into the source with a burning stone in her arms and dared the river to take her.

It hadn't taken her. It had taken the boat instead, and everyone agreed afterward — Wren most solemnly, Bramm most grudgingly — that this was mercy dressed as payment. The river remembered what it was given. It also, apparently, remembered what was returned.

The valley had asked them to stay. That was the part Felix still turned over at night, the part that felt least like anything he'd earned. The towns sent word upriver through the autumn: come to Ashport, come to Bellhaven, let the keepers be restored. And Wren, gaunt and patient as driftwood, had sat with the town councils one by one and laid out terms that made some elders sputter. Stewards, not owners. No tolls, no claims, no power over any mile of water. Only memory. Only keeping watch. Only the promise that what happened to the source would never happen again because someone would be there, watching, remembering, answerable to the river and to the people both.

"They'll hate us within a generation," Wren told Felix once, on the walk between towns, his breath steaming in the cold. "That's how you'll know it's working. Power gets loved. Keeping gets resented. Resentment means we're not owning anybody."

Felix had thought about that for a long time. He was fifteen now, nearly sixteen, and keeper's blood ran thin in him — thin enough that the stone had looked at him across the amphitheater of the source and waited, patient as water, for his hand. Thin enough that when he stood at certain bends of the upper river now, he could feel something underneath the current, vast and slow and aware, turning its attention toward him the way a sleeping animal turns toward a familiar footstep.

His family had done this. Generations of them, faces he'd never know, names worn off the stones of drowned Bellhaven. They had kept the watch and died keeping it, and the river had flooded their town to hide its own stolen heart, and none of them — not one — had left a record that a boy could find and read. Just the poster in a river-town window: *REWARD FOR KEEPERS OF THE SOURCE.* He'd folded it into his pocket nine months ago and never unfolded it since. He didn't need to. He knew what it said. He knew what it meant.

He was the last of them unless he chose otherwise. So he chose otherwise. He said yes.

* * *

Jonah was at the rail when Felix came up from below, the way she was at the rail most mornings now, coffee in hand, watching the water go by with an expression he'd spent months learning to read and still only half understood.

The old expression — the one from the lower-Vell days, the restless one, the one that treated every bend as an escape route — was mostly gone. What replaced it was quieter. Harder to name. Sometimes he caught her looking at the banks as they passed, at the farmsteads and ferry crossings and kids swimming off the pilings in summer, and her face did something complicated, like a woman reading a letter she'd written long ago to someone she'd become.

"You wanted me at the wheel," Felix said.

"Mm." She didn't look over. "Bramm says the current's easy today. Says the boat practically steers herself." A pause. "I want to see what you do when nobody needs you to steer."

Felix took the wheel. It was warm under his hands — Bramm had installed a little stove pipe near the pilot house, a luxury the old Provision had never afforded — and the boat answered him with the lazy obedience of a creature that trusted where it was going. Downriver, toward the lower Vell, toward the towns where this whole story had started with a dockmaster's careless mention of traffic on forbidden water.

They passed the mouth of the narrows where Sable's gunboat had cornered them. The rapids beyond it, the ones no captain would attempt, looked almost gentle in the low summer water — white lace over dark stone, singing to itself. Odile's scar ached in cold weather, she said; today it wouldn't trouble her at all.

They passed the drowned town. Bellhaven's rooftops were visible again now, breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, and the valley had voted — after long argument, after Wren spoke for an afternoon and a night — to leave them there. A marker. A memory. Boats gave the place a wide berth out of respect, and children on the shore were taught the names of the streets that ran beneath the water. Felix looked at the rooftops and felt, for the first time, not dread but something adjacent to it. Recognition. Those had been his people's streets. The river had hidden them to hide its wound, and now that the wound was closed, the river seemed content to let the past lie where it lay, roof-ridge by roof-ridge, in plain sight.

"You're steering well," Jonah said.

"You're not watching me steer. You're watching the water."

"I'm doing both. Multitasking is a captain's disease. There's no cure." She drank her coffee. Then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe twice a month: "Do you remember the day I found you?"

Felix's hands stilled on the wheel, just for a breath, then resumed. "You didn't find me. I stowed away."

"You stowed away badly. I heard you sneezing in the line locker before we cleared the first bridge." She turned and leaned her back against the rail, facing him, the way she did when she'd decided a conversation deserved her whole attention. "Nine years I ran that river like it owed me something. Or like I owed it something and couldn't pay. Both, probably. And then a sneezing kid in a line locker, and I thought — well. Here's one more thing I can't outrun."

"You could have put me off at Marlow Bend."

"I could have put myself off at Marlow Bend, the number of times I thought about it." Her mouth did the thing that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one. "Every port, I thought it. This one's safer for him. This one's got a school. This one's got no posters on the walls." Her eyes flicked to him, sharp and soft at once. "I knew about your family before you did, Felix. Found out the second year. Kept it shut in my chest like ballast. Told myself I was protecting you."

"Were you?"

"No." She said it plainly, without flinching, and he understood that this was the discipline she'd learned at the source — the discipline of not lying to yourself even when it cost. "I was protecting me. You were the one good thing I'd done in nine years, and I figured if I never said your name out loud in the same sentence as *keeper*, the river couldn't take you back." She looked out at the water. "Rivers don't work that way. Took me losing a boat to learn it."

Felix steered. The current ran easy beneath the hull, and he could feel it — really feel it, the way he'd been able to feel it more clearly each month since the source — a great slow attention underneath the water, neither kind nor cruel, simply present, simply keeping. Like Wren said keeping should be.

"Do you regret it?" he asked. "The boat."

Jonah was quiet long enough that he glanced over. She was watching the wake unspool behind them, that long pale seam in the water that closed itself up as fast as it opened, leaving no record, no scar.

"I regret plenty," she said finally. "I regret the theft. I regret nine years of treating the Vell like a road instead of a home. I regret that Odile bled and Bramm aged ten years in one storm and you—" she stopped, started again. "But the boat? No. The boat was a debt paid. Debts aren't regrets, Felix. They're just the price of finally stopping."

"Stopping," Felix repeated. He tested the word. For nine years — for all the time he'd known her — stopping had been the thing Jonah Brightwater feared worse than drowning. Every port was a departure. Every calm was a held breath. He'd watched her sleep with one boot on, once, and understood even then that she was a woman waiting for the knock.

"And here we are," he said, "going four knots down the middle of the river with nowhere to be."

"Disgraceful," Jonah agreed. "A captain should always be fleeing something. It keeps the crew honest."

But she was smiling now, actually smiling, and she came and stood beside him at the wheel rather than leaning on the rail apart from it, and together they watched the lower Vell open ahead of them — wide, brown-gold, unhurried, dotted with the small honest traffic of a free river: a grain barge, a pair of fishing skiffs, a ferry crossing with its cable taut and singing. No gunboats. No sealed cargoes. No locks bought and chained. Just water doing what water does when nobody claims it.

Odile's bell rang from the galley — lunch, and lunch on the new Provision was not a suggestion — and Bramm's head appeared briefly from the engine hatch, gray-stubbled and grease-smudged, nodded once at the smoothness of the running, and disappeared again, which from Bramm was a parade.

Wren came up last, moving slowly along the rail, and stopped at the stern where he liked to stand, facing backward, watching where they'd been. Felix had asked him once why. Wren had said: *A keeper watches both directions. That's the whole job.* Today the old man's face was peaceful, and when the wind lifted his hair Felix could see the source-stone's faint mark on his temple — the pale whorl that all the old keepers had carried, and that now, faintly, was beginning to show on Felix's own skin, like a watermark surfacing in paper.

The river ran easy beneath them. Felix realized, with a small quiet shock, that he couldn't remember the last time he'd dreamed of drowning.

* * *

Toward evening they tied up at a landing that had no name worth repeating and no reason for their stop except that Jonah wanted mussels from a man who sold them off a flat-bottomed skiff, and Odile wanted to argue with the man about his prices, and Bramm wanted whatever Odile was cooking regardless. The mooring lines went out. The gangplank dropped. And the Provision sat still in the golden water, rocking gently, going nowhere, needing to go nowhere, and nothing about the stillness cost anything at all.

Felix stood on the deck while the others went ashore, and he rested his hands on the rail, and he felt the river hold the boat the way the morning sky had been held — perfectly, without spilling.

Jonah came back up the gangplank alone, earlier than the rest, carrying a paper sack of mussels and something else folded under her arm. She crossed the deck and stood beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they'd gotten good at that, the two of them. Silence had stopped being a wall between them somewhere in the last reach of the upper river and had become, instead, a room they could share.

Finally she handed him the folded thing. Cloth. Old cloth, oilcloth, stiff with age.

He unwrapped it and went still.

The nameboard. The original one — scorched along one edge, water-darkened, the gilt letters faded but legible, dredged up months ago by a salvage crew working the pool below the source and sent downriver with a note: *Thought she belonged to you.*

PROVISION.

"She's yours, you know," Jonah said quietly. "Not mine. I named the first one after a promise I was running from. You're the one who kept it." She tapped the board once with her finger, right on the O. "This goes on the new boat. Tonight, before Odile gets sentimental about it and makes a speech."

Felix held the board and couldn't speak for a moment. The letters were warm under his thumb, or his hands were warm, or the evening was — it hardly mattered which.

"We could mount it tomorrow," he managed. "At the dock. Properly."

"We could." Jonah looked out at the water, at the last light lying along it like a path of hammered gold leading everywhere and nowhere in particular. "Or we could nail it up tonight, right over the pilot house, and in the morning we'll point her upstream. Wren says there's a stretch above the old source country nobody's mapped since the floods. Says the river's changed its mind about some of its channels." She smiled, slow and real, a woman with nowhere to flee and no reason left to run. "Thought we might go see what it remembers."

Felix looked at the nameboard, and at the river, and at her — this hard, scarred, stubborn woman who had once been the most restless soul on the whole Vell and who now stood at a rail with nowhere to be, asking him, in her sideways fashion, whether he'd stay aboard.

"Yeah," he said. "Let's go see."

Above them, the new nameboard caught the last of the light. Below them, the river ran on, easy and awake, carrying them gently toward whatever came next — and for the first time in nine years, for the first time in either of their lives, going there was not the same as getting away.

It was just going home, at four knots, with the whole evening ahead of them.
