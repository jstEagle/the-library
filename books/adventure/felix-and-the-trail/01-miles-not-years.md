## 1. Miles, Not Years

The river didn't care what name you gave it. That was the first thing Sebastian Amberdale had learned about the Salween, and the last thing he still believed in.

"Two strokes left," he called over the engine noise. "Then hold."

The six tourists in the raft did what tourists always did — three of them paddled hard, two paddled in the wrong direction, and one, a Dutch banker named Cor with sunburned shins and a GoPro strapped to his forehead, simply stopped and filmed. Sebastian adjusted for all of it without thinking, leaning his weight against the current the way a man leans into an old argument he's already won a hundred times.

The rapid came up fast and brown, a standing wave the locals called the Widow's Tooth because it had, allegedly, taken somebody's husband sometime before anyone now living could remember. It was a Class III on a good day, a Class IV when the snowmelt ran heavy, and today it was somewhere between — enough to make the banker scream with delight, enough to soak everyone to the skin, not nearly enough to matter.

That was the point. That was the whole architecture of his life now: water that thrilled but never threatened. Rivers where the worst thing that could happen was a lost paddle and a bruised ego. He'd spent ten years building exactly this, rapid by rapid, contract by contract, a career made entirely of almost-danger, and he could run the Salween's commercial stretch in his sleep because he had, more than once, done precisely that.

"Widow's Tooth," he announced, because the tourists liked the name, liked the little frisson of mortality it gave them at a price they could afford. "Paddle on my count. Three. Two—"

The raft hit the wave and the world went white and loud and then, as always, mercifully ordinary again. Cor was whooping. A woman from Manchester had lost her hat. The river carried them all downstream without comment, the way it carried everything — silt, secrets, the occasional drowned man — with the same indifferent muscle.

Sebastian counted the rapid. Rapid forty-one of the season. He didn't count years anymore. Years required looking backward, and backward lay a map he never unfolded.

* * *

The dock at Ban Mae San was half wood, half rust, entirely his. He'd leased it from a widow who asked no questions, which was the most valuable feature of the property. His company — if three rafts, a truck with a failing transmission, and a laminated sign could be called a company — was called Amberdale River Runs, which was itself a small act of cowardice, since *Amberdale* wasn't the name anyone would be searching for. The blacklist databases held him under a different surname, one he'd shed like wet clothing a decade ago and never once missed.

He tied off the raft, paid the two local guides he hired per-run — cash, no paperwork beyond a receipt book from a stationery shop in Chiang Rai — and sent the tourists up the bank toward the guesthouse where they'd drink warm beer and exaggerate the Widow's Tooth until it became a Class V in the retelling.

Cor lingered, peeling off his helmet. "Good run," the Dutchman said. "You've done this a while, ja?"

"A while."

"You have the look. Like a man who reads water the way I read spreadsheets." Cor laughed at his own comparison. "Where did you learn? Alps? Norway?"

"Here and there."

"No, really. Where?"

Sebastian smiled the smile he kept for questions like this, the one that was friendly and closed as a shop door. "Here and there," he said again, and clapped the man on the shoulder, and that was the end of it. It always was. People accepted a guide without a past the way they accepted a river without a source — it bothered them for a moment, and then the beer arrived.

He watched them go, then stood alone on the dock doing the thing he did every evening, the ritual that had replaced every other ritual he'd ever had: he looked upstream.

Not far. Just to the bend, where the Salween narrowed and the jungle pressed in and the water went from tourist-brown to something older and greener. Upstream was where rivers kept their beginnings, and beginnings were the one commodity Sebastian Amberdale refused to trade in. He looked at the bend the way other men looked at old photographs — briefly, hungrily, and then away.

Ten years ago he had been a different man with a different name, and that man had loved maps. Had owned them, studied them, drawn corrections on them in pencil. That man had led expeditions up mountains whose names still lived in magazines, in lawsuits, in a single line of official finding that read *guide error, contributing factor*, four words that had ended everything and explained nothing, because the truth was a thing Sebastian had swallowed whole and it had been sitting in his chest ever since like a stone he couldn't cough up.

So: miles instead of years. Rapids instead of birthdays. He was forty-one years old and he knew it only because he'd counted the rapids — forty-one commercial runs this season, one for each year he declined to celebrate. It was a stupid system. It was the only system he had.

His cook, a laconic Shan woman named Hseng who worked the guesthouse kitchen and tolerated his existence with professional indifference, had told him once: *"You count everything except what matters."*

She wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with people who weren't wrong.

* * *

That night the letter was waiting at the guesthouse, which was strange, because nobody wrote to him. Letters required addresses, addresses required names, names required histories, and he had spent a decade making sure his paper trail was thin as monsoon light. What mail he got came by email, to an account under the name S. Amber, and consisted of deposit confirmations and the occasional invoice.

This was an envelope. Actual paper. Thick, cream-colored, expensive — the kind of stock that announced, before you broke the seal, that the sender had money and wanted you to know it. His name was typed on the front: *S. Amberdale*. Not Amber. *Amberdale.*

He stood in the hallway holding it for a long moment, listening to the rain start up on the tin roof, feeling the way a man feels when he steps on ground that should be solid and hears, faintly, a hollow sound underneath.

Hseng passed him with a stack of dishes. She glanced at the envelope, then at his face, and said nothing, which was her gift.

He took it to his room — a concrete box with a bed, a fan, and a locked footlocker he'd owned for ten years and opened twice — and sat on the bed and turned the envelope over. No return address. Postmarked Bangkok. Inside, something stiff shifted against the paper, and he thought: photographs, or a card, or —

Or a map.

His hands knew it before his mind admitted it. The weight, the fold, the particular slide of folded paper against an envelope's throat. Ten years of avoiding maps and here one was, delivered to his door, addressed in his real name by someone who clearly knew both names and didn't care that he did.

He opened it.

A card first, heavy stock, a single line of type:

*Mr. Amberdale — Some cargo is worth more than the river that holds it. I need a man who knows the Nam Kha. You are the only one I can find who won't say no. Come talk. M.V.*

And beneath the card, folded in quarters, the map.

He didn't want to unfold it. He heard himself think that, plainly, the way you hear your own voice in a quiet room: *I don't want to unfold it.* And then he unfolded it, because he was who he was, because ten years of discipline was apparently worth exactly one cream-colored envelope.

The Nam Kha.

It rose out of the page at him — the river, the gorge, the tributaries marked in someone's careful hand, and above the northernmost bend, printed small and merciless, the contour lines climbing into the massif he had not looked at, not once, not in a photograph or a dream he'd admit to, in ten years. The mountain had no name on this map. Whoever had prepared it had known better than to print the name. They'd just drawn the shape of it, and the shape was enough. The shape was burned into him at the same depth as his own signature.

Elias Thorne had died below that mountain. On it, technically — eight hundred meters below the summit ridge, in a whiteout, roped to two other men, one of whom had come down and one of whom hadn't. Sebastian had come down. That was the whole of his biography now, reduced to its cruelest verb. *Came down.* The inquiry had used fancier language. The blacklist had needed none.

He sat with the map until the fan had turned its slow circle a hundred times and the rain had gone from drumming to steady. Somewhere below, the Salween kept moving, keeping its own count of things.

*M.V.* He knew those initials the way you know a scar. Marguerite Voss. Rival wasn't the right word — rivals competed, and Marguerite had never needed to compete with him; she'd simply been better funded, better connected, and ruthless in the specific way of people who treat ambition as a moral duty. They'd shared stages at expedition conferences in another life. She'd once told him, in front of thirty people, that his routes were elegant and his judgment sentimental, and she'd meant both as insults, and both had landed.

She knew about the blacklist. Everyone in the industry knew about the blacklist; it was the kind of thing that got whispered into contracts as a clause. But she knew his real name. She knew the Nam Kha. She knew — the card said it plainly enough — that he wouldn't say no.

And that was the part that made him set the map face-down on the bed and stand up and walk to the window and watch the rain, because she was right, and she knew she was right, and some cold efficient part of her had calculated it to the decimal: whatever Sebastian Amberdale had built out of miles and avoidance, it had one load-bearing weakness, and her letter had found it on the first strike.

*Won't say no.* Because the Nam Kha was the river below the mountain. Because somewhere along that river, in the country he'd been barred from and the profession he'd been barred from and the decade he'd been barred from, there was a crate or a wreck or a grave or a truth, and Marguerite Voss knew which, and he didn't.

He picked the map back up. Forced himself to look at the mountain's blank space where its name should be, the cartographic courtesy of an omission that felt worse than any label.

Rapid forty-one, he thought. One for each year.

Tomorrow he'd tell Hseng to cancel the bookings. Tomorrow he'd dig the footlocker out from under the bed and open it for the third time in ten years. Tomorrow he'd get on a bus to Bangkok with a cream-colored envelope in his jacket pocket and a stone in his chest that had just, for the first time in a decade, moved.

Tonight he stood at the window a while longer, counting the rain, because it was the only thing left he knew how to count.
