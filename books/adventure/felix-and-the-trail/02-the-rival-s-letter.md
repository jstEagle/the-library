## 2. The Rival's Letter

The letter had been sitting in my inbox for eleven days before I opened it, which was a record even for me.

I have a system for mail. Bills get paid the day they arrive — a habit from the blacklist years, when a missed payment in Chiang Rai could turn into a deportation order in Vientiane. Fan letters get answered eventually, usually by Tomas, who enjoys impersonating me more than I enjoy being myself. And anything with a return address I don't recognize goes into a drawer at the bottom of the chart table, unopened, where it can't hurt anyone.

This one had no return address. Just a postmark from Luang Prabang and my name — not *Sebastian*, but *S. Amberdale* — typed on heavy cream paper that cost more per sheet than I made in a day. Heavy paper is either an invitation or a threat. I'd learned to wait and see which.

So it sat in the drawer while I ran three tourist trips down the Salween, counting rapids instead of birthdays, and it might have sat there until the rains if Marguerite Voss hadn't decided to stop writing and start showing up.

She arrived on a Tuesday morning, at low water, while I was hosing mud off the Meridian's deck. I heard her before I saw her — the particular sound of expensive shoes finding the one dry plank on a wet dock — and I knew the walk before I turned around. Ten years hadn't changed it. She walked like a woman who had already decided how the conversation would end and was simply giving you the courtesy of participating.

"Sebastian." She said it like she was testing whether the name still fit.

"Margo." I didn't give her the satisfaction of surprise. "You're a long way from Kathmandu."

"I'm a long way from everywhere. That's rather the point of me." She looked at the boat, then at the river, then at me, in that order, and I felt the assessment land like a checklist. "Still running tourists."

"Still running tourists."

"The Salween in March. Class twos and a sandbar picnic." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You used to call water like this bathwater."

"You used to call clients collateral. One of us has grown."

The smile finished forming. It didn't reach her eyes; it never had, not once in the fourteen years I'd known her, back when we were both young enough to think the mountains cared who summited them. We'd come up through the same guiding houses in the same seasons, taken the same certifications, drunk at the same terrible bars in Thamel, and competed for the same clients with a ferocity that everyone around us found hilarious and neither of us ever found funny. When the Thorne expedition went down, she'd been leading her own group on the opposite face. She'd watched the weather close over our mountain from two kilometers away, and afterward she'd said things to journalists — careful, reasonable things about decision-making under pressure and the responsibilities of lead guides — that helped take my licenses away.

I had never decided whether she'd done it out of conviction or ambition. I suspected she'd never decided either.

"I need a crew," she said.

"You've got a crew. You've got three crews. You've got sponsors' logos on your kayaks."

"Had." She stepped closer to the rail, and for the first time since she'd arrived she looked at something other than me — downriver, where the brown water folded around the pilings. "Things change. People retire. Partners die."

Something moved behind her voice when she said *partners*. I filed it away without examining it, the way you pocket a strange coin on a trail because you don't have time to look at it properly.

"I'm not interested," I said. "Whatever it is."

"You haven't asked what it is."

"I don't have to. You flew here — or took the long bus, which is worse — to find a blacklisted guide running class twos for honeymooners. Whatever you're offering, it's either illegal or cursed, and I've had my fill of both."

"It's salvage." She reached into her jacket — a good jacket, waxed canvas, the kind that lasts — and produced a folder, cream-colored, matching the letter I still hadn't opened. She set it on the rail between us like a card played face-up. "A wreck on the Nam Kha. Upstream of the concession line. There's a sealed crate in the hold that has to come out before the monsoon floods the gorge, and the permits that let anyone touch it expire with the first rise. Six weeks, maybe less. I need a boat, a diver, an engineer, and someone who can read that river."

"And you came to me."

"I came to the best river man within two thousand miles, which is an embarrassing sentence for both of us, but there it is."

I picked up the folder. I didn't open it. Opening it would be a step, and steps are how you end up places.

"Why me?" I said. "Really. You hate me. You testified —"

"I gave a statement." Her jaw tightened, just barely. "I described what the reports showed. If the reports were wrong, Sebastian, that was never my doing. But you'll notice I'm the only person from that world who's standing on your dock."

It was a good line. It was also a deflection, and we both knew it, and knowing it together created a strange little pocket of honesty between us, the kind rivals can afford that friends can't.

"The Nam Kha," I said.

"The Nam Kha."

"That's north Laos. That's —" I stopped myself before I said *below the mountain*, because saying it aloud would hand her something, and I had spent ten years learning never to hand Marguerite Voss anything for free. "That's restricted water. Concession territory. Half those gorges haven't been run since the French left."

"Which is why the pay is what it is." She tapped the folder once with one finger. "Open it."

I opened it.

The contract inside was clean — cleaner than clean, drafted by someone who understood exactly which clauses a blacklisted guide would look for. Liability held by her company, not me. Payment in three tranches, the last on delivery. A confidentiality section so thorough it amounted to: tell no one, including yourself, preferably. And a route map, printed on the same heavy cream stock, showing the Nam Kha from the concession post up through the gorges to a marked point on a bend where the river went narrow and green and deep.

I knew that bend. I had studied that bend. I had, for ten years, in hostels and ferry cabins and rented rooms across four countries, avoided looking at maps of that bend, and now here it was, rendered in precise cartographic detail, with a small red X where a boat had gone down.

"You know whose wreck that is," I said.

"I know whose ledger is in that crate."

"A ledger."

"An expedition ledger. Sealed. Private property of the estate." She said it smoothly, but I caught the seam in it — the half-second where she chose her words instead of speaking them. "There are people who want it recovered intact, and people who very much want it not recovered at all, and I am being paid by the former to outrun the latter. That's all you need to know."

"That's nowhere near all I need to know."

"No," she agreed. "But it's all you're getting today."

We stood there while the river went on doing what rivers do, which is pretend to be patient. A long-tail puttered past on the far bank, some kid hauling nets, and I watched him instead of her because looking at him was easier.

Here is what I should tell you about myself, since I'm apparently telling everything eventually: I am very good at not asking questions. It's the core competency of my entire adult life. When the inquiry board asked what happened above Camp Three, I said the mountain took him, which was true the way a locked room is empty. When other guides asked why I never appealed the blacklist, I said appeals cost money. When Tomas asked, once, gently, somewhere off the Mekong at night, I told him a story about weather that was true and also not the answer. Not-asking is a discipline. It's kept me fed and free and moving for a decade.

Marguerite stood on my dock holding a map of the one question I had spent ten years not asking, and she knew it, and she was letting me sit in it.

"You knew I'd say yes," I said finally.

"I knew you'd say no twice and yes once. I budgeted for the noes." She took the folder back, folded it closed, and held it against her chest — not giving it to me yet, making me want it, because she has always understood that I am a man who can refuse almost anything except wanting. "Three weeks from now the permits post downstream and the window opens. I need your answer by Friday, and I need your boat, your engineer if you have one worth the name, and your discretion. In exchange —"

"In exchange for what? The money's good but it isn't *that* good. Nobody pays that rate for a crate of paperwork."

For a moment she looked at me with something I hadn't seen from her in years — not the old contempt, not the professional polish, but a kind of tired attention, as if she'd been carrying this offer a long way and was glad to finally set part of it down.

"In exchange," she said, "for the chance to find out what actually happened up there. Because I've read every report, Sebastian, and I've talked to every survivor, and there were only two survivors, and one of them won't speak, and the other one is standing in front of me pretending he doesn't know exactly which bend that red X is on."

The river made its noise. The kid with the nets disappeared around the far bend. Somewhere behind me Tomas dropped a coil of rope on the deck, loud as a gunshot, and neither of us flinched, which is its own kind of confession.

"They'll ask questions," I said. "Concession officials. They'll want names on the manifest."

"Then you'll need a name to give them." She let that hang in the air between us, delicate and cruel, the way only she could. "Whatever name you've been using down here. Whatever you've been calling yourself for ten years. It ends at that permit post. You understand that. This job doesn't work unless the man who signs for the cargo is the man the mountain remembers."

And there it was. Not blackmail — she was too smart for blackmail, and too proud. Something worse: an accurate description. She had found the exact shape of the door I'd bricked over and was standing in front of it with a contract instead of a crowbar.

I thought about saying no. I want to be honest about that — I genuinely considered it, ran the numbers, priced out another season of class twos and sandbar picnics and letters in drawers. It would have been easy. Ease is the one thing I've always been able to afford.

But she'd said *the man the mountain remembers*, and underneath the resentment — ten years of it, layered like sediment, each season depositing a little more — something shifted. Not warmth. Nothing so kind. More like a current changing under a boat you'd thought was anchored: the sudden, sickening knowledge that the ground you chose was never ground at all, just water you'd stopped noticing was moving.

"Friday," I said.

"Friday." She shook my hand. Her grip was dry and strong and brief. Then she paused, one foot already toward the gangplank, and looked back with the expression of someone placing a bet she'd already hedged. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth coming from me — I always thought the reports were wrong. All of them. Every page."

"Then why help bury me?"

"Because I was twenty-nine and hungry, and because you never gave me anything else to believe." She said it without apology, which I respected, and without cruelty, which surprised me. "You could have fixed that any time in ten years, Sebastian. You chose miles instead."

She walked down the plank with that same unhurried certainty, shoes finding the dry boards, and got into a waiting car that had no business being on that road, and was gone.

I stood on the dock for a long time after. Long enough that Tomas came out to see what I was staring at, and I handed him the folder and told him to wake the others, we had a meeting at sundown. He read the route map upside down in my hands, and I watched his eyes travel upstream along the blue line of the Nam Kha to the red X, and I watched him decide — wisely, loyally — not to ask.

But I couldn't not ask anymore. That was the thing. That was the trap of it.

That night I dug the cream envelope out of the chart-table drawer and opened it at last, eleven days late. Inside was a single sheet, no salutation, no signature. Just a line typed in the center:

*The crate contains his handwriting. Some of it is about you.*

I read it four times. Then I went out onto the deck, where the Salween was running dark and patient under a moon doing its best, and I realized Marguerite had never needed to convince me at all.

She'd only needed to make sure I understood that the river I'd been avoiding for ten years had been flowing toward me the whole time.

Sundown was tomorrow. We had a crew to assemble, and forty miles of bad water between me and a bend I could draw from memory with my eyes shut.

I'd said yes once. Now I had to live in it.
