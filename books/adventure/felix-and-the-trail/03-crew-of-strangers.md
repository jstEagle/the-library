## 3. Crew of Strangers

Tomas Reyes had worked for men who lied about money and men who lied about women, and he'd learned to tell the difference by the way they held a cigarette. Sebastian Amberdale didn't smoke. He stood at the rail of the *Meridian* at six in the morning, watching the river the way other men watched a door they were afraid someone would come through, and Tomas decided in that first hour that this was a man lying about something bigger.

The job posting had been simple enough: four weeks upstream, salvage contract, hazard pay double the going rate. No destination named. That alone should have been enough to walk away. In ten years on the water — Mekong, Salween, Irrawaddy tributaries too small to have names tourists would remember — Tomas had learned that unnamed destinations meant unnamed trouble, and unnamed trouble meant somebody downstream was paying good money not to be found.

He needed the money anyway. That was the arithmetic of his life now, three years since Chiang Rai, and the numbers never came out clean no matter how many times he ran them.

* * *

Priya Nair arrived first, which surprised nobody who'd ever met her. She came down the dock at 5:40 with a canvas tool roll over one shoulder and a duffel over the other, looked at the *Meridian*'s engine housing for maybe ninety seconds without touching it, and said, "Who ran her last?"

"Previous owner," Sebastian said. "Charter work, coastal."

"She was run angry." Priya set her bags down and opened the tool roll with the economy of a surgeon laying out instruments. "Somebody pushed her past what she wanted to give and then blamed her for the noise."

Tomas, coiling line nearby, felt something loosen in his chest. He'd known engineers who talked about engines like machinery. Priya talked about them like animals that had been mistreated, and he trusted that instinct more than any credential.

She was forty, maybe, compact and gray-streaked, with forearms that said she'd spent decades doing the work other people supervised. When Sebastian offered the contract she read every line of it standing up, in the sun, while the dock cats wove around her ankles, and then she signed it and said only, "The pay is bad and you know it's bad, which means you're desperate or hiding something. I'm both, so we'll suit each other."

Sebastian almost smiled. Almost. It was like watching a man decide against a door.

Tomas knew the shape of desperation when he saw it because he wore it himself. His debts weren't dramatic — no loan sharks with baseball bats, nothing you could put in a story. Just numbers that grew while he slept, interest compounding in some office in Bangkok where a man named Somchai kept a ledger with Tomas's name in it and a patience that was running out the way a tide runs out. The salvage money would clear half. Half was a country he hadn't lived in for years.

* * *

Okonkwo came in on the afternoon ferry from the south, a big man moving with the deliberate calm of someone who had made peace with his own size. He carried one bag and a hard case that clinked, and when he stepped onto the deck he did it the way Tomas imagined other men stepped into churches.

"You're the diver," Sebastian said.

"I'm the diver." Okonkwo shook his hand and held it a beat longer than necessary, reading him. "You've got cold water in your history. I can always tell. Men who've worked warm rivers their whole lives hold a boat differently."

"Cold water?"

"Glacier melt. Snowmelt. Water that doesn't forgive." Okonkwo let go of his hand. "Doesn't matter. I'm not here to interview you. I'm here because the money is absurd and I need it, and because a man who pays absurd money for a quiet job is either a fool or frightened, and fools don't last long enough to hire divers."

"And which do you think I am?"

Okonkwo considered him with genuine attention, the way he might consider a knot before trusting it. "Frightened," he said finally. "But competent. Competent and frightened is survivable. I've built a career on it."

He signed without reading the contract. Tomas noticed that. Noticed also the way Okonkwo's eyes went, once, quick and involuntary, to the river's southern bend — the direction of the deep channels, the drowned things. There was a story there. Everybody boarding the *Meridian* that week seemed to be carrying a story they'd packed carefully so it wouldn't rattle.

Okonkwo's own reason surfaced on the second evening, unprompted, in the galley, over Anselm's rice. He'd been paid well once, he said, working salvage off platforms in the Caspian, and he'd been saving for his daughter's schooling in Lagos when a company restructure ate eleven months of wages and left him holding a termination letter and a plane ticket home he couldn't afford to use. "So now I dive for men like you," he said, not bitterly, just factually, the way you'd describe weather. "Bad money is still money. My daughter does not care where dignity went. She cares about school fees."

Anselm, washing dishes, said nothing. Anselm mostly said nothing.

* * *

The cook had appeared without anyone hiring him, which was the first strange thing. Tomas had handled crew logistics on a dozen boats and he knew how cooks got hired — last, cheapest, and usually after an argument. But when he'd asked Sebastian about provisioning, Sebastian had said, "Handled," and two days later there was Anselm on the dock with two crates of food stores and a knife roll wrapped in oilcloth, saying he understood the position was filled.

"By whom?" Tomas asked later, in the wheelhouse, keeping his voice down.

"By me." Sebastian didn't look up from the charts he was pretending to study. Tomas had noticed he studied charts of rivers far from wherever they actually were, as if familiarizing himself with places he intended never to go. "He comes recommended."

"Recommended by who?"

"Someone who owed me." And that was all, delivered flat, a door closed gently but completely.

Anselm himself was sixty-ish, lean, with the kind of face that had stopped registering expressions sometime around a decade ago. He cooked simply and superbly — rice, fish, greens, a sour soup he made from tamarind and memory — and he moved through the boat like a man visiting somewhere he'd been before and hoped not to be recognized. He ate last, alone, standing. He listened to everything. Once, when Priya was cursing a fuel line in three languages, Tomas caught Anselm smiling faintly at the deck, and the smile had recognition in it, the look of a man hearing a song from his twenties.

On the fourth night, Tomas brought him a beer he hadn't asked for and sat across the galley table, and after a while Anselm said, "You're trying to figure me out."

"I figure everybody out. Habit."

"Don't bother. I'm the least interesting thing on this boat." He turned the bottle slowly. "I worked expeditions. High altitude, a long time ago. Doctor, cook, whatever was needed. Then I stopped." A pause with weight in it. "There are jobs you leave and jobs that end. Mine ended."

"What happened?"

Anselm looked at him then, and his eyes were old in a way that had nothing to do with age. "Ask the captain," he said. "It's his story more than mine. I'm just the man who was there when it finished." He drank, set the bottle down with care. "The rice will be better tomorrow. I found proper galangal at the market."

And that was that. Tomas lay in his bunk that night assembling the picture the way he assembled everything — debtors' faces, tide tables, the small tells of men lying about cargo — and the pieces wouldn't sit together. A captain blacklisted from somewhere, though nobody said where. A cook who'd been on the expedition that ended him. Hazard pay for a salvage run to an unnamed river. Marguerite Voss's name on the contract, a woman Tomas had never met whose signature sat at the bottom like a hook waiting for a mouth.

* * *

It was Priya who found the first crack, though she didn't know it was a crack. On the fifth day, running engine checks, she called Tomas into the engine room and showed him the maintenance logbook.

"Look at the fuel entries."

Tomas looked. Numbers in a neat hand, dates, hours logged.

"Now look at the handwriting on the route notes in the front. Same pen, different pressure. Somebody wrote those route notes recently and backdated the ink dryness in their head." She tapped the page. "This log's been edited. Not much. Just enough to make our course look routine."

"Maybe the previous owner—"

"The previous owner's entries stop eight months ago. These edits are weeks old. Fresh." She closed the book and looked at Tomas with level, unaccusing eyes. "I'm not saying anything except that I'm saying it out loud so it's been said. You're the deckhand. Deckhands hear things."

Deckhands heard things, yes. And what Tomas had heard, in fragments, in the wheelhouse late at night when Sebastian thought everyone slept, was the captain talking quietly into the radio — not transmitting, just rehearsing, saying coordinates under his breath like a man memorizing a confession. Coordinates that matched no tourist run on any chart Tomas knew. Coordinates that pointed north and west, up into country where the rivers came down white and mean out of mountains, and where the permits, if any existed, would be the kind you didn't advertise.

He should have said something. Instead he did what he always did with knowledge that might be worth something: he kept it. Not to sell — not yet, not exactly — but because a man with debts learns that information is the only currency that appreciates while you sleep, and Somchai's patience was a clock ticking somewhere behind Tomas's ribs day and night.

He hated himself a little for it. He hated himself in the manageable way, the way you hate a callus.

* * *

The night before departure, the whole crew ended up on deck, which hadn't been planned and wouldn't have happened if anyone had planned it. Anselm had made the sour soup and there was beer, and the heat had broken into something almost kind, and the river below the dock moved dark and patient toward the sea.

Okonkwo told a story about a dive gone wrong in the Caspian, forty meters down in water like cold tea, and how he'd learned that panic is just oxygen burned stupidly. Priya told one about an engine fire in the Andaman Sea and the passenger who tried to save his luggage before his children. Even Anselm offered a fragment — a joke about altitude cooking, pressure pots and thin air — and then went quiet again, but a warmer quiet than before.

Then Priya, flushed slightly with beer, pointed her bottle at the wheelhouse, where Sebastian's silhouette stood at the window. "Our captain," she said. "Ten to one he doesn't sleep tonight either. Ten to one he's standing there doing sums he doesn't like."

"What kind of sums?" Tomas asked.

"The kind where you count what a thing costs against what it pays, and the thing costs more, and you go anyway." She shrugged. "I know the arithmetic. I did it getting on this boat."

"We all did," Okonkwo said. "That's why the pay is bad and we took it. Bad money finds bad moments." He raised his bottle an inch. "To bad money."

"To bad money," Priya echoed.

Anselm lifted his bottle without speaking, and after a moment Tomas did too, and the toast hung there in the dark over the water, four strangers bound by need and a contract none of them fully believed, drinking to the only honest thing among them.

Tomas stayed up after the others turned in. He sat on the coil of mooring line at the stern and watched the dock lights shiver on the current, and he let himself think clearly for five minutes, which was more than he'd allowed in months.

Here was what he knew: the destination was secret. The log was altered. The cook knew the captain's worst story and had crossed an ocean, or at least a region, to stand in his galley. The pay was double market rate for work that shouldn't warrant it. And Marguerite Voss — a name that made Sebastian's jaw tighten whenever radio traffic mentioned her — was the money behind it.

Here was what he didn't know: any of the reasons.

A lesser man would have walked. Tomas had walked from jobs before; walking was a skill like any other. But Somchai's ledger didn't accept walking as payment, and besides — and this was the part he examined least, turning it over quickly like a coin he didn't want to read — some stubborn, stupid part of him wanted to see the thing through. Wanted to know what a man like Sebastian Amberdale, who watched rivers like old wounds, was so afraid of finding upstream.

In the morning they cast off. The *Meridian* took the current heading north, against the grain of the water, into the narrowing green throat of the country, and Tomas worked the bow lines and felt the boat lean into the fight and thought: whatever's up there, we've already been paid to find it.

By noon, Sebastian had changed the filed course on the chart table — casually, thinking no one watched — and the new penciled line ran up a river whose name Tomas had to look for twice to believe.

The Nam Kha.

Tomas copied the coordinates into the back of his own notebook, in small careful figures, the way a man saves a ladder he might need to climb down later.

He didn't yet know who he'd sell them to. He only knew that someday soon, someone would pay.
