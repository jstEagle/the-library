## 4. Upstream

The Meridian burned diesel like a drunk burns money, and Priya loved her anyway.

She knelt in the engine room at dawn, sleeves rolled to the elbow, one hand flat against the block to read the vibration the way a doctor reads a pulse. Too much shudder on the third cylinder. Not dangerous yet. But the old girl was complaining, and Priya had learned long ago that machines, like people, told you the truth early and only once.

"Talk to me," she murmured, and pressed her ear to the housing.

The engine talked. It always did. Forty years of service in some Malaysian logging concern before Sebastian bought her for less than the price of a used motorcycle, and every one of those years lived in her joints. Priya had rebuilt the fuel injectors herself, re-wound the starter motor with wire she'd traded a flashlight for in Vientiane, replaced the raw-water pump twice. She knew this engine better than she'd ever known a man, and it had never once lied to her.

People were another matter.

She wiped her hands on a rag that had started life white and climbed back into the grey morning light. The river had changed overnight. Yesterday they'd been running the wide brown reaches below the confluence, banks so far apart you could barely shout across them, fishing boats waving as they passed. Today the gorges had begun. Limestone walls rose sheer on either side, close enough that the current compressed between them and stood up in ridges, like muscle under skin. The Meridian pushed against it at half throttle, bow lifted, engine laboring, and the sound of the water came off the rock walls doubled and strange.

Tomas was at the wheel. He steered well — she'd give him that. Quick hands, good instincts, the kind of boat sense you couldn't teach. But he'd been at the wheel since four in the morning without relief, and he hadn't called anyone to take over, and when Priya came up beside him he didn't quite look at her.

"You should sleep," she said.

"Later."

"That's what you said last night."

"I'm fine." His eyes stayed on the channel ahead, where the current piled white around a submerged spur of rock. "I like the morning water. It's honest."

Priya leaned against the rail and studied him sideways. Twenty-four, maybe twenty-five. Calloused hands that hadn't come from deck work — too fine, too careful, the hands of someone who'd done delicate work once and was paying off something now with his body. He'd shown up at the dock with a duffel bag and no questions, which should have been a comfort after years of hiring men who asked too many. Instead it sat wrong in her chest, the way an unbalanced load sits wrong in a hull.

Sebastian appeared from the aft cabin with two mugs of coffee, handed her one without asking how she took it. Black, three sugars. He remembered. That was the thing about Sebastian Amberdale nobody who'd only heard the blacklist stories would believe: he paid attention.

"How's she running?" He nodded at the deck beneath their feet.

"She'll hold." Priya blew on her coffee. "But we're burning thirty percent more fuel than the plan allows. This current isn't in any of the charts I've seen."

"It wouldn't be." Sebastian said it lightly, but his jaw did something small and tight when he looked upstream. "Charts stop being maintained up here about ten years ago."

Ten years. Priya filed that away with everything else she'd filed away since Marguerite Voss's contract landed in Sebastian's hands — the way he'd gone quiet reading the destination coordinates, the way Anselm had stopped humming in the galley, the way none of them had been told exactly where up the Nam Kha they were going until the permits were already signed and the money already half-spent.

Desperation made strange crews. She knew that. She'd signed on because the engineering fee would clear her mother's hospital debt in Chiang Mai with enough left over to actually visit her for the first time in two years. Okonkwo had his reasons, whatever they were; he kept them behind that enormous calm of his like coins in a closed fist. Anselm cooked rice and fish stew and said nothing at all. And Tomas —

Tomas, who hadn't slept, and wouldn't say why.

* * *

The seam let go just past noon.

Priya felt it before she heard it — a change in the Meridian's voice, a new note in the percussion of water against hull, and then the bilge alarm's thin electronic whine cutting through the engine drone. She was down the ladder before the second chirp.

Water in the forward hold, six inches and rising. She found it by hand, wading calf-deep through their crated supplies, feeling along the hull plating until her fingers found the weep: a seam near the chine, opened maybe eight centimeters, flexing with each impact of the current. Old weld. Older metal. The gorges were doing what gorges did — squeezing the boat against her own history.

"Sebastian!" she shouted up the companionway. "I need her beached or I need her slowed. Pick one!"

They slowed her. Sebastian brought the bow into an eddy behind a limestone spur while Tomas held position on the throttles, and Priya went over the side into waist-deep water with the welding kit and a marine epoxy patch kit and forty minutes of daylight she wasn't sure she had.

The cold came up through her boots first. Then the current found her, pulling at her thighs, trying to add her to its cargo. She braced against the hull and worked by touch more than sight — grinding the seam clean, laying the epoxy, pressing the fiberglass patch into place while the river shoved at her like a crowd trying to get past. Twice her feet went out from under her and she caught herself on the gunwale, swearing in Tamil, in English, in the private language of engineers everywhere.

Okonkwo appeared above her, silhouetted against the sky, holding a line he'd rigged without being asked.

"On my count," he said. "You're going to want to lean back."

"I'm going to want to finish."

"The river doesn't care what you want. Lean back on three."

She leaned back on three. The line took her weight, let her get her feet braced properly, and she finished the patch with the current still hauling at her and Okonkwo's arms steady as pilings above. When she finally hauled herself aboard, shaking, blue-lipped, triumphant, the whole crew was there — even Anselm, holding out a towel with an expression that might have been concern if concern were rationed.

"Bilge pump's handling the rest," Priya said through chattering teeth. "Give the epoxy an hour and she'll be sounder than she was this morning."

Sebastian crouched beside her, and for a moment the mask he wore — the easy guide's calm, the man who measured life in miles — slipped, and she saw underneath it something older and more tired.

"You didn't have to go in yourself."

"No," she agreed. "You could've lost the cargo instead."

Something moved through his face at the word *cargo* — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under the surface. Then it was gone, and he was clapping her shoulder and telling Tomas to make way downstream to the next anchorage, and Priya sat wrapped in her towel wondering why a salvage job for sealed crates should make a man look like that.

* * *

She found it that night, in the engine room, because the engine room was where she went when she couldn't sleep, and she couldn't sleep.

It started as routine. She ran the maintenance log forward, checking entries against fuel burn and hours, the way she did every night — not because she distrusted the crew, but because numbers were the only prayers she trusted. Hours run against distance covered against fuel consumed. Three columns that had to agree, and on a boat, always did.

Two of them agreed tonight.

The third didn't.

She went back through the book page by page, lamp held close. The handwriting was Tomas's — he kept the logs on the night watches, neat little figures, nothing fancy. Distances logged matched the landmarks they'd passed. Fuel burn matched the distances. But the hours didn't match the distances, not for the last two days, and the discrepancy wasn't random. It was consistent. As if the boat had been traveling faster than the Meridian could travel, or as if the distances written down weren't the distances actually traveled.

And there was something else. She tilted the page under the lamp. Two entries, dated three days apart, written in ink that was the same shade of fresh. The same pen, same pressure, same sitting. Someone had gone back and altered the record — not clumsily, not obviously, but altered it. Changed a bearing here. Adjusted a waypoint there. Shifted the logged route a few kilometers east of where they had actually run.

East. Toward water she hadn't seen from the wheelhouse. Toward a channel that didn't appear on the chart folded in the chart table, the chart that showed the Nam Kha simply ending, mid-gorge, in a blank space the cartographers hadn't bothered to fill.

Priya closed the book and sat very still in the engine room's oily dark, listening to the Meridian breathe beneath her.

Someone aboard was lying about the route. Not hiding it — that she could almost forgive; Sebastian himself was clearly hiding things, whole countries' worth of them. This was different. This was rewriting. This was someone making sure that wherever the Meridian had actually been, the paper trail said otherwise. And there was only one reason she could think of that a man would falsify a route in ink: so that someone else, reading it later, would believe they'd gone where they hadn't.

Or so that whoever was waiting for them wouldn't know where to look.

She thought of Tomas at the wheel at dawn, refusing relief, eyes fixed ahead. Thought of the duffel bag and the no-questions. Thought of the fine, careful hands.

Above her, footsteps crossed the deck — slow, deliberate, stopping directly over the engine room hatch. A shadow blocked the lamplight from the companionway. Held there. Moved on.

Priya didn't move. She sat with the logbook closed on her knee and her heart working harder than the third cylinder ever had, and she understood that the Meridian was carrying two cargoes now: the sealed crates lashed in the hold, and whatever it was that Tomas knew, and she was beginning to suspect the second one weighed more.

In the morning, she decided, she would watch the wheelhouse watch itself.

In the morning, the radio would crackle with weather from the south, and everything would get worse.
