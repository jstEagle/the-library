## 5. The Ferrywoman

The river had eaten the road again.

Lin stood at the edge of what the maps called Ban Nam Kha Noi and what her grandmother had called *the place where the water decides*, watching the brown flood tongue its way through the market stalls. The vendors had moved their tables up twice already. By evening they would move them a third time, or give up, or stand in the shallows selling fish to boatmen who paid too much because there was nowhere else to buy it. This was the arithmetic of the crossing village: everything measured against the water's appetite.

She was mending the ferry rope when she heard the engine.

Not her father's old two-stroke, which coughed like a smoker and announced itself half a mile out. This was something bigger, deeper, working hard against the current — a boat that did not belong here, pushing upstream into water that punished strangers. She tied off the rope and walked down the mud ramp to look.

The boat came around the bend below the temple hill, and Lin felt the recognition arrive before her eyes finished their work. A river cruiser, forty feet maybe, steel-hulled, patched at the seam with fresh welds that caught the light wrong. A working name on the bow, half-scraped: *MERI—*. She knew that hull. Not this exact boat, but that kind — the kind that came up from the south with money behind it and permits folded in waterproof sleeves, going where the concession men told them to go.

But it wasn't flying any company colors. And it was riding low, heavy with gear, and it was being handled — she narrowed her eyes — competently. Carefully. The pilot was reading the current instead of fighting it, which put him above most of the foreigners who came up this river believing the water owed them passage.

The boat angled toward the landing below the village, where the flooded plain made a shallow harbor of drowned grass. It came in slow, testing depth, and finally swung broadside and let its anchor run out into the murk.

Lin stayed by the ramp and waited. She had learned long ago that waiting told you more than asking. Strangers who needed something always showed you what it was before they opened their mouths.

A tall man came down to the rail first. Westerner, sunburned to the color of old brick, moving with the particular economy of someone who had spent years learning not to waste motion. He didn't wave. He didn't call out. He looked at the village, then at the flooded channel beyond it, then — briefly, professionally — at the sky, reading weather the way she read water.

Then he looked at her.

Lin had been looked at by hundreds of foreigners. They looked at her the way they looked at everything upriver: as an obstacle, an expense, a colorful detail for photographs they'd show people who would never understand them. This man's look was different. It went past her, almost, as if he were checking whether she was real — whether the village itself was real — and then it flinched away, quick as a hand pulled back from a hot stove.

She knew that flinch. She had seen it once before, ten years ago, on the face of a man standing in this same village with his pack still smelling of high altitude, telling a room full of elders that the climber who had taught a child to read maps was dead on the mountain.

*No,* she thought. *It can't be.*

But the river had brought stranger things than ghosts.

* * *

They sent two of them ashore in a rubber skiff. The tall man and a young deckhand whose nervousness arrived ahead of him like a smell. The tall one stepped onto the mud ramp without hesitation, which meant he'd done it ten thousand times, and stopped three meters from her — close enough to talk, far enough to be polite, the distance of a man who understood that here, he was the guest.

"You're the ferry," he said. His Thai was accented but real, learned from working people rather than phrasebooks. "We need to get through the channel north of the temple. Our charts end at the village."

"Your charts are right to end there." She kept coiling the rope as she spoke, giving her hands work so her face could stay idle. "The flood has changed everything since the last survey. What was deep is shallow. What was shallow is now the main channel."

"We were told you know the way through."

"Who told you?"

"A woman downstream. Marguerite Voss."

Lin set down the rope. So. That name again, drifting up the river like oil. Her cousin's husband worked the permit post at the concession line, and he had radioed two days ago about a salvage contract signed in Bangkok, sealed papers, a deadline that died with the first flood. Money moving quietly toward her river. Money always meant someone wanted something buried or something raised, and either way it was never good for the villages between.

"And what are you salvaging?" she asked.

The tall man paused. It was a small pause, well-controlled, but she had spent a decade watching men lie to officials and each other, and she could hear the gap where truth should have been.

"Cargo from a wreck upstream."

"What cargo?"

"That's between us and our client."

"Then your business is between you and your client." She picked the rope back up. "The channel is closed."

"It's not closed," the young deckhand said, too fast. "You just ferried a rice barge through yesterday. We watched from the bend."

Lin looked at him until he found somewhere else to put his eyes. Then she looked at the tall man, who had not defended the boy, which told her he hadn't ordered the spying, which told her something else — that he ran his crew by letting them see, not by making them obey.

"The barge belongs to my family," she said. "My father poled that channel for thirty years. I've poled it for twelve. I know where the sandbars sleep and where they wake up. You know nothing. If I take you through and you crack your hull on a bar that moved last night, you drown, and your client loses her cargo, and my village spends a week dragging your bodies out of the flood because no one else will do it." She let that sit. "Why should I take that risk for strangers who won't even say what they carry?"

The tall man was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had dropped into something plainer, less negotiated.

"Because we'll pay. Fairly. Because we'll follow your instructions exactly, every meter, no arguments. And because—" he hesitated, and this time the hesitation wasn't control, it was something rawer, "—because we're not going anywhere else. The permits die with the flood. It's now or never."

*Now or never.* How many times had she heard that from men on this river? And how many times had it been true?

"I don't need your money," she said.

That surprised him. Good. Surprised men spoke honestly.

"Then what do you need?"

"My own cargo goes downstream. To the concession post, and past it, to the district town." She nodded at the long tarpaulin bundle lashed under the eaves of her stilt house, where it had waited through four monsoons because there was never a boat going all the way down and never money enough to send it alone. "Take it with you. Keep it dry. Deliver it where I say. That's my price."

The tall man glanced at the bundle, then back at her. "What is it?"

"Letters."

"Letters?"

"Personal correspondence. Ten years' worth." She met his eyes. "From a man who used to come here. You may have heard of him. Elias Thorne."

Something happened to the tall man's face.

Lin had prepared herself for many reactions — blankness, polite ignorance, the careful shrug of a man who'd heard the name on a contract. She had not prepared herself for what actually occurred, which was that the whole architecture of him seemed to sway, very slightly, like a pole driven into soft ground. He caught himself immediately. But she had seen it. Ten years of watching the river, and she had seen it.

"You knew him," she said. Not a question.

"I—" He stopped. Started again. "Everyone in this business knew Thorne. He was famous."

"Famous men don't write letters to a ferryman's daughter in a village with no road." She stepped closer, and the young deckhand shifted uneasily, and she ignored him entirely. "He sat in my grandmother's house and drew maps on butcher paper so a nine-year-old girl could learn where the water went. He called me *nong faa* — little sky — because I kept looking up when he talked about mountains. He wrote to me every season until the season he died. And his last letter is in that bundle, and I have carried it for ten years because I could not make myself read past the first line." Her voice held. She made it hold. "So don't tell me everyone knew him. Tell me who you are."

The tall man looked at the river. For a long moment the only sound was the flood moving through the drowned grass, patient as time.

"Sebastian," he said finally. Just the name. No surname offered, no history attached, and the absence rang louder than any introduction.

"Sebastian," she repeated. "And is that the name your mother gave you, or the name you chose after the mountain?"

Behind him, the deckhand went very still. Sebastian didn't turn around, but Lin saw the tension move up his back like a current under calm water.

"It's the name I use," he said.

"Then use it carefully," Lin said, "because names are like channels. They shift. And if you steer by the wrong one, you end up on the bar."

* * *

They argued about it aboard the Meridian — she could hear the argument carrying across the water even before the skiff took her out to the boat. The engineer, a compact woman with grease to her elbows named Priya, thought taking an unknown local pilot through unknown water was exactly as dangerous as refusing. The diver, Okonkwo, a large quiet man who moved like the river couldn't touch him, pointed out that they had no alternative and the flood clock was running. The cook, Anselm, said nothing at all, but when Lin came up the ladder he looked at her for a long moment with an expression she couldn't place until she placed it: recognition. Not of her. Of the name she'd spoken. Of Thorne.

*Another ghost,* she thought. *This boat is carrying a hold full of them.*

Sebastian ended the argument the way she'd begun to expect he ended things — not loudly, but finally. "She comes. Her terms."

So they struck her price, and she brought her bundle aboard wrapped in triple layers of plastic and an old army blanket, and Priya stowed it in the driest corner of the hold with a care that surprised Lin until she saw how the engineer touched things — gently, precisely, like everything aboard was already half-lost.

They cast off at mid-afternoon, into the golden hour when the flood turned to hammered bronze and the drowned forest stood hip-deep in light. Lin took the bow position on the foredeck and read the water aloud, and Sebastian steamed the boat along her voice — *left of the dead tree, harder left, now straight, trust me, straight* — and within twenty minutes the crew stopped treating her like cargo and started treating her like what she was: the only person aboard who knew where the bottom was.

Except Sebastian. He watched her differently. Not doubting. Something worse than doubting.

When the channel widened and the danger eased for a stretch, he came forward and stood beside her at the rail, hands loose, eyes on the water.

"You said his last letter," he said. "You never read past the first line."

"No."

"Why not?"

Lin watched a water buffalo stand belly-deep in what had been a rice field in another life. "Because the first line says *Nong Faa, if you are reading this, then I did not come down*, and after that line there is nothing in the world I want to know badly enough to lose the not-knowing." She glanced at him. "You understand that kind of arithmetic. I can see it in you. You count things to avoid counting other things."

His jaw tightened. "You don't know anything about me."

"No," she agreed. "But I know a man who talked about his climbing partner the way you're trying not to talk about him. He called him Felix. Said Felix was the lucky one, the one who'd live forever." She turned to face him fully, and the last light caught them both, and she watched him try to breathe around what she'd just handed him. "He never told me Felix's real name. But he drew him on the butcher paper once — standing on top of the world, laughing. And you have his laugh, Sebastian. Or you had it. Before whatever happened up there took it away."

The boat moved through the drowned forest. Somewhere behind them, Anselm began cooking, and the smell of garlic drifted forward over the water, absurdly domestic.

"I'm not him," Sebastian said at last, very quietly. "Felix died on that mountain too. Whoever's standing here is somebody else."

"Then somebody else can help me deliver a dead man's letters," Lin said. "That's all I'm asking. Not redemption. Delivery."

She turned back to the water before he could answer, because she had said what the river required her to say, and more would only drown them both. But she felt him stay at the rail beside her a while longer, not speaking, two people keeping watch over the same ghost from opposite banks.

Below them, in the darkening hold, the letters slept. Above them, the first far thunder of the monsoon rolled down from the mountains like a door closing.

And at the stern, half-hidden by the wheelhouse, the deckhand Tomas keyed his radio low and quick, whispering coordinates into the dusk.
