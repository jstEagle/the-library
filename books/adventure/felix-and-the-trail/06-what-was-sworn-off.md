## 6. What Was Sworn Off

The Nam Kha.

I heard the name for the third time that morning — Marguerite had said it again on the radio at dawn, crisp and impatient, *make good time up the Nam Kha, we're on a clock* — and it was only then, watching the water slide green and heavy past the hull, that the name finished its slow work of digging through ten years of careful forgetting and found the thing underneath.

I had known. Of course I had known. I'd known since the second day, when Priya unrolled the river chart on the chart table and my eyes went to the head of the valley before I could stop them, to the blue serrated line where the tributaries braided out of the high country, and above that line, printed in small gray type the way maps print the things they consider settled fact: **Pk. 6410 — unnamed (local: Kha Ri)**.

Kha Ri. The White-Shouldered Mountain. Elias Thorne had laughed at the name when I first showed it to him, seven years before he died on it. *"The locals named it after your shoulders, Sebastian,"* he'd said, clapping me between the shoulder blades hard enough to stagger me. *"White-shouldered. That's you in the snow. That's us."*

I had signed a contract three weeks ago that said *Nam Kha River salvage* in black ink, and I had read every clause twice, because I always read contracts twice, and I had told myself the name meant nothing. Rivers share names the way people do. There are a dozen Nam Khas in Southeast Asia, half a dozen more across the border. It was coincidence. It was a different valley. It was far enough from anywhere I had sworn off that the permits wouldn't notice, that the blacklist wouldn't reach, that *I* wouldn't reach.

And then this morning the gorge had narrowed the way that gorge narrows, and the current had picked up that particular doubled note — deep water over shallow rock, a sound I could feel in my back teeth — and I had stood at the wheel with my hands easy on the spokes and realized I was reading the river by memory. Not by chart. By memory. Because I had run this water before, eleven years ago, with a client who liked to say the approach was half the climb, and I had known every bend before the Meridian reached it, and there is no version of that knowledge that isn't a confession.

Marguerite Voss had hired me to go home.

* * *

I didn't touch the wheel differently. That's the discipline of it, the thing ten years teaches you: the panic happens somewhere behind your sternum and the hands stay level, because if the hands go wrong the crew sees it, and if the crew sees it then everything I built — the miles-not-years life, the small honest runs down rivers with no history, the man called Sebastian who is only half a name — all of it starts coming apart at the seams like a patched hull.

"Seb." Tomas at my elbow, coffee tin in hand. "You want some? You've been standing there since—"

"Since dawn, yeah." I took the tin. It was too sweet, the way Anselm makes it for the deckhands, sugar against fatigue. "Good water today."

"You've been smiling at it," Tomas said. "That's new."

"I'm not smiling."

"You are. It's creepy. Like the engine smiling."

I almost laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway up my throat, and I drank the coffee instead and looked at the water and let the silence do what silence does on a boat, which is fill itself without anyone's help.

Tomas lingered anyway. He's twenty-four and he has the young man's conviction that silence is a problem to be solved. "You know this river," he said. Not a question. "You've been calling the bends before Okonkwo calls them off the depth sounder. Priya noticed. She thinks you've got the whole chart memorized."

"Priya should spend less time noticing things and more time sleeping."

"She also says the engine logs were altered." He said it lightly, testing, the way you drop a stone down a well to hear how deep it goes. "Before she came aboard. Someone changed the route entries."

"Old boats have old paperwork."

"Yeah," Tomas said. "That's what I figured." But he stayed one beat too long before he went forward, and I watched him go and filed the beat away, because I've spent ten years learning to read men on boats and something in Tomas's shoulders had been wrong since the permit post, some tightness that wasn't fear of the river. Fear of the river I could work with. Fear of something else was a leak I couldn't yet find.

I put it aside. I had a bigger leak.

* * *

Here is what I remember about the last morning on Kha Ri, and I want to be exact, because for ten years I have let other people's versions stand in place of mine, and exactness is the only currency I have left:

We were four days high. Camp Three sat on a shoulder of ice at 5,900 meters, tents lashed to deadman anchors because the wind came off the summit ridge like something with intent. Elias Thorne was fifty-eight years old and strong as a bridge cable and he had wanted this mountain for six years — not the summit, he said, though he wanted that too, but the northeast face, the long unclimbed line, the *problem*. Elias collected problems the way other rich men collected cars.

There were five of us high on the mountain. Me, leading. Elias. Dr. Anselm Roos, our expedition physician, a quiet Dutchman who brewed tea at altitude like it was a sacrament. And the Delacroix brothers, Rémy and Luc, hired ropes out of Chamonix, competent, ambitious, and — I understood later, much later, too late — carrying a side arrangement none of us had been told about.

The weather window was closing. Everyone agreed on that. The forecast gave us thirty-six hours, maybe forty, and the summit push was a fourteen-hour round trip from Camp Three in good conditions. Elias wanted to leave at two in the morning. I wanted to leave at midnight, to buy margin. We argued about it the way we argued about everything, cheerfully, over tea, and I won, because I was the guide and the mountain was mine to answer for.

At eleven-forty, Luc Delacroix came to my tent and told me his brother had taken the spare rope coil — the emergency rope, the one cached at the col below the ice cliff — down to Camp Two. To lighten the load, he said. To make room for camera gear, he said. And Rémy was already climbing, already an hour ahead, and Elias was already dressed, and I made the call that has been read back to me in a dozen languages by a dozen inquiry boards:

We went up anyway. Four of us, on short rope, without the spare.

What happened next has been written down so many times it barely belongs to me anymore. The ice cliff at 6,200 meters let go at nine-fifteen in the morning. Not a big release — the boards were very clear about that, a *localized serac failure*, as though the mountain had failed an inspection — but it took the fixed lines with it, and it took Rémy Delacroix, and it left Elias Thorne and me and Anselm stranded above the breach with one rope between us and a client whose left leg was broken in two places and who was, with terrible clarity, bleeding out into the snow faster than any helicopter could reach him.

Elias died at noon. He was conscious for most of it. He held my hand — my hand, gloved, useless — and he talked, because Elias never stopped talking, and near the end he said, *Sebastian. Whatever they say. You check the anchors.* And I didn't understand him then, and I was too busy trying to keep him alive to ask, and by the time I understood it the inquiries were over and the verdicts were in.

Because here is what the world decided: the guide had overridden standard protocol. The guide had pushed on despite missing safety equipment. The guide — blacklisted within the year, first Nepal, then Peru through some quiet word-of-mouth machinery I never saw working but felt everywhere — the guide had traded a client's life for a summit.

Nobody asked why the spare rope was at Camp Two. Nobody asked why the fixed-line anchors at the ice cliff, which I had personally placed and personally re-checked two days earlier, showed fresh tool marks when Anselm and I climbed down past them — cut, retied, cut again, someone re-rigging the route while we slept. Nobody asked, because asking would have required the answer to be somebody other than the disgraced guide, and the disgraced guide was convenient, and the dead man's family wanted someone to hate, and the surviving Delacroix brother had flown home to Chamonix and retained lawyers before Elias's body was even down the mountain.

Anselm knew. I think Anselm always knew. He stood beside me at the inquiry in Kathmandu and said nothing, and when I caught his eye across the room he looked at me the way you look at a man through glass, and then he disappeared into whatever lives doctors disappear into, and I stopped expecting the truth to arrive from anywhere.

I stopped using my full name on trip manifests. Then I stopped using trips that needed manifests. I came down to the big slow rivers of the south, where the clients want beer and stories and no altitude, and I measured my life in miles because miles don't ask questions. Miles just accumulate, indifferent, and you can pretend accumulation is the same thing as living.

Ten years. Two thousand miles a year, give or take. Twenty thousand miles of not thinking about a rope coil at Camp Two.

And now the Nam Kha ran under my keel, and Kha Ri stood somewhere up-valley behind the monsoon haze, white-shouldered, waiting, and I understood that Marguerite Voss had not hired a river guide. She had hired a ghost to walk back into its own graveyard.

* * *

She called at midday, when the Meridian was tied up at a sliding shelf of rock taking on fuel from drums Lin's people had ferried up that morning. Lin stood at the rail watching the transfer with her arms folded, saying nothing, the way she'd said nothing for two days now, except that her nothing had weight to it, a direction. Her nothing pointed at me.

The radio crackled. "Meridian, Voss. Status."

"Fueling. Six hours to the wreck site if the channel holds." I kept my voice flat, professional, the voice of a man with nothing behind his sternum.

"Make it five. The concession office says the flood front may come early this year. Days early." A pause, and then, in a tone I can only describe as delicate, the way a blade is delicate: "How are you finding the river, Sebastian? Familiar territory?"

The drum gurgled. Lin's eyes came around to me, slow, deliberate.

"Familiar enough," I said.

"Mm. I imagine it is." Static, and under the static I could hear her deciding something, the little breath people take before they stop pretending. "You haven't asked me what's in the crate."

"It's sealed. Contract says salvage, not inventory."

"The contract says salvage, yes. It says a great many things." Another pause. "It doesn't say why you, specifically. Did you wonder about that? A blacklisted guide, available cheap, exactly when I need someone who knows this watershed?" Her voice softened into something almost kind, which from Marguerite was worse than cruelty. "I need someone who knows the *mountain*, Sebastian. I need the man who was on Kha Ri."

The fuel drum sloshed dry. The pump handle squeaked. Somewhere upstream thunder stacked itself against the gorge walls, and I couldn't tell whether it was weather or just the river talking to itself.

"You knew," I said. "From the letter. From the dock. You knew the whole time."

"I've known for ten years where you went," Marguerite said. "Watching you was the only hobby I could afford. Now bring me my crate, and we'll both get what we came for." And she was gone, leaving the carrier hissing like water on hot iron.

Lin hadn't moved. She was still looking at me, and now there was no pretense in it at all, and I realized she had understood every word — her English was better than she let anyone believe — and more than that, I realized she had been waiting for me to understand it. Watching me the way you watch a man walk toward an edge he can't see.

"You knew him," I said to her. "Thorne. That's why you're here. That cargo of yours."

Lin looked upriver, toward the haze, toward the white shoulders hidden inside it. When she spoke her voice was low and even, a ferrywoman's voice, built for carrying things across.

"He stayed in my village," she said. "Before the climb. Eleven days. He taught me to read a contour map when I was nine years old." She turned back to me. "He wrote to us. Every year. Until the year he didn't."

Thunder again, closer, and this time there was no mistaking it for the river.

"And you," she said. "You are the guide they blamed."

Not a question either. I set the empty fuel drum down carefully, because my hands wanted to do something violent and there was nothing nearby worth breaking.

"Yes," I said. It came out rougher than I intended. "I'm the guide they blamed."

Something moved through her face — not pity, I'd have thrown pity back in the river, and I think she knew that — something more like recognition, the look of one carrier meeting another. She nodded once, and went aft to check her own cargo, and I stood at the rail with the mountain invisible up-valley and the letter of my contract burning a hole in the chart table below.

Marguerite had known. Had chosen me, priced me, aimed me like a tool at the one place on earth I had sworn off, and never once lied to me outright — she'd simply let me lie to myself, which is easier and cheaper and holds up better in arbitration.

Fine. Let it be so. If she wanted a ghost, she'd hired one, and ghosts have advantages over the living: we've already lost everything once, so there's nothing left to threaten us with.

I went below, pulled the contract from the chart table, and read it a third time. Clause fourteen, subsection C, the one I'd skimmed twice before because it was boilerplate: *All recovered materials remain the sole property of the chartering party, including documentary materials, and shall be surrendered intact and unexamined.*

Unexamined.

A crate full of paper, sealed, salvaged from a wreck on the river below Kha Ri, that Marguerite Voss did not want her hired guide to look at, delivered by the one man alive with a reason to.

Upstream, the thunder rolled again, longer this time, and Priya appeared at the companionway with the barometer in her hand and an expression I'd learn to dread in the days ahead.

"Sebastian," she said. "You're going to want to see this."
