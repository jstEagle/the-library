## 7. Monsoon Warning

Okonkwo had learned to read weather the way other men read faces, and the sky over the Nam Kha gorge that morning was wearing a lie.

It looked generous. Blue stacked on blue, a few white clouds drifting east with the lazy confidence of clouds that had nowhere to be. The river ran brown and quick below them, but nothing about it said *flood*. Nothing about it said *run*. A tourist would have called it a beautiful day to be alive on the water.

Okonkwo called it a beautiful day to check the radio twice.

He sat in the wheelhouse of the *Meridian* with the set's casing open beside him, soldering iron cooling on a folded rag. The radio was Priya's pride and his responsibility — she kept the engine honest, he kept their voice to the world honest, and between them they'd coaxed the old marine band back from the dead somewhere above the second gorge. It crackled now with the morning forecast relay out of Vientiane, then Luang Prabang, then a Thai station whose signal faded in and out like a man talking in his sleep.

He listened to it three times before he let himself believe it.

*—monsoon front accelerating south-southwest, landfall revised, repeat, revised — expected onset forty-eight to seventy-two hours, previously one hundred twenty — heavy precipitation, riverine flooding likely along upper tributaries—*

Forty-eight to seventy-two hours. The permits expired at first flood. Marguerite Voss had bought them a window, and the window had just lost half its glass.

Okonkwo set down the soldering iron and looked at his hands. Big hands, scarred at the knuckles from twenty years of wrecks and winches and cold water work off three continents. They didn't shake. That was the thing people got wrong about him — they saw the size and assumed slowness, calm as a side effect of mass. But inside he was counting, always counting, the way he counted when the rig went down off Bonny and he'd held four men breathing in an air pocket for six hours. Counting was how you stayed useful. Counting was how you didn't drown.

He was counting now: two days, maybe three. Forty miles of unknown channel ahead, if Lin's shortcut held. A patched hull seam that Priya trusted about as far as she could throw the *Meridian*. And a vote that hadn't happened yet but was going to, because Sebastian Amberdale was the kind of man who put things to a vote even when everyone already knew what he'd decided.

Okonkwo respected that and distrusted it in equal measure. Democracy on a boat was a fine thing right up until the water started coming in.

* * *

He found the crew gathering on the foredeck, drawn by some animal instinct or by Tomas, who'd climbed up onto the cargo crate and was waving the forecast printout like a man selling lottery tickets.

"Bad news travels fast," Tomas was saying. "Good news is we heard it at all."

"Get off the crate," Priya said. She was sitting against the rail with a wrench across her knees, grease to her elbows, hair tied back with what looked like electrical wire. "That crate is the only reason any of us are on this river."

"It's sealed. I'm not hurting it."

"You're standing on a dead man's property. There's a difference."

Tomas hopped down, grinning, but Okonkwo caught the flicker underneath the grin — the little stutter in him that had been there since the third day upstream, the way his eyes went to the horizon whenever anyone mentioned the concession post. Okonkwo had worked with enough men carrying debts to know the posture. He'd carried a few himself, once, in another life, in a port town where he'd signed papers he shouldn't have. Men with debts watched exits. Tomas watched exits.

Sebastian came out of the wheelhouse last, with Lin a step behind him. The ferrywoman had been aboard four days now, piloting them through water no chart acknowledged, and she still moved through the boat like a guest who refused to unpack — her few belongings in one woven bag, her eyes always half on the current, half on Sebastian. Okonkwo couldn't decide what he thought of her yet, except that she read water better than anyone he'd seen, including Sebastian, and that she never wasted a word.

"The front's early," Sebastian said. No preamble. He laid the forecast sheet on the deck box and weighted it with a carabiner against the breeze. "Landfall in two days, maybe less in the high country. When it hits, this river comes up six, eight feet in a night. Permits void at first flood. After that we're not salvagers, we're smugglers."

"Then we turn back," Anselm said quietly, from the galley doorway. The cook rarely spoke, and when he did the whole deck seemed to lean toward him, the way you lean toward a man who knows something. "We've come nine days upstream. Nine days back is nine days we don't have. But drowned is drowned either way."

"Turn back with what?" Priya said. "Half pay? Voss doesn't strike me as the forgiving type, and my engine room has a seam in it held together with prayer and epoxy."

"Your seam will hold," Okonkwo said.

Priya looked at him. "You don't know that."

"I know you," he said. "It'll hold."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softness exactly, but its cousin. She turned back to Sebastian. "I say forward. If we run downstream now we lose everything we came for and gain nothing but a longer ride home."

"Forward," Tomas said immediately. Too immediately, Okonkwo thought. "We're close. Everyone knows we're close."

"Anselm?"

The cook was quiet a long moment. His gaze had gone to the crate, to the stenciled letters on its side that none of them had been able to fully make out under the mud and marine growth. E.T., maybe. Or T.E. Something that had once meant enough to someone to seal shut and sink.

"Forward," Anselm said finally, and his voice had a strange grain to it, like a man agreeing to his own sentencing. "Some things need finishing more than they need surviving."

"That's not comforting," Priya said.

"It wasn't meant to be."

Four votes. All eyes went to Lin, who stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the water go by with the expression of a woman listening to a conversation she'd already had with herself.

"You don't get a vote," Tomas started.

"She gets a vote," Sebastian said. "She's the only one who knows where the channel goes."

Lin turned. She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, and last at Sebastian, and something passed between them that Okonkwo couldn't parse — some arithmetic done in silence, some debt being weighed.

"Forward," she said. "But not by the main channel past the narrows. Not with this sky. We take my father's line through the shallows tonight, while there's light, and we tie up in the cut bank before dark. If the flood comes early, the shallows drown first. You want to be in stone when the water rises, not sand."

"Then it's settled," Sebastian said. "Forward."

And that should have been the end of it. That should have been the moment the crew hardened into a single thing, the way crews do when the decision gets made and there's nothing left but the doing of it. Okonkwo had felt it happen on a dozen boats — that click, that settling, six strangers becoming one animal with one purpose.

He felt it start here. He felt it in Priya rolling up her sleeves and heading aft without being asked. He felt it in Anselm going back into the galley to cook a real meal, the kind you cook before hard days, rice and dried fish and too much chili because tomorrow nobody would want to eat anyway. He felt it even in himself, checking the dive gear, repacking the lines, oiling the winch.

But he also felt the other thing. The crack.

Because Tomas wasn't moving toward the work. Tomas was standing at the stern rail with his phone in his hand, angled away from the wheelhouse, and there was no signal up here — Okonkwo knew there was no signal, he'd mapped the dead zones himself — except that Tomas stood there anyway, holding the phone up toward the cliffs like a man offering incense to a god who never answered.

Except that yesterday, at the flooded crossing village, there *had* been signal. One bar, for eleven minutes, while Lin negotiated passage. Okonkwo remembered because he'd used those eleven minutes to call his sister in Lagos, and he'd noticed Tomas walk to the far end of the dock with his back turned, and he'd noticed, when Tomas came back, that particular lightness a man has after putting down a weight he's been carrying.

Okonkwo filed it. He didn't say anything. That was the discipline — you didn't accuse a man on a guess, not on a boat, not on a river, not when a wrong word could split a crew faster than any rock could split a hull. You watched. You counted. You waited for the number to come due.

* * *

They ran the shallows that evening with the sun going copper behind the western ridges, and it was the finest piece of boat handling Okonkwo had ever witnessed, and he included Sebastian in that judgment.

Lin drove. She stood at the wheel with one hand and read the water with her whole body, leaning into turns before the turns came, calling depths to Priya in a shorthand the engineer learned in about ten minutes because it was that good — *three*, meaning three feet under the keel, *sand*, meaning swing left, *stone*, meaning pray. The *Meridian* threaded a braided maze of channels that appeared on no chart, water so shallow in places that the brown bottom scrolled visibly beneath the hull, and twice they scraped gravel with a sound like teeth and twice Lin corrected before panic could form.

Sebastian stood beside her the whole way and said almost nothing. Just watched her hands. Just watched the water. Once, near dusk, he said something low that Okonkwo couldn't hear, and Lin answered with a single word, and whatever it was made Sebastian look at her the way a man looks at a door he thought was painted on a wall.

They tied up in the cut bank at full dark, wedged into a slot of limestone with high walls and good holding, exactly as promised. Stone, not sand. Above them the stars came out hard and cold, and the river muttered to itself below, and for an hour the boat was quiet in the way boats are quiet when everyone is too tired to talk.

Okonkwo took first watch. He liked first watch; it suited him, the counting hours, the listening. He sat in the wheelhouse with a thermos of Anselm's terrible coffee and the radio murmuring low, and around midnight the Thai station came through clean for the first time all trip, and the news was worse.

*—revised again — onset accelerated — upper tributary basins can expect flood stage within thirty-six hours — authorities advising all river traffic to seek secure anchorage—*

Thirty-six hours. He wrote it in the log in block capitals, because logs were sacred and block capitals were how you told the future the truth. Then he stepped out to relieve himself against the cliff wall, and on the way back he heard the voice.

Low. From the stern. Tomas's voice, but not Tomas's usual voice — stripped of the grin, stripped of the salesman's bounce, flat and fast and frightened.

"—yes, still together, all six — no, she's with us, the local woman, she's piloting — I don't know, past the narrows sometime tomorrow — coordinates, hold on—"

A pause. Paper rustling. Okonkwo stood in the shadow of the wheelhouse door and did not breathe.

"Eighteen degrees... north... zero-one-four... east... one-zero-two..." Another pause, longer. "I know. I *know*. But they voted today, all of them, they're going all the way — yes, even after the forecast — I can't stop them, they won't turn—" His voice dropped further, and the river nearly ate it. "Just tell them where we'll be. That's all I agreed to. Where, not whether. *Where, not whether.*"

Silence. Then, very softly, in a tone Okonkwo had heard from men in air pockets and flooded compartments, the tone of a man saying goodbye to something:

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Tell them I'm sorry."

Okonkwo withdrew into the wheelhouse without a sound. He sat down. He picked up the thermos. His hands were steady, the way they were always steady, and inside he was counting: one man bought, five lives on a boat, thirty-six hours of river left, and a question with no good answer — *who do you tell, and when, and what happens to a crew the moment trust becomes a thing you have to announce?*

Below the cut bank, the Nam Kha ran dark and fast toward the sea, carrying its message ahead of them.

And somewhere downstream, Okonkwo understood, someone had just written their position in a ledger of their own.
