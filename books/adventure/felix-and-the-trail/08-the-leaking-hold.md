## 8. The Leaking Hold

The seam I patched above the second gorge held for eleven hours. I know because I checked it every hour, on the hour, with a flashlight clamped in my teeth and my palm flat against the riveted plate like a doctor taking a pulse. Eleven hours of steady, dry steel. Then, somewhere past midnight, the river decided it wanted a word with me.

I was asleep on the engine room bench when I heard it — not a sound so much as an absence. The Meridian's diesel had a rhythm I'd learned the way you learn a heartbeat, and at 12:40 by my watch it stuttered. Half a beat of silence under the floor plates. Then a gurgle that had no business being inside a hull.

I was down the ladder before my boots were fully on.

The hold was black and already breathing water. My flashlight found it low on the port side: the patch I'd ground and epoxied and bolted two days ago, weeping along its lower edge in a thin silver line that thickened as I watched. Not a failure of my work. The plate behind it had flexed — the whole hull was working against the current like a jaw grinding, and somewhere below my repair the original seam had given up its long argument with fatigue.

"Sebastian!" I shouted up the ladder. "Hold's taking water! Port side, frame forty!"

Boots hit the deck above me. Then more boots. By the time I got the bilge pump cranked over, the whole crew was crowding the ladder — Sebastian first, always first, moving with that economy that made everything else look slow. Okonkwo came down carrying the spare pump on his shoulder like it weighed nothing. Tomas stood at the top of the ladder holding a flashlight and doing nothing useful, his face lit up yellow and tight.

"How deep?" Sebastian asked.

"Ankle-deep and rising. It'll outrun one pump."

"Then it won't." He was already stripping off his jacket. "Priya, you're on pumps. Okonkwo, with me — we shift cargo to starboard, get the seam above the waterline if we can. Tomas—"

Tomas flinched.

"—count crates. I want to know what's soaked and what's saved."

* * *

We worked four hours in the dark while the river pushed us downstream faster than we wanted to go. That was the ugly arithmetic of it: every minute we spent bailing was a minute the current spent carrying us backward, toward the concession line and the calendar both. Anselm appeared without being asked, handed down dry rags and a thermos of something hot and bitter, and disappeared again without a word. He did this three times. On the third time he stayed, wedging himself against the ladder with his sleeves rolled, passing towels until his hands went gray with cold.

By 4 a.m. we had it contained. Not fixed — contained. The seam still seeped; it would keep seeping until we could beach her and weld her properly, and there was no beaching her properly anywhere on this stretch. But the pumps could hold their own against it now, and half our supplies were ruined instead of all of them.

Half. I sat on an overturned crate in the gray light before dawn and took inventory of what the water had taken. Rice, gone soft and swelling in its sacks. Two cases of the diesel filters, rusted shut overnight. The medical kit Anselm kept locked — he carried it out himself, dripping, and sat with it open on deck for an hour, deciding what could be dried and what was dead. Most of it was dead.

And the charts. I didn't realize it until midmorning, when I went to plot our position and opened the chart tube and found the paper fused into a single wet brick. Every map we had for the next eighty miles, dissolved into pulp.

I carried the brick up on deck and set it down between us like a corpse.

"That's it," Tomas said. "That's the trip."

"It's not the trip," Sebastian said.

"We have no charts. We have half our fuel burned fighting current, half our food floating in the hold, and a hole in the boat. Explain to me how that isn't the trip."

Nobody answered him right away, because nobody had an answer. We drifted in the wide brown water with the pumps chugging underneath us like a failing heart, and the gorge walls rose on either side, green and indifferent, and I remember thinking that this was the moment trips die — not in the rapids, but in the quiet after, when everyone does the math at once.

Then Lin spoke.

She'd been at the stern rail since before dawn, watching the water the way she always watched it, as if reading something written there. Now she turned around.

"You don't need your charts," she said. "Not for the next thirty miles."

Tomas laughed. It wasn't a kind laugh. "You're going to navigate us through the Nam Kha gorges from memory?"

"No," Lin said. "From the water."

* * *

What she meant, she explained while we ate the last of the dry rice, was this: the main channel through the middle gorges was a lie. Deep enough on the government surveys, marked enough on the old charts, but it ran against the current the whole way and it was studded with submerged rock that shifted every flood season. That was why the wrecked boat upstream — the one whose crate we were chasing — had died there. It had trusted the channel.

But the river, she said, keeps a second road. Where the valley narrows, the flood each year lays fresh silt along the banks, and the shallow water along the inside edges runs smooth and fast and clear of stone. Too shallow for any boat our size, according to every rule Priya had been trained on. Except that a flat-bottomed hull, driven hard, doesn't float in shallow water. It planes. It skims. You ride the skin of the river instead of sitting in it.

"My father ferried this stretch for thirty years," she said. "He never once took the deep channel. He said the deep water is where the river keeps its teeth."

I looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked at the water. And I will be honest about what I felt, because it matters: I did not believe her. I am an engineer. I know what a loaded boat draws, and I know what happens to a hull that touches bottom at speed, and every calculation I could do in my head said her father's trick would open our seam like a can opener.

But the calculations also said we couldn't take the deep channel with a leaking hull and no charts. So it wasn't belief we needed. It was a choice between two ways of drowning.

"Show me," Sebastian said.

* * *

She showed us at the first narrows, and I have never been so happy to be wrong.

She stood at the helm beside me — she wouldn't take the wheel herself, said the boat should answer to its crew, but she read the water aloud like scripture. *Watch the surface. Where it wrinkles, rock beneath. Where it goes glassy and green, go there.* She had names for things I had only academic terms for. A "tongue" — the smooth V of fast water marking the safe line. A "tongue's shadow" — the dead eddy behind it where boats stall and spin. She made me throttle up to speeds that made my stomach climb into my throat, and then, at the exact moment I would have backed off, she said *now*, and the Meridian rose onto plane and skimmed across water I could have touched with an oar.

Six inches under the keel. Maybe less. I watched the depth sounder lie to me — it couldn't get a return off bottom at speed, just static — and I watched the hull hold, and I understood that everything I knew about rivers was knowledge of one dialect of a language Lin spoke natively.

Tomas stopped sulking somewhere around the second narrows. I saw it happen. He started hauling the sounding line off the bow without being told, calling depths back to us even though the sounder was useless, because Lin asked him to and because it gave him something to do with his shame. Okonkwo stationed himself amidships with a pole, ready to fend off, grinning like a man at a carnival. Even Anselm came out of the galley and stood in the rain-warm wind at the rail, and I swear the man almost smiled.

By afternoon we had run thirty miles of water that should have killed us, and the mood aboard had changed. Not joy exactly. Something more durable than joy — the specific pride of people who have done a thing together that none of them could have done alone. Lin stood at the rail eating rice Anselm had fried with the last of the dry stores, and Tomas brought her a second bowl without being asked, and she accepted it with a nod that cost her something, I think. Accepting things had never come easily to her.

I found Sebastian aft, checking the seam patch again. It was holding. Barely. I crouched next to him with the flashlight.

"She saved the trip," I said quietly.

"I know."

"Do you trust her?"

He was silent a long moment, wiping grease off his hands, and I watched him not answer the question I'd actually asked. There was something between those two — some recognition, some debt — that predated this boat and this river and possibly this decade. I'd seen it the day she came aboard, the way they'd looked at each other like two people reading the same letter.

"I trust her with the boat," he said finally. Which was not the same thing, and we both knew it.

* * *

We beached at dusk on a silt shelf Lin knew about — a place her father used to careen his ferry, she said, where the bank dropped away clean and the current swept the hull's shadow. Enough light left to work by. Okonkwo and I went over the side in the shallows with hammers and epoxy and a sheet of rubber belting, and the water was warm as bathwater and full of small fish that scattered from our elbows, and for twenty minutes it was almost pleasant, which neither of us said aloud because saying it aloud would have jinxed it.

The news from the hull was bad. Worse than bad. When I got the inspection light angled up under the plating, I found not one failed seam but three — hairline fractures radiating from the original wound like cracks in ice. The Meridian hadn't sprung a leak. She was coming apart, slowly, the way old boats do, all at once disguised as gradually. My patches were bandages on a patient bleeding internally.

I surfaced and told Sebastian. He took it the way he took everything — no change of expression, just a slight settling, like a man shifting weight onto the leg that still worked.

"How long?"

"With the patches? Days. Three, maybe four, if we stay gentle."

"And the concession post?"

"Five days at the pace we've been making. Six, if the deep channel's our only route."

Lin, standing on the bank with her arms crossed, said nothing. She didn't have to. We all knew what she was thinking: five days of her channels, and the boat might make it. Or might not. The river would decide, and the river had never once, in all her father's stories, decided kindly.

That night we camped on the shelf, and I lay awake listening to the pumps cycle in the dark, keeping their stubborn rhythm against the seep. Somewhere past midnight I heard footsteps on the deck above — soft ones, going forward, stopping near the bow. I came up quietly and found Tomas standing at the radio, not transmitting, just standing there with the handset in his hand like a man holding a phone he can't bring himself to dial.

He heard me and set it down too fast.

"Couldn't sleep," he said.

"Neither could I."

We stood in the dark looking at the water, and I thought about the engine logs I'd found altered back in the second week — the fuel figures that didn't add up, the route entries in a hand that wasn't Sebastian's. I thought about how Tomas counted crates during the flooding and how his count had come up two short, and how nobody had pressed him on it. I thought about the handset in his hand.

"Priya," he said suddenly, "if a man did something — something he couldn't take back — and then the people around him turned out to be—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. "Forget it."

"Forget what?"

"Nothing. Forget it."

He went below. I stayed on deck until the sky went gray, listening to the pumps, and thinking about the radio, and about how the flood front was supposed to arrive in four days, and how the river under us was already running higher than it had that morning.

Higher. I noticed it again at dawn — a full hand-width up the bank, and the color of the water had changed overnight, from brown to a thicker, yellower brown, the way it does when new country is draining into it from far upstream. New water. Water from places we hadn't reached yet, carrying news ahead of itself.

Lin was on the bank when I came down, staring at the current with an expression I hadn't seen on her before. It took me a moment to name it, because I'd only ever seen it on engineers reading a failed test.

Fear.

"The river's telling me something," she said, without turning around. "This rise isn't local. This is headwater. Something's opened up in the high country."

"Rain?"

"More than rain." She turned, and her eyes went past me, upriver, toward the gorges we still had to run. "My father had a word for water like this. He said when the river comes down yellow and early, it isn't rising. It's *arriving*."

Behind us, from the direction of the bow, came the sound of the radio crackling to life — and Tomas's voice, low and fast, giving numbers to someone.
