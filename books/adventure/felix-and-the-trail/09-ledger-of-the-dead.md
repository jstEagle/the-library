## 9. Ledger of the Dead

We should have waited for Marguerite's go-ahead. That was the contract — sealed crate, delivered intact, opened by her or her people at the concession post downstream. Priya had read the terms aloud on the first night, laughing at how paranoid they sounded: *no unauthorized access to cargo, no photographic documentation, no deviation from filed route.* We'd all signed anyway, because bad money doesn't come with good terms.

But it was past midnight, the Meridian tied up in a flooded side channel below a limestone overhang that kept the worst of the drizzle off the deck, and nobody could sleep. The river had gone loud since sundown — not the ordinary conversation of water over rock but something deeper, a pressure in the sound, like the river clearing its throat. Okonkwo had checked the radio twice. The flood front was still supposed to be four days out.

Four days was what they'd said in Peru, too, once. I'd learned not to trust forecasts the way you learn not to trust a rope you didn't tie yourself.

I came up from the engine room around one in the morning and found Lin sitting on the crate.

Not near it. On it. Legs crossed, hands loose on her knees, in the gray wash of the anchor light, looking at me like she'd been waiting for exactly this moment and had dressed for it accordingly — which is to say she looked entirely calm and entirely unsurprised, the way only two kinds of people can look: saints and liars, and I've met very few of the first kind.

"You couldn't sleep either," I said.

"No." She didn't move off the crate. "You've been walking past it all day. Every time you cross the deck, your eyes go to it. You count things when you're nervous — rapids, miles, rivets on the hull. Tonight you counted the straps on this box eleven times."

"I did not."

"Twelve now." She unfolded herself and stood, stepping aside with a small gesture, an invitation and a dare at once. "So open it. Whatever's inside has been weighing more than its weight since we loaded it. You might as well know what you're carrying."

"It's Marguerite's cargo."

"Is it?" Lin tilted her head. "It was pulled out of a wreck on this river. It was going somewhere before it sank, and it will keep going somewhere after you hand it off. But right now it's in your boat, under your watch, and the woman who hired you lied to you about where you were going." She said it without heat, just laying the facts out like fish on a market table. "In my village we have a saying: a sealed thing grows heavier every day it stays sealed. Eventually it sinks the boat."

I looked at the crate. Steel-banded plywood, stenciled with a shipping code that traced back through three freight companies to a logistics firm in Kathmandu that hadn't existed under that name in years. Salvaged from the wreck of a supply barge that went down in the Nam Kha gorge eight years ago — that much Marguerite had told us, and that much had checked out against the salvage registry Tomas pulled. What she hadn't told us was why a dead man's effects had been worth hiring a blacklisted guide and a crew of strangers to chase upstream through narrowing water on expiring permits.

Elias Thorne had been dead ten years. Everything of his that mattered had been dead ten years.

Except somebody wanted this box badly enough to pay for it twice — once to find it, and once, if Tomas's midnight radio calls meant what I thought they meant, to make sure it never surfaced at all.

"Get Okonkwo," I said. "And Priya. If we're breaking contract, we do it with witnesses."

Lin nodded once, as if I'd passed some test I hadn't known I was taking, and went below without a sound.

* * *

We cut the bands in the hold, because the hold had the only door that locked, and because I told myself it was about security and not about the fact that I didn't want the sky watching when I saw his handwriting again.

Priya worked the bolt cutters while Okonkwo held the light. Anselm appeared halfway through — he had a way of appearing, the cook, materializing in doorways with that flat, unreadable patience of his — and none of us asked him to leave. Tomas was asleep forward, or pretending to be, and I confess I didn't wake him. That decision would cost me later, though not in the way I expected.

The outer layer was packing straw and waterproof sheeting, professional work, done by someone who cared. Underneath: a document case, oilskin-wrapped, and beneath that, nested in a frame of bamboo splints lashed with wire, a ledger.

Not a modern one. A surveyor's book, canvas-bound, the kind with a pencil loop sewn into the spine and pages that shed rain if you were quick. I knew the type because Elias Thorne had carried one just like it on every route he'd ever drawn, and because I'd spent ten years avoiding the sight of one.

My hands stopped working before my mind agreed to stop.

"Sebastian." Priya's voice, quiet. She'd seen my face change. Everyone had.

"I'm fine," I said, which is what I always say, and opened the cover.

The first page was a title block in a draftsman's neat capitals: **NAM KHA SOURCE EXPEDITION — ROUTE LOG & SURVEY — E. THORNE, LEADER.** Below it, a date. Ten years and two months ago. Below that, in smaller letters, almost an afterthought:

*Felix keeps the pace honest.*

The name went through me like cold water through a wetsuit. Felix. His joke, his private name for me — the lucky one, the survivor, the man who lived while better climbers didn't. He used to say it on the mountain, grinning into the wind: *Slow down, Felix, the rest of us have families.* And I'd laughed, because Elias Thorne laughing made it impossible not to laugh, and because I was twenty-nine and immortal and the word felt like a gift.

It wasn't a gift. It was a prophecy with the wrong ending.

"Whose handwriting?" Okonkwo asked. He's a careful man; he asks questions he already knows the answers to, so you have to hear yourself say them.

"The expedition leader's," I said. "The man who died."

"On the climb that ended your career," Priya said. Not a question either. She'd figured it out days ago, back when the engine logs started bothering her, and she'd kept it to herself the way good crew do — until keeping it became its own kind of lie.

"Yes."

* * *

I read for two hours. I'll tell you what the ledger held, as straight as I can, because for ten years I've told this story crooked — told it small, told it around, told it not at all — and the crookedness has cost me more than the disaster ever did.

Thorne's log was meticulous. Barometric readings, snowpack notes, load manifests, the daily grind of a high-altitude expedition recorded by a man who loved the craft of it. The Nam Kha source expedition had been his last major push — a new line on a peak in the massif above this very river, the mountain whose meltwater was running black and heavy under our hull even now. I'd been his lead guide. I knew every entry before I read it, because I'd been there when he wrote them, hunched in the cook tent with his headlamp and his stubborn half-frozen fingers.

Then came the last week of entries, and the handwriting changed.

Not degraded — sharpened. Compressed. A man writing faster than comfort allows, writing because he's afraid he won't get another chance. The entries got shorter and denser, and the margins filled with notes that had nothing to do with route-finding.

*Fixed ropes on the shoulder cut short. 40m missing. R. says resupply error. Resupply manifest shows full length issued. Who signed it out?*

*R. changed the summit rotation without telling me. Puts himself and S.A. on the first rope team. Why?*

*Found a piton hammer in R.'s kit. We carry none. Asked him. He laughed.*

R. I didn't need the initials spelled out. There had been one R. on that mountain besides me and Thorne. Robert Voss. Marguerite's father. Her late partner, she'd called him on the radio, in that smooth corporate cadence of hers — and I'd taken it to mean business partner, because the truth was a shape my mind refused to make.

Robert Voss had been our high-altitude liaison, the man who handled permits and fixed lines and the money. The man everyone trusted because he was older and calm and generous with the good coffee. The man who, on summit morning, had made one small change to the rotation — moved himself onto my rope team, ahead of schedule, carrying gear he shouldn't have needed — and who had been fifty meters above me when the fixed anchors on the shoulder let go.

The official finding, afterward, was mine. Lead guide responsibility. Anchor failure due to improper placement. My placements. My rope. My name on the incident report, and then on the blacklist, and then nowhere at all, because a man with my record stops being a person and becomes a liability with a pulse.

I had spent ten years believing it. That's the part I need you to understand. I didn't dodge the truth — I *carried* it. Every mile of easy river, every tourist rapid, every birthday I didn't count, I carried the certainty that my anchors had killed Elias Thorne and broken his family and ended four other careers by association. I built a whole life on that foundation, low and floodproof and deliberately joyless, the way you build anything on ground you don't trust.

And here was Thorne's own hand, written in the cold while he still had hands to write with, saying: *the ropes were cut short, the rotation was changed, the man who did it laughed when I asked him why.*

He'd known. He'd known something was wrong and he'd climbed anyway, because Elias Thorne did not turn back from mountains, and because he trusted the rest of us to catch what he couldn't see. And the last entry, dated the night before the summit push, was four words long:

*If I fall, Felix knows nothing. Keep him clear.*

Keep him clear. He'd tried to protect me even then. Even from this.

I put the ledger down on the crate lid because my hands had developed a tremor I didn't recognize, and Priya put her hand flat on the pages before I could pick them up again.

"Breathe," she said. Engineer's advice. In through the nose.

"There's more," Anselm said.

We all turned. The cook had been silent so long I'd half forgotten he was there, folded into the shadow by the fuel locker, and now his voice came out level and terrible with certainty.

"There's more in that book, and there's more that isn't in it, and you're going to want both before you decide what to do." He stepped into the light. For the first time since he'd joined my crew, Anselm looked directly at me, and I saw that his eyes were wet and that he was not ashamed of it. "Because I was on that mountain too, Captain. I was the expedition doctor. And I've been cooking your eggs for six weeks trying to find the courage to say so."

* * *

The rain picked up against the hull. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled down the gorge like a barrel going over stairs, and the river under us answered with that deep pressured voice, louder now, insistent, a sound I was beginning to understand was not the monsoon arriving early but the monsoon announcing itself — the way a man announces himself in a doorway before he enters.

Okonkwo glanced at me. I shook my head once: *not yet.* Whatever the sky was planning, it would have to wait its turn behind Anselm.

The doctor sat down on the crate beside the open ledger, in the space Lin had warmed earlier, and began, quietly, to talk.
