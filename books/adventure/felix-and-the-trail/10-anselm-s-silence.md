## 10. Anselm's Silence

The rice burned.

Not badly — a dark crust along the bottom of the pot, the kind of thing that happens to every cook on every river trip at least once. But Anselm stood over it with the paddle in his hand and did nothing for a full thirty seconds, staring through the steam as if the answer to something were written there, and by the time he scraped the pot the damage was done. Bitter grain, half-ruined meal for six people who needed feeding.

Tomas noticed first. He always noticed food, the way hungry men do, and he made a joke about it — *chef's special, charcoal edition* — and Priya laughed from the engine hatch where she was elbow-deep in the cooling system, and Okonkwo took his bowl without comment because Okonkwo took everything without comment. Lin ate on the gunwale with her cargo cradled between her feet, that oilcloth bundle she never let out of arm's reach, and watched Anselm with her still, patient eyes.

Sebastian was last in line. He took his bowl, tasted it, and said nothing at all. Just looked at Anselm for one long moment over the rim, and Anselm felt the look go through him like a splinter finding its depth.

*He knows,* Anselm thought. *Or he's close.*

Ten years of practice told him otherwise. Ten years of ladling soup in expedition kitchens and river camps and one bad season on a tuna boat, ten years of being the quiet man at the edge of the fire whom nobody questioned because cooks are furniture, cooks are weather, cooks are not people you interrogate. Sebastian Amberdale looked at everyone like that. It wasn't suspicion. It was just the way a man looks when he has already survived the worst question anyone can ask him.

Anselm washed the pot. The river went by, brown and swollen with the early rains, carrying branches past them toward the lowlands like a crowd hurrying somewhere they didn't want to be.

* * *

It was the ledger that did it, of course. It had been doing it for two days now, sitting open on the deck crates whenever the light allowed, Sebastian hunched over Thorne's cramped handwriting like a man reading letters from the dead — which, Anselm thought, was exactly what he was doing. Pages passed hand to hand. Priya read some aloud. Lin translated the passages in the village dialect, the ones Thorne had written in a second script he'd learned from the elders upstream, and each translation landed on the deck like something dropped from a height.

And page forty-one — Anselm knew the number the way you know the number of a grave — page forty-one had come up at dusk yesterday, and Sebastian had read it three times, and then he had looked around the circle of faces and asked, quietly, "Who else was on that mountain?"

Nobody answered. Nobody could. And Anselm had sat there with his hands flat on his knees, feeling the truth press up against the back of his teeth like water against a failing seam, and had said nothing, and had gone below and cooked nothing, and had lain awake in the hold listening to the river work at the hull.

The patched seam held. That was the irony he kept returning to, lying in the dark. Priya's patch held fine. It was everything else that was leaking.

* * *

He found Tomas smoking at the stern near midnight, or Tomas found him — hard to say. The younger man was leaning on the rail with a cigarette cupped against the drizzle, and when Anselm came out to check the mooring lines he didn't move away, which itself was unusual. Tomas liked his secrets alone.

"You eat?" Anselm asked. Stupid question. Cooks ask stupid questions; it's part of the camouflage.

"Can't." Tomas flicked ash into the water. "You ever sell something you can't buy back?"

Anselm kept his hands busy with the line, checking the knot, rechecking it. "Once."

"What?"

"A diagnosis."

Tomas looked at him. In the lamplight spilling from the cabin his face was young and wrecked, the face of a man who had been doing arithmetic all day and hated every total. "You're not what you say you are," Tomas said. "None of us are. I figured that out by day two. But you—" He hesitated, and the cigarette trembled slightly, and Anselm understood that the trembling wasn't fear of Anselm. It was fear of himself, of what he might say next if someone gave him room. "You knew Thorne. Didn't you. You went white as the crate liner when they opened it."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upstream thunder muttered, a sound like furniture being dragged across the sky.

"I was his doctor," Anselm said.

It came out smaller than he'd intended. Smaller than the thing deserved. He had rehearsed this confession for a decade in a hundred languages and none of them, apparently, worked.

Tomas didn't gape. That was the strange mercy of it. He just nodded slowly, the way men nod at bad news they've half expected, and said, "The expedition that killed him."

"The expedition I walked away from."

"Walked away from, or was sent home from?"

Anselm wound the mooring line once around his fist, released it. "There's a difference," he said, "and I've spent ten years telling myself it matters."

* * *

Morning came gray and low, the monsoon front visible now even to people who didn't read weather — a bruised wall of cloud stacked up over the western ridges, eating the peaks one at a time. Priya called it from the engine hatch: pressure dropping, barometer falling faster than the almanac said it should. The flood was coming early. Everyone did the math without saying it aloud. Three days of permits, maybe less, and forty miles of river between them and the concession post.

They gathered under the tarp amidships to divide the remaining dry stores, and it was there — with rain starting to tick against the canvas like fingers testing a lock — that Sebastian closed the ledger, set it down, and looked directly at Anselm for the second time in two days.

"You cooked for him," Sebastian said. Not a question. "On the mountain approach. You knew his habits. Yesterday you moved the lantern off the chart table before I'd finished reading, and you put it exactly where he used to keep it. Left of center. I've only seen one other man do that, and he's dead."

The rain thickened. Under the tarp the six of them made a small lit island, and beyond the tarpaulin edge the river ran high and fast and indifferent.

Anselm set down the rice sack. His hands, he noticed distantly, were perfectly steady. They had been steady through worse.

"My name is Dr. Henrik Anselm," he said. "I was expedition physician on the Thorne ascent. All of it. Base camp to Camp Three." He heard his own voice as if from outside, calm, clinical, the voice he used for delivering things no one wanted to hear. "I signed the medical report afterward. The one that blamed altitude sickness and guide error. My signature is part of why yours is mud, Mr. Amberdale."

Sebastian didn't move. Lin's hand had gone still on her bundle. Tomas, at the edge of the group, had stopped breathing entirely.

"I want to be precise about what I'm confessing," Anselm went on, because precision was the only rope he had left, the thing he'd clung to while everything else drowned. "I didn't falsify the report. I wrote what I believed. Elias Thorne died of trauma, not altitude — I knew that even then, I saw the rope burns, the anchor scars, and I noted them. And then the expedition sponsors reviewed my notes and returned them to me edited, and I signed the edited version, and I told myself that a signature on paper changes nothing on the mountain. That the dead don't read reports. That a man with a suspended license and no testimony worth taking couldn't fight a consortium of lawyers anyway." He paused. Rain drummed. "I have repeated that sentence to myself for ten years, and it has never once been true enough to sleep on."

"What did the edits remove?" Sebastian asked. His voice was very controlled. Anselm recognized the control; he'd heard it in dying patients who wanted to hear the whole prognosis before they let themselves feel anything.

"The anchors," Anselm said. "The upper anchors on the fixed lines were cut, not failed. Partially cut — nicked so they'd tear under load, days later, above the icefall where nobody could inspect them. I examined the recovered hardware. I told the sponsors it was sabotage. They told me it was equipment fatigue. There were seven of them in the room and one of me, and my license was already hanging by a thread from an unrelated incident, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I agreed with them. Out loud. In writing. That's my crime. Not the cutting. The agreeing."

Lin spoke softly, translating herself almost unconsciously into their shared trade language: "Who cut them?"

And here Anselm felt the whole confession snag, the way a hook snags, and he shook his head before the silence could become an accusation.

"I don't know," he said. "That's not evasion. I've turned it over for ten years and I genuinely don't know. Four of us had access to the gear cache at Camp Two. Thorne trusted all four. One of them was me." He met Sebastian's eyes and did not look away, because looking away would be the one unforgivable thing. "So yes. I am on the list of possible men. I have been on my own list for a decade. If you want to put me off this boat at the next landing, I'll go, and I'll take my guilt with me, and it will fit in one bag because I've packed it lightly for years."

The rain hammered. Nobody spoke. Then Sebastian exhaled, long and slow, and Anselm watched something move behind his eyes — not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but recognition. The look of a man meeting another man who has also carried a name that isn't his.

"You examined the hardware," Sebastian said finally. "You held it."

"I held it."

"Then you know it wasn't me."

"I know it wasn't you," Anselm said. "I've known it for ten years. I'm sorry it took a river and a dead man's ledger to say it out loud."

Sebastian looked down at the ledger, at the pages in Thorne's handwriting, and when he spoke again his voice had gone rough at the edges. "He wrote here that he trusted his team completely. Right up to the last entry. Whoever cut those anchors, he never suspected them. He suspected the mountain." A pause. "He deserved better than all of us."

"Yes," Anselm said. "He did."

* * *

Afterward, the boat resumed its ordinary motions — Priya below with the engine, Okonkwo on watch, Lin at the sweep — but the air had changed, the way air changes after a storm passes, rinsed and thinner and harder to hide in. Anselm cooked lunch and it did not burn, and the crew ate it, and the act of feeding them felt for the first time in ten years like penance rather than disguise.

Tomas didn't eat.

Anselm found him at dusk in the lee of the cargo crate, sitting with his knees drawn up, and the younger man looked up at him with an expression Anselm had seen a thousand times across a hundred bedsides — the expression of a patient who has decided today is the day he asks the real question.

"You said four people had access," Tomas said. "To the cache."

"I did."

"And Marguerite Voss hired us. And someone's paying Tomas to make sure certain pages never reach the concession post." He laughed, short and joyless. "I keep doing the arithmetic, chef. I keep getting an answer I don't like."

Anselm crouched beside him. Below them, through the hull, he could feel the river rising — a subtle change in the boat's motion, the current gripping harder, the water climbing the banks in the dark. The flood front was hours away now, maybe less.

"Then tell me the answer," Anselm said gently, "before the river does it for you."

Tomas opened his mouth. And upstream, where the gorge narrowed to a black throat, lightning lit the cloud bank from inside — once, twice — and the thunder that followed rolled down the valley like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and both of them went silent, listening, as the first fat drops of the true rain began to fall.
