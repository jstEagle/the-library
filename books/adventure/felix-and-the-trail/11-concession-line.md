## 11. Concession Line

The permit post at Ban Houayxay sat where the Nam Kha gave up its arguments and became merely a river — wide, brown, obedient. Marguerite Voss had chosen the office deliberately. It had a veranda, a ceiling fan that turned with the dignity of a dying thing, and a window that looked out on the water so that whoever sat behind the desk could watch boats arrive and know, before anyone spoke, whether they carried success or apology.

She was not sitting behind the desk. The desk belonged to a district officer named Khamla, a small precise man with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried manner of someone whose authority did not depend on urgency. Marguerite stood at his window instead, watching the water, holding a radio handset she had not yet raised to her mouth.

"Madame Voss." Khamla tapped the ledger in front of him — an actual ledger, paper, ruled in faded blue lines, which struck her as either charming or ominous. "Your concession permits are stamped through the end of the month. But I must repeat what my colleague told you in Vientiane."

"The flood clause."

"The flood clause." He said it without pleasure. "When the monsoon front arrives and the river rises past the gauge mark on that post—" he nodded toward a red-painted pole driven into the bank, its markings half submerged already in ordinary high water, "—the channel is closed to concession traffic. Your permits do not pause. They void. Whatever your people are doing upstream when that happens, they will be doing it without legal standing, and my officers will not be able to help them. Nor you."

"And when does the front arrive?"

Khamla removed his glasses and polished them on his sleeve, which was his way of saying he was about to deliver news he did not enjoy owning. "The forecast has moved. Ten days ago we were told the eighteenth. Yesterday's bulletin says the fourteenth. This morning—" he set the glasses down carefully, aligned them parallel to the ledger's edge, "—this morning the station at Luang Prabang says possibly the twelfth. The system over the Bay of Bengal strengthened overnight. These things are like people, Madame. They change their minds."

Marguerite did the arithmetic without letting her face move. Twelve days from now, minus the forty miles of gorge between here and the wreck site, minus whatever the Meridian's tired engine could manage against current, minus loading time, salvage time, the thousand small delays that ate schedules like fish ate bait.

It would be close. It might be impossible.

Close was a word she had built a career on.

"Thank you, Officer Khamla," she said. "You've been very clear."

"I am also required to ask," he went on, folding his hands, "what exactly your concession involves. The paperwork says *salvage of historical materials*. That is a broad phrase."

"It's accurate."

"Historical materials." He turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected of being shaved. "From a wreck ten years old? On a stretch of river where, if my memory serves, there was once a climbing expedition that ended badly?" His eyes came up to hers, mild and unblinking. "I was a younger officer then. I remember the helicopters."

Marguerite felt the familiar tightening behind her sternum, the one that arrived whenever anyone mentioned those helicopters, those weeks, that mountain. She kept her voice level. "Then you'll understand why discretion serves everyone, Officer. Including your district."

Khamla considered this for a long moment. Outside, the fan creaked through another revolution. A long-tail boat puttered past the window, low in the water, piled with firewood, the boatman raising two fingers off his tiller in lazy salute.

"I understand many things," Khamla said finally. "What I understand best is that rivers keep their own records, and they charge heavily for copies." He slid a carbon copy of the permit across the desk toward her. "Your crew should hurry, Madame Voss. And they should be careful. The Nam Kha above the second gorge is not a river that forgives bookkeeping errors."

She took the paper. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady; it was one of the first things people learned about her and one of the last things they forgot.

* * *

The radio room was a shed behind the permit office, rented by the hour, smelling of kerosene and hot dust. Marguerite sat alone at the set with the schedule sheet flattened under her elbow and called the Meridian at the appointed time — 1400 hours local, channel 16, then over to working frequency 72.

Three tries before the crackle resolved into Priya's voice, thin and doubled by static, as if the engineer were speaking from inside a tin can rolling downhill.

*"Meridian actual. Go ahead, Voss."*

"Priya. Status."

A pause long enough to be insulting. *"Making way. We're above the ferry village now, into the narrows. Hull seam held after the patch. Engine's running warm but running."*

"Ferry village," Marguerite repeated. She had the chart open on the table beside her, the one she'd commissioned from satellite imagery and a dead man's notes, and she found the mark quickly: a scatter of stilt houses at the mouth of a side channel, marked in her own red ink with a question mark. "You took on a local pilot."

Another pause. Static filled it like water filling a boot.

*"A woman. Lin. She knows the channels above the falls better than any chart does. Sebastian trusts her."*

*Sebastian trusts her.* Marguerite wrote nothing down, because there was no need; she had spent ten years keeping files on Sebastian Amberdale, and the file had taught her that his trust was rarer than his skill and worth more than both. If he trusted this ferrywoman, then this ferrywoman mattered.

"Understood. Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to say it again until you're sick of it." She leaned toward the microphone. "The monsoon front has accelerated. Landfall estimate is now the twelfth, possibly earlier. When the river passes the gauge at the concession post, your permits void. Not suspend. Void. Everything you're carrying becomes contraband, every mile you travel becomes smuggling, and I cannot protect you, the cargo, or the contract. Do you copy?"

This time the pause was shorter, and colder. *"Copy. That's four days early."*

"Four days early is the new on time. I need you to run the engine harder, take the channels your pilot swears by, and cut every stop that isn't loading or floating. I want that crate at the concession post no later than the tenth. The ninth if the river allows."

*"Sebastian sets the pace on the river,"* Priya said. *"That was the deal."*

"Sebastian set the pace ten years ago too," Marguerite said, "and we all know how that ended. Tell him the terms have changed. Tell him the clock is real and it is running and sentiment doesn't wind it."

Static. Then, distantly, another voice on the Meridian's deck — male, low, indistinct — and Priya's hand evidently cupping the mic, because the next transmission came muffled, half-swallowed:

*"He's standing right here. Hold on."*

Marguerite waited. She looked out the shed's open door at the river, at the red gauge pole with its lowest marks already drowned, and she thought about how strange it was that a woman who had spent her life buying other people's time could never buy back her own.

The channel clicked. Sebastian's voice, when it came, was exactly as she remembered it from a decade ago and exactly as different: same gravel, less certainty.

*"Voss. What's this about the twelfth?"*

"You heard the forecast. Front's accelerated. Permits die at first flood. You have roughly six days of legal travel left, and you're still above the second gorge."

*"We know where we are."*

"Do you? Then you know the math doesn't work at cruising speed. Push the engine. Skip the villages. Get to the wreck, get the crate, get downstream. That's the whole job, Sebastian. It always was."

A silence on the line that had texture to it, weight, the particular quality of a man deciding how much truth to spend.

*"And what's in the crate, Marguerite? Since we're being honest about the job."*

Her finger rested on the transmit key. In the shed around her, the kerosene lamp hissed; somewhere beyond the wall, Khamla's clerks were closing the office for the afternoon, their voices rising and falling in a language she'd learned enough of to order food and sign contracts and nothing more intimate than either.

"Elias Thorne's expedition ledger," she said. "Salvage rights to it were filed by his estate three years ago and lapsed. I picked them up through a subsidiary. It's a document of significant historical value. Museums pay for such things. So do collectors."

*"Historical value."*

"That's what I said."

*"Because Anselm opened it."* The words came flat, deliberate, each one placed like a foot on uncertain ice. *"Two nights ago. Against my orders, though I've stopped pretending I can stop him. And the pages I've seen aren't about minerals, Marguerite. They're about people. Rations and rope lengths and who slept where, and then — the last week. Notes in the margins. About the fixed lines. About who checked them."*

The lamp hissed. Downriver, a kingfisher crossed the window's view like a thrown blue knife.

"There may be marginalia," Marguerite said. "Expedition ledgers often contain operational notes."

*"Operational notes."* A short sound that might have been a laugh if laughter ever got that cold. *"Priya says you asked her twice about page counts. Tomas won't look at me. And you just used the word 'minerals' before I said it. So let's not play the museum game. What do you want from Elias Thorne's handwriting, Marguerite? Say it plainly, or I seal this crate with epoxy and we finish the trip hauling ballast."*

She had prepared for this conversation. She had prepared versions of it in the shower, in taxis, in the gray hour before dawn when ambition stood at the foot of the bed like a creditor. She had prepared to lie, to deflect, to charm, to threaten. What she had not prepared for was the exhaustion that rose in her now, sudden and total, like floodwater finding a low place.

Because he was right. Because the museum story was gauze over iron. Because she had wanted this ledger for nine years, since the week after the funerals, when she had gone through Elias Thorne's effects with his widow and found the packing list that showed the field journal had been shipped downriver separately, ahead of the team, and had vanished with the supply boat that sank in the second gorge.

Nine years of knowing the answers to her questions were lying in forty feet of brown water. Nine years of filing permits and chartering sonar surveys and waiting for the concession regime to loosen enough to make recovery legal. Nine years, and now the crate was in the hands of the one man alive who had reason to burn it rather than read it.

"The ledger documents the survey work," she said, and her voice came out quieter than she intended, stripped of its boardroom lacquer. "Elias wasn't just climbing that mountain, Sebastian. He was mapping. The upper valley. There are mineral claims pending on that watershed — claims that go back to his expedition, to samples he carried out. My late partner and I financed that expedition. Both halves of it. The climb and the survey. The ledger contains his assay figures, his coordinates, his geological notes. Without them, the claims lapse. With them—" She stopped. Started again. "With them, they're worth more than everything I own."

*"So it's money."*

"It's *mine*," she said, and heard the crack in it, hated the crack in it. "Ten years of my life and my partner's money and my name on the filings. Yes, Sebastian. I want the pages with the numbers on them. The rest — the margins, the blame, the ghosts — the rest is yours. Take it. Publish it. Nail it to the courthouse door. I don't care. But the survey data comes out of that crate intact, or everything we've all spent on this — the boat, the permits, the miles — is ash."

The radio breathed static at her for five seconds, ten. She watched the gauge pole through the doorway and thought: *even now, even here, the water is rising.*

*"Anselm says the sabotage happened,"* Sebastian said at last, very quietly. *"He won't say who. Yet. But Marguerite — if the ledger names him, if it names who cut those lines — you understand I can't trade that. Not for claims. Not for anything."*

"I'm not asking you to trade it. I'm asking you to bring me the crate and keep your conscience as carry-on luggage. We'll sort the pages at the post, in front of witnesses, with lawyers if you insist. Fair division. I'll even let you read the margins first." She made herself smile, though there was no one to see it; some habits existed purely to hold the face together. "Six days, Sebastian. Ninth of the month. Crate at the concession post. Can you do it?"

Longer silence this time. And beneath the static, faintly, she heard something that chilled her more than any threat could have: rain. Real rain, drumming on a deck a hundred miles upstream, days ahead of any forecast, the monsoon sending its scouts.

*"Ask me when we're moving,"* Sebastian said. *"Meridian out."*

The channel died. Marguerite sat with the handset in her lap and the chart under her elbow and the lamp hissing its small indifferent song, and she allowed herself thirty seconds — she timed them on her watch — of being a woman alone in a hot shed with a dead radio and a decision.

Then she stood, folded the chart along its worn creases, and went to find Officer Khamla, because there was a second matter she needed to raise with him, quietly, off the record: the matter of a helicopter.

Not for rescue. Rescue was for people who had failed.

For retrieval — in case the river decided, as rivers did, to keep its records to itself.

At the permit office she found Khamla locking the document cabinet, and before she could speak, he glanced up at the sky — at the bruise-colored cloud building over the western ridgeline, hours early, fat with someone else's weather — and said, mildly, "Madame Voss. I believe your six days have begun shrinking."

Out on the water, far upstream where she couldn't see, the first rain was already falling on the Meridian's deck.
