## 13. Tomas's Price

Tomas heard the engines before he saw the boats.

That was the thing about debt — it had a sound. He'd learned it in Manila, in the back room of a pawnshop that smelled of mildew and old gold, when two men came to collect what his brother owed and Tomas had signed his name to keep them from breaking Raul's hands. Debt sounded like outboard motors idling too long at a landing where nobody should be landing. It sounded like laughter pitched just loud enough to be heard.

He was on the *Meridian*'s deck with a coil of line in his hands, pretending to work, because for three days now pretending to work was the only honest thing he'd done.

The village spread along the south bank below them — stilt houses, drying nets, children who had already adopted Okonkwo as a climbing frame. Lin's people. They'd tied up at midmorning to take water and let Priya dry the wiring she didn't trust, and Sebastian had said two hours, no more, because the permits were bleeding out day by day and everyone knew it.

Two hours. The boats arrived forty minutes in.

There were three of them, long and low, the kind of fast river craft that cost more than the *Meridian* had cost new. They came around the downstream bend in formation, fanned out, and cut their engines in unison so they drifted the last hundred meters in silence. Men who wanted to be seen arriving gently.

Tomas's hands went cold around the coil of rope.

He knew the lead boat. He'd stood on its deck eleven days ago in Vientiane, in a warehouse that smelled of diesel and money, and shaken a hand that was soft as wet bread, and taken an envelope, and told himself it wasn't betrayal, it was navigation — that Marguerite Voss's backers only wanted the route, only wanted to know where the salvage was headed, that nobody would get hurt, that a man with Raul's hospital bills and Raul's creditors couldn't afford a conscience priced in anything but small denominations.

The soft-handed man stepped onto the landing. He'd shaved since Vientiane. He wore a dry shirt, which on this river was a kind of armor.

"Tomas," he called up, cheerful. "You look terrible."

* * *

Sebastian came out of the wheelhouse like weather changing.

Tomas had watched this man for weeks now — watched him count rapids instead of sleeping, watched him flinch from maps of certain latitudes, watched him open Thorne's ledger with hands that shook and then go very still. He had catalogued every version of Sebastian Amberdale the way you catalogue a river: where it ran deep, where it hid rocks.

He had never seen this version. This one was quiet in a way that made the whole deck go quiet with him.

"You know these men?" Sebastian asked. He didn't look at Tomas when he said it. That was worse than looking.

"They're—"

"We're friends of the concession," the soft-handed man said, climbing the ladder without invitation. Two of his companions stayed at the bottom, and they weren't hiding what they carried anymore. "Aurelio Sanz. We've been trying to reach your employer. Radio's been spotty." He smiled at Sebastian with real warmth, the way a fisherman smiles at a fish. "Marguerite's backers have a concern about the cargo. A legal concern. Permits, provenance, that sort of thing. We thought we'd come discuss it face to face."

"The crate stays sealed until the post," Sebastian said.

"Of course. Of course it does." Sanz turned, and now he did look at Tomas, and the smile widened by exactly one tooth. "Doesn't it, Tomas?"

* * *

Here is what Tomas had told himself in the warehouse, counting the envelope's weight through the paper: *You are not selling the ledger. You are selling coordinates. Coordinates are just numbers. Numbers don't drown anyone.*

Here is what he had not let himself think: *They will come. They always come. And when they do, whatever they want next, you will already be the kind of man who says yes.*

Raul was twenty-six and had a wife and a daughter and a spine held together by titanium rods since the scaffolding collapse, and the men who held his paper did not believe in mercy or installments, and Tomas had been sending money home since he was fifteen, working decks from the Mekong to the Salween, and none of it — none of it — had ever been enough, because debt in his family was not a number. It was an inheritance. His father had died owing the same men. Tomas had understood, signing in that back room, that he was signing for both of them.

He had still signed.

And then he had sat across from Anselm at dinner while the old cook talked about Elias Thorne's handwriting, about the man's terrible jokes, about how he'd hummed when he charted, and Tomas had passed the rice and said nothing, and every night since he lay in his bunk doing arithmetic that never balanced: what the envelope weighed against what Anselm's voice sounded like when he said *Eli*, what Raul's spine weighed against a dead climber's daughter waiting somewhere downstream for her father's last words.

The arithmetic never balanced. That was how he'd known, before anyone said it aloud, that he had done something that could not be paid for.

* * *

"Everyone off the boat," Sanz said pleasantly. "Just for a conversation. Mr. Amberdale, the crew can stay aboard if you prefer, but I'd rather we spoke plainly."

"There's nothing plain about you," Priya said from the engine hatch, wrench in hand. She'd come up silently, oil-black to the elbows, and she positioned herself between Sanz and the hold without making a show of it.

"I'm hurt." Sanz examined his nails. "Mr. Amberdale. My principals' concern is simple. The ledger contains material — commercial material — that predates any valid claim. Mineral surveys. Concession boundaries. If those pages enter the public record at the permit post, a great many people lose a great deal of money, and some of those people are not gentle. The offer is this: the crate comes with us. Your contract is honored in full. Every man aboard is paid double, plus passage home." He paused, delicately. "Plus, for the crew member who facilitated this meeting, a bonus already agreed."

The word *facilitated* went through Tomas like a spear through water — no resistance until it hit bone.

Lin was standing at the rail near the gangplank. She hadn't spoken since the boats arrived. She looked at Tomas the way she looked at water she was about to cross: reading it, finding the channel, deciding whether it could be survived.

Sebastian said, "No."

"Mr. Amberdale—"

"No. And now you've said the quiet part in front of witnesses, so we're done talking." Sebastian's voice didn't rise. "Get off my boat."

Sanz sighed, and the pleasantness drained out of his face like water through a cracked hull, and what was underneath was much older and much simpler. "Then we'll wait for the flood with you," he said. "Three boats burn less fuel than one barge, Mr. Amberdale. We can follow you all the way to the post. Or—" he glanced at the sky, at the bruise-colored wall of cloud stacked up over the western gorge, "—we can be there first, and explain to the officials why your permits were used to smuggle stolen commercial property. Choose your story. Either way, the crate doesn't reach the daughter."

He went down the ladder. The boats backed off fifty meters and anchored, patient as crocodiles.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse, and Sebastian closed the door, and for a moment nobody spoke. Rain started against the windows — fat, warm, preliminary drops, the monsoon clearing its throat.

"He knew the route," Priya said flatly. "He knew where we'd tie up. He knew our schedule better than I did, and I run the schedule." Her eyes went down the line of them and stopped on Tomas, and stopped there. "Someone sold it."

Okonkwo shifted his weight. Anselm set down the tea he'd brought and folded his hands, and even that small motion had the finality of a man setting down something he wouldn't pick up again.

"Tomas." Sebastian didn't turn around. He was watching the three boats through the rain-streaked glass. "Tell me it isn't true."

And here was the moment Tomas had been rehearsing for eleven days, in the dark, in his bunk, in the language of excuses — *I was going to tell you, I only took the route, I never touched the crate* — and standing in the wet light of the wheelhouse with all of them looking at him, he found that every rehearsal was ash. There was nothing left but the thing itself.

"It's true," he said.

His voice came out smaller than he wanted. He made himself stand straight anyway, because if he was going to be this man, he was going to be him upright.

"In Vientiane. Before we cast off. Sanz's people found me — they knew about Raul, they knew everything, they'd bought his debt months ago, I think maybe they bought it *for* me, I don't know, maybe it doesn't matter. They offered to clear it. All of it. In exchange for the route. Just the route, they said. Waypoints, timing, where we'd stop." He swallowed. "I told myself it was just numbers. I'm sorry. I'm — God, I'm sorry. I didn't know about the ledger then. I didn't know what they wanted destroyed. But I knew someone wanted something, and I took the money anyway, and every day since I kept telling myself I'd fix it somehow, and I never did, because that's what I am. I'm the guy who doesn't fix it."

The rain thickened. Somewhere below, a child laughed, oblivious, and the sound was unbearable.

"Thorne," Anselm said quietly. "The sabotage pages. If Sanz burns that ledger, Eli's name stays dirty forever, and whoever rigged that climb walks free." He wasn't angry. Somehow that was worse. He just looked tired, and old, and like a man who had buried enough friends. "You understand what you sold, Tomas? Not coordinates. The truth about how a man died."

"Yes," Tomas said. "I understand it now."

"And us," Priya said. "Forty miles of rising water with those three behind us. You understand that too?"

"Yes."

Sebastian finally turned from the window.

Tomas braced for it — the fist, the marlin spike, being put ashore in the rain with his bag, which would be fair, which would be merciful compared to what he deserved. He had been put ashore before, metaphorically, his whole life; he knew the choreography. Anger first, then the sentence, then the door.

Sebastian looked at him for a long time. And what was in his face wasn't anger. It was recognition — the specific, terrible recognition of a man who had spent ten years refusing to explain a disaster, who had let the world write its own story about Elias Thorne's death because telling the truth cost more than he could pay. Tomas had sold a route. Sebastian had sold ten years of silence. They were both men who'd chosen the cheaper lie.

"I'm not going to hit you," Sebastian said. "I want you to know that I want to. But hitting you fixes nothing, and I've had enough of things that can't be fixed." He crossed to the chart table and put one finger on the map, on the drowned forest between them and the concession post. "So here's what happens instead. You're going to earn this back in rope and river miles, starting now, starting with the worst water any of us has ever run. And when we reach the post — if we reach it — you're going to stand in front of Thorne's daughter and the officials and tell them exactly what you told us. On record. Your name attached to it."

Tomas's throat closed. "Yes," he managed.

"That's not mercy," Sebastian said. "Mercy would be putting you ashore. This is work." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but related to one, the way a cousin is related. "Welcome to the crew."

Priya made a sound that might have been disgust or might have been laughter. Okonkwo clapped Tomas on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, which was its own verdict. Anselm picked his tea back up.

Only Lin didn't move. She was still at the rail, outside the wheelhouse, rain running off her chin, and when Tomas came down the steps she was waiting for him.

"My father ferried this river thirty years," she said. "Men lied to him. Men stole from him. Once a man sold the location of his crossing to bandits, and three travelers died." Her eyes were steady, dark water. "My father still carried him across the river the next morning. Do you know why?"

Tomas shook his head.

"Because the river doesn't ask what you were yesterday. Only what you're carrying today." She nodded once, toward the three boats lying patient in the rain. "They are following us into the flood, yes? Then they will learn what we learn. The river judges everyone equally." She turned to go, then paused. "Your brother. The one with the broken spine. When this is done, we will find a way. The village knows people in Vientiane who are not gentle either, but who are *ours*."

She walked away toward her father's barge, and Tomas stood in the rain and discovered that shame and relief weigh almost exactly the same, and that after years of carrying one, the other felt almost identical on his shoulders — except lighter, except lighter.

* * *

By late afternoon the storm front had eaten the western sky whole, and the river had gone the color of gunmetal and begun to lift, log-boils spinning past the hull, the current audibly stronger, a deep animal pull. Sanz's three boats rode their anchors with lights coming on, one by one, like eyes opening.

Sebastian stood at the wheel studying the channel ahead, where the gorge narrowed and the drowned forest began. Priya had the engine warmed. Lin was coiling lines. Okonkwo checked the crate's lashings a fourth time.

Tomas took his position at the bow lines, where the worst of the spray would hit, where he belonged.

"Front's early," Priya said quietly, watching the sky. "Hours early, maybe less."

Sebastian's hand tightened on the throttle. Through the rain, far upstream, the river made a sound Tomas had never heard before and understood immediately, the way animals understand fire — a long, low, gathering roar, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Hold on to something," Sebastian said. "All of you. It's coming."
