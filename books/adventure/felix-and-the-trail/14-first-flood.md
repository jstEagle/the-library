## 14. First Flood

The river told us before the sky did.

I'd been asleep in the wheelhouse chair, boots off for the first time in six days, when the sound woke me — a low grinding under the hull, like the Meridian was chewing stone. I came up out of the chair with my heart already running. The water outside had changed color overnight. It had gone the brown of strong tea left too long, and it was moving faster than it had any right to move this far ahead of schedule.

Okonkwo was already on deck. He stood at the rail with the radio pressed to his ear and his face lit by the instrument panel, and I knew before he spoke.

"Front's here," he said. "Weather station at Luang Prabang confirms it. They're calling it three days early." He lowered the radio slowly, the way you lower something that might bite. "Sebastian, they're calling it *days* early."

"How far behind us is the wall?"

"Behind us? Nothing's behind us." He pointed upstream, into the dark. "It's coming down the gorge right now."

I looked at the water again and did the arithmetic I'd been doing all week without letting myself finish it. The Nam Kha drains a hundred valleys I'd never let myself look at on a map. When the monsoon front hits those headwaters, everything they hold comes down this channel at once, and the river doesn't rise so much as it arrives.

"Tomas," I called. "Get everyone up. Priya, I want the engine warm and the anchor chain free."

Tomas appeared from below with his shirt half-buttoned and his eyes already wide. He'd been sleeping badly for days — we'd all noticed, nobody'd said anything — but tonight he moved like a man who'd been waiting for exactly this alarm, maybe hoping for it.

"On it," he said, and went.

Priya came up the companionway wiping grease from her hands, took one look at the water, and said a word in Tamil that needed no translation. Then she dropped down the ladder into the engine room without another word, because that was Priya: fear converted directly into torque.

Lin stood at the bow rail, where she'd spent most of every night since her village. She didn't turn around when I came to stand beside her.

"It will not be gradual," she said. Her English was precise, learned from a dead man's books, and she used each word like a tool set down carefully. "My father called it the river standing up. One hour the water is high. The next hour the water is everywhere."

"How long do we have?"

She considered the current, the color, the smell of it — mud and crushed vegetation and something mineral underneath, the mountain itself being carried away. "Two hours," she said. "Perhaps less."

The Meridian sat at anchor in a widening of the gorge, forty miles below the wreck site, forty miles above the concession post, tied to a granite shelf that had held boats since before either of us was born. Forty miles of drowned forest, collapsed banks, and rapids that would be unrecognizable by morning. Three days until the permits died. And now no margin at all.

I got everyone on deck. Six faces in the rain-light: Okonkwo calm the way deep water is calm; Priya coming up from the engine room with her hair plastered down; Anselm holding a lantern like a man carrying a candle through a cathedral; Tomas unable to meet anyone's eyes; Lin watching me, always watching me, as if my face were a page she was still learning to read.

"The flood front hits within two hours," I said. "When it does, this anchorage becomes part of the river. We can't run downstream — the barge draws too much for the shallows once the banks go under, and the gorge below is suicide in flood water. So we ride it out here, tied to the shelf, and we pray the lines hold."

"And if they don't?" Priya asked.

"Then we swim."

Nobody laughed. Nobody expected to.

* * *

We spent the next hour preparing for a disaster we couldn't stop and could only survive. That's most of guiding, if I'm honest — not the romance the brochures sell, but the slow work of making catastrophe inconvenient instead of fatal.

Priya double-lashed the engine and sealed the bilge pumps open. Okonkwo broke out the life vests and made everyone put them on, including Lin, who wore hers like an insult and kept it buckled anyway. Anselm moved the food stores up from the flooded-prone hold and lashed them to the cabin roof under tarps, working with a methodical grief, as though he were packing a house he'd lived in for years rather than a boat he'd cooked on for nine days.

And the crate — Thorne's crate, the ledger of the dead — we moved to the highest point on the vessel, lashed to the wheelhouse roof rail with four separate lines, wrapped in the last of the waterproof sheeting. I did the final knots myself. My hands remembered rope the way other men's hands remember prayer.

"You could leave it," Tomas said quietly, beside me. He wasn't looking at the crate. He was looking at his own hands. "If it comes to choosing. Between the crate and—"

"There's no choosing," I said. "Not yet."

He nodded and walked away, and I watched him go and felt the shape of the thing I'd been refusing to see all week press against the inside of my chest. A man doesn't ask whether you might abandon the cargo unless he's already priced what abandoning it would buy him. I'd seen that look before. I'd worn it myself, ten years ago, on a mountain, when I was the one who knew something the others didn't.

But there was no time to pull that thread. The river was pulling its own.

* * *

It came at 3:40 in the morning, and it came the way Lin's father must have described it: the river stood up.

There was no dramatic crest, no movie wave. There was a sound first — a long rolling thunder from upstream, continuous, like the gorge itself clearing its throat — and then the water simply rose. A foot in minutes. Two feet. The Meridian swung hard on her anchor line and the bow plunged into a swell that hadn't existed thirty seconds earlier, and spray came over the rail in sheets, warm as bathwater, smelling of the whole drowned watershed.

"All hands!" I shouted, and the storm became our whole world.

What followed was four hours of the hardest work any of us had ever done, and most of it blurs together now the way trauma blurs — flashes, fragments, hands on rope.

I remember the first line parting. The stern spring snapped with a report like a rifle and the boat slewed sideways, and Priya was out on the pitching deck in seconds with a knife in her teeth cutting loose the tangle before it fouled the propeller. I remember Okonkwo hauling the spare anchor to the bow, moving across a deck that tilted thirty degrees one way and then the other, planting himself like a man who had decided the ocean owed him nothing and he owed the ocean nothing back.

I remember Lin. She never raised her voice. While the rest of us fought the boat, she watched the water — actually watched it, reading the surface the way Priya read gauges — and twice she put her hand on my arm and said, simply, "Now," and both times I shifted the remaining line to the cleat she pointed at, and both times the surge that followed would have torn that cleat out of the deck if the load hadn't been moved. She'd grown up on this river. She knew its grammar better than it knew itself.

And I remember Tomas on the bow line.

The main anchor dragged at some point past five — I heard the shudder come up through the hull, the horrible granular scrape of flukes losing their bite in scoured gravel — and after that we were only postponing. The Meridian swung broadside to the current, and the current, drunk on a hundred valleys, pushed her toward the eastern bank, where the drowned forest began: trees standing hip-deep in floodwater, their branches waiting at the exact height of our superstructure.

"Hard over!" Priya screamed from the engine room, giving us everything the old girl had, and it wasn't enough, and we all knew it wasn't enough, and we drove her anyway.

The first tree took the starboard rail. The second took the wheelhouse roof, and the crate's lashings sang like guitar strings. The third took the hull.

I felt it more than heard it — a long tearing along the port side, the sound of planking being opened like an envelope — and the Meridian shuddered, listed, and began, with the terrible patience of all large dying things, to lie down in the water.

"Off the boat!" I roared. "Crate first! Crate first!"

Here is what I will carry to my grave, and what I'll carry without shame: six people chose the crate. Not one of them hesitated. Okonkwo and Tomas cut the lashings while Priya and Anselm braced them against the list, and Lin and I hauled the crate — two hundred pounds of sealed timber and a dead man's handwriting — onto the roof, and from the roof we passed it hand over hand into the flooded shallows where the forest thinned near a gravel bar that had been underwater an hour ago and was, by some mercy of eddy currents, emerging now.

The water was waist-deep and moving like a conveyor belt. We formed a chain in it — Lin upstream, breaking the current with her body, then me, then Okonkwo, then the others — and we passed the crate down the line and dragged ourselves after it, and behind us the Meridian settled onto the drowned trees with a groan I felt in my teeth.

I got the last person ashore — Anselm, who weighed nothing and apologized the whole way — and turned to watch her go.

She went slowly. The river took its time about it, almost courteous, rolling her once against the trees, stripping away the tarps and the deck gear and nine days of our lives, and then sliding her under the surface between two trunks, and the brown water closed over the name on her bow, and that was that.

Ten years I'd measured my life in miles of river, and the river had just repossessed the measuring stick.

* * *

Dawn came gray and relentless, rain falling straight down as if the sky had given up on weather and settled for plumbing.

We stood on the gravel bar, six people and one crate, and took inventory of what the world still contained. Life vests, yes. The emergency bag Anselm had the presence of mind to grab — flares, a tarp, a medical kit, three days of food if we lied to ourselves about portion sizes. Priya had saved her tool roll. Okonkwo had the radio, dry inside his jacket, though the battery was at half and the concession post was forty miles downstream and probably out of range behind the gorge walls. Lin had a knife and a length of rope and an expression that suggested she'd survived worse mornings, which later she confirmed she had.

Everything else — the boat, the engines, the dive gear, the tents, the paychecks we'd never collect — was somewhere under forty feet of monsoon, distributed along two miles of drowned forest.

"We could have died," Priya said finally. She said it not as complaint but as discovery, testing the fact's weight. "Any of us. All of us. And we stood in that water arguing about a box of paper."

"Not paper," Anselm said quietly. Everyone looked at him. He was staring at the crate, and his face had the look of a man attending a funeral he'd avoided for a decade. "You don't understand what's in there yet. But you will."

Nobody asked him to explain. Something in his voice closed the subject like a door.

Tomas sat apart on a fallen log, soaked through, arms around his knees. He hadn't spoken since the boat went down. I caught him looking at the crate with an expression I couldn't file — not greed, not anymore, whatever it had been on the night he keyed the radio. It looked closer to terror. As if the crate had become the thing that was going to kill him, one way or another, whichever direction he turned.

I should have gone to him then. I tell myself I would have, if the radio hadn't crackled.

It was Marguerite's voice, thin and furious through the static, patched through some relay post upstream: *"Meridian, Meridian, confirm status. Confirm status immediately."*

Okonkwo looked at me. I took the handset.

"This is Amberdale. The Meridian is lost. Total loss, crew safe. We're on a gravel bar, mile marker—" I looked at Lin. She held up fingers. "—kilometer sixty-one. Permits expire in three days. We need extraction or we need a miracle."

Static. Long enough that I thought the relay had died. Then Marguerite again, and the fury had drained out of her voice, replaced by something colder and more careful, which from Marguerite was far worse:

*"There is no extraction, Sebastian. Every launch on this river is pulled for flood season as of this morning. You knew the terms. The permits void at first flood — and the flood has arrived."*

"The flood arrived early. You know it did. The forecast said—"

*"The permits say what the permits say."* A pause, and when she resumed, her voice had dropped into the register she used for closing deals. *"But concessions are negotiable. Always have been. Get the cargo to the post before the channel closes and certain conversations can be had. How soon can you move?"*

I looked down the river. Where the channel had run yesterday there was now a single churning brown sea from bank to bank, forty miles of it, punctured by the crowns of drowned trees. On foot, along the collapsing banks, through rain and rising water, carrying two hundred pounds of crate.

"Three days," I said. "Maybe."

*"Then you'd better start walking."* And she signed off, and the static filled the space where her ambition had been, and we all stood there a moment in the rain, six castaways and a dead man's ledger, listening to the river eat the world.

It was Lin who broke the silence. She'd been studying the downstream bank, and now she turned to me, and the rain ran off her chin, and she said:

"My father's ferry barge is hauled up at Ban Houayxay. Two miles inland. It is old, and it leaks, and it has no engine — only poles and a rudder and the current." She paused. "It is also the only thing on this river that can float in the next three days."

I looked at her. She looked back, and beneath the rain and the exhaustion there was something else in her face — the thing she'd been watching me for since the crossing village, the reason she'd boarded a stranger's boat with no cargo and no explanation.

"But it carries a price," she said. "And the price is a conversation, Sebastian. About a man whose letters I have carried for ten years, and never delivered."

The rain hammered the gravel. Downstream, the flood rolled on toward the concession post, eating our three days one hour at a time.

"Tell me on the way," I said.
