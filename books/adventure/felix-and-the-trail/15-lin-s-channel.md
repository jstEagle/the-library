## 15. Lin's Channel

The barge had not moved in nine years.

Lin stood at the edge of the flooded banana grove where her father's ferry lay half-sunk in the shallows, its hull green with river moss, its deck a garden of silt and seedlings. The monsoon had come early and the water had risen to meet it, lapping at the gunwales as if the river were trying to reclaim what her father had borrowed from it all those years. She had told herself she came to look. To count the rot. To prove to herself it was impossible.

She was lying, and she knew it.

Behind her, up the slope through the drowned trees, the strangers were doing what strangers did — salvaging what they could from the wreck of their own boat. She could hear Priya's voice carrying over the water, clipped and precise, cataloguing losses. Okonkwo hauling crates onto high ground. The one called Tomas silent, which was itself a kind of noise. And Sebastian Amberdale — or whatever his name truly was; the village elders had called him Felix before he'd ever corrected them, and he had let them — standing knee-deep in the current with Thorne's ledger strapped to his chest like a child.

Nine years since she had stood on this deck. Nine years since her father died and left her the ferry route nobody used anymore, because the new road on the far bank had taken every passenger who could afford a bus ticket. The *Naga's Daughter*, he had named her. Lin had been fifteen when he launched her, sixteen when he taught her to read the water the way other fathers taught daughters to read faces. Seventeen when the letters started arriving.

She waded out. The water was warm and brown and moving faster than it should have been for the season, and every step reminded her that the flood front the radio had promised was not coming in three days. It was coming tomorrow. Maybe tonight.

The barge rocked when she pulled herself over the rail, and something inside the hull shifted with a groan like an old man turning in bed. She knelt and pressed her palm flat against the deck planking. Wet, but solid. Her father had built her of ironwood because ironwood did not forgive carelessness but did forgive time. He used to say: *A boat is a promise you keep every day or lose forever.*

She had stopped keeping the promise. That was the truth under everything else.

* * *

She found Sebastian at the tree line, wringing out a rope that no longer mattered, and she did not ask him politely. Politeness was for people with time.

"Your boat is dead," she said. "I watched her go. I'm sorry."

He looked up. There was river water in his hair and something older than river water in his face. "So are we," he said. "Forty miles short, three days of permit, and no way down the channel except swimming."

"There is a way." She heard her own voice go careful, the way it went whenever she spoke of her father's things. "But it isn't free."

Priya appeared at his shoulder, wiping grease from her hands onto cloth that was already beyond saving. "We have almost nothing left to pay with."

"I don't want money." Lin looked past them both, at the crate sitting on the high ground under a tarp, at Okonkwo standing over it with his arms crossed like a man guarding a grave. "I want passage. For my cargo."

Sebastian's eyes narrowed. "What cargo?"

"You'll see it when you agree to carry it." She knew how that sounded. She said it anyway. "That's the price. You carry it sealed, you carry it whole, and you deliver it where I tell you to deliver it. You don't open it, you don't read it, and you don't ask me again what's inside."

"That's a strange bargain," Priya said slowly, "from a woman whose passengers we've been reading all week."

Lin almost smiled. Almost. "Your cargo is paper about a dead man's mountains. Mine is paper from a dead man's hands. We are not so different, you and I."

Sebastian went very still. She had learned to watch him — it was the first thing she'd learned about him, back at the crossing, before she even knew why she was watching — and stillness was his tell. When he was afraid he moved. When something struck bone, he stopped.

"Elias wrote letters," he said quietly. Not a question.

"To this village." She held his gaze and made herself not flinch. "He stayed here six weeks before the climb. He fixed our pump. He argued with my father about the ferry schedule until they became friends. And he taught a girl who couldn't sit still how to read contour lines in the dirt with a stick." She paused. "Me. He taught me. Then he walked up your mountain and never walked down, and the letters kept coming for two years after, and I never delivered a single one."

"Why not?"

"Because delivering them meant admitting he was dead." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she understood that she had been carrying them ten years the way he had been carrying his mountain. "My father asked me to take them to the families upstream — Elias had sponsors, teachers, a woman in Vientiane who paid for the school books. I put them in a waterproof box and I hid the box, and every year I told myself next season. Next dry season. Next year." She heard her voice flatten. "Next year is tomorrow now. The flood will take my father's house, and the box sits under its floorboards, and if I don't move it tonight it goes into the river with everything else."

Nobody spoke. Down the slope, Anselm was building a cook fire out of wet wood with the patience of a man who had failed at harder things.

"So," Lin said. "There is a barge. My father's. Half-sunk, mostly sound, engine seized — though your engineer looks like a woman who enjoys impossible engines. She can run the shallow channel along the east bank, the one no outside pilot knows, the one my father used when the main channel flooded. It's narrow, it's fast water, and there's a gorge midway where the river drops and does not forgive." She looked at Sebastian directly. "I can take you forty miles in two days on her. Maybe. If the engine lives and the hull holds and the river permits. Three maybes and a perhaps."

"And in exchange," Sebastian said, "we carry your box."

"Your hands on the ropes beside mine. Your crew poling when the engine dies, and it will die. And when we reach the concession post — if we reach it — my letters go with your ledger into whatever room holds the record. Delivered. Witnessed. On paper, at last." She swallowed. "Ten years late is better than never. I have to believe that, or I have nothing."

* * *

They brought Priya to see the barge at low afternoon light, when the rain had thinned to a gray drifting mist that made the whole valley look like something remembered rather than seen.

Priya walked the deck twice without speaking. She opened the engine hatch and stared down into it for a long time, and Lin watched her face do the arithmetic of rust and seizure and hope.

"Lister diesel," Priya said finally. "Old military surplus. My grandfather had one on his fishing boat off Kochi." She reached into the housing and pulled out a fistful of green corrosion and held it up like evidence. "Seized solid. Probably ten years solid."

"My father died and I let her fill with rain," Lin said. "Say what you're going to say."

Priya wiped her hands and looked at the hull lines instead of at Lin, the way engineers look at boats, which is more honestly than they look at people. "Ironwood planking on teak frames. Whoever built her built her to outlive his grandchildren." A pause. "The planks below the waterline will be soft. The seams will weep no matter what I do. But she doesn't need to be seaworthy. She needs to be river-worthy for forty miles, and those are different religions." She turned. "Give me until midnight and a fire hot enough to work steel, and I'll tell you whether the engine turns. If it turns, we float. If it doesn't, we float anyway, on poles and current and stupidity, and I'd rather know which boat I'm dying on."

Lin felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for nine years. "You talk like my father."

"He sounds like he was right a lot," Priya said, and climbed down into the engine bay.

* * *

Night came down black and loud. The rain returned in earnest, hammering the tarp over the crate, and by the light of Anselm's fire the crew worked like the crew they had become — Okonkwo and Tomas rolling barrels of salvaged fuel up the bank, Anselm feeding everyone rice and river fish with the calm of a man performing surgery, Priya's silhouette bent inside the engine housing while sparks spat orange against the dark.

Lin sat by the fire with Sebastian, and between them, wrapped in oilcloth, she placed the ledger.

He had barely let it out of his reach since the hold. Now he set it down between them deliberately, and she understood it was a gesture, and understood also that gestures were the only language he trusted himself to speak.

"The pages I read," he said. "Before the flood took the boat. There's an entry from the night before the summit push. Thorne writes that someone re-rigged the fixed lines at Camp Three. He doesn't say who. He says he was going to turn back, and then—" Sebastian stopped. His jaw worked. "Then the entry just ends. Next page is the descent log in another hand."

"Anselm's hand," Lin said. "I've seen his writing on the supply manifests my father kept. He signed for quinine in the spring of that year."

Sebastian looked at her sharply. "You knew Anselm was connected to the expedition?"

"I knew a doctor stayed at the village with him. I didn't know he was the same man until I saw his hands last week. Doctors' hands remember things." She fed a stick to the fire. "Everyone on this river is carrying someone, Felix. Some of us just hide the weight better than others."

"Don't call me that."

"It's what the village calls you. It's what the old photograph calls you." She said it gently, and watched it land anyway, the way a stone lands in still water. "Elias named you. Lucky one. The one who lived. You've spent ten years punishing yourself for surviving, and I know that look because I've worn it in a mirror every time I walked past the floorboard where his letters sleep."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, Priya shouted something triumphant and wordless, and Okonkwo laughed — a deep, rare sound, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"If we do this," Sebastian said, "the river will try to kill us. The gorge especially. Your father's channel through the narrows — can you actually read it? After nine years?"

Lin thought of the map drawn in dirt with a stick, of a tall foreign man laughing at her mistakes, of her father's voice saying *a boat is a promise*. 

"No," she said honestly. "Not alone. But I remember enough, and you'll be on the ropes, and between my memory and your fear we might make a working pilot." She reached across the fire and touched the oilcloth over the ledger, once, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you need to trust you. "Two cargoes, Felix. Yours is the truth about how a man died. Mine is the truth about how he lived. Neither one is worth anything floating in a flooded house or buried in a crate. They only mean something delivered."

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain drummed the tarp. Down the bank, the *Naga's Daughter* rocked in the dark, her hull groaning as the river rose around her, impatient, hungry, alive.

"All right," Sebastian said at last. "We carry yours. You carry ours. One cargo." He extended his hand across the fire, and Lin took it, and his grip was cold from the river and steady as a cleat.

From the darkness above them, Priya's voice rang out, hoarse and exultant: "She turns! Engine turns! Barely — she smokes like a temple — but she turns!"

And then a second voice, smaller, from the edge of the firelight where Tomas had been sitting apart from everyone all evening, a radio handset half-hidden against his chest, its green dial glowing like an eye:

"They know we're moving," Tomas said. "Someone's already launched from the concession post. And it's not Marguerite's launch."
