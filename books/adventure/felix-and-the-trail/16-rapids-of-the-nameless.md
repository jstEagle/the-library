## 16. Rapids of the Nameless

Okonkwo had dived in water that could kill you before — cold enough to stop a heart, deep enough to swallow light — but he had never done it on purpose, at speed, inside a gorge with no name on any map he trusted.

Lin called it the Rapids of the Nameless. She said it without ceremony, the way she said everything, one hand on the tiller of her father's barge, the other pointing upstream to where the river narrowed into a throat of black limestone.

"The old people would not name it," she said. "To name a thing is to claim you understand it."

"And do we?"

"No." She looked at him, and there was something almost like apology in it. "But we go anyway."

The barge was forty feet of rot patched with hope. Her father had built it to ferry rice and goats across slack water, not to run whitewater, and every plank seemed to know it. The engine — Priya's third resurrection of it in two days — knocked and coughed below decks like a man arguing in his sleep. Their cargo rode amidships under tarps: Thorne's crate wrapped in three layers of plastic and Lin's letters bundled in oilcloth beside it, because somewhere between the village and the first rapid, the two cargoes had stopped being separate things. Okonkwo understood that now. You did not carry a dead man's ledger down a river for money. You carried it because the river had decided you would.

Sebastian stood at the bow with a pole, scanning the water the way Okonkwo imagined he'd once scanned icefalls — reading texture, reading threat. Ten years of running easy tourist water had not softened him. It had just taught him patience, which was worse, or better; Okonkwo hadn't decided.

"Scout says the drop is runnable center-left," Sebastian called back. "After that, a mile of standing waves, then the gorge pinches. If the rudder holds, we hold."

*If.* Okonkwo had heard a lot of *if* since the Meridian died. He'd learned to stop hearing it as doubt and start hearing it as arithmetic.

Tomas was coiling rope at the stern, and he'd been coiling the same rope for ten minutes, over and over, because his hands needed work or they'd shake. Okonkwo had watched him since the confession at the village landing — watched him take the mercy nobody offered and start paying it back in labor. Up before everyone. Last to eat. First to grab the ugly jobs. It didn't erase what he'd sold. But rivers were generous that way: they gave a man exactly one place to stand, and whatever he'd been before, he was what he did next.

"Okonkwo." Priya's head came up through the engine hatch, face smeared with grease. "I want you to hear this before we commit. The shaft bearing is shot. I've got maybe an hour of hard running in it. After that, if anything fouls the rudder, we steer with poles and prayers."

"Poles I have," Sebastian said. "Prayers are Anselm's department."

From the stern, where the old doctor sat wrapped in a blanket despite the heat, Anselm raised his tin cup an inch. "Prescribed daily," he said. "Side effects include drowning."

Nobody laughed, but everybody smiled a little, and that was the same thing on a boat like this.

Lin brought the barge around the last bend, and the gorge opened its mouth.

* * *

It was worse than the scout report. Reports always were; water didn't write them.

The river dropped away through a staircase of holes, each wave taller than a man, and the walls closed in until Okonkwo could have touched both sides with a pole if he'd dared let go. The sound was total — not loud so much as *everywhere*, a pressure in the chest, in the teeth. Spray hung in the air like weather that had given up on falling.

"Center-left!" Sebastian roared, and Lin put them in.

The first hole hit like a truck. The barge rose, hung, and slammed down hard enough to crack something below — Okonkwo heard it through his boots, felt Priya's curse come up through the deck planks. Water came aboard in tons, found the scuppers too small, and stayed.

Then the second wave, and the third, and the world became white noise and green water and Lin's voice cutting through it all, flat and certain: "*Left. Left. Hard left.*"

They threaded it. Okonkwo would replay those ninety seconds for the rest of his life — Sebastian's pole biting, Tomas hauling himself along the gunwale to add his weight where Lin pointed, Priya coaxing a dying engine through a gap the width of a coffin — and then the standing waves took them and the steering went strange.

Not gone. *Strange.* The barge answered the tiller a half-beat late, then a full beat, then swung when it shouldn't.

"Fouled!" Priya screamed up through the hatch. "Something's on the rudder!"

Ahead, the gorge pinched. The river funneled into a slot barely twice the barge's width, and beyond it Okonkwo could see the reason the old people hadn't named this place: a recirculating hole the size of a compound, chewing foam, fed by the whole weight of the monsoon-swollen Nam Kha. A boat without steering in that pinch would go sideways, broach, and be eaten.

Options ran through his head fast and all of them were bad. Cut the rudder free from above? Couldn't reach it — the tiller linkage sat behind a rotten transom board, underwater at speed. Beach it? No bank. Both walls were vertical stone slicked black with a decade of flood lines.

Which left the water.

He'd made the decision before he finished thinking it, which was how he knew it was right. In twenty years of salvage diving he'd learned that the good decisions arrive already made, and your job afterward is just to survive having made them.

"Tomas! Rope!" He was already kicking off his boots, shrugging out of his shirt, checking the knife on his belt. "Belay me off the stern cleat. Thirty feet, no more. If I signal, pull. If I don't—" he met the younger man's eyes, "—pull anyway."

"I can go instead—"

"You can't dive. This isn't a request." He said it without heat. There wasn't time for heat. "Rope. Now."

Tomas moved. And here was the thing Okonkwo noticed even then, even with the roar filling his skull: Tomas's hands didn't shake anymore. They'd shaken for days after the confession, fumbling knots, dropping things. Somewhere in the last two days of poling drowned forest and bailing gray water, the shaking had stopped. He tied a proper rescue bowline around Okonkwo's chest, cinched it, tested it twice, and gave the signal-ready slap on the cleat like a man who had done it a hundred times.

Maybe shame, properly carried, was just another kind of strength. Okonkwo filed the thought away and jumped.

* * *

The Nam Kha hit him like a fist made of the entire sky.

Cold first — glacial melt braided somewhere far upstream into the flood-warm water, a seam of it that closed around his chest and squeezed. Then the tumble: the current took him, rolled him, slammed him against the hull, and for three full seconds he was nothing but a body in a machine, tumbling in brown chaos with no up and no down.

Training held. Elbows in. Chin tucked. Exhale slow through the nose — never hold your breath in whitewater, the air pocket in your chest is a buoy you can't afford to lose. Count. One. Two. Three. The rope snapped taut and dragged him alongside the hull, and he got a hand into the trailing line and hauled himself hand over hand toward the stern while the river tried to peel him off like a label.

He found the transom board by feel — rotten wood gone soft as bread — and pulled himself under the counter of the barge into the relative calm of the wake eddy, where the water swirled backward and the noise dropped from unbearable to merely terrible.

There was just enough light. Gray-green, shifting, the underside of the world.

The rudder hung in its fittings like a broken wing. Wrapped around it, tight as a tourniquet, was a tree — a whole tree, trunk thick as his thigh, branches stripped to spikes, snagged on the rudder stock and dragging its root ball through the water like an anchor with ambitions. Every swing of the tiller fought that root ball. Every wave drove it deeper into the fittings.

Okonkwo got his knife out and started cutting.

The wood was waterlogged and dense as ironwood. His blade bit shallow. He braced his boots against the transom frame, hooked his arm through the rudder stock so the current couldn't take him, and sawed. The rope at his chest went slack and taut, slack and taut — Tomas paying it out and taking it up, keeping him connected to the world of air, reading the rhythm of the boat above him without being told. Good hands. The hands of someone who had decided, finally, what side of the rope he stood on.

A branch spike caught his forearm and opened it. He felt the cold water turn warm around the cut and thought, distantly, *Anselm will lecture me,* and kept sawing.

His lungs began their argument. Sixty seconds under, seventy. The knife worked through the main limb — halfway, two-thirds. Above him the barge groaned as the gorge walls narrowed, the current accelerating, the slot coming fast. He could feel it through the hull, a rising hum, the river gathering itself.

Eighty seconds. Spots bloomed at the edge of his vision, soft and gold.

He remembered, absurdly, his grandmother's kitchen in Lagos, the smell of pepper soup, the way she'd said: *the river gives back what you give it with both hands.* He'd been seven. He'd thought it was about fish.

The limb parted.

The tree swung free, the current grabbed it, and for one horrible instant the root ball pendulumed straight at him — he shoved off the transom, the rope snapped tight, and Tomas *pulled*, hauled him up the hull like a fish on a line, out of the path of forty feet of dead timber cartwheeling downstream into the slot they still had to run.

Okonkwo broke the surface gasping, and the first thing he saw was Tomas leaning back against the rope with his whole body, boots braced, face purple with effort, screaming — "Got you! I got you!" — and meaning it with everything he had.

The second thing he saw was the slot, fifty meters ahead, and Lin's voice rising over the roar:

"*Steer! We have no time — steer!*"

* * *

He came over the gunwale half-drowned and bleeding and went straight for the tiller, because there was no version of this where he rested first. Priya appeared beside him, threw a rag at his arm — "Pressure, later stitches, move" — and dove below to give the dying engine everything it had left.

The barge answered. Late, heavy, dragging its wounded rudder through water full of tree debris, but it *answered*. Lin stood at the helm beside Sebastian, and the two of them flew the boat together — her reading the water in the language she'd grown up speaking, him putting the pole in exactly where she pointed, the barge threading the slot with a hand's width on either side.

The hole waited beyond.

"Hit it straight," Lin shouted. "Speed is life. Do not let it turn us."

They hit it straight.

The bow buried. Green water climbed the deck to Okonkwo's waist. The whole barge stood on its nose, hung there — a full second, an hour, he couldn't say — and then the current released them and they punched out the back side into the long calm below the gorge, wallowing, shipping water, alive.

The silence afterward was enormous. Not quiet — the river still roared behind them — but the silence of a body after a scream, everything spent.

Tomas sat down on the rope coil and put his face in his hands. Okonkwo watched him shake now, finally, all the shaking he'd saved up, and knelt beside him and gripped the back of his neck the way you grip a brother.

"You pulled at the right moment," Okonkwo said. "Every time. That's the whole job. That's the whole thing anyone can do."

Tomas looked up, eyes red. "I sold these people."

"You bought your way back today," Okonkwo said. "Full price. Paid in advance." He squeezed once and let go. "Now bail. We're low in the water and the day isn't done."

* * *

They stopped an hour downstream on a gravel shelf below a waterfall that fell out of jungle so steep no one had ever cut a trail to it. Anselm stitched Okonkwo's forearm with fishing line boiled in river water, complaining the entire time in the voice of a man who had stitched worse in worse places and knew it, and Okonkwo let him, watching the crew instead.

Priya had the hatch open, elbow-deep in the engine, cursing it tenderly. Lin stood apart, facing upstream, one hand resting on the tarps over her father's letters — guarding them, or drawing strength from them, or both. Sebastian crouched at the waterline washing the mud from his forearms, and Okonkwo saw something in the set of his shoulders he hadn't seen in ten days of travel.

He wasn't counting miles. For the first time since Okonkwo had known him, Sebastian Amberdale was looking downriver — at where they were going, not at what they'd left — and his face had no avoidance in it at all.

"We're thirty miles out," Sebastian said, when the stitching was done. "Permits die tomorrow at sundown. The barge will make it if the engine does, and the engine won't." He looked at Priya, who shrugged: confirmation. "So we pole the last miles through drowned forest, and we pray the flood doesn't close the channel early."

"It will close early," Lin said quietly. "It always does."

"Then we're faster than it is."

Okonkwo flexed his stitched arm, testing, and looked downriver at the brown immensity of the flooded Nam Kha carrying them toward the concession post, toward the daughter waiting there, toward whatever truth cost and whoever paid it.

That was when Priya, still at the radio, went still.

"Vessel ahead," she said. "Fast launch, running lights on in daylight. Flying private colors." She turned, and her face said everything before her voice did. "It's flying Voss's flag. And it's coming to meet us."
