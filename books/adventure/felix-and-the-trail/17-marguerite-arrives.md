## 17. Marguerite Arrives

The launch came around the bend of the drowned forest like something released from a cage, white hull, twin engines, a wake that flattened the reeds on both banks. It ran straight at them for half a mile before it eased off and fell in alongside the barge, close enough that Sebastian could read the name on its stern: *Voss et Fils*, though there had been no fils for twenty years.

Marguerite Voss stood at the rail in a dry field jacket, hair tied back, looking like a woman who had never once been rained on. Behind her, two crewmen held lines coiled and ready — not thrown, just held. A courtesy. A threat. With Marguerite it was always both.

"Ahoy the barge," she called over the water, and her voice carried the way it always had, pitched to carry across a glacier moraine or a boardroom without changing register. "You look terrible, Amberdale."

"You should see the other boat," he called back.

Priya cut the engine to an idle. The barge wallowed in the combined chop, and Lin moved to the sweep oar without being asked, holding their heading against the current with small, economical pulls. Okonkwo drifted toward the bow with his stitched arm tucked against his chest, saying nothing, being large. Anselm appeared from under the tarp with a kettle in one hand as if this were a social call, which, Sebastian thought, in a way it was. Everything with Marguerite was social. That was what made her dangerous.

"Mind if I come aboard?" she asked, already swinging a line across.

"You were going to anyway."

"I was going to anyway," she agreed, and came over the gunwale with the ease of someone boarding a vessel she'd owned in a previous life. Her boots found the wet deck and she looked down at the water sloshing over the planks, at the sodden crates, at the four exhausted people and one ferrywoman keeping them pointed downstream. "My God. You actually did it. You actually put Thorne's crate on a floating coffin and pointed it at the concession post."

"It's called improvising."

"It's called a death wish with cargo." She crouched, touched the deck, rubbed the water between her fingers. "This barge has been aground since before the last monsoon. I know because my surveyors photographed it. Its freeboard is—" she glanced at the waterline, "—generous. In the way that a drowning man's optimism is generous."

"We're managing."

"You're sinking." She stood, brushed off her hand, and finally looked at him directly, and there it was, the thing he'd spent ten years not missing: the particular attention of Marguerite Voss, which made you feel like a specimen she'd been keeping in formaldehyde until the moment was right. "Sebastian. Let's talk."

* * *

They talked at the stern, away from the others, though everyone pretended not to listen, which meant everyone listened. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. Downriver, through the haze, the channel narrowed where the flood was already beginning to pinch it shut against the far bank.

She didn't open with the offer. She opened with the mountain.

"Do you remember," she said, "the last time we stood on a riverbank together? Before all this?"

"No."

"Liar." She smiled without warmth. "It was the Dudh Koshi. You were twenty-six and insufferable and you told me you'd climb anything I could afford to insure. And I said, *Sebastian, one day your luck will run out*, and you said—"

"*Luck isn't a thing you spend,*" he said. "*It's a thing you waste.*"

"There he is." Something moved behind her eyes, too quick to name. "Ten years, Sebastian. Ten years running tourists down class-three water in countries with no extradition for negligence. Do you know what I've done in ten years? I built the largest high-altitude logistics operation in Asia. I have helicopters. I have permits in eleven jurisdictions. I have a waiting list for Everest that would embarrass a cardiologist." She paused. "And every year, when the season ended, I looked up guides who weren't afraid of anything, and none of them were you."

"That's very flattering."

"It's not flattery. It's inventory." She turned to face him fully. "Here's what I know. Here's what I've known since the day I read the incident report and didn't believe a word of it. Elias Thorne died on the North Col approach because someone cut the fixed lines at camp two. Not weather. Not error. Someone took a knife to forty meters of rope in the dark, and then everyone stood around afterward agreeing not to say so, because the only man who might have named the saboteur was the man who got blamed for everything else."

The river filled the silence between them.

"And you think," Sebastian said carefully, "that I know who did it."

"I think you've spent ten years refusing to say so, which is either innocence or loyalty, and I've watched you long enough to know you're not innocent. Nobody who avoids maps the way you do is innocent." She let that sit. "I also think the ledger in that crate says so. In Elias's handwriting. Page by page, day by day, right up until the night he died. He knew, Sebastian. He wrote it down, and he sent it down the mountain, and it sank in a river, and now you're carrying it to his daughter like some kind of penitent postman."

"He asked me to. Before—"

"I know what he asked you to." Her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in poured concrete, and then it was smooth again. "I was engaged to him. Did you know that? Everyone forgets. Everyone remembers the expedition, the disaster, the blacklist, the inquiry. Nobody remembers that I was supposed to meet him in Kathmandu when he came down. I bought a dress."

Sebastian said nothing. There was nothing to say to a dress.

"So here it is." She reached into her jacket and produced a folded document, dry, laminated, absurdly official-looking amid all this water. "Your licenses. Nepal, Peru, both reinstated, effective on signature. Your name cleared in the incident record — amended findings, formal apology from the association, the works. Your blacklist gone. Your name yours again." She held it out. "In exchange for the pages naming the saboteur. Just those. Tear them out, burn the rest if you want, I don't care about the mineral rights, I don't care about the route notes. Give me the name, and walk back into your life."

* * *

He looked at the document for a long time.

Not because he was tempted — or not only because he was tempted, and the not-only was the shameful part, the part he'd examine later, alone, on some other river. Because the document was real. He could see the seals. He could see his own name printed in the field marked *GUIDE OF RECORD*, spelled correctly, spelled the old way, the way it hadn't been spoken aloud in a decade except by lawyers and debt collectors and one ferrywoman who'd learned it from a dead man's photograph.

Ten years of miles instead of years. Ten years of rapids counted instead of birthdays. Ten years of being no one, professionally, meticulously, and here was a woman on a leaking barge offering to hand him back to himself for the price of one name.

"The saboteur," he said slowly. "You want the name."

"I want justice."

"You want the name."

"I want," she said, and stopped, and started again, quieter, "I have spent ten years believing that Elias died because the mountain is the mountain. And then three weeks ago I learned there's a ledger, and I learned it says otherwise, and I have not slept properly since. So yes. I want the name. Say it and I'll make sure the rest of your life is everything the last ten years wasn't."

Behind him, he heard Lin shift at the sweep oar. He didn't turn. He didn't need to. He could feel the whole barge listening — Priya with her altered engine logs, Okonkwo with his stitched arm, Anselm with his guilt folded up small in his apron pocket, Tomas somewhere aft not pretending anymore.

"Marguerite," he said. "Do you know why I never explained? At the inquiry. Why I sat there and took it?"

"Because you were protecting someone."

"Because I didn't know." He watched her face change. "That's the truth nobody wanted. Not the association, not the press, not me. I stood on that ice and I saw the cut lines and I knew it was a blade and I could not tell you whose hand. I had suspicions. Suspicions aren't testimony. So I said nothing, and silence looked like guilt, and guilt was easier for everyone than ignorance, so they gave my silence a verdict and I took it, because taking it was the only honest thing left."

"And now?"

"Now I've read the ledger." He looked downriver, at the closing channel, at the brown water eating the banks. "Now I know."

Her breath went out slowly. "Then say it. Right here. No paper, no record. Just say it to me, woman to guide, and take the damn licenses."

"The ledger goes to Thorne's daughter."

"She's a child. She was six when he died. What does she care about—"

"She cares," Sebastian said, "because it's hers. All of it. The name, the pages, the truth, the blame, whatever's in there — it belongs to the family of the man who wrote it, and I am not the man who decides what gets torn out before delivery. I'm the postman. I told you that on your dock, and you laughed."

"I'm not laughing now."

"No," he agreed. "You're not."

He handed the document back. She didn't take it. It hung in the air between them, laminated and useless, until he set it on the gunwale and let the river decide, and the river took it, and that was that.

For a moment he thought she might order her crewmen aboard. He saw her weigh it — the launch, the lines, the arithmetic of four exhausted people against two fresh ones — and then he saw her look past him, at Lin, at the sweep oar, at the village girl Elias Thorne had taught to read maps, and something in the calculation changed.

"You always were," she said at last, "the most infuriating man on any river."

"It's a gift."

"My father," she said, and her voice did the concrete-cracking thing again, worse this time, "climbed with Elias. Partnered with him. Funded half his expeditions. If your ledger names him—"

"It doesn't name him."

She went very still. "What?"

"It doesn't name him, Marguerite. It describes him. There's a difference, and the difference is exactly the size of the distance between suspicion and testimony, and I've lived inside that distance for ten years, and I will not cross it on your behalf or anyone's until it's crossed properly — witnessed, recorded, on the record at the concession post, where it counts." He stepped closer. "You want the name? Come to the post. Sit in the room. Hear it said out loud with officials present, same as everyone else. That's the only version of this that survives."

"And if the name is my father's?"

"Then you'll hear it the way I heard it," he said. "In ink. In his own hand. And you'll still be standing afterward, because you're Marguerite Voss, and standing is what you do."

The rain thickened again. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the gorge like furniture being moved. Priya called out that the channel ahead would hold maybe an hour, maybe less, and Marguerite looked at her watch, and then at the barge, and then at Sebastian, with an expression he'd last seen on a glacier fifteen years ago — the expression of a woman deciding whether to kill him or marry him, and knowing, even then, that neither option was quite available.

"Full speed to the concession post," she said to her crew. "We'll be there first. We'll be there when you arrive." She swung back over the gunwale onto her own deck, then turned, one hand on the rail. "Amberdale. When they read that ledger out loud — if it's him — I want to be standing next to you. Not across from you. Next to you. Understand?"

"Understood."

"Don't sink. I've invested too much in hating you."

The launch pulled away in a rooster tail of spray, running lights blazing uselessly against the grey afternoon, and grew small, and rounded the bend, and was gone.

Lin brought the barge's head back around into the current. "Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"Was she telling the truth?"

Sebastian watched the white water where the launch had been. "About wanting the name? Yes. About everything else—" he shook his head slowly, "—ask me after the concession post."

Ahead, the channel was narrowing exactly as Lin had promised, the flooded forest pressing in from both sides, branches dragging at the water like fingers. And coming downriver against the current, riding low and fast, was a second boat — a government patrol skiff, blue and white, with two officials in the bow and a loudhailer already raised.

Priya read the stencil on its hull through her binoculars and swore softly in three languages.

"What is it?" Tomas asked.

"Concession authority," she said. "They're coming to meet us."
