## 18. The Saboteur's Daughter

Marguerite Voss had spent thirty years learning to read rivers from the deck of a boat, and she had never once learned to read her own hands.

They were shaking now. She watched them do it — watched them grip the rail of her launch as it idled in the drowned forest off the Nam Kha's main channel, watched the knuckles whiten and the tendons stand up like guy-wires under strain — and she thought, with a kind of clinical detachment that frightened her more than the shaking did: *this is what my father looked like in his last year.*

Henrik Voss had been a big man, a careful man, a man who checked knots twice and never trusted a rope he hadn't tied himself. In the photographs from his expedition years he stood at the center of every group like a keel, broad-shouldered, sunburned, certain. Marguerite had grown up on those photographs. She had grown up on the stories too — the first descent of the Tsangpo gorge, the winter traverse of the Karakoram, the clients who came back alive because Henrik Voss did not make mistakes.

He made one mistake. It was on a mountain she had never been allowed to name in his presence, above a river she had never been allowed to mention, and it had killed a client named Elias Thorne and ended the career of a young guide named Sebastian Amberdale, and for ten years Marguerite had built an empire of salvage contracts and river concessions on top of the grave of that mistake, the way you build a road over a sinkhole, fast and heavy, so nothing underneath can rise.

The launch's radio crackled. "Voss, this is Amberdale." His voice came through thin and wet with static, the barge engine growling behind it. "We see you. We're coming alongside on your port side. Nobody touches the crate."

"Understood," she said, and her voice held. Her voice always held. That was the other thing she'd inherited from him.

* * *

She had prepared for this meeting the way she prepared for everything: exhaustively, coldly, with contingencies nested inside contingencies like river channels inside a delta. She knew Sebastian Amberdale would refuse money — he'd refused it once already, ten years ago, when the insurers' settlement offer sat on the table and he walked out of the hearing room without touching it. She knew he couldn't be threatened; a blacklisted man has nothing left to take. So she had built the offer out of the only material that mattered to him.

Reinstatement. His licenses restored. Nepal reopened to him. Peru. The mountains themselves, handed back across the table like a returned passport.

And all she wanted in exchange was a handful of pages. Ledger pages, in Elias Thorne's handwriting, naming the saboteur.

She had told herself a hundred stories about those pages on the flight up from Vientiane. That Thorne had been paranoid, mistaken. That the sabotage theory was a dying man's fantasy — because by the end, near the ledger's final entries, the handwriting had deteriorated into something barely legible, and Marguerite had read enough of her father's old field notes to know what altitude sickness and guilt did to a man's pen. That even if someone had cut the fixed lines on the West Couloir, it could have been anyone. A porter with a grudge. A rival outfit. Weather and bad luck wearing the mask of intention.

She had told herself these stories the way you tell yourself a river is safe because you've run it before. Right up until the water changed.

Because three days ago, in the flooded hold of the Meridian, before the crate ever reached Sebastian's hands, Marguerite had done something she had not planned to do. She had opened it.

The salvage contract gave her that right — clause eleven, sole discretion of the contracting party — and she had exercised it alone, at night, in the cabin of her launch, with a pry bar and a headlamp and her heart going like a trapped bird. She told herself she was verifying the cargo. She told herself a great many things that night.

Elias Thorne's ledger lay inside a waterproof sleeve, and Elias Thorne had written like a man who expected to be read after death. Careful dates. Careful altitudes. And on the third-to-last entry, dated four days before the climb, a passage Marguerite had now read so many times she could recite it against the dark of her eyelids:

*Someone has been at the anchors. Rethreaded the upper station on the couloir line — knot is Henrik's double-fisherman, I'd know it anywhere, but the tail is wrong, too short, dressed lazy. H. says the fixed lines are fine, says I'm seeing ghosts at this altitude. Maybe I am. But I'm writing it down so somebody knows I saw it.*

Her father's knot. Dressed lazy. Henrik Voss had not dressed a lazy knot since 1974.

Marguerite had sat in the cabin until dawn with the ledger open on her knees, and somewhere in the grey hours the stories ran out, and what was underneath them rose up through the sinkhole after ten years, and it was not a mystery anymore. It was just her father, at fifty-eight, tired and frightened of being replaced, cutting the lines on a route he'd decided was too dangerous to be seen failing — or perhaps not cutting them at all, perhaps only letting them rot, which was worse, because it meant he hadn't even had the courage to be a saboteur, only a negligent old king who let the mountain do his firing for him.

It didn't matter which. Both men were her father. Both men were dead. And Elias Thorne had known, and written it down, so that somebody would know he saw it.

*Somebody knows,* Marguerite thought now, watching the barge come through the drowned trees, poled by five figures moving with the slow coordination of people who have stopped being strangers. *I'm the somebody. God help me, I'm the somebody.*

* * *

The barge drew alongside with a groan of wet timber. Sebastian Amberdale stood at the bow, and ten years had carved him down to essentials — leaner, greyer at the temples, eyes the color of water over slate — but she recognized the set of his shoulders from the hearing room, from the mountain rescue photographs, from every image of the man who had carried her family's sin on his back for a decade without knowing whose it was.

Behind him: the engineer Priya, soaked to the waist, arms crossed. The diver Okonkwo, a rope coil over one shoulder like a bandolier. The cook Anselm, who was not a cook — she'd run his name weeks ago, Dr. Anselm Reyes, expedition physician, Thorne's expedition, and she had hired him onto her own crew list without letting herself think about why. And Tomas, the deckhand, standing slightly apart, wrists loose, a man who had sold them and been forgiven for it and still didn't know what to do with the forgiveness.

And Lin. The ferrywoman. Standing beside Sebastian in a way that was not touching but was closer than touching, the way two pilings stand that have taken the same current for years.

"You look terrible," Marguerite said, because ritual mattered, because the script was armor, and she needed her armor for another sixty seconds. "All of you. The barge looks worse."

"The barge got us here," Sebastian said. "That's more than most things manage."

"I have an offer."

"I know. You've had it since the radio call. Let's hear it anyway. Out loud. With witnesses." He glanced back at his crew, and Marguerite understood: he wasn't performing for them. He was making sure they heard whatever she said next, so that no version of this conversation could later exist that they hadn't all witnessed. He had learned that trick the hard way. She had taught it to him, in a sense, or her father had.

"Full reinstatement," she said. "Every license. Nepal, Peru, Argentina — I'll make the calls myself, and my calls carry weight in three ministries. Your blacklist ends today. Not appealed — ended, expunged, as if it never happened." She paused. "In exchange, the pages naming the saboteur come to me. Only those pages. The rest of the ledger goes where you're taking it. To the daughter."

The river slapped against the hulls. Somewhere in the drowned forest a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"And if I say no?"

"Then the blacklist stands, and you spend the rest of your life running tourists down warm rivers, counting rapids instead of birthdays." She had meant it as a blade. Watching his face, she realized it landed as something else — as a description he had already lived past, the way you describe a town you've emigrated from.

"That's the offer," he said. "Here's mine."

"I didn't come here to trade counteroffers."

"No," he agreed. "You came here because you've already read the pages. Didn't you? Clause eleven. You opened the crate."

The shaking started again in her hands, and this time she couldn't stop it, and she saw him see it, and there was no cruelty in his face at all, which was somehow unbearable.

* * *

"My father tied that knot," she heard herself say.

It came out before she had decided to say it, the way floodwater comes through a seam — not through the door you braced, but through the crack you never thought to check. She gripped the rail. She kept talking, because once the seam went, the whole thing went, and thirty years of holding had apparently been load-bearing.

"Elias Thorne wrote that the rethreaded anchor was tied in my father's double-fisherman. Dressed lazy. My father never dressed a lazy knot in his life — except at the end, except that season, when his hands were starting to go and he knew it and wouldn't say it, wouldn't retire, wouldn't—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She shut it down, breathed, started again lower. "He died four years ago. Heart, in his sleep, in a house full of framed photographs of mountains he'd climbed and one mountain he never talked about. I found the insurance file after the funeral. The Thorne claim. There was a memo in it, internal, unsigned, recommending the sabotage question be closed without investigation. Closed. By the company he co-founded. The company I now run."

Sebastian said nothing. Behind him, nobody moved. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"I bought this salvage contract to burn those pages," Marguerite said. "You understand that. I want you to understand it fully, because I'm about to ask you for something worse than handing them over, and you should know exactly what kind of woman is asking. I was going to destroy the evidence. Not to hide it from the world — the world forgot Elias Thorne years ago, the world doesn't care. To hide it from *me*. Because as long as those pages existed unread, my father was still possibly innocent. As long as no one read them, he was both things at once. The hero in the photographs and the ghost in the ledger. And I could go on building roads over the sinkhole." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You open the box, though. You open the box and he's just one man. One tired, frightened, guilty old man who killed someone and let a twenty-nine-year-old guide carry it."

"Tomas carried something like that," Sebastian said quietly. "Two weeks ago. We didn't feed him to the river."

"Don't," she said. "Don't be generous at me. I can't afford it yet."

"Then here's what I can afford." He stepped to the gunwale, close enough that she could see the salt dried white on his jacket, the exhaustion underneath his eyes like silt under clear water. "You want the pages destroyed so no one ever names your father. I'm offering you the opposite. I'm offering you the truth — witnessed. All of it. On the record, at the concession post, today, while the permits are still live. What Thorne wrote. What Anselm saw. What I did and didn't do on that mountain. Everything, spoken aloud, entered into the record, with your name attached as the person who brought the ledger in."

"Why would I—"

"Because destruction protects him the way a landslide protects a village. It buries the evidence and everyone in it. Your father stays a photograph forever, and Thorne's daughter spends her life knowing her father died under a cloud, and I stay a blacklisted man who 'refuses to explain,' and the record stays a lie. That's what burning buys. You know it. You've watched it work for ten years — it's worked on *me*." His voice didn't rise. It did the opposite; it dropped, became almost gentle, and that gentleness was the hardest thing she'd faced all day. "Or you let it be true out loud. Henrik Voss, 1951 to four years ago. Great mountaineer. Made one catastrophic choice at the end, and it killed a good man, and it wrecked an innocent one, and his daughter found out and chose — at real cost, at the cost of her own company's interests — to tell it straight. That's also a true story, Marguerite. It's just a harder one. Harder than ash. Ash is easy."

"You're asking me to hand my father's body to the river."

"I'm asking you to stop standing on it."

* * *

She looked past him then, at the crew on the barge, because she couldn't look at him a moment longer. Priya's face was unreadable and steady. Okonkwo watched her the way he probably watched water — for what it was doing, not what it claimed. Anselm Reyes met her eyes and nodded once, slowly, a doctor confirming a diagnosis she'd already made herself.

And Lin. The ferrywoman looked at her with an expression Marguerite recognized from the inside: the face of someone carrying cargo for the dead, who knows exactly how heavy it is, and who delivers it anyway.

"My father taught me to read rivers," Marguerite said. "First lesson: the river doesn't care what you wish it were doing. Fight that, and it kills you. Accept what it's actually doing, and it might let you through." She wiped her face with the back of her hand — roughly, quickly, the way you'd strike a match. "When we reach the post, I'll sign as contracting party. Full disclosure. The pages stay in the ledger. Nothing comes out. Nothing gets burned."

Sebastian exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a pack at the end of a portage.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Thorne's daughter is waiting at the post. She should hear it from you first. Before the officials. Before the record. Woman to woman, father's child to father's child."

Marguerite looked downstream, toward the concession post she could not yet see, where the channel narrowed and the floodwater stood three fingers deep on the banks and rising, where a stranger with her father's crime in her lap waited for a delivery that had taken ten years to arrive.

"All right," Marguerite said. "Start the engine. We're losing the channel."

Behind her, her helmsman throttled up, and the launch swung downstream into the rising brown water, running ahead of the flood, carrying — for the first time in her life — nothing hidden.
