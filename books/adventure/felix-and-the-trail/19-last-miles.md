## 19. Last Miles

The barge was dying. Priya had known it since the second hour, when the pump she'd rigged from a bilge hose and prayer started cycling faster than any pump should. Every fifteen minutes, then every twelve, then every nine. Water doesn't negotiate. It just waits for you to get tired.

She knelt in the hold with her ear against the planking, listening to the river come through.

"Talk to me," Okonkwo said from the hatch above, his silhouette black against a sky the color of wet slate.

"Seam's opening along the old repair. Whoever glassed this hull last did it with resin that had already gone off — it cured like biscuit." She pressed her thumb into the seam and felt it weep under the pressure of the current. "I can slow it. I can't stop it."

"Slow is enough?"

Priya sat back on her heels and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn, the same numbers over and over hoping they'd improve with repetition. Forty miles to the concession post. The flood front behind them, gaining. The channel ahead narrowing into drowned forest where the water had no banks anymore, just treetops and current and whatever lay hidden underneath. Three days on the permits. Maybe two, if the officials downstream were the kind who read the word *monsoon* and closed early.

"Slow has to be enough," she said.

* * *

They'd left the Meridian's wreck at first light, or what passed for first light under a sky that had forgotten how to be anything but gray. Sebastian had stood on the mudbank for exactly one minute — Priya had counted, because counting was what she did instead of feeling — and then he'd turned and started loading the barge without a word. That was the thing about him she'd finally understood somewhere around mile three hundred: grief didn't stop him. It just changed his gait.

Lin's father's ferry barge was forty years old, flat-bottomed, built for crossing a village channel, not for forty miles of flooded river. Its engine was a marine diesel older than Priya, and it ran the way old men run — stubbornly, loudly, with visible complaints. But it floated. For now, floating was the whole job description.

The cargo rode amidships: Thorne's crate, wrapped in tarps and lashed to a frame Lin's father had built for hauling livestock. Beside it, wrapped just as carefully, Lin's own cargo — the letters, oilcloth-bound, that she'd carried for twenty years without delivering. Two cargoes, one purpose. Anselm had said that at the loading, quietly, the way he said everything, and Priya had watched Lin look at him like he'd handed her something fragile.

Now Priya watched the water instead. It had risen another foot since morning. She knew because she'd marked the bollard with grease pencil, and the marks were disappearing one by one like a countdown nobody had asked for.

* * *

The drowned forest began at noon.

From the outside it would have looked almost beautiful — the river spread wide and shallow across what had been teak plantation, the trees standing in rows to their waists, their leaves still green, still trying to photosynthesize in a world that had become ocean overnight. From inside it, it was a maze. Current came at them from directions current shouldn't come from, funneled between trunks, eddying behind root masses, and every few hundred meters the water dropped over some submerged log or bank edge with a sound like a door closing.

"Engine off," Lin called from the bow. "We pole."

Priya killed the diesel gratefully — it was drinking fuel they didn't have and shaking the seam apart faster than the river alone would. Silence fell, except for water and wood and breathing.

There were six poles aboard. Sebastian took the bow. Tomas took the stern beside her at the tiller. Okonkwo and Anselm worked the midships, and Lin moved between them all like someone conducting an orchestra only she could hear, calling depths and channels in a voice pitched low enough not to carry across the water.

*Toward the big fig. Left of the dead one. There's a fence line under us — follow it, the posts mean solid ground.*

A fence line under four feet of floodwater. Priya thought about the engineering of that — a farmer's boundary becoming a navigation aid, knowledge passing from father to daughter, from village to stranger. Her hands were blistered in four places and she found she didn't care. This was why she'd said yes to bad money in the first place, though she hadn't known it then. Not the salvage. This. Work that meant something, done with people who'd stopped pretending otherwise.

"Tomas." Sebastian's voice, from the bow. Not sharp. Worse than sharp — level. "Your side. The trunk coming up on your right."

Tomas sawed his pole clear of a snag and swung the barge's stern six inches, maybe eight, and the trunk slid past with a hand's width to spare. Nobody cheered. Everybody breathed.

That was the new arithmetic too: Tomas had sold them once, confessed it, been forgiven by a man who had no business having forgiveness left to give, and now he poled like a man paying down a debt at compound interest. Priya caught herself watching him sometimes the way she watched gauges — looking for the needle to jump. The needle wasn't jumping. Whatever else the river had taken, it seemed to be leaving the truth of people behind after it passed.

* * *

The pump cycled at seven minutes when the rain started.

Not monsoon rain — that was still behind them, a gray wall on the southern horizon that Lin checked every hour with the expression of a woman reading an enemy's mail. This was just ordinary rain, steady and cold, and it found every gap in Priya's rain jacket within ten minutes, so she gave up on staying dry and went below to fight the seam properly.

The old repair was failing along eighteen inches of its length. She cleaned it as best she could with a rag and river water, dried it with the heat gun until the wood smoked faintly, and laid fiberglass over it with the last of the resin she'd hoarded since the Meridian. Her hands shook — exhaustion, cold, the particular tremor of someone who has been awake for thirty hours doing math that keeps getting worse. She steadied them against her knee and made herself work slowly, because fast and wrong was worse than slow and right.

Above her, through the deck planks, she could hear the poling rhythm change. Six poles don't stay in sync naturally; someone has to keep time. She realized, lying there with resin on her fingers, that it was Anselm. The cook, the doctor, the quietest man aboard, tapping his pole against the gunwale on the offbeat — *tick, stroke, tick, stroke* — and the others falling into it without discussing it, the way bodies fall into each other's rhythms when they've shared enough danger.

She finished the patch, pumped out eleven minutes' worth of accumulation, and lay back against the crate with her eyes closed for ninety seconds. Ninety seconds was what she allowed herself. Through the tarp she could feel the corner of the crate against her shoulder blade, solid and dry, and she thought about what was inside it — a dead man's handwriting, a lie ten years old, a truth that had cost everyone aboard something different.

Her job was to keep it dry. Everything else was somebody else's chapter.

"Priya." Lin's voice through the hatch, urgent but controlled. "Water's dropping half a foot ahead. Sandbar ridge. We either go around — two hours lost — or over."

"Over," Priya said, before she'd fully decided. Then, louder: "Over. I'll reinforce the keel strakes."

She heard Sebastian repeat it forward, heard the poles shift, and got up to do the next impossible thing.

* * *

They grounded twice on the sandbar ridge.

The first time was gentle — a shudder, a scrape, momentum carrying them up and over onto the shallows beyond while everyone leaned to one side like ballast with opinions. The second time was not gentle. The barge hit a submerged stump that the dropping water had uncovered between Lin's call and the bow's arrival, and the impact drove the patched seam open again with a sound Priya felt in her teeth.

She was below within seconds, headlamp on, watching water thread through her morning's work like it was unwrapping a gift.

"Two hours," she told the hatch. "Give me two hours and lighter loads on the deck."

They unloaded. That was the part she'd remember later, when she tried to explain to people who hadn't been there what the last miles had actually been like. Six people standing waist-deep in floodwater in a drowned forest, passing crates and barrels and coils of rope hand to hand onto a sandbar that existed only because the flood hadn't reached it yet, working in silence except for Anselm's count — *tick, stroke, tick* — repurposed now into a lifting chant. The crate went last, carried by all of them together, Sebastian and Okonkwo taking the heavy end without being asked, Lin walking backward up the bar guiding them by voice.

On the sandbar, they waited. Priya stayed below with the seam, drying, cleaning, laying glass, working by feel as much as sight because her headlamp battery was dying and she was rationing the dark. Above her she could hear the others redistributing weight, arguing in low voices about what could be jettisoned and what couldn't. She heard Marguerite's name once, and the mineral rights, and then silence on that subject forever, as if the whole crew had signed something none of them remembered signing.

When she came up, the light had gone bronze and strange — storm light, the light that comes before weather makes its decision. The barge floated free again, riding higher because they'd left half their supplies on the sandbar, stacked under tarp with a note weighted by a stone: coordinates, and *for whoever finds this, they're yours*. Lin had written it. Priya had watched her do it, and watched her hand hesitate over the words, and understood that leaving supplies for strangers was a kind of faith Priya didn't have yet and wanted to.

"How long?" Sebastian asked her.

"The patch holds if the water stays calm. The water will not stay calm." She met his eyes. "Ask me again in an hour."

He nodded like that was the most honest answer anyone had given him in ten years, and maybe it was.

* * *

The last ten miles were the ones Priya would measure everything against for the rest of her life.

The drowned forest ended and the river gathered itself back into a single channel, which should have been mercy and wasn't, because a single channel meant current, and current meant speed, and speed meant the failing seam took every shock directly. The pump cycled at five minutes. Then four. They rotated poling shifts in pairs because no one could sustain it alone — Sebastian and Okonkwo, then Tomas and Anselm, then Lin alone at the bow because she refused to rotate out, because this was her father's channel and her father had never rotated out either.

Priya ran the engine in short bursts when the channel straightened, conserving fuel, conserving the hull, conserving everything. She kept the arithmetic running out loud now, because the crew had earned honesty: *"Pump's at four minutes. Fuel's at three hours. Permits die tomorrow at flood closure, and the flood closes when it closes, not when we arrive."*

Nobody complained. That was the strangest part. Ten days ago these had been strangers hired for bad money, each carrying a private reason to say yes, and now they moved like a single mechanism with six parts, and the reason wasn't courage or loyalty or any word big enough to put on a cover. It was just that they'd run out of everything except each other, and it turned out each other was enough.

Somewhere past mile thirty-five, Tomas started singing.

It was barely singing — a low, cracked thread of some dockside song in three languages, tuneless, half-swallowed. Anselm picked it up. Then Okonkwo, harmonizing badly. Lin laughed for the first time in two days, a short startled sound, and even Sebastian's mouth moved at the corner. Priya, at the tiller with her blistered hands wrapped in tape, let herself sing one line, off-key on purpose, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the Salween.

Exhaustion, she thought, is just exhaustion until it's shared. Then it becomes something else. The engineers probably had a term for it. She preferred the song.

* * *

At dusk they saw the smoke.

Woodsmoke from the concession post, thin and vertical in the dead air before the storm — a plume standing straight up like a signal flag planted by civilization. Below it, when the channel bent, lights. Kerosene and generator, a cluster of buildings on the last high ground, and figures moving on the levee with lanterns, small at this distance, unmistakably human.

Lin raised her father's binoculars. Lowered them slowly.

"They're wading the levee path," she said. "Marking the channel with stakes. When the flood front arrives, they'll pull the marker boats and close the post." A pause. "The front is visible from the south bend. Maybe two hours behind us. Maybe less."

Two hours. Forty miles had become four, and four miles had become a race against a wall of water they could not see but could all feel, the way you feel a train through the rails before you hear it.

Priya brought the engine up to full throttle for the first time all day and held it there, and the old diesel screamed, and the seam let go of her morning's patch in the first minute, and she let it. The pump ran continuously now. It didn't matter. Four miles was close enough to swim, and they all knew it, and knowing it made the boat expendable in a way that freed everyone. The only cargo that mattered was lashed amidships, dry under its tarps, riding the last miles of a river that had tried very hard to keep it.

The lights grew. The levee grew. Figures on the levee stopped moving and turned toward the water, and one of them raised a lantern high and swung it — three arcs, pause, three arcs. Lin answered with the flashlight, three arcs, pause, three arcs, and Priya understood that somewhere upstream of all of them, a language older than permits was being spoken fluently.

Then the barge's engine died.

Not sputtered — died, mid-scream, fuel starved, and the current took them sideways toward the levee's outer bank where the water was tearing past toward the flood plain. Poles came off the roof rack before Priya had finished swearing. Six poles hit water at once. And in the last four hundred meters, with the permit clock bleeding out and the flood wall audible now — a low continuous roar from the south, like the river clearing its throat — the crew of the late *Meridian* and the late ferry barge of Lin's father rowed and poled and hauled themselves the final stretch through water that fought them the whole way.

The barge grounded on the levee ramp with the bow out of the water and the stern settling, and hands were there — official hands, uniformed hands, pulling them up the slope — and Priya stood on solid ground for the first time in thirty-six hours and discovered her legs had opinions about it.

Sebastian was already at the crate, cutting lashings with a borrowed knife, checking the tarps with his palms the way you check a child for fever. Dry. All corners dry.

Behind them, the barge sighed, rolled, and settled into the levee mud with its seams finally open to the sky, done, retired, its last cargo delivered. Lin put her hand on its rail and left it there a moment. Priya looked away to give her that.

An official with a clipboard was shouting about time stamps. Another was shouting about the channel closing. And down the levee path, hurrying through the lantern light with a rain coat clutched at her throat, came a woman Priya had never seen but recognized instantly anyway — the set of the shoulders, the way she walked like the ground owed her nothing.

Thorne's daughter. Waiting at the end of the river, the way she'd apparently been waiting for ten years.

Sebastian lifted the crate from the barge and settled it into his arms, and Priya saw him take one breath — one long, deliberate breath, the kind you take at the base of a mountain — before he started up the levee toward her.

The flood wall's roar crossed the south bend behind them, and the lanterns on the levee began, one by one, to swing their warning.
