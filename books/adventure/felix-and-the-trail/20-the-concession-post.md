## 20. The Concession Post

The barge died a mile from the post.

Not dramatically — nothing on this river had been dramatic in days, only relentless. The old hull simply gave up its argument with the water, settling into the flooded channel like a tired animal lying down, and Priya called it first, her hand flat against the deck planking.

"She's done. Ten minutes of float left, maybe."

"Then we walk it in," Sebastian said.

They walked it. Forty miles had become four, and four became one, and the last mile they stood chest-deep in brown water pushing the crate ahead of them on a raft of ferry planks lashed with rope that had been Lin's father's, everyone's shoulders under the lashings except Okonkwo's, because Okonkwo swam point with the crate tethered to his waist, reading the current by feel and calling the turns in a voice gone hoarse from shouting through the drowned forest.

The concession post rose out of the flood like something remembered rather than seen — a stilted compound of government timber and corrugated tin, the flag on its pole hanging wet and motionless, the landing dock submerged to within a foot of its surface. Officials were already wading out to meet them. Sebastian counted five, then six, trousers rolled to the thigh, a clipboard held above the waterline like an offering, and behind them on the dock, under a tarp rigged against the rain, a woman stood with her arms crossed and did not move.

He knew her before he could see her face. He knew her by the way she stood — weight forward, chin level, the posture of someone who had spent ten years waiting for a door to open and refusing to look like she was waiting.

Elias Thorne's daughter.

"Channel closes in twenty minutes," one of the officials shouted over the rain. He was young, with a laminated badge in a plastic sleeve and the particular desperation of a man whose paperwork was about to be invalidated by weather. "Permits void at first flood stage. You are past flood stage. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand," Sebastian said. "We're here."

"You're *late*."

"We're here," Sebastian said again, and put his shoulder back under the crate.

They got it up the submerged ladder steps, Okonkwo lifting from below and Tomas hauling from above, and the crate came over the dock rail streaming water, the seal Marguerite Voss had paid to have broken and resealed still legible through the mud: THORNE EXPEDITION — PROPERTY OF E. THORNE — HOLD AT CONCESSION. The officials crowded around it with their clipboards and their rain-slicked forms, and the young one kept saying something about void permits and liability, and Sebastian let him talk, because the words didn't matter anymore. They were the sound of a world he had been living at the edge of for ten years, and he was finished living there.

The woman under the tarp came forward.

Up close she was maybe thirty-five, with Thorne's jaw and Thorne's gray eyes and none of his warmth, or rather all of his warmth banked down to coals, held there so long it had become its own kind of cold. She looked at the crate. She looked at Sebastian. Her eyes went to his hands, to the rope burns crossing them, and stayed there a moment as if reading them.

"You're Amberdale," she said.

"Yes."

"The guide who killed my father."

The rain filled the silence. Behind him, Sebastian heard Lin shift her weight, heard Priya draw breath to speak, and raised one hand slightly without turning. *Mine,* the gesture said. This one was his to carry.

"No," he said. "I'm the guide who was there when my client made a choice above his ability, and I froze, and another man's decision got written into my name because I let it. Your father chose the line. I failed to stop him. Those are different things, and for ten years I let people call them the same thing because the same thing was easier to survive."

She stared at him. Water ran off the tarp edge between them in a thin silver thread.

"That's not what the reports said."

"No," Sebastian said. "It isn't."

* * *

The young official wanted them inside, out of the rain, signatures collected before the channel closed and the whole transaction became legally impossible. There was a room at the back of the compound with a desk, a diesel lamp, a recording device — an old cassette machine, absurdly, with a microphone on a coiled cord — because concession posts on rivers like this still took spoken declarations for the archive, a custom older than the permits themselves. Anything said into the machine became part of the river's record. Anything in the record could be entered into evidence anywhere in the country.

"Standard procedure for salvage delivery," the official said, setting the microphone on the desk between them. "State your name, your permit number, and a summary of the recovery. It doesn't need to be long."

Sebastian looked at the microphone.

Ten years ago he had sat in a room like this one — smaller, colder, in Kathmandu — while men with laminated badges asked him what happened on the mountain, and he had said almost nothing. Not because he was protecting himself. Because he was protecting Elias Thorne, dead and unable to defend his own judgment, and because saying *he chose the line* felt like handing a grieving family a knife and calling it closure. So he had taken the blame whole. He had signed the reports. He had watched his licenses die and thought: this is the price, and it is fair, and I will pay it quietly.

And the price had grown interest. A decade of miles instead of years. A crew of strangers. A rival's contract. A river he had sworn off carrying him back to the exact spot where everything ended, where a ledger waited in a sealed crate to tell him that even his guilt had been misallocated — that Thorne's own notes described a fixed anchor replaced with a bad one, a rope cut at the sheath, sabotage dressed up as error, and Sebastian's name attached to it because someone needed a living man to hold the sin.

Anselm knew who. Anselm had confirmed the sabotage and stopped short of the name, his doctor's discretion curdling into cowardice, and Sebastian had not pressed him. Not yet. That account was still open.

But the rest of it — the freezing, the failure, the ten years of running — that was his, and he was done paying rent on it.

"Before I sign," Sebastian said, "I want to make the declaration full."

The official frowned. "Full?"

"On record. Everything. What happened on the mountain ten years ago, and what happened on this river, and what's in this crate, and why three separate parties wanted it destroyed or buried." He pulled the chair out and sat, and the others arranged themselves along the walls — Priya and Okonkwo dripping, Tomas standing apart by the door as if unsure he'd earned a wall, Lin beside the window with her satchel of letters held against her chest, and Marguerite Voss, who had arrived an hour behind them by launch, standing just inside the doorway with rain still beaded on her shoulders, listening.

Marguerite had come to take the pages about mineral rights. Sebastian had told her, on the water, with the gorge roaring past, that she could have the truth or she could have her father's name protected, but not both, and that the choice was no longer hers alone. She had not answered. She had followed them here anyway. He took her presence in the room as an answer of a kind.

The official glanced at the cassette machine, shrugged, and pressed record. The spools turned.

"Declaration of salvage recovery, Nam Kha concession, delivered by the crew of the vessel Meridian, sunk, and the ferry barge of Kham village, abandoned. My name is Sebastian Amberdale. Permit number—" he recited it from memory, the way you remember a cell number you've dialed a thousand times, "—and I'm going to start ten years ago, because the crate on this desk doesn't make sense without it."

He told it plainly. No performance, no self-flagellation, no polish. The climb. Thorne's decision above the last anchor, made against advice, made in weather that had no business being climbed in. Sebastian's freeze — three seconds, maybe four, an eternity measured in falling bodies. The descent. The investigation. The report he had signed because signing it protected a dead man's reputation and gave the living somewhere to put their grief, and grief needs somewhere to go or it turns inward and eats the vessel holding it.

Then the ledger. How they found it. What was in it — Thorne's handwriting, meticulous, increasingly troubled, documenting the swapped anchor hardware, the sheath damage consistent with a blade rather than abrasion, the entries naming supply purchases Thorne never made. And Anselm, present in the room now, who stepped forward unasked and said, in a voice like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long it had grown into his spine: "I was expedition doctor. I saw the equipment manifest discrepancies. I said nothing. I am saying it now."

He did not name the saboteur. But he looked, once, briefly, at Marguerite Voss — and Marguerite met his eyes without flinching, and Sebastian understood that whatever remained private in this room would not remain private much longer, and that Marguerite had known, walking in, that this was the price of the truth she'd come to claim.

Tomas spoke too. Unprompted, voice cracking, he stated into the record that he had sold the route to parties acting for Marguerite's backers, that those parties' intent was destruction of the ledger, and that he repudiated it. Mercy had been given to him once already, on the river; this was him paying it forward the only way he could.

When it was done, the official stopped the tape, labeled it, and slid the delivery form across the desk. Sebastian signed — *Sebastian Amberdale*, the full name, the first time in ten years he had written it on anything that mattered — and pushed the ledger across to Thorne's daughter.

She opened the crate right there, ignoring the officials' protests, and lifted the ledger out with both hands, and for a long moment she didn't read it. She just held it, thumbs resting on the cover, the way you hold something that is simultaneously a wound and a gift.

"He taught me to read maps," she said finally, not looking up. "Contour lines. He said a map was just a promise the land makes about itself." She closed her eyes. "You froze."

"I froze."

"And then you spent ten years letting the world call you a killer so his name would stay clean, and it wasn't even true, and he *knew*—"

Her voice broke. Sebastian let it break. Some things you don't help a person through; you just stand in the weather with them.

Lin moved from the window then, and set her satchel on the desk, and drew out the letters — Thorne's letters to the village upstream, written in the weeks before the climb, which Lin had carried undelivered since she was a child because delivering them meant admitting he was never coming back. "These are yours too," Lin said simply. "He wrote about you. Every letter. He said his daughter read maps better than his guides."

Thorne's daughter made a sound that was not quite laughter and not quite weeping, and took the letters, and for a while nobody in the room said anything at all, and the rain came down on the tin roof like applause from a very large and indifferent audience.

* * *

Outside, the channel closed.

They heard it more than saw it — a change in the river's voice, a deepening, the brown water climbing the last foot of the dock stairs and then swallowing them entirely. The young official stood at the railing watching the flood take the landing and said, half to himself, "Twenty minutes. You made it with twenty minutes."

"We've had worse margins," Okonkwo said.

Marguerite found Sebastian at the railing afterward. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist, and the flood spread below them, vast and slow and indifferent, carrying branches and roof tin and one entire floating outhouse toward the delta.

"My father's name is in that ledger," she said. "By tomorrow it'll be in every file that cites this one."

"Yes."

"They'll dig up everything. The company. The concessions. All of it." She turned the ring on her finger — her father's ring, he realized, worn on the wrong hand. "I spent six years trying to buy its destruction. And you're telling me the truth was the cheaper option."

"I'm telling you the truth was the only option that holds weight," Sebastian said. "Everything else collapses eventually. Ask the river."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to surrender he'd ever seen on her, and it looked, up close, less like defeat than like a woman setting down something heavy and discovering her arms still worked.

"The daughter wants to meet tomorrow," Marguerite said. "Both of us. She says if her father's name is going to be dragged through the record, it gets dragged accurately — and she wants witnesses who were actually there." She looked out at the water. "She also asked about the ferrywoman. The one who carried the letters."

"Lin will be there."

"And you? Under what name?"

Sebastian watched the flood move. Somewhere downstream, the Salween waited, and beyond it a registry office with a blank line where a name belonged, and for the first time in ten years the blank line didn't frighten him.

"My real one," he said. "I'm told it fits."

Behind them, through the compound window, lamplight fell across the desk where the ledger lay open, and Thorne's daughter bent over her father's handwriting, reading maps he'd drawn of a mountain she'd never see, and Lin stood beside her, pointing at contour lines, translating a dead man's promises into a living language.

Tomorrow there would be inquiries, and testimony, and the long machinery of reckoning. Tomorrow Anselm would finish his confession and Tomas would face his debtors and the river would keep what the river keeps.

Tonight, Sebastian Amberdale stood at the railing of the concession post, soaked to the bone, boatless, permitless, and lighter than he had been in a decade — and down the flooded lane, a lantern bobbed toward him through the rain, carried by a ferrywoman who had one question left to ask him.
