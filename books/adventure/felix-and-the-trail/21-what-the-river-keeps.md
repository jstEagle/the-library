## 21. What the River Keeps

The water went down the way grief does — not all at once, but in stages, each morning revealing a little more of what had been taken and a little more of what remained.

Lin stood on her father's landing three days after the flood crested, watching the Nam Kha shed its brown fury back into its banks. The barge was tied where they'd left it, listing slightly to port, hull scarred but whole. Her father's ferry route, dead for six years, had made one last run and survived it. She found she didn't know whether to thank the river or curse it.

Behind her, the village was already moving. That was the thing about people who lived with water — they didn't wait for permission to begin again. Roofs were being rethatched. The schoolhouse, which had been standing empty since the government teacher stopped coming two years ago, had its doors propped open to dry out the interior. Children ran between the drying racks with an energy that seemed almost indecent after what they'd all just been through.

And at the far end of the landing, sitting on the same crate he'd carried forty miles through drowned forest, Sebastian Amberdale was mending a net.

He was bad at it. She'd watched him for twenty minutes now, and his hands kept doing the work of a man who understood rope but not knots, pulling tight when he should have eased off, losing loops he'd already made. He hadn't asked for help. He wouldn't. But every few minutes his eyes came up — not to the river, not to the mountains at its head, but to her door, as if checking that something inside it still existed.

Lin untied her apron, folded it over the rail, and walked down to him.

"You're strangling it," she said.

He looked up. In the flat light after flood, without the spray and urgency of the river, his face was older than she remembered from the photograph — the one the elders kept in the temple chest, the one with young Thorne's arm around a laughing guide whose name no one in the village had ever learned until last week. *Felix*, Thorne had written in the ledger's margins. *My lucky one.* Ten years of exile had carved him down to essentials: gray at the temples, hands like weathered rope, eyes that flinched from nothing except kindness.

"It's holding," he said.

"It's dying." She knelt beside him and took the net from his hands. "Watch. You give the line slack first. Everything on this river works by giving before you take."

His fingers hovered, then withdrew. He watched her hands move — three quick motions, the knot closing soft and sure — and Lin thought about how many times she'd watched those same hands do hard things well: hold a tiller in white water, dive into a flooded hold, carry a crate up a hillside while the monsoon tried to peel the skin off the world. And how badly they did easy things.

"Where did you learn that?" he asked.

"My father. Before—" She stopped. Finished the knot instead. "Before the river took the ferry business and gave us nothing back."

"He piloted this crossing for thirty years," Sebastian said quietly. "Thorne wrote about him. In the ledger. Page sixty-one — he called him 'the only honest mapmaker on the Nam Kha.'"

She hadn't known that. Something moved through her chest, warm and painful, like blood returning to a numb limb. "You read all of it?"

"Twice. On the barge, at night, while everyone slept." He looked out at the water. "I wanted to know who I was before I decided whether to say it out loud."

"And?"

"And I'm still deciding."

* * *

Marguerite Voss left on the fourth day.

Lin watched from the temple steps as the woman's launch backed away from the landing, riding high and light — she'd transferred her salvage claim to the village council in a document signed with a hand that shook only slightly, and she'd paid, in cash, for the rebuilding of the schoolhouse before anyone had asked her to. When Priya asked her why, Marguerite had looked at the mountains for a long moment and said, "Because my father stole enough from this valley. It's time someone paid some of it back."

Then she'd climbed aboard without goodbye, and the launch had grown small against the current, and gone.

Tomas did not go with her.

Lin found him that afternoon at the ferry shed, hauling out her father's old planks and sorting them into piles — sound wood, split wood, wood beyond saving. His hands were steady now. Whatever debtors had chased him downriver, whatever price he'd sold their route for, he seemed to have set it down somewhere between the rapids and the concession post, the way a man sets down a load too heavy to carry another step.

"I talked to your uncle," he said, not looking up. "About the crossing."

"The crossing is dead."

"The crossing is *sleeping*." He held up a plank, sighted along it, set it aside. "Your father's charts are still in the shed. The channel markers just need replacing. I've run worse water than this for worse pay." He finally met her eyes, and there was no charm left in his face, only the plainness of a man offering the only thing he had. "Let me rebuild it. Not for money. For—"

"For penance?"

"For *work*," he said. "Penance is cheap. Work costs something every single day."

Lin studied him. Behind him, the shed's doorway framed the river — high and slow now, carrying branches and silt and the occasional drowned coconut toward the sea. Somewhere under that surface lay the wreck of the Meridian, and the old boat's grave, and everything else the flood had claimed.

"All right," she said.

He blinked. "Just like that?"

"No. Not just like that." She picked up a plank and handed it to him. "You'll start with the worst jobs. You'll learn my father's channels until you can run them blindfolded. And when the elders ask me if I trust you, I'll tell them the truth — that you betrayed us, and then you dove into a gorge to save strangers, and I don't know which of those things is bigger yet. Neither will they. We'll find out together, one season at a time."

Tomas took the plank. His jaw worked once, twice. "That's fair," he managed.

"It's more than fair. It's a chance." She turned back to the woodpile. "Fair would have been leaving you at the concession post."

* * *

Evening came gold and slow. The village cooked outdoors, sharing what the flood had spared, and Lin found herself at the fire next to Anselm, who had eaten three bowls of her aunt's fish stew and pronounced it, in his quiet way, better than anything he'd made in ten years of running from his own memories.

"You're staying too?" she asked.

"A while." He stirred his bowl. "There are medical supplies to inventory. Wounds to check. And—" he paused, choosing words the way he chose herbs, carefully, "—there's a man on your landing who hasn't let anyone look at the gash on his forearm since the gorge. I'd like to treat it before it treats him."

"He won't come to you."

"No. But he might come to you, if you asked him to walk with you past my table." Anselm's eyes crinkled. "Doctors learn early which doors open for whom."

* * *

She found Sebastian at dusk, standing at the end of the landing where the water lapped quiet against the pilings. The mountains upstream had gone violet and then black, and the first stars were out over the ridge where, ten years ago, a rope had failed or been cut — where a man named Elias Thorne had fallen into legend and a man named Felix had fallen into hiding.

Lin stopped beside him. For a while neither spoke. The river said enough for both of them.

"Anselm wants your arm," she said finally.

"I know."

"He'll wait until you're ready. He's good at waiting. So am I." She turned to face him. "But there's something I've been waiting to ask, and the flood is going down, and the permits are settled, and there's no current left to blame for delay. So I'm asking now."

He braced, the way men brace when they think a blow is coming. "Ask."

"What name will you use now?"

The question landed exactly where she'd aimed it. She watched him take it — watched his breath go shallow, his hands close on the railing, his eyes fix on the middle distance where the river bent toward the gorges.

"Felix was never my name," he said at last. "It was a joke. Thorne's joke. The lucky one who lived." A muscle worked in his jaw. "For ten years I told myself I answered to it because it kept me safe. But that's not true. I answered to it because it was easier than being Sebastian Amberdale — the man who came down the mountain alone."

"And now?"

"Now I know I didn't come down alone by choice." He said it steadily, but his voice roughened on the last word. "Now I know whose choice it was. Now there's a ledger with his handwriting saying so, and a daughter who's read it, and a record at the concession post with my voice on it telling the whole story." He exhaled, long and slow. "The blacklist will take time to lift. Maybe years. Maybe never."

"That wasn't my question."

"I know." He turned to look at her, and in the dark his eyes were wet, though his voice stayed level. "Sebastian," he said. "My mother chose it. My father signed it. It's on my first passport and my worst regret and every contract I broke trying to disappear." His hand opened on the railing, palm up — an offer, or a surrender. "Sebastian Amberdale. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

Lin nodded slowly. Then she reached into her pocket and brought out the thing she'd been carrying for three days, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp: a page torn from a notebook, soft with age, covered in a handwriting she'd known since childhood — the big looping letters of the man who taught her to read maps.

She put it in his open hand.

"Thorne wrote this the night before the climb," she said. "He gave it to my father to keep, in case. My father kept it thirty years too long because keeping things was easier than delivering them. I promised myself I wouldn't make his mistake." She nodded at the page. "Read it."

In the last light, by the glow of the landing lamp, Sebastian read aloud, his voice low and unsteady:

*"Felix — if you're reading this, I got careless or the mountain got lucky. Either way, don't you dare spend the rest of your life paying for my altitude. Go down. Stay down if you have to. But live somewhere green, near water, with people who argue with you. Luck isn't a name. It's what other people decide to be for you, if you let them."*

The river moved beneath them, dark and patient, carrying everything it carried.

"He was right about one thing," Sebastian said, folding the page with great care. "Luck isn't a name."

"No," Lin agreed. "But it's a good nickname. And you're stuck with it either way — half this village heard the story at the concession post, and the children are already calling you *Felix-phi*. Uncle Felix." She smiled, small and real. "You can sign Sebastian on official papers. But here, you'll answer to both."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse, as if the machinery of it had seized sometime in the last decade and only now, in the humid dark above a mended river, begun to turn again.

"Both," he agreed.

Lin looked out at the water one last time. Downstream, the flood was receding mile by mile, uncovering sandbars and old moorings and the bones of boats that had served their seasons. Upstream, the gorges waited, indifferent and eternal. And here, on a landing built by a dead ferryman and inherited by his daughter, a man was learning to hold two names at once without dropping either.

Tomorrow there would be planks to sort and channels to relearn and a schoolhouse to fill and a registry at the district office where a signature waited — *Sebastian Amberdale*, inked fresh, the first true line of a new ledger.

Tonight there was only the river, and the lamp, and the two of them standing in the space where grief and gratitude meet and cannot be told apart.

"Come inside," Lin said. "Anselm has your arm to fix, my aunt has questions about your intentions, and the children want to know if Uncle Felix can really swim a gorge."

He straightened. Rolled his shoulders like a man taking up a pack he'd finally chosen to carry. And when he followed her up the landing, he did not look back at the water — not because he was done with rivers, but because for the first time in ten years, he knew exactly where he'd find them tomorrow.
