## 22. Felix and the Trail

The registry office at the concession post smelled of wet paper and diesel, and it had a line out the door, because half the river's guides had come downstream on the same flood we did.

I stood in that line holding a pen I'd borrowed from the clerk, and for ten years I had not held a pen over anything that mattered. Contracts for tourist runs, sure. Receipts for fuel. The small administrative debris of a life measured in miles. But never a name. Not my name.

The man ahead of me was arguing about a lapsed certification from Luang Prabang. The clerk was patient in the way of people who have heard every argument a river can produce. Outside the window, the water had begun its slow retreat from the steps of the post, leaving a brown tideline on the concrete like the river's own signature, marking how far it had come and where it stopped.

Lin stood beside me with her arms crossed, watching me watch the line.

"You could still walk out," she said. "There's a road now. There's always been a road."

"I know."

"You've spent ten years pretending there wasn't one."

"That's not what I was doing."

"No," she said. "It's worse. You were pretending there wasn't a door." She nodded at the registry book, visible through the office window, an enormous ledger — everything in this country is a ledger, I thought; Thorne would have laughed at that — with columns for names and licenses and rivers. "It's just paper, Sebastian."

"It's just paper," I agreed. "That's the whole problem."

* * *

Here is what I never told anyone, not Marguerite, not the inquiry board in Kathmandu, not Priya or Okonkwo or Tomas, not even Anselm, who carried his own dead like ballast: Elias Thorne called me Felix.

Not Sebastian. Not Amberdale, not *guide*, not *the man responsible*. Felix. It started as a joke on the approach trek, the third day out from the village, when I slipped crossing a scree field and came up laughing instead of bleeding. He pointed at me with his ice axe and said, *Felix. The lucky one.* And because he was Elias Thorne — famous, generous, a man who named things the way other men claimed them — the name stuck through six weeks of expedition, through base camp and the icefall and the long gray waiting, until the whole team used it, until it felt less like a nickname than a fact about me.

Felix. The lucky one who lived.

He said it last on the mountain too. I have gone over those minutes so many times they're worn smooth as river stone, and I know exactly what he said, because Anselm's confirmation of the sabotage didn't change it, only reframed it, the way moving a lamp changes a room without changing the furniture. The fixed line had been cut — cut by Thorne's own sponsor's man, by Marguerite's father, to force the summit push onto an unsecured route that would make their claim to the mountain's survey data unassailable, or some arithmetic of greed I still don't fully understand and no longer need to. What I know is that the anchor failed, and Thorne fell past me, and he caught my wrist, and the rope burned through my glove, and he looked up at me with the whole sky behind him and said:

"Live, Felix."

And then he let go.

Or the rope gave. Or the wind took him. For ten years I told myself all three versions in rotation, because each one assigned the guilt differently, and guilt was the only inheritance I had. I went down the mountain carrying his ledger in my pack — his field copy, which he'd made me carry because Felix carries the luck — and when the inquiry asked what happened, I said nothing, because nothing I could say would bring him back, and because part of me believed I deserved the blacklist, deserved Nepal closing its doors, deserved Peru following, deserved the slow exile down lesser rivers into warmer water where nobody died and nothing mattered.

I measured my life in miles because miles don't ask questions.

But here is the thing about the ledger, the thing I understood finally standing in Lin's village while the elders touched its pages like they were touching his hand: Thorne didn't write *live, Felix* as a curse. He wrote pages and pages after that night — no. Before. That's what broke me open in chapter and verse, reading his handwriting by lamplight while the flood rose: the entries before the climb, the ones naming the sabotage he suspected, the ones describing a village downstream where he'd taught a girl to read contour lines, the ones planning to come back, to build a school, to run the Nam Kha with a ferrywoman's daughter as his pilot. He had a future planned. He'd written me into it. Not as the man who let go. As the man who stayed.

Felix was never the lucky one who lived despite himself. Felix was the one entrusted with the living.

I just refused delivery for ten years.

* * *

Marguerite left on her launch two days after the concession post, before the water even crested. She came to me on the steps, in the rain, with her father's name still raw between us, and she didn't offer reinstatement again. She offered something smaller and harder.

"He taught you to read maps too," she said. "Didn't he. That's why Lin looked at you like that."

"He taught everyone something different."

"He taught me accounting." Her mouth twisted. "I'm going to fund the school. The village school. Don't make it strange."

"It's already strange."

"Yes," she said, and for one moment she was not my rival, not the woman who'd hired my crew with a lie folded inside a contract, but a daughter standing in the rain over a father's grave she'd dug herself with a pen. "Write your name down, Sebastian. He'd hate what you did with the hiding."

Then she was gone, upstream against the current, which was wrong in every way, and somehow right.

Tomas stayed. That surprised everyone except, I think, Tomas. His debtors had scattered when the flood took their landing, and the money he'd been paid to sell our route sat in a waterproof bag he kept trying to give away — to Lin, to the elders, finally to Priya for hull repairs on a boat that no longer existed. In the end Lin took it and put it in the school fund without telling him, and when he found out he cried on the dock like a boy, and Okonkwo put a hand on his neck and said, "Now you're paid honestly. Start over." He's rebuilding the ferry route with Lin's cousins. He radios me coordinates sometimes, for no reason, just to prove he can be trusted with them.

Priya and Okonkwo headed south for the season, new contracts, better boats. Priya shook my hand like a foreman closing a job well done, which from her is a love letter. Okonkwo hugged me hard enough to crack a rib and said, "You dove for us in the gorge. You know that, right? You weren't the lucky one. You were the one who made luck." I'm keeping that.

Anselm went back to medicine. Real medicine, licensed, his confession at the concession post having done what confessions do — lightened nothing, but straightened everything. He sent a postcard that said only: *The doctor recommends rivers.*

And the Meridian is at the bottom of the Nam Kha, forty miles of drowned forest from where she sank, and I find I don't mourn her. Boats are miles. I'm done with miles.

* * *

The line moved. The clerk called next, and it was me.

The registry book lay open under the fan, and the column for my district — the Nam Kha corridor, the river below the mountain, the river I had sworn off in every way a man can swear off a place — waited with a blank line.

The clerk looked at me. Lin looked at me. Somewhere outside, the floodwater ticked against the steps, going down, going home.

"Name?" the clerk said.

I thought about all the names I'd signed in ten years. S. Amberdale, initial only, on tourist manifests. A false smoothness of initials and aliases in Peru. The way I'd trained myself to hear "Sebastian" as someone else's luggage.

Thorne called me Felix. The inquiry called me guide-of-record. The blacklist knew me by number. But none of those were mine to choose anymore, and the whole lesson of the ledger, of the flood, of Lin standing beside me not holding my hand yet but about to, was that a name isn't something you're given or something you hide behind. It's something you claim, out loud, in ink, witnessed.

"Sebastian," I said. "Sebastian Amberdale."

I wrote it. Full name, both words, letters steady. The clerk stamped it without ceremony, which was correct. Nothing needed ceremony. The truth doesn't.

Lin took my hand as we came out onto the steps, her fingers fitting into mine like the last piece of a channel only she knew how to read. Below us the Nam Kha ran high and brown and alive, carrying everything downstream — silt, branches, the old boat's ghost, ten years of my silence — toward the sea.

"What will you count now?" she asked. "If not miles."

I watched the water. I thought about a man on a mountain saying *live, Felix*, and a girl learning contour lines, and a school that would open in the dry season with Marguerite's money and Thorne's name over the door, and a ferry route being rebuilt plank by plank by a man paying off a debt no one was charging him for.

"Seasons," I said. "Rapids. Kids who need to learn maps."

She smiled. "That's not counting. That's belonging."

"Yeah," I said, and squeezed her hand, and turned my face into the wind coming off the water — the same wind, maybe, that had been there on the mountain, that had taken him and spared me and waited ten patient years for me to understand the difference between surviving and living. "I know."

The river ran on. So would we.

Downstream, at the bend below the concession post, where the channel narrowed and the first rapids of the dry season were already beginning to show their white teeth, a new boat was tied up at the landing — narrow-beamed, shallow-drafted, built for the channels only Lin knew. Painted on its bow, still wet, in a hand I recognized as Tomas's careful lettering, was a single word.

**Felix.**

Lin saw it too. She laughed — the first full laugh I'd heard from her since the flood — and pulled me toward the dock.

"Well?" she said. "The trail's not going to run itself, Felix."

And for the first time in ten years, I answered to my name.
