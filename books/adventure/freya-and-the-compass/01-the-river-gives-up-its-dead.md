## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been eating the bank all week, and on the last morning of the season it finally spat something back.

Lior Dunmore heard it before he saw it — a hollow knock of wood against stone, carried up through the gray water like a knuckle rapping at a door. He was waist-deep in the crossing shallows, testing the ford for his client, and the sound came again, patient, rhythmic, as if whatever made it had all the time in the world and he did not.

"Something down there?" Pellam Voss called from the bank. The old man stood with his hands in the pockets of a coat too fine for the trail, wool the color of wet slate, watching the water with the expression of a man who had paid good money to be cold and was determined to enjoy it.

"Log, maybe." Lior waded toward the sound. "Or a root ball. Floods pull all kinds of things loose."

The flood had come through nine days ago — an early, angry thaw-swollen spate that had torn the color out of the river and left it running the brown of steeped tea. It had rearranged the valley while Lior slept: moved a boulder the size of a cart horse fifty yards downstream, sheared a stand of alders off at the knee, and peeled away six feet of the eastern bank like skin from fruit. He'd walked the damage the next morning with his hands jammed in his pockets, doing the arithmetic every guide does after a flood — what's changed, what's dangerous, what's gone. The river never gave anything back. That was the first law he'd learned up here, younger than memory. The mountain takes; the river carries away. You budget your life accordingly.

So when the knocking came again — three soft blows, deliberate as a signal — he felt the wariness settle over him like a second coat. The wilderness had never handed him anything for free. Every fish he'd landed had cost him patience, every sheltered camp a search, every safe crossing a negotiation conducted in cold water. A river that *gave* you something was a river setting a hook.

He found it snagged in the roots of a fallen spruce, half-buried in the new gravel bar the flood had built. Not a log. Not a root ball.

A chest.

It sat wedged in the tangle of black roots like an egg in a nest, its lid just breaking the surface of a pool the flood had left behind, rocking gently each time the current pushed past it. Knock. Knock. Knock. Wood against stone, patient as a clock.

Lior stood in the shallows and looked at it for a long moment without moving.

Chests did not belong in rivers. Chests belonged in attics, in cellars, in the holds of ships, in stories told badly by men who wanted something from you. In thirty years of walking these valleys he had pulled drowned hikers out of pools like this one, and once a whole raft, and more times than he could count the bloated carcasses of deer. Never a chest. The river gave up its dead, yes — but this wasn't dead. This was *lost*, and lost things had owners, and owners had ways of finding people who found their property.

"You've gone quiet," Pellam said behind him. The old man had come down to the water's edge despite the mud, which told Lior more than any question could have. Curiosity was the one fee everyone paid gladly.

"There's something in the roots."

"What kind of something?"

Lior didn't answer. He worked his way along the gravel bar, boots finding the channels between stones by feel, and crouched at the edge of the pool.

The chest was small — no bigger than a sea trunk's stubborn child, two feet long, bound in iron-strapped oak gone black with decades of wet. Water wept steadily from its seams. And set into the center of the lid, catching what little light the morning offered, was a plate of brass the size of his palm, stamped with a design the flood had scrubbed clean.

He reached out and wiped the brass with his thumb.

A seal. Some kind of crest — a starburst, or a compass rose, or both, the lines worn shallow but still sharp enough to catch a fingernail. Around its rim ran lettering in an alphabet he didn't recognize, all vertical strokes and closed loops, dense as stitching.

His thumb stayed on the metal longer than it needed to. The brass was cold, colder than the water around it, which made no sense and which he decided not to think about.

"That's not local work," Pellam said. He had come out onto the bar after all, mud sucking at his expensive boots, and now he leaned over Lior's shoulder with the unhurried interest of a man reading someone else's mail. "That script. I've seen it in old ledgers. Coastal trade records, forty years back." He paused. "Before the annexation."

Lior said nothing. The word sat in the air between them like a stone dropped in a well, falling and falling without hitting bottom.

*Before the annexation.* Everyone up here spoke of those years the way you spoke of a fire that had taken a neighbor's house — carefully, in the past tense, with your eyes on the middle distance. The old nation had owned this coast once, all of it, the ports and the passes and the fishing villages strung along the shore like beads on a wire. Then came the regime change, the flags coming down, the maps redrawn overnight by men in distant offices, and the coast became a borderland, and the borderland became a question, and the question got answered by salvage fleets and customs cutters and men who didn't knock. Lior had been a boy when it happened. He remembered mostly the silence afterward — how certain words stopped being said, how certain roads stopped being walked, how the villages on the far side of the headlands simply stopped appearing on charts, as if the sea had swallowed them instead of the politicians.

He had spent his whole adult life being careful about exactly this kind of thing. Careful about what he carried across lines. Careful about who he guided and where. Careful because a man without papers learns early that attention is a debt that always comes due.

And now a river had deposited a sealed chest bearing the crest of a dead country ten feet from his boot.

"Leave it," Pellam said. There was no particular urgency in his voice, which somehow made it worse. "Floods move things. Someone will come looking."

"Someone always comes looking," Lior agreed. But he was already uncoiling the rope from his shoulder harness.

* * *

The chest weighed what a chest full of water weighs, which is to say it fought him the whole way. He got a loop of rope under the iron banding, braced against the fallen spruce, and hauled, and the roots held it greedily until they didn't — a groan of wood, a suck of released water, and then it came free all at once and nearly took him off his feet. They dragged it up the bank together, Pellam pulling with surprising strength for a man whose hands had never done a day of honest labor, until it lay in the alder shade above the flood line, streaming.

Up close, the craftsmanship was better than it had any right to be. The iron straps were hand-forged, riveted with a regularity machines couldn't match. The oak staves were tight as barrel work. And the lock — a heavy brass mechanism set flush into the lid — showed no rust at all. Thirty years, forty, in a river, and the metal gleamed like it had been oiled yesterday.

"Sealed against water," Pellam murmured. He'd produced a silk handkerchief from somewhere and was wiping mud from the lid with fastidious little strokes, like a man uncovering a face. "Military grade, once. Or diplomatic." His eyes flicked up, quick and shrewd. "You know what you're holding?"

"I know what it looks like."

"And what does it look like?"

Lior straightened. Downstream, the river went on talking to itself, brown and busy, carrying off everything else it had stolen this season. Above them the ridgeline stood gray against a sky the color of dishwater, and the wind had begun to carry the smell of the lowlands — warm air, thick air, the monsoon still weeks out but already leaning on the horizon like a drunk on a doorframe.

"It looks like trouble," he said. "It looks like something somebody sank on purpose, and the river changed its mind."

Pellam smiled thinly. "And yet you're not walking away."

"I'm thinking about it."

"No, you're not." The old man crouched, studying the seal again. "You're a careful man, Dunmore. I could see that from the first mile — you read water like other men read contracts. So tell me why a careful man is standing over a chest full of somebody else's secrets with a crowbar in his hand."

Lior looked down and realized Pellam was right. At some point in the last minute he had taken the pry bar from his pack. He turned it over in his gloved hands, feeling its familiar weight, and tried to find the honest answer.

Because the wilderness had never given him anything. Because he was forty-one years old and his record was a blank page where a life should be — no land, no name on any register, no papers beyond a guide's license renewed yearly by officials who looked through him rather than at him. Because the river had knocked three times on the inside of that lid like something asking to be let out, and some stubborn, stupid, starving part of him wanted to believe that for once, just once, the world was handing a thing to *him*, specifically, deliberately, instead of taking.

"Bad reasons," he said finally.

"The best ones usually are." Pellam stepped back. "Well. Your river, your grave. Open it or don't."

* * *

The lock surrendered easier than it should have. The pry bar found the seam, Lior leaned his weight into it, and the brass mechanism — corroded nowhere, seized nowhere — clicked open like it had been waiting.

Inside, dry as a sermon, lay paper.

Documents, dozens of them, folded into bundles and wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age. Coin — heavy gold pieces, unworn, stacked in leather rolls that crumbled at his touch. And beneath them, nested in a bed of tarred wool, an object that stopped his breath for a moment:

A compass.

Not a surveyor's instrument, not a cheap pocket thing. This was a navigator's compass, its case of dark horn banded in brass, its crystal thick as a porthole. The card beneath it was ivory-white and marked not with the usual points but with fine radiating lines converging on a single symbol at the center — the same starburst as the seal on the lid.

And the needle was wrong.

Lior knew compasses the way priests knew scripture. He had navigated by them in whiteouts, in fog so thick it drank the light, in canyons where the rock itself lied about direction. A needle trembles, settles, forgives. This one didn't tremble at all. It swung slowly, deliberately, ignoring north entirely, and settled pointing east-southeast — out of the valley, down the watershed, toward the coast. Toward open water.

He turned the compass ninety degrees. The needle swung after it, unhurried, and settled again on the same bearing. East-southeast. Patient as the knocking had been.

"That's not possible," Pellam said quietly.

"No," Lior agreed. "It isn't."

He should have put it back. He understood that even then, standing in the alder shade with the monsoon building somewhere beyond the ridgeline and the river muttering below. Men like him survived by treating impossible things the way they treated thin ice — noting them, circling wide, moving on. The documents were trouble enough: he could see official crests among the folds, wax seals the color of dried blood, letterheads in that same dense stitched alphabet. The coin was worse — gold drew eyes the way blood drew sharks. And the compass was worst of all, because a compass that ignored north wasn't an instrument anymore. It was a message. Somebody had built it to point somewhere, and somewhere meant *there*, and *there* meant leaving the mountains where Lior Dunmore was invisible and competent and safe.

But he didn't put it back. He stood there turning it in his hands, watching the needle hold its stubborn bearing, and felt the strange sensation — unfamiliar, unwelcome, unmistakable — of being *chosen*. Of the wilderness, which had spent thirty years taking from him, finally putting something in his palm.

The needle pointed east-southeast. Toward the coast. Toward the drowned places. Toward everything he had spent his life walking uphill to avoid.

"Dunmore," Pellam said, and there was something new in the old man's voice now — not caution anymore but a kind of pity. "I'm going to say something, and then I'm going to finish my holiday and go home, and neither of us will speak of this morning again. That seal. That script. My grandfather traded along that coast before the annexation, and he told me once that the only thing worth less than a dead nation's paper was the life of a man caught carrying it." He buttoned his coat with slow precision. "Burn the documents. Melt the coin if you must. But that compass — that compass belongs to people who are still alive, and they are not forgiving people, and they do not forget faces."

He turned and started up the trail toward the lodge, expensive boots finding the stones without slipping, and did not look back.

Lior stood alone in the alder shade with the chest open at his feet and the compass steady in his hand, pointing its impossible point, and the wind came down off the ridge carrying the first true smell of the coming monsoon — wet earth, far-off salt, a storm still gathering over waters he had never seen.

The needle didn't waver.

Neither, he realized with a sinking that had nothing to do with fear, did he.
