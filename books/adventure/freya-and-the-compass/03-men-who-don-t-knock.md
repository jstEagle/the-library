## 3. Men Who Don't Knock

The knock came at the wrong hour and in the wrong way.

Lior had spent eleven years learning the grammar of sounds at his cabin above the Kestrel Narrows — the tick of the iron stove cooling, the groan of frost settling into the roof beams, the particular silence a mountain makes when something on it is moving that shouldn't be. So when the sound came at half past two in the morning, it wasn't the knock itself that woke him but the quality of it: three raps, evenly spaced, patient as a debt collector. Nobody who meant him well knocked like that at half past two. Friends pounded. Clients apologized through the door. This was the knock of men who already considered the door theirs.

He lay still in the dark and counted.

Four more raps. Then a voice, low and unhurried: "Mr. Dunmore. We know you're awake."

*We.* The word did more damage than the knock.

Lior rose without lighting the lamp, bare feet finding the cold boards by memory. His rifle hung on its pegs by the door — he took it down, checked the chamber out of habit rather than hope, and set it back. Six men behind that door, maybe, if they were professionals, and professionals didn't come to a guide's cabin without accounting for rifles. Shooting one of them would only tell the rest exactly where he stood, and it would make him a killer instead of a man with a story. He'd been blacklisted once already for a story nobody believed. He wasn't going to hand them a corpse to make it stick.

"I'm coming out," he said, loud enough to carry. "Door's open."

It wasn't, quite — he left it an inch shy of the latch so it would swing slow — and he stepped sideways into the shadow between the stove and the woodbin as the door moved and three shapes poured through it like water finding a breach.

They were not the harbor kind of muscle, the soft-bellied enforcers who leaned on dockworkers and broke fingers for gambling debts. These were leaner. Rain-slicked oilskins, no insignia, boots wrapped in cloth so they made no sound on his own floorboards in his own house. The one in front carried a lantern with the shutter half closed, letting out only a blade of light, and he swept that blade across the room the way a man reads a page — gear rack, chart table, the iron bed, the sealed chest sitting where Lior had left it under the upturned skiff hull he used as a workbench.

"Where is it?" the lantern man asked. Not *what*. *It.*

"Where's what?" Lior kept his voice level, bored even. "You've broken into my home at night. You tell me what you're looking for and I'll tell you whether I've seen it."

The lantern came up and caught him full in the face. He let it. Blinking was weakness; he held the light the way he held weather, unimpressed.

"You pulled something out of the Vell," the man said. "Three days ago. Flood-scoured bank below the old ferry cut. A client saw you hauling it."

"My client saw me hauling driftwood." Which was true, in its way; Tam Osterby had watched him drag the chest ashore and had laughed himself hoarse at the idea that the river had finally paid Lior Dunmore for anything. "The Vell gives up dead sheep and other people's fences every spring. If you want driftwood, take your pick."

The lantern man studied him for a long moment, and Lior understood that the study was the point — that this was a man trained to let silence do the work of hands. Behind him, the other two had begun to move through the cabin with terrible courtesy, lifting things, setting them down, opening drawers on runners greased so they wouldn't squeak. They weren't searching. Searching was what you did when you didn't know. They were *inventorying*, and the difference turned Lior's stomach cold.

"Your name came up in Grayharbor," the lantern man said conversationally. "Meridian registry interest. A chest bearing a certain seal, recovered from tidal water, reported by a fisherman who sells information cheaper than he sells herring." He smiled without warmth. "You should have burned whatever you found, Mr. Dunmore. That's free advice. It's the last free thing you'll get from the Meridian Star's people."

The name went through Lior like a splinter working toward bone. He hadn't known what the seal meant when he'd pried the chest open — brass-stamped, some dead country's crest, a compass inside whose needle swung to nothing on any map he owned. He'd taken it to a whispering appraiser in Grayharbor who had gone gray around the mouth, pushed it back across the table, and told him to burn it and forget the river ever gave him anything. Lior had thought the man was being dramatic. Men who whispered instead of bargained, he'd learned long ago, were usually just cowards.

Apparently not this time.

"It's in the chest," Lior said, because lying about its existence would only prolong this, and prolonging this meant more of his life inventoried by strangers. "Under the skiff hull. Take it and go."

"We'll take everything and go," the lantern man said mildly. "That's how this works. You'll sit down."

"I'll stand."

One of the others — big through the shoulders, a face like a closed fist — crossed the room faster than a man that size had any right to, and put Lior against the wall with a forearm across his throat. Not choking. Just *presenting* the option. Lior let his weight settle, kept his hands open at his sides, and looked past the man's shoulder at his own cabin being dismantled around him.

They worked methodically. The chart table first — his survey maps of the upper Vell, fifteen years of pencil annotations, current speeds marked in his own shorthand, safe lines through the Narrows drawn and redrawn until the paper wore thin. Folded, not torn. Taken. Then the gear rack: his rope, the good rope, hundred-and-twenty-foot kernmantle he'd bought with an entire season's guiding money; his ice tools; the canvas pack his father had carried before him, patched at both hips with sailcloth. His sleeping roll. His spare boots. The tin box under the floorboard by the hearth where he kept his papers — such as they were, the thin and humiliating file of a man the licensing board had struck off six years ago on testimony he still couldn't disprove. They read every page by lantern light while he watched, and one of them laughed quietly at something, and that laugh did more damage than the forearm across his throat ever could.

The chest itself they opened right there on the floor. The lantern man lifted the compass out with two fingers, turning it, watching the needle swing its mad, magnet-defying arc, and something flickered across his face that might have been hunger or might have been fear, and Lior couldn't tell which frightened him more.

"That's the one," said the fist-faced man, still holding Lior to the wall.

"And the documents?"

"Gone through. Nothing but old paper and coin."

"The seal stamp?"

"Here." A third man held up the brass die from the chest's lid, and the lantern man took it, weighed it, and dropped it into an oilskin pouch that clinked.

Then they turned to the rest of the cabin, and Lior understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the compass had never been all of it. They were taking the stove iron's worth of his life because taking it was the message. *You are a man we can enter. You are a man whose things fit in our pouches. You are prey that happened to be holding something we wanted, and prey doesn't get to keep the den.*

"Your client," the lantern man said, shrugging Lior's pack onto his own shoulders — Lior's pack, worn smooth where his spine had shaped it — "will be spoken to. Anyone you've shown the compass to will be spoken to. If you go to the harbormaster, the harbormaster has been spoken to. If you go to the licensing board—" he smiled again, that bloodless smile— "well. They already know you, don't they, Mr. Dunmore?"

They left the way they'd come, soundless, and the last thing the lantern showed him was his own chart table, stripped bare, the wood underneath pale and unstained where fifteen years of maps had lain. Then the door shut, and the mountain's silence came back in like cold water filling a hull.

* * *

Lior sat on the floor of his own house for a long time.

Not stunned — he refused the word, refused the softness in it. He was doing arithmetic. That was all. The stove was gone, which meant cold, which meant he had perhaps an hour of comfort before the cabin's chill became the whole night's chill. The food was gone, or nearly; they'd missed the smoked trout cached in the smokehouse twenty yards downhill, because they'd been in a hurry and hurry was a flaw he could use. The rope was gone, the tools were gone, the pack was gone, the papers were gone.

The papers hurt worst, and he examined that hurt the way he'd examine a suspect snowpack, probing for the layer that would slide. It wasn't the license — that was a dead thing anyway, a name on a list that had ruined him. It was the letter from his mother, thirty years old, the only one she'd written that hadn't been a bill or a scolding. They'd read it by lantern light and packed it away with the charts, and somewhere out there in the rain it was riding in a stranger's pouch like contraband, like evidence, like a thing that belonged to the Meridian Star's people now.

He got up. He lit the stub of candle he kept hidden in the chimney flue — because a man who lived alone above a flood river learned to keep small redundancies — and he looked at what they'd left him.

A blanket. An axe. The clothes he stood in. And, tucked beneath the upturned skiff hull where the chest had been, nothing at all — except that when he lifted the hull, his hand found the loose board at the back of the workbench, and his heart did something complicated, because the board was still loose, and behind the board, wrapped in waxed canvas, was the compass.

He'd moved it there two nights ago, after the appraiser's warning, telling himself it was superstition, telling himself a man didn't hide a piece of brass from the dark. But the wilderness had taught him one law above all others: *the river never gives you anything for free, and neither does anyone else.* He'd hidden it the way animals cache food — not because they expect the thief, but because the ones that didn't cache weren't ancestors to anything.

Now he sat on the cold floor with the compass in his palm and turned it in the candlelight. The needle swung, settled, swung again — pointing stubbornly southeast, toward water, toward a coast he'd never guided anyone to, toward a wreck called the Meridian Star that apparently had living people who behaved like this. Men who didn't knock. Men who took a dead woman's letter in an oilskin pouch and laughed reading it.

The cold fury came up in him slowly, the way floodwater comes up a valley — not a wave but a rise, steady, total, changing the shape of everything it touches. He catalogued it as it rose. They had taken his gear, and gear was replaceable; he'd replaced it once already at nineteen, walking out of a wrecked expedition with nothing but a knife, and he could do it again. They had taken his charts, and charts could be redrawn; the Narrows lived in his head better than on any paper. They had taken his papers, and his papers had been a wound anyway.

But they had made him prey. They had entered his house on wrapped boots and inventoried his life and told him whom he could speak to, and they had done it with the calm of men who had done it a hundred times to a hundred people who stayed quiet afterward, because staying quiet was what prey did.

Lior Dunmore had been many hard things — orphan, blacklistee, a name the rivers passed along with warnings attached. He had never once been quiet.

He dressed in everything he owned. He ate the smoked trout cold, standing up. He took the axe, then put it back — an axe was a confession — and took instead the skinning knife and the firesteel and the candle stubs and the waxed canvas. He filled a waterskin from the rain barrel. Last, he wrapped the compass against his ribs, under his shirt, where its small swinging weight lay against his heartbeat like a second pulse that knew something he didn't.

Then he went out into the rain and did not take the trail.

The trail was where they'd watch, if they watched at all. Instead he dropped over the bank behind the smokehouse, down through the alders to the flood channel, where the spring melt had torn a gully through the hillside and the county, in some forgotten budget year, had buried a storm drain to carry the overflow under the old ferry road. He'd mapped it years ago the way he mapped everything — idly, obsessively, in case. The grate at the headwall had rusted through on one side. He peeled it back, and the smell of wet stone and old iron rose up to meet him, and he went in on hands and knees with the candle stub burning in a niche ahead of him, crawling through the black throat of the mountain while the rain hammered the ground above.

It was a bad passage and he loved it immediately. It asked nothing of him but patience and gave him back the one thing the night had stolen: the feeling of being competent in the dark, of knowing more than the men hunting him, of the wilderness — even its buried, engineered corners — testing him on terms he understood. Twice the drain narrowed and he had to exhale fully to squeeze through, cheek flat against slime-cold brick, the compass pressing its round shape into his sternum like a fist. Once water ran deep enough to soak him to the waist, and he laughed, actually laughed, a short ugly sound in the dark, because they had taken his boots' mates and left him one pair, and now those were soaked too, and none of it mattered, because they didn't know where he was, and he knew where they would be.

Grayharbor. By dawn, or the day after. The lantern man had said the harbormaster had been spoken to, which meant the fleet's reach ran through the port, which meant the port was where the fight was. Lior had spent six years avoiding ports, avoiding officials, avoiding the whole paper world that had chewed him up and spat him onto this mountainside. Every instinct said run higher, run farther, winter alone in the high country and let the compass rot in a drawer.

But the needle pointed southeast, and the men who didn't knock had made the mistake of leaving him alive, and alive and angry was the most dangerous condition a blacklisted guide could occupy.

He came out of the drain's lower mouth near the ferry ruins, gray light just beginning to bleed into the east, the Vell running high and brown and loud below him. He crouched in the alder roots, soaked, shivering, owning nothing but a knife, a firesteel, and a compass that lied about north — and he found, to his surprise, that he felt lighter than he had in six years. There was nothing left to protect. There was only forward.

Downstream, far off, a ship's horn sounded through the rain — deep, patient, twice — the voice of something big moving toward the coast.

Lior wrung out his sleeves, turned his back on the burned-out cabin above, and started walking.
