## 4. The Archivist

Nadia Voss had been watching the mountain man for three days before she let him see her.

It was not sentiment that made her careful. It was arithmetic. In the nine years since the Vessarine government dissolved itself in a single night of radio silence — since the archives burned and the harbors filled with ships that were not hers — she had learned that everything worth having was also worth killing for, and the compass was worth more than most. So she counted. She watched him buy rope in the chandlery on Harrow Street and pay with coins that rang wrong against the counter, old minting, dead-country silver. She watched him ask no questions at the ferry office and too many at the customs house, where a clerk's face went carefully blank when Lior Dunmore said a word he should not have known. She watched him sleep badly, sitting up in the lee of a sea wall with his back to stone and his hand near his knife, the way men sleep when they have already been robbed once and expect to be robbed again.

He did not know he was being read. That was the first thing she liked about him.

The second thing she liked about him was that he lied well. Not smoothly — smoothly was for diplomats and thieves with good lawyers — but thoroughly, the way a man lies when lying has kept him alive. When the fishwife asked where he'd come from, he said *upriver*, which was true, and *passing through*, which was not, and both statements were delivered with the same flat calm, so that the truth and the falsehood were indistinguishable even to him, probably. Nadia recognized the technique because it was her own. Exiles were all cartographers in the end; they drew maps of themselves, coastlines of invented history, and lived inside the borders.

She had followed the compass's wake across four hundred miles of coast. Rumors moved faster than ships: a chest pulled from a flood-scoured bank, a seal that made an appraiser go pale, men who didn't knock. The rumors had a shape, and the shape was the Meridian Star, and the Meridian Star had a shape too — a black hull, a stolen registry, a captain named Orlan Vey who had sailed out of Vessarine waters one final night carrying eleven crates of her nation's patrimony and the salvage claim that proved them his.

Her father had drawn the charts those crates held. Her father had died believing they would come home.

So: three days of watching, and then rain, and then the decision.

* * *

She chose the evening tide for it, when the harbor emptied and the light went the color of tarnished brass. He took his supper apart from the tavern crowd, on the breakwater, where the stones were slick with spray and the wind came off the water carrying salt and diesel and the far rot of kelp. She sat down beside him without asking, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and set a folded chart between them on the wet rock.

He did not startle. That surprised her. Most men startled.

"You've been following me," he said. His voice was low, unhurried, the voice of someone accustomed to being heard over weather. "Since the chandlery."

"Since before the chandlery." She let the wind take a strand of hair across her face and did not fix it. Let him look at her and try to place the accent — the vowels that flattened wrong, the consonants that came from a country whose name was now a legal fiction. "You bought rope you didn't need. You looked at the tide tables twice and memorized them once. And yesterday you stood outside the customs house for forty minutes deciding whether to go in, and then didn't."

"Long way to tell me I'm being careful."

"A long way to tell you I'm careful too." She unfolded the chart. It was old paper, foxed at the folds, drawn in ink that had gone brown as dried blood. The coastline it depicted did not exist anymore; the sea had taken the eastern third of it, and the government had taken the rest. But the lagoon was there, and the reef, and the wreck marked in her father's small exact hand: *Meridian Star, bearing 214, six fathoms, mud bottom.*

Lior looked at the chart for a long time. Long enough that she saw him decide something — she could almost hear the ledger closing behind his eyes.

"I don't know what you think I have," he said.

"A brass compass with a needle that ignores north." She said it plainly, watching his hands rather than his face. Hands told the truth earlier than faces did. His right hand shifted, very slightly, toward the inside pocket of his coat, and stopped. "A chest stamped with the double tide of the Vessarine Republic. A seal that marks its bearer as heir to a salvage claim the old regime buried under thirty years of paperwork and, eventually, under water."

The wind gusted. Somewhere behind them a gull screamed and was answered.

"And you are?" he said.

"Nadia Voss." She gave the name like a coin tossed on a counter — here, take it, see if it spends. "Cartographer. Third survey office, Port Amalin, before Port Amalin stopped being anywhere." She watched the name land. He knew it. Everyone from the old waters knew it, or had known it, or had drowned in the knowing. "My people built those reefs' charts. My father drew the one you're looking at. The compass in your pocket was forged in Amalin's instrument works, serial prefix VS, and it was carried out of our territory against every law my nation ever wrote by a captain who is currently sitting on a dredge fleet that calls itself a salvage concern."

"And what do you want?"

"The truth," she said, and heard how it sounded, and laughed shortly. "No. First the truth. Then the compass."

* * *

They talked while the tide turned. It was, she would think later, less a conversation than a negotiation conducted in the grammar of two people who had both survived by withholding.

He told her a version of the river story. Chest, flood bank, brass stamp. He did not mention the appraiser. He did not mention the men who had torn his cabin apart, though she saw the absence of his gear — no pack, no climbing iron, a guide traveling light as a fugitive travels — and she did not press, because pressing was a currency she might need later. He told her he'd found the compass and hadn't yet decided what it was worth.

"That's a lie," she said.

"It's a true statement about the future."

She almost smiled. Almost. "You've decided it's worth more than money. Men who find things worth only money sell them within a week. You've had yours for—" she counted, deliberately, letting him see her count— "eleven days."

"Twelve."

"Twelve days, and you're still carrying it, and you spent last night sleeping against a wall instead of taking a room." She turned to look at him fully for the first time. Up close he was younger than his hands suggested, weathered in the specific way of men who worked above the treeline, with eyes the pale gray of glacier melt. "Someone frightened you badly enough to make you careful, and not badly enough to make you run. That means you know what it is, Mr. Dunmore. You know, and you haven't given it back, and you haven't sold it. Which leaves exactly one explanation, and it's the one I'm hoping for."

"Which is?"

"You want it to mean something."

He was quiet for a while. The tide sucked and chuckled among the breakwater stones. Far out, the harbor lights of the fleet's tender ships strung along the horizon like a low, hostile constellation — she watched him notice them, and watched his jaw set.

"My father was a river pilot," Lior said finally. "Blacklisted for running cargo past a toll nobody had the right to charge. He died stateless. I grew up stateless. Papers matter to me the way water matters to a drowning man." He looked at her. "I don't know yet what this thing means. But I know what it feels like, and it feels like it belongs to somebody."

Nadia felt something loosen in her chest — hope, dangerous, uninvited — and immediately began building the scaffolding around it. Guarded. Everything guarded. Nine years of exile taught you that hope without armor was just grief arriving early.

"Then listen," she said, "because I'll only say this once, and I'll say it straight, which is more than anyone else in this story will give you."

She told him about the Meridian Star. Not the wreck — the ship, before the wreck. A survey vessel out of Amalin, legitimate once, captained by a man named Orlan Vey who had spent a decade earning trust he intended to spend all at once. On the last night of the Republic, when the ministries were shredding their own records and the radio bands went silent one frequency at a time, Vey had loaded his hold with the national archive's maritime collection: the original surveys, the grant charts, the salvage registries, the instruments of office. Including a compass that was not merely an instrument. Including a claim — sealed, stamped, legally alive under maritime conventions that outlive the countries that sign them — establishing the Voss family as rightful salvors of anything recovered from Vessarine sovereign waters.

"He didn't steal treasure," Nadia said. "Treasure is coins. He stole *standing*. He stole the paperwork that says who owns the dark under the water. And then he sold pieces of it, over years, to men with dredges, until the fleet that hunts us now owned enough of the map to call itself the map."

"And the village?" Lior said quietly.

She went still. "What village?"

"The one the fleet drowned." He said it without drama, the way he seemed to say everything, and somehow that made it worse. "I've heard it twice now, in two ports, always whispered. Always after the third drink. Nobody will say the name."

Nadia looked out at the water. The tide had turned; the current ran the other way now, dragging the harbor's refuse seaward, and somewhere beyond the reef line, under sixty feet of cold green murk, her hometown lay in the dark with its boats still moored in a row.

"Sarnholt," she said. "Its name was Sarnholt. And when I find out the rest of what happened there, Mr. Dunmore, the man who did it is going to learn what a drowned nation does with its dead."

* * *

They sat until full dark. At some point she produced bread and hard cheese from her satchel and split it without asking, and he ate his share without thanking her, and the transaction felt more honest than any contract either of them had ever signed.

"So here is where we stand," she said at last, brushing crumbs from her knees. "You have a compass that answers to my bloodline — don't argue, I've read the instrument-works specifications, I know what the VS-series needles were tuned to. I have the knowledge to use it. The fleet has guns, dredges, and a head start. And the monsoon has a schedule that cares nothing for any of us. Six tides, roughly, before landfall closes the reef passages."

"Six tides," he repeated. He wasn't asking her to confirm it. He was weighing it, the way he'd weigh a route across rotten ice — testing each step before committing weight.

"There's a coastal freighter, the Corvane, loading at pier three. She sails tonight for the southern ports. The fleet chartered her sister ship, which means her manifests get waved through." She paused. "There's room in her bilge space for two people who don't exist."

Lior looked at her for a long moment. In the dark his face was mostly shadow and the gray gleam of his eyes, and she realized she had made a speech assuming his answer, and that he had noticed, and that he was deciding whether to be flattered or insulted.

"Why me?" he said. "You clearly don't need me. You have the charts, the name, the claim. I'm a blacklist with legs."

It was the sharpest question he'd asked all evening, and it deserved a lie, and she found — irritatingly, dangerously — that she didn't want to give him one.

"Because the reef passage into that lagoon has killed every pilot who tried it under power," she said. "Because whatever happens in the next six days, someone on my side of this needs to know water the way the fleet knows ledgers. And because—" she stopped herself, and started again, more carefully— "because I've spent nine years trusting no one, and it's kept me alive and gotten me nowhere. You looked at a compass that points at a drowned country and didn't sell it. That's the best reference anyone's offered me in a decade."

The wind shifted, bringing the smell of rain from inland. Out on the black water, the fleet's lights blinked their slow, patient blink.

Lior Dunmore stood, rolled his shoulders like a man accepting a load, and looked down at her.

"Pier three," he said. "What time does she sail?"

"Midnight tide."

"Then we'd better not knock on the way aboard." He held out his hand, and after a moment's hesitation — measuring, always measuring — she took it. His grip was dry and strong and honest as a knot in good rope.

Neither of them mentioned the thing each was still hiding from the other. Neither of them needed to. Two liars had measured their truths against each other all evening, and found, to their mutual surprise, that the weights balanced — almost.

Above the harbor, the first thunder of the coming monsoon muttered on the mountains like a clock clearing its throat.
