## 5. Six Tides

The charts smelled of salt and old iron, the way all documents from the drowned coast did. Nadia Voss had spent eleven years learning to read that smell — to separate the honest rot of paper kept too long in damp air from the deliberate aging of forgeries. She spread the chest's contents across the floor of Lior Dunmore's ruined cabin with the reverence of a priest laying out relics and the suspicion of a woman who had been lied to by better men than this.

He watched her from the doorway, arms crossed, one shoulder against the frame as if he might still bolt. A mountain man out of his element, she thought, though she was beginning to suspect he was never quite in anyone's element but his own.

"Tell me what you're looking at," he said.

"I'm looking at a survey chart of the Vessarine Shelf," she said, "drawn by someone who knew these waters intimately, because they drew the reef line wrong on purpose."

"Wrong on purpose."

"The reef doesn't curve like that. I've mapped it. My father mapped it before me." Her finger traced the inked arc without touching it. Paper that old would take an oil stain from skin alone, and she could not afford to damage what might be the only surviving copy. "But whoever falsified it wanted any reader to believe there was open water where there is actually a lagoon. Hidden behind a false reef line. Reachable only through a passage that drowns twice a day."

Lior crouched beside her, keeping his distance from the documents the way he'd kept his distance from her since the night she'd found him — politely, warily, like two animals circling the same kill. "A lagoon."

"A drowned harbor. The kind of place you hide a ship when you don't want it found." She pulled the second sheet free of its oilcloth sleeve, and her breath caught despite herself, despite eleven years of discipline. It was not a chart at all. It was a tide table — hand-calculated, annotated in a script she recognized before her mind caught up to her eyes.

Her father's script. The narrow downstrokes, the way he crossed his sevens. She had learned her letters copying that hand at a table no bigger than a hatch cover, while the exile settlement's lamp oil rationed itself down to nothing and he'd insisted she practice anyway, because *a people who lose their records lose their claim, little bird, and then we are only refugees*.

She did not tell Lior any of this. She smoothed the sheet flat and let him believe her expression was scholarly interest.

"This is a tidal almanac," she said instead. "For the Vessarine coast. Sixty years out of date, but tides are patient things. They repeat."

"Tides repeat," Lior echoed. He said it the way a man repeats something he intends to argue with later.

"Every 18.6 years, the lunar cycle returns to the same relationship with the equator. The great tides, the spring extremes — they come back around. Whoever calculated this knew the Meridian Star went into that lagoon at a specific low water, and they wrote down which one." She looked up at him. His face was hard to read in the lamplight — weathered, closed, the face of a man who had learned early that expressions were debts waiting to be collected. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? This isn't treasure lore. This is navigation data. Someone intended to come back."

"Someone who sealed it in a chest and threw it in a river," Lior said.

"Or someone who died before they could come back, and whose chest traveled the way everything travels off that coast — downstream, into other people's hands." She let that sit between them. The chest had been stamped with the seal of the Vessarine Salvage Compact, the dead nation's registry mark, and Lior had already learned what that mark cost him: a ransacked cabin, a black market appraiser whispering *burn it*, and now an exile archivist camped in his wreckage eating his rice. He had every reason to hate the sight of it.

He didn't say anything. He sat back on his heels and waited, which she was learning was his way — a man who conserved words like water in a drought.

So she worked.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon and into the blue hour, cross-referencing the almanac against the ephemeris tables she carried in a waterproof roll against her spine, the way other exiles carried knives. The cabin had no table left — the enforcers had smashed it looking for the compass — so she knelt on the floorboards, and the splinters found her knees, and she ignored them.

Outside, the season was turning. She could hear it in the way the wind had begun to arrive from the southeast instead of the northeast, carrying a wetter weight, a different smell — green rot and warm rain, the monsoon's outriders. The fishing boats in the cove below had stopped going past the headland. The gulls were moving inland. Everything with sense was preparing to leave.

Everything with sense, she thought, and bent lower over the tables.

The mathematics came slowly and then all at once, the way tide turns. The almanac's annotations gave her the anchor point: a low water at the Vessarine shelf measured at 0.4 meters above chart datum, occurring at dusk, on a date sixty-one years gone. She converted the epoch. She corrected for the secular drift in the lunar nodes. She ran the harmonic constituents from memory — the M2, the S2, the K1, the numbers her father had drilled into her until she could recite them like prayers, because the exile settlements had no observatories and no funding and their cartographers had to be their own instruments.

The answer assembled itself in her hands like something alive.

Six days.

Six days until the next low water matching the annotation — a true spring low, the lowest of the season, the kind of tide that happened once or twice a year and never apologized for missing you. After that, the sequence degraded. The following lows would leave half a meter more water over the reef passage, then a full meter, and then the monsoon would make landfall and close the question entirely for weeks. Possibly months. Possibly, given what the fleet would do with uninterrupted access, forever.

"Six days," she said aloud, to the room, to herself, to the ghost whose handwriting she was touching.

Lior looked up from the doorway where he'd been mending a torn pack strap with the grim patience of a man restoring order to small things because large things refused to be ordered. "Six days until what?"

"Until the window opens. And closes." She sat back on her heels. Her knees ached. Her eyes burned. She realized she hadn't eaten since morning and couldn't remember whether that was today's morning or yesterday's. "The wreck sits in a lagoon behind a false reef line. The passage over the reef has maybe a fathom of water at the lowest tide of the season — enough for a shallow skiff running it at slack water, nothing more. That lowest tide is six days from now. After that, the monsoon arrives, and the whole coast becomes unsurveyable for weeks. No diving. No salvage. No approach."

"And the fleet?"

That was the question underneath all her questions, and she made herself answer it honestly, because she had decided — sometime in the last day, watching him barter for rice with a fishwife who clearly owed him nothing and got paid anyway — that she would lie to this man as little as the mission allowed. He noticed lies the way he noticed weather.

"The fleet's survey vessel was resupplied at Port Callas three days ago. I have a cousin in the harbormaster's office — a cousin of convenience, but reliable for cargo manifests." She tapped the chart. "They loaded dredging equipment. Chain, cutting gear, compression chambers. That is not a salvage kit, Lior. That is an extraction operation. They intend to raise hull sections, and they intend to do it fast, before the monsoon, exactly the same window we're looking at."

"They know where it is."

"They've always known roughly where it is. What they've lacked is the precise passage through the reef — the lagoon is defensible by geography alone. You can stand off outside it for a year and never find the door." She met his eyes. "Unless someone gives them the door."

His jaw tightened. She watched him do the arithmetic she wanted him to do: the ransacked cabin, the men who didn't knock, the efficiency with which they'd torn his life apart and left without taking the one thing that mattered. They hadn't known what they were looking for. But someone had told them where to look.

"You think they'll find it," he said. "The passage."

"I think they have money, divers, and six days, and that geography keeps secrets badly from people willing to sacrifice equipment to learn them. They can run sounding lines across the entire outer reef in three days. The false curve on this chart will slow them — if they're working from the same falsified surveys the hydrographic office published, and they will be, because my people made sure of that sixty years ago." She permitted herself one flicker of pride for the dead cartographers who had misdrawn a coastline so thoroughly that three governments still used the error. "But a meter of steel dragged across coral finds truth eventually."

"So we have six days, and so do they."

"We have six days, and they have six days, and they have a ship and we have—" she gestured at the gutted cabin, the broken table, the storm drain he'd escaped through, "—your winning personality."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, perhaps, in a man who kept such things lean.

* * *

Night came down over the cove like a lid. Nadia stood in the doorway where the table used to be and listened to the water work the rocks below — a sound she had grown up inside, and been exiled from, and chased across four borders to hear again. It sounded different here than in the settlements inland. Softer. The sea here hadn't learned yet that it was disputed.

Lior built a fire small enough to hide and cooked rice and dried fish without asking whether she wanted any, setting her share on a clean board within reach of her hand. She noted that. Men who fed you without negotiation were either trustworthy or very skilled at seeming so, and she had survived long enough to know the difference usually revealed itself at the worst possible moment.

"The fleet departs tonight," she said.

He looked up from the fire. "You said six days."

"To reach the lagoon. Their home port is nine days' sail from the Vessarine shelf in good weather, and the leading edge of the monsoon will give them bad weather. If they want salvage time inside the window, they sail tonight." She turned her cup in her hands. It was his cup, chipped, ugly, and she held it like something precious because it was the first thing anyone had handed her in weeks without wanting something catalogued in return. "Which means tonight is also our last chance to move ahead of them. Every hour they're at sea is an hour we can be at sea. But if we're still standing on this coast when their masts clear the headland—"

"We're not," Lior said.

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite agreement. It was the voice of a man testing a decision's weight before committing to it, the way he'd test ice. She waited.

"Say it plainly," he said. "What happens if we get there first. All of it. What you actually want."

She had rehearsed versions of this answer for eleven years — for tribunals that never convened, for patrons who wanted maps and not truths, for the council in exile who sent her out with blessings that cost them nothing. None of the rehearsals fit this smoky room and this suspicious man and the tide tables drying under her knees.

"My grandfather helped draft the Vessarine Salvage Compact," she said. "Before the annexation. Before the fleet. The compact says that wrecks of registered vessels belong to the nation of registration, and salvage rights descend through the registered salvor's line — through blood, through documents, through seals like the one on your chest. The nation is gone. The courts that honored the compact are gone. But the compact's insurance syndicates still exist. They still honor claims, because refusing to honor them would cost them more than paying." She looked into the fire. "The Meridian Star was registered under the compact. Its captain stole what he carried out of our waters — records, patrimony, things that were ours before they were valuable. The fleet that sank my village did it to bury the theft. If I reach that wreck and raise the claim beacon under compact law, the site is sealed. Legally. The insurers enforce it, because the insurers would rather enforce an inconvenient law than establish that none of their laws bind anyone. And then the evidence in that hull — the captain's logs, the manifests, the proof of what the fleet did — stops being a story and becomes a case."

"And the village," Lior said quietly. "The one they drowned."

"The village," she said, and her voice did something she didn't authorize it to do — thinned, cracked at the seam. She pressed on before he could decide whether to be gentle, because gentleness right now would drown her. "My mother is a name on a memorial wall in a settlement that isn't on any map. There were forty-one boats in that harbor. I have spent eleven years being told to wait, to document, to build a file strong enough for a court that keeps postponing its own existence. Six days, Lior. For the first time since I was nineteen years old, the mathematics and the justice are pointing the same direction at the same moment. I am not going to miss it."

The fire popped. Down in the cove, the tide was turning, and she heard it — the long exhale of the ebb, the sound her father had called the sea counting its change.

Lior fed a stick to the flames. When he spoke, it was slowly, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ground.

"I've spent ten years being a man the ports blacklist and the rivers won't vouch for. I know what it is to have the law exist purely to keep you moving." He looked at her across the fire, and for the first time since she'd found him, he wasn't measuring her. He was just looking. "Six days. Then we need a boat."

Relief hit her like warm water, sudden and total, and she hated how visible it must have been, and let it be visible anyway. "I know where there's a skiff."

"Of course you do."

"It belongs to a man who owes me a favor and fears me slightly. The combination is reliable."

"And after?" he asked. "After the beacon, after the monsoon. What does a person do with a country that comes back?"

Nadia looked at the tide tables spread across the floor, at her father's handwriting holding its shape after sixty years in the dark, patient as water, certain as tide. Somewhere south, a fleet was coiling its mooring lines in the dark, and somewhere east, a lagoon waited behind a lie drawn in ink, and somewhere inside her, a girl who had copied letters by lamplight was finally, finally being handed the instrument she'd been trained to become.

"After," she said, "we make them wish they'd let sleeping nations lie."

She began rolling the charts. Outside, far down the coast, she imagined she could see it — a smudge of lights rounding the southern headland, masts against the last grey band of sea. The fleet, putting out to sea tonight, racing the same six tides.

Six tides. She counted them the way her father had taught her to count everything: backward from loss, forward toward claim.

The first one started now.
