## 6. Stowaway Water

The freighter was called the *Cormorant's Mercy*, which Lior took as a bad joke the sea had already made once and would make again. She sat low in the harbor at Greywater Quay, rust bleeding down her flanks like old tears, her deck crusted with salt and fish scale and the particular grime of a ship that had stopped pretending to be anything but a workhorse. She was loading for the southern run — dried cod, timber, drums of pitch — and she would make one stop before the open water: customs inspection at Port Calloway.

Which meant they could not be on deck when she reached it.

"You're certain about this captain," Nadia said. She stood beside him in the shadow of a net-drying shed, hood up, hands buried in the sleeves of a coat two sizes too large. She had bought it that morning from a ragman with coin she claimed came from an archivist's stipend paid in a currency that no longer existed, spent in a port where nobody looked too closely. "You've never met him."

"I've met his kind." Lior kept his eyes on the gangway, on the stevedores trundling barrels up into the hold. "A man who runs a coastal freighter is a man who has learned that rules are weather. You don't argue with them. You wait them out."

"And if he decides to sell us to the fleet instead?"

"Then we're in the water, and I'm a better swimmer than either of us would like."

She didn't laugh. He was beginning to understand that Nadia Voss rationed laughter the way a drowning man rations breath — not out of stinginess, but because she'd learned what happened when you spent it too freely. In the three days since she'd found him, since she'd stood in the doorway of his burned-out cabin with rain running off her chin and said *I know what that compass is*, he had catalogued her the way he catalogued weather systems: pressure dropping, front building, no telling yet whether it would break as rain or something worse.

He checked the harbor clock. Two hours to slack tide, when the *Mercy* would take on her last water and cast off. Two hours to become invisible in a town where invisibility had been stripped from him like bark from a birch.

Because that was the thing about a blacklist. It wasn't a wall. It was a smell. Somewhere in the ledgers of the coastal authority there was a page with his name on it, and every harbormaster, every customs clerk, every bored young officer with a clipboard and a quota of inspections to fill had been taught to catch that smell. *Lior Dunmore. Guide, unlicensed. Papers irregular.* The word *irregular* had followed him for eleven years, through six ports and two provinces, and it meant exactly nothing and absolutely everything: it meant that any man with a stamp could ruin his day, his season, his life, and sleep soundly after.

"Walk," he said quietly. "Don't look at the ship. Look at the fish market like you're deciding what to buy."

They moved along the quay in the gray drizzle that passed for fair weather here, past crates of herring gone silver-eyed and stiff, past a woman gutting mackerel with a speed that made the knife look like part of her hand. Nadia played her role well — too well, maybe, pausing over the stalls with a scholar's genuine curiosity, and Lior had to touch her elbow once, lightly, to remind her that curiosity was a luxury for people whose faces weren't known.

"The boarding ladder is aft," he murmured. "Starboard side, below the fenders. There's a gap between the hull and the pilings — maybe eighteen inches of black water. We go in under the quay, swim the gap, and climb while the stevedores are on their meal break. Nobody watches a ship during the meal break. That's the one law of harbors that never fails."

"And the hold?"

"There's a false bulkhead behind the pitch drums. Old smuggler's trick — the *Mercy* has been carrying more than cod for years, or she wouldn't survive. We'll have air, we'll have darkness, and we'll have until Port Calloway."

Nadia looked at him then, really looked, the way she did when she was weighing a chart against the coastline it claimed to describe. "You've done this before."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it. But the silence answered for him, and he felt the old shame rise in his throat like bile — the shame of being a man who knew the gaps in things, who had learned ports from underneath, who could tell you the taste of bilgewater in four different harbors. His father had been a licensed guide with a framed certificate and a government pin. Lior had inherited the mountains and none of the paper, and somewhere between the two facts he had become a man who moved through the world sideways, through doors left unlatched, through the blind spots of other people's attention.

*The wilderness has never handed me anything for free,* he thought, watching the gulls wheel and scream over the fish waste. *And neither has civilization.*

* * *

The gap between the hull and the pilings was colder than he'd promised. The harbor water of Greywater Quay carried the flavor of everything the town wanted forgotten — fish blood, tar, sewage, the sweet rot of something organic and unnameable — and it closed over him like a hand. He went under the fender line with a lungful of air, felt the barnacled hull scrape his shoulder, and surfaced in the slot of darkness between ship and shore, breathing as quietly as a man can breathe when his whole body wants to gasp.

Above him, Nadia dropped from the quay edge without a splash. She swam the eight feet to the ladder with efficient, angry strokes, hauling herself up onto the lowest rung, water streaming off her. She didn't complain. He noted that, filed it away. People who didn't complain in cold water were either very tough or very frightened, and he suspected she was both.

The aft door stood ajar — the cook's shortcut to the dock, propped with a brick, exactly as the harbor gossip had promised. Inside, the *Mercy* smelled of diesel and boiled cabbage and decades of cigarettes. Lior moved down the companionway with the particular softness of a man who had spent his life not startling animals, past the galley where a radio played something tinny and mournful, and found the cargo hold by counting doors.

The pitch drums were stacked three high amidships, their stenciled labels facing outward, and behind them — yes — the gap. A false bulkhead, plywood painted to match the steel, held shut with a hook-and-eye that some long-ago smuggler had installed with more optimism than skill. Lior lifted the eye free of the hook and swung the panel inward.

Darkness. Straw. The ghost-smell of whatever the *Mercy* had carried last season — spice, maybe, or people. He tried not to think about that last possibility and failed.

"In," he whispered. "Quiet as the dead. If anyone comes aboard before we sail, we're rats."

"Rats don't carry compasses that point at sunken nations," Nadia murmured, but she went in, folding herself down against the inner hull, and Lior followed, drawing the panel shut behind them until only a thread of light remained.

They waited. Waiting was its own craft, and Lior was a master of it. He had waited out storms in rock shelters, waited out avalanches in snow caves, waited out a blizzard once for thirty-one hours listening to his own heartbeat and deciding, hour by hour, that it was worth continuing. So he settled into the straw, slowed his breath, and let the ship talk to him. The creak of the hull working against its moorings. The thud of the last barrels going aboard overhead. Voices — the mate shouting numbers, the cook complaining about the cabbage, and once, closer, heavy boots passing within arm's reach of the false bulkhead, pausing—

—and moving on.

Beside him in the dark, he heard Nadia exhale, slow and controlled, and realized she had been holding her breath for the entire length of the boots' passage. Without thinking, without deciding to, he found her hand in the dark and pressed it once — a guide's signal, the same one he used with clients on exposed ledges: *I'm here. Keep moving.* Her fingers were cold and they gripped back harder than the signal required, and neither of them let go for a long time after.

* * *

The *Mercy* cast off at slack tide with a shudder that ran through the whole hull like a animal shaking off rain, and then there was the motion — the long, rolling sway of open water that turned the darkness around them into something almost gentle. Through the thread of light at the bulkhead's edge, Lior watched the harbor slide away, the breakwater lighthouse, the gray hills beyond, and felt the peculiar grief of every departure: even leaving places that had tried to destroy you, you left something.

They ate hard bread and dried apples from Nadia's pack, chewing slowly because noise traveled in a hull the way rumor travels in a small town. They spoke in whispers, heads close, and the closeness was necessity and was also not entirely necessity, and Lior was honest enough with himself to notice that.

"Calloway at dawn," he said. "Customs launch will come alongside. Six men, maybe eight, with a dog if the inspector's ambitious. They'll walk every deck and poke every crate, because Calloway's inspectors get promoted by what they find, not by what they miss."

"So we stay here."

"We stay here and we pray the dog is lazy." He turned the compass case over in his hands — he hadn't let it leave his body since the cabin, sleeping with it strapped against his ribs like a second heart. In the dark he couldn't see the needle, but he could feel it, or imagined he could, that wrongness tugging at the edge of his awareness. Pointing south-southeast, always, toward drowned water and a nation that existed now only in archives and exiles. "There's a problem, though. If they find us, I can't talk my way out. My name is in their book. One fingerprint, one glance at my face, and I'm in a Calloway cell waiting for whoever pays the most to collect me."

"And me?"

"You they might release. You've done nothing they know about."

"Not yet," Nadia said, and there was something in her voice that made him glad of the darkness, because he didn't want to examine it by daylight.

* * *

Dawn came to the hold as a slow brightening of the thread of light, gray turning to pearl. With it came the engines slowing, the rattle of anchor chain, and the sound Lior had been dreading: a launch bumping against the hull, voices calling up, the clang of the boarding ladder taking weight.

Boots on deck. Many boots. And beneath them, claws on planking — quick, businesslike, the sound of a dog doing a job it loved.

Lior pressed himself flat into the straw and pulled Nadia down beside him. Through the crack he watched shadows move across the hold's mouth, heard the inspectors working forward to aft with the methodical boredom of men who had done this four hundred times. A crate lid pried. A barrel tapped. The dog snuffling along the deck plates, closer, closer—

It stopped at the false bulkhead.

Lior's heart went quiet and cold, the way it did above the treeline when the weather turned. He could hear the animal breathing, hear its nails clicking as it circled, and then a voice — young, tired, ambitious — saying, "What's behind this stack?"

"Pitch drums," said the mate, whose name Lior still didn't know and whose loyalty he had bought with nothing but a nod through a galley door three weeks ago via a fisherman who owed him. "Sealed at the chandlery. You want to crack forty drums of pitch on my deck, be my guest, but your inspector signs for the cleanup."

Silence. The dog whined. A hand — Lior saw the shadow of it — touched the edge of the false panel, the plywood he and Nadia had pulled shut with a hook-and-eye designed to fool no one who actually looked.

And then the ship's bell rang twice, and another voice called from the deck above — the captain, gravel-voiced, annoyed: "Inspector! You want to inspect, come sign my manifest first. I've got a tide and a schedule and neither one waits for your paperwork."

The hand withdrew. The boots moved away. The dog went with them, reluctantly, dragging its leash, and Lior lay in the straw listening to the footsteps fade and feeling the sweat cool on his neck, and thought: *that was the closest call, that was the one, that was—*

The panel opened.

Not the inspectors. Not the dog. The mate — a squat man with a face like a fist and eyes that had seen everything the coast could offer — stood in the gap with a lantern, looking down at the two of them tangled in straw like something the tide had deposited.

For a long moment nobody moved. Then the mate said, "Captain says you're to stay put till we clear Calloway roads. Also says—" he paused, and something that was almost humor crossed his face like a cloud shadow, "—says next time, use the real latch. The hook-and-eye's been broken since before you were born. Any inspector worth his stamp knows it."

He dropped a bundle into the straw — bread, a jug of water, a stub of candle — and closed the panel.

In the lantern-less dark afterward, Nadia's shoulder was against his, and he could feel her heartbeat through the layers of wet wool, fast still, gradually slowing. Neither of them spoke for a while. When she finally did, her whisper was rough.

"You knew the latch was broken."

"I guessed. Ships like this don't keep secrets; they keep habits. I just didn't know which habit." He leaned his head back against the hull. "Could have gone worse."

"Could have gone much worse."

"Yes." And then, because the fear had cracked something open in him and honesty was leaking out through the fracture: "Eleven years I've been doing this. Slipping past the stamps and the ledgers. Eleven years of being a smell people are trained to catch. You get used to it alone. You get good at alone." He turned his head; he could just make out her profile, the sharp line of her jaw against the gray light. "This is the first time it hasn't been alone. I don't know what to do with that."

Nadia was quiet a moment. Then she said, "My exile council has a saying. *No one crosses open water in a single boat.*" Her hand found his again in the dark, deliberate this time, nothing accidental in it. "It means the same thing yours means. You just say it worse."

Despite everything — the dog, the boots, the broken latch, the six days burning down toward monsoon — Lior laughed, quietly, into the dark of the hold, and felt the sound do something to the shame that eleven years hadn't.

Above them, the *Cormorant's Mercy* gathered way and turned her bow south, toward the open sea and the drowned nation waiting at the end of a compass needle.

And below decks, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that froze the laugh in Lior's throat: the mate's boots returning, stopping at the bulkhead, and a low voice saying, "Change of plan. There's a fleet tender riding off our stern quarter, and she's been following us since Calloway roads."
