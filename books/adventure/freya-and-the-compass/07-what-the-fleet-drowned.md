## 7. What the Fleet Drowned

The tavern was called the Drowned Rat, which Nadia supposed was either honesty or a joke so old it had fossilized into architecture. It sat at the crooked end of Port Calloway's fish quay, where the boards of the walkway had been replaced so many times that no two planks matched, and the whole structure seemed to be held together by tar, habit, and the stubbornness of men who refused to drink anywhere else.

She and Lior came in out of the rain just after dark. The monsoon was still six days south of them, but its outriders had arrived early — squalls that came off the water in gray sheets, hammered the tin roofs for twenty minutes, and moved on, leaving everything smelling of salt and ozone and wet rope. Inside, the air was a different weather entirely: pipe smoke, fry oil, spilled beer gone sour in the seams of the floorboards, and beneath it all the low animal warmth of forty bodies packed against the sea's cold.

Lior found them a table in the corner with his back to the wall — he always took the seat with his back to the wall, she'd noticed, the way a man does who has spent years being looked for — and went to the bar while she kept her hood up and her eyes moving. The compass rode against her ribs inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her own body heat. She had stopped thinking of it as an object somewhere over the last two days. It was closer to a heartbeat now, something she carried because stopping would mean dying.

The man they'd come to find sat alone at the end of the bar, and he was exactly what the freighter's cook had described: big through the shoulders even now, with hands like cured leather and a white beard trimmed short, drinking something amber at the pace of a man who had made peace with however many nights remained to him. His name, the cook said, was Anselm Roke. Twenty-two years on salvage decks. Eleven of them, before he quit, on the *Corvane* — flagship of the Verrin Salvage Fleet.

Nadia watched Lior set down two cups and say something low to him. She saw the old man's face do a complicated thing — suspicion first, then a flicker of recognition at whatever Lior said next, then something older and sadder settling over him like a tarp. He picked up his glass, considered it, and jerked his chin toward the empty table beside his.

They moved. Nadia sat across from him and put back her hood, and she watched him look at her face — really look, the way men of the coast looked at women from the drowned archipelago, searching for the country in her cheekbones, in the shape of her eyes, in the vowels that would come out when she spoke.

"You're one of theirs," Roke said. Not a question. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"I'm Voss," she said. "Of Sarnholt. On the Isle of Merren."

The glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a long moment the tavern noise seemed to organize itself around that silence — the dice game by the stove, the argument about fuel prices, the rain starting up again against the shutters — all of it continuing, indifferent, while between the three of them the temperature dropped.

"Voss," Roke repeated. He set the glass down without drinking. "There was a Voss on the *Meridian Star*'s claim papers. Old man Voss. Cartographer."

"My father."

The old deckhand closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet, and he didn't seem embarrassed about it. "I've been waiting eleven years for someone to sit across from me and say that name," he said. "I'd begun to think nobody would ever come."

* * *

He talked the way old sailors talk — in circles at first, testing the currents, making sure of them before committing his whole weight. He wanted to know how Lior had found the chest, and Lior told him, plainly, about the flooded river and the brass seal, and Roke listened with his head cocked like a bird hearing a sound underground. He wanted to know if anyone else knew, and Lior said the fleet's enforcers had already torn apart his cabin looking for it, and Roke grunted at that as if it confirmed some private arithmetic.

Then he asked Nadia to tell him about Sarnholt, and she understood that this was the toll. He would not give them anything until he knew what she was carrying.

So she told him. About the village on the leeward side of Merren, where the houses were built on stone pilings because the tide owned the streets twice a day and everyone simply planned around it, the way you plan around a relative with moods. About the drying racks that smelled of kelp and herring in autumn. About the chart house on the point, built by her grandfather, where three generations of Vosses had drawn the waters of the archipelago — the reefs, the deep channels, the lagoon behind Serpent's Tooth where the old nation's ships had sheltered in the war nobody outside remembered anymore. Her father had taught her to draw depth soundings before she could write her own name. *The sea keeps records,* he used to say, tapping the paper. *We're only copying.*

"The fleet came in the spring," Roke said, when she finished. "Eleven years ago this spring." He turned his glass slowly on the table, around and around, like a man steering something small and doomed. "You have to understand what the *Meridian Star* was. Not a wreck — a vault. The old regime's reserve fleet went down in that lagoon in the last week of the war, scuttled rather than surrendered, and the *Star* was the registry ship. Manifests, bullion ledgers, the claim archives. Everything the old nation owned on paper went down with her, and paper, under water, in cold lagoon silt, keeps better than you'd think."

"And your fleet found her," Nadia said.

"We found the approach." He laughed, but there was nothing in it. "That was the problem. The lagoon opens through a channel that's only passable at slack water, and the channel threads between reefs that will open a hull like a letter. The charts that showed the safe line were —" he nodded at her — "yours. Your family's. The Voss survey. Every working copy was destroyed when the regime fell; the originals were supposed to have gone down with the *Star*. The fleet spent two seasons losing boats in that reef before anyone admitted they couldn't buy their way in. So they went to get the people who could read the water."

Nadia's hands had gone still in her lap. She was aware of Lior beside her, very quiet, watching her the way you watch ice on a river in spring.

"They sent agents to Merren," Roke said. "Quiet ones, first. They offered the council money for the survey rights. The council refused — not out of loyalty to a dead country, mind, but because selling the charts meant selling the lagoon, and the lagoon was where their grandfathers were buried in their boats. Then the quiet ones left, and the loud ones came." He drank, finally, a long swallow. "There was a storm forecast that week. A bad one. The fleet's meteorologist swore it would swing north. The village got no warning worth the name — a fishing boat or two went out with word, hours late, hours. And the seawall at Sarnholt had been cut, Mrs. Voss. Not broken. Cut. We found the marks afterward. Saw cuts, below the waterline, where no storm surge reaches until it's already too late."

The tavern noise receded. Nadia heard her own pulse instead, slow and enormous, like surf heard from far inland.

"How do you know?" she asked. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "How do you know what you found afterward? You were crew."

"Because I helped look." Roke's eyes were steady on hers, and there was no comfort in them, only the terrible honesty of a man who had run out of ways to hide from himself. "After the water went down we went in with the recovery gear. Forty-one dead, they counted. And among the wreckage of the chart house — the original surveys, sealed in lead tubes, floating in the reeds. Dry. Someone had taken them out ahead of the flood and stashed them high, planning to come back, and the flood had other plans. The fleet's officers collected those tubes themselves. I stood on the deck of the *Corvane* and watched Captain Illane Vay lock them in the strongroom, and I heard her tell her mate that the village was a tragedy of weather, and I believed her, because believing her was easier than the alternative, and I was twenty-six years old and paid by the season." He turned the glass again. "It took me five more years and two more jobs like that one to stop believing her. By then the *Meridian Star* was open. They'd gotten in through the channel using your father's charts, and they'd stripped her — bullion, ledgers, the claim archive, all of it — and burned the paper records in the Corvane's boiler to keep them off any future register. And every year since, the Verrin Fleet has grown bigger and cleaner and more respectable, and no one has ever once said the word *Sarnholt* in a courtroom, because there is no courtroom, because the country that village belonged to doesn't exist, and a crime needs a victim with standing, and the drowned can't file papers."

Nadia became aware that her cheeks were wet. She didn't wipe them. She let the old man see it, because hiding it felt like doing the fleet's work for them.

"The compass," she said. "My father's compass. It was on the *Star*?"

"It was in the strongroom manifest. A surveyor's bearing-compass, silver-cased, marked with the Voss arms." Roke almost smiled. "Your father carried it aboard the *Star* himself, the last month of the war, when he delivered the final charts. The story among the old crew was that the needle didn't point north. That it pointed home. Superstition, we thought." He looked at her coat, at the shape beneath it, and she didn't deny it. "And it went down with the registry chest when the fleet scuttled the *Star*'s aft compartment to hide the true extent of what they'd taken. Insurance fraud on top of everything else. They reported recovering a fraction of the manifest and wrote the rest off as unrecoverable." He shook his head slowly. "And now a river coughs up a chest with the old seal, and the fleet tears a guide's cabin apart looking for what's inside it, and a Voss walks into the Drowned Rat asking after the *Corvane*. Eleven years, and the ledger starts balancing itself."

* * *

Later — much later, when Roke had gone up to his rented room above the chandlery with Lior's coin and Lior's promise that no name of his would surface in any of it — Nadia stood at the tavern's back door under the dripping eave, looking out at the harbor.

The Verrin Fleet rode at anchor in the roadstead, three ships of them, resupplying before the monsoon window closed. Their running lights doubled themselves in the black water, neat and patient and obscene. Somewhere on the largest of them, a man named Vay's successor — or perhaps Vay herself, grown old and respectable — was counting days to slack water over charts her officers had stolen from a drowned chart house.

Lior came to stand beside her. He didn't say anything for a while. That was one of the things she had decided to trust about him: he didn't fill silence the way most men did, with noise mistaken for help.

"Forty-one," she said finally.

"I know."

"No. You know a number. I know the names." She pressed her palm flat against the doorframe, against the wet wood, as if she could take a sounding of it. "Old Maret who taught me knots. The Ferro twins, who were nine. My father's apprentice, Tam, who was going to take over the chart house." Her voice did not break. She had spent eleven years making sure of that, and she would not spend it here. "Roke thinks the fleet will run the channel within the week, before the monsoon. He says they've hired pilots out of Calloway who don't ask questions. He says the *Corvane* carries a dredge rig now — they'll widen the channel if they have to, blast the reef, whatever it takes to get the last of the strongroom cargo before the insurers' deadline lapses."

"That's the wreck's grave they'd be blasting."

"That's my family's grave they'd be blasting." She turned to look at him, and the harbor lights caught the wet on her face and made it shine. "Two days ago this was about a claim, Lior. Papers. Rights. A chest with a seal on it. I told myself that's what it was, because that was a thing I could carry." She looked back out at the anchored lights. "It isn't that anymore. Whatever happens in that lagoon, someone is going to answer for Sarnholt. Not in a courtroom — there's no courtroom, he's right about that. But the sea keeps records. My father taught me that. And I am going to make sure the record gets read."

Lior was quiet a moment longer. Then he said, in the flat, practical voice of a man who had already moved past the question of whether to the question of how: "Roke mentioned the fleet sails on the morning tide. If we're going to beat them through the reef passage, we need a boat tonight — and the harbor master knows my face."

Nadia pushed off the doorframe. Under her coat, against her ribs, the compass lay still and certain, pointing not north but home.

"Then we'd better steal a fast one," she said.
