## 8. The First Tide

The harbor at Port Calloway slept like something drugged.

Lior had watched it for two hours from the shadow of a net-drying shed, counting the rhythms of the place the way he'd count the rhythms of any wild thing before approaching it. The tide was falling toward its low-water pause — slack water, the hour when the sea forgot which way it was going and everything moved slow and stupid. Fishermen's boats knocked gently against their moorings. A customs launch sat dark at the government pier, its crew gone ashore to drink or sleep or both. And at the end of the public slip, tied with a bowline that even in the bad light Lior could see was tied by someone who didn't respect knots, lay a skiff.

She was maybe five meters of weathered fiberglass, an outboard canted up on her stern, a fuel tank that sloshed when the wind pushed her hull. Working boat. Honest boat. The kind of boat that had carried a hundred quiet mornings out past the breakwater and brought them home again.

"She'll do," Nadia said beside him.

Lior didn't answer right away. He was listening to the harbor breathe. Somewhere behind them, up the hill, a dog had started barking twenty minutes ago and stopped, and the silence it left behind felt like held breath. The air smelled of diesel and brine and the green rot of the mangrove flats across the bay, and underneath all of it, faint as a rumor, the mineral smell of a storm still three days out but already leaning on the horizon like a drunk against a doorframe.

"Slack water lasts forty minutes," he said finally. "Maybe fifty if the moon's feeling generous. After that the channel starts running and it runs hard."

"Then we'd better not be sentimental about the knot." Nadia was already moving, low along the pilings, and Lior followed because following her had become, somewhere over the last two days, a habit he hadn't decided to form.

They reached the skiff without a sound worth naming. He cut the bowline with his knife — no sense leaving evidence of a struggle where there hadn't been one — and lowered the painter into the water so the hull came off the piling soft as a hand sliding free of a glove. Nadia dropped into the stern. Lior took the tiller side, found the fuel line, squeezed the bulb until it firmed, and said a small private word to the engine, the way his mother used to say words to the river before they launched their leaky punt into the flood current. Not a prayer. An introduction. *This is us. Be reasonable.*

The outboard caught on the third pull, coughed, then settled into a low burble that sounded enormous in the sleeping harbor. Lior eased the throttle forward and the skiff slid out past the moored boats, past the dark customs launch, past the breakwater's last black shoulder of stone, and into open water.

Behind them, Port Calloway went on sleeping. Ahead of them, the reef passage waited, a seam of navigable water threading through coral that had been shredding hulls since before anyone drew a chart of this coast.

* * *

Nadia spread the chart across her knees and weighted its corners with the compass case, a spare carabiner, and one bare foot. In the red glow of the tiny shielded lamp she'd insisted on buying with the last of their shared money, the reef looked like a wound — white coral heads scattered through blue water like bone fragments, and between them, drawn in faded ink by some hydrographer dead fifty years, a single dashed line marked *Passage — 1.8m at MLWS — local knowledge advised.*

"Local knowledge," Lior read aloud. "That's cartographer for *we have no idea.*"

"That's cartographer for *the man who knew died before he could tell us,*" Nadia said. "There's a difference. Barely." She traced the dashed line with one finger. "The passage runs northeast for about four hundred meters, then dog-legs left around the outer head. At slack water there should be a meter and a half under the keel at the shallowest point. Our keel draws—" she glanced at him.

"Half a meter, loaded light. We're loaded light."

"Then we have a margin of one meter." She said it flatly, the way she said most numbers, as though numbers were neutral territory. But he'd learned to hear what lived under her flatness. One meter of margin meant every swell, every momentary set of the current, every error of steering, ate directly into the space between floating and becoming part of the reef.

"And after slack?"

"After slack the tide turns and the whole lagoon inside starts draining through that gap. Four hours of ebb through a channel that narrow—" She made a small gesture with her hand, water pouring over an invisible sill. "It won't be rapids. It'll be worse than rapids. Rapids are honest. This will be confused water, standing waves, eddies that grab you sideways. Nobody runs it on the ebb unless they're trying to die memorably."

"Then we go now, or we don't go."

"We go now."

He opened the throttle.

The skiff rose onto plane and the night changed. It always did — he'd felt it a thousand times guiding clients down rivers, the moment when a boat stops displacing water and starts riding it, when the world narrows to the strip of illuminated surface ahead and the noise becomes a wall that keeps out everything except the next ten seconds. Spray came off the bow in cold needles. The wind tore at his collar. Behind him he heard Nadia laugh once, short and startled out of her, and he thought: *there you are.* Two days of charts and silences and careful truths measured out in teaspoons, and it took thirty knots of salt wind to shake anything loose.

The reef announced itself before they saw it. First as a change in the swell — the long ocean rollers beginning to stand up and sharpen as the bottom reached for them. Then as sound: a low, continuous grinding hush ahead, the voice of surf working coral, patient as erosion. Then, as his eyes adjusted, as shapes: pale heads of coral showing black against the slightly-less-black water, foam ghosts crawling over them, and beyond, the vast breathing darkness of the open sea.

Lior killed the throttle to half and let the skiff settle back onto the water. From here on, speed was just another way of dying.

"There," Nadia said, pointing left of the loudest surf. "The gap. See the color?"

He saw it. Between two lines of breaking white the water lay smooth and very deep-looking, a corridor of glass maybe thirty meters wide, running back into the reef like a road into a forest. The moonless dark made it look almost gentle.

Almost.

"Steer for the left edge," he murmured, more to himself than to her. "Not center. Center's where the current wants you, and I want to argue with it, not agree."

"You want to *argue* with the ocean."

"I want to keep my options open. Same thing."

He lined up the approach, watching the swell sets the way he'd watch rapids from the bank — counting the big ones, finding the pattern, learning the sea's sentence structure. Seven swells, then a larger one. Seven, then larger. The ocean repeating itself, patient, telegraphic. Every wilderness spoke in patterns; the trick was believing the pattern right up until the moment it lied.

"Now," he said, and gave it throttle.

* * *

The passage took them in like a throat.

Walls of coral rose on either side — not walls exactly, but the suggestion of them, pale shelves just under the surface where the swell stood up and broke in slow motion, curtains of lace-white collapsing inward. The skiff threaded between them at half-throttle, engine pitched low, and Lior steered by a grammar older than charts: the color of the water, the lean of the foam, the way the current laid a thin V of ripple across the surface pointing where it wanted them dead.

"Left," Nadia said. "Chart says the dog-leg comes up fast."

"I see it." He did — a pale elbow of reef swinging out of the dark, the channel bending hard around it, and the water in the bend moving wrong, moving *backward*, an eddy spinning off the corner like a fist closing. He swung wide to starboard to avoid the pull, and the skiff's hull kissed the edge of the standing wave and shuddered, and for one full second the stern stepped sideways and the whole night tilted—

—and then the hull bit again, and they were through, and the channel straightened and widened and the surf fell astern into a mutter.

Nadia exhaled. Lior realized he'd been holding his own breath and let it go slowly, professionally, as though he'd never doubted.

"One meter of margin," he said.

"Spent wisely."

Ahead the water opened into the broad dark lagoon-side shallows, and beyond them — he could feel it more than see it, a change in the quality of the distance — the wreck's lagoon, the Meridian Star, the reason for all of it, waiting behind another mile of reef and one more gate of coral.

That was when the seventh swell lied.

* * *

He never saw it properly. That was the thing he would remember afterward, telling it later in his head on ridgelines and riverbanks: not the wave itself but the *absence* it left, a hole in the pattern. The sets had been seven-and-large, seven-and-large, metronome-steady, and he had built his whole line on that rhythm, timing the shallowest shelf for the trough between sets. But out beyond the outer reef, where the deep water stacked against the falling tide, something had crossed — a groundswell from a storm a thousand kilometers south, arriving unscheduled, unannounced, folding itself into the local swell the way a stranger folds into a crowd.

The water ahead of them simply rose.

No warning curl, no hiss of shoaling. Just the entire surface lifting, a single vast shoulder of water assembling itself out of the dark, taller than the masthead light of any fishing boat, moving with the unhurried authority of geology. Lior's body understood it before his mind did — the same cold instant of recognition he'd felt twice in his life, once above an avalanche slope and once in a river gorge when the water level jumped a meter in a breath.

"Brace—"

The word barely made it out. He wrenched the bow square into the face of it, because you take a wave like that head-on or you roll, those were the only two endings ever written for that story, and then the world became vertical.

The skiff climbed. The engine screamed as the propeller cavitated, biting air instead of water, and the bow pointed at nothing — at stars, at the wet black sky — and Lior threw his weight forward over the console, both hands welded to the tiller, riding the climb the way he'd once ridden a flooded river's standing wave, reading the hull's balance through his palms, keeping the nose down, *down*, because a skiff that goes over backward at the crest dies upside down in a coral garden.

At the top, for one suspended heartbeat, they hung. He saw the far side of the passage lit silver by the broken wave's wash, saw the reef heads grinning, saw Nadia's hand locked around the chart table and her eyes fixed on him — not on the wave, on *him*, waiting to see what he would do, trusting him to do it, and something in his chest cracked open like ice going out in spring.

Then the drop.

They fell down the wave's back face and the hull slammed flat and the impact went through his spine like a struck bell, and green water came aboard over the bow — a quarter ton of it, maybe more, the deck awash, boots suddenly weightless in swirling seawater, the engine choking — and Lior did the only things that mattered, in the only order they mattered: throttle up so the prop kept grip, bow down so the water ran aft, and *pump*, bail, move, because a skiff swamped in a reef passage is a coffin with good views.

"Bail!" he roared, and Nadia was already moving, snatching the plastic bailer from under the thwart, throwing water over the side in great practiced arcs — and he registered, in the animal back room of his mind where guides keep their inventories, that she bailed like someone who'd done it before, who'd grown up around boats, around *this* coast, and filed the fact away with all the other facts she fed him a spoonful at a time.

The engine coughed. Caught. Coughed again — water in the fuel, or the plugs — and the skiff wallowed, sluggish, sitting low, the second set of swells marching in behind them with the patience of creditors.

"Come *on*," Lior said to it, to the machine, to the night, in the tone he used on frightened clients and stubborn rivers, and the outboard cleared its throat and fired, and the propeller bit, and the skiff answered.

They planed out of the danger zone trailing water and adrenaline, the bilge gradually winning its argument with the sea, until the deck was only wet instead of drowning and the reef heads fell away astern and the water beneath them turned from hostile gray-green to the deep, calm, ink-black of sheltered lagoon.

Lior throttled down. His hands were shaking now — they always shook *after*, never during, which he'd long ago decided was the body's idea of a joke — and his heart was going like a drumline, and he discovered, to his complete lack of surprise and considerable surprise simultaneously, that he was grinning.

He couldn't remember the last time he'd grinned.

* * *

They beached the skiff on a crescent of shell sand inside the inner lagoon, hauling her well above the tideline among the shadows of wind-bent casuarina trees. The land here was a cay barely wider than the beach, a rib of coral and sand and scrub that existed at the ocean's sufferance. No lights anywhere. No people. Just the sound of surf on the outer reef, softened now to a lullaby, and overhead a spill of stars the port cities had forgotten how to make.

Nadia sat down abruptly in the sand and started laughing — quietly, helplessly, both hands pressed over her mouth like the laughter might escape and be heard by someone, though there was no one within a day's sail.

"What?" Lior said.

"You argued with the ocean." She tipped her head back to look up at him, sand in her hair, salt on her face, and in the starlight he could see the shape of the woman she must have been before the exile, before the archives, before whatever grief had taught her to measure out truth in teaspoons. "And it argued back. And you *won*. You steered us up the face of a wave the size of a building like it was a Tuesday."

"It nearly wasn't a Tuesday."

"It was magnificent." She said it simply, without performance, and then seemed to hear herself and looked away, out at the black lagoon, and the shutters came back down — but slower than before. Less completely. "I haven't been on open water since they expelled us. Eleven years. I forgot what it—" She stopped. Started again, choosing. "Thank you. For the steering."

"For the record, the ocean cheated. That swell wasn't in the forecast."

"The ocean has never once read a forecast. That's rather the point of you, isn't it? Mountain people and sea people — we both love something that refuses to promise us anything." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "My father used to say the reef doesn't test you. It just is. The testing is a courtesy you perform for yourself."

Lior lowered himself into the sand beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and looked out at the water. Somewhere out there, past the far rim of the lagoon, the Meridian Star lay drowned in her own private harbor, and somewhere beyond *that*, the salvage fleet was burning fuel through the night to arrive first. They had beaten the fleet through the passage. They had a lead measured in tides, not days. Three tides, maybe, before the monsoon closed the book on all of it.

For once — and the thought arrived with the strange, ringing clarity of the wave's crest, and stayed — the wilderness was testing him on his own terms. Not the terms of a blacklist, of papers he didn't have, of ports that closed and names that opened doors only outward. Here there was no bureaucracy, no seal, no claim-jumpers with lawyers. There was just water and skill and nerve, the oldest contract there was, and tonight he had honored his side of it.

He reached into his jacket for the compass — habit now, checking it the way other men checked watches — and unfolded it on his knee.

The needle was swinging. Slow, drunken arcs, wandering through the dark like something searching a house it used to live in. He frowned. On the crossing it had pointed true enough toward the lagoon, wavering with the boat's motion but holding its bearing. Now, ashore, beached, stable, it circled.

"Nadia."

She looked. Watched the needle wander. And then, absently, the way you reach to steady a flickering lamp, she put her hand over the compass — just her palm resting lightly on the case, fingers curled over the bezel.

The needle stopped.

Stopped dead, mid-swing, and settled — not north, not any north Lior had ever sailed by — but steady, unwavering, fixed on a bearing that ran straight out across the black lagoon toward the drowned heart of the reef. Toward the Meridian Star. It pointed the way a plumb line hangs. The way a dog points. The way a thing points when it has finally come home.

Lior looked at the compass. At her hand on it. At her face, which had gone very still, very carefully blank, in the particular way she went blank whenever a truth got too large for the teaspoon she'd allotted it.

"Nadia," he said quietly. "What else haven't you told me?"
