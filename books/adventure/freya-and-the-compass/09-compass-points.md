## 9. Compass Points

The island had no name on any chart Lior had ever bought, which was exactly why he'd chosen it. A crescent of coral sand and casuarina trees tucked behind the outer reef, close enough to the passage they'd run at slack water that he could still hear the swell detonating against the barrier like distant artillery, far enough that no patrol skiff would bother looking. He beached the stolen skiff in the shadow of a fallen palm, dragged it above the tide line until the hull groaned in protest, and then stood for a long moment with his hands on his hips, watching Nadia Voss walk the beach like she was reading it.

She crouched at the wrack line, turned over a piece of driftwood, examined the sand's grain where the last high tide had feathered it. She did everything that way — as if the world were a document and she was checking it for forgery. Three days he'd known her, and he still couldn't decide whether that habit made him trust her more or less.

"Fire small," she said, without turning around. "Smoke carries."

"I know how to build a fire."

"You know how to build one for clients who want their tea by six." She straightened, brushing sand from her knees. "This is different. This is a fire for people who don't want to be found."

He didn't argue, because she was right, and because arguing with Nadia Voss was like arguing with a riptide — you spent energy and ended up exactly where she wanted you. He gathered dead casuarina needles instead, dry as bone, and built a fire the size of two cupped hands in a pit dug below the wind line. She watched him do it and gave a single nod, which from her was practically applause.

They ate salted fish from the freighter's stores and drank water that tasted faintly of the jerrican's plastic. The sun went down behind them in a long smear of copper, and the reef went quiet in that particular way reefs have after dark — not silent, but murmuring, ten thousand small mouths feeding in the dark. Lior had slept alone under that sound for fifteen years of guiding seasons. Tonight it felt different, and he didn't examine why.

It was Nadia who took out the compass.

She'd been carrying it since the lagoon passage, wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, and all day Lior had told himself the arrangement was practical — she was lighter, quicker through the surf, better suited to keeping a small object dry. But he'd felt its absence against his chest like a missing tooth, and when she unwrapped it now, in the firelight, he understood that something had shifted between them on the water, some unspoken custody arrangement neither of them had negotiated aloud.

The compass sat in her palm. Brass, old, heavy for its size, the crystal scratched nearly opaque except for a hand-width of clear glass over the card. The needle hung there, trembling.

Lior leaned forward despite himself. "Which way is it pointing?"

"Toward the lagoon." She said it flatly, but her thumb moved across the case's rim, back and forth, a tell he'd catalogued days ago. "Northeast by east. It hasn't wavered since we cleared the reef."

"Let me see."

She hesitated. It was only a hesitation — half a heartbeat, maybe less — but Lior had spent his career reading hesitation in clients about to step onto rotten ice, and he caught it the way he caught everything: instantly, and forever.

She passed it over.

The needle swung.

Not wildly — that would have been easier to explain. It swung slowly, deliberately, the way a man turns away from someone he's decided not to speak to, and settled pointing south-southwest, out toward open water, toward nothing. Lior turned the compass in his hand. The needle held its bearing, indifferent to the rotation, indifferent to him. He held it level, tilted it, walked three paces down the beach. South-southwest. Nothing there. No iron, no wreck, no reason.

"That's not right," he said.

"No," Nadia said quietly. "It isn't."

He brought it back to the fire and set it on the flat stone between them, and they both watched the needle point its pointless direction like a dog staring at an empty doorway. The fire cracked. Somewhere down the beach a crab clicked its claw twice and went silent.

"When I hold it," Lior said slowly, working it through the way he worked through a route, testing each hold before committing his weight, "it points at nothing. When you hold it—"

"It points home."

* * *

He should have let it go. That was the old Lior talking, the one who'd learned young that questions were debts and debts got collected. Fifteen years of guiding had taught him that every group has one secret, and the smart move is to pretend not to notice it — the marriage that's ending, the illness being outrun, the money that doesn't match the story. You get paid, they get their week in the mountains, everyone keeps their fictions intact.

But the needle kept pointing at nothing, and the nothing had a shape now, and the shape was every hour since the river.

"The seal," he said. "In the chest. The documents named an heir. I assumed—" He stopped, started again. "I assumed the claim was mine. That's what the papers said. That's what the appraiser reacted to. That's why the fleet tore my cabin apart looking for me."

"Yes."

"But the compass answers you."

Nadia fed a needle-thin stick of driftwood into the fire. Her face was lit from below, half gold and half shadow, and she gave him the profile of a woman deciding how much truth a situation could bear.

"My father made it," she said. "Or had it made — commissioned it, when I was nine years old. It wasn't a navigation instrument, not really. It was a key dressed as a compass. The Meridian Star's captain stole it along with everything else he stole, and it sat in that chest at the bottom of a flooded river for eleven years waiting for—" She gestured, a small precise motion. "For whatever the river intended."

"And it works by bloodline."

"By bloodline, yes. There are alloys in the needle — my father's notes called the process *lodestone of the house*. It holds a fixed bearing set at its making, and it ignores everything else unless—" She stopped.

"Unless what?"

"Unless it's held by the family it was tuned to. Then it finds the bearing it was given. Otherwise it just... wanders. Points at nothing. Or worse." Her mouth twisted. "There were stories that the old regime copied the technique for prison instruments. Compasses that pointed at whoever was hunting you. But those are stories."

Lior looked at the compass lying on the stone between them, its needle steady in its wrongness, and felt the ground shift under assumptions he'd been standing on since the riverbank. He was a man who navigated by verifiable things — snow bridges tested with a probe, weather read off cloud strata, distances paced and counted. He had built his whole survival on the principle that the world tells you the truth if you ask it correctly.

The world had been lying to him for weeks. Or rather — people had, using the world as their accomplice.

"The claim," he said. "The heir the chest named. Tell me it isn't you."

Nadia didn't answer fast enough.

* * *

The silence lasted four breaths. He counted them, because counting was what he did instead of shouting.

"It's my mother's name on the salvage grant," Nadia said finally. "And mine, appended. The Voss family held the lawful salvor's rights to the Meridian Star's final voyage. The captain knew it. That's partly why he ran — he was carrying cargo he had no paper for, out of waters he had no license in, and the compass was the one thing aboard that could prove whose it was. My father died trying to keep it from him. I've spent eleven years in exile assuming it was lost."

"So the chest wasn't addressed to me."

"The chest was lost. The river chose you." She met his eyes then, and there was no apology in her face, which he noticed, and noted, and filed. "I told you in the harbor that the compass belonged to my people. That was true. I didn't tell you the claim named my family because I needed you to believe we were partners, not that you were carrying my inheritance for me."

"And if I'd refused? If I'd taken the compass to a buyer in the port, like I was going to?"

"Then I would have followed you and taken it back, or failed and been taken myself." She said it without drama, the way she reported depths and currents. "You were the fastest route to the wreck. I used you. I'm telling you now because on that reef today, when the swell came through the passage and you put us through it anyway — you deserved better than arithmetic."

Lior stood up. His legs wanted to pace and he let them, walking the tide line where the sand was firm and cool, the stolen skiff a black shape behind him, the reef muttering its endless argument out in the dark. Anger came up in him like groundwater, slow and cold and everywhere at once. Not the hot fury of the men who'd trashed his cabin — this was older and more familiar, the specific anger of a man discovering that even his defections had been managed.

Every port official who'd ever looked through him. Every blacklist that had followed him from country to country for a mistake he'd made at nineteen. He had spent his whole adult life being the instrument other people navigated by, and here it was again, in miniature, on a nameless beach: he'd been the vessel. She'd been the bearing.

"You could have told me at any point," he said, coming back to the fire. "Any point, Nadia. On the freighter. In the tavern. Last night."

"If I'd told you on the freighter, you might have put me off at the next port."

"Would I have?"

"I don't know." And that, at least, was honest — he could hear it in her voice, the first time her certainty had cracked. "That's the worst part. I've lied to kings' customs men without my pulse moving. I lied to you and hated it, and I couldn't tell whether that was guilt or fear, and I couldn't afford either, so I kept the compass in my jacket and told myself I'd tell you when the tide allowed."

"The tide allows now."

"The tide allows now," she agreed.

He crouched and picked up the compass. Held it out flat on his palm between them. The needle drifted, lazy, meaningless, pointing its south-southwest nowhere. He watched her watch it, and saw something in her face he hadn't seen before — not calculation. Hunger. Grief wearing hunger's clothes. Eleven years of exile distilled into a look at a brass instrument that remembered her father.

"What else," Lior said. Not a question. A door held open, with him standing in it.

"Lior—"

"What else haven't you told me. All of it, tonight, while I'm still the man who ran that reef passage for you. Because tomorrow I might just be the guide again, and guides get paid and go home, and you'll have your compass and your claim and no one left who knows what you sacrificed to get them." He turned the compass slowly in his palm; the needle ignored him, faithful to nothing. "I've been nobody's partner my whole life. I know what it looks like when someone's decided I'm cargo."

Nadia reached into her jacket — he tensed, an old reflex — and drew out not the oilcloth but a folded square of paper, soft with handling, its creases going gray. She unfolded it carefully, like a thing that could die of rough treatment, and laid it beside the compass in the firelight.

A chart. Hand-drawn, faded, the ink gone brown. The lagoon, unmistakably — the same horseshoe of reef he'd memorized from the chest's documents — but marked with annotations in a tight, slanted script, and with soundings in a unit system no modern hydrographic office used. And in the corner, a seal. The dead nation's seal. Pressed into wax that had gone the color of dried blood.

"This is the original survey," Nadia said. "My father drew it. The copies in your chest were made from it. And there's a marking on it, in the channel, that I didn't understand until tonight, until I heard you talk about slack water and rogue swells and the way the reef breathes." Her finger touched a cluster of fine strokes in the passage. "These aren't rocks, Lior. They're hulls. Small craft, moored in a line across the channel floor. Deliberately. Sunk deliberately, in a row, the way you'd sink them for a breakwater — or a grave."

The fire popped. Out past the reef, the swell boomed once, twice, and went quiet, as if listening.

"The village boats," Lior said. He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way you know weather before it arrives.

"The village boats," Nadia whispered. "My father marked them the year before the fleet came. And tomorrow, or the day after, we dive that channel — and I need you to understand that when we find them, I won't be able to think clearly, and I will need someone beside me who can." She looked up at him, and for the first time since the harbor, since the storm drain, since the river, she looked tired. "So. What else haven't I told you? Ask me anything, and I'll answer it, and then you decide whether you're my partner or my guide. I'll pay a guide. Partners I can't replace."

Lior looked at the compass on his palm, pointing faithfully at nothing, and thought about the flooded river that had handed him a dead nation's chest, and the appraiser whispering *burn it*, and the cabin with its shattered door, and the reef passage with the swell towering over the transom — and underneath all of it, the simple, stubborn fact that he had not walked away yet, on any of the hundred mornings walking away had been possible.

"One question," he said.

Nadia waited.

"The hostages. The fishing village the fleet took pilots from. You knew before the deckhand told us. You knew before we ever boarded the freighter." He held her gaze. "Didn't you."

Out beyond the reef, the tide had begun to turn, and somewhere in the dark the channel they'd have to run again was already filling with bad water — and with the drowned boats of a village that no longer existed, waiting in their silent row for whatever came next.
