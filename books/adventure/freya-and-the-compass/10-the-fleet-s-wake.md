## 10. The Fleet's Wake

Nadia saw the smoke before she saw the boats.

It rose from the headland in a thin gray column, too domestic to be a fire, too deliberate to be anything else — the kind of smoke a fishing village made when it cured its catch for winter, or when it burned what it could not carry away. She stood at the lip of the sea cave where they had sheltered since dawn, salt spray drying white on her sleeves, and watched that column bend and straighten in the wind like something alive.

"Lior," she said.

He was already beside her, binoculars raised — his own, salvaged from the wreck of their stores, one lens cracked so that everything he looked at wore a hairline scar across its face. He passed them to her without a word, which was how she knew he had seen it first and had been waiting, out of some courtesy she hadn't asked for, for her to see it too.

Through the cracked lens the village resolved: a crescent of low houses above a shingle beach, drying racks, three stone jetties. And moored off the nearest jetty, riding at anchor with the ugly confidence of things that did not expect to be challenged, two of the fleet's picket launches. Their wake-lines spread across the bay in long pale fans, and between them, on the sand, moved figures with rifles slung across their backs the way farmers carried scythes — casually, as extensions of themselves.

"They're not looting," Lior said quietly. "Look at the racks. The fish is still hanging."

She looked. It was true. The village had not been raided; it had been occupied. Nets lay stacked and untouched. A dog trotted the tideline unbothered. Whatever the fleet wanted here, it wasn't property.

"It's pilots," she said, and heard her own voice go flat, the way it went flat when her mind was moving faster than her mouth wanted to follow. "They can't get through the reef without local hands. The outer passage shifts every season — even their charts are five years old, and the storm two winters back redrew half of it. They've done this before. In my grandmother's time the survey ships hired pilots openly. Now they just take them."

Lior took the binoculars back. For a long moment he said nothing, and Nadia watched him watch the village, watched the muscles work along his jaw the way they did when he was reading water — some private arithmetic of current and consequence running behind his eyes.

"How many people in a place like that?" he asked.

"Forty. Fifty, maybe. Fewer in monsoon season — half of them will have moved the boats upriver already." She paused. "The ones still there are the ones who couldn't leave. Old ones. Stubborn ones. People who thought the season would hold."

"The season won't hold," Lior said. "That's rather the point of all this."

* * *

They retreated deeper into the cave to argue, because argument on an exposed headland felt like shouting into a room full of mirrors. The cave ran back thirty feet into the cliff, its floor a chaos of tide-smoothed stones, its ceiling hung with dried weed that whispered when the wind found the entrance. Someone had been here before them — decades ago, by the look of it. Charcoal marks on the far wall: a boat, a sun, a row of tally strokes. Children counting something. Nadia touched the marks once and took her hand away.

"Two tides," Lior said. He crouched over the chart they'd copied from the chest, weighted at the corners with stones, its edges soft as cloth now from handling. "Maybe two and a half if we push through the night again, and you remember what the night cost us. The lagoon channel opens at slack water tomorrow evening. If we miss it—" He drew his thumb along the reef line, that serrated wall of coral and old ship-stone that guarded the Meridian Star like a jaw. "—we wait four days for the next window, and the monsoon makes landfall in three. There is no version of this where we arrive late and still arrive."

"I know the arithmetic," Nadia said.

"Do you?" He looked up at her, and there was no cruelty in it — that was the thing about Lior Dunmore, she was learning; his bluntness never carried contempt, only the terrible honesty of a man who had survived by calculating exactly. "Because I do. I've spent ten years doing nothing but arithmetic like this. Clients want the summit by noon or the weather kills them. You leave the slow one behind or you bury both. That's the whole profession. Everyone who tells you otherwise has never guided anyone anywhere."

"And the hostages?"

He flinched. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a breath taken through the nose — but she caught it, and she hated herself a little for how much satisfaction there was in catching it.

"There are no hostages yet," he said. "There's a village with soldiers in it and smoke on the headland. We don't know what happens next. We don't know that anything happens next. Fleets anchor off villages all the time—"

"You know that's not what this is."

"I know what it looks like." He stood, and the cave seemed smaller with him standing, though he was not a large man — it was something about the way he held himself, coiled, always braced against weather. "I also know what the fleet looks like when it's hunting us specifically. Every hour we spend playing rescue is an hour they gain on us. And if they reach the lagoon first, if they raise whatever they're raising off that wreck before we can seal the claim—" He stopped himself. Started again, quieter. "If that happens, then your village died for nothing. Then everyone in it died for nothing, and everyone who ever drowned in that lagoon, and the whole point of any of this turns to salt water. You can't save everyone and still arrive in time, Nadia. That's not cynicism. That's just the tide table."

She turned away from him, toward the cave mouth, toward the light.

The truth was that he was right, and she had known he would be right, and she had brought them around this headland anyway — had argued for the inside route, the one that passed within sight of the coast, telling herself it was safer from the fleet's offshore pickets when really, in the honest ledger she kept beneath her ribs, she had wanted to see the shore. Her shore. Not hers, not legally, not anymore — the nation that owned this coastline existed now only in sealed chests and exiled memories and a compass needle that answered bloodline — but hers in the way a scar is yours. She had not stood on land that remembered her people in eleven years. She had told herself the route choice was tactics.

And now the land had shown her why it remembered. Smoke, and soldiers, and nets stacked untouched because the hands that worked them were being kept for something worse than fishing.

"My father piloted for them once," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came out anyway, the way water finds a crack. "Before the sinking. Before everything. The Meridian Star's captain — Reiner Voss, no relation, the name is common on our coast — he paid well and he asked no questions, and my father was young and the village was poor, and he took the Star through the outer passage eleven times. He used to say the captain had a navigator's soul trapped in a merchant's body. He liked him." Her voice did something strange on the last word, thinned and roughened. "Eleven times. And the twelfth time, they didn't need a pilot anymore, because they'd already copied his charts. And the thirteenth time, they needed the village gone."

Lior had gone very still. In the cave's dim light his face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, but he didn't interrupt, and she understood that this was his form of respect — silence, total and deliberate, the silence he'd give a client describing how they'd broken their ankle.

"When the Star went down — and it went down badly, badly enough that the fleet's own insurers started asking questions — the village knew things. My mother's cousin had seen the cargo manifests. The pilot logs were in our archive. So the fleet came back, and they didn't come to ask." She wrapped her arms around herself, though the cave was not cold. "Fourteen boats went out that morning. None came back. The official record says a squall. The squall that day, according to the harbor master's log — which I have read, Lior, I have read every line of paper my country ever produced — was force two. A breeze. You could have sailed a child's dinghy in it."

The charcoal tallies on the wall. Children counting something. Boats, maybe. Days. Names.

"So when I tell you those people on that beach are hostages, I am not guessing," she said. "I am remembering. The fleet does not take villages by accident. It takes them because villages hold the only maps the fleet cannot buy, and because dead witnesses cost nothing, and because somewhere in the ledgers of whoever owns that flagship, forty lives balance neatly against one salvage claim worth more than the gross product of this entire coast. That is what justice outside the law costs. That is the price list. I have been reading it my whole life."

* * *

Lior sat down on a stone, slowly, the way a man sits when his legs have opinions of their own.

For a while neither of them spoke. Outside, the tide was turning; they could hear it, a change in the voice of the water against the rocks, the long exhale of the ebb beginning. Somewhere below, a gull argued with another gull about something small and mortal.

"I pulled a man out of the Ilse rapids three years ago," Lior said at last, to the chart, not to her. "Client. Experienced. Ignored my turn-back call because he'd paid for the full traverse and he intended to get the full traverse. Went into the strainer field. I got him out — barely, stupidly, should have killed us both — and you know what he did? He sued me. For the rescue. Said I'd created the conditions by leading him there at all." He laughed, short and airless. "Lost my last guiding certification over it. That's the blacklist. That's the papers I don't have. One man who wouldn't listen, and a river that didn't care, and a court that decided the wilderness was my fault."

He looked up at her then, and his eyes in the cave gloom were the color of the water over deep reef — dark, and holding more than they showed.

"So believe me when I tell you I understand wanting to save people. I have the scars and the court filings to prove it. But I learned something in that rapids, Nadia, and it took me years and everything I owned to learn it: you cannot pull someone out of water that's determined to take them unless you're standing on ground that holds. Right now we don't have ground. We have a stolen skiff, four days of bad water, a compass that answers to you and not to physics, and a claim that's only worth anything if we live to raise it. If we go into that village, we're not rescuers. We're two more bodies the fleet doesn't have to explain."

"Then we don't go into the village."

He blinked. "What?"

"We don't go into the village." She crouched beside the chart, and her mind, which had been a fog of grief for the last hour, suddenly cut clean, the way it did when the problem finally had edges she could grip. "You're right about the arithmetic — arriving late saves no one. So we don't trade arrival for rescue. We make the rescue *cost them the arrival.*"

Lior leaned forward despite himself. She could see the guide in him waking up, the part of him that loved an impossible line because impossible lines were the only ones worth reading.

"The fleet needs pilots," she said. "Local pilots. Which means somewhere on that flagship or in those picket launches, there are frightened fishermen being told to steer a hundred million credits of salvage tonnage through a reef that eats hulls. And frightened pilots make mistakes. Slow mistakes. Wrong-channel mistakes. *Grounded-on-a-reef-at-midnight* mistakes." She put her finger on the chart, on the outer passage, on the serpent's coil of shoals the storm two winters past had redrawn. "We don't need to free them. We need to make sure that whatever they're forced to do, they do it badly. A false light here. A moved marker there. The fleet trusts its stolen charts — and its stolen charts are five years old, and I have spent eleven years memorizing the difference."

"That's still walking into their guns."

"It's walking near their guns, at night, on water you know better than they do, with a man whose entire life is reading exactly this kind of water." She met his eyes. "You said the wilderness forgives nothing. Fine. Then let's give it a reason to be merciless — but let's aim it. Aim it at them."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, quicker, the voice of a man measuring a route he hadn't dared look at until now.

"If we shift their markers, they ground a launch. Maybe two. That buys hours — maybe a tide. But Nadia, if it goes wrong, if they catch us—"

"Then they catch two smugglers," she said, "and the claim dies with us, and the village dies anyway, and the monsoon closes over everything. Yes. I've done the arithmetic too." She stood, and her knees ached, and she was so tired down in her bones that the tiredness had become almost a kind of clarity. "But hear me, Lior Dunmore, because I will only say this once. I did not cross an ocean on forged papers and eat bilge-water bread in a freighter's hold and photograph a murdered village's memory out of a captain's safe so that I could sail past my people's coast at a respectful distance while the same fleet that drowned them adds forty more names to the same ledger. If the price of justice is that I arrive late — then I will find a way to make late arrive anyway. That is the whole of my profession. Everyone who tells you otherwise has never lost a country."

The wind shifted outside the cave, and the whispering weed overhead stilled, and in the sudden quiet they both heard it — faint, from the direction of the village, carried flat across the water.

An engine. Not the picket launches' idle. Something heavier, turning over, testing itself.

Lior was at the cave mouth with the binoculars before she finished rising. When he lowered them, his face had gone the color of the ash drifting from the headland fire.

"They're launching," he said. "Barges — two of them, heavy-lift, riding low. And Nadia—" He swallowed. "They're putting the villagers aboard. All of them. They're taking the pilots *with* the fleet."

Nadia closed her eyes. Behind them, in the dark of the cave, the compass in its oilcloth wrapping gave no sign at all — but she felt it anyway, the way you feel a hand on your shoulder, pointing toward the lagoon, toward the wreck, toward everything that was taken and everything that was owed.

Two tides. Two barges. Forty souls sailing into the jaws of the thing that had already eaten their grandmothers' world.

"Get the skiff ready," she said. "We're going to make them regret learning how to navigate."
