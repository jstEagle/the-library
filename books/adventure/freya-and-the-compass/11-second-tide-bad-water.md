## 11. Second Tide, Bad Water

The squall came out of the southeast like something that had been waiting for them all day and had finally lost patience.

Lior smelled it before he saw it — that green, mineral stink of rain dragged across open water, the smell of a storm still miles off but already thinking about you. He had learned it as a boy on the high passes, where weather announced itself in the creak of ice; here on the sea it spoke through the nose instead of the ears, but the grammar was the same. *I am coming. Decide what you are going to do about it.*

He decided by trimming the skiff's sail flat and taking the tiller himself.

"We can run for the leeward islets," Nadia said. She was bailing again — she seemed to spend half her life bailing — and her voice came up from the bottom of the boat, half-swallowed by wind.

"Too far. We'd be caught in the open with our sails down." He nodded at the black line of coast to starboard, a rumor of hills under the last bruised light. "We punch through. It's eight miles of bad water, then it's behind us."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we'll have a very short conversation about my judgment."

She almost smiled. In three days of shared salt and shared silence he had catalogued her smiles the way he once catalogued route lines on a map: rare, load-bearing, not to be trusted with more weight than they could carry. This one flickered and went out as the first gust hit them broadside and the skiff lay over until the gunwale kissed the sea.

* * *

The rain arrived not as rain but as a wall being pushed at them, a gray curtain you could hear coming across the water like applause from an enormous indifferent audience. Then it was on them and the world shrank to the length of the boat and the width of his two hands on the tiller.

Lior had steered whiteouts on the glacier fields. He had crossed avalanche terrain at night with a client hypothermic against his back and no rope long enough to matter. None of it had prepared him for how *loud* the sea was when it wanted to be — the wind screaming through the stays, the rain hammering the deck planks like fists, and underneath it all the deep arrhythmic groan of swell meeting swell, the ocean clearing its throat.

"Keep low!" he shouted. "Keep your weight amidships!"

Nadia had already figured that out. That was the thing about her that kept surprising him: she didn't need instructions twice. She moved through the pitching boat like someone who had grown up on water and been exiled from it, which, he supposed, was exactly what she was. She wedged herself against the mast step and held the bailer between her knees like a weapon.

The first wave broke over the bow and took their water stores with it.

He heard it happen more than saw it — the jerry cans lashed aft gave one collective groan, the lashings parted, and they were gone, four cans of fresh water swallowed whole by the sea that had made them necessary. The chart case went too, the spare charts he'd bought in port with money he didn't have, unrolling into the dark like pale ghosts before the water ate them.

"No," Nadia said, and it wasn't a word so much as a wound.

"Let them go."

"That's our water, Lior. That's—"

"I know what it is." He hauled the tiller across as the bow buried and rose, streaming. "Grieving costs balance. Balance keeps us alive. Grieve later."

* * *

They grieved later, or rather they didn't, because there was no time. The squall walked with them for six hours, sometimes easing enough that he could see the shape of her face across the boat, sometimes tightening again without warning so that the world became noise and cold and the animal arithmetic of keeping a small hull pointed somewhere other than death.

His hands were the worst part. Salt water found every crack in them, every old rope burn, and lit them up like signal fires. By the third hour he could no longer feel the tiller properly and had to steer by watching the angle of the sail against the invisible sky, reading the wind through the canvas the way a blind man reads a face.

Nadia crawled forward once, to check the anchor line coiled in the bow — he hadn't asked her to, hadn't thought of it himself, and the fact that she had thought of it settled into him somewhere below the ribs and stayed there. When she came back she pressed something into his hand: a strip of dried fish, softened in seawater, terrible.

"Eat," she said. "You've been steering since dusk."

"You need it more."

"I need you conscious more. Eat."

He ate. It tasted like salt and leather and, absurdly, like kindness. He couldn't remember the last time someone had told him what he needed instead of what they wanted from him. Clients told him where to take them. The fleet men had told him what to surrender. Even the appraiser with his whispered warnings had been, in the end, only managing his own risk. But this woman — this exile with her dead nation folded up inside her like a flag — handed him fish in a storm because his hands were shaking.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it so plainly that it embarrassed them both, and she turned away to bail.

* * *

Past midnight the squall's spine broke. The rain thinned from wall to veil to mist, and the wind swung around and died, and suddenly there was silence so complete that Lior's ears rang with it. Stars came out in ragged patches, torn open like bandages, and beneath them the sea lay down in long slow swells, exhausted, forgiving nothing but at least finished punishing.

They were nine miles off his intended landfall. He knew this without instruments, the way he knew altitude by the taste of the air — by the set of the current against the rudder, the color of the water under the stars, the smell of land that wasn't there yet. Nine miles. They had maybe three hours of darkness left.

Nadia slumped against the thwart, soaked to the marrow, her hair plastered to her skull, and looked at him across the ruined little boat with an expression he couldn't name at first. Then he could. It was the look of someone reassessing a map — finding that a river marked as impassable ran clean and deep after all.

"You've done this before," she said. "Not this exactly. But this."

"Storms? Everyone's done storms."

"Not like this. You read the water like it's a language you were raised in." She paused. "You weren't raised anywhere, were you? Not really."

The question should have stung. It landed instead like a diagnosis — accurate, delivered gently, by someone qualified to make it. He let the skiff drift on the long swells and considered lying to her, and found he didn't have the energy, or perhaps the desire.

"My mother died when I was seven. My father I never met. The state gave me a name off a list and a bed in an institution and a file that followed me to every border post on the coast." He watched the stars rather than her. "You know what it's like, having a file? People stop looking at you and start looking at it. After a while you start doing the same. You become paperwork that occasionally walks around."

"The compass," she said quietly. "It points at things. Real things. Maybe that's why you won't put it down."

He turned that over. It was too perceptive by half, and he resented it, and he was grateful for it, and these two feelings sat together in his chest like passengers who refused to acknowledge each other.

"Maybe," he said.

* * *

Dawn found them beached in a cove he chose by instinct and confirmed by smell — the sweet rot-vegetable breath of a freshwater seep at the head of the shingle. Fresh water. He was out of the boat before it grounded fully, kneeling in the wet sand, digging with both hands until a muddy lens formed and began to clear, and there it was: water, cold and faintly tannic and the finest thing he had ever tasted.

Nadia knelt beside him and drank from cupped hands, and mud streaked her cheek, and she laughed — actually laughed, a short rough sound like something breaking free.

"You dug a well," she said. "In a beach. In ten minutes."

"It's not a well. It's a seep. And it took me twelve minutes, don't flatter me."

"How do you know these things?"

How did he know them? Nobody had taught him. Nobody had claimed him. The wilderness had, though — the rivers and passes and storms had taken the orphaned boy the institutions couldn't place and trained him in their own curriculum, and now everything he knew had come from teachers who never once told him they loved him. He heard himself say, "Because when nobody claims you, you learn to claim the ground under your feet. Every bit of it. Where the water hides, where the rock will hold, where the weather turns. It's the only ownership I've ever had."

Nadia was quiet for a while. The tide slid out behind them, unhurried, patient as a debt collector.

"My people had a saying," she said at last. "*The sea remembers who fed it.* We fed it our dead for four hundred years, and it remembered us right up until the fleet came." Her jaw tightened. "But that's tomorrow's argument. Today—" she gestured at the wrecked skiff, the empty water cans' ghost-lashings, the two of them mud-streaked and salt-crusted and alive, "—today we're not dead, and I find I want to know why you keep making that happen."

"Occupational habit."

"Lior."

He looked at her. In the gray morning light she looked younger than her file — than whatever story she'd built about herself — and older too, worn smooth in places where grief had handled her often. He realized he had stopped measuring her as a liability or an asset somewhere in the sixth hour of the storm, when she'd braced his shoulder without being asked and held the tiller true while he freed a fouled sheet, and their hands had worked the same problem in perfect unison, and neither of them had said anything about it afterward because nothing needed saying.

Competence, he thought, was its own kind of intimacy. Two people who could each trust the other's hands in the dark had already exchanged more truth than most people managed in a lifetime of talking.

"Nadia." He said her name carefully, testing it. "When we reach the lagoon — whatever's in that wreck — you'll take it to your council. I know that. I've known it since the reef passage. I'm not asking you to promise me anything."

"Good," she said, "because I wouldn't."

"But I'm asking you to tell me one true thing. Just one. Whatever it is. Because after tonight I'm past the point where I can do this on half-truths, and I think—" he exhaled, surprised at how hard the next part was, "—I think you are too."

She studied him for a long moment. The tide sighed. Somewhere inland a bird complained about the morning.

"The compass was my father's," she said finally, and her voice cracked on the second word and steadied itself through sheer discipline. "That's the one true thing. There are others, and they're heavier, and you'll hate me a little when you hear them. But not today. Today we drink, we patch the sail, and we make the next tide."

"That's two promises and one truth."

"It's a down payment." Almost a smile. "I'm good for the rest."

* * *

They worked through the morning in the easy rhythm the storm had built between them — she patched canvas while he re-rigged the lashings, and neither spoke much, and the silence had changed texture entirely. It was no longer the silence of two liars conserving ammunition. It was the silence of people who had seen each other drenched and afraid and competent, and had drawn their own conclusions, and filed them where conclusions go to wait.

He caught himself, once, watching her work and thinking: *this is what it would be like to have a partner.* The thought alarmed him more than the squall had. Squalls ended. Thoughts like that took root.

They launched on the afternoon tide, running north along the coast toward the outer reefs, and it was near sunset — the light going copper, the water lying flat as hammered metal — when Nadia stiffened at the tiller and nodded toward the shore.

A man stood on the point above the cove ahead. Alone. He had the build of someone who had spent years on a ship's bridge, and he was holding something up toward them, angled against the fading light.

Glass. A signal mirror, catching the sun.

Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes. The old fleet alphabet, spelled out plain as a shout across the water.

Nadia's face went white. "That's Harl Vennik," she breathed. "Fleet navigator. He defected — or says he did. He's been hunting us since the port."

Lior watched the mirror flash and felt the day's hard-won calm drain out of him like water through a cracked hull. Nothing on this coast stayed quiet for long. Not the sea, and certainly not the people sailing it.

"He knows where we are," he said. "And now we know he does. Come about — we decide what to do with him before dark."
