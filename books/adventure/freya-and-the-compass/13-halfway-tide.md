## 13. Halfway Tide

The tide came in around their ankles like a slow confession, and Nadia sat with her back against the sea cave's cold stone and rehearsed the truth for the hundredth time.

She had been rehearsing it since the second night, actually — since the squall, since Lior had bailed the skiff with a cooking pot and his bare hands and never once said *I told you this was a bad idea*, since he'd wrapped her in the one dry blanket without being asked and then sat shivering at the tiller like it was nothing, like men gave away warmth every day. That was when the rehearsal had started. Because it was easy to lie to a man you didn't respect, and she had stopped not respecting him somewhere between the reef passage and the drowned boats, and now the lie sat in her chest like swallowed ballast.

Three tides left. The monsoon was a bruise on the southern horizon, purple-black and swelling. Somewhere beyond that bruise, the fleet's dredge groaned against the Meridian Star's hull, and its lights burned all night over the lagoon like a city that had no right to exist there.

Lior came up the shingle from where he'd cached the skiff under netting and palm fronds. He moved the way he always moved — economical, quiet, a man who had learned early that noise cost something. He set down two fish he'd speared at the reef edge and looked at her, and she knew, with the terrible clarity of someone who had spent her life reading documents, that she was out of time to rehearse.

"You've got your thinking face on," he said. He crouched by the driftwood fire they'd built deep in the cave's throat, screened from the sea side. "The one where you're deciding how much of the map to show me."

Nadia almost laughed. Almost. "Is that what I do?"

"It's what everyone does." He cleaned the fish with short, precise strokes of his knife. "Clients do it. Port officials do it. I did it for ten years, telling myself the blacklist was a clerical error." He glanced up. "So. How much of the map?"

*Now*, she thought. *Or never.* And never would mean arriving at the lagoon tomorrow with a partner who learned the truth from the claim document itself, read by lantern light in the wreck's flooded hold, and who would look at her the way people looked at forged charts — with everything good they'd believed about her suddenly suspect.

She pulled the compass from the oilskin wrap at her belt.

Lior went still. She'd noticed that about him — the stillness wasn't fear, exactly. It was attention, total and animal, the way he must have stood still on scree slopes listening for the sound of rock about to move.

"This belonged to my father," she said.

The fire popped. Out past the cave mouth, the tide kept climbing, patient as a creditor.

"His name was Tomas Voss. He was a hydrographer — a surveyor of coasts, before the coast stopped existing." She turned the compass over in her hands, let the firelight run along the brass. "You've seen the needle ignore north. You've seen it find the wreck instead. What you haven't asked is why a mass-produced instrument would do that, and the answer is that it isn't mass-produced. My father built it. There were three like it ever made, and two are at the bottom of the strait with the survey fleet when the regime scuttled them. This one he carried out in a satchel against his chest, along with me, on the last boat."

Lior set the knife down. He didn't say anything. That was the thing about him that unnerved her most — he listened the way deep water holds weight, without any visible effort, and you sank further into your own words than you meant to.

"The compass doesn't point by magnetism," she went on. "It points by resonance. My father tuned it — to a frequency, if you want the word, though it isn't really a frequency, it's more like..." She searched for it and hated that she had to search; she was the archivist, words were supposed to be her instruments. "Like a key cut to a lock. The lock is the Meridian Star. He surveyed that ship. He helped design her salvage systems, her hold configurations, the beacon cradle in her stern. When the captain stole her out of harbor — stole her, and everything she carried, which was our nation's entire maritime archive and treasury — my father built the compass so that someday someone could find her again. It answers to the ship. But it answers better to us. To Voss blood. You saw that on the beach two nights ago. You held it and it wandered. I held it and it locked on like a hound scenting."

"I did see that," Lior said quietly. "I told myself it was the wind, or my hands shaking."

"Your hands don't shake. I've watched them."

Something moved through his face — not anger yet, but the ground-swell of it, the way the sea lifts before it decides to break. "Go on," he said. "There's more. There's a reason you're doing this tonight."

"There is." Her mouth was dry. She reached into the same oilskin and drew out the folded papers — the ones from the chest, the ones she had told him were registry fragments, salvage law, background. Some of that had even been true. "This is the claim."

She unfolded it and laid it flat on the stone between them, weighting the corners with two pebbles and the compass itself. The old script ran in columns, stamped twice with the seal of a country that existed now only in archives and in the mouths of exiles: the double trident over the wave-line, the motto worn nearly smooth — *THE SEA REMEMBERS ITS OWN.*

"It's a letter of salvage commission," she said. "Issued under the old maritime code. It names the rightful salvor of the vessel Meridian Star, should she ever be found." She made herself say the next part plainly, without softening, because softening would be another lie. "It names my family. Voss. My father was granted the commission the week before the ship was stolen. It was never rescinded, because the government that issued it was destroyed before anyone could rescind it — and under the terms of the insurance conventions the fleet's own underwriters still honor, an unrescinded commission from a recognized predecessor state is valid. It's the whole legal architecture of what we're doing, Lior. The beacon, the sealing of the site, the claim. It works because the name on this paper is mine."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. She watched him read — or pretend to read; he could read, she knew that, but a man can stare at a page while the real reading happens elsewhere, behind his eyes, in the ledger where he keeps accounts of people.

"So when you found the chest," he said at last, "in the river. When you found me."

"When you found the chest," she corrected gently, "it was already looking for me. Or for my father, and he's eleven years dead, so it settled for the nearest thing." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I need you to understand that none of the danger was false. The fleet wants the wreck. They'll kill for it — we both know that now, after the village, after the hostages. Everything I told you about the tides, the window, the monsoon, the reef — all true. Every chart I decoded, true. If you walk away tonight, I will still go to that lagoon, and I will probably die there, and the claim will still be real."

"But you didn't need me," Lior said. "For the finding. For the opening. Maybe not even for the claiming." His voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "A compass that answers your blood. A claim with your name on it. You needed muscle, maybe. A guide. A man who knows water and won't ask questions."

"Yes," Nadia said. "And no. And I don't know anymore, and that's the truth, and I'm tired of carrying the version of the truth that was tidier."

He looked at her then, fully, and she made herself hold it. This was the part she had dreaded through a hundred rehearsals — not the accusation, which she deserved, but the arithmetic happening behind his eyes. The recalculation of every shared fire, every bailed boat, every moment on the reef passage when he had grinned through the spray like a man finally being tested in his own language. How much of that had been real, and how much had been her spending him?

Because here was the thing she hadn't put in any rehearsal: she didn't know the answer herself anymore. That was the rot at the center of a long deception — eventually you couldn't find the seam between the performance and the person.

"My father used to say," she heard herself begin, and stopped, and started again, "my father used to say that a chart is a promise. Every line you draw is a promise to whoever follows it. That the worst sin in cartography isn't error — error is honest, error gets corrected. The worst sin is drawing a coastline that flatters the reader. Making the passage look safer than it is, because you want them to sail it." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I drew you a beautiful coastline, Lior Dunmore. I made the passage look safe because I wanted you to sail it. And then somewhere in the middle of the water I forgot which parts were ink and which parts were me."

The tide had reached the lip of the cave now, sliding across the shingle in long silver tongues, retreating, coming again. Lior picked up the claim paper. He read it properly this time, top to bottom, his lips moving slightly over the old formal phrases — *rightful salvor, lawful successor, seal and witness* — and Nadia sat in the silence and let it have her.

When he spoke, his voice was strange. Not cold. Stripped.

"The river gave me that chest," he said. "First thing the wilderness ever handed me free. I remember thinking that. Standing on the bank with it dripping, thinking, well, either my luck's changed or the bill comes later." He set the paper down. "Bill came later. Men who don't knock. Everything I owned gone. Fine. I chose to keep going after that. Nobody made me." He rubbed his jaw, and she saw the exhaustion on him suddenly, days of it, salt-scoured. "But I'd like to know one thing, and I want the actual answer, not the flattering coastline. On the beach. When the needle steadied for you and spun for me. You watched me doubt my own eyes. You let me think I was losing my nerve."

"I know," Nadia said. "That was the worst of it. That was the part I couldn't forgive while I was still lying, so I just — didn't look at it. Which is what cowards do with charts too. We mark the shoal and then fold the map so we don't have to see it."

"And now?"

"Now the monsoon is forty hours out and there are three tides left and I am done folding the map." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough that the firelight touched them both. "Here is everything, in order, and then you can decide what I am to you. One: the compass is my father's and it is leaving that lagoon in my hands or not at all. Two: the claim is mine and I will raise the beacon myself if I have to swim to the stern with it in my teeth. Three—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding the catch would be one more flattering line on the chart, "—three, I could do all of it alone and I would fail. The channel cutting, the picket, the valves, whatever we do about that dredge — I can't dive it, I can't read water the way you do, I can't hold a boat in a cross-current to save my life, literally. I recruited you under false pretenses and then discovered the pretenses were accidentally accurate. I do need you. Just not for the reasons I gave."

Lior was quiet for a long time. Outside, the surf worked the reef with its patient fists. Far off, faint as a headache, the fleet's dredge throbbed through the water.

"The deckhand in the tavern," he said finally. "The village. Your home."

"Kestrel Reach." The name hurt going out, the way it always did. "Yes. What he described — the burning oil on the water, the boats sunk at their moorings — those were our boats. I told you it was my people's patrimony the captain stole. I didn't tell you the fleet murdered the town to keep the theft quiet, or that I've spent eleven years collecting testimony in exile, page by page, and that the captain's log aboard that survey barge is the first primary evidence anyone has ever gotten close to." She looked at her hands. "I also didn't tell you that some nights I don't care about the claim at all. Some nights the treasure means nothing and I'd burn every coin in that hold for one hour inside a courtroom with that captain. And some nights I wake up wanting the wreck itself so badly I can taste brass. Both things are true. People are both things. I should have trusted you with that from the start."

"You should have," Lior agreed. No cruelty in it. Just the sound of a man setting a weight down on the far side of a scale.

Nadia nodded. She had expected this conversation to end one of two ways — with his fury, or with his departure, the skiff gone from the shingle when she woke. She had not prepared for the third possibility, which was that he simply sat there, turning it over, a guide assessing a route: *is this slope climbable, given what the weather has done to it?*

"If we do this," he said at last, "there are conditions."

Her breath went out of her. "Name them."

"One. From tonight, no versions. If you learn something, I hear it within the hour, even if it makes you look bad. Especially then." He held up a second finger. "Two. The claim goes in the waterproof roll with the charts. I carry it half the time. If you drown, it survives, and if I drown, you'll know exactly where it is because you'll have been carrying it yourself half the time. Three—" and here he paused, and something in his face shifted, softened by a degree, the way ice shifts before it becomes water, "—three. When this is over, whatever's over, you tell me the rest of it. Your father. The crossing. All of it. Not because I need it for the job. Because I've spent ten years being a man nobody told things to, and I find I'd rather hear your whole story than your useful half."

Nadia discovered she was crying, quietly, without drama, the way the tide came in. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and hated herself a little for it and let it happen anyway.

"That's not a punishment list," she managed. "That's just — partnership."

"It's what I'd ask of any client on a mountain," Lior said. "Turns out it's what I'd ask of anyone at all." He nudged the compass across the stone toward her with one finger. "Your father's. Yours. I only ever borrowed it."

She took it. The brass was warm from the fire, warm as a living thing, and the needle lay still for once, resting, pointing at nothing — or at everything, at the whole dark water where the ship waited with her father's work bolted into her bones.

"Three tides," Nadia said.

"Three tides," Lior agreed. Then he tilted his head, listening, and she heard it a heartbeat later — the thing that had stopped him. An engine note on the wind, wrong for the dredge, wrong for the picket boats. Higher. Faster. Coming down the coast toward their stretch of empty shore.

Lior was already moving, killing the fire with sand, pulling the netting off the skiff. "Somebody's running a searchlight pattern along the headland," he said, low and fast. "Nadia — somebody knows these islands aren't empty."

* * *

They launched into the last of the flood tide with the oars muffled in rags, and the light swept the headland behind them like a lighthouse gone mad, and somewhere ahead in the dark, between them and the lagoon, something was waiting that hadn't been waiting an hour ago.
