## 15. Two Tides

They came through the reef passage at dawn, and the sound of them arrived before the sight.

Lior heard it first as a change in the water inside the cave — a tremor in the pools that pooled between the rocks at his feet, a shiver running through the shallows like the sea itself flinching. Then the low diesel thunder rolled across the lagoon's mouth, and he pressed himself flat against the stone lip of their hiding place and looked out through the curtain of salt-stale air and saw them: three hulls moving in formation through the channel, gray and white and flying no flag he recognized, which was its own kind of flag.

The *Corvane*. The dredge barge with its crane arm folded against its shoulder like a sleeping heron. And behind them, riding low and heavy in the water, a supply tender whose deck was stacked with salvage cages — steel boxes built to hold things that had spent years underwater and did not want to leave it.

"They made the passage," Nadia said beside him. Her voice was flat, stripped of everything but fact. "The turncoat pilot got them through after all."

"He didn't get us killed first," Lior said. "Small mercies."

She didn't laugh. He hadn't expected her to. For two days since they'd caught Halvard Voss's navigator holding a signal mirror to the morning sun over the outer reef — two days since Lior had taken the mirror and thrown it into deep water and told the man to swim for whatever shore would have him — they had run on cold rations and colder silence, skirting the picket boats, diving the ghost reef where the drowned village's fishing boats still hung moored in their impossible row, and arriving here, at this cave in the lagoon's northern wall, only hours before the fleet itself.

Now there was nothing left to do but watch.

* * *

The lagoon was not what Lior had imagined. Charts had shown him a bowl of sheltered water behind the reef, four hundred meters across, ringed by coral shelves that broke the swell before it could build. What the charts had not shown him was the color — a green so deep and so still it seemed less like water than like glass laid over something older, something patient. The wreck of the *Meridian Star* lay somewhere beneath that green, and even from the cave's height Lior could see the shadow of her: a long dark shape on the white sand bottom, forty meters down, listing to port, her superstructure furred with coral until she looked less like a ship than like a reef that had decided, once, to be a ship.

The fleet anchored in a triangle around her. Anchors went down with chain-rattle that echoed off the cave walls. Within an hour the *Corvane* had swung her crane arm out over the water, and divers began going down in pairs, tethered lines paying out behind them, bubbles rising in silver strings.

Armed men stood along the *Corvane*'s rails. Not many — eight, maybe ten — but enough. Lior counted the weapons he could see: rifles slung, one mounted gun on the tender's bow, and the casual, bored posture of men who had done this before and knew no one was coming to stop them.

"Two tides," Nadia said.

He turned. She sat with her back against the cave wall, knees drawn up, the compass open in her cupped hands. In the dim light its needle hung steady, pointing not north but out through the rock toward the wreck, toward her father's ship, toward the claim that bore her family's name. She hadn't closed it in hours. Lior understood why. It was the only thing in the world right now that was pointing anywhere at all.

"Two tides," he agreed. "Then the monsoon makes landfall and every boat in this water becomes driftwood."

"Two tides," she said again, as if the repetition might change the arithmetic. "And they're already lifting."

* * *

They watched all morning.

Lior had spent fifteen years reading water for a living — reading it for clients who paid him to keep them alive, reading it alone when there was no one to pay, reading it in flood season when the rivers took back what they'd lent. He knew how to watch without being seen, how to lie still on cold stone until his body forgot it was a body. But he had never watched anything like this: the slow, methodical dismemberment of a grave.

By midday the divers had rigged lift bags on something amidships. The *Corvane*'s crane took up the strain, and the water below the barge bulged, swirled, and gave up its first prize — a crate the size of a coffin, streaming silt, swinging up into the light while the armed men leaned on the rails and someone laughed.

Nadia's hands had gone white around the compass housing.

"That's the chart locker," she said quietly. "That's where my father kept the surveys. The hydrographic work. Forty years of mapping those reefs." Her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "They'll sell the charts and melt the instruments and no one will ever know what was lost."

Lior said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound. They were two people in a hole in a rock, with a stolen skiff hidden in a cove two kilometers south, a knife each, half a canteen of water, and a compass that answered bloodline instead of magnetism. Against that: three ships, ten guns, and a crane that could lift a house.

Despair, he thought, was a strange thing. It didn't arrive like a wave. It arrived like a tide — slow, total, covering everything you'd built on the shore until you couldn't remember what dry ground looked like.

* * *

In the afternoon the wind shifted.

It came around to the northeast, and it carried the smell with it — that particular smell Lior knew from a lifetime of mountains, the smell of weather gathering itself beyond the horizon. The sky over the open sea had begun to organize. Clouds that had lain scattered all week were now stacking, tower over tower, in a wall so distant it was almost beautiful, lit gold at its base by the lowering sun.

The monsoon, six days early by some reckonings, on schedule by others. Two tides away by everyone's.

The fleet knew it too. Lior watched the tempo change aboard the *Corvane* — more divers in the water, shorter surface intervals, the crane cycling faster, crates coming up two and three at a time now, the tender shuttling loads from barge to hold with the urgency of men racing a clock they could hear ticking. Captain Sarel Vane was not going to wait out the storm. He was going to strip the wreck bare and run for deep water before the barometer finished falling, and if the reef or the law or the dead objected, well — the dead had never objected successfully yet.

"There," Nadia breathed.

A diver surfaced near the *Corvane*'s stern, waving both arms overhead. A second followed, gesturing down. On the barge, a knot of men gathered at the rail, and Lior saw the captain himself come forward — a small figure in a white shirt, unmistakable even at this distance because everyone else moved around him the way water moves around a stone.

"They've found the strongroom," Nadia whispered. "It has to be. That's where the manifest says—" She stopped herself. Closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet, and furious about being wet. "My father's seal is down there. The original grant. The thing that proves the claim was ours before it was anyone's."

"And they're going to pull it up and burn it," Lior said.

"Or keep it. Keep it and own it." She looked at him, and in the failing light her face was all angles and shadow. "Everything we came here to do, Lior. Everything. And we're sitting in a cave watching them carry it away piece by piece."

He wanted to tell her they'd find another way. He was a guide; lying to clients about the difficulty of a route was practically part of the service. But she wasn't a client, and the route had no other way, and they both knew it, and the silence between them filled up with the sound of the crane winching history out of the sea.

* * *

Dusk came down over the lagoon like a lid.

The fleet worked lights on through the evening — floods on the *Corvane*'s deck, underwater lamps blooming in the green like pale jellyfish — and the salvage went on around the clock now, because there was no time left to waste and no authority left to answer to. From the cave, the whole operation had a terrible beauty: the lights, the bubbles, the crane swinging its slow pendulum, the wall of storm clouds beyond the reef flickering with heat lightning like a sky remembering fire.

Lior lay on his stomach at the cave mouth and watched, and hated, and did the arithmetic again, and got the same answer every time. Outgunned. Out of time. Out of options.

Except.

He frowned. Shifted his weight. Watched again.

The *Corvane* had repositioned twice since morning, walking her anchors closer to the wreck as the divers pushed deeper into the hull. And each time she moved, her propellers had churned the shallow water at the lagoon's eastern edge, where the reef shelf rose nearest the surface — the same shelf, Lior realized, that formed the throat of the channel they'd need to escape through. The same shelf the charts called fragile. The same shelf Nadia's father had spent forty years mapping, warning, pleading with the shipping companies to respect.

He watched the barge swing on her anchor chain as the evening breeze freshened. Watched her stern wash boil against the shelf. And then — when the tender came alongside to take on a load, too fast, too close, her wake slamming the shallow water — he saw it.

A sound reached the cave a full three seconds later, rolling across the lagoon like far-off artillery: a deep, grinding crack, felt in the stone under Lior's chest before it reached his ears.

"What was that?" Nadia was beside him instantly.

"Listen."

Another wake. Another grind — longer this time, a groaning, tearing note that traveled through the water and the rock and up into Lior's teeth. Out on the shelf, in the fleet's own floodlights, he could see the water discoloring, a plume of white rising from the coral where the tender's wash had scoured it, and beneath the plume, visible even at this distance as a dark seam in the pale shelf —

— a crack. A crack in the reef itself, opening like a wound.

"Nadia." His heart had changed rhythm entirely. Not despair anymore. Something faster, sharper, more dangerous. "How thick is that shelf?"

She stared at him. "What?"

"The eastern shelf. Your father's surveys. How thick, how sound, how much weight can it take?" He grabbed her arm, and she let him, her eyes going wide as she followed his pointing hand to the plume of scour, the dark seam, the barge sitting fat and complacent directly above it. "Look at what they're doing. Look at where they're doing it. They've been churning that shelf all day — props, anchors, wakes, the crane swings rocking the whole barge on her chains. Your father spent forty years telling the world that shelf couldn't take heavy traffic. Nobody listened because nobody cared. Now look who's parked on top of it."

Understanding moved across her face slowly, like weather.

"The reef," she said. "The reef is cracking."

"The reef is cracking," Lior said, "and they don't know it, because the men who knew are dead or exiled, and the charts that proved it are in a crate on their own deck." He laughed — a short, wild sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who has just seen the wilderness offer him a weapon. "They came in here thinking the only danger was the monsoon. They think the reef is furniture. But it isn't furniture, Nadia. It's alive, and it's wounded, and it's directly under their keels."

She was already pulling the compass from its wrapping, holding it up, watching the needle swing — not north, never north, but out and down toward the wreck, toward the strongroom, toward the last tide that would ever be low enough to cross that cracking shelf on foot or by skiff.

"If the shelf fails," she said slowly, working it through, "the channel closes. Nothing gets out. Not the *Corvane*, not the tender, not the treasure, not the crew. They'd be trapped in this lagoon when the monsoon makes landfall."

"Not just trapped." Lior watched another wake slam the shelf, watched the white plume spread, watched the seam grow by inches in the fleet's own merciless lights. "Sunk. All of them. The whole rotten fleet, delivered to the bottom by the very reef they've been ignoring since the day they burned your village."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The crane swung. The bubbles rose. Somewhere out on the blackening horizon, the storm wall pulsed with silent lightning, counting down.

"It might not fail," Nadia said at last. "Reefs are tougher than people think. It might hold."

"It might," Lior agreed. "If we do nothing. If we sit in this cave for two more tides and let them load every crate and sail away rich." He turned to face her fully, and found her already looking at him, and in her eyes the grief was still there but it had caught fire at the edges. "Or we help it along. We find out how close it is to failing. We find out what it would take."

"That's murder, Lior. If that shelf goes with crews aboard—"

"It's justice," he said, "if we make sure the ones who deserve it are the ones standing on it when it goes — and no one else. Their captain chose to anchor on a dying reef to rob a grave. Let him live with the geology." He looked back out at the lights, the crane, the plume of white rising off the wounded coral like breath from a splitting lung. "We have two tides, Nadia. One to learn exactly how that shelf fails, and one to make sure it fails at the right moment, in the right way, with the evidence in our hands and the hostages nowhere near it."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she looked down at the compass in her hands — her father's compass, answering her blood across forty fathoms of green water — and when she raised her head, she was smiling, and it was the most frightening thing Lior had seen in weeks.

"My father's surveys are in that crate they pulled up this morning," she said. "The structural maps. The load tables. Everything we need to know about how that shelf dies." She was already moving toward the cave's inner passage, toward the skiff, toward the water. "We're going to steal them back."

Out on the lagoon, the *Corvane*'s floodlights blazed, and beneath her keel, patient as the drowned, the reef went on cracking.
