## 16. The Wreck Speaks

The supply skiff came out of the mangroves at dawn, riding low with crates of diesel and drinking water, and Nadia Voss stood in the stern of it wearing another woman's name.

The woman's name was Petra Ilves, and she had sold it to them for a night's lodging and a promise. She ran provisions to the salvage fleet twice a week from the fishing village up the coast — the one that still existed, not the one that didn't — and she was small and brown-haired and unremarkable in exactly the way Nadia needed to be. The fleet's quartermaster knew her by sight but not by face. That was the whole architecture of the plan: be known without being seen.

Lior crouched at the tiller, keeping his head down beneath the canvas awning, letting Petra's cousin handle the boat. He had argued against this for two hours the night before, quietly, in the way he argued — laying out the currents and the guard rotations like stones across a river, showing her every place she could drown. Then he had stopped arguing and started helping, which was how she knew he understood. There was no other way into the survey barge. There was no other way to the log.

"Quartermaster checks the manifest," Petra's cousin said, eyes on the channel. "He doesn't check you. You're just hands. Unload, keep your cap down, don't talk unless they talk first."

"And if someone does talk first?"

"You're from Kessel Point. You don't like boats. You took this work because it paid." A shrug. "Nobody asks a woman who doesn't like boats why she's quiet."

The fleet came into view around the headland the way weather comes — gradually, then all at once. Three hulls anchored in the roadstead outside the reef passage: the flagship *Corvus* with its crane arm folded like a sleeping insect, a tender, and moored closest to the lagoon mouth, the survey barge *Halcyon*, flat-decked and ugly, bristling with sonar masts and winches. Beyond it, through the gap in the reef, Nadia could see the lagoon itself, green-gold in the early light, and the tip of the Meridian Star's mast breaking the surface like a bone pushed up through skin.

Twenty-two years since she had seen that mast. She had been nine years old, standing on the seawall at Vessa while the salvage ship burned on the horizon, and her father had put his hand on her head and said, *Remember where it lies.* Not where it sank. Where it lay. As if the wreck were a body in state, and someone would come someday who needed to pay respects.

She pressed her thumbnail into her palm until the pain sharpened her. Not now. Grief was a luxury for after.

* * *

The quartermaster was a heavy man with sunburned ears and a clipboard laminated against the spray. He counted crates, not people. Nadia hauled diesel drums up the *Halcyon*'s boarding ladder with her cap pulled low and her shoulders rounded into a laborer's stoop, and no one looked at her twice, because the fleet had never learned to look at the people it stepped over. That was the lesson of Vessa. That was the lesson written in drowned houses at the bottom of a lagoon. Men who believe some lives are cargo will always, eventually, leave the ledger open.

The deck was chaos of purposeful industry — divers' racks, air hoses coiled like intestines, a chart table under an awning where two men argued over a sonar readout. Nadia carried her drum aft, past the galley hatch, past the radio shack with its antenna wire humming faintly in the wind, to the stern compartment the cousin had described: the captain's office, door open because the *Halcyon* was a working vessel and working vessels did not lock their papers against their own crews.

Captain Emeric Rask. She turned the name over as she walked. She had never seen him. None of the exiles had, not clearly — he had commanded from the *Corvus* the night the gunboats went into Vessa harbor, and afterward he had become a rumor with a salary, a man whose signature appeared on salvage permits granted by a maritime authority that still, absurdly, honored the paperwork of a nation it had helped bury. But his log would have his hand in it. Logs always did. Captains wrote things down because the sea made them superstitious about memory, and superstition was a hole in armor no amount of lawyers could patch.

The office smelled of coffee and mildew. A bunk, a desk bolted to the deck, a swivel chair worn shiny at the arms. On the desk: a mug, a tide almanac, a stack of requisition forms. And beneath the almanac, spine out, a bound book with a brass clasp — the ship's log, current volume.

Nadia set down the empty drum carrier, crossed to the desk, and listened.

Engines idling below. Winch hydraulics cycling. Two men laughing near the bow, the laugh of men on their third hour of waiting for something else to happen. The radio crackling a weather advisory — monsoon formation, ninety hours out, landfall probability rising. Ninety hours. Lior had said six tides; the sky was now saying the same thing in its own language.

She slid the log free.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Twenty-two years of wanting this moment and her hands were steady as a surgeon's, turning the brass clasp, opening to the current month, scanning backward through entries in a blunt, disciplined hand: *Dredge ops suspended, swell 2m. Survey line 7 complete. Fuel received from Corvus.* Mundane. Bureaucratic. The voice of a man doing murder as accounting.

Two volumes back. The cousin had said Rask kept three years aboard; the older ones were in the cabin safe, unreachable. But two years back might be enough, because two years back was when the fleet had returned to the lagoon for its second campaign, and men who return to a crime scene grow careless about what they admit to paper.

*March 14.* Her father's birthday month. She hadn't planned to notice; she noticed anyway.

*Anchored Vessa roads. Local officials cooperative re: site security. Village access restricted per agreement.*

Cooperative. Restricted. Per agreement. She read the words twice, tasting each one, feeling how cleanly they scrubbed the truth — as though the village had been a hazard to navigation rather than four hundred people, as though the gunboats had been a courtesy. This was the language the fleet used even in private, she realized. Perhaps especially in private. Lies repeated long enough became the only dialect a man had left.

Then, further in:

*Incident report appended. Two local craft attempted to breach exclusion zone during night ops. Warning shots fired. Craft sunk. Casualties: 3 (est.). No further action required. Recommend relocation of remaining population prior to next season — presence complicates salvage security.*

No further action required.

Nadia stood very still in the mildew-smelling room with the log open in her hands, and somewhere below her feet the engines turned over, patient as a heart, and she thought: *I am reading my neighbors' death certificate, and the man who signed it is drinking coffee forty meters from where I stand.*

The names were there too, appended in an incident summary clipped inside the back cover — the fleet kept everything, evidence of its own tidiness. *Marta Oun. Teodor Oun. Child, name unrecorded.* Marta Oun had taught her to gut fish on the seawall when she was seven, laughing at Nadia's clumsiness, saying the sea forgave beginners but not quitters. Teodor had repaired nets. The child — the child had a name, whatever this page said. Names were the first thing the drowned lost and the last thing the living owed them.

Her hands were still steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. Somewhere in the last twenty-two years she had become a person who could hold a death certificate calmly, and she did not know when that had happened, and there was no time to mourn the version of herself it had cost.

* * *

The camera was a salvaged press camera, film-loaded, wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket. Lior had procured it from a fisherman in exchange for repairing the man's outboard, and he had shown her how to work it in the dark of the sea cave with the patience of a man teaching a knot: *shutter here, wind here, hold your breath on the click.* He had also shown her something else, without meaning to — the way his jaw set when he talked about what the photographs were for. Not treasure. Testimony. He understood, in the wordless way of men who had been blacklisted themselves, what it meant to have your existence denied by paper. He wanted to make paper that could not be denied.

She worked fast. The incident page, flat under the porthole light. The exclusion-zone order with its official stamp. The casualty estimate. The recommendation of relocation — *relocation*, that gentle word for burning a village's boats and shelling its seawall until the survivors scattered to camps that did not want them. Page after page, breath held on each click, winding the film with fingers that refused, stubbornly, to tremble.

Footsteps in the passageway.

She had the log closed and the almanac replaced over it before the shadow reached the door, and she was bent over the empty drum carrier, retieing a strap that needed no tieing, when the second officer looked in.

"You the new runner?"

"Petra's girl," Nadia said, from Kessel Point, disliking boats. Her accent sat correctly on her tongue; she had practiced it against Lior's ear for an hour while he corrected her vowels with the gravity of a man defusing a mine.

"Petra's short-handed."

"Petra's short-tempered," Nadia said, and the officer laughed, and the laugh was the ugliest sound she had heard in weeks, because it was warm and human and came from a man whose wages were paid out of a drowned village. She smiled at him. She let him see her smile. She catalogued his face — she was her father's daughter; she catalogued everything — and she thought: *when this is broadcast, when the tribunals come or don't come, you will remember being kind to me, and it will not save you, and I will not be sorry.*

Vindication, she was learning, tasted like ash. You waited twenty-two years for proof and when you held it, it weighed nothing. Paper did not weep. Paper did not stand on a seawall and say a grandmother's name aloud. Proof was only a key, and keys were worthless without a door, and doors were worthless without someone left alive with the standing to walk through them.

That was the arithmetic that had hollowed her out on the crossing from exile: of everyone who had a right to judge Captain Emeric Rask, the list was short and getting shorter. Her father, dead. The council, scattered. The village, a grid of foundations under ten fathoms. If she failed, the log would rot in this mildewed room and the truth would remain what it had always been — a thing the sea knew and the courts did not care to learn.

So she would not fail.

* * *

She finished the run. Carried water crates to the galley, signed nothing, spoke little, kept her cap low and her shoulders round. From the deck she allowed herself one look at the lagoon through the reef gap — one look at the mast, at the dredge barge already moored over the site with its suction pipes trailing into the green water like feeding tubes — and then she looked away, because looking was a luxury and she had a skiff to catch.

The *Corvus* lay across the roadstead, and as Petra's cousin swung the loaded skiff back toward the mangrove channel, Nadia saw activity on its deck that had not been there at dawn: armed men at the rail, a launch being lowered, a signal lamp stuttering toward the picket boat at the reef mouth. Something had changed. Something had spooked them, or hardened them, and either way the window they were all racing was narrowing from both ends — monsoon behind, suspicion ahead.

The cousin saw it too. "They're twitchy," he muttered. "Twitchy fleets shoot first at supply runners."

But the launch did not turn toward them. It ran instead toward the fishing village — the living one, the one that still existed — and Nadia watched it go with a cold spreading under her ribs that had nothing to do with the spray.

* * *

Lior met her at the mangrove cut, waist-deep beside the hidden skiff, and read her face before she said a word. He was good at that. He read water and weather and people with the same instrument, some calibrated patience underneath the wariness, and right now what he read made him wade closer and take the oilcloth bundle from her hands as if it were something breakable.

"You got it."

"I got it." Her voice came out flat. "Thirty-one exposures. Incident report, casualty list, the relocation order, Rask's own signature. It's all there. It's all—" She stopped. The ash taste rose again, and she let him see it, because hiding it from him had started to feel like a lie she couldn't afford. "It's paper, Lior. My neighbors are names in an appendix. *Child, name unrecorded.* And the man who wrote that drank his coffee this morning forty meters from me, and I smiled at his officer, and none of it — none of it changes anything yet. Proof is nothing. Proof is only the beginning of the debt."

Lior turned the bundle over once, carefully, then looked at her. "Then we find someone to collect," he said. "There are maritime authorities that still honor the old nation's filings. Your council has standing. The insurers—"

"The insurers honor anything until it costs them." She laughed, short and bitter, and climbed into the skiff. "I spent twenty-two years believing that if I could just get the truth into the light, justice would do the rest. Like a plant. Add water." She shook her head. "Justice isn't a plant. It's a fire, and someone has to strike the match, and everyone who had the right to strike it is dead or drowned or nine years old on a seawall remembering."

"Not everyone," Lior said quietly.

She looked at him. He was wringing water from his sleeve, not meeting her eye, the way he did when he said things that cost him.

"You're not nine years old anymore," he said. "And you're not alone. And the hostages those launches are collecting right now — the ones from the fishing village — they're going to need someone with standing too, and soon, or they'll be names in somebody's appendix." He finally looked up. "How much film is left?"

"Nine exposures. Why?"

"Because I counted six guards on the *Corvus*'s launch, and I counted the fuel drums on that skiff, and I've been thinking about sea valves." His jaw did the thing it did. "But first — the log. Can your council receive a transmission from the coast station at Tarn? Because if it can, we send these pages tonight, before the monsoon takes the antennas down. Let the world start judging while we finish the work."

Nadia held the oilcloth bundle against her chest and felt, for the first time since the seawall, something that was not ash. Small. Embers, maybe. Enough to strike against.

"Tonight," she said. "Before the next tide."

Out beyond the mangroves, the horizon had begun to stack itself with cloud, blue-black and patient, and the first far-off thunder rolled across the water like a drum being carried toward a trial.
