## 17. One Tide

The horizon had begun to lie.

Lior stood at the mouth of the sea cave with the binoculars Nadia had salvaged from the wreck of her old life — brass-cased, salt-pitted, the left lens clouded like an eye going blind — and watched the sky do something he had only seen twice in twenty years of mountain work, and never over open water. The squall line wasn't building. It was *arranging* itself, tower by tower, the way a landslide arranges itself in the hour before it lets go. Cumulonimbus stacked on cumulonimbus, anvils flattening out at altitudes that made his neck ache to follow them, and beneath it all a color he had no good name for: not green, not slate, but the bruised yellow-gray of air that has been wrung dry of every gentle thing it ever held.

"Landfall tomorrow night," Nadia said behind him. She was crouched over the charts again, weighting their corners with stones because the wind through the cave mouth kept trying to take them. "Maybe the night after. The fishing crews are pulling everything in. You can hear it."

He could. The lagoon's outer villages had gone quiet in the way harbors go quiet before weather — engines hauled, nets dried, children called indoors by voices pitched just slightly too high. Even the gulls had stopped arguing with each other. The whole coast was holding its breath, and the fleet didn't care.

Through the glasses, the dredge sat in the middle of the lagoon channel like a wound that refused to close. *Iron Meridian*, the flagship's workhorse, forty meters of rust-orange hull and crane arm, its spud legs driven into the reef shelf where it squatted day and night, chewing coral and silt and whatever else the Meridian Star had scattered across two hundred years of silence. Floodlights burned on her deck even now, in daylight, because the fleet's captain wanted salvage running around the clock against exactly the window Nadia was counting down. Barges shuttled between dredge and flagship. Armed men walked the dredge's rails in pairs. And beneath all of it, invisible from this distance but visible enough in Lior's mind, the crack.

He'd seen it yesterday, swimming the cave's seaward throat while Nadia kept watch. A fissure in the reef shelf, running from the dredge's anchor scar toward the deep channel — old damage, probably, worsened by the spuds' pounding, a seam in the living rock as long as a fishing boat and opening and closing with the tide like something breathing. The reef was already dying there. It would not take much to finish it.

It would take a ship.

"Lior." Nadia's voice had the flatness she used when she'd read his face and didn't like what she'd found. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The one you had before you cut the picket line. The one you had when you decided to swim the outer reef alone." She rose, brushing sand from her knees. "Say it out loud. Whatever it is. I want to hear how impossible it sounds before I start believing it."

He lowered the binoculars. His hands were steady, which surprised him, because inside his chest everything was moving very fast and very quietly, the way water moves under ice.

"The dredge," he said. "We sink it in the channel."

* * *

She didn't laugh. That was the first thing he noticed, and the thing that frightened him most. Nadia Voss looked at him for a long moment with those gray eyes that measured everything — distances, tides, men — and then she walked to the chart and put her finger on the channel, and he understood that some part of her had already done this arithmetic and had been refusing to say it aloud.

"Explain," she said.

"The monsoon closes the window. Once it makes landfall, nothing works out here — no dive ops, no barge transfers, no insurance surveyor is going to sign off on salvage in thirty-knot gusts and eight-foot confused seas. The fleet knows it. That's why they're running the lights all night. They're racing us and they're racing the storm, and they think the storm is the only enemy." He moved beside her, his finger tracing the channel's dogleg. "But look at this. The channel is narrow here — maybe sixty meters of navigable water between the reef walls. Deep enough for the barges and the flagship's boats, barely deep enough for the flagship herself at low tide. Everything the fleet needs comes through this one throat. Fuel. Food. Salvaged cargo going out. If the dredge goes down in the channel—"

"You trap them," Nadia said slowly. "Inside the lagoon. With the wreck. With the claim beacon site. Until the monsoon passes and the maritime authorities arrive."

"Until the monsoon passes and their own insurers arrive. Their captain isn't afraid of us. He's afraid of paper. Piracy charges void his coverage, and without coverage his crew is a bunch of armed men looting a grave in disputed waters. We broadcast the log evidence, we seal the beacon, and we strand them on top of the crime scene with no way to run." He heard his own voice and didn't recognize it — calm, almost gentle, the voice he used with clients on the third day of a climb when the summit still looked impossible and he needed them to keep putting one foot above the other. "They mutiny or they surrender. Either way, the hostages walk free and the wreck stays ours until the law gets here."

"And the dredge crew?"

The question landed where questions like that always land, low in the stomach. Lior made himself hold her gaze.

"We sink it empty."

* * *

They spent the afternoon doing what they always did when the plan was insane: making lists. Nadia wrote; Lior talked, pacing the cave's dry back chamber where the tide never reached and the air smelled of stone and old bird nests.

The dredge kept four men aboard at night, six during operations. Watch rotations meant the engine room was manned, but the sea valves — the great flooding intakes that fed her pumps — were below the waterline in compartments that were checked once per watch, not continuously. The hull was old, older than the fleet's paint jobs suggested; Lior had seen the rust weeping from her seams through the glasses, orange tears streaking down black steel. She was a working ship kept alive by maintenance schedules, and maintenance schedules assumed nobody was coming for her with a cutting torch at slack water.

"The problem," Nadia said, tapping her pencil against the chart, "is that sinking a ship is easy. Sinking a ship *where you want it* is the hard part. She'll list, she'll drift, she'll settle wherever the current decides. Sixty meters of channel. If she goes down crooked, half the throat stays open and their boats squeeze past her."

"Then we don't sink her straight down." Lior crouched, took the pencil, drew the channel cross-section from memory — he'd dived it twice now, and his memory for underwater terrain was the one gift the mountains had given him that worked at sea. "We flood her bow compartments first. She goes down by the head, fast, and the current pushes her stern around as she settles. She pivots. Comes to rest diagonal across the throat, like a door swinging shut." He looked up. "I've seen rivers do it. Deadfall in a flood channel doesn't block straight — it blocks *wedge-shaped*, because the water turns it as it drops. The sea will do our steering for us if we give it the right shape to steer."

Nadia stared at the drawing. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, careful, the way people speak near something sleeping. "You're talking about scuttling a hundred-and-fifty-ton vessel in a confined channel during a rising storm, on breath-hold and stolen tools, with a hostile crew topside."

"Yes."

"And getting out."

"That's the part I'm still working on."

* * *

Night came down heavy and windless, the false calm before the squall line arrived, and Lior couldn't sleep. He climbed to the cave's upper ledge where he could see the lagoon, and found the dredge's floodlights burning their ugly white hole in the dark, and sat with his back against stone and let himself feel what he'd been holding at arm's length all day.

Terror. Not the clean terror of a storm or a rockfall — he knew how to be afraid of things that could kill him quickly, and he'd built a life on that knowledge, respected it, negotiated with it like a neighbor whose moods he understood. This was different. This was the terror of a man about to make the wilderness do something cruel on purpose. Every rule he'd lived by — respect the water, don't fight the current, never ask the mountain for more than it offers — said that what he was planning was blasphemy. You didn't *use* the sea. You survived it. You read its moods and stayed humble and came home.

But the village was down there. He'd seen the drowned boats moored in their silent row, ghost-white in the green light, and he'd understood that the sea keeps records no court will ever read unless someone forces the issue. The fleet had drowned a village to hide its theft, and the same fleet was now anchored over the proof, chewing it up for profit, and in six tides — five now — the monsoon would erase everyone's options and the story would end the way stories like this always ended, with powerful men sailing home rich and a drowned nation staying drowned.

The wilderness forgives nothing. He'd learned that at fourteen, in a river that took his brother and gave back nothing, in a valley that blacklisted him for surviving it. Forgiveness wasn't on offer anywhere in the natural world. But mercy wasn't either — and that was the thought that finally settled him, sitting in the dark with the dredge lights burning across the water. The sea wasn't cruel. It was *indifferent*. Indifference could be aimed. Give the storm a reason to be merciless and it would be merciless in both directions, equally, honestly, without malice. The fleet had spent years assuming the ocean belonged to them. Tomorrow night he would introduce them to the other party in that arrangement.

Footsteps on the ledge. Nadia sat down beside him without asking, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"My father used to say the compass doesn't point north," she said eventually. "It points *home*. He said every instrument lies except the ones that answer to blood." Her hand found his in the dark — not tenderly, exactly; more like a climber taking a rope. "If this goes wrong, you drown in that channel and I spend the rest of my life knowing I let you. If this goes right, you're still a man who sank a ship full of sleeping sailors. I need you to tell me you've counted both costs. Because I have, and I can't carry them alone."

"I've counted them." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "The valves are in the after pump room. Four men aboard, two asleep, one on bridge watch, one walking rounds. Rounds take eleven minutes — I timed them last night. The pump room hatch is aft of the accommodation block, away from the lights. I go down at slack water, cut the valve stems' retaining collars, open the floods, and I'm out through the bilge discharge before the compartment is knee-deep. By the time anyone hears water where water shouldn't be, she'll already be answering the helm of the current instead of the fleet." He paused. "And if I'm wrong about any of it, the sea collects the fee. I know the price. I'm telling you I'll pay it."

"Don't say it like that."

"Why not? It's true. It's been true since the river took—" He stopped. Twenty-two years, and he had never finished that sentence for anyone. In the dark, with her hand around his, he found he could. "Since the river took Tam. I was supposed to be watching him. The valley decided I was careless, and the valley's decision followed me onto every permit application, every guide registry, every port manifest for the rest of my life. A man without papers is a man the world has already sentenced." He turned toward her, though he could barely see her face. "This is the first time in my life I get to choose what I'm guilty of. Let me choose well."

Above the lagoon, the first outriders of the squall line slid across the stars, and the dredge's floodlights caught the leading edge of the rain — a silver curtain advancing over the water like something being drawn closed.

* * *

They spent the next day preparing, and preparation was its own strange comfort, the way it always was — the body busy so the mind couldn't scream. Nadia stripped wire from the abandoned fish camp and wound it into lifting slings. Lior tested the stolen cutting torch's remaining fuel, then cursed and tested it again, because the gauge lied and he needed it not to. They ate dried fish and hardtack and drank rain caught in the tarp, and neither of them mentioned that the torch fuel was enough for one attempt, or that slack water tomorrow fell at 0340, or that the swell forecast for the outer reef would make the approach crossing the worst water either of them had ever volunteered for.

At dusk the squall line's vanguard hit — not the monsoon proper, just its herald, thirty minutes of horizontal rain and thunder that rolled through the cave like freight trains coupling. When it passed, the air smelled scrubbed and electric, and out over the lagoon the dredge's lights burned on, stubborn, arrogant, certain that steel beat weather.

Lior stood at the cave mouth and watched the lightning walk along the horizon in long silent strokes, counting the seconds, reading the storm the way he'd once read avalanche slopes — not for whether it would come, but for exactly when.

"Five tides ago you told me the window was closing," he said. "You were right. So we close it first."

Behind him, Nadia began checking the torch a fourth time.

Slack water: 0340. The channel: sixty meters. The margin: none.

He picked up the sling, and the sea, indifferent and enormous, waited to see what he would ask of it.
