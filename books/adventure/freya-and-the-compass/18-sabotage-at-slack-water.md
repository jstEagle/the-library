## 18. Sabotage at Slack Water

The dredge hung at anchor in the channel like a dead thing that hadn't been told yet.

Lior lay on his belly at the lip of the sea cave, salt water lapping his chin, and watched the last light go out of her. She was called *Iron Meridian* — he'd read the name off her stern through Nadia's borrowed lens, and the irony of it had sat in his chest like a swallowed stone all afternoon. The fleet's flagship. The ship whose wake had carried the men who drowned a village and buried a nation's claim under disputed water. She rode low with salvage gear, floodlights burning along her gunwales, and beneath her keel the channel ran thirty feet deep over sand — deep enough for her to leave, if they let her.

That was the whole plan, in the end. Not to fight her. To make sure she couldn't run.

"You're breathing wrong," Nadia said behind him.

He turned. She sat cross-legged in the dark of the cave, the compass open in her lap, its needle trembling toward the lagoon the way it always did when she held it — steady as a plumb line, patient as grief. She'd wound a strip of cloth around her wrist where the reef had opened it two nights ago, and she was watching him with that flat, measuring look she used when she was deciding whether to say something true.

"I'm breathing fine."

"You're breathing like a man rehearsing." She closed the compass. "Rehearsing is how people die down there. You go under already having decided, or you don't go under at all."

He almost laughed. Almost. "Is that an exile's proverb?"

"It's a diver's." She set the compass aside and crawled forward beside him, shoulder to shoulder, both of them looking out at the fleet lights smeared across the black water. "Tell me again."

"The sea valves." He kept his voice low, though the nearest picket boat was four hundred yards off and the wind was rising; the monsoon's outriders were already combing the horizon into long gray ridges. "Four of them, below the waterline, aft. I saw the schematics in the survey barge photographs you took. They're old. Original fittings — the whole fleet runs on refits and prayer. If I open two of them wide, she takes water faster than their pumps can argue with. They'll have to beach her or sink her, and either way she doesn't clear the channel before the tide turns."

"And if they catch you down there?"

"Then I've bought you time to raise the beacon anyway." He said it plainly, the way he'd state a river depth or a weather window, and watched her face do the small complicated thing it did when he refused to flinch. "You get into the lagoon on the last low tide. You seal the site under the old law. Whatever happens to me, the claim stands. That's the arithmetic."

"That's not arithmetic," Nadia said quietly. "That's a man dividing himself by zero."

* * *

They went in at slack water, because slack water was the only hour the sea forgot to be cruel.

Lior had waited his whole life for tides — guided clients around them, timed crossings by them, buried friends because he'd misjudged one by eleven minutes. Slack water was the held breath between ebb and flood, the moment the current stood still and forgave nothing but permitted everything. He slipped off the rocks on the seaward side of the point with his cutting kit strapped tight to his chest and a small tank on his back, and the water closed over him like a door.

Below, the world went green-black and enormous. His torch beam carved a tunnel through the murk, silt hanging in it like snowfall in a headlamp. Somewhere above, the *Iron Meridian*'s hull blotted out the stars — a mile of steel shadow, barnacled along her waterline, her propeller hanging motionless in the gloom like a rusted flower the size of a wagon wheel.

He swam the length of her keel. It was strange work, intimate work, moving hand over hand along the belly of the thing that had hunted them across six tides. He could hear her even here: the thrum of generators through the steel, the groan of a working ship, voices transmitted through the hull as shapeless murmurs, the way you hear a river talking under ice. Men up there playing cards. Men up there sleeping. Men up there who had burned a village and would sleep fine tonight.

He found the first valve housing by feel before he found it by light — a collar of corroded bronze where the schematic promised, furred with weed, warm from the machinery beyond the bulkhead. He got the guard plate off with two strokes of the wrench, and the cutting torch hissed alive in his fist, a blue star burning underwater in its shroud of argon.

The first valve took four minutes. The second took five, because his hands were cold and the fitting fought him, and somewhere in those five minutes the slack water ended.

He felt it before he understood it — a change in the pressure against his body, a slow lean in the silt, the sea clearing its throat. The flood tide, coming home early, shoved ahead of the squall line the way rivers shove ahead of rain. The water around him began to move, gently, then less gently, dragging at his fins, pushing him against the hull.

He was opening the third valve when the compartment above him flooded.

He never heard the decision being made aboard — no alarm reached him through the water, or none he could distinguish from the ship's ordinary noise. What he felt was a shudder run down the keel like a word spoken through a wall, then a gout of bubbles bursting from somewhere near the waterline, silver and violent, and then the third valve housing, half-cut, gave way all at once under his hands as something inside the ship let go.

The sea came in.

Not around him — *through* him, through the space he occupied, a brown roaring column erupting from the ruptured fitting, and it took him the way a river takes a man who has stepped into the wrong seam of current. It slammed him against the hull, rolled him, tore the torch from his grip so that its beam went tumbling away into the green like a falling star. His regulator bucked in his teeth. His gauge console whipped around on its hose and cracked him across the cheekbone, and for one white instant the whole world was impact and noise and the animal certainty that he was going to die inside the machinery of this ship, folded into her like a letter into an envelope.

And then — calm.

It came the way it always came, the old gift the mountains had given him, the thing that made him good at his work and unfit for every other kind. The water roared and Lior's mind went quiet and precise as a level bubble. *Downflooding compartment,* he thought. *Aft hold, probably. They'll have a watertight door. The door will be shut or it won't. Either way the suction dies when the space equalizes.* He counted. One. Two. Three. Four. On five the roar stuttered, gulped, and began to die as the compartment filled and the sea lost its appetite.

He was still holding the valve wrench. He didn't remember deciding to keep hold of it. His hands had decided for him, somewhere below thought, and now they finished what they'd started — three hard turns on the fourth valve, opening it wide while the ship shuddered around him like a struck animal. Two valves cut clean. One torn open. One opened by hand. Four ways for the ocean to come in, and the monsoon twenty miles out and closing.

*Good enough,* he thought. *Nothing is ever perfect. Good enough is what the wilderness pays.*

Then he kicked free of the hull and the *Iron Meridian* began to list.

* * *

He felt it through the water before any surface evidence reached him — a slow, ponderous lean, the great mass of her rolling a few degrees to port, the way a sleeping man rolls toward a sound. Bubbles sheeted up past him in ropes. Somewhere far above, faint as a memory, an alarm began to cry — a thin electronic wail filtered through forty feet of sea, almost gentle, absurdly domestic.

Lior ascended slowly, the way training demanded and terror argued against, bleeding off his depth in careful increments while the wreck of his plan and the wreck of his body negotiated terms. His left arm wasn't answering properly. His cheekbone had opened; he could feel his blood dissolving into the water around his mask, a dark thread whipping away astern. His tank read a quarter. The current was running flood now, hard, shoving him along the hull toward the stern and the open channel beyond it, and if it carried him out past the point he would be swept into the reef passage and the rising sea and that would be the end of the arithmetic entirely.

So he did the only thing that made sense. He stopped fighting the current and swam *with* it, angling for the stern, and let the flood tide carry him down the length of the dying ship like a leaf down a gutter.

The stern passed overhead in stages. First the rudder, vast and weeded. Then the propeller, beginning — God help him — to turn, slow idle revolutions stirring the water into a slow vortex as someone in the engine room answered the alarm. Then the counter, and beyond it open water, gray-green and getting grayer, the squall line visible now even from below as a bruised ceiling pressing down on the world.

He surfaced in the propeller wash.

For three seconds he bobbed there in the churning foam with the screws turning somewhere beneath his feet, close enough to feel the pull of them in his spine, and the *Iron Meridian*'s deck rose above him at its new angle, floodlights swinging wildly, men shouting in three languages, a bosun's whistle shrieking. Nobody saw him. He was a piece of debris, a log, a shadow among shadows, and the ship that had drowned a village could not spare one pair of eyes for the man drowning her.

He swam. Not fast — fast was death, fast was noise and spray and a searchlight sweep — but steadily, side-stroking low in the water, letting the flood do the work, aiming for the black slot of the sea cave two hundred yards north where a woman with a bandaged wrist was watching the water and deciding, minute by minute, whether he was dead.

His arm failed him fifty yards out. He felt it go — a hot wire of pain from shoulder to wrist, the arm simply declining further orders — and he switched to a one-armed crawl and kept coming, because that was the whole of his philosophy and always had been. *You don't get to choose the river. You only get to choose whether you're swimming when it ends.*

The cave took him like a mouth.

* * *

Nadia had a fire going. Of course she did — a tiny, screened flame in the back of the cave, sheltered from the wind, with a pot of water already steaming. She hauled him out of the shallows by his good arm with a strength that always surprised him, stripped his harness, pressed her palm flat against his chest as if checking that the heart was still where he'd left it.

"Listed," she said. "I saw it. Eleven degrees, maybe twelve. The whole harbor lit up like a festival."

"Four valves." His voice came out scraped, barely his. "Two cut, two forced. She's taking water faster than she can pump. By morning she'll be aground in the channel or on the bottom of it."

"Your face is bleeding."

"Is it." He became aware of it properly then, the throbbing heat of it, the taste of copper. He started laughing — quiet, uncontrollable, the laugh of a man standing at the far edge of fear and finding nothing there. "She nearly took me with her. The suction. I counted to five and it let go."

Nadia crouched in front of him, and in the firelight he saw that her eyes were wet, which she would deny later, and that she was angry, which she would deny forever. "You said you'd buy me time. You didn't say you'd spend yourself doing it."

"Same currency." He leaned his head back against the stone and looked up through the cave mouth at the sky, where the first outriders of the monsoon were crossing the stars in long ragged sheets. "How long until the last low tide?"

"Nine hours. Maybe ten, if the storm slows the ebb." She was wrapping his arm without asking, efficient and rough, her hands steadier than her voice. "The skiff can make the lagoon entrance at slack. After that the channel closes for two days."

"Then we go at slack." He closed his eyes. The exhaustion was arriving now, vast and tidal, but underneath it sat the other thing, the calm, still sitting in him exactly where it had settled forty feet down in the dark. He had gone under having already decided, and he had come back up, and the price had turned out to be an arm and some blood and a torch lost to the sea. Cheap. The wilderness had charged him more for less. "Nadia. Listen. When we're in the lagoon — whatever happens on that wreck, whatever the council needs — the beacon goes up first. Before anything else. Promise me."

She was quiet a moment. Through the cave mouth, far across the black water, the *Iron Meridian*'s alarms wailed on, and her list had grown — he could see it now even at this distance, her deck lights tilting against the horizon like a painting hung crooked.

"I promise," Nadia said. And then, softer, almost to herself, watching the flagship settle by degrees into the channel she would never leave: "My father used to say the sea keeps honest books. Every entry gets collected eventually."

Lior looked at the listing ship, at the storm coming down on both of them like a verdict, and found he wasn't afraid. The tide would turn in nine hours. The monsoon would land tonight. And somewhere out there in the dark, the fleet was about to learn what Lior Dunmore had learned young, in a river that blacklisted him before he had a name:

The water always comes in. The only question is whether you've built something worth floating when it does.

He slept for two hours, sitting up, his bad arm bound to his chest — and woke to Nadia's hand on his shoulder and her voice low and urgent in the dark.

"Lior. Wake up. There are lights moving on the point — the fleet's put boats in the water. They know someone opened their valves."
