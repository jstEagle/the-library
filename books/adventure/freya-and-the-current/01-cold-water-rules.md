## 1. Cold Water Rules

There are three rules to salvage work, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

One: the water keeps what it takes. You don't get it back because you're clever or brave or owed. You get it back because you're patient, and even then only what the water feels like surrendering.

Two: never dive a wreck you can't name. Every hull has a history, and history is where bodies live.

Three: when something feels wrong on the river, believe it the first time.

I'd been following rule three for twenty years and breaking rules one and two for nearly as long, and that morning off the Kestrel delta, with the *Magpie* riding low on her lines and forty tons of ferry hull swinging beneath our crane, all three rules sat in my chest like swallowed stones.

"Two minutes!" Sef's voice crackled up from below, bright as a boy's even through the comm static. "She's cutting clean!"

I leaned over the rail. The Kestrel ran green-brown and slow out here where it met the sea, carrying silt from half a mountain range in its arms, and sixty feet down Sef was a pale flicker in the murk, torch beam sweeping along the cut line we'd spent three days placing. The ferry had gone down eleven years back with nobody aboard but her mooring lines and somebody's bad luck—a barge collision at the delta bar, an insurance settlement, a routine job for a crew like ours. Routine was good. Routine paid for diesel and dried fish and Tam's endless war against his own machinery.

"Cutting clean," I repeated into the mic. "Don't rush it. Water's got all day."

"So have we?" Sef asked, and I could hear the grin in it. Nineteen years old, cheeks still round with baby fat he'd grow out of or drown first, and the best set of lungs I'd ever put on a diver. He'd come to us two seasons ago off a fish-processing boat, all elbows and hunger, and Ilsa had taken one look at him and said, *He'll do until he doesn't.* So far he'd done.

Above me, the crane groaned. Tam's voice came through the deck plates before it came through the radio, the way it always did—through the bones of the ship rather than the air. "Hydraulics are warm," he said, appearing at my elbow like a bad smell and a worse opinion, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before the war. "Warm means angry. Angry means she quits on us mid-lift."

"Then we lift slow."

"Slow costs fuel."

"Fuel costs less than a snapped cable dropping four tons of steel through our deck." I didn't look at him. With Tam you never looked at him during an argument; eye contact made him dig in like a tick. "Run her at sixty percent and complain about it. Complaining's free."

"Nothing's free," Tam said, but he went aft, and a minute later the engine note dropped into the low, grudging hum that meant he'd done exactly what I asked while reserving the right to be miserable about it. Twenty-two years he'd been my engineer, back to when the *Magpie* was a coastal freighter with rust for paint and I was a girl who'd bought her with insurance money I still couldn't talk about. He'd fixed every leak, replaced every plate, rebuilt the engine twice, and complained through all of it without one day's interruption. It was how he loved things. Grumbling was Tam's version of prayer.

The cut came free at 0940 with a shudder I felt through my boots, and then the crane took the strain and the ferry's stern section rose out of the Kestrel like something being born wrong—black with silt, streaming weed, gulls screaming around it as if the river were objecting to the surgery. Forty tons of dead ship swinging in the morning light. Beautiful, if you had the sickness for it. I had the sickness bad.

"She's yours," I told Tam.

"She's a pig," Tam said fondly, and began the slow dance of settling her onto the barge cradle, cursing the winch operator, the weather, the ferry's original builders, and the concept of iron itself, in no particular order and with real affection.

That left me on the rail with Ilsa, who stood at the wheelhouse window with her coffee going cold in her hand, not watching the lift at all.

Ilsa watched everything except what was in front of her. That was the job. She navigated by a kind of permanent suspicion, eyes moving across the horizon in sweeps, cataloguing, discarding, returning. Fifteen years on the river police before they'd let her go in one of their quiet purges—the ones where your record comes back spotless and your locker's already cleaned out—and she'd brought the habit with her like a scar. On the *Magpie* it passed for paranoia. Off the *Magpie*, twice now, it had passed for the reason we were all still breathing.

"You see it too," she said. Not a question.

"See what?"

"Don't." She lifted her chin toward the southeast, where the delta channels braided out into the sea through miles of reed and mudflat. "Third time this morning. Same bearing."

I found it. A grey shape sitting low in the haze, maybe four kilometers out, holding position near the channel marker at Gull Point. Patrol boat lines. Navy-grey, though the navy hadn't worked these waters since the river authority absorbed them a decade ago, and the river authority hadn't patrolled past the second channel since the concession filings closed the upper Kestrel two years back.

"Maybe they're lost," I said.

"Navy boats don't get lost."

"River authority, then."

"Authority boats don't sit still for three hours." Ilsa drank her cold coffee anyway, the way she did when she wanted her hands busy and her mouth shut. "They moved when we moved. Stopped when we stopped. They've been mirroring us since dawn, Elodie. Somebody's reading our work order."

The stern section touched down on the cradle with a clang that rang across the water, and Tam killed the crane and immediately started an argument with it about chain tension. Sef broke surface at the swim step a few minutes later, hauling his mask up, grinning through the silt smeared across his face like war paint.

"Cleanest cut of the season! Did you see her come up? Like she changed her mind about drowning." He climbed aboard, dripping, young enough that the cold didn't seem to reach him. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong," I said.

"He's asking why you're both staring at the horizon," Ilsa said.

Sef turned. Squinted. "Is that a patrol boat?"

"It's a boat," I said. "Boats float. Sometimes near other boats."

"Since when do navy boats watch salvage crews?" Sef asked, and there it was—that flicker under the grin, the one I'd been pretending not to see for three hours. Nineteen years old and already learning the oldest lesson of the water: competence doesn't keep you safe, it just makes you interesting.

"Since never," I said. "Which is why we're going to finish the job, get paid, and go home. Tam!"

"I heard." From the engine room, muffled. "We're done here in an hour if the current behaves. Current's not going to behave."

* * *

We spent the hour doing ordinary things loudly and visibly, because that's what you do when you think you're being watched—you perform normalcy like a play with an audience of one grey shape on the horizon. Tam chained the stern section down and swore at it. Sef hosed the mud off the deck and sang something tuneless and obscene. I logged the lift in the work book with a pencil stub, noting times and tonnage in my careful, boring hand, boring on purpose.

Ilsa stood her watch at the window and said nothing for fifty-five minutes.

At 1050 she said, "They're coming."

I looked. The grey shape had resolved into motion, closing at a steady eight knots, no hurry in it, no urgency. Just approach. A patrol cutter, thirty meters, navy-grey with the paint worn to primer at the waterline, and no markings I could read at that distance. No ensign. No registration numbers. A boat with no name, which on the water is a bigger lie than a man with no name, because boats are honest about everything else.

"Everyone breathe," I said. "We're a licensed salvage outfit doing a documented job. We've got nothing but paperwork and mud."

"Speak for yourself," Tam muttered, emerging onto the deck wiping his hands. "I've got contraband opinions."

The cutter slowed a hundred meters off our beam and held there, close enough to see figures on her bridge, far enough that faces stayed smears. A loudhailer coughed once, feedback whining across the water, and a voice came through flattened and official:

"*Salvage vessel, identify yourselves and state your cargo.*"

"Cargo's a ferry stern," I called back, cupping my hands. "Eleven years drowned. What's it to you?"

A pause. Long enough to be insulting.

"*Your work area falls within a restricted notification zone. Be advised that further operations in this sector require concession coordination.*"

"We're outside any concession line," Ilsa called, and her voice had that flat cop-cadence I'd learned meant she was reciting law at someone like ammunition. "Delta waters are public registry. We hold a provincial salvage permit, number and date available on request."

Another pause. Then, mildly, terribly: "*Noted.*"

And the cutter turned away. Just like that. Eight knots back toward the haze, unhurried, unbothered, a dog that had walked over to smell something and decided it wasn't time yet.

We stood on the deck and watched it shrink.

"Well," Sef said finally. "That was weird."

"That wasn't weird," Ilsa said quietly. "Weird is when things don't make sense. That made sense. Somebody wanted us to know we'd been seen, counted, and filed. That's a message. Messages mean somebody's planning something."

"Or some bored officer wanted his lunch," Tam said. But he said it looking at the wake, not at us, and his rag had stopped moving.

I looked at the water. The Kestrel slid past our hull, green-brown and patient, carrying its silt down from the mountains, from the valleys we never worked anymore, from the concession country where the permits had names nobody would say out loud. The river didn't care about patrol cutters or messages or men on bridges with smudged faces. The river just kept what it took.

But standing there, with the diesel ticking as it cooled and forty tons of somebody else's drowning chained to our deck, I felt it settle over me like the mist off the water: the sensation of being watched back. Not by the navy. By the wilderness itself—or whatever wore the wilderness now, some new thing with money and lawyers and grey boats, moving into the old currents the way silt moves, silent and total, until one morning the channel you've run your whole life is marked *closed* and nobody will tell you who closed it.

"Log it," I said finally. "Time, position, description, no markings. If anybody asks, we were never bothered. If nobody asks—" I picked up my cold tea and found it tasted like the river smelled. "If nobody asks, we remember anyway."

Ilsa wrote it down in her small square hand. Sef went back to hosing the deck, glancing southeast between strokes. Tam disappeared below to argue with his engine, and through the deck plates I could feel the *Magpie*'s old heart beating, steady, stubborn, mine.

Three rules. Believe the water the first time.

The water had spoken. I just didn't yet know what it had said.
