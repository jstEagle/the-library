## 2. The Contract Nobody Signs

The man from the delta came back on a Tuesday, which I only remember because Tuesdays were when Tam did the engine teardown and therefore when our whole operation smelled like diesel and grievance. He arrived by water taxi this time, no navy escort trailing him, and he'd traded the pressed shirt for something that pretended to be casual—linen that had been ironed anyway, because some men can't help it.

"Corvan," he said again, offering his hand to nobody in particular until I took it.

"You found us," I said.

"I told you I would." He smiled like that was charming instead of faintly menacing. "Your dock is easy to find. Your reputation was harder."

We sat at the folding table under the tarp where we do all our business, which is to say where we drink bad coffee and argue about money. Corvan declined coffee, which I took as a data point. He produced a folder instead—actual paper, actual leather, the kind of folder that costs more than Sef's dive kit—and laid it flat between us without opening it, the way a card player sets down a hand he already knows wins.

"Before we begin," he said, "I want to say how impressed I was with the Merrow job."

"The Merrow job," I repeated.

"The hull salvage off the delta last month. The freighter with the cracked keel. You recovered two hundred tons of cargo in weather your competitors wouldn't sail in."

I kept my face still. The Merrow job had been three weeks of misery and a near-drowning, and we'd been paid half of what we were owed by an insurance outfit that then ghosted us. But apparently somewhere in the world there existed a file with our name on it, and someone had read it closely enough to know our worst month.

"We got lucky," I said.

"You got good. There's a difference, and I've spent twenty years learning to tell them apart." He opened the folder. "I have work for you. Upriver work."

Tam chose that exact moment to fire the compressor, so the next thirty seconds were lost to mechanical screaming, which I suspect Tam timed deliberately and which I will never prove. When it coughed down to its idle rumble, Corvan hadn't moved. He just waited, patient as silt.

"Upriver," I said. "How far up?"

"Kestrel headwaters. Past the old logging camps." He slid a chart across the table, and I'll admit my eyes went to it before my caution could stop them. It was a proper hydrographic survey chart, annotated in a neat bureaucratic hand, and someone had drawn a circle around a bend of river about ninety miles north of anywhere we'd ever worked. "There's a wreck there. Survey vessel, the *Aldernay*. She went down in a storm last spring with her equipment aboard. My client wants that equipment recovered. Intact, dry, and quiet."

"Equipment," Tam said, appearing at the edge of the tarp with a rag in his hands and grease to his elbows, wearing the expression of a man who has heard a word used as a fig leaf before. "What kind?"

"Hydrographic instruments. Sonar units. Sampling gear." Corvan recited it like a shopping list he'd memorized for a test. "Research-grade. Expensive. The client's insurance settlement depends on demonstrating the loss, and the recovery, of specific items."

"And the client is?"

"A mining concern."

"Which one?"

"One you haven't heard of." He said it smoothly, but not smoothly enough. Nobody who works concessions says *a mining concern* the way normal people say it. Normal people name names, because names are how you get paid. Evasiveness is what you buy lawyers for.

I looked at the chart again. Ninety miles up the Kestrel put us well past the last public ramp, well into territory I knew was stitched over with concession boundaries—I just didn't know whose. The Kestrel above Mile 30 had been closed to general traffic for two years now. Permits, checkpoints, the whole apparatus. We'd all watched the patrol boats go by and made jokes about it and never once asked too loudly, because asking loudly on the Kestrel has consequences.

"The Aldernay went down in a storm," I said. "Spring storms on the upper Kestrel are ugly, sure, but a survey vessel isn't a barge. Those hulls are built for that water. What actually sank her?"

Corvan spread his hands. "The report says storm damage. I'm not the investigator, Ms. Dravenmoor. I'm the man paying for a recovery."

"That's usually the answer that makes me walk away."

"It's also the answer that comes with triple rates."

And there it was. He said it plainly, no coyness, because Corvan understood something fundamental about salvage crews: we are romantics about the water and pragmatists about everything else. Triple rates. I did the arithmetic before I could stop myself—three times our standard day rate, plus equipment fees, plus a completion bonus that would retire the *Marrow's* refit loan twice over. It would cover Sef's certification exams. It would let Tam rebuild the port engine properly instead of nursing it on prayer and scavenged gaskets. It would put a year of breathing room between us and the particular hunger that lives at the bottom of every small business.

Triple rates don't buy labor. They buy silence, or they buy risk, and usually both. I know that. Knowing it and feeling the pull of it are two different animals, and they were both sitting at my table drinking my bad air.

"Terms," Corvan said, and dealt out contract pages like he was dealing solitaire.

I read contracts the way I read water—looking for the thing underneath the surface. Most of it was standard: scope of work, indemnification, payment schedule, the usual armor. Then I hit clause fourteen and slowed down.

*No photography or imaging of any kind within the operational zone. No radio transmission regarding the operation, its location, or its findings, on any frequency, for any purpose, during or after the engagement. All recovered materials remain the exclusive property of the client. Crew will sign non-disclosure instruments binding in perpetuity.*

"No photography," I said aloud.

"Client preference."

"No radio disclosure. Ever."

"Standard for sensitive sites."

"Sensitive," Tam said from his post by the compressor, not even pretending to work anymore. "It's a drowned boat full of sonar boxes. What's sensitive about it?"

"What's sensitive," Corvan said, with the first hint of steel I'd heard from him, "is my client's proprietary information. Survey data has commercial value. If a competitor learned what that vessel mapped before she sank—"

"Then why not hire the company that owns the data?" I asked. "Why hire a salvage crew from the delta?"

"Because the company that owns the data no longer exists as an operating entity, and because you come recommended for exactly this kind of discretion." He tapped clause fourteen. "You want to photograph your own work for your portfolio, take jobs elsewhere. This one pays for silence. That's the product. Everything else is packaging."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one luxury this story eventually costs me everything else to keep: part of me wanted to sign right then. Part of me looked at the numbers and the chart and thought, *ninety miles upriver, one wreck, ten days' work, and we're solvent through next winter.* That part of me is the part that keeps the lights on and the crew fed, and I don't apologize for her. She's the reason any of us eat.

But another part—the part that grew up reading water, that knows the river tells you what it's carrying if you watch long enough—was doing different math. Counting the evasions. A client with no name. A wreck with an implausible cause of death. Photography forbidden on a job where documentation is half of professional practice. Radio silence binding in perpetuity, which is the legal way of saying *you will know something forever that you may never say.* And underneath it all, the plain fact that a man this polished had come to a scruffy delta crew instead of one of the three legitimate heavy-salvage outfits on the coast, any of whom could have mounted this job with better equipment than ours.

You skip the professionals when you don't want professionals asking questions.

"I need to talk to my crew," I said.

"Of course." Corvan closed the folder and stood, unhurried. "I'll be at the Anchorage Hotel until Friday. The offer improves if you answer by tomorrow—it's a seasonal window, and my client is conscious of weather." At the edge of the dock he paused, and gave me a look I couldn't fully parse. Not a threat. Something more like sympathy, which was worse. "Ms. Dravenmoor. Whatever you decide—decide it knowing that I chose you because you're careful. Careful people survive this industry. I'd prefer you survived this job."

He left in the water taxi, and the wake rocked the *Marrow* against her fenders, and none of us spoke until the sound faded.

* * *

We held the vote that night in the wheelhouse, which is where we hold everything that matters, because the wheelhouse is the only room on the boat with a door that locks and a window on the world.

I laid out the terms straight, no varnish. Triple rates. Ninety miles into closed water. No cameras, no radios, perpetual silence, and a client who wouldn't give a name. Then I did what a captain is supposed to do and shut up.

Tam went first, because Tam always goes first; patience is not among his many virtues. "It's a trap," he said. "Flat out. I've been on the wrong side of enough deals to smell one, and this one reeks. No-name client, no photos, no radio—that's not a salvage contract, El, that's a script for a disappearance. We go up that river, we find whatever they actually want us to find, and then we're loose ends with diving certifications." He shook his head. "Money's good. Dead men don't spend it."

"You've spent plenty while dead, as I recall," Ilsa said mildly, and Tam grunted, because that was fair and he knew it.

Ilsa voted last, always, after everyone else had shown their cards. She's our navigator, which means her entire profession is deciding where to go based on incomplete information, and she treats decisions the way she treats charts—with a ruler and a suspicious eye. "The geography bothers me more than the clauses," she said. "Ninety miles up puts us inside the concession belt. Every mile of that water is permitted to somebody. If Corvan's client owns those permits, fine—they can do what they like on their own ground. But if they *don't*—" she turned the chart toward herself and studied the circled bend, "—then we're being hired to operate illegally inside somebody else's fence, and the fence has armed patrols. Either way, clause fourteen starts making sense. They're not hiding the sonar equipment. They're hiding the location."

"From whom?" Sef asked.

"That," Ilsa said, "is the question they're paying us not to ask."

Sef was nineteen that year and the newest of us, and he'd been quiet through the whole discussion in the way he gets quiet—not absent, but coiled, listening with his whole body. When he finally spoke, he didn't argue the trap or the geography. "How much is the completion bonus?" he asked, and when I told him, he nodded slowly and looked at his hands. "My mother's clinic in Port Serrat closes in March. The funding's gone. If I sent her even half of—" He stopped, jaw working. "I'm not saying it's safe. I'm saying I know it's probably stupid and I'd still do it. For that number."

Nobody laughed at him. On the *Marrow*, we don't laugh at honest motives, however naked.

Tam glared at the chart like it had insulted his mother. "Fine. Fine. Here's how I see it. It *is* a trap, probably. But traps pay triple, and we're careful, and careful people survive—his words, and I hate that I remember them. So: we go, but we go paranoid. Cameras hidden anyway. Positions logged in code. And if anything upriver smells worse than the contract does, we abort and eat the loss, and I will personally tell Corvan why in language he can bill me for."

"That's not a yes," I said.

"That's a yes with conditions. It's the only kind of yes I give."

Ilsa folded her hands. "I'll navigate it," she said. "That's my vote. Someone should know the way back out, and I want to see what's up there with my own eyes. Two years of closed river and burned checkpoints—everyone on this coast talks about it and nobody looks. I'd like to look." Which was Ilsa's private reason, and she kept the rest of it behind her teeth, the way she keeps everything.

And me? My reason I kept too. Partly it was the money—I won't pretend otherwise, and the crew deserved to know the money mattered, and they did know. But partly it was the *Aldernay* herself. A survey vessel, sunk in a storm that shouldn't have taken her, in closed water, with a fixer offering fortunes for her cargo and forbidding anyone to look too hard. I've spent my life pulling things out of rivers, and rivers keep everything, and sooner or later everything comes up. If there was something rotten up that bend, I wanted to be the one who saw it first, with my own eyes, before anyone could tell me what I'd seen.

That's not a professional motive. I didn't offer it to the room.

"All in favor," I said.

Three hands. Mine made four.

"I'll call him in the morning," I said, and Tam muttered something about fools and money and went to check the compressor one more time, because that's how Tam prays.

* * *

Later, past midnight, I stood alone on the aft deck watching the delta breathe. The tide was turning; the channel markers swung on their chains; somewhere across the flats a ship groaned against its berth like a thing dreaming. The water was black glass, and under the black glass was everything the river had ever taken, holding it all, giving nothing back until it decided to.

Corvan's folder was under my arm. I'd read clause fourteen four more times by lantern light. *Binding in perpetuity.* I kept thinking about that phrase, turning it over the way you'd turn a shell in your fingers. Contracts like that aren't written to protect secrets. Secrets die with their moment. Contracts like that are written by people who already know what the secret weighs, and who mean to make sure that whoever finds it can never put it down.

Triple rates, I reminded myself. Ten days' work. A winter's breathing room.

Out in the dark, far upriver where nobody from the delta ever went, the Kestrel ran cold and quick over whatever it was hiding, and I felt it watching us the way deep water watches—a patience older than permits, waiting to see what we'd bring it.

In the morning I called Corvan and said yes. He thanked me warmly, and told me to fuel for twelve days rather than ten, because the client preferred margin.

I should have asked him then what kind of work needs margin built into its lies.

I didn't. That's the truth of how these things start—not with a mistake you can point to, but with a hundred small surrenders, each one reasonable, each one paid for in advance at triple rates.
