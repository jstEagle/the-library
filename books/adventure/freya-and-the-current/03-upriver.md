## 3. Upriver

I've navigated the Kestrel for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things about rivers. One: they always win eventually. Two: they remember everything you did to them. Three: when a river starts acting strange, you don't ask it why. You turn around.

We didn't turn around.

I had the chart table folded down and the old paper charts spread over it—paper, because the concession zone ate satellite signals the way Tam ate the last of the coffee ration, without apology and without witnesses. The channel upriver from the delta narrowed fast after Mile 12, threading between gravel bars that shifted every spring flood like furniture moved by a drunk. I'd run this stretch twice before, years ago, back when there was traffic on the upper Kestrel. Barges hauling survey gear, fishing skiffs, a mail boat that ran a weekly loop through the villages with a horn you could hear from two bends away.

There was no mail boat now. There was nothing now. That was the first thing wrong.

"Channel's good," Sef called from the bow, where he stood with the sounding pole like some kind of medieval knight who'd lost his horse and gotten a job. "Four meters. Maybe four and a half."

"Maybe," I said, because optimism was not in my job description. "Call it three and a half and keep calling it that until we're past the bar."

Tam was below, doing something percussive to the port engine, which had developed a knock somewhere around the delta that he insisted was "character." Elodie stood at my shoulder, arms crossed, watching the banks go by with an expression I'd learned to read the way sailors read weather. It wasn't worry, exactly. It was the look of someone counting exits.

"You've been quiet since the patrol boat," she said.

"I'm always quiet. Quiet is what they pay me for."

"They pay you to navigate."

"Same thing. A navigator's whole job is knowing where you are and deciding whether you want to be there." I tapped the chart at a bend marked with a faded pencil annotation in my own handwriting: *shoaling, 2019, trust nothing.* "And right now I'm deciding I don't love where we are."

Elodie didn't answer that. She just kept watching the banks, and I went back to watching the water, and between us we watched enough for the whole crew.

The second thing wrong showed up around Mile 20.

The Kestrel runs tea-colored most of its length—tannin stain off the peat uplands, dark as brewed blackleaf, so clear in the shallows you can count the stones. Except past Mile 20 it wasn't. It was milky. Not silt-milk, which is gray-brown and gritty and settles out overnight in a bucket. This was pale, almost blue-white, opaque as skimmed milk, and it didn't settle. I dipped a cup over the side and held it up to the light and watched nothing happen. Whatever was suspended in it was fine as smoke and patient as death.

"That's not normal," Sef said, appearing at my elbow with the specific gravity of a young man who has just discovered a problem and wants credit for it.

"No, it is not."

"Is it dangerous?"

"It's a river full of something somebody put in it. So yes. In the way that everything upstream of a mine is dangerous." I poured the sample into a glass jar and stoppered it, because Ilsa Voss does not leave evidence floating around uncollected. Eleven years on salvage crews teaches you that the difference between a story and a lawsuit is whether anybody thought to keep the jar.

Tam came up from the engine room wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white, looked over the side, and spat. "Tailings fines," he said. "Ground rock. Somebody's crushing ore upriver and washing the waste straight into the current."

"The concession hasn't even started extraction," Elodie said. "That's what Corvan told us. Pre-extraction survey. Equipment recovery."

Tam shrugged with his entire philosophy: *companies lie, water doesn't.* He went back below. The knock in the port engine continued, characterful.

* * *

The first checkpoint was at Mile 31, or had been. We smelled it before we saw it—burned timber and scorched paint riding downstream on the wind—and then it came around the bend: a low concrete building with a boom gate across a road that came down to the riverbank, the whole structure blackened top to bottom. The gate itself had been cut apart with a torch and left lying in the weeds like a dead snake. Someone had spray-painted something across the checkpoint wall in letters two meters high, but the fire had taken most of it. What survived was one word in the local dialect that I happened to know, because my mother taught me her grandmother's tongue along with her grandmother's grudges.

*Liar.*

"Cozy," Tam said, coming up on deck with a mug of something that smelled like it could strip varnish.

"When was this burned?" Sef asked. He was crouched at the rail, young enough to still be curious about things instead of just afraid of them.

"Look at the regrowth," I said. The weeds around the foundation were waist-high and seeding; the scorch on the concrete had weathered soft at the edges. "Two seasons minimum. Maybe three."

"So the concession closed this river before Corvan ever called us," Elodie said slowly. "Before the survey vessel ever 'wrecked.'"

"Or the locals burned it out and the company let it stay burned," Tam said. "Cheaper than staffing it."

Nobody had anything cheerful to add to that, so I took us through the bend and left the checkpoint behind, though I noticed nobody turned around to watch it go. You can tell a lot about a crew by what they refuse to look at.

* * *

Mile 44 was Harrow Bend, and Harrow Bend used to be a village.

I knew it from the mail boat days—a scatter of stilt houses along the east bank, drying racks hung with fish, kids who raced the mail boat in dugouts and always lost and always laughed about it. I remembered the name of the woman who ran the fuel float there, because she'd sold me diesel at a fair price and told me a joke about tax inspectors that I still use, carefully, in the right company.

Harrow Bend was empty.

Not ruined—empty. The houses stood on their stilts, intact, doors shut, windows staring. The drying racks were bare. The dugouts were pulled up on the bank and stacked upside down in a row, neat as coffins waiting. No smoke from any chimney. No dog. Villages have a sound, even at rest—chickens, hammers, argument, laughter carrying across water. Harrow Bend had the sound of the river slapping against pilings and nothing else, and I have been in graveyards louder than that.

"Buyouts," Elodie said quietly. She'd gone forward to stand beside Sef at the bow. "Corvan's paperwork mentioned relocation easements. Standard clause."

"There's no standard clause that empties forty houses," I said from the helm. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Somebody paid every family here to leave, and paid them enough that they left the houses standing, which means they were paid more than the houses were worth, which means whatever's happening upriver was worth more than a village."

Sef had gone very still at the bow rail. He was looking at the near shore, at the mud line exposed by the season's low water, and I was about to tell him to mind the channel when I saw what he was looking at.

Footprints. Dozens of them, pressed into the dried mud above the waterline, all pointing away from the village. Small ones mixed among the large. A whole community walking down to the water one last time, probably to load their boats, except the boats were still here, stacked and upside down, so maybe they walked down to say goodbye instead. Maybe the company bused them out by road and the footprints were just the last evening, the last swim, the last time anyone stood in the shallows of the river that had fed them for a hundred years.

"Keep us moving," Elodie said, and her voice had the captain's steel in it, but her eyes stayed on those footprints until the bend took them away.

* * *

We made camp that night at Mile 52, in the lee of a gravel island, running anchor lights dim per Tam's opinion that being visible was worse than being found. I drew first watch, which suited me. I don't sleep well in the concession zone. I don't think any of us did, though Tam snored like a landslide anyway, which either meant he slept fine or that his conscience made less noise than his sinuses.

The river talked all night. Rivers do—they slap the hull, gurgle past the anchor line, mutter over the bars. But this stretch of the Kestrel talked differently. Thicker. Slower. The milky water moved with a kind of reluctance, like it was carrying too much weight, and when I dipped my hand in during my watch—purely scientific, purely stupid—the film of it clung to my skin and wouldn't rinse clean. It dried faintly slick, faintly sweet-smelling, like the ghost of a chemical nobody should have been pouring into a living river.

I sat with the jar of samples beside me and the charts under my lamp and I did arithmetic I didn't want to do. If the contamination started two seasons ago, matching the burned checkpoint, then the survey team hadn't discovered the pollution. They'd arrived after it. Which meant the company hadn't hired them to find a problem—it had hired them to certify that there wasn't one. And when the surveyors' instruments refused to lie, well. Ships sink. Rivers rise. Accidents happen, especially to people whose reports are inconvenient.

I'm a navigator. I deal in positions and courses, in the honest geometry of where things are. And every instrument I owned was telling me we were sailing deeper into something with no honest exit.

* * *

It was Sef who found the boot.

Morning of the third day, Mile 61, a long slow reach where the river widened and shallowed and the milky water spread thin over gravel shoals. Sef was at the bow with the pole, calling depths, when he suddenly stopped calling and just stood there, pole planted, looking down.

"Sef?"

He didn't answer. I eased throttle and brought us alongside him, and Elodie came forward, and together the three of us looked down into the shallows at a child's boot.

It was small—smaller than my hand. Rubber, red once, sun-bleached now to the color of old brick, with a crack along the sole and river moss furring the laces. It sat half-buried in the gravel bar in six inches of that pale, opaque water, toe-down, heel-up, exactly the way a boot falls when the foot comes out of it and doesn't come back for it.

"It could've washed down from anywhere," Sef said. His voice was careful, the voice of someone handling something fragile. "Kids lose boots. Kids lose boots constantly. I lost four boots before I was ten."

"Sure," Tam said from the cockpit. He'd come up without anyone hearing him, which was Tam's gift. "And villages empty themselves. And rivers turn to milk on their own. And checkpoints burn themselves." He looked at the boot a long moment. "Get it out of the water, boy. We're not leaving it for the next crew to find."

Sef reached down and lifted it, and water drained out of it in a thin thread, and he held it in both hands like it weighed forty kilos. Nobody said the obvious thing. The obvious thing was that a child had worn that boot in this river, in these shallows, and that the child was not here now, and that no search party had ever come looking, because the people who would have searched had been paid to be elsewhere.

Elodie took the boot from Sef gently and set it on the chart table next to my jar of river water. Evidence, accumulating. A jar of poison and a small red boot, sitting on my navigation charts like two exhibits in a trial nobody had convened yet.

"We're thirty miles from the wreck site," she said. "Anyone wants to vote on turning around, vote now. After the wreck site, we're committed—there's no fuel cache, no safe harbor, no road back. It's the wreck and then the range, or the wreck and then home, and home closes at the next checkpoint."

I looked at the boot. I looked at the charts, at the pencil lines of my own hand marking channels I'd trusted for a decade, in a river that had apparently been dying for two seasons while I ran other waters and heard nothing.

Nobody voted to turn around. Tam checked the engines. Sef went back to the bow. I opened the throttle, and the *Kestrel Queen*—Tam's joke of a name for her, our ugly loyal barge of a salvage boat—pushed on upriver into the milk-white current.

That night, moored at Mile 70 with the mountains of the Serrat Range visible ahead for the first time, blue and jagged and holding the last of the daylight, Sef came and sat beside me at the rail. He didn't say anything for a while. Neither did I. That's the thing about Sef—he's young, but he knows when silence is the conversation.

"My sister had boots like that," he finally said. "Red ones. She wore them until her feet outgrew them and then cried for a week."

"What happened to her?" I asked, because I didn't know much about Sef's life before the crew, only that he'd joined at nineteen with a diver's calm and a hunger to be useful that bordered on religious.

"Nothing. She grew up. She's a schoolteacher now, two hundred miles from any river." He turned the words over. "She got to grow up."

"Yeah," I said. "She got to grow up."

Downriver, behind us, somewhere past the burned checkpoints and the empty villages and the miles of poisoned water, the world we'd come from went on with its ordinary noise. Ahead of us the mountains waited, and beyond them the wreck, and beyond the wreck—whoever had done all this, and whatever they'd do to keep it buried.

At dawn we'd reach the survey site coordinates. At dawn, the questions would start having answers, and I already suspected I'd preferred the questions.

I was right. But that's the next part of the story, and it starts with Elodie going over the side into water that no living thing seemed willing to touch.
