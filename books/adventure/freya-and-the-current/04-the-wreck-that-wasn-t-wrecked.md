## 4. The Wreck That Wasn't Wrecked

The Kestrel up here runs green-brown and cold enough to make your teeth ache through the regulator, and I've been in water like it long enough to know that cold is just the river's way of telling you it doesn't want you there. I dropped down the mooring line at first light with Sef on the second line behind me, because Corvan's contract said two divers minimum on any penetration and even a contract written by lawyers has the occasional good idea.

Visibility was maybe three meters. Four if you were feeling generous, which I wasn't. The sonar had put the survey vessel—the *Meridian Star*, according to the registration numbers Corvan's dossier included, though the dossier spelled it *Meridien* twice, which told me something about how much anyone cared—sitting upright on the bottom at nineteen meters, canted fifteen degrees to port.

She was exactly where they said she'd be. That was the first thing that bothered me.

* * *

Here's what twenty years of salvage teaches you about wrecks: they're honest. A ship dies the way anything dies—messily, unevenly, leaving evidence of the whole process scattered around her. A storm casualty lies where the storm put her, usually broken, always dragged, sand scoured into patterns that read like a confession. A fire leaves charring in a specific grammar. Grounding leaves a wound along one side and a trail of hull paint leading away from it like blood from a cut throat.

The *Meridian Star* looked like she'd been set down by a crane.

Upright. Level, more or less. Sitting in the silt with her keel line straight and her superstructure intact, windows unbroken, railings still bolted in place. Sef drifted alongside me and I saw him turn his head slowly, taking it in, and even through the mask I could see the question forming.

I gave him the flat-palm signal: *hold*. Then I swam the length of her.

Nineteen meters down in a river that floods every spring, you'd expect silting, scour, at least some rearrangement. The spring currents on the Kestrel move house-sized logjams. This wreck had been sitting here—I checked the marine growth later, but even underwater I could see the tell—long enough for a good half-centimeter of silt film over every horizontal surface, and not one thing out of place. No debris field. No trail of contents spilled when she went down. Galleys don't sink with their pots still racked. Wheelhouses don't go under with the barometer still hanging plumb on its bracket.

But hers did. I hung in the green murk outside the wheelhouse window and looked at that little brass instrument swinging gently in my exhaust bubbles, dead level, and felt the thrill of discovery arrive a full minute before the understanding of what it meant caught up with it.

That's how it always works for me. First the hunter's joy—*found it, it's here, it's real*—and then, arriving like cold water through a bad seal, the other thing. The thing the joy was pointing at.

Nobody sinks a ship this neatly by accident.

* * *

We worked the exterior first, per procedure. Sef ran the hull survey while I checked the damage points against the incident report Corvan's people had provided—a boiler explosion, allegedly, in heavy weather, loss of all hands, tragic, insurance settled, move along.

There was no explosion damage.

What there was, was a hole. One hole, low on the starboard side aft, just above the turn of the bilge, roughly a meter across, edges bent *outward*. I put my glove against the metal and traced the fracture lines and my heart started doing something complicated in my chest, because those edges weren't torn the way blast edges tear. They were clean. Sheared. Cut almost, if you wanted to be romantic about it, and I did not want to be romantic about it, because romance underwater gets people killed.

Sef came around the stern and found me there. I pointed at the hole, then made the gesture we use for *shaped charge*—fist, open palm, fist again—and watched him go very still in the water. Divers learn that gesture young and hope never to use it professionally. Scuttle charges are placed by people who want a ship on the bottom, and they're placed by people who are standing safely on another deck when they go off.

A boiler explosion doesn't blow outward through the starboard bilge strake. A boiler explosion makes a ship look like God stepped on it. This was a single, tidy, professional breach, placed below the waterline where it would flood fast and quiet, sized to drop her before anyone aboard could launch a skiff.

I looked through the hole into the engine room. The machinery sat in there gleaming faintly in our lights, coated in silt but whole. No scorched paint. No warped plates. A boiler that exploded leaves a signature you can read from across an engine room.

This one hadn't so much as coughed.

* * *

We went inside through the breach, because that's what we were paid for—Corvan's "survey equipment," whatever survived of it—and because I needed to see more, and because part of me, the part I keep on a short leash, wanted to walk through the rooms where eight people had died and listen to what the walls had to say.

Ships talk. Not mystically—structurally. Everything that happens aboard leaves a record in how things fall, what breaks, what survives. People drowning in a sinking ship leave a record too, and I have seen enough of both to read them, and I would give a great deal to unread some of it.

The *Meridian Star*'s interior read like this: the life raft cradles on the aft deck were empty. Good, you think—they got out. Except the immersion suit lockers were full. Eight suits, eight sets of boots and thermal underlayers, all accounted for, all still stowed. The skiff davits were in their cradles, brake locks engaged. And in the mess, drifting gently in the current of our passage, a coffee mug turned end over end, unhurried, unbroken, settling toward a table that had waited months for it.

They never tried. Whatever happened, it happened fast—but not *explosion* fast, because nothing else in the ship was damaged. It happened *ordered* fast. Or it happened at night, without warning, and the river closed over them before anyone understood the ship was dying.

Or—and this was the thought I kept pushing down like a body in a flooded compartment—it happened slowly, and they knew, and someone made sure they couldn't leave.

I'm getting ahead of myself. At the time, in the dark, breathing compressed air in a dead ship's belly, I filed it all away and kept working. That's the discipline. Feel it later. There's always a later. Sometimes there isn't, but you can't dive thinking that way or you'll never go in the water at all.

* * *

The survey equipment was in the aft lab compartment, and it was junk. Corrosion had eaten the electronics past recovery; whatever data the instruments held lived in memory chips that four months of river water had long since dissolved. We bagged what was worth bagging—Sef took photos with the salvage camera until I remembered clause nine, no photography, and made him stop, and he gave me a look through his mask that said everything Tam had said at the dock and more.

It was while Sef was bagging the lab gear that I noticed the floor.

Silt settles evenly. That's the whole principle of it—it falls out of suspension the same everywhere, given time and stillness. So when you're swimming through a sunken ship and you see a patch of deck where the silt lies thicker than everywhere around it, or thinner, your hindbrain flags it before your forebrain knows why.

Aft of the lab, below the waterline, in a compartment the plans called a void space—no equipment, no access hatch shown on the drawings Corvan's dossier included—there was a rectangle of deck plate cleaner than the rest. Scrubbed-looking. Recently disturbed.

And bolted to the deck inside that rectangle, a strongbox.

* * *

I want to be honest about the next few minutes, because honesty is the only currency I've ever trusted and I spend it grudgingly. My first instinct was to surface. Call it done, bag the junk electronics, collect Corvan's money, and let the river keep its secrets. Twenty years of salvage will teach you that the most profitable word in the trade is *enough*.

My second instinct was the one I followed, and it wasn't brave or noble. It was covetous. Because a strongbox bolted below the waterline in a void space that doesn't appear on the ship's plans is not equipment. It's not cargo. It's something somebody wanted hidden so thoroughly that they sank a ship on top of it, and things like that are either worthless or worth everything, and my hands were already on the bolts before my better judgment finished clearing its throat.

Four bolts. Stainless, new, unlike anything else in that compartment. Someone had installed that box *before* the sinking—installed it knowing the ship was going down, or installing it as part of making the ship go down. The box itself was a hard case, the kind surveyors use for instruments, sealed gasket, pressure-rated. The river had gotten in anyway, a little; everything gets in eventually. But the case had done its job.

Inside: a field recorder, military-grade, foam-cushioned, dry. A spare battery pack still in shrink wrap. And a laminated card with a handwritten label in careful block letters:

**RADIO LOG — FULL RECORD — DO NOT TRUST COMPANY CHANNELS**

Eight words. I read them four times through my mask, my breath loud in my own ears, my exhaust bubbles rising past portholes that showed nothing but green.

Below the recorder, wrapped in a plastic bag, a second item: a portable sample kit, used, with six sealed vials of sediment, each labeled in the same block hand with coordinates and dates. The last date was two days before the *Meridian Star* went down.

* * *

I signaled Sef. He came back through the compartment trailing his lift bag and his curiosity, and I showed him the box, and I watched the same sequence cross his face that had crossed mine—thrill first, horror second, in that order, because we're salvagers and that's the order we're built in.

He pointed at the recorder, then at me, then made the question mark. *Take it?*

Clause seven of Corvan's contract: recover all survey equipment. Clause nine: no photographs. Clause eleven, the one Tam had read aloud at the dock in a voice like a man reading his own autopsy: all recovered materials to be delivered intact and unexamined to the client's representative.

A radio log is not survey equipment. A radio log is testimony. And clause eleven meant Corvan didn't want us to know what we were carrying—which meant Corvan knew, which meant everyone who sent us up this river knew, which meant the polite men with the airboat we'd seen at the delta knew, and the whole architecture of the thing assembled itself in my head while I hung there in the dark with my gloves around a dead crew's last words.

They hadn't hired us to recover equipment. Equipment was the excuse. They'd hired us because divers find things, and they needed to be sure nothing was ever found—or, failing that, to be holding it themselves when it was, along with the only witnesses to its existence.

Us. We were the contingency plan for the evidence. Recover it, deliver it, disappear it, and then, presumably, be disappeared ourselves, quietly, somewhere upriver where the concession borders nobody checks.

I looked at Sef. He looked at me. And neither of us said a word, because you can't say a word at nineteen meters with a regulator in your mouth, and also because we had already decided, both of us, in the same instant, without discussing it, and we both knew the other one knew.

I closed the box. Stowed it in my gear bag, under the junk electronics, where a box that size wouldn't show. And we went up the line together, slow, watching our decompression, two professionals doing a routine job, while below us the *Meridian Star* sat level and neat on the riverbed with her life rafts unused and her suits unstolen and her boiler unexploded, keeping the rest of her secrets the way the Kestrel keeps everything—coldly, patiently, and for free.

* * *

Tam met us at the surface ladder. He took the gear bags without a word, hauled them onto the deck of our own boat, and then he looked at my face—just my face, nothing else—and I watched him understand it, because Tam reads faces the way I read wrecks.

"Elodie," he said. "What's in the bag?"

"Survey equipment," I said.

He held my eyes for a long moment. Somewhere behind him, Sef was stripping his tank rig with hands that weren't quite steady, and Ilsa stood at the helm with the engine idling, watching the treeline the way she'd been watching it since the delta, and the river slid past us, green and opaque, carrying its milky secret toward the sea.

"Right," Tam said finally. "Survey equipment." He slung the bags below decks, and his voice, when it came again, was quieter than the water. "Then I suggest we finish the job and get paid."

Which is how I knew he'd already opened the bag.
