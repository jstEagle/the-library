## 5. Voices in the Box

The strongbox came up on the third lift, and I was the one holding the line when it broke the surface, so I got to feel its weight before anyone told me what it meant.

Two hundred pounds of steel and secrets, swinging on the crane cable like a hanged man. Water sheeted off it in ropes. It had been bolted below the waterline of that survey vessel—bolted, not stowed—and Elodie had spent forty minutes of bottom time finding it with her hands in the dark while I hung at twenty feet watching my gauge needle and trying not to think about how cold the Kestrel ran this far upriver. Colder than it should be. The river up here ran milky, like milk poured into glass, like something ground too fine suspended forever in the current. Tam said it was rock flour from glaciers. Tam said a lot of things.

"Easy," Elodie called from the water's edge, mask pushed up on her forehead, voice muffled through the regulator she hadn't quite cleared yet. "Deck's slick. Let it down slow."

I let it down slow. The box settled onto the deck plates of our own boat with a clang that went through my boots and up my spine, and for a second nobody moved. Then Tam was there with a pry bar and Ilsa with a light, and Elodie stood dripping over it like she'd given birth to it, which in a way she had, because she'd known where to look when the rest of us would've stripped the wheelhouse and called it done.

"That's not survey equipment," Ilsa said.

It wasn't. Survey equipment is sonar units and sample cases and GPS rigs, things with handles and labels. This was a ship's safe, the old kind, the kind with a dial you spin and a handle you throw your shoulder into. Somebody had welded a mounting bracket to its base and bolted the whole assembly to a frame girder below the waterline, which is not where you put anything you ever want to see again. That's where you put things you want the river to keep.

"Corvan said equipment," Tam said. He said it flat, the way he says everything, but I've worked with him long enough to hear the question underneath.

"He did," Elodie said. She looked at me. "Sef. You're smallest. Get your hands in the gap."

Which is how I ended up flat on my back on a wet deck with both arms buried to the shoulder inside a dead ship's safe, fishing around in silt and cold iron, while three people I trusted stood over me waiting to find out what we'd actually been paid to steal.

My fingers found paper first. Bundles of it, swollen soft as lungs. Then something hard and rectangular with a rubberized casing, and my heart did a strange thing—not speeding up, slowing down, going quiet and careful, the way it does right before a dive when the water goes still.

"I've got something," I said.

* * *

We dried what could be dried and laid it out in the wheelhouse under the running lights, because night had come down while we worked and none of us suggested stopping. The paper was log sheets, mostly gone to pulp, ink bloomed into gray ghosts. But the recorder survived. It was a field unit, the kind mining companies issue their survey teams—ruggedized case, hand crank for charging, a radio log function that stamps every transmission with date, time, and frequency. Someone had wrapped it in oilcloth and packed it in grease before sealing it in the box. Someone had wanted it to last.

Someone had also, I noticed, drilled the case full of holes so it couldn't float if the box ever failed. Belt and suspenders. Drown it twice.

Elodie turned the crank. The battery indicator crawled up from nothing, and the little screen flickered, and there they were: forty-one logged transmissions, timestamped across eleven days, all from the *Meridian Star*, all addressed to the concession office frequency that didn't officially exist.

"Play them," Tam said.

"Could be nothing," Ilsa said. "Could be boring logistics."

Nobody believed her and she knew it. She'd already pulled her chair around to face the speaker.

Elodie pressed play.

The first entries were exactly what Ilsa hoped for. Boring. A team lead named Okonkwo reporting sample intervals, weather, a broken winch. Voices with static in them, tired voices, people doing a job at the end of a long day. I found myself relaxing, almost disappointed. Salvage is mostly disappointment; you get used to building your hopes out of scrap.

Then entry nineteen, day six, and the boredom died.

*"—repeat, tailings pond two is compromised. Seepage into the aquifer channel, we're reading heavy metals at—I don't have a number, the meter maxes out, it just maxes out. Requesting immediate suspension of operations and downstream notification. This reaches the Kestrel in weeks. Weeks, do you copy?"*

The reply wasn't logged—the recorder only kept outbound—but you could hear the shape of it in the pauses. Okonkwo transmitting again an hour later, angrier, wetter-sounding, like he was standing in rain or maybe just sweating through his shirt in the cold.

*"I am not reclassifying anything. You want me to call it seasonal variance? Call it yourself. I'm putting my name on what's in that water."*

Entry twenty-four: *"Downstream village wells are testing positive. Children are drinking this. Do you understand me? I need written orders if you want silence, and I will keep copies."*

And then the entries changed. Shorter. Flatter. A different voice sometimes—a supervisor type, corporate-smooth even through the static—transmitting summaries instead of reports. *"Operations proceeding normally. Sample results within tolerance."* The word *tolerance* doing terrible work. And threaded between those, Okonkwo's transmissions getting more desperate, more clipped, like a man who knows he's being edited out of his own expedition.

Day ten, entry thirty-eight: *"They've recalled our extraction flight. There's no weather on this mountain. They're leaving us here. If anyone receives this, we are moving overland toward the pass at—"*

Static ate the coordinates. Or someone had eaten them. I'll never know which, and that's its own kind of wound.

Entry forty. Day eleven, 0412 hours. Just breathing for the first few seconds, and a whisper: *"Engines on the river. Not ours."*

Then screaming. I won't dress it up or make it literary. It was screaming, several voices, and something loud and mechanical over it, and it lasted eleven seconds and cut off mid-breath, mid-word, a sound amputated, and then the recording just sat there hissing into the wheelhouse while the four of us didn't move.

Elodie stopped it. Her thumb hovered over the button a long time before she pressed it.

* * *

Here's what I know about myself: I'm twenty-three years old, I took this job because salvage pays better than the canneries and because underwater is the only place my head gets quiet, and until tonight the worst thing I'd ever witnessed professionally was a drowned snowmobile. I am not equipped for this. I want to say that plainly, because everyone in that wheelhouse was pretending to be equipped, and the pretending was almost worse than the recording.

Tam broke first. He got up, went out onto the deck, and stood at the rail smoking, which he doesn't do anymore, which tells you everything. Ilsa sat very still with her hands flat on the chart table like she was pinning it down against weather. And Elodie played entry forty again. Twice. Listening for the engines, counting seconds, doing the thing she does—turning horror into data, because data can be used and horror just sits in you like swallowed hook.

"They scuttled it," she said finally. "Clean charges along the keel line. We saw the cuts. They sank a ship with eight people's proof bolted to its belly and figured the river would hold it until the permits lapsed."

"And then it's theirs," Ilsa said quietly. "All records inside the concession boundary. Legal erasure. Corvan isn't paying us triple rate to recover equipment. He's paying us to be the ones who touch it last."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark the river kept doing its work, patient, carrying whatever it carries.

"So we put it back," I heard myself say.

Everyone looked at me. I hadn't planned the words; they'd come up on their own, like a diver coming off the bottom too fast.

"We seal it back up, we sink it deeper, we tell Corvan the wreck's a bust. Storm damage, nothing recoverable. We take the kill fee and we go home." My voice cracked on *home*, which embarrassed me. "Because whoever did this drowned eight people, and we just pulled their murder weapon out of the water, and now we're holding it, and I don't—I don't want to be holding it."

Silence. The bad kind, the kind with weight.

"Sef," Elodie said, gentle, which was worse than if she'd shouted. "If we put it back, it stays lost. Those people stay a storm casualty. Every kid drinking that water stays a statistic with no author." She turned the recorder over in her hands. "You think I haven't thought about putting it back? I've thought about throwing it in the Mariana Trench. But we've heard their voices now. Okonkwo put his name on it. He said that on the record—he *insisted*. Men who insist on their names deserve to have them read out loud."

"That's not our job."

"No," she agreed. "Our job was hull salvage at triple rate. Our job stopped being our job about an hour ago."

Tam came back in smelling of smoke and river. He looked at each of us, then at the recorder, and said, "Security boat passed the point twenty minutes ago. Running dark. Slowed down when they passed us." He tapped ash over the side. "So the question's already answered for somebody. Question is whether it's answered for us."

That's the moment it landed, really landed, the moral vertigo of it—not the horror of the recording, I could survive the recording, but the arithmetic underneath it. Eight people had died because they refused to be quiet. We were now in possession of the exact object their deaths were purchased to destroy. Which meant every hour we held it, we weren't salvagers anymore. We were evidence. Living, breathing, subpoenaable evidence, and there are only two ways evidence gets resolved: it gets entered into the record, or it gets destroyed.

I looked at my crewmates and understood that we were all doing the same math and arriving at the same terrifying sum, and that no one was going to say it out loud, because saying it out loud made it real and binding.

"First light," Elodie said. "We run downriver. All the way down. And somewhere with signal, we find out who still answers on the emergency bands." She closed the recorder's case and set her palm flat on it, the way you'd steady a wounded bird. "Until then, nobody sleeps deep."

I drew first watch, which I requested, because I couldn't have slept anyway. I sat in the bow with a thermos of coffee gone bitter and watched the milky river slide past, thinking about Okonkwo's voice saying *I'm putting my name on it*, thinking about how the last thing that man did on this earth was scream into a machine, and the machine had waited under sixty feet of cold water for strangers to come crank it back to life.

Around two in the morning, I saw the lights.

Not the security boat—these were on the ridge above the east bank, three of them, spaced evenly, stationary. Watching. Nobody posts three lights on a wilderness ridge at two in the morning unless they want whoever's on the river to know they're seen.

I didn't wake anyone. I just sat there with my heart going like a bilge pump and understood, with total clarity, that the choice Elodie thought we'd made at the chart table had already been made for us, days ago, by a man named Corvan with a smooth voice and a contract nobody signs.

We weren't the crew anymore.

We were the loose ends.
