## 6. First Contact

I was elbow-deep in the compressor when the airboat came, which is how I'll always remember it — with my hands full of someone else's machinery and grease up to my forearms, the way I've spent most of my life.

The sound reached us before the boat did. Airboats don't sneak. They announce themselves like a drunk uncle at a wedding, that flat propeller roar bouncing off the water and the drowned trees, getting louder until conversation on deck just stops being worth the effort. Sef was up top rinsing gear. He shaded his eyes, squinted east down the channel, and said, in the tone of a man remarking on weather:

"Company."

Ilsa came out of the wheelhouse with her binoculars before he'd finished saying it. That's Ilsa. She doesn't run toward trouble or away from it; she just gets a better look at it than anyone else, faster, and then decides what the rest of us are allowed to feel about it.

"Airboat," she reported. "Flat-bottom, twin rudders, concession grey. Two men visible. One of them has a long gun slung, not held." A pause while she refined the picture. "Not held is supposed to be friendly."

"Supposed to be," I said, and wiped my hands on a rag that had been black since before Sef was born, because there wasn't any point pretending to be anything but what we were: a salvage crew caught red-handed doing salvage work, in a river nobody was supposed to care about, being visited by men whose salaries came out of a mining budget nobody would name.

Elodie came up from below. She'd been logging dive times, or so she claimed; I'd have bet the boat she'd been sitting in the dark listening to that strongbox recording again through headphones, wearing her face down to something smooth and unreadable. She looked at the airboat for maybe three seconds.

"Tam, kill the compressor noise. Sef, get below and stay there — you're the youngest face we've got and I don't want them counting heads and liking the arithmetic. Ilsa, logbook open, make us boring." She rolled her shoulders once, like a woman settling into a dress that didn't quite fit. "And somebody put the kettle on. Nothing says 'honest working crew' like tea nobody wants."

* * *

Here's a thing about me that isn't a secret to anyone who's worked with me longer than a week: I don't trust smooth. I'm an engineer. Smooth is what surfaces look like right before they shear. Give me a weld with a little ripple in it, a bearing with a whisper of complaint, a river with an eddy where the chart says none should be — those things tell you the truth. It's the pretty ones that lie.

So when the airboat swung alongside our hull with the delicacy of a man who'd done it a hundred times, and the tall one at the helm lifted two fingers off the throttle in a gesture that was almost a wave, almost an apology, my back teeth started their slow grind.

"Morning," the tall one called. He was maybe forty, sun-cured, wearing a concession security windbreaker over a flannel shirt, and if you cropped the jacket out of the photograph he could have been any fisherman on any river from here to the coast. The gun across his chest ruined the crop. "Voss. Concession security. This is Reyes." The second man was younger, broader, and stood the way men stand when they want you to notice they're standing well. His rifle stayed slung. His eyes didn't.

"Morning," Elodie called back, easy as Sunday. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"So are you." Voss smiled like it was a joke between neighbors. "Salvage?"

"Hull work." Elodie nodded at the Kestrel's Daughter, our barge, our home, our whole floating argument for why we weren't rich. "Compressor blew a gasket two days back, and we found some plating on a client's wreck needs patching before we can lift it. You know how it is. Everything downstream of everything else."

"You're working a wreck?" Voss's voice stayed pleasant. Pleasant the way ice is pleasant until you step on it. "Which one?"

"The Meridian Star. Went down in the spring flood, up past the narrows. Client's an insurance outfit. They want the winch off her before the season turns." She said it all without a hitch, without a breath in the wrong place, and I stood there holding my rag and thought: *she's rehearsed this.* Not that morning. Not after we opened the box. Before. Somewhere between finding that scuttle charge cut clean as a butcher's cut and surfacing with the coordinates of a strongbox bolted under the waterline by hands that wanted it forgotten, Elodie Dravenmoor had already decided what story we were telling, and she hadn't told a single one of us.

I want to be fair to her here, because history will probably not be, and because I owe her more than fairness. But let me lay it out the way I saw it from the deck, with company eyes on me and my heart going like a bad pump.

There was no Meridian Star. There was no insurance client. There was a survey vessel called the *Aldernay* sitting in forty feet of cold brown water with her hull opened by charges placed by professionals, and there was a sealed steel box in our hold that talked in dead men's voices. And our captain was lying to armed men with the fluency of a woman reading her own handwriting.

"Meridian Star," Voss repeated. He said it slowly, tasting it. "Don't know her."

"Small boat. Local contract." Elodie shrugged. "Most of them are."

"And your client? Insurance outfits usually clear their recoveries through the concession office. Liability reasons." He smiled again. "Paperwork reasons."

"You'd have to ask them. We're hired hands. They point, we pull." She tipped her head toward the kettle steam drifting out of the galley hatch. "Can I offer you tea? You look like men who've been running that thing since dawn."

For a moment — and I've replayed this moment more times than I've replayed most moments in my life, and I've replayed plenty, being a man who lies awake cataloguing mistakes the way other men catalogue debts — for that moment I genuinely couldn't tell whether Voss was going to take the tea or take the boat.

He took the tea.

* * *

They came aboard one at a time, which Ilsa later told me was procedure — never both boots off the ground at once, never both guns out of reach of their own hull. Voss sat on our deck bench with his mug cradled in both hands like he meant it, and asked questions that sounded like nothing.

How long on the river. Where based. Delta side, Elodie said, true enough. Crew of four, she said, and my stomach dropped through the deck plates, because we were five, and Sef was below decks breathing through his teeth, and she'd just told an armed man our number and made it smaller.

But Voss didn't blink. Maybe he hadn't counted. Maybe he had, and filed it. Maybe that was the test and she'd failed it and we'd all find out later, at speed, in the dark. That's the thing about these conversations — you don't learn the results for days, sometimes never, and meanwhile you go on breathing like the outcome was ever in doubt.

Reyes walked the deck. Slowly. He looked at the crane, at the dive flags, at the compressor I'd been strangling, and he looked longest at the hold hatch, which was shut, and dogged, and had a coil of chain rope piled on it that I had personally piled there twenty minutes earlier without knowing why, just because the pile needed building and that spot needed weight. Instinct, Tam. Sometimes instinct is just fear with calluses.

"Good set-up," Reyes said. He had a voice like gravel poured slow. "You do much work inside the concession?"

"First time up this far," Elodie said. "River's quiet up here. Quiet's good for work."

"Quiet's good for lots of things." He let that sit. Then, mildly, terribly mildly: "Hear anything odd lately? Radio chatter, flares, anything?"

The kettle hissed behind me. Ilsa, at the wheelhouse rail with her logbook, turned a page with a paper sound loud as a shot.

"Not a thing," Elodie said. "We keep the radio off mostly. Saves the battery, saves the headaches. River's better company anyway."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a man closing a ledger, and went back to stand beside Voss, and the two of them had a silent conversation that lasted three seconds and contained, I am certain, entire paragraphs.

"Well," Voss said, standing, handing back his mug with another of those neighborly smiles. "Thanks for the tea. Listen — you're inside active concession waters, so a few housekeeping items. No photography of shoreline installations. No radio transmissions on any band without prior clearance. If you come across debris, equipment, anything that looks official — survey markers, instrument packages, government property — you report it to the concession office at Mile 12 and leave it where it lies." He paused at the rail, one boot on his deck, one on ours. "There's been some loss of company property upriver. We're trying to account for it. You understand how accounting works."

"I do," Elodie said. "It's why I got into salvage. Everything's accounted for eventually. Just a question of who does the counting."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes then — not anger, not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man meeting another professional in an unexpected place. "Safe diving," he said, and dropped down into the airboat, and the propeller roared, and the flat grey hull slid away downriver into the mist off the water, taking its silence with it.

We watched it go until the sound was gone. Then longer.

* * *

Sef came up the ladder white as a drowned thing. "Did they buy it?"

"Get the anchor watch," Elodie said. "Then yes."

"They counted heads, Elodie." Ilsa's voice was quiet and level, which anyone who knew her understood was the loudest she ever got. "You told them four. You told them four while Sef was ten feet below their feet. If they come back—"

"They won't come back tonight."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Elodie said. "It isn't." She set her mug down on the bench with a small precise click, and looked at the three of us, and I watched her decide — watched it happen, live, the way you watch a breaker throw — how much truth she was going to spend.

Here's what I knew already, and I'll own it: I'd known since the night before. Since she'd surfaced from the *Aldernay* with that look on her face, the one she gets when she's found something in the dark that the daylight won't forgive. Since she'd had Ilsa rig the tarp over the hold hatch and posted Sef on watch and said, "Nobody opens that box again until I say," in a voice that closed doors. She'd decided then. Decided that whatever was in the box, we were keeping it, and keeping it meant lying, and lying to men with rifles meant she'd already priced out what happens when the lie fails. She'd done all that math alone, in the dark, in her cabin, and then come up on deck and made tea like a woman with nothing heavier on her mind than gasket schedules.

A captain's prerogative, you could call it. Command means carrying decisions so the crew doesn't have to. I've heard the speech. I've given parts of it myself, back in the years I don't testify about.

But standing there on my own deck, with the mist still hanging where the airboat had been, I felt the shape of it plain: she hadn't protected us from the decision. She'd made it without us, and now we were going to live inside it whether we'd consented or not, and the only mercy she was offering was that we'd never have to say aloud what we'd chosen. Because choosing out loud is a different act. Choosing out loud makes fingerprints.

"Tam," she said. "That chain rope on the hold hatch. Your idea?"

"It needed piling somewhere."

Her mouth did the thing that on a less careful face would have been a smile. "Keep it there."

"The box stays aboard?" Ilsa asked. Flat. Testing.

"The box stays aboard," Elodie said. "Under the ballast matting, port side forward, under the spare anchor chain. If anyone boards us again, it's scrap iron in a rusted crate and it's been there since the delta, and Sef will swear to it, and Tam will swear to it, and Ilsa—" she met her navigator's eyes "—will have logged it there three weeks ago, because Ilsa's logbook is always right."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked up her pen, opened the logbook, and wrote something, and dated it, and blotted it, and I did not ask to see the date she used. That was the moment I understood we'd all voted. Nobody raised a hand. Nobody said the word. But Ilsa falsifying a log is Ilsa signing a confession, and she did it standing up, in daylight, without being asked twice.

And Sef — young Sef, who found a child's boot in the shallows two days ago and hasn't been the same boy since, who asked me last night in the engine room, quietly, "Tam, what if the people on that ship didn't drown? What if they were drowned?" — Sef just nodded and went aft to take the anchor watch like a man reporting for a shift he'd already agreed to work.

So that was the vote. Unanimous, unspoken, unrecorded except in ink that was already a lie and in the particular way four people stopped looking at each other and started looking at the same stretch of empty river.

* * *

I went below that evening to check the box. Not to open it — I'd heard enough of it through a bulkhead to last me — just to confirm the ballast matting sat right, that the chain above it read as ordinary clutter, that if Reyes or his kind came back with crowbars they'd find forty minutes of honest labor between them and the voices.

The engine room was warm and smelled like diesel and my whole life. I knelt by the crate with my palm flat on its lid, the way you check a patient for a pulse, and I'll admit what I felt, because this chapter of my life deserves honesty even if I gave the rest of it to worse causes: I was scared. Properly scared, the old kind, the kind from before I learned to be dangerous instead. Because I'd heard the last entry on that recorder. We all had, that first night, before Elodie locked it away. A man's voice, reporting contamination numbers that would end careers and start trials, and then the numbers stopping, and then shouting, and then water sounds, and then nothing. Forty feet down, in a box bolted shut by the men who made the nothing happen.

And now that box rode in my engine room, under my chain, in my care, and two polite men in grey jackets had already asked the exact right question about it, and our captain had lied to them beautifully, and the beautiful lie had a lifespan measured in hours or days at best.

Elodie found me down there. She came down the ladder quiet, sat on the tool chest across from me, and for a while neither of us said anything, because we're both the kind of people who think silence is a form of conversation.

"You're angry," she said finally.

"I'm thinking."

"You grind your teeth when you're angry and you clean tools that are already clean when you're thinking, and you're doing both, so." She nodded at the wrench in my hand. Third time I'd wiped it. "Say it."

"You decided before you told us." I set the wrench down. "The lie, the plan, the box staying aboard — all of it, cooked and served, and we got handed forks. That's not a crew, Elodie. That's passengers."

She took that without flinching, which is either courage or practice. "If I'd put it to a vote, you'd have voted yes."

"That's not the point."

"It's entirely the point. You'd have voted yes, and then every one of you would carry the vote like a stone, and when it goes wrong — and Tam, it may go wrong — you'd spend the rest of your lives knowing you chose it. This way you get to be angry at me. Anger's lighter." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time all day she looked tired, actually tired, the smooth surface scuffed down to the grain. "I need you angry at me and loyal to each other. That's the configuration that survives. I've seen the other one. It doesn't."

I looked at her a long time. The compressor ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above us Sef's boots crossed the deck, steady, on watch.

"And if I'd voted no?" I asked.

She held my eyes. "Then I'd have put you and the box ashore at Mile 20 with enough fuel to get home, and I'd have gone on without you, and I'd have hated every mile of it. But I'd have done it. Because that box is the only true thing left of eleven people, and it doesn't get to die in the dark just because the dark is well-armed."

Eleven people. She'd counted. Of course she'd counted.

I picked the wrench back up and wiped it a fourth time, and put it away, and said the only thing left to say, which was: "Chain rope stays on the hatch. And Elodie — next decision this size, you wake me. Even if I vote wrong. Especially if I vote wrong. That's what crew means."

She stood, and offered her hand, and I took it, and that was the whole ceremony.

"Next decision this size," she said, climbing the ladder, "there won't be time to wake anybody."

I laughed, because it was funny, in the way gallows humor is funny — which is to say not at all, which is to say completely.

She was right, of course. She usually is, God help us. Because two nights later, Ilsa's satellite phone rang with a voice from the delta that paid our wages, and everything Elodie had decided alone turned out to be only the first draft.
