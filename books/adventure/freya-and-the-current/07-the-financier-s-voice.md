## 7. The Financier's Voice

I've always trusted machines more than people. A compass doesn't lie to you to spare your feelings. A depth sounder doesn't smile while it's planning to drown you. So when the satellite phone crackled awake at 0300 with a call from a number that wasn't on it—because we'd never dialed Corvan from this handset, and he shouldn't have had this number at all—I felt my stomach drop through the hull before my brain caught up.

The boat was anchored in a backwater of dead channels, engine cold, running lights off, everything dark except the chart table where I sat pretending to review a course I already knew by heart. Sleep hadn't been coming easy since the strongbox came up out of the Kestrel. It doesn't come easy for any of us now, but I'm the navigator; I'm used to lying awake doing arithmetic against disaster. That night the arithmetic kept producing the same ugly sum: Corvan knew too much about where we were.

"Answer it," Tam said from the doorway. He'd heard the ring too. Of course he had. The man sleeps like a cat with one ear rotating.

Elodie was already there, hair tied back, face composed into something neutral and professional. She picked up the handset before the third ring, which is a discipline—you never want whoever's calling to know they woke you.

"Go ahead."

Corvan's voice came through the little speaker with that oiled patience I remembered from the contract meeting, the voice of a man who has never once hurried because he's never once needed to. "Captain Dravenmoor. Glad I caught you."

"You have a strange definition of *caught*," Elodie said. "It's three in the morning."

"Time zones," he said pleasantly, which was a lie, because we were in his time zone. "I wanted to confirm your timeline. You're what, two days behind schedule?"

"Weather," Elodie said. "River's low in the side channels."

"Mm." A pause, long enough to be deliberate. "And the equipment? Recoverable?"

Here it was. The moment where the whole shape of our lives got decided by how Elodie answered one question. I watched her hands on the chart table, steady as a surgeon's.

"Some of it. Water damage. We're cataloguing."

"Cataloguing," Corvan repeated, tasting the word. "Good. Thorough. That's why I hired you." Another pause. "One more thing, Captain. There's been a change to the security arrangement. The concession's protection detail will be conducting a compliance inspection of your vessel within the next twelve hours. Standard procedure. They'll want to see everything you've recovered."

"Everything," Elodie said flatly.

"Everything. And Captain—" and here his voice did something I'll be chewing on for the rest of my life, something almost gentle, almost regretful, a man apologizing to a lamb about the weather at the slaughterhouse—"—my advice is full cooperation. These are not flexible people. And the terms of your contract, section nine, do authorize them to act on my behalf regarding material breaches."

"Material breaches," Elodie said.

"Undisclosed recoveries. Concealed cargo. You understand."

"I understand," she said, and hung up without saying goodbye, which told me everything about what she thought her odds of needing pleasantries with Corvan again were.

For a moment nobody moved. The river lapped at the hull. Somewhere across the black water a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"He gave us twelve hours," Tam said finally. "That's not an inspection window. That's a courtesy countdown."

"He said *act on my behalf*," Sef said. He was sitting on the bench by the dive locker, knees up, looking younger than his twenty-three years and older than me. "Act how? What does *act* mean in contract language?"

"It means whatever the man with the gun decides it means," Tam said. "Section nine is a kill clause dressed in paperwork. I've seen it before. Fishing fleet up north, years ago. Crew found something in a net they weren't supposed to find. Contract had a clause just like it. Boat burned at anchor with everyone aboard. Official finding: stove malfunction."

"And nobody asked questions?" Sef said.

"Nobody was left to ask them."

I looked at Elodie. She was staring at the dead handset like it might say something else, something better. When she spoke, her voice was level, but I've navigated beside this woman through six seasons of bad water, and I can read the currents under her surface the way she reads mine.

"They're coming to search the boat," she said. "Which means right now, they believe the recorder might still be aboard. Which means right now, we still have a choice they don't know we've made."

"And what choice is that?" Tam asked, though I think he knew. I think we all knew. There are moments on a crew when a decision gets made collectively without anyone calling a vote—the same way we'd all chosen silence when the airboat came, nobody saying it aloud, everybody knowing. This was one of those. The recorder with the screaming man on its last entry was ours now. Not Corvan's. Not the company's. Ours, in the way that a debt is yours, in the way that a grave is yours.

"We hide it," Elodie said. "We hide it so well that when they tear this boat apart and find nothing, they have to decide whether we're liars or whether the box never existed. And either way, we stay alive long enough to get it somewhere it matters."

* * *

Hiding a thing is navigation. That's how I thought about it, anyway, because thinking about it as navigation kept my hands from shaking. You don't hide a thing by putting it somewhere dark. You hide it by making every path leading to it look like water no sane person would sail. You make the approach look worse than the destination.

The recorder itself was a military-surplus field unit, sealed in a rubberized case the size of a ship's chronometer box, dense as a brick of lead. The survey team had bolted their strongbox below the waterline inside the hull of their own vessel—which told us they'd understood exactly what was coming for them—and I kept coming back to that. The dead men had hidden their truth in the most hostile environment they could reach. There was a lesson in it. The best place to keep a secret is somewhere the searcher has to pay in skin.

We considered the options out loud, quietly, in the dark of the main cabin. The fuel bladder—no, first place anyone looks, and kerosene smell clings. Under the deck plates—Tam could cut access in twenty minutes, but a proper search team carries stud finders and borescopes and boredom, and boredom finds everything. Inside the engine block—too hot, would cook the electronics if we ever ran hard. Sef suggested sewing it into a salvage bag and sinking it in the channel with a marker buoy, and we all looked at him until he flushed, because a marker buoy is a confession floating on a string.

"The ballast tanks," I said.

Everyone turned.

"Portside tank. The forward one." Our old girl carried water ballast in two steel tanks amidships, flooded and pumped out depending on load. The forward port tank had a slow seep at the lower weld seam—we'd never fixed it properly because the fix required cutting the tank open, and who cuts open a ballast tank? So we compensated. Every captain compensates. It was the one flaw on the boat we'd stopped logging, the one thing we'd agreed, without ceremony, to live around. "Nobody goes in that tank. Not us, not a surveyor, not a thief. It's the one compartment on this vessel with a reputation. Even *we* avoid it."

Tam grunted, which from him is a standing ovation. "Seep means it's half-full of river water and silt. Electronics won't love it."

"So we seal it better than it sealed itself." I was already pulling the tank drawings from the binder—I keep drawings of everything, it's a disease—and spreading them under the red lamp. "There's an access plate on the outboard face, sixteen bolts. We glass the recorder into a waterproof shell, strap it to the baffle plate inside, above the waterline of the tank itself, and rebed the plate with fresh gasket. To anyone looking, it's the same rusted, seeping, worthless tank it's always been. To find the recorder, they'd have to pump the tank, cut the plate, and crawl into a space we ourselves treat like a coffin. Searchers don't do that. Searchers check the obvious hiding places and then they check the crew."

"That last part," Sef said quietly, "is the part I don't love."

Nobody answered him, because none of us loved it either, and lies need silence to set properly, like epoxy.

We worked through the rest of the night watch. Tam cut the plate while I kept the tools quiet and Elodie stood the bow watch with binoculars, watching the channel mouth for running lights that didn't belong. Sef mixed epoxy in the galley and managed, in the process, to glue two of his own fingers together, which under the circumstances struck me as the most honest thing any of us had done in days. The recorder went in wrapped in waxed canvas inside a glassed shell, strapped to the upper baffle where even flood water wouldn't touch it. Then the plate went back on with a new gasket, and Tam smeared the weld seam with fresh corrosion streaks he brewed up from salt water and a steel wool pad, aging the repair backward in time. By dawn the tank looked exactly as neglected as it had for six years.

Then we cleaned up. This is the part people forget about hiding things: the work leaves fingerprints everywhere. Metal filings in the bilge. Epoxy smell in the galley. A missing hour in everyone's sleep. We scrubbed, we aired, we scattered the filings in the scrap bucket with genuine junk, and by the time the sun came up gray through the mist, the boat was just a tired salvage boat again, and four tired salvagers were just people eating bad coffee and waiting.

* * *

They came at 0900, which meant Corvan's twelve hours had been a rounding error or a test, and neither possibility comforted me.

Two boats. The airboat we'd met before, with the polite man whose name I refused to learn—he introduced himself again anyway, some clipped syllables with a rank attached—and behind it a gray utility skiff with four more men and a hard-shell equipment case that I would have bet my license contained a borescope. They boarded without asking, which answered the question of what kind of inspection this was going to be.

What followed was six hours of the most thorough violation I've experienced outside of a customs shed in a country I won't name. They pulled the deck plates. They ran the borescope down every void they could reach. They opened the fuel bladder and dipped it. They went through our personal kit with gloved hands, reading Elodie's logbooks page by page, unrolling Tam's charts, shaking out Sef's duffel onto the deck where his mother's photograph landed face-up in a puddle and he had to stand there watching a stranger pick it up, turn it over, and put it down without apology. I catalogued my own fury the way I catalogue hazards—noting it, marking its position, refusing to let it steer.

I'll give the polite man this: he was good. He didn't accuse. He chatted. He asked about the river levels, about the wreck, about whether we'd found much of value, and every question was a hook dressed as small talk. Elodie handled him like weather—steady, bored, slightly deaf to implication. At one point he stood directly over the forward port ballast tank's inspection fitting, and I felt my heartbeat in my teeth, and he rapped the hull twice with a knuckle and said, "She take water here?" and Tam said, "Every boat takes water somewhere," and the man laughed like that was the truest thing he'd heard all week and moved on.

Invisibility, I thought, watching him go. Six years of being nobody worth searching. All those seasons of small contracts and honest drudgery, and I'd resented the smallness. Now it was armor. Nobody hides in greatness. You hide in being forgettable, and we had been forgettable our whole working lives, and today it bought us our lives.

Before they left, the polite man paused at the rail and looked at Elodie with something that wasn't quite a smile.

"Clean boat," he said. "Mr. Corvan will be pleased."

"I aim to please," Elodie said, and her voice didn't shake, and I marveled at her, and I also understood, with total clarity, that we had maybe days. Maybe hours. Men like that don't report failure and go home. They report failure and wait for a second opinion, and second opinions come with rifles.

* * *

After the skiffs faded upriver, we stood in the wreckage of our searched lives—cushions unzipped, lockers gutted, Sef reassembling his duffel with the photograph pressed flat between two shirts—and nobody celebrated. Celebration would have been premature. Survival isn't a result; it's a holding action.

"They'll be back," Tam said. "Or worse, they won't be back, and something else will happen instead. Boats catch fire. Anchors drag. Rivers eat careless crews."

"Then we don't give them a river," Elodie said. She looked at each of us in turn, and what was in her face wasn't the smooth professionalism from the phone call. It was something rawer. "Corvan sold us. I keep turning it over looking for another reading and there isn't one. Triple rates, no witnesses—that was the contract from the start. We were never supposed to come back. The money was the bait and section nine was the trapdoor." She exhaled. "So here's where we are. We hold proof that a company murdered eight people, and the company knows we hold it, and the only people we can't trust anymore include the man paying us. The recorder stays in the tank. The tank stays boring. And we start figuring out, tonight, how to move it somewhere the company's arms can't reach."

"Where?" Sef asked. "There's nothing upriver but the concession. Downriver is the delta, and the delta is watched."

I had the charts out before I realized I was doing it, hands moving on their own, the navigator's reflex: when there's no good answer, look harder at the map. My finger traced the river up past the wreck, past Mile 40 where the charts went vague and hand-annotated, into the foothills where the contour lines crowded together like teeth.

"Not upriver by water," I said slowly. "But the range is there. And ranges have passes, and passes have trails, and trails have—" I hesitated over the penciled notation in the chart margin, faded, in handwriting that wasn't ours. Someone had written *V.* next to a bend at Mile 40, years ago, with a date. An old supply note, maybe. A name from the river's older, stranger economy. "Trails have people," I finished. "Somebody marked this stretch. Somebody who knew it well enough to write on a chart."

Elodie leaned over my shoulder and looked at the pencil mark for a long time.

"Then tomorrow we go meet somebody," she said.

That night I took the last watch, alone in the wheelhouse with the river breathing against the hull, and I did the arithmetic again, the real arithmetic this time. Fuel: enough to reach Mile 40 and lie low. Food: ten days if we ate like monks. Odds: I didn't run the numbers, because for the first time since the strongbox came up dripping, I didn't want to know them. Some courses you steer by faith and spite, and we were on one now.

Behind me, below the deck plates, inside a rusted tank full of silt and secrets, eight dead voices waited in the dark for us to carry them somewhere they could finally be loud.

Upstream, the mountains were already wearing their first white collar of snow.

I turned the wheel and brought us about.
