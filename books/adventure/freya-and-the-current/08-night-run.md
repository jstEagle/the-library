## 8. Night Run

There's a superstition among salvage crews that you never name your boat, because a named boat is a boat you can grieve. I'd broken that rule eleven years ago when I painted *Magpie* on her stern in letters my mother would have called ostentatious and my father would have called accurate. A magpie steals shiny things. So did we. And now I was going to drown her on purpose, and the superstition turned out to be exactly right.

I stood at her rail at dusk and did the arithmetic one more time, because arithmetic is what I do instead of crying.

The airboat had come at noon. Polite men with polite rifles and a polite list of questions, and Elodie Dravenmoor, who has lied to customs officers, insurance investigators, and two separate husbands, had lied to them so smoothly that Tam later accused me of enjoying it. Maybe I had. That's the part nobody warns you about — how good it feels, the first clean lie in a situation where the truth is a death sentence. You tell it and something in your chest goes *click*, like a hatch sealing, and after that you're a different kind of animal.

They'd left. We'd watched their wake flatten out against the far bank and waited an hour past prudent, and then Corvan's satellite call had come through on the frequency Ilsa was monitoring, because our financier apparently didn't know we'd found his spare handset in the survey equipment manifest. Or maybe he did know, and wanted us to hear it. Men like Corvan don't make mistakes that small.

*"Recovery team is expendable,"* a voice had said, flat as a ledger line. *"No witnesses returns. Confirm."*

And Corvan — Corvan, who'd shaken my hand at the Marlowe dock with both of his, who'd told me about his daughter's diving medals — had said, *"Confirmed."*

So. Arithmetic. One boat we couldn't keep. Four people who intended to stay alive. One strongbox full of dead men's voices that somebody very well-funded wanted at the bottom of the Kestrel forever.

The math only worked one way.

* * *

Tam hated the plan for exactly forty minutes, which for Tam is a kind of religious observance. He hates every plan for forty minutes. Then he starts improving it, and once Tam starts improving things, the thing is as good as done.

"You're not scuttling her mid-channel," he said, crouched over the engine housing with a spanner and a face like a man being asked to euthanize a dog with a hammer. "You open the seacocks in the shallows off the drowned forest, bow-first into the snags, port side down. Flood pattern reads like she hit a submerged log running dark. Any diver they send down sees impact trauma on the hull plating."

"You've thought about this before," I said.

"I've thought about everything before. It's my curse." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "I can fake impact damage. What I can't fake is four bodies, so we better hope they take the bait without checking the river bottom for crew."

"They won't check," Ilsa said from the chart table. She had the flooded forest marked in pencil — old logging cuts from sixty years back, drowned when the company built the lower weir, a maze of black trunks standing in six feet of tea-dark water. "Security doesn't dive. Security burns things. Look at the checkpoints upriver. They're arsonists with badges. They'll see a burned camp and a sunken boat and write *deceased* in triplicate and go home pleased with themselves."

"And if they don't?" Sef asked. He was twenty-three and trying very hard to sound like someone who'd considered all outcomes, which mostly meant he was chewing the inside of his cheek raw.

"Then we're ghosts with wet boots," I said, "and we improvise."

Sef looked at me for a long moment. He's got his father's habit of looking at you like your answer might be load-bearing. "You're not scared," he said finally.

"Oh, I'm terrified," I told him. "I'm just terrified productively. There's a difference, and the difference is motion."

That got half a smile out of him, which was the whole point. You can't put four frightened people in a dive skiff at midnight; fear compounds like interest. But you can put four people in a skiff who are each pretending, for everyone else's benefit, to be the brave one. It's the oldest trick in salvage. The water doesn't care how you feel. Neither does the plan.

* * *

We spent the last hours of daylight making *Magpie* look lived-in and doomed.

Tam rigged the galley stove to fail catastrophically — a propane feed he could crack from the dock with a length of fishing line, so the fire would start after we were gone and burn hot enough to explain away anything forensically inconvenient. Ilsa scattered the cabin with the ordinary archaeology of our lives: a playing card under the table leg, her reading glasses folded on the chart, Sef's awful pop music still queued on the galley speaker. I took down the photograph of my father from above the wheel — the only thing aboard I couldn't replace — and put it in my jacket, and felt like a thief in my own house.

Then we loaded the dive skiff. She's an ugly little thing, low-slung, matte grey, the kind of boat that exists to be overlooked. Medical kit. Two days of rations sealed in waterproof bags. The strongbox, wrapped in a tarp and lashed amidships where it couldn't shift, heavier than its contents by a factor I tried not to think about. Dead men's voices weigh something. Not physically. But you feel them.

Sef carried the recorder itself in a dry bag against his chest, inside his exposure suit, and refused to let anyone else touch it. I understood. There are objects that want a specific custodian, and ever since he'd opened that box on the wreck, the recorder had wanted him. I didn't argue with it. I've been diving long enough to know when the river has opinions.

At full dark, I went below and opened the seacocks myself. Tam offered. I said no.

There are jobs a captain doesn't delegate. Signing for cargo. Telling a family. And this — kneeling in the bilge of the only home I've kept longer than any relationship, turning two brass wheels and feeling the Kestrel come up to meet me through the floor of my own boat. Cold water over my knuckles. Rising slow, patient, the way the river does everything.

"Sorry, old girl," I said. "Nothing personal. You were always going to sink eventually. At least this way it's on our terms, and there's a story in it."

The river didn't answer. Rivers never do. That's the whole trouble with them.

* * *

We ran her into the drowned forest at quarter past midnight, engine at idle, navigation lights off, Ilsa calling snags from the bow in a whisper that carried further than shouting would have. Black trunks rose around us out of water the color of steeped bark, close enough to touch, some of them still wearing the notches loggers cut six decades ago. The moon was a rumor behind cloud. Somewhere a bittern made a sound like a boot pulled from deep mud, and Sef flinched hard enough that I heard his teeth click.

"Breathe," I murmured.

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like a man being charged rent per breath. Slow it down."

He laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real — and I counted that a victory. Four nights ago this kid had been thrilled just to be on a contract with triple rates. Now he had a bullet-shaped piece of evidence strapped to his sternum and a price on his head, and he laughed anyway. I decided then and there that if we got out of this, I was keeping him. Not as crew. As family. There's a difference, and the difference is that you don't pay family.

We brought her alongside a fallen trunk — a giant, half-submerged, its root plate standing out of the water like a broken wall — and Tam killed the engine, and the silence that followed was enormous. River silence isn't empty. It's crowded. Insects, frogs, water working at wood, the whole drowned forest metabolizing the dark.

"Now or never," I said, and hated how much it sounded like a line from a bad film.

Tam opened the remaining valves and cracked a seam joint he'd pre-weakened near the waterline, and *Magpie* began to go down the way he'd promised: bow-first, port side down, unhurried, dignified even. She settled against the snag field and the black water came up her deck like a tide coming for something it had always owned. Her mast light — the little amber bulb Tam had wired himself, back when — stayed lit until the water took it, and then there was just a fading glow under the surface, like a coal breathed on.

Nobody spoke. Sef had his fist pressed to his mouth. Ilsa was watching the boat with her jaw set in the way she watches weather she can't do anything about. Tam put one hand flat on the gunwale of the skiff, as if steadying himself, though the water was glass.

"She was a good boat," he said at last.

"She was a thief," I said, "and so am I, and thieves don't get funerals. They get legends. Come on."

We backed out of the snag field on paddles, quiet as herons, and I did not look back. Looking back is how you turn grief into cargo, and we were already carrying enough.

* * *

We were maybe two kilometers downstream, tucked into a cut between two drowned trunks with the tarp over the skiff, when the night behind us turned orange.

I heard it before I saw it — a low whump, felt more than heard, the sound of a propane-fed fire finding its appetite. Then the glow climbed over the tree line to the north, staining the undersides of the clouds, and the smell arrived a minute later on the breeze: burning diesel, burning paint, burning canvas. Our camp. Our tents, our compressor, the galley tent where Sef had burned rice three nights running, the folding chairs, the drying racks, everything that had been ours at supper time.

"They're thorough," Ilsa said quietly. She had binoculars up, though there wasn't much to see but light. "Two vehicles. They're walking the perimeter. Making sure."

"Making sure of what?"

"That nothing walks out of the fire." She lowered the binoculars. "Which means somewhere in the last hour, they started doubting the boat."

My stomach dropped through the hull of the world. "The boat read right. Tam's work was—"

"Tam's work was perfect. But perfect is a smell too, sometimes." She passed me the binoculars. "Look at the shoreline south of the burn. They've got a light sweeping the water. Methodical. Left to right."

She was right. A white beam was stroking the riverbank in slow passes, and every pass came a little wider, a little closer to the drowned forest's edge. They hadn't bought it. Not entirely. Some careful man in a concession uniform had looked at a sunken boat and a burned camp and thought: *where are the bodies?*

"We move," I whispered. "Now. Deep into the snags, engine off, paddle only. If that light touches us, we're four heads in open water with nowhere to run."

We paddled. God, how we paddled — silent strokes, the skiff sliding between black trunks while the searchlight swept the shore behind us and the fire roared and the smoke rolled overhead thick enough to taste. Once, the beam swung out across the open channel and caught the mist, and for three heartbeats the whole world was a white wall, and I sat frozen with my paddle dripping, certain we were dead, absolutely calm about it in the way you get when terror maxes out and wraps around into something like peace.

The beam moved on.

We went deeper. An hour, maybe two, threading the drowned forest until the firelight was a memory on the clouds and the searchlight a rumor, until the only sounds were our own breathing and the water talking to the trees. Then, in a pool of darkness under a canopy of branches, I called the halt, and we tied off, and the four of us sat in a twelve-foot skiff in the middle of a flooded forest, alive, unfindable, and completely alone.

It should have been the worst moment of my life. My boat was at the bottom of the river. My camp was ash. My bank account belonged to a man who had sold my death for less than the cost of the fuel his men had used to light it.

Instead — and I'll be honest here, because if I can't be honest in my own head, the whole exercise is pointless — instead I felt *light*. Giddy, appalled, adrenaline-bright light. For eleven years I'd been Elodie Dravenmoor, salvage contractor, a woman defined by licenses and invoices and the polite fictions of maritime law. All of that had burned with the camp. What was left in the skiff was something older and simpler: a woman in the dark with three people she'd die for and a box of truth that powerful men wanted drowned.

They'd made us ghosts. Nobody had stopped to ask whether we'd be *their* ghosts.

"Elodie." Sef's voice, barely a breath, from the bow. "The recorder. It's warm."

"That's your body heat, kid."

"No." He'd pulled the dry bag halfway open, and in the starlight I could see him holding the field recorder against his ear like a shell. "It's cycling. It's been cycling since the wreck — I thought it was standby, but it's not. It's been recording *us*. The whole time. Every word since I opened the box."

The silence in the skiff got very large.

"It has our voices on it," Ilsa said slowly. "Our names. The security contact. Corvan's call."

"It has everything," Sef said. His eyes were wide, and I realized the horror on his face was braided with something else, something fierce. "Elodie — it's not just their confession anymore. It's ours too."

I looked at the little machine sitting in a twenty-three-year-old's hands, blinking its patient red eye in the dark, holding the voices of the drowned and the voices of the living in the same magnetic throat. Evidence, yes. But also a mirror. Everything we'd said since the box opened — every lie, every plan, every act that had turned us from contractors into fugitives — was in there beside the screaming.

"Well then," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me, "we'd better make sure the rest of what it records is worth hearing."

Tam snorted softly in the dark. "First order of business, ghost crew: we need a heading. Because I know where Vashti lives."

I turned. "Vashti."

"The hydrologist. Mile 40, up the old logging spur. Disgraced, armed, and meaner than a conger in a net." He was already reaching for the chart. "If anyone can get four ghosts over the Serrat Range before the snow, it's the man the company ruined and forgot to kill."

The red eye blinked. Somewhere behind us, the fire died down to embers over the grave of a boat named for a thief.

"Paddle east," I said. "Mile 40."
