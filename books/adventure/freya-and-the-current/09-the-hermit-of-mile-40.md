## 9. The Hermit of Mile 40

I want it on the record that I found her first.

Not Elodie with her navigator's instincts, not Tam with his encyclopedic memory of every fuel cache between the delta and the divide—me. Sef, nineteen years old, shot through the shoulder forty hours earlier, feverish enough that the flooded forest kept rearranging itself into shapes that made no sense and one shape that made too much: a woman standing in a doorway made of driftwood and corrugated tin, holding a shotgun the way you'd hold a broom, like she was tired of it but couldn't imagine putting it down.

"Please," I said, which is not what we'd agreed to say. We'd agreed on nothing, actually, because Tam had been against stopping at all and Ilsa had been for it and Elodie had been doing that thing where she goes very quiet and everyone else fills the silence with their own arguments until she picks one. I just walked up to the door because my legs were done voting.

The woman looked at me for a long time. Long enough that I noticed things I probably shouldn't have noticed in my condition—the smokehouse behind her cabin, the solar panel wired to a car battery, the rain gauge mounted on a post with markings cut into the wood by hand, fine as a jeweler's work. Then she looked past me at the others standing in the treeline like four bad decisions wearing waders.

"You're the salvage crew," she said. "From the Kestrel."

"We haven't told anyone we're here," Elodie said carefully.

"You don't have to." The woman lowered the shotgun. "Word travels upriver faster than boats do. Come inside before the light does. It gets dark early here, and I don't keep lamps burning where they can be seen from the water."

Her name was Vashti. Old Vashti, if you asked anyone in the concession—which nobody did anymore, because there wasn't an anyone left within sixty miles to ask. Mile 40, the marker said, though the marker was lying; everything out here lied except her rain gauge.

* * *

Inside the cabin it smelled like woodsmoke and iodine and something green growing in jars along the south wall. She sat me down in the only chair—a real chair, with arms—and went to work on my shoulder without asking permission, which I respected, because everyone else had spent two days asking me how I felt and I was tired of having an opinion about it.

"The bullet's still in," she said, probing with fingers that were gentler than they looked. "Who took it out?"

"Nobody did," Tam said from the doorway. "We poured antiseptic in the hole and hoped."

"Hoped." She said it the way you'd say *amateur*. "Sit down, all of you. You're making my floor nervous."

She boiled water. She laid out instruments on a cloth so clean it hurt to look at—forceps, a probe, a curved needle, all of it older than me and better cared for than most people. And while she worked she talked, not to me exactly, more to the shoulder, as if the shoulder were the patient and I was just the furniture it came attached to.

"Through and near the joint. Missed the artery by a finger's width, which means whoever shot you was either bad or merciful, and out here those are usually the same thing." A pause. "Concession security?"

Silence. Four people discovering simultaneously that silence can be loud.

Vashti snorted. "You can lie to yourselves all you want. You're in my house now, and in this house the truth is a tool like anything else. Dull tools get people killed." She glanced up at me, and her eyes were pale gray, the color of the river when it ran wrong. "You're feverish. You'll say true things anyway. Might as well get ahead of it."

So we told her. Not all of it—not the recorder, not yet—but the shape of it. The contract with clauses nobody signs. The wreck that had been scuttled clean instead of storm-broken. The airboat with polite men and long guns. The satellite call Ilsa had taken, the voice on the other end telling their employer's man to leave no witnesses, and Ilsa hiding the box in a ballast tank while pretending to cry about engine trouble.

When we got to the part about scuttling our own boat, Vashti stopped working.

"You sank your own ship," she said.

"It was sinking anyway," Tam said. "Slowly. We just helped it commit."

"And your camp?"

"They burned it after we left."

She nodded slowly, like a doctor confirming a diagnosis she'd already written in the chart. Then she went back to my shoulder, and the forceps went in, and I learned something new about the distance between bravery and screaming. I bit down on the leather strap she'd put between my teeth and thought about the river, the good parts of it, the way it had sounded before any of this, back when it was just water and work and Tam complaining about the bilge pump in three languages.

The bullet came out in a small bright dish. She held it up to the lamp.

"Concession issue," she said. "Nine-millimeter, factory loads. They don't even reload—they buy new brass by the pallet. That's your tax money, boys and girls, assuming any of you pay taxes, which given the company you keep I doubt."

"What do we owe you?" Elodie asked. Always practical, our captain. Even bleeding, I loved her a little for it.

"For the surgery? Nothing." Vashti wrapped the wound in clean linen, quick and sure. "For the soup you're about to eat and the floor you're about to sleep on? Nothing. For the answers you're going to ask for around midnight when you think I'm asleep?" She smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but it was an honest one. "Those are free too. I've been waiting six years for someone to come up this river asking questions. I'd have paid you."

* * *

The soup was fish and something rootlike and too much salt, and it was the best thing I have ever eaten, and I will fight anyone who says otherwise. Fever does that. So does nearly dying. Everything tastes like being alive.

Tam ate three bowls and then, because he is Tam, started inspecting her stove. By the time he was done he'd re-seated a warped flue plate and pronounced the whole setup "surprisingly sound for junk," which from Tam is a love letter. Ilsa spread her charts on the table and Vashti leaned over them and went quiet in a different way than Elodie goes quiet—hers was the quiet of someone reading a letter from home.

"This trail here." Ilsa tapped a line she'd drawn herself, dotted, uncertain. "Along the ridge above the tailings channel. Does it go through?"

"It goes through," Vashti said. "It also goes past two sensor posts the company pretends are weather stations. In summer. But you're not traveling in summer, are you? You're racing the snow." She pulled a pencil stub from behind her ear and began to draw, fast, sure, correcting Ilsa's map with the confidence of someone who had walked every line of it. "Here. The old survey cut. Abandoned when the concession changed hands, never re-surveyed. It stays under the treeline for eleven miles, then climbs through the col at—" she paused, pencil hovering, some private weather crossing her face, "—at the pass they call the Pass of Names. After that you're exposed, and after that you're beyond my help, and after that you're on your own, and I'm sorry, but that's the truth of it, and in this house we use dull tools on nothing."

"Why do you know all this?" Ilsa asked. Not rude. Just tired, and past the point where tired people pretend not to wonder.

Vashti set down the pencil.

"Because I drew the first map," she said.

And that was how we learned what she was. Concession hydrologist, senior, twelve years. The woman who had sampled the Kestrel headwaters before the first shovel of the first pit went in. The woman whose baseline data—the numbers every later measurement was judged against—had been hers. The woman who, six years ago, had run a routine downstream assay and found heavy metals at concentrations that made her rerun the test three times because she didn't believe it, and then a fourth time because she didn't want to believe it.

"I filed a report," she said, in the flat voice people use for stories that have scarred over. "A proper one. Chain of custody, duplicates, the works. My supervisor thanked me, sealed it, and two weeks later I was reassigned to a drainage study four hundred kilometers north. When I refused, I was dismissed—for falsifying data, if you can credit the irony. My credentials revoked. My house on the compound reassigned. I was given seventy-two hours to leave the concession zone." She looked around her cabin, at the jars and the smokehouse and the rain gauge. "So I left. Seventy-two hours upriver. Far enough that they'd forget me, close enough that I could keep sampling."

"Keep sampling," Elodie repeated.

"Somebody has to count the damage," Vashti said simply. "Even if nobody reads the count. Especially then." She nodded toward the south wall, at the jars of green. "Watercress. Grows fat downstream of the outfall. Full of metals. I grow it in clean water brought down from the ridge, and every season I take a sample of the river and seal it and date it and put it in a box. Sixty-one samples. One day somebody might want them."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire ticked. Outside, the forest did its enormous quiet work of being a forest, indifferent to us and to the company and to everything, which I found more comforting than I can explain.

It was me who broke the silence, because fever makes you honest and honesty makes you stupid, and I said:

"The recorder. From the wreck. The survey team's radio logs."

Vashti went very still.

"They reported contamination," I went on, words tumbling because I couldn't stop them. "Hours before the accident. Before the boat went down. The last entry is—"

"I know what the last entry is." Her voice hadn't risen, but it had changed, gone thin and hard as wire. "I heard the traffic that night. Everyone on the concession band heard it. Then the band went quiet and the company said storm, and there was no storm, and I knew, and knowing is its own kind of drowning." She turned and looked at each of us in turn, ending on Elodie. "You have it. The logs. That's why security is hunting you. That's why your own financier sold you."

Elodie didn't confirm it. She didn't deny it either. She just met the old woman's eyes, and whatever passed between them needed no nine-millimeter brass to make it binding.

"Then hear me," Vashti said. "You cannot go back downriver. You cannot stay. Winter comes early to the Serrat, and when the first snow seals the passes, the concession permits lapse—and when the permits lapse, everything inside the boundary becomes company property again. Records. Wrecks. Bodies. Evidence. All of it, legally theirs, legally erased. Whatever you're carrying has to be outside the boundary before the snow, or it might as well be at the bottom of the Kestrel forever."

"Where's outside?" Ilsa asked.

"The mining registry office. Company town, east side of the range. There's a records clerk there whose job is to receive filings, and a filing, once received, is law." Vashti almost laughed. "Their own machine. Their own paper. It's the one door they can't weld shut, because their whole empire runs on paper looking legitimate."

* * *

I slept in the chair, mostly, drifting, and woke once in the deep night to find Vashti awake across the room, feeding the fire stick by stick, and I didn't pretend to be asleep because I was too tired to perform.

"My brother fished the Kestrel," I said. Which was true, and which I hadn't said out loud since the delta, since before any of this. "Downstream water. He always said the fish tasted like pennies some seasons. We laughed about it."

Vashti fed the fire another stick. "How old were you?"

"Twelve."

"And now you're carrying proof of what was in that water over a mountain so it can't be buried." She poked the coals. "There's a symmetry in that. Ugly, but real. The river sends its debts upstream eventually. It just takes its time."

"I'm scared," I said, because apparently the night had decided I was going to say true things whether I wanted to or not.

"Good," she said. "Scared means your arithmetic is right. The ones who aren't scared are the ones who've miscounted." She got up, stiff, and draped a blanket over me that smelled of cedar and decades. "Sleep, boy. Tomorrow I walk you to the foot of the climb, and after that the mountain decides what happens to you, and mountains are fairer than companies but less forgiving."

I slept. Really slept, for the first time since the airboat, in a stranger's chair in a stranger's house at the end of a drowned road, and it was the strangest mercy of the whole terrible trip: that belonging doesn't care where you find it. Mine found me at Mile 40, in a cabin full of watercress and sealed evidence, held together by a disgraced scientist and a woodstove and sixty-one dated samples of a poisoned river, waiting all these years for someone to come asking.

In the morning, gray and cold and smelling of coming snow, Vashti stood in her doorway with a rifle slung across her back and a pack on her shoulders that looked far too heavy for a woman her age, and said:

"Well? The mountain won't get shorter waiting. Let's go see how much of it you can survive."

And that was how Old Vashti came to be walking us into the Serrat foothills—her map, her trail, her war, finally with soldiers.
