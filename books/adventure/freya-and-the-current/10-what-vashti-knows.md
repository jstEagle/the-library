## 10. What Vashti Knows

I had not hosted guests in eleven years, and it showed. My cabin at Mile 40 had one chair, one kettle, and a floor that announced every footstep like a town crier. Four salvagers crowded into a space built for one bitter woman and her grudges, and the youngest of them—the boy, Sef, gray-faced with fever he was pretending wasn't there—had the gall to look grateful.

Gratitude. There's a currency I hadn't handled in a decade. It spends badly out here.

They'd come up the old survey cut in the dark, which meant someone had told them about the survey cut, which meant Elodie Dravenmoor had done her homework on me before she ever knocked on my door. I respected that. I also resented it, because being known is the first step toward being used, and I had retired from both.

"You were concession hydrologist," she said, not asking. She sat in my chair because there was only one and she was the kind of person who took the chair without apology. "Twelve years. Then you weren't."

"Then I was a rumor," I said. "Rumors are safer. They don't have addresses."

Tam stood by the door like furniture that could shoot. The navigator, Ilsa, had already found my maps—of course she had—and was reading them upside down from across the room, which told me everything I needed to know about her. And Sef sat on my bunk with his shoulder bound in river linen, watching me with the particular attention of someone who has recently learned that the world will kill you for knowing things.

The recorder sat on my table between us. A field unit, Kestrel Survey designation stenciled on the case, its contents transferred twice now onto whatever backup media Ilsa carried sewn into her coat lining. I hadn't played all of it. I'd played enough. Eleven years of listening to that river run milky past my door, and here was the voice of the man who'd measured what I'd only suspected, reporting it up the chain in the flat professional diction of someone who believed the chain worked.

It didn't work. That was the whole story, really. Everything else was weather.

"You want to know if it's enough," I said. "You're sitting there wondering whether a recording is evidence, whether an inquiry would take it, whether any of this ends with anyone in a courtroom instead of a ditch." I poured tea for four, then remembered the fifth cup was the one I'd broken the winter I stopped expecting company, and gave mine to Sef. "I can't answer that. What I can tell you is when."

Elodie waited. Good instinct. People who talk over hydrologists get drowned data.

"The permits," I said. "The concession. Everyone treats it like a fence, but it's a lease. The consortium holds extraction rights under provincial mining code, section whatever-the-number-is-this-year, and those rights are bounded by permit term. Twenty years, renewable. The current term expires—" I looked at the window, at the light going long and amber through the larches, doing arithmetic I'd been doing since September. "Nineteen days. Maybe twenty. First snow closes it."

"Snow doesn't close anything legally," Tam said.

"Snow closes everything legally." I let that sit. "Here's the mechanism, engineer, because you'll want the mechanism. When a permit lapses, the concession resets. Full environmental remediation review, fresh baseline surveys, new filings. And here's the beautiful part, the part some lawyer earned a boat on: all records generated within the concession boundary during the prior term are classified as proprietary operational data. Company property. Sealed. The survey team's logs, their samples, their contamination readings—all of it becomes the company's own paper, held in the company's own vault, discoverable by no one, admissible nowhere. The crime doesn't just get buried. It gets *notarized*."

Sef shifted on the bunk. "So after first snow—"

"After first snow, that recording is a souvenir. A ghost story. You could play it in a tavern and they'd call it fiction, because officially no survey ever reported contamination on the Kestrel, and officially the records say whatever the records say, and the records belong to the people who wrote them." I drank my tea, which had gone bitter, which felt appropriate. "Before first snow, it's evidence. Evidence has a jurisdiction. Evidence can be filed."

"Filed where?" Ilsa asked, not looking up from the maps.

"Mining registry office. Corren Falls, the other side of the Serrat Range. Not a regional annex—the main registry, where filings create public record the moment the clerk stamps them. Once it's stamped, it exists outside the concession's legal gravity. No permit expiry reaches backward and un-stamps a document. Even the consortium's lawyers can't un-ring that bell, and believe me, they've bought bells before."

"And the range?" Elodie asked. She asked it the way you ask a doctor about a prognosis. Already braced.

I got up and went to the wall where I kept the map I actually trusted—not the printed ones, which lie by omission, but the one I'd drawn myself over eleven years of walking ridgelines with a barometer and a grudge. I spread it over the table, over the recorder, which felt right. The truth under the terrain.

"Serrat Range. Three passes high enough to cross before the snowpack makes them suicide. Kettle Gap, south—longest route, six days in good weather, and it's watched, because it's the route the company uses for its own traffic. Duncairn, north—steeper, colder, and the approach crosses forty kilometers of open scree where a tracker team with optics would find you inside a day. And the middle one." I put my finger on it. "The Pass of Names."

Nobody laughed at the name. They'd been on the river long enough by then to have lost their appetite for laughing at things.

"It's called that because everyone who's crossed it left a cairn, and not everyone who left a cairn came back down the other side. It's the shortest route—four days, maybe five with your wounded boy—and it's unwatched for exactly one reason: nobody uses it. The company doesn't bother guarding a pass that kills more travelers than it passes through." I straightened. "Which means we'd be racing the front with no shelter above the treeline, carrying a man with a hole in him, over ground where a sprained ankle is a death sentence."

"We," Tam repeated. Flat. Not hostile. Just weighing the word.

"I'm coming." I said it before he could make it a question. "You think I'm staying here? After eleven years of drinking that river's water and knowing? You'll need someone who knows the trails, and more than that—" I looked at Elodie, because this part was hers to hear directly. "More than that, I need to be the one who puts it on the record. I flagged anomalous turbidity readings three times in my last year. Three memos. All three came back marked *resolved — insufficient basis*. I know who wrote *insufficient basis* on them. I've spent eleven years telling myself I quit rather than sign the fourth memo. This is the fourth memo. I'm delivering it personally."

* * *

That night they slept—or lay down and called it sleeping—in my cabin and the woodshed, and I sat up with the lamp turned low, listening to the river.

You hear a river long enough and it stops being water. It becomes a ledger. Every season writes entries: the spring surge, the summer clarity, the autumn drop. For eleven years the Kestrel had been writing entries in a hand nobody upstream would read. Milky in the wrong months. Warm in the wrong places, where groundwater came up through fractured rock carrying the leachate from tailings ponds that officially did not leak. I had bottles of it in my cellar, dated and labeled, a museum of evidence that was worth nothing because I was one disgraced woman with a cellar full of mud.

Now there were five of us and a recording, and suddenly the mud had witnesses.

I don't want to romanticize it. I want to be precise, because precision is the only prayer hydrologists have. Here is what is precise:

In nineteen days, the legal architecture that makes their recording matter dissolves. Not gradually—it goes all at once, like ice going out in spring, except in reverse. One day there is a jurisdiction where a clerk's stamp creates fact; the next day there is a company town with a locked registry and a boundary line behind which everything true becomes proprietary. The consortium doesn't need to destroy the recording after first snow. They only need to wait. Time is their employee. Time works cheaper than gunmen and never asks questions.

And here is the other precise thing, the thing I said to Elodie on her second night under my roof while Sef burned with fever in the next room and Tam sharpened a knife that didn't need sharpening:

"They won't wait for the snow," I told her. "People think companies are patient. Companies are patient about paperwork and impatient about everything else. Right now you are a loose end with legs, and every day between now and expiry is a day you might reach a stamp. So they will spend whatever it costs to stop you before the weather does their work for free. Understand what you're racing. You're not racing winter. Winter is the finish line. You're racing the men who can't afford to let you reach it."

She took that in the way she seemed to take everything—quietly, completely, filing it somewhere behind her eyes where the decisions lived. I was beginning to understand that Elodie Dravenmoor made decisions the way rivers cut canyons: slowly, invisibly, and then all at once the landscape was different and there was no arguing with it.

"What happens to us," she said, "if we make it?"

"Depends what 'it' means. If you mean the registry—a stamp, a filing number, a public record. After that, you're not evidence anymore, you're witnesses, and witnesses have protections. Thin ones. But thin is not nothing, and out here thin is luxurious." I banked the lamp. "If you mean afterward—testimony, inquiries, years of lawyers—I'll be honest with you, Captain. The law moves like a glacier and the company owns most of the valley it melts into. Some of you may not live to see the end of it. But the recording will. Records outlive their carriers. That's the entire point of records. It's why they fear them."

* * *

On the third morning, Ilsa found me splitting wood behind the cabin, because apparently I still split wood like a woman with something to prove, and stood there in the cold holding two mugs of tea I hadn't poured.

"Vashti. The boy's fever broke in the night."

"Good."

"He's asking to walk today. Tam says another two days. They're going to argue about it." She handed me a mug. "I wanted to ask you something first. Off the crew's books."

I waited, leaning on the axe.

"The backups. I keep them on three media, distributed, like you said. But you said something the first night—*before first snow, it's evidence*." Her eyes stayed on the treeline, on the smoke-colored sky pressing down out of the northwest. "How sure are you about the date?"

I considered lying to her, or softening it, and rejected both. She was the navigator. Navigators deserve true coordinates even when the coordinates are bad.

"Look at the sky," I said. "That haze isn't cloud, it's Arctic air arriving early. My knees have been wrong about rain and right about snow for thirty years, and they've been singing since Tuesday. I'd give you fifteen days. Maybe twelve."

Ilsa nodded once, the way people nod when a number confirms what they already feared, and went inside to start the argument about whether Sef could walk.

I stayed out a moment longer. The river ran past below the bank, gray-green and patient, carrying its silt and its secrets toward a delta where none of this had started and none of it would end. Nineteen days ago I'd have said the Kestrel kept its own counsel and always would.

Now there was a deadline written in weather, and five people in my cabin crazy enough to read it aloud.

I went inside to pack.
