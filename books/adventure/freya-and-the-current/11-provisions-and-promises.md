## 11. Provisions and Promises

I've robbed three places in my life, and I can tell you the honest truth about all of them: the planning is the fun part. The planning is where you're still a genius. It's the last forty seconds before contact, when the plan meets the world and the world starts editing, that separates the professionals from the obituaries.

The depot at Mile 52 was supposed to be easy. That was my assessment, and I gave it with confidence, because I'd spent twenty years being confident in exactly the wrong ways before Elodie fished me out of a coastal jail and gave me something better to be confident about.

Concession supply depots follow a pattern. I'd worked security for one of these outfits long enough to know it—before that job went bad, before the thing I did that I don't talk about at campfires. A fuel bladder, a generator shed, a locked container of dry goods, and two men on rotation who spend ninety percent of their shift playing cards and the other ten percent resenting each other. The generator runs the floodlights, so you cut the generator first. The lock on the container is ceremonial; the hasp bolts are what actually hold it, and bolt cutters make short work of ceremony.

We watched it for six hours from the treeline across the haul road. Two guards. One walked his circuit like a man doing penance, slow and thorough. The other sat in the guard hut with a thermos and a radio he checked every eleven minutes, which told me he was bored, not vigilant, and bored men are the most predictable creatures on earth.

"Eleven minutes," I said to Elodie when we pulled back into the trees. "That's our window if we need one. In and out in nine."

She looked at me for a long moment. Tam, she didn't say, but I heard it anyway. *You've done this before.*

"I have," I said, because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere around Whiteout Camp, or maybe it was Mile 40, or maybe it was the day she'd paid my fine and never once mentioned it since. "And I hate that I have. But I have."

Vashti drew us the layout from memory—the depot predated her disgrace by a decade, and concession infrastructure changes slowly, the way rot changes slowly. Sef insisted on coming. Of course Sef insisted on coming. The kid had a hole punched through one shoulder that we'd been cleaning with boiled river water and the last of Vashti's antibiotics, and a fever that came and went like weather, and still Sef looked at me with those bright, stubborn eyes and said, "You'll need eyes on the road."

"You need to lie down."

"I've been lying down for four days. I'm very good at it now. Let me do something else."

Elodie made the call, and I understood it even though I hated it: we couldn't leave Sef alone at the cache site with Vashti, not with tracker teams working the valley, and we couldn't spare Ilsa from carrying the recorder—Ilsa and the strongbox were inseparable now, joined at the hip like a mother and an unexploded shell. So Sef came, armed with a rifle Sef could barely raise above waist height without wincing, positioned at the treeline where the job was supposed to be standing still and watching.

The plan was beautiful. I want that on the record. Nine minutes, in and out, rice and fuel cells and the medical kit from the depot's own first-aid locker, which had a proper surgical kit in it because concession medics are better equipped than most rural hospitals and twice as indifferent.

*

The generator died at 0340 with a sound like a sigh, and the dark came down over the depot so completely that for a moment I couldn't find my own hands.

That's the part they don't put in the stories, the good parts of the old trade. The dark is a country. You cross it by touch and count. I crossed it to the container while Elodie stood watch at the corner of the fuel bladder, and I got the hasp bolts off in under four minutes, which even now, even after everything, gave me a small filthy pride. Some skills don't rust. They just wait.

Rice into the duffel. Fuel cells. The medical kit, red and white, heavier than it looked, and I remember thinking—actual thought, actual words in my head—*this is going well.*

Never think that. The moment you think it, the world hears you.

The radio crackled in the guard hut. Not the eleven-minute check. Something else. A voice, flat and official, saying words I caught only in pieces through the tin wall: *road team, overdue, sweep the depot perimeter.*

They weren't calling the depot. They were warning it. Somewhere up the haul road a tracker team was running late, running sloppy, running toward us, and the bored man with the thermos was about to become a vigilant man with a flashlight.

I slung the duffel and moved for the treeline, and I was fast, faster than I've been in years, fear burning the fat off everything unnecessary. Elodie fell in beside me. Fifty meters of open ground between the container and the trees, moonless, and we were going to make it, I could feel us making it—

Then the flashlight swept the treeline and found Sef.

Not Sef hiding. Sef standing up. Standing up out of the ferns with the rifle shouldered, firing two shots down the haul road at shapes I hadn't even seen yet—a scout team moving quiet along the tree margin, flanking wide, the way I would have flanked, the way any professional would—and Sef, feverish, half-armed, nineteen years old, saw them before I did and opened fire to pull them off us.

The third shot wasn't Sef's.

Sef went down sideways into the ferns and I went down after Sef, and the world contracted to muzzle flash and shouting and Elodie's voice cutting through it like a blade: *"Tam! Go!"*

I went. Not away. Down. I got my hands under Sef's arms and hauled, and Sef weighed nothing, nothing, this kid who used to eat like a dockworker now light as driftwood from four days of fever and bad water, and blood came warm over my wrists from somewhere high on the chest, the shoulder again, the same shoulder, the wound torn open and worse.

A round cracked off a trunk an arm's length from my head. Bark stung my cheek. I didn't duck. I want to say that plainly, because I'm not brave, I've audited my own courage many times and the numbers come back poor—but there was no version of me that let go of that kid, and so the question of bravery never came up. My body decided things my conscience had been arguing about for weeks.

Elodie covered us. I heard her rifle, measured and unhurried, three shots, then a pause, then two more, and someone on the other side screamed, and I hated how much I was grateful for her competence and hated more that her competence meant she'd shot a human being, some conscript probably, some bored man with a thermos and a family, and that we were making him scream in the dark over rice.

Over rice. Over fuel cells. Over a medical kit I was now using, God help me, to patch the hole that getting the medical kit had caused.

*

We reached Vashti's cache an hour before dawn, and I carried Sef the whole way, and I will carry the weight of it longer than I carried the weight of Sef.

Vashti took one look and turned field surgeon, and she was good, better than good—her hands steady, her voice flat in the way that flatness is a kindness—and she repacked the wound by lantern light while Sef bit down on a rolled sleeve and made sounds that I will hear for the rest of my life. Small sounds. Trying not to make them. Nineteen years old and trying not to inconvenience anyone while bleeding.

I sat outside the tarp afterward with my back against a stump and shook. Not from the exertion. From the arithmetic.

Here's the sum I kept doing. Justice—that great shining word we'd been hauling over the mountains like a second strongbox—required the recordings to reach Registry. Reaching Registry required crossing terrain that killed people. Crossing that terrain required food and medicine. Food and medicine required robbing depots. Robbing depots got Sef shot. And Sef was shot covering *me*, covering my beautiful nine-minute plan, my professional window, my expertise.

So: how much blood per unit of justice? What was the exchange rate? Because I could do that math, I'd done darker math in a former life, and the answer kept coming back ugly. The company had drowned a survey team to bury a number. We were going to deliver that number. And on the way we were leaving a trail of our own casualties, our own screaming men in the dark, our own kid with a hole in him, and if the truth required this much blood to carry, what exactly was it worth? What kind of truth only survives if teenagers bleed for it?

Elodie came and sat down next to me without asking. She did that. She had a genius for arriving exactly when silence needed company.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what."

"Whatever you're chewing on. You get a look. Like a man doing taxes."

I laughed, or something near it. "Is it worth it?" I said. "That's the whole question. Is the truth worth Sef's shoulder? Worth that guy screaming in the ferns? Because the company did its math already, Elodie. They ran the numbers on those surveyors and decided they were cheaper dead. And I keep wondering if we're doing the same math with different numbers and calling it justice instead of profit."

She was quiet a long time. Long enough that the river below us filled the silence, patient as ever.

"The difference," she finally said, "is that they never once asked the question. You're asking it at three in the morning with shaking hands. That's the entire difference. That's the whole moral universe right there—it fits inside whether you ask."

"It doesn't bring Sef's shoulder back."

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't. Nothing does. But Sef chose. That matters too. Nobody conscripted that kid into standing up in the ferns." She looked at me, and in the lantern spill her face was tired and certain at once, which shouldn't be possible and was. "You want absolution, Tam. I can't give it. But I can tell you the alternative—we stop, we hide, we let the permits lapse, we let them erase those people twice—isn't cleaner. It's just quieter."

Quieter. Yes. That was the word for it. Evil rarely shouts; it files paperwork. And I had spent enough years on the quiet side of things to know that the quiet side always wins unless somebody stands up in the ferns and makes noise.

"I'll carry Sef tomorrow," I said. "All day. Every day if I have to."

"I know," Elodie said. "That was never in question."

*

Sef woke near midmorning, feverish but lucid, and the first thing that kid did was apologize. To me. For missing the third scout.

I didn't trust my voice, so I pressed the new medical kit's morphine syrette into Sef's hand and said, "Next time miss all of them," and Sef grinned through the gray face and said, "No promises," and I turned away fast because my eyes were doing something unprofessional.

We broke camp at noon. The cold front Vashti had been reading in her joints all week was visibly assembling itself over the Serrat Range now—a gray wall building behind the peaks like a debt coming due—and above us, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a helicopter working the valley, methodical, grid by grid.

They knew we were here. They'd known since the depot. Every hour we spent in the lowlands was an hour they spent tightening.

Vashti stood at the head of the trail with her pack on and her rifle slung, looking older than she had at Mile 40, like the depot had cost her something too—maybe the last illusion that exile meant safety.

"The foothills start there," she said, pointing at a notch in the tree line where the forest gave up and the rock began. "Two days' climb to the first bivouac, if the weather holds. If it doesn't hold—" she shrugged, a small terrible shrug, "—then the mountain decides, and the mountain doesn't read permits."

I shifted Sef higher on my back. Light as driftwood. Stubborn as ballast.

"Then we'd better outrun the weather," I said.

Nobody laughed. Above the notch, the first flakes were already coming down—small, dry, early, wrong—and Vashti watched them fall with an expression I'd learn later was the exact face of a woman counting days she no longer had.
