## 12. The Ascent Begins

Ilsa Voss had spent eleven years navigating rivers, and in all that time she had learned one iron law: the water always tells you where you are, if you're willing to listen. Current speed, silt color, the smell of a bend two miles before you reach it—the river is a gossip with a grudge against silence.

The mountains told you nothing.

That was my first thought as we stood at the tree line on the third morning out of Mile 40, looking up at the Serrat foothills rising gray and indifferent into cloud. No current to read. No channel markers. Just rock and lichen and a cold wind coming down off the high country like it had somewhere better to be. I'd charted our route from Vashti's hand-drawn maps—actual paper, drawn in pencil, annotated in a hydrologist's cramped shorthand—and even folded flat on her kitchen table they'd looked like a dare. Up close, they looked like a suicide note with contour lines.

"Everyone check their boots," Vashti said. She was seventy-one years old and moved through brush like a rumor, all suggestion and no evidence. "Blisters now are amputations later."

"She's cheerful," Sef murmured. His shoulder was strapped tight under his jacket, the depot bullet's souvenir still angry and hot beneath the dressing. He'd refused to stay behind at the cabin, and Vashti had backed him, which surprised everyone including Vashti. "The boy walks or he dies slow," she'd said, which was apparently her version of bedside manner. "Better he dies fast on a mountain doing something."

Tam shouldered the heaviest pack without being asked—he always took the heaviest pack, as if weight were a debt he was paying down—and Elodie did a last count of the rope, the pitons, the tinned food we'd carried up from the depot raid that had cost us Sef's blood and very nearly more.

"Four days to the pass if the weather holds," Elodie said. "Three if it doesn't and we're stupid. Five if we're careful." She looked at each of us in turn, the way she did before a dive, checking for panic the way a pilot checks gauges. "Nobody's stupid. Nobody's careless. We move like the mountain is trying to kill us, because it is, and so is everything behind us."

Optimism, Quintana-style. I found it oddly steadying.

We started climbing.

* * *

The first day was kind to us, which I distrusted immediately. Kindness from terrain is just cruelty saving its breath.

Vashti led us up a drainage she called the Old Cut—a logging slash from some concession attempt thirty years back, long abandoned, the stumps rotted soft as bread. It gave us grade without exposure, cover without deadfall, and by midday we'd made six kilometers of what the map promised would be the easiest ground of the whole crossing. Sef kept pace. That was the day's real victory. Every hour I caught myself listening—not for him falling behind, but for the small hitch in his breathing that would mean infection winning. So far: nothing. Tam checked the dressing at every halt with the tenderness of a man defusing something, and Sef bore it with the particular patience of the young being fussed over, which is to say he complained constantly and meant none of it.

It was me who heard the aircraft first. Not because my ears are better—they're not—but because I'd spent the morning listening for exactly that instead of letting the mountain fill my head the way it wanted to.

A drone. High, thin, circling two ridges east.

"Cover," Vashti said, already moving, no drama in it, the way you'd say *lunch*. We folded into a stand of black spruce while the sound swelled and faded and swelled again, patient as tide. Through a gap in the canopy I watched it pass—a fixed-wing airframe, company gray, flying a search grid so methodical I could have plotted it myself. They were working the river valley below us, sweeping the banks where our scuttled boat had washed ashore in pieces.

"They'll find the wreck of the *Maren* today or tomorrow," Elodie said quietly. We'd named the skiff after nobody; she'd named it after the archivist's brother, though she hadn't told Maren that. "They'll find the dive gear we left. Then they'll know we went up, not down."

"And then the trackers come," Tam said. "On foot. Men who like this country."

"I like this country," Vashti said.

"That's what worries me."

When the drone finally droned off westward, we climbed another three hours in silence broken only by boot-scrape and breathing, and made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang that Vashti swore had held snow-shadow since before the concession existed. Cold camp—no fire, cold rations, the recorder passed between watches like a sleeping infant nobody wanted to wake.

I took second watch. The stars over the Serrats were obscene in their quantity, the Milky Way thick as spilled milk, and I sat there thinking about how the same sky hung over the tracker teams' fire, wherever they were, and how the light reaching both of us had left its star before any of this started, before the survey vessel, before the permits, before men decided a river was worth more dead than alive.

Elodie relieved me at midnight. Before she took the watch she crouched next to me and said, "You plotted us a good line today."

"It was Vashti's line."

"You translated it. There's a difference." She squeezed my shoulder once, briefly, the way you'd test a mooring line, and settled in facing downslope, toward whatever was coming.

* * *

Day two, the mountain stopped being polite.

The Old Cut ended at a headwall where the drainage simply gave up, and Vashti took us east along a shelf she called the Ledge—which turned out to be generous nomenclature for a sloping ramp of shattered limestone maybe two meters wide in its best sections and considerably less in its worst. Below the Ledge, the slope fell away four hundred meters into a boulder field that from above looked like the mountain had vomited its own teeth.

"Rope," Vashti said, and clipped herself to the line she'd rigged while the rest of us were still staring. "One at a time. Three points of contact. If you slip, you fall inward, toward the wall, and you shout nothing, because shouting makes people turn their heads and turning heads is how two people die instead of one."

"Mercy is expensive up here," Tam muttered, echoing the thought I hadn't yet said aloud. He went second, after Vashti, so he could anchor the rest of us.

Sef went fourth. I watched him cross with his bad arm tucked close, using his legs and his good shoulder, jaw set, eyes never leaving his feet except once—once—to look across the gap at the view, the whole Kestrel valley laid out below us, silver and winding and poisoned, and I understood the look completely. You climb toward something like that partly to see it. To see what you're leaving and what you're carrying.

I crossed last, coiling the rope, and when I came up beside them Vashti was standing very still, head tilted, listening.

"What?" Elodie breathed.

"Cairn," Vashti said. "Downslope. Someone built one recently enough that the lichen hasn't taken." She moved to it with that rumor-quiet walk and knelt. It was a small stack, five stones, deliberate. Under the top stone, wedged flat: a strip of cloth, sun-bleached, and a tin tag stamped with a survey-team marking.

Nobody spoke for a moment. We all knew whose those were. We'd listened to the voices in the box. We knew how the survey team's story ended—or thought we did. This said they'd gotten farther than the water. This said some of them had run for the mountains, and built trail markers, and hoped.

"They came this way," Sef said softly. "Trying for the pass."

"Some of them," Vashti said. She put the tag back under the stone, carefully, the way you'd close a coffin lid. "The pass has a name among the old crews. Pass of Names. Now you know why." She stood, and her face in the gray light was unreadable, which from Vashti was itself a kind of eloquence. "We're walking in the tracks of people who didn't make it. Walk better."

We walked better.

* * *

The trackers found our scent on day three, or rather their dogs did—we heard the baying at dawn, faint and wrong-sounding in the thin air, coming up the drainage we'd left the day before. A half day behind us. Maybe less. Dogs don't stop for blisters.

"Two teams, probably," Vashti said, reading the sound the way I read current. "They split at the Old Cut mouth. One's following our line. The other will try to cut ahead to the pass—that's the smart play, and these aren't dumb men. Concession security hires locals when it needs mountain work. Men I trained, some of them." Something moved behind her eyes when she said *trained*, and I decided not to ask.

"So we don't take the direct route," Elodie said.

"We take the direct route faster." Vashti unrolled her map on a flat rock and weighted the corners with stones. Her finger traced a line that made my stomach drop just looking at it. "The Scarp. It's exposed, it's loose, and if anyone's watching it through a scope, we're dead on it. But it cuts six hours off the approach to the col, and six hours is the difference between beating that cold front and wearing it."

"The front," I said. I'd been watching the clouds build all morning over the western peaks—lenticulars stacking like plates, that smooth lens shape that means wind screaming at altitude and weather inbound. "How bad?"

"First snow. Maybe tonight, certainly tomorrow. After that, the passes start closing in earnest, and the permit clock—" she tapped the map, where Registry sat beyond the range like a promise someone else intended to break, "—runs out regardless of what the sky does. Snow doesn't care about your deadlines. It just ends them."

Tam looked at the Scarp line for a long time. "Sef?"

"I'm fine," Sef said, in the tone of a man who was approximately sixty percent fine and lying upward.

"He's fine," Tam translated.

"We go," Elodie said. And then, because she is who she is, she added: "But we rope everything, and if anyone says stop, we stop. Even if stopping kills us. That's the rule. The mountain gets a vote but not a veto."

* * *

The Scarp was exactly as advertised, which is to say it was a place where the mountain had been having an argument with gravity for ten thousand years and neither side had won. Loose shale over slab, angle steep enough that you climbed with your hands more than your feet, the whole slope whispering and ticking around you as stones shifted under weight. We roped at fifteen-meter spacing and moved in Vashti's rhythm—one voice calling holds, the rest echoing them down the line, a litany of *solid, solid, loose, solid* that became almost prayerful.

Halfway up, the slope spoke louder.

A section the size of a door let go under Tam, and for one full second he rode it, arms pinwheeling, before he threw himself sideways onto a slab and locked there, breathing hard, pebbles rattling down past Sef below him and away into the abyss with a fading hiss.

"Solid?" I called, hating the word even as I used it.

"Solid," Tam called back, in a voice that suggested the slab and he had reached an understanding of limited duration.

We froze for two minutes after that, all of us plastered to the slope, listening—for more sliding, for the drone, for anything. What we got instead was Sef, from below, laughing. Quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a man whose body had decided fear was a luxury it could no longer afford.

"What," Tam demanded.

"Nothing. Nothing, it's just—I got shot in a supply depot two weeks ago and *this* is the part that scares me."

"Shooting's personal," Tam said, resuming the climb. "Rockfall doesn't care about you at all. It's honest, at least."

"There's a lesson in there," Elodie said, "and I refuse to learn it."

We topped out onto the col approach as the light went copper and strange, the lenticulars overhead now glowing from within like lamps. Behind us, far below in the drainage, I caught it once through the binoculars: movement. Two figures, maybe three, tiny with distance, moving with the unhurried competence of men who knew exactly where they were going because they knew exactly where we were going too.

Half a day. Maybe less. And the front arriving sometime in the night to erase every track, every cairn, every trace—including, possibly, us.

"Camp here or push for the col?" Tam asked.

Elodie studied the sky, then the ridge, then each of us. I watched her do the arithmetic I'd watched her do a hundred times on the river—depth versus bottom time, risk versus reward—except now the currency was daylight and the exchange rate was lives.

"We push," she said. "If the storm catches us on the col, we dig in and ride it. If it catches us here, we're pinned in the open with trackers behind us." She shouldered her pack. "Besides. Somebody built cairns on that pass hoping someone would follow. Might as well be us."

We climbed into the dying light, five small stubborn shapes on a blackening ridge, and behind us the first flakes came down fine and dry as ash—the mountain beginning, gently, almost kindly, to bury the evidence.

Ahead, on the col, something glinted. Glass, catching the last sun.

Binoculars. Watching.

Waiting to see if we were stupid enough to keep coming.
