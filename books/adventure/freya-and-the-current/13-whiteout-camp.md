## 13. Whiteout Camp

The storm did not arrive so much as it was always there, waiting above the treeline like a creditor who knows your schedule.

We'd made nine miles that morning—good miles, hard-won miles, the kind Vashti would have called "honest" if she were still with us—and then the sky closed like a ledger. One minute I could see the ridge line to the west, black and clean against a pewter sky. The next minute there was no west. There was no ridge. There was white, moving sideways with intent, and the temperature dropped so fast my teeth ached in their roots.

"Camp," Tam said. Not a suggestion. Tam doesn't suggest things when the wind is doing forty knots; he states them, the way gravity states itself.

We dug into the lee of a granite shoulder and built what mountaineers politely call a bivouac and what I call a hole you hope not to die in. Two tarps stretched between ice axes and ski poles, snow walls packed knee-high, both sleeping bags shared between three bodies because we'd left half our kit burned on the riverbank along with the *Maren Kade* and everything else we used to be. Ilsa got us oriented by compass before her fingers stopped working properly, which took about four minutes. Then we sat in our hole in the mountain and listened to the world erase itself.

"Sef," I said. "Talk to me."

He was curled against Tam's side, which was where he'd been since the col, since Vashti turned around with a rifle and told us—in that dry hydrologist's voice, as if reporting a flow rate—that we'd better keep moving or her sacrifice would be wasted on people who dawdled. Sef had been quiet ever since. Quiet is normal for Sef. But this was a different quiet. This was the quiet of a nineteen-year-old running a fever of a hundred and two and pretending, for our benefit, that he wasn't.

"I'm fine," he said.

"He's lying," Tam said.

"I'm lying," Sef agreed, and then laughed, a small cracked sound, and I felt something in my chest pull tight like a knot cinched down.

Ilsa found the wound under his jacket by feel, in the dark, without complaint. The depot bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder two days ago, and we'd cleaned it with vodka and optimism, and apparently optimism had run out first. The skin around the entry was hot and tight and wrong-colored, and when Ilsa pressed near it Sef made a sound he tried very hard to turn into a cough.

"It's infected," she reported. "Properly. We need antibiotics."

"We need a hospital," Tam said. "Since we're listing needs."

"We need to move tomorrow," I said.

Nobody argued, which was its own kind of argument. Above the treeline in a storm, movement kills you. Staying put kills you slower. Those are the options; the mountain presents them without commentary.

* * *

The first day of the storm, we spent our calories telling stories.

This is a thing cold does to people. It strips away the ordinary insulation between one mind and another. You huddle for warmth and the warmth gets into your words too, and suddenly you're saying things you'd never say at sea level, in daylight, to colleagues. I've seen hardened salvage divers confess love affairs, crimes, and childhood fears into the dark of a dive boat cabin, all because the water outside was black and the heater was dying and there was nothing else to do but be honest.

So we rationed food—half a brick of climbing cheese each, a fistful of oats eaten dry because melting snow for cooking cost fuel we couldn't spare—and Ilsa talked about her sister in Vellport who thought she worked marine insurance, and how she'd have to decide eventually whether the truth would disappoint her more than the lie. And Sef, feverish and glassy-eyed, talked about his father's fishing boat, the *Little Mercy*, and how he'd learned to hold his breath in the hold among the ice tanks, betting older deckhands he could outlast them, and how his father had died not at sea but in a hospital bed, which Sef considered a professional embarrassment.

"And you?" Sef asked me, turning those fever-bright eyes my way. "You know everything about everybody. You collect secrets like other people collect bottle caps. What's yours?"

"I don't have any," I said. "I'm an open book. A very boring book. Mostly about mooring knots."

"Liar," said all three of them, almost in unison, and even through the misery of it I felt something loosen in my chest—a laugh trying to happen, the body insisting on hope.

But they let it go. That night. They let it go, and I lay between Ilsa and Tam listening to the wind try to peel the mountain apart, and I understood that they were being polite now so they could be merciless later, and that later was coming.

* * *

The second day, the storm settled in like it owned the lease.

Sef was worse. He'd stopped shivering in the night, which Ilsa flagged immediately as bad news—the body giving up on thermoregulation, spending its last reserves elsewhere. We wrapped him in every layer we could strip off ourselves and still function, and Tam held him against his chest inside the shared bag like a man warming a bird, and Sef drifted in and out, mumbling about the pass, about cairns, about names. He'd been saying names in his sleep. Names none of us recognized. I chose to believe they were fishing crew from the *Little Mercy* and not something worse.

By midday the food situation had resolved itself into arithmetic: one tin of fish, six oat packets, a stub of cheese, and a chocolate bar Ilsa had been hoarding with the discipline of a customs officer. Two days' food for three functioning adults and one burning boy, if the storm broke tomorrow. If it didn't—

"Don't," Tam said, reading my face. "Don't do the math out loud. Math out loud is how crews die."

"Math out loud is how crews plan."

"Math out loud is how crews panic." He shifted Sef gently. "Elodie. Come here a minute."

There was a tone in his voice I hadn't heard in eleven years of working together. Tam has maybe four tones: gruff, gruffer, sarcastic, and the rare one he uses when an engine is about to fail catastrophically and he wants everyone calm while it happens. This was the fourth tone, pointed at me.

I crawled over. Outside, the wind screamed its one long note. Inside, Tam looked at me across the space of eighteen inches, which in a storm shelter might as well be a courtroom.

"You're going to ask me for something," he said. "Tomorrow, or the day after. When it gets bad enough. You're going to ask me to leave him."

The cold went through me in a way the weather hadn't managed.

"No," I said.

"You'll dress it up. You'll say we can't carry him fast enough to beat the front, that slowing down kills all three instead of one, that he'd want—" 

"Tam."

"—and I'm telling you now, before you spend the breath, so you can save it: the answer's no. And if you want to know why, really know why, then you should finally hear what I've been carrying since Port Calloway."

I didn't say anything. The wind filled the silence for me.

"You remember the *Coriander*," he said.

Of course I remembered the *Coriander*. Everyone in coastal salvage remembered the *Coriander*, though most of them only knew the legend: the ore barge that went down off the Serrat coast with nine souls, hull breach in a calm sea, no distress call, no explanation. The wreck that made insurers invent new categories of suspicion. I was twenty-four. I'd just lost my own boat—my father's boat—to a bankruptcy that smelled of arson, and I was hungry in the way young people get hungry, which is to say dangerously.

"I ran the engine room on a tug that winter," Tam said. "Legit work, mostly. But the owner, Doss Meric—he did side jobs. Moving cargo that didn't want manifests. People, sometimes. And that March, he came to me with a job. Simple. Take the tug out past the shelf buoy, meet the *Coriander*, transfer some drums, come home. Triple pay. No questions clause right there in the handshake."

My stomach went cold in a way unrelated to altitude.

"The drums were ballast," he said. "That's what I was told. That's what I believed, right up until the *Coriander* listed. Right up until I heard the screaming carry across the water, because sound carries over water at night, Elodie, it carries like nothing else, and there were people on that barge who weren't supposed to be there, and Doss's plan—the plan I helped make possible—was that nobody would ever be able to prove they'd existed."

"You testified," I said slowly. "After. There was an inquiry. You—"

"I lied at the inquiry." His voice didn't waver, but his hands, wrapped around Sef's sleeping form, had gone rigid. "I gave them Doss. Doss was guilty of plenty, God knows, and he hung for the parts he actually did. But I lied about the drums. I said I never loaded them. I said I was below when the transfer happened. Because if I admitted what I'd done, I'd have done ten years, and my daughter was six years old, and I told myself a lie that had a little girl standing on the end of it."

"Where is she now?"

"With her mother. In Vellport. She thinks I fix boats." A pause. "Which is even true, these days. That's the joke of it. Eleven years of honest work and I still can't tell her the true beginning of it."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you need to know what kind of man is saying no to you." He met my eyes. "I already drowned nine people once, Elodie. Not with my hands—but close enough that my hands have never felt clean since. I am not leaving that boy on this mountain. Whatever it costs. Whatever it slows. I will carry him on my back through the pass if his legs go, and if the trackers catch us because of it, then they catch us, and at least I'll stop owing the dead."

The wind howled. Sef murmured something in his fever—I caught the word *buoy* and the word *father*—and Tam's whole body bent around the sound like grass around a stone.

"That's why you signed on with me," I said. It wasn't a question. "Eleven years ago. Broke, disgraced, one rusted dive skiff. You could've worked anywhere."

"You posted a notice at the harbor office. 'Crew wanted, fair shares, no questions asked.'" The ghost of a smile moved under his frost-chapped beard. "First time in my life I'd seen those words meant honestly. Everybody else who says 'no questions' means 'because the answers are criminal.' You meant it because you figured a man's past was his own ballast. I decided I'd haul your boat until I died or paid it off. Some days I'm not sure which comes first."

I reached across and put my hand over his knuckles, both of ours buried in the sleeping bag's warmth, and neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing else, and because in the economy of men like Tam, that confession had cost more than the gold in the strongbox ever would.

Secrets are currency. I've always known that—it's how I fund expeditions, trading what I know to people who need to know it. But I'd never had someone spend one on me like that. Not to buy anything. Just to show me the mint where his guilt was coined.

"All right," I said finally. "Nobody gets left. New rule. Write it in the ship's articles."

"We don't have a ship."

"Then write it somewhere that counts."

* * *

The storm broke at dawn on the third day, the way storms do—abruptly, almost apologetically, leaving behind a world so clean and silent it felt like sacrilege. Blue sky. Sun on snow so bright it hurt. The Serrat Range stood revealed around us in every direction: white fangs, blue shadows, and somewhere north, past the Pass of Names, the descent toward Registry and whatever waited for us there.

Sef woke lucid for the first time in thirty hours, which frightened me more than the fever had. Lucid meant he could feel how bad his shoulder had gotten. He looked down at the angry red streaks crawling toward his armpit and said, very calmly:

"So. Antibiotics."

"There's a concession patrol station at the foot of the pass," Ilsa said. She'd been awake, I realized. She'd probably been awake all night, compass in hand, planning. "Medical locker. Vashti's maps show it. It'll be stocked—it's a company station, they stock for emergencies."

"A company station," Tam repeated flatly. "Meaning guards."

"Meaning guards," Ilsa agreed. "Or meaning empty, if the concession's pulling staff downhill ahead of permit expiry. Every post on these maps has been thinning out since Mile 40. They're consolidating. They may have already abandoned it."

"Or they haven't," I said, "and we walk into the arms of the people hunting us, with our shooter barely able to lift a rifle."

"Or," Sef said, and his voice had that cracked, feverish steadiness that I was learning to dread, "you leave me here with the tent and the fish tin, and the three of you move fast and light, and come back for me on the way down."

"No," Tam and I said together, so quickly and so identically that Sef blinked.

"New rule," Tam added. "Ship's articles."

"We don't have a—"

"Articles," Tam said, in the tone that ends arguments, and began breaking camp.

I stood a moment longer, looking south the way we'd come, back down the long white slope toward the col where Vashti had turned around with a rifle and a nod. Somewhere down there, the wind was erasing her tracks, erasing the narrows, erasing whatever had happened after we crested out of rifle range and forced ourselves not to look back. The wilderness forgives nothing. But it keeps faith. I'd promised myself that on the riverbank, watching our old life burn, and I intended to keep being worth its faith a while longer.

"Elodie." Ilsa handed me the map, her gloved finger on a small square marked with faded survey ink. "Patrol station. Twelve miles. Downhill most of the way."

Twelve miles. One feverish diver. One ex-con carrying more than luggage. One navigator with a plan and one captain with a promise she'd made twelve hours ago and already meant more than her own name.

I folded the map.

"Then let's go get our antibiotics," I said, "and pray the company forgot to lock the medicine cabinet on its way out the door."

Behind us, the storm's wreckage glittered innocently in the sun. Ahead, the pass waited with its cairns and its names. And below it all, twelve miles of downhill, a locked door with company paint on it—and either salvation or a gunfight, depending entirely on whether the concession had finished abandoning its sins yet.
