## 14. The Pass of Names

Here is what fever does to you: it makes the world honest.

Not kinder — honest. When I was small my grandmother told me that sickness strips a man down to what he actually believes, and everything else is just clothing. I didn't understand her then. I understood her at fourteen thousand feet, with my shoulder cooking itself from the inside and the sky so close I could have bitten it, walking through a field of stone piles that all had something to say to me.

I want to be clear that I knew they were cairns. Even delirious, some part of me kept its footing on the factual side of the fence. Cairns. Stacked rocks. Trail markers left by climbers, by surveyors, by smugglers and pilgrims and every other kind of fool who ever looked at the Serrat Range and thought *yes, through there.* The rational part of me catalogued them the way Tam had taught me to catalogue salvage: count them, note their condition, move on.

The other part of me — the part running a temperature of a hundred and four — heard them talking.

*You're late,* said the first one, a knee-high stack of slate near the switchback where the trail left the last stunted pine behind. *We waited.*

*Everyone's late,* said a second, taller, its stones furred with lichen the color of old bruises. *That's how we know you're coming.*

Ilsa walked ahead of me, breaking trail through six inches of crusted snow, and every hundred meters she'd stop and look back, and I'd give her the nod, and she'd keep going. I had gotten very good at the nod. It's a versatile instrument, the nod. It can mean *I'm fine.* It can mean *don't ask.* It can mean *if you stop for me again I will sit down in this snow and you will have to carry me, and you cannot carry me, because Vashti is gone and Tam is carrying most of the gear and Elodie is carrying the recorder like it's a sleeping child, so please, please, keep walking.*

Mostly it meant that last one.

* * *

Let me tell you about the fever honestly, since honesty is apparently the theme of the day. It started as heat in the shoulder — Sef's shoulder, shot two days ago at the depot, a through-and-through that should have been nothing, that would have been nothing if we'd had antibiotics and warmth and rest instead of a cold camp and river water boiled over a juniper fire. By the morning we entered the foothills it had become a red ladder climbing my collarbone, each rung a little hotter than the last. By the time we hit snowline, the ladder reached my jaw and the rungs started moving.

Fever has a texture. Mine was wet. Everything was wet — the air, the light, the inside of my skull. Sounds arrived delayed, like the mountain was broadcasting me back to myself off a distant relay. And underneath the wetness ran a current of perfect, lucid clarity, arriving in flashes, the way lightning arrives inside a storm. In those flashes I understood things completely and then lost them completely, and the losing was worse than the fever, because for half a second I'd know exactly who I was and exactly how much trouble we were in, and then the water would close over it again.

In one flash I understood the cairns.

* * *

There were hundreds of them. That's the thing nobody tells you about high passes in country like this — the ones that sit on the edge of maps, the ones with no names on any chart we owned. They accumulate. Every expedition that ever tried the shortcut, the escape route, the road-not-on-the-invoice, left its stones. Some were neat, deliberate, three or four courses of flat rock set by someone with time and calm. Others were single boulders perched on boulders, shoved upright by hands too cold and too frightened for masonry. A few were just rubble heaped in a rough cone, the way you'd pile stones if you were doing it fast, in wind, while looking over your shoulder.

Vashti had told us about the pass before the col took her. She'd sat across our smoke-thin fire at Mile 40 and drawn it in spilled tea on the tabletop: *the trail goes up the north rib, crosses below the seracs, and there's a saddle — the locals used to call it the Pass of Names, though your map won't say so. People marked it. That's all a name is, girl. A pile of stones that says somebody was here.*

Somebody was here. Everybody was here. That was the problem.

*Count them,* Tam had said at the treeline, not joking, or rather joking in the way Tam jokes, which is to say as a form of navigation. *Cairns mean traffic. Traffic means the route works. But count them anyway, because if they stop, we've left the route.*

So I counted. Fever-math is its own discipline. I counted in the flashes of clarity and guessed between them, and somewhere above the eleventh switchback my count passed ninety and stopped being arithmetic and became liturgy. Ninety-one. Ninety-two. Each one a person or a party or a hope. Each one saying, in the only language stone knows: *we came this far.*

*This far,* agreed the cairn at the base of the rib, *and no farther.*

*Shut up,* I told it, out loud, and Ilsa glanced back, and I gave her the nod.

* * *

The seracs came after noon — blue-green walls of ice hanging off the eastern shoulder, groaning now and then in a register I felt in my teeth, or maybe in my fever, hard to say. We crossed beneath them in single file, fast, the way Vashti had drilled us: *don't bunch up, don't stop, don't look up.* I looked up. Of course I looked up. I'm twenty-three years old and shot and feverish; looking up is exactly the sort of decision available to me.

What I saw made the fever go quiet for a moment, which I recommend if you can arrange it.

Ice, yes. But also, wedged in a crevasse mouth forty feet above the trail: a rusted frame. Aluminum tubing, the ghost of a sledge or a stretcher, half-swallowed by thirty years of ice. And beside the trail itself, half-buried in a drift, a corner of green canvas. Survey green. The same green as the tarps we'd seen rotting on the wreck in the Kestrel, the same green stamped with the logo of the company whose name appeared on no permit anyone would show us.

Elodie saw it too. She stopped — she wasn't supposed to stop, none of us were supposed to stop — and knelt, and brushed snow from the canvas with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd touch a face.

"Boots," she said. "Under here."

Nobody said anything. There wasn't anything to say. The survey team hadn't all died on the water. Some of them had gotten out. Some of them had climbed. Some of them had come up this exact rib, under these exact seracs, carrying whatever they could carry — evidence, probably, the same evidence we were carrying, or the human remains of colleagues, or just themselves — aiming for this pass, for the registry beyond it, for the same justice we were aiming for.

And they had stopped here.

*Welcome,* said the cairns, all of them at once, in the voice the fever built for them, which was my own voice pitched down and slowed. *We've been expecting you for thirty years.*

* * *

I need to describe the big one. The central cairn, at the saddle approach, where the trail bent west into the final climb. Everyone who writes about mountains writes about summits, and everyone is wrong; the important places are the saddles, the places between, the doors. This door had a keeper.

It stood man-high and wide as a cart, built of river-rounded stones hauled — hauled, at altitude, by hand — from the valley below, because there are no rounded stones up there, only sharp ones. Someone had carried every one of those smooth rocks uphill. Around its base lay smaller cairns arranged in an arc, dozens of them, like a congregation. Like mourners. And driven into the frozen ground at its foot, a steel stake, and wired to the stake, a plate. Corroded almost blank, but I got close enough — closer than I should have, Ilsa pulling at my pack strap, me shrugging her off, the fever making me brave in the stupid way — and read the letters that survived:

**K— R— SURVEY / SEVEN SOULS / WE TRIED**

Seven souls. The survey team had been nine. Two had died on the water, in the "accident," in the screaming cut short on the recorder Elodie carried against her chest like a second heartbeat. Seven had climbed out. Seven had made it past the burned checkpoints, up the milky river's tributaries, into the foothills, onto this trail, past ninety-odd cairns of earlier failures, to this door.

And then winter, or the company's trackers, or the altitude, or all three in concert, had closed the door behind them.

"We tried," I said aloud. My voice came out cracked and strange, a stranger's voice. "Yeah. We're trying too."

The fever translated the silence that followed. In the silence, the whole pass spoke — not spooky, not supernatural, I want to be clear about that, I don't believe in ghosts, or I didn't, or I believe in them differently now — it spoke the way any accumulation speaks. A library speaks. A graveyard speaks. Ninety generations of stacked stone saying the one thing stone says: *you are not the first, and unless you finish what you carry, you will not be the last, and the pile will grow by one.*

* * *

Here's the thing about communion with the dead: it doesn't comfort you. I'd expected — I don't know what I'd expected. Some cold peace. Instead what I got was anger, arriving clean through the fever like a blade through wet cloth.

They had done everything right. The survey team. They'd found the contamination, documented it, recorded it, and when the company decided to drown the proof along with them, seven of them had the wit and the guts to get out of the water. They'd crossed country we've only begun to suffer through. They'd carried their own strongbox — or copies, or testimony, or simply themselves, witnesses being evidence enough — toward the same registry office, under the same expiring permits, racing the same first snow.

And the snow had won. Or the bullets had. Either way, the permits expired, the concession reset, the records became company property, and the crime went into its thirtieth year of being called an accident. All that effort, all that courage, all those stones carried uphill — spent. Filed under weather.

And now here we were, four half-dead salvagers re-running their route with their recorder, and the mountain offering me, in my fever, a simple accounting: *the difference between you and them is nothing yet. Nothing yet. You haven't crested. You haven't descended. You haven't broadcast. Right now you are exactly seven souls' worth of unfinished business, and the pile is patient.*

I stood at the big cairn longer than I should have. Long enough that Tam came back for me, took my arm across his shoulders without asking, and walked me up the bend. He smelled of woodsmoke and engine oil and effort. He didn't say anything clever. What he said was: "Save your breath, kid. You'll want it at the top." Which is Tam for *I know, I see it too, we'll grieve later, grief keeps.*

But I made myself a promise there, standing in the arc of mourners with my shoulder on fire and my head underwater. I promised the seven souls, and the ninety cairns, and the two still screaming on the tape, and Vashti holding the narrows behind us with a rifle and no chance. I promised them that if I had to crawl the last mile on the arm that still worked, the recordings would reach the transmitter. That this time the pile would not grow. That this time somebody would come back down the other side and send word, and someday someone would stand at this saddle and add one stone — one stone, deliberate, calm — that meant *finished.*

* * *

The last hour is mostly missing. Fever takes payment in memory; that's the exchange rate. I have fragments: the wind rising, singing through the saddle rocks in a thin whistle like breath through teeth. Ilsa's hand on my elbow, steady as a rail. Elodie ahead, small against the sky, the recorder's case strapped tight to her back, her silhouette bent into the wind the way a flag bends. Snow starting — fine, dry, horizontal, the leading edge of the front Vashti had warned us about, the deadline written in weather, arriving on schedule.

And I have this: at the crest, when the pass finally opened beneath us — the long western descent, scree and shadow, and far below, impossibly far, a thread of valley and the idea of the lowlands and the registry — I stopped counting cairns. Because there weren't any more. The trail down was bare. Whatever came over this pass and survived hadn't needed to mark it. Or whatever came over and died had fallen somewhere the stones couldn't follow.

"Almost there," Ilsa said, and her voice had a crack in it, and I couldn't tell if the crack was hers or mine or the fever's.

Then Tam, who had gone ahead to scout the descent line, came back up the trail at a pace that erased every tired bone in my body, because Tam did not move like that unless the news was bad.

He dropped to a crouch beside us, breathing hard, and pointed down-slope to where the trail narrowed between rock walls — the col, the choke point, the one place the mountain made us walk in single file with no cover and no way around.

"There's smoke," he said. "Down in the narrows. Fresh."

Behind us, the storm swallowed the pass and all its names. Ahead, somebody was waiting in the one door we had left.
