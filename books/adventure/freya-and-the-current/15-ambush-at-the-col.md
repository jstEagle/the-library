## 15. Ambush at the Col

There's a saying among engineers that I've lived by most of my life: everything fails eventually, and your job is just to be somewhere else when it does. I've applied that philosophy to marriages, business ventures, and one memorable stint running engines for a man who turned out to be a war criminal with excellent taste in generators. It's kept me alive through things that should have killed me.

Standing in the wind at the top of the Serrat Range, watching Vashti study the col ahead of us like a woman reading her own death warrant, I felt the old philosophy start to crack.

* * *

We'd come up out of Whiteout Camp on the third morning, when the storm finally spat us loose and the sky went that hard, rinsed blue that means the cold has stopped fooling around. Sef was walking on his own, which was either a miracle or a lie—we'd find out which. His shoulder had gone bad in the tent, hot to the touch, and he'd spent two days sweating through fever dreams about his mother's kitchen, apologizing to people who weren't there. Tam, he'd say when he surfaced, don't tell Elodie. As if I hadn't been the one changing his dressings while she slept three feet away.

The pass itself was called—by Vashti—the Pass of Names, though she said the name wasn't official, that no map carried it. The survey team had called it something else, she thought. Something corporate and bland. But the cairns along its spine had names scratched into them, or sometimes just dates, or sometimes nothing at all, just stones stacked by hands that wanted to be remembered by somebody, anybody, even strangers passing through on their way to die.

Vashti led us up the last pitch with the economy of a woman who'd walked this ground before it belonged to anyone worth fearing. She moved like an old river: you didn't notice how fast she was going until you looked back and saw how far behind you'd fallen.

"The col," she said, when we reached the saddle, "is the only place they can take us cheaply."

I liked that she said *cheaply* instead of *easily*. It suggested she'd already done the arithmetic on what taking us would cost them, and she intended to inflate the price.

"Options?" Elodie asked. Elodie always asked for options even when there weren't any. It was a courtesy she extended to the universe.

"We go over the shoulder, north side. Adds six hours, maybe eight with Sef." Vashti squinted into the wind. "Or we cross here, open ground, four hundred meters, and pray."

"I'm tired of praying," Elodie said. "It hasn't been paying out."

"It pays out eventually," Vashti said. "Just not always to you."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about high altitude: sound behaves strangely. Or maybe it doesn't behave strangely and I just noticed it for the first time because for once in my life I was listening properly. A rock clattered somewhere below the north shoulder—a small sound, a pebble sound—and it arrived at my ears twice, once immediately and once a half-second later, off the cliff face across the valley.

Two sounds from one stone means two surfaces. Two surfaces means someone else's boots.

"Elodie." I didn't whisper. Whispering carries worse than talking; everyone knows this except people in films. "North shoulder. We've got company."

She didn't ask how I knew. That's one of the reasons I'd have followed her into hell, and have, more or less. She just looked where I was looking, went very still in the way she does—all that salvage-diver stillness, the kind you learn hanging in black water waiting for your eyes to adjust—and then said, quietly, "Down. Everyone down. Vashti—"

But Vashti was already moving, and not down. She was moving toward the eastern lip of the col, where a fracture in the rock made a slot maybe two meters wide, walls rising sheer on both sides. The narrows. Every mountain has one, if you're lucky, a place where geography does your fighting for you.

"They'll push us onto the saddle," she said. "Open ground, their rifles, our backs to the drop. So we don't give them the saddle. We give them the slot."

"And we go through it?" Sef asked. He was gray-faced and swaying, and I wanted to put him over my shoulder like a sack of cement but he'd have fought me, and neither of us had the strength left for that fight.

"We go through it," Vashti said. "Somebody makes them regret following."

Nobody said anything for a moment. In my experience those silences are where the real decisions happen, spoken ones being mostly theater afterward.

"I'll do it," I heard myself say, because apparently I was still capable of surprising myself. "I'm the slowest. You all know it. Slowest man holds the door."

"You're not holding any door," Elodie said. "Tam—"

"I ran guns through the Ansel Gap for eleven months," I said. "You knew that. Part of why you hired me, if we're honest with each other, which we rarely are. I know what a chokepoint costs and I know how to make a rifle last." I looked at Vashti. "Give me the slot and fifteen minutes."

Vashti looked at me for a long time. There was something in her face I couldn't read—not pity, not exactly respect. Recognition, maybe. One exile reading another.

"No," she said finally. "You're carrying Sef if he goes down. And Elodie needs you on the far side, because I'm sixty-three years old and my knees sound like gravel in a drum, and if it comes to running, you're the one who runs with the boy." She took the rifle off her shoulder—the old concession-issue thing she'd been cleaning every night like a rosary—and checked the magazine with a click that sounded very loud in the thin air. "The slot's mine. I've been walking toward this for nine years. Don't take it from me now that it's finally here."

"That's not—" Elodie started.

"It's exactly what it is," Vashti said. "Go."

* * *

I want to record accurately what happened next, because later there will be inquiries—I'm writing this knowing that—and inquiries flatten things. They turn sacrifice into diagram. So: it was cold enough that my exhaled breath crackled. The light was the flat silver of early afternoon. Sef smelled of fever and iodine. Elodie had the recorder wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, under her jacket, the way you carry a child in a country where children get stolen.

And Vashti walked to the slot, sat herself down behind a boulder with the ease of a woman settling into her own kitchen, and told us to stop wasting her daylight.

We crossed the saddle low and fast, bent double, four hundred meters of scree that tried to roll out from under every footstep. Halfway across, the shooting started.

Not at us—at the north shoulder, where our trackers had been flanking. Vashti had seen them before any of us did, or had guessed them, or had simply decided that if she was going to hold a narrows she might as well hold it against whoever came. Her rifle spoke in that unhurried way of hers, single shots, spaced like a metronome, and each shot was answered by silence or by shouting, and both were fine with me.

We made the far lip. I got Sef over it mostly by lifting and partly by threatening him, which he later claimed not to remember. Ilsa went last, walking backward, watching the slot, because Ilsa watches everything—that's her whole nature, a navigator's nature, the refusal to stop looking.

Through the gap, framed between the rock walls, I could see Vashti's boulder and a slice of sky above it. I could see muzzle flash. I could see, briefly, a figure in concession gray trying to work around the eastern wall, and I could see him stop trying.

"Vashti!" Elodie shouted it into the wind, and the wind took it apart.

From the slot, unhurried as ever: "Six hours to the tree line. Follow the cairns. Don't stop for weather, weather won't stop for you." A pause, filled with two more shots. Then, quieter, almost amused: "Tell them at the registry a hydrologist sent them. They'll want to know who to blame."

Elodie stood at the lip of that col with the truth of a murdered survey team pressed against her sternum, and she tried to go back. I know because I caught her arm, and I know because I've caught her arm before, in other years, in other water, and she fights like a diver—smooth, strong, committed—but she stops when you hold on long enough, because underneath everything she knows that the living are the cargo.

"He's hit," Ilsa said. Not Vashti—one of theirs, down on the shoulder, visible for a second between rocks. "She's holding them. She's actually holding them."

"She's out of rifle," I said, because I could hear it, the spacing stretching, the metronome winding down. A rifle tells you the truth about its situation if you listen.

What happened in the next ninety seconds I will set down plainly. Vashti fired until she had perhaps four rounds. Then she stopped firing, and her voice came out of the slot one more time, clear and level, pitched to carry: "They're coming around the west wall. Go now. All of you. This isn't a request, Elodie Dravenmoor, and I outrank you—I've been dead longer than you've been alive."

Then the shooting resumed, but different—shorter bursts, closer, uglier—and Ilsa grabbed Elodie's pack strap and I put my shoulder under Sef's good arm and we went down the south side of the Serrat Range at a pace that would have killed us on a good day and nearly did anyway.

Behind us the sound faded the way sound does at altitude, arriving late and thinner each time, until it stopped being gunfire and became memory. Somewhere in that fading, between one echo and the next, Vashti stopped answering. I don't know the minute. I want to know the minute. Engineers need the failure point; it's how we sleep at night, knowing exactly when and how the thing gave way. I will never have it. That is part of what she cost me, and I suspect she'd call it a bargain.

* * *

We descended through the afternoon in the ugly, stumbling gait of people who are running and pretending to hike. Sef lasted two hours on his feet and then his knees informed him of their resignation, and I carried him pickaback down switchbacks cut into the mountainside, his fever heat soaking through my jacket like a stovetop against my spine.

At some point he said, near my ear, "Did she—"

"Not yet," I said. Which was a lie, probably, but the kind you're allowed.

He was quiet a while. Then: "She counted me out of the water at Mile 40. When I cramped in the channel. Did you know that? First day. She just reached in and pulled me out like I weighed nothing."

"She weighed you plenty," I said. "She just didn't let it show. That was the whole trick of her."

Below us, the valley opened into the company's country—terraced slopes, tailings ponds glinting like dirty coins, the smoke of the town we were walking toward. Registry. Martial law. A front door that was barred. Everything the plan required us to enter anyway.

Elodie stopped once, on a ledge, and looked back up at the col. The light was going copper. From this distance the slot was invisible, just another shadow in the rock, and the mountain wore its violence the way it wore everything, without comment.

"She knew," Elodie said. Not asking. "Back at Mile 40. She knew where this ended."

"Everybody knows where it ends," I said. "That's not the question. The question is what you carry on the way." I shifted Sef's weight higher on my back. "Come on. We're her evidence now. Let's not get lost."

Elodie looked at the col a moment longer. Then she turned downhill, and we went, four ghosts and a recorder, descending into the machine that had killed the survey team and bought the river and erased the villages, carrying the one thing it couldn't buy.

The snow line was coming down to meet us. We could see it on the opposite slope, a white tide creeping lower with every hour, patient as paperwork.

By nightfall, the passes behind us would be closed.

By morning, so would everything else.
