## 16. Descent Into the Company Town

You can tell a lot about a mountain by what it leaves behind. The Serrat had spent three days spitting us back down its western face, and what it left us with was this: Sef feverish and gray-lipped on Tam's back, Vashti's absence like a missing tooth we kept probing with our tongues, and me navigating by a compass that had developed opinions of its own somewhere above the treeline.

"Left," I said, and corrected myself. "No. Right. The left is a cliff."

"Confidence inspiring," Tam muttered. He shifted Sef's weight higher. Sef made a sound that wasn't quite a word and wasn't quite a moan, and we all pretended it was neither.

We'd been descending since dawn through scrub pine and scree, following a drainage that Vashti had described to us back at Mile 40, back when she was alive to describe things. "The west fork," she'd said, drawing in the dirt with a stick. "Follow it down and you'll come out above the company road, two miles shy of Registry. There's an old survey cut through the trees. The company made it when they thought the mountain might make them rich. Then they found out it wouldn't, and they abandoned it, but the cut's still there, wide enough for a cart, and it runs straight as a sermon all the way to town."

Straight as a sermon. I thought about that now, picking our way down through the drainage, because the sermons I'd known had all been long and winding and ended somewhere you didn't want to be.

Sef had stopped talking sometime in the night. That was the thing that scared me. Sef without words was like a river without water—technically the same object, but wrong in every way that mattered. The wound in her shoulder had gone from angry to quiet, which Tam said was worse, and Tam had spent enough years patching up people who'd been shot in places they shouldn't have been shot that I took his word for it.

"Water," Sef said, suddenly, clearly, and we nearly wept with relief.

Tam lowered her against a boulder and held the canteen to her lips. She drank like a small animal, in quick guilty sips, watching us over the rim as if we might take it away.

"How are we doing?" she asked, which was her way of asking *how bad is it*, which was her way of asking *am I going to die*, which was the question none of us would answer honestly, so we answered it dishonestly in chorus: "Fine. Good. Almost there."

The recorder was wrapped in my spare shirt inside my pack, riding against my spine like a second heart. I'd taken it from Tam two days ago without discussion. That was the arrangement we'd fallen into without ever speaking it aloud: Tam carried Sef because Tam was strongest, Elodie walked point because Elodie couldn't stand to walk anywhere else, and I carried the recordings because I was the navigator, and navigation, at its root, is the discipline of deciding what matters and what can be lost. The recorder mattered. Everything else in my pack could go into a crevasse and I'd grieve for a day and sleep fine the night after.

We came down out of the drainage in the late afternoon, into the survey cut Vashti had promised us, and there it was: a corridor of even-aged birch growing up through an old logging swath, the ground underneath soft with thirty years of leaf-fall. It was the first easy walking we'd done in a week, and it should have felt like mercy. Instead it felt like being swallowed. The cut ran straight and level toward a glow on the horizon that wasn't quite sunset, and I understood, with the particular dread of a navigator seeing her destination for the first time, that the glow was the town.

"Light pollution," Elodie said. She'd stopped at the edge of the cut, one hand on a birch trunk, studying the horizon the way she studied a wreck from the surface—reading what the water was willing to tell her before committing to the dive.

"A town that size burns a lot of diesel," Tam said.

"A town that size shouldn't be burning *anything*," Elodie said. "Registry is a permit office and a company store. It's four hundred people when the survey crews are in. Vashti said half that now."

We stood there a moment, three wreck divers and a dying girl, looking at a sky that glowed wrong.

"Come on," Elodie said.

* * *

We reached the overlook above the town an hour after full dark, and I made Tam put Sef down so we could all look at what we'd walked off a mountain to reach, and I wish now that we hadn't looked, because seeing it didn't help. It only told us the size of the thing.

Registry sat in the valley like a thing that had been set down rather than grown. The company road came into it from the east, lit end to end with sodium lamps, and along the road, at intervals, were checkpoints. Not the burned-out skeletons we'd passed upriver—these were alive. Floodlights. Sandbags. Vehicles. I counted six checkpoints between the town limits and the cluster of buildings at the center, and at the center itself was a compound we'd never seen on any map: a ring of prefabricated buildings around a helipad, with two helicopters on it, rotors tied down, and a fence that caught the light and threw it back in a way that said *razor wire* even at this distance.

"That's new," Tam said.

"That's everything," Elodie said. "That's all new."

And it was. Vashti had described Registry as a tired little place, a company town in its long afternoon—shutters half-closed, a store, a registry hall with a brass plaque and a clerk who'd worked there forty years. What lay below us was a town under occupation. Vehicles moved through the streets in pairs. Men with rifles stood at intersections. And everywhere, everywhere, the lights.

"Permit expiry," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat. "It's in—" I did the math, and the math made my stomach drop. "Thirty-one hours. Noon the day after tomorrow."

"They're locking the vault," Tam said.

"They're locking the whole *town*," Elodie said.

I understood it then, all of it, in one cold flood. The permits didn't just expire—they expired *legally*, and when they did, everything inside the concession boundary became company property in the eyes of the law. The records. The wreck. The water. The dead. And a town full of witnesses, sealed behind six checkpoints with the permits running out, wasn't a town anymore. It was a filing cabinet. And we were standing on the ridge above it, planning to walk in through the front door of a filing cabinet and ask it, politely, to testify against its owner.

Sef started laughing. It was a thin, feverish sound, and it scared all of us.

"What?" Tam said.

"Six checkpoints," Sef said. "And we were going to *walk in*." She pressed her good hand over her eyes. "We came down a mountain for that. Vashti died on a mountain for *that*."

"Sef—"

"No. She stayed behind with a rifle so we could carry a box to a *building*, and the building's a *fortress*." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I want to go home. I want to go back to the boat that doesn't exist anymore and dive on wrecks that don't have dead people in them."

Nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie. The wind came up the valley and moved through the birches behind us, and below us the searchlights swept the company road in long patient strokes, back and forth, back and forth, like a lighthouse that had decided the sea was the enemy.

"We're not walking in the front door," Elodie said finally.

"Oh good," Tam said. "A plan."

"It's a start of a plan."

"Those are my favorite kind. The kind that don't exist yet."

Elodie crouched at the edge of the overlook, studying the town's geometry the way she'd study a hull breach—looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the pressure would find its way through. "The registry hall is on the north side of the square. Away from the compound. The old part of town. If there's a sewer, a storm drain, a culvert under the road—towns like this are built fast and cheap, and fast and cheap means gaps."

"Fast and cheap also means a company that cuts corners," I said. "Which means the same engineer who left the gaps probably filed the maps." I thought about it. "Vashti's cabin. She had company engineering surveys. Hydrology reports. If she kept the storm drainage maps for the valley—and she'd have kept them, she kept *everything*—"

"Then we know where the gaps are," Elodie said.

"Vashti's cabin is four days back the way we came," Tam said.

"Vashti's *cache*," I said. "She told us. Mile 40, the root cellar under the burned ranger station—she said if we ever needed paper, it was there. She wasn't talking about maps, but she kept her maps with her paper. It's two miles east of the road. We passed the turnoff this morning."

Elodie was quiet a moment. I watched her do the thing she does, the thing I'd followed her into wrecks for—the arithmetic of risk, run cold and fast, with no room for what anyone wants.

"Tonight we rest," she said. "Tomorrow we go for the cache. Tomorrow night we come back to this overlook, and we go in under the town, or we don't go in at all."

"And if there's no way under?" Sef asked. Her voice was small. "If the town's sealed and the maps are gone and we've got thirty hours and a fever?"

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched Sef's unwounded shoulder, lightly, the way you'd touch a hull to hear what it's telling you.

"Then we find another way," she said. "There's always another way. The water taught me that. Every wreck is sealed until it isn't."

* * *

We made cold camp in the birches, off the cut, back in the trees where the light from the valley couldn't find us. No fire. Tam doctored Sef's wound by flashlight held in his teeth, and I sat with my back against a trunk and held the recorder in my lap, and for a while I just listened to the town below us.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a company town at night: it hums. Generators. Pumps. The valley's whole water table ran through that place, and the company ran it, and the sound came up the slope like a heartbeat—steady, mechanical, indifferent. Somewhere down there was a registry hall with a brass plaque and a clerk, and somewhere down there was a helipad with helicopters that could be in the air in four minutes, and somewhere down there were men whose job, as of a satellite phone call two weeks ago, was to make sure we never arrived.

I turned the recorder over in my hands. It was nothing to look at. A gray box, dented, with a company stamp worn half away. The dead men's voices were inside it, screaming, and the machine held them the way amber holds insects—perfectly, indifferently, forever.

"Can't sleep either?" Tam lowered himself down next to me. He'd left Sef wrapped in both sleeping bags, fever-sweating but breathing evenly, and Elodie sitting watch with the rifle Vashti had left us.

"I keep thinking about the front door," I said. "The registry hall. There's supposed to be a clerk there. Maren, Vashti said. A woman who's worked there forty years. And I keep thinking—she's in that town tonight. Behind the checkpoints. With the lights on. And she doesn't know we're coming, and by noon the day after tomorrow, everything she's spent forty years filing becomes the company's property, and her brother becomes the company's property, and nobody will ever even be able to *prove* he existed inside that boundary."

Tam was quiet for a while.

"You know what I was thinking about?" he said. "The fuel depot."

"The what?"

"When we were on the overlook. I wasn't looking at the checkpoints. I was looking at the fuel depot—east side, by the road, big white tanks. Company towns like that, the fuel's the soft spot. Everything runs on diesel. The checkpoints, the generators, the helicopters. You take away the fuel, all those lights go out, and a town that's built on lights doesn't know how to be dark." He was quiet again. "If it came to it. If there was no way in and no way out and the clock ran down. A man could do a lot with a fuel depot and a bad attitude."

I looked at him. "That's not a plan, Tam. That's a funeral."

"Didn't say it was a plan. Said I was thinking about it." He picked up a twig and snapped it and dropped the pieces. "Vashti stayed behind so we could get here. I'm not planning to waste it. But I'm not planning to walk away empty, either. There's a difference between being careful and being done, and I'm a long way from done."

Below us, the searchlights swept the road. Back, and forth. Back, and forth. The town hummed its one note, patient as pressure at depth, and I understood that we were looking at the machine itself—not its hired guns, not its airboats and its burned camps, but the thing underneath all of that, the thing that had drowned a survey team and bought a river and put razor wire around a filing cabinet. It didn't hate us. That was the worst part. It didn't even know we were here. We were a rounding error to it, a variance, a line item that would resolve itself at noon the day after tomorrow when the permits expired and the law, which had been its law all along, closed like water over our heads.

"Get some sleep," Tam said. "Tomorrow we hunt for a map."

I slept in fragments, and in the gray hour before dawn, Elodie woke me with a hand on my shoulder, and her face in the flashlight's spill was doing something I'd only seen once before—on the wreck, at the moment she found the scuttle charges. Not fear. Recognition.

"Look at the road," she said.

I crawled to the edge of the overlook. The eastern sky was going the color of dishwater, and down in the valley the company road was coming alive—not with the paired patrols of the night before, but with a column. Trucks, maybe eight of them, coming in from the east at speed, and behind them, kicking up dust, an airboat trailer, and behind that a vehicle with a high antenna that I recognized because I'd spent a week hating everything about it.

Concession security. Coming down out of the hills. Coming *home*.

"They're coming back from the mountain," Elodie said. "All of them. They're pulling everyone in before expiry." She was quiet a second, and then she said the thing we were both thinking, said it flat, so it couldn't be mistaken for anything else:

"They're not locking the town because we're coming. They're locking the town because *everyone* is coming. Every loose end on that mountain is being gathered into one room, and the door closes at noon the day after tomorrow."

The column reached the first checkpoint and the barrier arm lifted and the trucks rolled through, and the searchlights swept the road, back and forth, back and forth, patient as the tide.

"We need that map," I said. "Today. Before they finish counting everything they think they own."

Elodie nodded once, already turning back to the trees, to where Tam was waking Sef, to the two miles of drainage between us and Vashti's cache and the last thirty hours of the law's patience.

"Then let's go get it," she said, "before the vault closes."
