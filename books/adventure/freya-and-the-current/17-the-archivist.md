## 17. The Archivist

The town of Registry was named by someone with no poetry in them at all, and I say that as a woman who once christened a dive skiff *Second Thoughts*. It sat in the valley mouth like a fist of gray stone, all company housing and company stores and a company clock tower that told company time. Every window on the main street had been shuttered since the lockdown began. Martial law has a particular smell—diesel, wet wool, and the specific silence of people who have decided not to be home.

We came down off the Serrat scree at dusk looking like what we were: three drowned ghosts dragging a fourth who was half-septic and fully unconscious. Ilsa had cut her hair with a fishing knife somewhere above the treeline, which made her look like a convict or a saint depending on the light. Tam looked like Tam, which is to say like a man who could fix your generator and then rob you. I carried Sef's rifle and most of Sef, and I had stopped counting the days since I'd been warm.

Maren Oduya found us before we found her.

That's how she told it later, anyway—that she'd been watching the treeline for three nights because her brother used to watch it too, back when he worked the survey lines, and grief makes you superstitious about horizons. She stepped out of an alley between the feed store and the shuttered assay office carrying a lantern she didn't bother to hide, and when Tam's hand went under his coat she said, very calmly:

"If you shoot me, you'll want to shoot me again in about ten minutes, because I'm the only person in this town who can get you into the registry building without a bullet in you first."

Tam looked at me. I looked at the lantern. The lantern won.

* * *

Her rooms were above the assay office, two floors of books and canned peaches and a wall covered in river charts annotated in a handwriting so precise it looked printed. She fed us barley soup that tasted like the best thing ever cooked by human hands, which tells you more about the last week than about the soup. Sef lay feverish on her bed, muttering cairn names, and Maren changed his dressing without flinching, with the brisk tenderness of someone who had bandaged worse.

"You're the salvage crew," she said. Not a question. "The one that died."

"Reports of our death," Tam said, "are extremely accurate."

"Corvan filed the paperwork himself. Vessel lost with all hands, weather event." She set down the bowl. "I process filings. That's my job. Everything that happens inside the concession becomes paper eventually, and every piece of paper comes across my desk." Her eyes went to the recorder bag Ilsa hadn't let out of arm's reach since Mile 40. "Except some things don't become paper. Some things stay voices."

I should have lied to her. I want that on the record. I am, by trade and temperament, a liar of considerable skill—I had talked our way past an airboat full of armed men with nothing but a smile and a fabricated cargo manifest. But I was tired down to the marrow, and there was something in the way she'd said *voices*, the way you'd say the name of someone buried wrong.

"The survey vessel," I said. "The *Kestrel Star*. We found her radio log."

The room went very still. Even Sef's fever-muttering seemed to pause, though that was probably coincidence.

"Dane," Maren said. "Dane Oduya. Junior hydrologist. He was twenty-six." She turned to the chart wall and touched a point on the upper Kestrel with two fingers, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "He sent me one letter from upriver. He wrote that the water tasted sweet and the mountains were kind and he'd found a place he wanted to show me someday." A pause. "Then the company sent me a form. Condolences, standard issue, box seven checked. Weather event."

"Weather event," Ilsa repeated flatly, from the corner where she'd been sharpening nothing in particular.

"I've spent four years reading that form," Maren said. "Four years of processing permits and filings and expiry notices, and every single day I read it and thought: either I'm crazy, or this is the most expensive lie ever typed." She finally looked at the recorder bag the way a starving woman looks through a bakery window. "Play me the last entry."

Ilsa hesitated. In the flooded forest, on the mountain, in the whiteout—we had protected those recordings like relics, played them for Vashti once and never again, because hearing the screaming twice felt like a sin against the screamer. But Maren had just told us her brother's name and age and the exact wording of a letter, unprompted, in a voice that didn't crack until the end, and I decided—I decided before telling anyone, which is apparently what I do now—that she'd earned it.

Ilsa thumbed the wheel. Static. Then Dane Oduya's voice filled the room, young and scared and professional right up until professionalism stopped being possible: *"—tailings breach at the lower impoundment, repeat, the containment has failed, the readings are off every scale we brought, this is not storm damage, I'm telling you the numbers are—"*

Cut short. It always cut short. It would cut short forever.

Maren stood with her hand pressed flat against the chart wall until the recording ended, and then she stood a while longer. When she turned around her face was dry and terrible and calm, the face of a woman who had been loading a weapon for four years and had just been handed ammunition.

"Tomorrow at noon," she said, "the concession permits expire. At 12:01, everything inside these boundaries—the survey data, the incident reports, the environmental filings, the *bodies' paperwork*—legally reverts to company custody. There's a clause. Nobody reads the clauses except clerks." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was hopeful. "But here is what nobody at the company understands about clerks. We read everything. And we remember."

* * *

She laid it out over the next hour while Tam dozed sitting up with the rifle across his knees and Ilsa watched the street through a gap in the shutters.

The official channel was dead; she said it plainly, no drama. The registry's intake system routed through a company liaison office, and the liaison officer—a soft man named Breck with a boat and a mistress and debts—had been Corvan's eyes inside the walls for years. Anything Maren filed officially would reach Breck's desk within the hour, and Breck would burn it, and then security would come for whoever had filed it. She knew because she'd tested it once, quietly, with a falsified tonnage report, and watched it vanish, and watched Breck smile at her across the filing room the next morning like a man checking whether a trap still held bait.

"So we don't file," she said. "We *serve*. Different legal animal entirely. A formal notice of evidence preservation, served directly on the federal circuit judge's circuit clerk in Harrow Bay, with affidavit of transmission. Once it's in the federal mail stream—even just logged—it exists outside the concession. Outside the clauses. The company can burn the registry to the ground and the notice still lives in a federal docket somewhere, being tedious and immortal."

"That sounds," Tam said, waking up exactly enough to be suspicious, "easy."

"It isn't. The registry's transmitter is locked down. The telegraph line was cut Tuesday—officially a storm, unofficially a man with pliers. The mail coach stopped running when the lockdown started. Which leaves us with the old ranger station relay on the ridge above town." She tapped the chart wall again, a switchback trail inked in faded blue. "It's decommissioned but the hardware's sound. My brother helped maintain it, summers, when he was home. He showed me the access panel when I was nineteen because he thought it was funny that a clerk could bring a whole valley's emergency net back from the dead with a screwdriver and a swear word."

"And security?" I asked.

"Is sweeping the town at dawn. House to house. They're looking for three divers and a dead woman's guide, and they'll find empty rooms and a locked registry and a clerk at her desk doing her job, because that's the one thing they will never suspect—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes that wasn't quite humor, "—is that the machine itself is going to testify against them. Through its own paperwork. Filed correctly. In triplicate."

I liked her immediately and completely. There is a type of person who survives unbearable things by becoming precise, and I trust them the way sailors trust lighthouses.

* * *

But hope, in my experience, is a current—you don't fight it and you don't surrender to it, you read it, constantly, because it changes. So after Maren fell asleep at her own table, mid-sentence, with a pen still in her hand, I woke Tam and Ilsa and we did the thing crews do: we took her apart.

"She cried for maybe ten seconds and then went tactical," Ilsa said. "Either genuine or the best performance I've seen since Elodie talked to that airboat."

"Her brother's on the recording," Tam said. "You can't fake the way she stood there. You can fake tears. You can't fake standing like your skeleton's been replaced with wet rope."

"Corvan bought our financier," Ilsa said. "Why not her?"

"Because clerks are cheap and invisible," Tam said, "and nobody buys what they don't see. That's also why she's dangerous. To us, if she's lying. To them, if she isn't."

I listened to them argue and added my own weight where it was needed, and by the time the company clock tower struck three we had done the arithmetic every desperate crew does: the odds of her being false were smaller than the odds of making noon without her. Small odds aren't zero odds. But we had crossed a mountain range on small odds. Small odds were our native currency.

Still, I took precautions. While the others slept I copied the strongest entries onto a second medium and hid it in the lining of my coat, and I told Maren none of this, and I hated myself a little for it, and then I remembered Vashti standing in the narrows of the Pass of Names with a rifle and no plan to come back, and I hated myself less. Trust is a resource. We were nearly out. You ration what's left.

* * *

Dawn came gray and armed.

We heard the sweeps start at first light—boots in the street, doors knocked with the flat of a rifle butt, a woman two houses down crying quietly about her husband's papers. Maren dressed in her registry uniform, gray and buttoned and utterly unremarkable, and pinned her clerk's badge on with the care of a woman arming herself.

"The station relay needs about forty minutes of work to bring up," she said, handing Ilsa a key ring heavy enough to anchor a skiff. "The maintenance hatch is behind the fuel shed on the ridge road. My brother's tools are in the green kit, third shelf, and if you lose the small Phillips head I will haunt you personally." She looked at Sef, asleep and burning, and her voice gentled. "He stays here. I'll register him as a cousin with typhoid. This town has typhoid every winter; nobody questions typhoid."

"And you?" I asked.

"At nine o'clock I walk into the registry building, sit at my desk, and begin preparing a service packet for federal transmission. Affidavit, exhibit list, chain-of-custody statement." She straightened her badge. "Everything has to be perfect. If the judge's clerk finds one irregularity, the whole notice dies in a drawer. Dane was fastidious. He'd have wanted it fastidious."

I caught her wrist at the door. I don't know why I did it—maybe because she was walking into the same building that had processed her brother's death as a checkbox, wearing its uniform, carrying our truth in a folder under her arm, and it seemed important that somebody say it aloud.

"Maren. Whatever happens at noon—your brother gets heard today."

She looked down at my hand, then at me, and for one moment the precision cracked and I saw the nineteen-year-old with a screwdriver and a swear word, the sister, the girl who'd watched the treeline for four years waiting for something to come down out of it.

"He always said the river keeps records too," she said. "He said sediment doesn't lie, it just waits for someone to core it." She opened the door onto the gray street and the sound of boots. "Go bring up my transmitter. I'll go make the machine confess."

* * *

The ridge road was a mile of bad gravel and worse luck. We made the fuel shed as the sweep reached the assay office below—I know because we heard the knock, and Maren's voice, clear and bored and perfectly clerical, saying *"One moment, gentlemen, I'm not decent"* through the door, buying seconds with bureaucracy. Ilsa had the panel open in six minutes. Tam swore at the capacitors in three languages. By 11:20 the old ranger relay was humming, warming, alive, its vacuum tubes glowing like a hearth in a house where everyone had died.

At 11:40, Maren's voice came up the relay wire from the registry building, patched through on the maintenance loop, and she was no longer bored. She was reading names. Dane Oduya, junior hydrologist. Petra Voss, field tech. Nine names, then the tailings figures, then the timestamps proving the reports preceded the sinking by eleven hours—and underneath it all, faintly, the sound of the sweep team hammering on the registry's front door, and Maren Oduya's voice never once changing pace, because she had rehearsed this for four years in her head and she would be damned before she rushed the ending.

The transmitter's power needle climbed into the red.

Below us, the fuel depot lit up orange—Tam's diversion, early, loud, beautiful—and the hammering stopped as every boot in town turned toward the fire.

I put my hand on the transmit key and looked at Ilsa, and Ilsa nodded, and somewhere between the ridge and the burning depot I understood that whatever happened in the next sixty seconds, the current had already turned.

I keyed the mic. Every emergency band in the valley opened like a throat.

"This is Elodie Dravenmoor, salvage vessel *Second Thoughts*, transmitting the radio log of the survey vessel *Kestrel Star*—"
