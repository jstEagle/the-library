## 18. Deadline Dawn

I woke to the sound of Tam sharpening a knife, which is Tam's way of saying good morning, good luck, and I love you, all in one economical gesture.

The window of Maren's back room showed a sky the color of dishwater, and the church bell across the square said six. Twelve hours, give or take. At noon the concession permits expired, and whatever the law was before midnight would be whatever the company said it was after. Vashti had explained it once, back when she was alive and explaining things, with her particular talent for making arithmetic sound like a death sentence. *Records become company property, boy. Witnesses become trespassers. Trespassers become statistics.* She'd had a way with numbers.

My shoulder throbbed under the bandage, a hot dull drumbeat. Fever, probably. Ilsa had looked at it that morning with the expression of a navigator reading a storm front, which is to say she said nothing and recalculated everything.

"Eat," Tam said, and put a tin of cold beans in front of me like he was dealing cards.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're going to climb a mountain on a hole in your shoulder. You'll want the beans."

He was right, which was annoying, because Tam was right about most things and had built an entire personality around never admitting it. I ate the beans. They tasted like tin and surrender.

Elodie came in from the front room where she'd been sitting with Maren, and I knew before she spoke. You learn to read Elodie Dravenmoor the way you read water — the surface tells you almost nothing, but the current underneath is legible if you've spent enough years drowning in it. She had the look of a woman who'd finished a calculation she didn't like.

"They're sweeping at nine," she said. "Maren's cousin works the depot gate. Company trucks, door to door, starting with the registry quarter. They want us in a room with the permits when they expire." She sat down across from me. "So we're not going to be in a room."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, fit on the back of a fuel requisition slip, which felt appropriate.

Maren had confirmed what we'd feared: the registry office itself was compromised. Whatever we handed a clerk behind that counter would be in Corvan's files by evening, filed under *shredded*. But the town had been a ranger district before it was a company town, and the old ranger station still stood on the ridge above the north quarter — decommissioned, unmaintained, and, according to Maren's brother's old field notes, still wired to an emergency transmitter. The company had never bothered to tear it down because the company had never bothered to think about anything it didn't own.

The recorder went into a dry bag. The dry bag went into Ilsa's pack. Ilsa and I would take the maintenance trail up the ridge, reach the station, and put the survey logs out on every emergency band in the region while the sun was still climbing. Elodie would meet us there — she had a longer route, through the drainage, in case the direct trail was watched.

And Tam.

Tam stood by the door with his knife back in its sheath and a jerry can of diesel sitting at his feet like a well-behaved dog, and he said, "Fuel depot catches fire around nine-fifteen," in the tone other people used for *I'll pick up milk*.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The fuel depot was the company's staging point for the sweep. Trucks, generators, the airboats on trailers. A fire there would pull every gun in the concession north and east, away from the ridge, away from us.

"That's a diversion," I said, stating the obvious, because someone had to and because saying it out loud made it slightly less enormous.

"It's a fire," Tam corrected. "Diversions are elegant. This is just me, a can of diesel, and a bad attitude."

"Tam—" Ilsa started.

"Don't." He held up a hand. "I've been running from things my whole life. Owed Elodie since before you were diving, Sef, and I've paid it back in installments, and I've always kept a little back, because that's what I do, I keep a little back. So let me spend it all at once for once." He looked at me, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do, which was go gentle. "You carry the recorder, boy. I carry the matches. That's the whole theology of it."

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. I watched her decide not to argue, and I understood that this, too, was a kind of love — the kind that respects a man's right to his own sacrifice.

"Nine-fifteen," she said. "Not before. And you walk out of that depot, Tam Voss. You hear me? You light it and you *walk*."

"Always do," said Tam, who had never once in his life walked away from anything, and grinned like a man who knew it.

* * *

We left the back room in ones and twos, the way you do when you don't want to be a group anymore, just weather. Ilsa and I went out through Maren's cellar and up the alley behind the tannery, and the town at dawn was the quietest I'd ever heard it. Not peaceful quiet. Held-breath quiet. Shutters closed. Nobody on the streets except a company patrol truck idling at the square, its exhaust curling up into the cold like the town itself was smoking.

My shoulder had opinions about the climb from the first step. The maintenance trail was steep and slick with frost, switchbacking up through pines that got skinnier and darker the higher we went. Ilsa set a pace just short of cruel and didn't apologize for it, and I didn't ask her to, because the clock was the real enemy and we both knew it. Every hour we climbed was an hour the permits were burning down toward noon like a fuse.

"Tell me something," I said, mostly to keep my mind off the fire in my shoulder.

"Something," said Ilsa.

"You're a fountain."

"I'm conserving oxygen. You should try it." But after a hundred meters of silence she said, "My father worked a concession. Not this one. Up in the Verren hills, when I was small. Same setup — permits, company town, the whole liturgy. One day the water went bad and the company said it hadn't, and everyone in town had to decide, every single one of them, whether they'd drunk bad water or good water. And they all decided good, because deciding bad meant losing everything." She stepped over a fallen log without breaking stride. "I've been waiting thirty years to be on the other side of that decision. So walk faster."

I walked faster.

* * *

We heard the fire before we saw it — a low whump rolling up the valley like the river clearing its throat — and then we were above the treeline, on the bare shoulder of the ridge, and we could see the whole town laid out below us in the gray morning light.

The fuel depot was burning beautifully. Black smoke standing straight up in the windless air, a column you could read from the moon. Even from here I could see company trucks converging on it, headlights still on in the daylight, and I thought: *that's Tam's work, that column, that's him.* And then I thought: *and is he walking out of it,* and the thought had no answer, and I kept climbing because the alternative was standing on a mountain crying, and Ilsa doesn't wait for criers.

"There," she said.

The ranger station sat in a saddle below the summit, a squat timber building with a lightning-scarred mast rising from its roof, guy wires singing faintly in the wind. It looked abandoned the way old honest things look abandoned — patiently, as if it knew better than to hurry.

The door wasn't locked. Nothing in this town was locked anymore; the company had replaced locks with the assumption that no one would dare. Inside: dust, a desk, a cot, a wall of dead batteries, and the transmitter — a big old emergency-band rig bolted to a bench, its brass fittings green with age, and when Ilsa threw the switch and the dial lamp lit up amber, I made a sound I'm not proud of. Something between a laugh and a prayer.

"Generator's in the shed," Ilsa said, already moving. "Maren said fuel's in the can behind it. Go."

I went. The shed generator caught on the third pull, coughed, caught again, and settled into a chugging rhythm that made the whole station tremble like something waking from a long sleep. The transmitter's needle swung alive. Ilsa found the log book on the desk, opened it to the last entry — dated eleven years back, a ranger noting elk on the north slope, *all quiet* — and beneath it, in fresh ink, she wrote the frequency bands and the time.

0841.

"Elodie should read it," I said. "It's her—"

"Elodie's not here yet." Ilsa checked her watch, then the window, then the trail. The smoke column from the depot was leaning east now, the wind picking up, and somewhere down in that smoke I had to believe a gruff old man was walking away with his hands in his pockets. "And we don't know what's coming up that trail behind her. If we wait and they come, nobody reads anything." She set the recorder on the bench and looked at me. "You found it, Sef. In the box, under the waterline. It's yours more than anyone's."

My hands were shaking. Partly the fever. Partly not.

I'd heard the logs twice now, once in Vashti's kitchen and once alone in the dark of Maren's cellar, and both times the last entry had gone through me like cold water — the survey chief's voice going from clipped professional reporting to screaming in the space of forty seconds, the sound of men understanding, all at once, that the company had decided they were a rounding error. Eleven years that voice had sat in a strongbox at the bottom of the Kestrel, patient as a stone. It could wait a few more minutes for Elodie. But it didn't have to.

I keyed the transmitter. The bands were open — every emergency frequency in the region, the ones every boat, every station, every patrol truck was required by law to monitor. The law. Funny thing, the law. It had been on their side at midnight and it was on ours at noon, and both sides of the day were made of the same paper.

"This is Ranger Station Six, Serrat Ridge," I heard myself say, and my voice came out steadier than it had any right to. "Beginning broadcast of recovered radio logs, survey vessel *Kestrel Star*, concession 44. For the record. For whoever's listening. It's almost noon, and the permits are about to mean nothing — so listen while they still mean something."

I pressed play.

The survey chief's voice filled the station, flat and tired and professional, reporting tailings seepage in the lower channel, contamination readings off every safe scale, *requesting immediate suspension of discharge, requesting, requesting* — and Ilsa stood at the window with her watch open in her hand, counting us down toward eleven fifty, and the smoke column rose over the town, and somewhere below us Elodie was climbing through the drainage toward the sound of her crew's voice coming off a mountain.

The needle on the transmitter held steady. The voice on the tape did not. It started to shake. It started to run. It said *they're coming back with pumps, they said it was contained, it isn't contained, it was never*—

And then the wind gusted, the mast wires sang, and through the station's small window, down on the maintenance trail, I saw them: four men in concession gray, moving fast, rifles slung, maybe twenty minutes behind Elodie and closing.

Ilsa saw them at the same moment. She didn't swear. She just turned to me, calm as tide tables, and said, "Keep reading, Sef. I'll handle the door."
