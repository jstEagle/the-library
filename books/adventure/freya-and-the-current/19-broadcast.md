## 19. Broadcast

The ranger station at the summit of the Serrat Range looked like something the mountain had tried to swallow and given up on. Half its roof was gone, snow drifting through the rafters in lazy spirals, and the door hung from one hinge like a drunk leaning on a friend. But Ilsa had been right—the old transmitter sat in the corner under a tarp of mouse-chewed canvas, squat and green and government-built, the kind of machine they made back when the assumption was that things should last.

Sef slumped against the wall beside it, gray-faced, her shoulder wound wrapped in Vashti's spare shirt and half our gauze. She'd insisted on coming. I'd insisted harder that she stay below with the skiff, and we'd settled it the way everything got settled on this crew: she looked at me until I ran out of arguments worth having.

"Power's the question," Tam said, already elbow-deep in the transmitter's guts. He'd found a hand-crank generator bolted to the floor beneath forty years of dust, and he was testing the flywheel with the expression of a man greeting an old enemy. "Crank gives us maybe ten minutes of transmit per full charge. Ten minutes to read however much of that log survives."

"Then I read fast," I said.

Ilsa had the recorder out of its oilcloth wrapping, and for a moment none of us touched it. It was just a metal box with a reel inside, dented, river-stained, smaller than a loaf of bread. Six people dead—eight, counting Maren's brother and the hydrologist who'd signed the first contamination report and then stopped signing anything—and all of it, every lie, every order, every scream, folded up inside something you could carry one-handed.

Outside, the storm was arriving on schedule. The front had come up the western face all afternoon, a gray wall eating the ridgelines, and the first real flakes were starting to come down thick and sideways. First snow. Permit expiry at noon tomorrow. Somewhere down in the company town, lights were moving—searchlights, probably, sweeping the lower slopes where Tam's diversion fire was supposed to be pulling every security man off his chair.

Tam checked his watch. "Fuel depot goes up in twenty minutes. Maybe less if their night shift drinks on the job." He grinned, but it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "After that, every gun in the concession comes up this mountain looking for whoever lit it."

"So we're on the clock twice," Sef said. "Weather and war."

"We were always on both clocks," Ilsa said quietly. "Now we can see them."

* * *

Tam got the generator turning at 4:47 by his watch. The flywheel fought him for the first few cranks, then caught, and the transmitter's dial lamps glowed amber, then steadied. He swept the bands while I unspooled the log notes Ilsa had transcribed during the climb—every timestamp, every call sign, every phrase I needed to hit so that even a half-awake listener on some distant coast would understand what they were hearing.

Emergency marine band. Aviation guard frequency. The concession's own channel, because I wanted them to hear it too—I wanted the men with rifles to know exactly what they were climbing toward and exactly how pointless it was.

"This is Elodie Dravenmoor," I said into a microphone older than I was, my voice sounding strange and flat in my own ears. "Aboard the survey vessel *Kestrel Star*, eleven days before her reported loss, eight crew members recorded the following transmissions. I am reading them verbatim. This is not commentary. This is testimony."

And then I read.

*"—tailings pond two is breached, repeat, breached. We're reading dissolved metals at forty times discharge limits in the east fork. This isn't seepage, this is a structural failure, and it's going into the Kestrel—"*

My voice held. I remember being surprised by that. Somewhere behind me Sef had gone very still, and Ilsa stood with her hand flat against the transmitter housing as if she could steady the signal by touch.

*"—company rep on site says stand down, says our instruments are miscalibrated. Requesting independent verification. Denied. Requesting evacuation of downstream villages. Denied."*

The crank generator whined as Tam fed it. Ten minutes of charge, he'd said. I read faster.

*"—they've moved the sample chain of custody. Our own lab results are being routed through the concession office now. We are being edited."*

That one. That one I almost couldn't say aloud, because I'd spent three weeks feeling exactly that way—edited, redlined, written out of my own story by people who owned the paper the story was printed on. The survey team had known. They'd known what was happening to them, the same way we knew, and they'd kept recording anyway because a record was the only weapon left when every other door closed.

The last entry was ninety seconds long. I'd listened to it once, on the boat, and sworn never again alone. Now I read the transcript with my voice level and my hands shaking, which felt like the correct division of labor.

*"—they're coming up the river. Two boats, no running lights. Corvan's office confirmed our departure time, they knew—we never told anyone at the office. If anyone hears this, the contamination data is in the strongbox, it's real, check the east fork, check the villages, check the—"* 

And then nothing. Static where a man's name used to be.

I let the static sit in the air for five full seconds before I spoke again. Let it broadcast across every band Tam had opened. Somewhere out there in the dark, in fishing shacks and ranger posts and company barracks, people were hearing a dead man's last sentence end mid-word.

"The crew of the *Kestrel Star* did not die in a storm," I said. "They died because they told the truth, and the truth was worth more than they were. Their names are in this log. Their evidence is now public. Come and find us if you want it. We'll be the ones still standing here."

Tam killed the transmit switch and sagged against the generator. "Charge's spent. Recharge takes six minutes."

"Then we do it again," I said. "Every entry, again. Until the snow takes the antenna or they take us."

* * *

We got through the second full reading and halfway into the third before the world outside changed.

It wasn't sound first. It was light—a flicker through the broken roof, orange where no orange should be, far down the western slope. Tam went to the doorway and came back with his face lit from below by something burning miles away.

"Fuel depot's up," he said. "Early. And it's bigger than I planned, which means either their storage was sloppier than advertised or somebody else decided to help." He paused. "Or they torched it themselves to blame us. Can't rule anything out anymore."

"Doesn't matter," Ilsa said. "Look at the road."

Headlights. A convoy of them, strung down the switchbacks like a lit fuse, crawling upward through the falling snow. They'd stopped pretending to search. They were coming straight at us, because there was only one ranger station on this ridge and only one reason anyone would be in it.

"How long?" Sef asked. Her voice was thin but steady.

"At their speed, in this weather? Forty minutes. Maybe thirty-five if they stop caring about driving off the cliff, which they will."

"Third reading finishes in nine," Tam said. "We finish it."

So we finished it. I read the breach report and the denied evacuations and the screaming cut short while the headlights climbed and the snow thickened and Sef reloaded Ilsa's rifle with her good arm because Ilsa was holding the recorder above her head like a lantern, like proof of concept, like a thing that could not be allowed to be in the room when the room stopped existing.

At 5:31 the transmission ended for the last time. Tam pulled the master breaker, and the station went dark except for the snow-light through the roof.

"Now we leave," I said. "Nobody dies for a machine that already did its job."

* * *

They nearly caught us in the doorway anyway.

I heard the airboat engines—or whatever they'd brought up the logging road, engines of some kind—cut off below us, and then boots on scree, and then a flashlight beam swung through the trees and found the station's broken roofline. We went out the back, down the slope side, into the rocks, and the beam swept past the empty doorway behind us like a hand patting pockets after the thief has already gone over the fence.

But they had radios, and radios meant coordination, and within minutes there were lights on two sides of us, moving up the talus field in the patient, spread-out way of people who have done this before and are paid whether it's hard or easy.

Tam stopped at the top of the scree chute—the long gray run of loose stone that fell away toward the tree line, four hundred meters of ankle-breaking descent that we'd climbed two days ago cursing every step.

"I can slow them," he said.

"No," I said, and it came out of me before thought, pure reflex, the way you grab someone's collar without deciding to.

"Somebody has to," he said. "You've got the story. Sef can't run fast. Ilsa's carrying the box. I'm the fastest one here and the most expendable, and I've been waiting three weeks to spend myself on something that finally adds up." He was already pulling the flare kit from his pack—the depot raid's leftovers, six incendiary flares we'd stolen alongside the antibiotics. "There's a dry draw up-slope, north side. Scrub pine, deadfall, a decade of dry summers. I put fire between us and them, they stop to deal with it or they burn."

"They could just go around."

"In the dark, on scree, in a rising storm? They could. They won't want to." He grinned at me, and this time it reached all the way. "Elodie. You taught me the cold water rules. Rule one: the current doesn't care about your plans. Rule two: sometimes you swim away from the wreck instead of toward it. I'm swimming away. Go."

What I remember most clearly is that I didn't argue, because there was nothing true left to argue with. What I remember second-most is grabbing his sleeve anyway, and him prying my fingers off one at a time, gently, like handling something fragile, and saying, "Depositions. Coastal city. Clean clothes. You promised me I'd hate it. Don't you dare make a liar out of me."

Then he was gone, uphill, into the dark, a big man moving quiet over stone, and thirty seconds later the north slope bloomed orange.

Fire does something to a night that even explosions don't. It makes the darkness feel personal. The flames roared up through the scrub pine in a wall between us and the trackers, and the wind off the storm front grabbed it and pushed it sideways across the slope, and somewhere in the roar I heard shouting—security men falling back, radios crackling, the whole careful machinery of the hunt jamming itself on one stubborn engineer with a handful of flares and a grudge against the arithmetic of who gets sacrificed.

"Go," Ilsa said, and we went.

Down the scree, all four hundred meters of it, sliding and catching and sliding again. Sef descended on her seat mostly, Ilsa hauling her by the good arm, and I went ahead kicking footholds into the loose stone like I was digging graves for every version of myself that had ever believed this would be simple. Above us the fire climbed. Below us the snow was laying itself down over the trail, erasing us as fast as we passed, the mountain doing quietly and for free what we'd bled to do for ourselves.

Halfway down, Sef started laughing. High, breathless, slightly unhinged laughter, the kind that lives next door to sobbing.

"What?" Ilsa gasped.

"It's broadcasting," Sef said. "The truth is actually—it's actually out. Right now. On every band. There's no taking it back. Some guy on a trawler two hundred miles south is listening to a dead man describe a tailings breach and there is nothing Corvan or the company or God himself can do about it." She laughed again, wilder. "We put it in the water and the current carries it. You can't recall the current."

No, you can't. That was the whole point of currents. That was the first thing the river ever taught me, back when I was young enough to think salvage was about treasure: whatever goes into the water belongs to the water, and the water delivers it where it will, on its own schedule, indifferent to everyone's plans.

The truth was in the water now. All of it. Every timestamp, every denial, every scream cut short. Loose across a thousand receivers in the dark, multiplying, unstoppable, and all we had to do was survive long enough to matter less than the message.

We reached the tree line as the station above us disappeared into smoke and snowfall. Behind us, faint through the fire-roar, I thought I heard Tam's voice—shouting something, maybe at the security men, maybe at the mountain, maybe just because shouting felt right at the top of the world with everything burning.

Ahead, down through the black timber, the lights of the company town glowed in the valley like a trap set with bait.

"Registry closes at noon," Ilsa said, checking her watch with numb fingers. "Maren said the inquiry needs a physical filing before expiry or the permits reset and everything in the concession becomes company property—including, presumably, us."

"Then we walk," I said, and put my shoulder under Sef's good arm, and started down toward the town, the fire, and whatever was waiting at the bottom of the mountain with our names on a list.
