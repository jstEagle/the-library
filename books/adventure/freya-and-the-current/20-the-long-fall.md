## 20. The Long Fall

The snow started as a rumor and became a fact somewhere above the tree line, and by the time we reached the first stunted pines it had become a policy—total, indifferent, covering everything equally, the way companies cover everything equally until it's inconvenient.

I carried Sef.

I want that on the record, in whatever inquiry eventually gets written, in whatever deposition some clerk with clean hands transcribes: Ilsa Vey, navigator, carried Sef Ondar down the Serrat Range on her back, and if anyone tells you otherwise, they are lying, drunk, or Tam.

Not that Tam didn't offer. He offered every twenty minutes, which was generous of him given that he'd set a fuel depot on fire four hours earlier and was still moving like a man whose lungs had filed a formal complaint. But someone had to walk point and read the slope, and Tam reads terrain the way I read charts—instinctively, obsessively, with a low continuous muttering—and so the carrying fell to me, and the muttering fell to him, and Sef, for his part, contributed a fever and an arm slung around my neck and a running commentary that was mostly apology.

"I can walk," he said, for the ninth time.

"You can't," I said.

"I could walk *some*."

"You could walk some straight off a cliff, which is the only walking I'm interested in facilitating right now."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough turned into a sound I didn't like, wet and deep, the sound of a body conducting business it shouldn't be conducting. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder two days ago in a depot that smelled of diesel and bad decisions, and Elodie had cleaned and packed it with the calm of a woman defusing something, but wounds don't care about calm. Wounds care about warmth, and dirt, and the two days we'd spent pinned in a whiteout eating the last of the food. Sef's fever had come up like a tide. His skin was hot as a stove plate. Every time I shifted him on my back I felt that heat through both jackets and thought: *infection, sepsis, the arithmetic of delay.*

So we went down. Down through the falling snow, down through the pines, down toward the company town and the registry office and the noon deadline that was coming for us whether we came for it or not.

* * *

You want to know what the Serrat Range is like in a snowfall at night, with a wounded man on your back and searchlights somewhere below you?

It's like being inside a bell that someone is ringing very softly, forever.

The snow eats sound. That's the first thing. Your own footsteps vanish an inch from your ears. Your breathing goes nowhere. The world contracts to the radius of your own body and then, cruelly, expands again every time the wind shifts, because the wind carries things: the groan of ice in the creek beds, the far-off complaint of the ridge, and—twice in the first hour—the flat mechanical whine of drones running their search patterns over the lower slopes.

The company had lights. Of course they had lights. They had drones and thermals and a perimeter around Registry town that Ilsa Vey, professional navigator, had spent the whole climb trying not to think about, because thinking about it made it real, and unreality was the only advantage we had left.

"Contact left," Tam murmured, and we folded ourselves into the shadow of a granite spine and watched a drone slide past below us, maybe sixty meters down the slope, its light sweeping the trees in slow, patient arcs. Patient was the wrong word. Nothing about it was patient. It was *systematic*, which is worse. Patience is a virtue; systematicity is a schedule, and schedules don't get bored, don't get cold, don't have a wounded man's fever heat soaking through their jacket into their spine.

The drone passed. We moved.

Sef had gone quiet, which frightened me more than the talking. I craned to check him and found his eyes open, tracking me, lucid in the way of the very feverish—too lucid, seeing things at a higher resolution than the rest of us.

"Ilsa," he whispered. "The pass. The cairns."

"I know."

"They were *people*." His breath fogged against my ear. "The survey team went through the pass and they were people and nobody—"

"Nobody came for them," I said. "I know. That's why we're doing this."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "My shoulder doesn't hurt anymore."

"That's not good, Sef."

"I know," he said, with the gentle reasonableness of the deeply poisoned. "I'm just saying. It's interesting."

I adjusted his weight and kept walking, and made myself a promise I had no right to make: that he would live to be bored again. Boredom as a life goal. Somewhere warm, with bad coffee and nothing happening, ever, for years.

* * *

We came down out of the trees an hour before dawn into the bowl where the company town sat glowing in its moat of floodlights.

From the ridge it had looked like a jewel. From here, close, it looked like what it was: a machine that had digested a valley. Registry town. Rows of company housing, the smelter's dead hulk, the administration block with its flag, and on the far side, squat and windowless and lit like a shrine, the mining registry office—the one building in the concession that the company did not own, could not own, had never figured out how to own, because some older law, some federal statute written by people who had maybe once been sincere, required that mining claims be filed and sealed in a public registry beyond the company's reach.

The permits expired at noon. When they expired, everything inside the concession boundary—the records, the wrecks, the deaths, the survey team's last screaming hours on that recorder—became company property. Legally. Cleanly. The way the river became theirs, and the valley, and the silence.

Unless the registry was sealed first. Unless a judge, a real one, a federal one, looked at what was in that building and said *no, this stays, this gets read.*

Which was why Elodie was currently climbing toward the old ranger station on the ridge behind us with the recorder in her pack, toward a transmitter that Vashti had sworn still worked, to read the survey logs out loud on every emergency band she could reach. And why Tam had peeled off an hour ago toward the fuel depot with a thermite charge and the expression of a man about to do something he'd already forgiven himself for.

And why I was standing in the treeline with Sef on my back, looking at the town, doing the math.

The plan—the word is doing charitable work—was that I'd bring Sef down through the drainage culvert on the town's north side, the one Vashti had described before she stayed behind at the col with a rifle and the last of our luck. Maren, the registry clerk, would meet us. Sef couldn't run, couldn't fight, couldn't even stay awake for long stretches now, but I wasn't leaving him on a mountainside, and I wasn't leaving him in the open, and the culvert was the only door into town that the sweep patterns didn't cover.

"Vashti's culvert," Sef mumbled, as I picked my way down through the rocks toward the drainage. "She'd hate that they named it after her."

"It's not named after her. I named it that in my head."

"Still," he said. "She'd hate it."

Vashti, who had taken a bullet meant for Elodie and stayed behind at the narrows with a borrowed rifle and no intention of coming back. Vashti, who was either dead or a prisoner by now, and who had told us, in her dry hydrologist's way, exactly where the water went, because water always goes somewhere, and so do people who follow it.

The culvert was where she said it would be. A concrete throat under the perimeter road, half-choked with ice, running meltwater out of the town's guts into the creek. I went in low. The water came to my knees and then my thighs, and Sef's weight shifted, and for one bad moment we both nearly went down, and I thought, with a clarity that felt almost funny: *drowned in a drainage ditch carrying a boy who was shot stealing bandages. There are worse obituaries. Not many.*

But we came through. We came out the far end into the town's back alleys, into the smell of coal smoke and canteen grease, and I crouched behind a stack of pallets with Sef breathing shallow against my neck, and waited for Maren.

* * *

She was late.

Not very late. Seven minutes. But seven minutes in a town under martial law, with sweep teams working the streets in pairs and loudspeakers announcing a curfew in the company's flat, courteous voice—*residents are reminded that the concession period expires at twelve hundred hours; all records and persons within the boundary remain subject to company jurisdiction*—seven minutes is a long time to crouch behind pallets with a feverish boy and count the searchlights.

When Maren finally came, she came fast and wrong, hunched in a coat that didn't fit her, and the first thing she said was not *hello* or *follow me.* It was:

"Is it done? Is she transmitting?"

"I don't know. We've been in a culvert."

"Then we move," she said, "because if it's not done by noon, none of this—"

She stopped. Because the loudspeakers stopped.

All of them. Across the whole town, the flat courteous voice cut off mid-syllable, and in the silence that followed there was a new sound, faint and crackling, coming from everywhere at once—from the loudspeakers themselves, from the radio in the guard post across the alley, from the little weather unit clipped to Maren's coat. A woman's voice. Elodie's voice. Reading.

"*—fourteen twenty-two hours, day forty-one. Tailings pond three has breached the subsurface liner. We're reading contamination in the Kestrel main channel at—*"

Elodie. On every band. In every ear in the concession, in the guard post, in the administration block, in the company's own flat courteous machines. Reading the dead back to life, entry by entry, in her salvage-captain's voice with its rough edges, and the last entry coming, the one we'd all heard once in the flooded forest and never managed to forget, the screaming one, cut short—

Maren made a sound. Small. Her brother had been on that survey vessel; she'd told Elodie that in a registry office back room with her hands flat on the desk like a woman holding down a map. Now she stood in an alley with her hand over her mouth and her eyes shining, listening to the company's own loudspeakers broadcast her brother's murder.

"*—this is Elodie Dravenmoor, salvage master, vessel Kestrel Runner. The recordings I am reading are the final radio log of the Meridian survey team. I am transmitting from the Serrat ranger station. The concession permit expires at noon. Do not let this expire with it.*"

Then the loudspeakers died all at once, cut at the source, and the town went back to its curfew silence, and somewhere a whistle blew, and boots started running.

"Registry," Maren said, grabbing my sleeve. "Now. While they're all looking at the ridge."

* * *

I don't remember most of the walk to the registry. I remember Sef's weight. I remember the searchlights swinging toward the ridge, the whole town's attention yanked upward toward the station where our captain was reading the dead into every ear in the valley, and I remember thinking: *she did it, she actually did it, the impossible, stupid, magnificent thing, and now we just have to survive her success.*

Maren got us in through a records loading dock, past a night clerk she paid in something other than money—I didn't ask—and into the archive proper, the long cold room of filing cabinets and sealed boxes where the concession's paper soul lived. She sat Sef down in a chair among the records, wrapped him in a thermal blanket, and started pulling files with shaking hands.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Sealing the record." Her voice was steadier now, the clerk's voice, the one that lived under the grieving sister. "The broadcast is out, but broadcasts can be denied. Discredited. 'A salvage pirate reading doctored audio.' The registry is different. If a federal judge orders an inquiry and the registry is sealed pending it, then everything in this room is evidence. The survey filings. The water readings the company submitted and then withdrew. The permit applications with the falsified hydrology. It all stays. It all gets *read*."

She pulled a drawer, and another. And that was when the radio on the clerk's desk—a stolen thing, a company-issue unit Maren had been running on a private frequency—crackled and spoke.

Not Elodie. A man's voice, tired and official and very far away.

*"—all stations, all stations, this is Federal Circuit Judge Anselm Rook, sitting in emergency session. In response to a broadcast transmission received at oh-four-forty hours, I am issuing a preservation order. The Kestrel concession registry is sealed, effective immediately, pending federal inquiry into the deaths of the Meridian survey team. Company security personnel are instructed that any interference with registry records, or with persons seeking the registry's protection, will be treated as obstruction of a federal proceeding. I say again: the registry is sealed. The record does not expire at noon. The record does not expire at all."*

Silence. The real kind, the kind after a bell.

Maren sat down slowly on a filing cabinet, like her legs had been unplugged. I stood there with snowmelt soaking my boots and Sef's fever heat still ghosting along my spine, and I waited for the catch. There's always a catch. There had been a catch in the contract, a catch in the wreck, a catch in the fixer's smile, a catch in every single day since Corvan had offered us triple rates for a job nobody would sign. My whole nervous system had been braced for the catch for so long that its absence felt like a physical sensation, like stepping off a boat after a long passage and having the ground refuse to sway.

"Did we—" Sef started, from his chair, his voice a feverish rasp. "Did that say judge? An actual—"

"A federal judge," Maren said. Her laugh came out cracked down the middle. "He heard it. Somebody heard it and *called it in.*"

I looked down at Sef. He was grinning, which was a terrible idea given his condition, a lopsided, exhausted, disbelieving grin, the grin of a boy who had found a child's boot in the shallows of a poisoned river and had carried the weight of that boot all the way up a mountain and back down again.

"We did it," he said. "We actually— Ilsa. We did it."

"Don't celebrate yet," I said, because someone had to, because the whistle was still blowing outside, because Tam was still out there in a burning fuel depot, because Elodie was still on a ridge with security converging on the ranger station, because a judge's voice on a radio is not the same as boots on the ground, and the company had never once in its life let a piece of paper stop it from doing what it was going to do.

But my voice cracked when I said it. Both of us heard it. Sef's grin got worse.

Outside, the searchlights on the ridge had stopped searching. They were converging—half a dozen beams folding in on the ranger station like a hand closing. And the loudspeakers, which had been silent since Elodie's voice cut out, crackled back to life with a different voice now, a company voice, no longer courteous:

*"—all security elements, the ranger station is to be secured. The transmitter is to be silenced. The individuals are to be—"*

A pause. A very long pause, the length of a lawyer arriving at a microphone.

*"—the individuals are to be detained. Detained. Pending federal inquiry."*

Maren and I looked at each other.

"They got the order," she breathed. "They got the judge's order *live.* Their own security is listening to their own radios."

Which meant Elodie was, too. Which meant that somewhere on that ridge, our captain—surrounded, outnumbered, out of everything except the truth—had just heard the same bell we had.

I picked Sef's blanket tighter and went to the loading dock door to watch the road up to the ridge, and what I saw, through the falling snow, was the thing I will carry longer than the culvert or the cairns or the fever: a single figure coming down the switchbacks at a dead run, coat open, pack bouncing, with two security officers flanking her at a respectful distance—no rifles raised, no handcuffs out, walking her down like men who had very suddenly, very recently, stopped being sure whose side the law was on.

Elodie reached the bottom of the ridge, and the searchlights found her, and she stopped, and she looked directly across the town—across the alleys, the pallets, the culvert, the whole geography of our flight—directly at the registry's loading dock, as if she could see me standing there.

She raised one hand. Not a wave. A fist, held up, the way you hold something you've carried a very long way and are not, under any circumstances, going to put down.

Then she turned and walked, escorted, toward the administration block, toward the inquiry, toward the daylight—and behind her, on the ridge, the transmitter kept broadcasting to an empty station, reading the survey log over and over into the falling snow, because nobody up there had thought to turn it off, and nobody down here was going to be the one to try.
