## 21. Testimony

The suit they gave me didn't fit.

That's the first thing I want on the record, before anything else: justice, as administered in the coastal city of Vell Harbor, comes in off-the-rack sizes and expects you to grow into it. The clerk had offered me a choice of navy or charcoal, and I'd chosen charcoal because navy looked too much like deep water and I'd had my fill of deep water for one lifetime. The jacket hung off my shoulders like a borrowed skin. I kept tugging the cuffs down over my wrists, and Tam kept watching me do it with the expression of a man watching someone else's boat take on water.

"You look like a lawyer," he said.

"That's the cruelest thing you've ever said to me."

"You've known me eleven years. I've said worse at your birthday parties."

We were in the corridor outside Hearing Room Four of the Federal Inquiry Annex, a building that smelled of carpet shampoo and institutional coffee and the particular despair of people waiting to say true things to strangers who would write them down. Through the frosted glass, a bailiff's silhouette moved. Somewhere a photocopier was eating someone's afternoon.

Ilsa sat with her back very straight, hands folded, the way she sits when she's navigating by instruments alone. She'd worn an actual dress. I had never seen Ilsa in a dress. It was like seeing the *Marguerite*—God rest her scuttled hull—with a figurehead. Sef sat beside her with his arm in a sling that matched nobody's outfit, his shoulder still knitting itself back together under a doctor's orders he treated as loose suggestions. He kept flexing the fingers of his good hand against his knee, counting something. I'd learned by now that when Sef counts, he's not nervous. He's anchoring himself to a number that can't lie to him.

"Fourth time through this," he said quietly. "The deposition. They keep asking the same questions different ways."

"They're building a wall," Ilsa said. "Brick by brick. Every time you answer the same, the wall gets stronger."

"And every time I answer," Sef said, "I hear the screaming again."

Nobody had anything to put against that. The photocopier ate on.

* * *

Here is what I learned about testimony, in the three weeks we spent giving it: the law does not want your story. The law wants your story cut into pieces small enough to fit in a form.

The inquiry counsel was a woman named Osgood, silver-haired, patient as erosion, and she was very good at her job, which was making the truth legible to people who had never been cold. "Describe the water conditions on the day you located the vessel." "State, for the record, how you came to possess the recording device." "Did you, at any point, enter the concession zone with knowledge that your permits were invalid?"

That last one had teeth in it, and we both knew it. Because yes. Of course yes. Every mile upriver after the security boat found us, we were trespassing. Every camp we burned, every depot we raided, every frozen pass we crossed—we were criminals the whole way to the summit of the Serrats, and the only thing that made us anything else was a radio broadcast that reached a federal judge before the bullets reached us.

"Answer the question, Ms. Dravenmoor."

"I'm deciding how much truth fits in the room," I said.

Osgood almost smiled. "All of it does. That's the point of the room."

So I told her. All of it. The milky river. The child's boot in the shallows. The scuttle charges set clean and professional, from inside, by the crew's own hands. The strongbox bolted below the waterline, hidden from anyone who came looking, waiting for anyone who came looking harder. And the recorder, and the voices—Halewick, the survey team lead, steady as a depth gauge in the early logs, reporting tailings seepage in concentrations that would poison the Kestrel watershed for a generation. Reporting it to the company. Reporting it again. Reporting it with the calm of a man who believed his employer would rather fix the problem than drown the messenger.

And then the last entry. I did not play it in the hearing room. I described it, which was worse, because describing it meant I had to say the words myself: Halewick's voice, no longer steady, saying *they're coming back, they said they'd bring the pumps and they're coming back with—* and then the sound, and then nothing, and then the river keeping the secret for eleven months until a salvage crew with a bolt cutter and bad instincts pried it loose.

When I finished, the hearing room was very quiet. Somewhere in the gallery a man was crying, quietly, into a handkerchief, and I would learn later he was the father of one of the survey divers, a woman named Ochoa who had held the recorder for the final entries. He came up to me afterward and shook my hand with both of his and couldn't speak, and I couldn't either, and we stood there in the ugly carpet like two people meeting at a grave.

That was the part the forms couldn't hold. That was the part I carried out of the room every day, back to the rented apartment where four salvagers in clean clothes kept bumping into furniture.

* * *

The news came on a Tuesday, in the way news does when it has been rehearsed for weeks: Corvan arrested at a marina two hundred miles down the coast, trying to board a boat he'd bought under his nephew's name. The inquiry had suspended the Kestrel concession pending a full environmental and criminal investigation. The company's stock had fallen off a shelf. Three executives had "resigned to pursue other opportunities," which is what accountability looks like when it's wearing a suit.

Tam read it off his phone at the kitchen table, and none of us cheered. I'd expected—hoped for—some release, some moment where the last three months clicked into place and became worth it. Instead Ilsa got up and washed a dish that was already clean. Sef went to the window and looked at the harbor, where the water was brown-green and honest and didn't have anything hidden in it that he knew of. Tam set the phone down face-up, like a card he was done playing.

"He'll get twelve years," Tam said. "Maybe fifteen. The men who shot at us on the mountain, most of them will walk. The ones who gave the orders will do time. The ones who signed the orders—" He shook his head. "The signature people always retire to somewhere warm."

"Vashti won't get anything," Sef said to the window. "She's dead. You can't un-fire a dead woman. You can't give her back her name while she's alive to hear it."

But they did the next best thing, which arrived a week later in an envelope with a federal seal: a formal posthumous clearing of Dr. Vashti Okonkwo, concession hydrologist, whose 2019 findings of tailings contamination had been suppressed, whose dismissal had been engineered, whose subsequent "unauthorized presence" in the concession had been cited in company security briefings as justification for—here the language went cold and careful—"escalated interdiction protocols." Interdiction protocols. She took a bullet at the col holding a narrows with a borrowed rifle so that we could walk out of it, and the paper that finally said *she was right all along* called it interdiction.

Ilsa read it aloud at the table, all of it, every clause, because she said Vashti would have wanted the whole document read, footnotes and all, by someone who understood them. When she finished, we sat there. Then Tam said, "She'd have hated the footnotes," and Ilsa said, "She'd have annotated them," and for a second we laughed, and the laugh hurt like a wound reopening, which is how I knew it was the right kind of laugh.

* * *

The worst day was Osgood's turn with Tam.

I wasn't in the room—I wasn't allowed to be—but I waited in the corridor, and when he came out he looked like a man who'd been diving too deep on old air. We got coffee from the machine that dispensed something legally distinct from coffee, and he wrapped both hands around the cup and stared into it.

"They asked about the depot," he said. "The raid. The guard I—"

He stopped. The guard. The young concession security man who'd come around the fuel racks faster than Tam expected, and who'd taken Tam's crowbar across the jaw, and who'd spent three days in a hospital and would carry a plate in his face for the rest of his life. Tam had testified to it in the mountain debrief, matter-of-fact, a cost of the operation. But in a hearing room, with a stenographer, under oath, matter-of-fact stops being available.

"They asked if I'd considered alternatives," Tam said. "I said no. I said I was thinking about Sef bleeding out against a fuel drum, and I wasn't thinking about anything else. And the counsel wrote it down, and it's on the record now, forever: Tam Voss, no alternatives considered." He drank the not-coffee and grimaced. "You know what the hell of it is, El? I'd do it again. Same swing, same angle. And I don't know what that makes me."

"It makes you someone who carried Sef four miles down a scree slope," I said.

"That's what it made me. Not what it makes me." He turned the cup. "The law's got a scale, and it weighs everything, and some things don't balance. You put the recordings on one side and a boy's shattered jaw on the other and the scale just sits there. Doesn't tip. Doesn't forgive. Just sits."

"Then maybe the scale's not the whole instrument," I said.

He looked at me then, the way he'd looked at me across eleven years of birthdays and bad contracts, and said, "You always did talk like a woman who's never had to pay for anything," which was unfair and true, and we let it sit between us, because some things don't balance and some friendships do anyway.

* * *

Sef testified last. I sat in the gallery for that one, because he asked me to, and because the gallery was where the families sat—Ochoa's father, and a woman whose brother had driven the survey skiff, and a thin man in a good coat who turned out to be Halewick's son, twenty-six years old, with his father's exact steadiness in the set of his mouth.

Sef told them about the boot. The child's boot in the shallows at Mile 40, small, sun-bleached, laces still tied. He'd carried it out of the water and couldn't say why, and he'd carried it the whole way up the Serrats and back down, and it sat now on the evidence table in a plastic bag, and when Osgood asked him why he'd kept it, Sef was quiet for a long time.

"Because the river doesn't count things," he said finally. "The river just takes. Somebody has to count."

Then they played the last log entry in open session—Osgood had fought for that, and won—and I watched the gallery while it played. I watched the families listen to the voices of their dead reporting a catastrophe that had been reported, received, and answered with scuttle charges and an airboat. Halewick's son didn't cry. He sat with his hands flat on his knees, and when it ended he nodded once, like a man confirming a position fix, and I understood that for him the worst part was over. The not-knowing had been the worst part. The screaming, terrible as it was, had at least been an answer.

Sef came out of the room gray-faced, and Ilsa put her arm around him, and he let her, which he almost never did, and we stood in the corridor while the photocopier ate on, and nobody said anything, because there wasn't anything, and it was enough.

* * *

On our last evening in Vell Harbor, Osgood bought us dinner at a restaurant with cloth napkins, and we were, all four of us, catastrophically out of our element. Tam held the wine list upside down until Ilsa turned it around. Sef asked the waiter if the fish was local and then, when told it was, asked what the water quality was like, and the waiter laughed, and Sef didn't, and the laughter died in the waiter's throat in a very instructive way. Ilsa ate with the focused efficiency of a woman provisioning for a crossing. I kept waiting for someone to hand me a bill of lading I could actually read.

"To the *Marguerite*," Tam said, raising his glass. "Scuttled with honor."

"To Vashti," Ilsa said.

"To the survey team," Sef said. "Halewick. Ochoa. All of them. They got there first. They just didn't get out."

We drank. Under the table, my boot touched Sef's boot, and neither of us moved it, and that was the toast that mattered.

Osgood, to her credit, kept the sentimentality rationed. But over coffee she looked at me and said, "You understand the inquiry can only reach so far. The company will restructure. Some of the names will surface again, wearing new logos. The Kestrel will get a cleanup plan with a decade of appeals attached. This isn't the end of anything. It's the end of the beginning, if you're feeling generous."

"I'm not feeling generous," I said. "I'm feeling like a woman in a suit that doesn't fit."

"Good," Osgood said. "Distrust the fit. It's when the suit fits that you should worry." She signed the check with a fountain pen, an affectation I decided to forgive her. "One more thing. There's a recovery contract being drafted—state-funded, this time, everything aboveboard. Diving the wreck properly. Recovering remains. Someone suggested your crew by name." She looked at me over the coffee cup. "The suggestion came from the registry office. A clerk named Maren."

I felt that land in my chest like an anchor finding bottom. Maren, who'd opened the back door of the registry for us with her brother's name in her mouth. Maren, who'd told me the official channel was compromised and then become the official channel anyway, one forged routing slip at a time.

"She said," Osgood went on, "that the river should be dived by people who already know what's down there. And that you'd want to bring them home properly."

"We would," I said.

"The contract pays standard rates." Osgood's mouth twitched. "No triple. No clauses about silence. You can photograph anything you like."

"Standard rates," Tam said, wounded. "After everything."

"Standard rates," I said, "and the work is the point. Tam. It's the point."

He grumbled, but I saw him do the arithmetic he always did—the one where the column that matters isn't money—and I saw it come out the way I knew it would.

* * *

We flew out the next morning. From the air, the coast looked like a circuit board, all its secrets flattened into pattern. Somewhere north and inland, under the early snow, the Serrats were white to the summits, and the passes were sealed, and the cairns on the Pass of Names stood buried to their shoulders. Vashti was up there somewhere, in the company of every expedition that never came back, and the mountain was keeping her the way it keeps everything: without forgiveness, and without letting go.

Sef had the window seat. As we banked north he pressed his good hand flat against the glass, and I didn't ask what he was looking at, because I already knew: the Kestrel delta, a brown-green fan of honest water, waiting.

"Spring," he said.

"Spring," I said.

And I started, in my head, the list I always start—the tanks, the lines, the lift bags, the permits, the names—and found that for the first time in months the list was short, and clean, and had nothing on it but work worth doing, and people worth doing it with, and a river that remembered everything, and was finally, finally going to be made to say it out loud.
