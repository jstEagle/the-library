## 22. The Current Remembers

The Kestrel in spring is a different animal than the one we fled. In autumn it ran milky and sullen, carrying the concession's sins downstream like a man trying to smuggle evidence past a checkpoint. Now, with the snowmelt swelling it and the willows leafing out along the banks, it just looks like water. That's the trick of rivers. They don't hold grudges. They don't hold anything. They take what you give them and move on, which is more than I can say for any of us.

We came back the way we'd left—except slower, and legal, and with a federal writ folded in a waterproof case in Ilsa's chart bag. Six months of depositions, subpoenas, men in good suits asking us to repeat the worst weeks of our lives until the words wore smooth as river stones. The inquiry had done its work in its own time. The concession was suspended. Corvan was in custody, singing a version of events that made him sound like a bystander at his own crimes. And somewhere in a warehouse in the capital, sealed under evidentiary tape, sat a field recorder that had cost two lives and nearly a third.

But the survey team was still down there.

That was the thing that wouldn't let me sleep in my clean apartment in the coastal city, with its view of harbor cranes instead of treeline. Four men and two women who'd gone into the water in November, who'd tried to walk out over the Serrat and been stopped by weather and worse, whose bones the company had written off as equipment losses on some ledger nobody would ever audit. Maren had given me their names. All six of them. She'd copied them out by hand from personnel files the company never bothered to purge, because purging meant admitting there was something to purge.

"You don't have to do this," she'd said, when I told her we were going back. "The inquiry can send a recovery team."

"The inquiry sends teams," I said, "to places where nothing's going to bite them. We know that river. It's ours."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I didn't expect. She reached across the table and touched the back of my hand, once, like sealing a deal. "My brother hated cold water," she said. "He said it was honest. You always know exactly how much it wants to kill you."

* * *

Tam rebuilt the *Maggie's Wake* from the keel up—or rather, he built her successor, because you can't rebuild a boat you scuttled yourself in a flooded forest while security burned your camp behind you. He named the new one the same thing anyway. "Boats are like names," he told me, tightening a flange bolt with hands that still carried the white scars of frostbite from Whiteout Camp. "You don't retire them. You pass them on."

Sef came back too, against medical advice and his mother's explicit wishes, with a shoulder that ached when the barometer dropped and a new habit of touching the scar through his shirt like checking a wound hadn't learned any tricks. He was nineteen now, or close enough, and he'd stopped being the kid who found a child's boot in the shallows. I don't know what he'd become instead. Something older. Something that stood watches without being asked and slept light and looked at the water like it owed him an explanation.

Ilsa navigated. That was all anyone needed to say about Ilsa. She'd carried a septic boy down a mountain through falling snow and searchlights, and when the stolen radio crackled the news about the federal judge, she'd laughed once—a short, cracked sound—and kept walking. Some people process joy by stopping. Ilsa processed it by finishing the job.

We went upriver in the last week of the thaw, past the burned checkpoints, past the emptied villages where a few stubborn lights had come back on—people returning to houses they'd sold under duress, because a suspended concession means the buyout contracts are void and the wilderness keeps faith with those who keep faith with it. The water ran clear for the first time since the mining started. Tam sampled it three times a day with a kit Vashti had left behind, and three times a day he'd squint at the results and grunt, which in Tam's language meant *clean*, and possibly also meant *she'd have liked seeing this*.

At Mile 40 we tied off for the night, at the bend below Old Vashti's cabin. The cabin itself was gone—security had burned it in December, after the inquiry started, one last petty erasure—but the stone chimney still stood, and around it someone had planted potatoes. Someone was living there. We saw smoke.

Ilsa went up alone, because she was best at knocking on doors without frightening them, and came back twenty minutes later with a woman in her fifties wearing a hydrologist's old field jacket and an expression of wary hospitality.

"Renna," she said. "Vashti trained me, before." Before, in the way people up here said it. Before the company. Before the permits. Before everything went under.

She fed us nettle soup and wouldn't hear of payment, and when I asked her why she'd come back to a burned cabin in a poisoned valley, she shrugged. "Somebody has to keep the records," she said. "Water doesn't lie, but it does forget, if nobody writes it down. Vashti taught me that too."

* * *

The dive itself took four days.

The wreck sat where we'd found it, in forty feet of green-brown current off the drowned timber, and the spring floods had rearranged everything around it—silted her stern, torn away part of the superstructure, draped the whole thing in a season's worth of weed so she looked less like a ship than a reef deciding whether to become land. But the hull held. Scuttle charges leave a signature, and even under the silt, even after a winter, you could read it: the neat, deliberate wounds of a ship killed on purpose by people who knew exactly where to put the explosives.

We worked in pairs, in shifts, running lines from the surface because the current near the bottom could take an unwary diver and fold her into the drowned trees. Tam stayed topside most of the first day, running the winch and the air, hating every minute of it—he was an engineer, not a swimmer, and he'd tell anyone who'd listen that God intended humans to remain on the dry side of a hull. Sef dove with me. His shoulder limited his lift capacity, so he took the light work: photography, mapping, the careful documentation the original crew had been forbidden to do. Every frame he shot was a small act of restitution. *You were not allowed to be seen,* those photographs said. *So we're looking.*

The first body we found on day two, wedged in the engine room where the man had clearly crawled seeking air that wasn't there. I won't dress it up. Eight months in cold fresh water is eight months too long, and what we brought up was a shape that had been a person, and the person's name was Dario Maren—the archivist's brother, though we didn't learn that until we surfaced and radioed the name ahead. Ilsa relayed it to the recovery coordinator, and there was a silence on the line long enough that I understood Maren was standing next to her, and had asked to be the one to hear it first.

We wrapped him in canvas and weighted the bundle gently and marked the spot, and Sef cried inside his mask where nobody could see except me, and I pretended not to see, because that's a courtesy divers extend each other.

Five more, over three days. Two on the bridge, together, which told its own story—they'd stayed at their posts. One tangled in the wheelhouse ladder. One in the flooded galley, sitting in a chair, which was somehow the worst of all of them, that terrible domesticity, a person sitting down in the place where they ate. The last one we found outside the hull entirely, drifted into the drowned timber, arms open, face turned up toward a surface she'd almost reached. Forty feet of water between her and the sky. She'd gotten farther than the others.

We recovered all six. Every one. Tam insisted on it, standing on the deck with his arms crossed, telling the federal recovery liaison that no, we weren't leaving at five of six because the current was picking up. "They waited eight months," he said. "The current can wait a day."

On the last dive, Sef found something else, wedged behind a bulkhead plate: a lunch tin, rusted shut, and inside it a photograph of six people laughing on a dock somewhere, sun on their faces, none of them knowing. He brought it up and dried it carefully and we put it in the box with the remains, because it belonged to them, and things belong to the dead before they belong to anyone else.

* * *

The burial happened in the coastal city, in a cemetery with a view of the harbor, which is a poor substitute for a river but is at least water. Maren spoke. She didn't cry until the end, and then only briefly, and what she said I'll keep, because some words aren't mine to spend. Six flags, six names read aloud into official record, six holes in ground that will stay holes, which is more than the company ever gave them.

Afterward, in the parking lot, Sef put his hand on my shoulder—the good one, he'd say, grinning, though there wasn't anything funny in his eyes—and asked, "Are we going back up? To Mile 40?"

"We're going back up," I said.

* * *

We went in early summer, when the passes were open and the high country smelled like meltwater and stone. Just the four of us, plus a pack mule Tam had talked a farmer into renting and immediately regretted interacting with. We climbed the same route we'd fled down in the snow, and it looked different without the white terror draped over it—gentler, almost, green meadows and larch and the small industrious sound of pikas. But I remembered where every step had cost us. The mountain keeps its own ledger too.

At the Pass of Names, we stopped. The cairns were still there, dozens of them, the stone prayers of everyone who'd crossed or tried to. We added stones for our own dead. Sef built one for the survey team, six stones in a ring, and set the rusted lunch tin at its base, empty now, the photograph returned to the altitude where those faces had been heading. Ilsa added a stone without a word. Tam added two, because Tam has never in his life done anything at the standard quantity.

And then we walked down to Mile 40.

Vashti's chimney still stood against the sky. Renna had rebuilt a proper cabin beside it, and there were now two other families within walking distance, and a school met twice a week in what used to be a concession depot, and there were gardens. The valley was coming back the way a wound comes back—not healed, but closing, granulating, doing the slow patient work tissue does when the infection is finally named and treated.

I didn't build Vashti's cairn at the cabin. That wasn't right. She'd died at the Col, holding the narrows with a rifle and no intention of coming home, buying us twenty minutes with the only currency she had left. So we climbed back up—two days' detour, Tam grumbling magnificently the whole way—to the high col where the narrows pinched between the rock walls, and there we built it.

Flat stones, fitted tight, the way she would have appreciated: a hydrologist's cairn, engineered, load-bearing. Ilsa carved the letters with a chisel while Tam held the light. Not a eulogy. Just:

**VASHTI — SHE READ THE WATER**

It seemed like enough. It seemed like everything.

While they worked, I stood at the edge of the narrows and looked down the trail toward the pass, and I thought about what the wilderness had taken from us and what it had given back, and I realized the accounting didn't balance and never would, and that this was fine. That was the whole lesson, maybe. The wilderness forgives nothing—it took Vashti, it took the *Wake*, it took pieces of Sef and Tam and Ilsa and me that we'll never get back—but it keeps faith. It remembers who read the water and who poisoned it. It holds the bodies until someone comes. It opens the passes every spring, no matter what happened the winter before, and says *try again.*

A river doesn't forgive. A river continues. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

* * *

We went back down to the Kestrel, and Renna fed us again, and on the last evening Sef and I walked out to the shallows where he'd found the child's boot, a lifetime ago, in another season.

The boot was gone, of course. Rivers don't keep things. But the village upstream had lights in it now, real lights, families, and a kid came down to the bank while we stood there—a girl, maybe seven, fishing with a stick and a bent pin, utterly unimpressed by us. She pulled out a grayling about the size of her forearm, held it up triumphantly, and released it because her mother had told her the fish were still getting healthy and to throw back the small ones.

"Eat them next year," she informed us, with the authority of a government minister, and went back to her stick.

Sef watched her for a while. Then he said, quietly, "Do you think it sticks? The inquiry, the suspension. Or does it all go back?"

I thought about Corvan in custody, singing. About the permits, voided. About Maren in her office at the registry, rebuilding files the company had treated as disposable. About a judge in the capital who'd listened to a pirate broadcast read over emergency bands by a salvage diver with salt-cracked hands, and had chosen, against all precedent and pressure, to believe her.

"I think," I said, "that somebody has to keep the records. Water doesn't lie, but it does forget."

Sef smiled—that new, older smile—and threw a pebble into the current, and we watched it go under.

The Kestrel took it and moved on, clear and cold and honest, running toward the delta and the sea, remembering everything in the way that water does: not by holding on, but by passing through, and passing through, and passing through, carrying whatever we're brave enough to give it.

There's a contract on my desk back in the city. Triple rates, a fixer I've never heard of, a wreck in southern waters. Tam already smells a trap.

We sail Thursday.
