## 1. The Tide That Owes Nothing

The tide went out like a debt being called in.

Freya felt it before she saw it — a change in the harbor's breathing, the water pulling back from the pilings with a long, wet sigh, as though the bay itself had decided, after forty years of patience, to show what it had been hiding. She was halfway up the ladder of the *Maren's Luck* with a crate of frozen herring on her shoulder when the deckhand beside her stopped hauling and said, quietly, the way men say things they don't quite believe:

"Look at that."

She looked.

The bay was draining. Not the ordinary retreat of an afternoon tide but something vast and unhurried and wrong-seeming, the water sliding back past the channel markers, past the kelp line, past the old mooring rings bolted into rock that hadn't seen sun since her grandmother would have been young. Mudflats emerged, gleaming and brown-gold, stitched with channels that braided and re-braided like veins in a wrist. Gulls came down screaming onto ground no gull had stood on in living memory. And out past the breakwater, where the charts showed nothing but open water and a hazard symbol the local fishermen tapped twice for luck, the sea peeled away from a wall of black stone — and there, low in that wall, a mouth opened into the dark.

"The Tollhouse," someone said behind her. "God help us. It's the Tollhouse."

She'd heard the name in her three months on this coast, always in the same tone — half joke, half superstition, the way people talk about things their great-grandparents paid to see and their grandparents got bored of and their parents forgot entirely. A cave at the bottom of the bay, exposed only when the tide dropped lower than it had any business dropping. For a century, so the story went, a man with a lantern and a tin box had charged tourists a coin apiece to walk into the cold and come back out again. The Tollhouse. Admission charged. No refunds.

The crate of herring was going soft against her shoulder. She climbed the rest of the ladder and set it down, and found she couldn't make herself turn back to work.

* * *

Her name — the name she wore now, the one stitched into the tag of her oilskin like a costume label — was Freya. Just Freya, the way dockhands are named: short, forgettable, belonging to nobody in particular. She'd chosen it off a gravestone in a churchyard two hundred miles south, a girl who'd died at nine years old in 1911 and therefore could never contradict her. It fit well enough. Names were like boots; you broke them in or you limped.

Rosalind Whitlock was dead. Not literally — though there were days, hauling crab pots in the rain, when she thought the literal version might have been cleaner. But professionally, legally, in every way that mattered to the world that issued permits and certifications and blacklists, Rosalind Whitlock had been finished four years ago, and Freya was what crawled out of the wreck. A woman with strong hands and no history. A woman who fixed ropes beautifully and never explained why. A woman who flinched, very slightly, whenever anyone asked where she'd learned anything at all.

She stood at the rail of the pier as the tide kept falling, and the crowd kept growing, and the Tollhouse kept opening like a wound the sea had finally agreed to let breathe.

There were maybe two hundred people on the mudflats by mid-afternoon. Children ran the channels with buckets. Old men pointed at things buried in the silt — a ship's knee, a boot, the ribs of something that might once have been a skiff — and told stories that grew longer with each telling. Someone had brought a generator and a floodlight, absurdly, though the light wouldn't be needed for hours yet. The air smelled of iodine and old rope and the particular mineral breath of seabed that has been waiting a long time to be looked at.

Freya did not go down among them. She stayed on the pier, mending a net she didn't need to mend, because looking was dangerous and wanting was more dangerous still, and she knew the difference between the two the way she knew the difference between weather and climate: by having survived both.

But she watched the cave.

It sat perhaps forty meters out from the base of the headland cliff, its floor now bare and dripping, its entrance tall enough to walk into upright — a black arch in black stone, fringed with bladderwrack that hung down like the hair of something drowned. The charts called it a sea cave, unremarkable, one of dozens along this coast. The locals called it the Tollhouse because of the man with the tin box, and because everything old gets a name that makes it smaller than it is.

She could read the rock from here. That was the trouble with being what she was under the name she wasn't — you couldn't stop reading. The strata dipped toward the cave at a shallow angle, sedimentary over something older and harder, and where the two met there was a seam, a weakness, the kind of line water finds and widens and keeps. Caves like that went deep. Caves like that went *in*. And caves that went in along a seam of softer stone sometimes went through, into country the sea had never touched, dry passages, air, distance.

She caught herself calculating the tide tables. Low water had been at 13:40. Spring tide, the lowest in decades — the radio had been saying it all week with the giddy tone of newsreaders discussing a comet. The next low would be tomorrow, nearly as deep. After that the cycle turned, and the door would close for another lifetime, maybe forever, as the coast here was subsiding a few millimeters a year, slow as a held breath.

One day. Maybe two. Then the Tollhouse shut its till and went back to sleep.

"You're staring," said Marta, the harbormaster's wife, coming up beside her with two cups of tea she hadn't been asked to bring. Marta brought tea the way other people brought opinions: constantly, and whether wanted or not.

"I'm working."

"You're staring at the Tollhouse like it owes you money." Marta handed her a cup anyway. "Everyone is. Half the town's down there taking photographs of mud. You'd think the sea had never gone out before."

"It hasn't. Not like this."

"No." Marta sipped her own tea and followed Freya's gaze out to the arch. "My grandfather saw it when he was a boy. He said his father took him down and they walked right in, far enough that the daylight gave up, and his father told him the cave was where the sea kept its accounts. Everything the water took, it wrote down in there. He wouldn't go past the dark. Said the Tollman might be keeping books."

"And do you believe him?"

"I believe my grandfather was frightened of the dark and dressed it up as respect." Marta shrugged. "You're not going down there."

It wasn't a question, which meant Marta already knew the answer, which meant Freya's face had done something it shouldn't have. She made herself smile, easy and dockhand-dull. "Why would I? It's a hole full of mud and tourists."

"Mm." Marta looked at her a moment too long, then took pity, or pretended to. "Tide turns around six tomorrow morning. If a person were foolish enough to want a look before the crowds, that'd be the hour." She drained her tea, set the cup on the rail, and walked away without another word, which was Marta's way of being kind.

Freya stood a long time with the cooling cup in her hands, watching the light fail over the drained bay, and admitted to herself what she'd been refusing to admit since 13:40.

She was going in.

* * *

That night she didn't sleep so much as lie in her rented room above the chandlery and listen to the building settle, and beneath that, fainter, the sound of the bay refilling itself — water moving over ground that had briefly been land, reclaiming, erasing. She thought about all the times in her life she had walked toward a thing her instincts screamed at her to walk away from, and how every one of those walks had cost her something, and how she had never once regretted paying.

That was the sickness in her, if she was honest, and she tried to be honest in the dark when no one could hear. Most people's survival instincts worked. Hers had been ruined by training, by altitude, by years of standing at the mouths of places that killed people and walking in anyway because someone had to know what was inside. Halvard used to say she had a compass where her caution should be. He'd said it laughing. He'd also taught her half of what she knew, and he was dead, and the other half of what she knew had gotten her exiled from two countries under a name she no longer answered to.

Restless. That was the word. Three months of herring crates and net-mending and being nobody, and the nobody was starting to chafe like wet wool. She had built smallness on purpose — small room, small job, small circle, small lies — and she had believed smallness would be peace. Instead it was a cage sized exactly to her body, and tonight the sea had gone and opened a door in the side of it, and the door was forty meters of mudflat away, and it would close by Sunday.

In the morning, at ten past five, in the gray before the gray lifted, she went.

* * *

The mudflats at dawn were a different country than they'd been at noon. The tide was near its bottom, a little deeper even than yesterday, and the flats lay silent and silvered under a sky the color of dishwater. No tourists yet. No children with buckets. Just her, and the gulls picking at the wrack-line, and the Tollhouse standing open at the edge of the returning water like a held breath.

She crossed the flats fast, boots sinking to the ankle in the firmer channels, testing each step the way you test ice — weight rolling heel to toe, reading the surface, giving the soft patches the wide berth her feet chose without consulting her. Forty meters took four minutes. At the cave mouth she stopped, and the smell came out to meet her: cold stone, salt, rotting kelp, and underneath it something older and sweeter, the mineral exhale of deep rock, the smell of places that have never been rained on.

Her whole body said *no*. Every nerve she owned lined up and voted against this — lone entry, unknown cave, tidal window measured in hours, no partner, no one knowing where she was. This was the exact shape of decision that had filled the accident reports she'd spent four years not reading about herself. A competent person alone. An obvious risk. A body that would be found, or worse, not found.

She stood in the entrance and let the instinct speak, fully, respectfully, the way you let a frightened animal bark itself quiet.

Then she went in anyway.

Not recklessly — she was incapable of reckless, that was the curse of it. She'd taken the chandlery's spare coil of line over her shoulder, a headlamp and two backups, chalk, a knife, a foil blanket rolled tight as a fist. She noted the time: 5:20. Low water at 6:02. She'd give herself ninety minutes and turn at eighty-five regardless of what she found, because discipline was the only family member who had never left her. She marked the entrance with a cairn of three stones just inside, where the surge wouldn't take them, and she counted her steps as she moved into the dark, because numbers were a rope you could climb back up.

The first chamber was a disappointment, and she loved it immediately. A high vault of wet black stone, floored with shingle and stranded kelp, lit blue-gray by the light falling in behind her. Tourist depth. Tin-box depth. She could see the old iron ring bolted into the wall where the Tollman had strung his guide-rope, rusted to lace, and the ghost of a painted arrow on the rock, pointing deeper, faded to a rumor. On the walls, someone a century ago had scratched initials and dates, dozens of them, the graffiti of people who had paid their coin and wanted proof they'd been brave.

She went past the daylight.

The passage narrowed, turned, narrowed again, and the character of the rock changed under her hand — smoother, older, the seam she'd predicted from the pier showing itself as a band of pale calcite threading the black like a vein of lightning frozen mid-strike. Dry shingle gave way to dry gravel gave way to dry clay. No water. No surge. The sea's voice fell away behind her until the silence was total, the specific silence of underground, which isn't absence of sound but presence of pressure, the mountain listening back.

And the air moved. Faintly. Against her cheek, cool and steady, from *inside*.

Air moves through caves the way blood moves through bodies — it doesn't travel without somewhere to go. A draft meant a second opening, a system, depth. Her heart picked up a rhythm it hadn't been allowed in four years, and she hated herself for the joy of it and kept walking.

Forty minutes in, counting steps — six hundred and eleven — the passage opened without warning into a chamber the size of a chapel, and her lamp found the far wall, and she stopped so abruptly that her own echo arrived late.

The wall was covered in writing.

Not graffiti. Survey notes. Columns of figures in fading pencil, bearings and distances and depths, marching across the stone in a neat, disciplined hand she recognized before she understood that recognizing it was impossible. She knew that handwriting the way you know a voice on the telephone after years of silence. She had watched it fill notebooks across two continents, in tents and huts and the lee of boulders while storms argued outside. She had learned her craft copying its loops and its ruthless, beautiful economy.

Below the last column of figures, small and centered, three letters:

**H.V.**

Halvard Voss. Dead four years. Buried — she had assumed — under whatever mountain he'd died mapping, in whatever country had finished burying him after the first one finished blaming him.

The draft breathed against her face, steady, patient, coming from a crack in the chapel wall at knee height, a gap barely wider than her forearm, running back into the black beyond the reach of any lamp she carried.

Freya stood in the cold with her mentor's initials glowing in her beam and the tide beginning, somewhere behind her, to turn, and she understood with perfect clarity that the sea had not drained the bay for the tourists, or for the town, or for the gulls.

It had drained it for her.

She knelt at the crack and put her eye to it, and far back in the dark, impossibly, she saw the glint of metal — a case, wedged in the stone, waterproofed, waiting — and behind her, distant but unmistakable, the first whisper of returning water touched the shingle of the entrance chamber, and began to rise.
