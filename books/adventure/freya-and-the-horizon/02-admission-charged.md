## 2. Admission Charged

The dark inside the Tollhouse was not like other darkness. Freya had known the black of high-altitude nights, the blind whiteout of storm, the suffocating felt of snow caves she'd dug with her own hands and slept in while the wind tried to unmake her. This was different. This dark had been sitting in the mountain for a century, undisturbed, patient as a debt, and when her headlamp's beam cut into it, the light seemed to cost something — swallowed a few feet out from her face, absorbed by stone that had never once needed to be seen.

She stood in the entrance chamber with water to her shins, cold climbing through her boots in slow increments, and made herself do the thing she always did: count.

One breath for the ceiling — low, maybe eight feet at the mouth, rising toward a shadowed throat deeper in. Two breaths for the exits — one, behind her, already shrinking as the tide turned; the sea gave nothing back for free. Three breaths for the sound — drip, drip, the long exhale of water finding its way down through channels older than any map of them, and beneath that, faintly, a hum she couldn't place, like the cave itself was thinking.

Her survival instincts said *leave*. They had kept her alive on Kestrel Pass until nothing could have kept anyone alive there, and they had kept her alive since, through six years of small anonymous work under a name she'd chosen off a gravestone in a churchyard two counties over. The instincts were good instincts. She had learned to trust them the way you trust a dog that has bitten someone: respectfully, and with your hand ready.

But the hunger was louder today.

It had been louder for months now — this restless gnawing, this sense that the life she'd built out of smallness was a coat that no longer fit, that she was a woman of forty-one years walking around inside the shape of a much smaller one. Dockhand. Seasonal laborer. The woman who fixed things and asked no questions and drank alone at the Anchor & Chain and was, as far as Saltmere knew, exactly what she appeared to be: nobody, from nowhere, going nowhere slowly.

The tide had owed her nothing this morning. It had drained the bay anyway, pulled back farther than old Marta Kessler at the chandlery said it had gone in fifty years, farther than the oldest charts bothered to record, and it had laid bare the mouth of the Tollhouse like a secret told in sleep. And Freya had looked at it across four hundred yards of gleaming, dripping seabed — barnacle-crusted rock, stranded starfish curling their arms against the air, the whole intertidal world gasping in sudden daylight — and something in her chest had said *go* before her mind had finished forming the word *should*.

So she had gone. Alone, against every rule she taught herself after the pass, because there had been no one else to go with and because some part of her had stopped caring about rules that only protected her emptiness.

Now she waded deeper.

* * *

The tunnel narrowed and rose. She climbed out of the water onto a shelf of rock furred with pale lichen, and here the sea's reach ended — the walls were dry, dusted with the fine grey silt of centuries, and the air tasted of stone and iodine and something older, mineral, like licked iron. Her lamp swept across walls marked with soot smears, old ones, and her pulse ticked up despite herself. People had come in here with torches. People had come in here and wanted to remember they had.

The locals' stories crowded in unhelpfully. The Tollhouse, they called it, because a hundred years ago the ferryman who worked the bay had claimed the cave took a toll — a hat, a boot, once, according to the version told at the Anchor & Chain, a whole man who went in sober and came out the far side of his own life. Tour operators had charged sixpence a head to stand at the mouth and gawk at the dark. There were carved initials on the lower walls, generations of them, hearts and dates and boasts, all of it below the tide line now except what the lowest tides exposed, and Freya ran her glove along a few without reading them. Names meant nothing here. That was part of why she'd come.

The passage bent left, then opened.

The chamber was bigger than the front of the building she rented her room above — thirty feet across, domed, the ceiling lost in black above the reach of her beam. A natural skylight had collapsed somewhere in the deep past; a chimney of broken rock let down a thread of grey daylight, thin as wire, that touched the floor in a pale coin. Around that coin of light, arranged as if deliberately, as if someone had used the chamber the way a clerk uses a desk —

Freya stopped moving entirely.

There were crates. Rotted to slats, mostly, their stenciling illegible, their contents long since claimed by rot or rats or the damp. But against the far wall, set up on a ledge above the highest mark of even this historic tide, sat a case. Metal. Green-grey with age but intact, its corners still square, its latches crusted but whole. And stenciled on its lid, in paint that had faded to a ghost but not surrendered:

**H.V. — SURVEY — DO NOT FLOOD**

Freya stood very still and let the letters happen to her.

She knew those initials the way you know a scar. Halvard Voss had put them on everything — his ice axes, his tin mugs, the flyleaf of every book he owned, always in that same blunt carpenter's hand, always followed by the word he loved best. *Survey.* Not expedition. Not adventure. Survey: the humble, patient, radical act of writing down what is actually there. He used to say it was the most political thing a person could do with a pencil. She'd laughed at him once, twenty-two years old and invincible, and he hadn't laughed back. "Maps are promises," he'd said. "Every line you draw, somebody downstream believes it. So you'd better be telling the truth."

Halvard Voss had been dead for eleven years. Officially. The company's statement had been admirably brief — lost during an unauthorized survey of concession lands, body unrecovered, condolences extended to no one because there was no one, which was how Halvard had liked it and how, Freya understood now, the company had preferred him.

And his survey case was sitting in a sea cave under a tourist town, three hundred miles from anywhere Halvard had ever been reported to be.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, the way she'd once watched them shake on a mountainside while she tied off a rope she didn't believe would hold. Then she crossed the chamber, knelt on wet stone, and got to work.

The latches fought her. Corrosion had welded them half-shut, and she worked them with her knife point and a flat stone, gently, cursing under her breath in the old language Halvard had taught her for exactly this purpose — swearing at equipment being the one prayer he'd endorsed. The second latch gave with a crack that echoed off the dome. Inside, nested in tarred canvas gone stiff as bark, wrapped in oilcloth sealed with wax that still smelled faintly of pine when her nail broke through it:

A map.

Waterproof paper, the good kind, the kind that cost more than a dockhand earned in a month. She unrolled it across her knees with the care of a woman handling scripture or explosives, and her lamp threw its lines into sharp relief, and she read it, and the floor of the world tilted.

It was the River Vane. All of it. From the estuary towns to the upper gorge to the country beyond the gorge where the maps simply stopped — where the concession boundaries ran, where the permits held, where the hatching on every official chart dissolved into blank paper and the words *UNSURVEYED — CLOSED TO ENTRY* in the company's tidy bureaucratic hand. Halvard's map did not stop. Halvard's map went through. His inked line followed the river up past the gauging stations, past the falls, into the limestone country, and there, where the blankness began on every lawful map, his line ended at a small, precise, unmistakable symbol: a spring, drawn in section, emerging from the flank of the mountain itself. The source of the Vane. Headwater of the river that fed eleven towns and one very profitable concession, annotated in Halvard's blunt hand:

*Measured flow inconsistent with licensed abstraction. Records falsified upstream. Original gauge readings enclosed. If I am not able to publish, whoever finds this should ask why the company needs a legend to keep people away from a hole in a mountain.*

Enclosed. Her fingers found the bottom of the case. Beneath the map, a second oilcloth packet, heavier, rectangular, the wax seal on it unbroken. She didn't open it. Some instinct older than curiosity told her not to open it here, in the dark, with the tide turning and her name — her real name, the burned one, the blacklisted one — attached in every file the company kept to the man whose initials were stenciled on this box.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing sitting in her chest like swallowed ice. Halvard Voss had trained Rosalind Whitlock. Had vouched for her on her first certification, had signed her field logbooks, had taken her onto the Kestrel Pass survey that ended with fourteen names on a memorial wall and hers on two national blacklist registries. If anyone ever connected the drowned guide Rosalind Whitlock to the dockhand called Freya, and then connected either of them to Halvard Voss's last survey —

The dread arrived all at once, total, physical, a cold hand closing around the back of her neck. The past hadn't just surfaced. The past had been waiting in the dark for fifty years, waterproofed, patient, addressed in a hand she'd know anywhere, and the tide had rolled back and laid it at her feet like a delivery.

She repacked the case with hands that wouldn't quite obey. Map, packet, canvas, lid. Latches. She lifted it — lighter than she expected, which frightened her more than weight would have — and started for the entrance, and that was when the light hit her.

* * *

"Well," said a voice, cheerful and oily as bilge, "that's not a souvenir from the gift shop."

The beam came from the tunnel mouth — a powerful handheld lamp, backed by the silhouette of a broad man in a yellow rain jacket with the logo of Bayview Cave Tours stitched on the breast. Freya knew him distantly the way you know everyone in a town of nine hundred: Denny Oakes, second-generation tour operator, the kind of man who measured his life in season passes and grudges. He stood on the dry shelf with his light pinned on her and the case in her arms, and behind the light she could hear at least one other person breathing, younger, hanging back.

"Tide's turning," Freya said. Flat. Dockhand-flat. "You'll want to clear out."

"Tide's turning, sure." Denny stepped closer, and his beam dropped deliberately to the stencil on the lid, and she watched him read it, watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes — H.V., SURVEY, and above all the word he'd understand best, which was *value*. "Interesting. Very interesting. You know, my dad used to say there was stuff in the deep chambers. Company stuff, from before the concession. Everyone said he was full of it." He smiled. She could see the smile clearly, which meant he wanted her to. "Everyone said a lot of things were full of it."

"Put the light down, Denny."

"Or what?" He laughed, genuinely delighted. "You'll report me? To who? Look at you. No permit, no guide registration — oh, I checked, by the way, when you first showed up asking about work. Nobody named Freya exists. No last name anybody knows. Woman lives over the chandlery, fixes ropes better than a rigger, keeps to herself." He crouched, bringing his face into the edge of his own lamplight, and his expression was almost friendly, which was the worst version of it. "A woman like that finds something valuable in a cave she broke into on company-adjacent land, during a closed tide window, alone — well. That's not a find. That's evidence."

The case was heavy in her arms now, though it hadn't gained an ounce. Behind Denny, the younger shape shifted — a boy, she thought, sixteen maybe, holding a coil of spare rope and looking everywhere but at her, unhappy, a son dragged along on his father's errand.

"I don't want trouble," Freya said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"No," Denny agreed warmly. "You really don't. Trouble follows a paper trail, and you've spent six years making sure you haven't got one. Which means—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, a showman closing his best bit— "we don't need papers, do we? We can keep this simple. Neighborly. You leave the case with me, walk out, and nobody ever has to learn what else might be in your pockets. Or—" he shrugged, and the shrug was a door opening onto a room she'd spent six years boarding shut— "the harbormaster gets a call tomorrow morning. Curious tourists get a story. And somebody with a badge starts pulling a thread on a woman with no history, and threads, friend, have a way of unraveling all the way back."

The water sounds had changed. She could hear it even through the blood in her ears — the return of the sea, a low gathering note in the outer tunnel, the tide coming home to collect. They had minutes, not choices.

"The case stays with me," Freya said quietly. "But you're right that we can keep this simple."

Denny's eyebrows went up, pleased.

"You saw me carrying an old box out of a cave," she said. "That's all you saw. You tell anyone anything more — invent one detail, add one word about who I am or where I came from — and I will spend every hour I'm not working docks making sure Bayview Cave Tours loses its insurance, its mooring lease, and its safety certification, in that order, and I'll do it with paperwork so clean it'll make you weep. I'm very good with paperwork, Denny. It's the one thing I've had time to practice."

Silence. The boy behind him made a small sound, half a laugh strangled at birth.

Denny studied her, and whatever he found in her face made the showman recalculate. "Weekly," he said finally. "Fifty a week, cash, and I keep my mouth shut and my dad's stories stay stories. That's neighborly."

"That's extortion."

"That's Saltmere." He grinned, and stepped aside, sweeping his light courteously toward the exit like a maître d'. "Pleasure doing business. Mind the tide — she comes back mean."

Freya walked past him with the case against her chest, through the rising water, out into the enormous silver evening where the sea was already crawling back across the seabed it had briefly renounced. Behind her, in the dark, the Tollhouse waited with whatever else it held.

Fifty a week she could survive. What she couldn't survive, walking back across the wet shining flats with Halvard Voss's last truth in her arms, was the question that had surfaced with it: if the records were falsified, and Halvard had died proving it, then the company that ruined her name had a reason to ruin it — and the map in her hands led straight to the reason, and the permits protecting that country expired in weeks.

She reached the shingle bank as the first wave hissed over the cave mouth behind her, sealing the Tollhouse for another fifty years.

A voice behind her, on the seawall, dry and amused and utterly unfamiliar: "That's a survey case, unless I miss my guess. And you're not a surveyor."
