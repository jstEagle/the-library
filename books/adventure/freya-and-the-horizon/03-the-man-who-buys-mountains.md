## 3. The Man Who Buys Mountains

Augustin Vale had bought three mountains in his life, though only one of them appeared on any deed.

The first had been a tungsten claim in the Cordillera that killed two of his surveyors and made him rich enough to stop counting. The second was a glacier in the Svalbard archipelago, purchased for the pleasure of watching it calve from a heated tent with brandy in hand — a vulgar thing he did at forty and regretted at fifty. The third was not a mountain at all but a river: the Vane, or rather the paper shell around it, a concession held by the Meridian Water & Power Company that gave them the legal right to seal its headwaters behind a padlock of permits for another twenty years.

He had come to buy the lock before someone else welded it shut.

The harbor town smelled of diesel and kelp and old rain, the way coast towns always did, and Augustin stood on the seawall in a coat too good for the weather and let the wind have at him while he read the town the way he read everything: as a ledger. There were eleven buildings that mattered — the permit office, the company agent's house with its new roof, the chandlery, the hotel with its peeling sign promising HOT BATHS as if baths could be anything else. Everything else was scenery. Scenery was what you paid for after the deal closed.

Below him, the bay lay drained and glistening, the lowest tide in half a century according to every newspaper he'd read on the train up. The famous Tollhouse cave stood open like a pulled tooth, and a knot of locals stood around it the way people stand around anything free. He'd heard the stories on the boat — a century of tourists charged sixpence to gawk at a hole in the rock, and now the sea had opened it for nothing, which offended everyone in equal measure.

"Mr. Vale."

His man Pell had materialized at his elbow, as Pell did, thin as a filing cabinet and twice as organized. "The agent will see you at nine. I've taken the liberty of scheduling interviews with local guides through the afternoon. Six candidates." A pause. "Seven, if you count the woman who works the docks."

"Why wouldn't I count her?"

"No references. No certifications on file. She goes by Freya — single name, no surname anyone will give me. She loads herring and repairs gear and once, reportedly, talked a party of lost clam-diggers off the headland in fog by shouting directions for four hours." Pell consulted his notebook. "I included her because the story annoyed me. Stories that annoy me are usually load-bearing."

Augustin smiled despite himself. Thirty years of buying expeditions had taught him that Pell's irritations were worth more than other men's enthusiasm. "Put her last. If the day produces nothing, let her be the dessert."

* * *

The company agent's office smelled of varnish and fear, which was the natural perfume of any room containing a man about to lose control of an asset. Agent Corben was forty, pink, and sweating through a shirt that had been ironed within an inch of its life. Behind him on the wall hung a framed aerial photograph of the upper Vane valley — all granite shoulders and green water, the river threading through it like wire through wax.

"The concession," Corben said, "isn't for sale."

"Everything is for sale. Some things are merely priced honestly." Augustin set his cane against the chair — he used it more for authority than need these days, though his left knee had earned it honestly enough on the Kestrel approaches years ago — and folded his hands. "Your permits lapse at season's end. Meridian has no capital to renew them; I've read your parent company's filings, Mr. Corben, and so has my bank. After the lapse, the headwater reverts to open tenure, and there are three consortiums circling who would strip that valley for gravel and call it progress. I am offering to renew the permits myself, keep the source sealed, and pay your office a management fee that will fund your pension and your successor's pension. You may say no. But say it knowing what no costs."

Corben looked at the photograph of the valley as if it might advise him. It didn't. Men like Corben always caved eventually; the trick was making them feel they'd chosen the moment.

"I'll need to cable the board," he said.

"Of course." Augustin rose, took his cane. "Cable them tonight. And Mr. Corben — the board will ask whether I intend to develop the source. Tell them no. Tell them I'm a sentimental old man buying a view. It happens to be true, and it happens to be none of their business."

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter, and the first of his guide interviews waited in the hotel parlor like a row of unopened letters.

* * *

They were, without exception, men who had learned the river from postcards.

The first had guided salmon fishermen for twenty years and knew every pool on the lower thirty miles, which was roughly as useful as knowing every tile in a bathroom when what you needed was the plumbing. The second had climbed in the ranges to the south — real climbing, Augustin granted him that — but when asked about the gorge above the falls he described it entirely from photographs, and when asked about the siphon where the river went underground he said, cheerfully, "Nobody's been through. Probably can't be done." The third brought testimonials. The fourth brought a lawyer. The fifth brought his father, who had attempted the source in 1941 and turned back at the second gorge, and who narrated the failure in such loving detail that Augustin understood the son had inherited not the ambition but the turning back.

By late afternoon the parlor smelled of wet wool and disappointment. Pell sat in the corner making small, merciless notes.

"And the dockhand?" Augustin asked.

"She hasn't come. I sent word this morning."

"People who work docks don't leave work because a note asks nicely." Augustin stood, joints announcing themselves. "We'll go to her, then. I find it improves the negotiating posture — theirs, not mine."

* * *

He found her at the chandlery, or rather he found her hands first.

That was how he would remember it afterward, in the way that certain moments arrive in memory out of order, the significant detail arriving before the scene that contains it. Through the open door of the chandlery's back room he saw a pair of hands working a length of rope across a wooden fid — splicing an eye into the end of a mooring line, the strands tucked in a pattern he hadn't seen since he was a young man paying his dues on expedition crews in the years when rope still held lives the way chain holds anchors.

It was a double-braid splice, but that wasn't the thing. Anyone competent could do a double-braid splice. The thing was the taper — the way she'd thinned the core out over four feet instead of the lazy eighteen inches most riggers managed, so the eye would run through blocks without a hitch, so the line would fail somewhere honest along its length instead of snapping at the transition like a lie catching up with a story. Nobody tapered like that anymore. That taper had been invented by alpine riggers in the thirties and passed hand to hand like contraband, and Augustin had last seen it done by a Norwegian named Halvard on a glacier that later figured very badly in the newspapers.

He stopped in the doorway and watched.

She was younger than he expected — thirty, perhaps, though dock work ages people selectively, and there was weather in her face that could have belonged to forty. Dark hair cut short and practical. A canvas jacket gone soft as cloth-of-gold from use. She worked without looking at the rope, the way you only work when your hands have been trained past the reach of your attention, and she was talking to the chandler's boy, something low and easy that made the kid laugh.

Pell cleared his throat behind him. She looked up.

Her eyes did something interesting. They went from stranger, to assessment, to — briefly, quickly, professionally — exit routes. Augustin had seen that exact sequence before, on people who had reasons to know where the doors were. Then it was gone, smoothed under a dockworker's pleasant blankness.

"You're the one sending notes," she said.

"I'm the one buying mountains," Augustin said. "Or trying to. The mountains are being difficult."

"They usually are." She finished the tuck, whipped the throat of the splice with twine in six fast turns, and bit the end clean. "You here about the guide work? Your fellow already came by. I told him I don't hold papers."

"Papers tell me what a person is permitted to do. I'm more interested in what they can't help doing." He nodded at the rope. "Who taught you that taper?"

Something moved behind her face — a door closing in a house where all the doors had supposedly been sold with the property. "A friend," she said. "Dead now."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. He'd have hated the sympathy and loved the funeral." She coiled the line in flat, perfect loops and set it aside, and only then did she give him her full attention, arms crossed, weight settled. It was the stance of someone deciding how much of a conversation to allow. "So. You're hiring a crew to go up the Vane before the permits die. Everyone in town knows. Everyone in town also thinks you're mad, or a speculator, or both."

"Both is efficient."

"What's at the source?"

The question came flat and fast, no curiosity in it at all, which meant it was the most curious question she'd asked all day. Augustin had spent forty years learning the difference between questions people asked to hear answers and questions they asked to measure him. This was the second kind.

"A river," he said. "The beginning of one. I'm seventy-one years old, Miss Freya. I've owned things that could be weighed and things that couldn't, and the ones that couldn't are the only ones I kept. I want to stand where the Vane starts before some consortium puts a fence around it and charges admission. That's the whole of it. Whether you believe it is between you and your own experience of old men."

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, the tide was coming back in, filling the bay with that vast slow breathing the sea does, indifferent as arithmetic.

"I don't hold papers," she said again. "And I don't do interviews."

"This isn't one. This is an old man asking a favor." He took a card from his coat — heavy stock, his name embossed in a restraint that had cost extra — and laid it on the bench beside the coiled rope rather than handing it to her, because he had noticed that she did not like to take things from hands. "Lead wages. Double what Corben pays his rangers. A crew of your choosing, equipment budget without a ceiling, and my absolute word that when the trip ends, whatever name you're using then is the only name I'll ever write down."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the quay a winch started up, complaining.

"That's a strange offer to make a stranger," she said.

"It's a strange offer to make anyone. That's why it works." He turned for the door, then paused, because there was one more thing, and he was too old to leave things unsaid that wanted saying. "The taper," he said quietly. "I saw it last on a glacier called Kestrel, a long time ago. The man who did it that way went by Halvard. He had a student that season. Clever girl. Hands like yours."

He felt her stillness the way you feel a change in air pressure before weather.

"I don't know the name," she said.

"No," Augustin agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He tapped the card once with his cane and walked out into the gray afternoon, Pell falling into step beside him, and only when they were clear of the chandlery did Pell venture:

"You know who she is."

"I know whose hands those are," Augustin said. "Which in my experience is considerably more reliable information." He looked back once at the harbor, at the water rising to swallow the Tollhouse again, keeping its appointments. "Whether she is Rosalind Whitlock or Freya or the Queen of Sheba matters less than the fact that she splices rope like Halvard taught her, and Halvard is the only man who ever mapped the route we need. Find out everything about her, Pell. Gently. And do not — under any circumstances — use the name Whitlock where she can hear it."

"And if she refuses?"

Augustin smiled at the wet street, at the gulls arguing over the fish guts, at the whole damp indifferent world.

"She won't," he said. "People like us always say no first. It's how we check whether the yes is safe."
