## 4. Blacklisted Twice Over

The room above the chandlery cost eleven marks a week and smelled of tar, old rope, and the particular damp that gets into plaster when the sea is your nearest neighbor. Freya sat on the edge of the narrow bed with the survey case open across her knees, and she did not touch it. She had not touched it since she'd carried it up the stairs an hour ago, since she'd bolted the door out of a habit so deep it had outlasted the name it was built to protect.

Halvard's initials were stamped into the leather. H.V., in the blocky capitals he'd cut himself with a knife because he never trusted anyone else's tools, not even a stamp-maker's. She knew those letters the way you know a scar. She'd traced them once, years ago, on the spine of his field journal, back when tracing them meant nothing more than that she was tired and he was late coming down off the ice.

Outside, the tide was turning. She could hear it — the slow, patient slap of water reclaiming the bay, filling in the channels it had briefly exposed, erasing the Tollhouse cave mouth inch by inch as though the whole afternoon had been a mistake the ocean intended to correct. By morning there would be nothing but green water over black rock, and the tourists would come anyway, and pay their two marks, and squint at a swell in the surface while the tour operator told them whatever story sold best that week.

She should burn the map. That was the sensible thing. The pragmatic thing. She had gotten very good, these last four years, at doing the pragmatic thing, and every one of those things had cost her a piece of something she couldn't name and couldn't afford to lose and lost anyway.

She didn't burn it.

Instead she did what she always did when her hands needed work and her head needed somewhere else to be: she took out her repair kit and began re-waxing the stitching on her left boot, and she let the past come up the way the tide came up — uninvited, unstoppable, covering everything.

* * *

Kestrel Pass had been beautiful that morning. That was the first thing people always got wrong about it afterward, the ones who wrote the reports, the ones who testified, the ones who'd never stood in it. They described it like a wound. But Rosalind remembered blue sky. She remembered the light coming off the cornice above the col so hard and clean it made her eyes water behind her goggles, and she remembered thinking — actually thinking, with the part of her mind that was supposed to be keeping everyone alive — *this is the best day of the season.*

She was twenty-six. She had been leading for three years under Halvard's outfitting company, Vane & Whitlock Expeditions, and she was good. Not competent-good, not passed-the-exam-good. Good the way some people are good at hearing music — she could read a slope the way other people read a page, feel the load in a snowpack through her boots before any probe confirmed it, know which client was going to freeze up on the fixed lines before he knew it himself. Halvard used to say she had river sense even on rock, which he considered the highest compliment available to him, because Halvard believed everything in the world was secretly hydrology.

There were nine clients on the traverse. A mixed party, the kind outfitters take because winter is long and the accounts are thin: two bankers, a retired schoolteacher named Mrs. Okafor who kept a bird list and ticked off a lammergeier on day two like it was a religious event, a honeymooning couple who held hands even on the moraine, a journalist writing a piece about high-altitude tourism, and three young men from the capital who had bought the trip as a birthday present to themselves and treated the mountain like a bar they'd paid to get into.

The route over Kestrel Pass was standard. It had been run for forty years. It was in the guidebooks. And on the morning of the eleventh of March, in the fourth year of a drought that had everyone talking about how mild the winters had become, the standard route killed six of the ten people standing on it.

The avalanche released from the eastern bowl at 9:40 in the morning. Later — much later, in rooms with tables and maps and men in ties — they would establish that the fracture line ran at a depth of seventy centimeters, that the slab was wind-loaded, that the weak layer was depth hoar buried in November and preserved by the drought's thin insulating cover. All true. All knowable. Rosalind had known none of it, or rather she had known all of it in the way a doctor knows a disease they've never seen present this way. She had dug her pit that morning at first light. She had run her column test and gotten results that said *stable enough*, because the drought had done something strange to the layering — had built a pack that tested clean column by column and failed wholesale slope by slope, a thing she had read about in papers and never once seen in fourteen years of mountains.

She would learn later that Halvard had seen it once, in the Karakoram, in '89. He never told her that until after, when telling her could do no good, when he sat across from her in a lawyer's office and said, *I have felt exactly what you are feeling right now, and I survived it, and so will you, though I cannot tell you how.*

At 9:38 she had stopped the party on the shoulder below the col for a drink. Standard practice, rest stop, ninety seconds. It saved four lives and she has never once been able to feel glad about that arithmetic, because the same ninety seconds put the other six exactly where the mountain wanted them.

She heard it before she saw it — a sound like the pass clearing its throat, deep and wet and wrong. She turned and the eastern bowl was moving, the whole face of it, a white ocean deciding to pour itself downhill, and she screamed the only command that mattered, *LEFT, GO LEFT,* and four of them went left and lived, and six of them went where the terrain channeled them because there was nowhere else to go, and the slab took them at forty kilometers an hour into the funnel below the col.

Mrs. Okafor. The honeymooners. Two of the three young men. The schoolteacher's body came down against the debris fan with her binoculars still around her neck, the lens caps on, because she'd been saving them for the view from the top.

Rosalind dug for thirty-one hours. She dug until her hands stopped registering cold, until the rescuers physically pulled the shovel out of her grip, until Halvard arrived from the valley and wrapped his arms around her from behind and said, in his flat northern voice, *Rosie. Rosie. There's no one left to find.* She kept digging after that too. That was the part the inquiry never understood — not the thirty-one hours, but the hours after, the ones that served no purpose at all except that stopping felt like killing them again.

* * *

Freya set down the boot wax. Her hands were steady. They were always steady now; that was the first thing she'd trained back after Kestrel Pass, before her sleep, before her appetite. Steady hands. You can lead a rope team with shaking hands for about a week before someone notices, and then you're finished, so you make them be steady, and eventually the steadiness goes all the way down and becomes its own kind of numbness, and you tell yourself that's recovery.

The map lay in the case, dry and creased along its old folds. She hadn't looked at it yet. Not properly. She'd looked at it in the cave, by the failing light of her headlamp, long enough to understand what it was — a survey of the upper Vane, the headwater country, the sealed concession — and long enough to see Halvard's handwriting in the margins, small and dense and certain. But looking at it properly meant sitting with it the way she was sitting with the memory now, and she wasn't ready, and she suspected she might never be ready, and she picked the boot back up and worked the wax into the welt because the alternative was grief and grief was a luxury the new name couldn't afford.

Because here was the thing about the inquiry. Here was the thing nobody tells you about disasters: the mountain gets a paragraph, and the survivor gets a career.

The official report ran to two hundred pages. It found, in language of exquisite carelessness, that "the lead guide failed to adequately assess avalanche hazard," that "a more conservative route selection was available," that "the decision to continue past the morning's rest point reflected an overestimation of the party's capabilities." It did not mention the drought. It mentioned the drought once, in passing, as a background condition. It did not mention that the same week, two other parties had crossed the pass without incident, or that the regional authority's own forecast bulletin — the one Rosalind had checked at dawn by lamplight, the one she still had the carbon copy of — rated the danger *moderate*. It did not mention that the concession company whose permits governed the valley had refused, that season, to fund the remote weather stations that would have given her real data instead of a bulletin compiled from valley-floor readings forty kilometers away. The company's name appeared in the report exactly twice, both times in the acknowledgments.

Her name appeared four hundred and eleven times.

She counted. In the worst month, the month the report went public, she sat up nights counting, because counting was a task and tasks kept the other things at bay. Four hundred and eleven times. Her name, her birth name, the name her mother gave her, printed and reprinted until it stopped sounding like a name and started sounding like a verdict.

Two countries banned her. The first formally — a blacklist notice, license revoked, ineligible for guiding certification in perpetuity, the word *negligence* appearing in the operative clause. The second didn't bother with formality; a border officer looked at her passport, looked at her, and said, *We know who you are,* and stamped her entry refused without further process, and that was somehow worse, the casualness of it, the way a person's whole life can be closed by a man at a desk who doesn't even look up.

And the families — some of the families forgave her. That's the detail that undid her worse than the officials did. Mrs. Okafor's son wrote her a letter, a kind letter, saying his mother had died doing the thing she loved in the place she loved and that he did not blame her, and Rosalind read it forty times and wept and could not answer it, because answering it felt like accepting absolution she had no right to, and not answering it felt like another death she'd caused. The banker's widow did not forgive. The widow hired lawyers and went on television and said, *That woman walked my husband into a graveyard,* and the camera loved her, and why wouldn't it? Grief makes excellent television. Rosalind watched the broadcast once in a hotel lobby in a city where nobody knew her, and something in her chest closed like a valve and never fully reopened.

Halvard fought for her. That had to be recorded somewhere, even if only here, even if only in the privacy of her own skull where no inquiry could subpoena it. He spent his savings on lawyers. He petitioned the association. He wrote letters — dozens of them, long ones, furious ones, the handwriting getting worse as the months went on, as if the injustice were a physical weight pressing down through the pen. He got her ban reduced in the first country from permanent to twelve years. Twelve years. She would have been thirty-eight. He called it a victory and she watched him say it and saw that he believed it, and she loved him for believing it, and she understood, in the same moment, that she could never go back. Not because of the ban. Because of the four hundred and eleven times. Because anywhere her name was known, she would forever be walking around inside the disaster, wearing it, and every client who shook her hand would be shaking hands with the report, and every morning briefing would begin with the question nobody would ask aloud: *is she safe?*

So she did the pragmatic thing.

She let Rosalind Whitlock die.

Not dramatically — no faked drowning, no burned identity, nothing that would interest the authorities. She simply stopped feeding the name. She moved to the coast, three hundred kilometers from anywhere she'd ever been known, and she became Freya — a name she chose from a book of myths in a secondhand shop because it belonged to a goddess who traveled, and traveling was the only future left to her. She registered with the dock labor office. She hauled crates. She learned to splice line and read tides and keep her mouth shut about knots, because a dockhand who ties a guide's bowline too well gets asked questions, and questions were the one cargo she could not afford to ship.

It worked. That was the terrible thing — it worked. Four years of it. The world forgot Rosalind Whitlock the way the world forgets everything, efficiently and without malice, and Freya the dockhand became a fixture, a known quantity, a woman who kept to herself and worked hard and owed nothing to anybody. She had a room, a trade, a routine. She was safe.

She was also, she understood now, sitting on this bed with a dead man's initials under her hands, not alive. There is a difference between surviving and being alive, and the difference is roughly the size of a self. She had traded the self that killed six people — the self the report built, the self the newspapers drew — for a smaller one, a serviceable one, a hiding one. And she had told herself for four years that this was wisdom. Pragmatism. The clear-eyed acceptance of what the world had decided about her.

But the tide today had shown her a cave that the sea keeps secret nineteen hours out of every twenty-four, and she had gone in, alone, against every instinct she owned, and she knew why. Not for the money the tour operator would now bleed out of her. Not even for the map. She'd gone in because some part of her was starving, had been starving for four years, for anything that proved the hiding wasn't the whole of her. And the sea — indifferent, enormous, owing her nothing — had answered with a dead man's initials and a route to the one place on earth her past had buried its deepest secret.

* * *

Downstairs, a knock. Two knocks, then a pause, then one — the pattern the tour operator had specified, smug as a signature. Payment due. Or renegotiation. With men like Corvus Dill, every payment was a renegotiation; the debt was the product.

Freya closed the case. She slid it under the bed frame, into the dark, next to the boots and the spare line and the small locked box that held the only other objects she owned from her former life: a compass with a cracked bezel, and a photograph of a man in a red parka laughing at something outside the frame.

Then she straightened, rolled her shoulders, and put on the face — the flat, watchful, slightly bored expression of a dockhand with no history — and went down to meet him.

Dill stood in the street below her window, hat tipped back, counting coins into his palm with the satisfaction of a man handling something that obeyed him. When he saw her at the door he smiled wide, and the smile had teeth in it, and behind the teeth, behind the smugness, she caught something else — a flicker of calculation, quick as a fish turning. He'd had the case appraised, or tried to. Someone had told him something about waterproofed survey cases bearing certain initials, about what certain initials might mean to certain buyers, and the price of his silence had just changed.

"Morning, Freya," he said pleasantly. "Funny thing. I had a fellow in yesterday — collector type, passes through every spring. Saw me sketching the cave from my booth, asked what else the tide brought up." He weighed the coins in his hand. "I told him: sometimes the tide brings up things folks thought were sunk for good."

He let that sit between them, grinning, letting her do the arithmetic he wanted her to do.

"And I got to thinking," Dill went on, "about how a dockhand comes to recognize a survey case on sight. Comes to it quick, too — quicker than I did, and I've been selling that cave to tourists for eleven years."

The gulls screamed over the harbor. The tide was fully in now; the Tollhouse was gone, swallowed, keeping its counsel. Somewhere upstream, three hundred kilometers north and half a world away in every way that mattered, the River Vane ran down out of sealed country toward a source that would be legally entombed in six weeks' time.

"Name your number," Freya said.

Dill's grin widened. "Oh, I don't want your money, girl. Money runs out." He tucked the coins away and patted his breast pocket, where a folded paper sat — she could see its corner, could see it was a telegram blank. "What I want is a partner. See, I sent a wire last night. To a gentleman down south who buys mountains. Told him the tide gave up a treasure, and I know who can read it."

He tipped his hat, cheerful as a ferryman.

"He'll be here Thursday. Best decide who you are before he arrives."
