## 5. Six Weeks of Paper

Augustin Vale had spent sixty-one years learning that the most expensive things in the world were never sold — they were merely made available, briefly, to those who knew how to ask. Mountains. Silence. The particular quality of light at six thousand feet in the last week of September. None of it appeared on any ledger he kept, and yet all of it was priced, and all of it could be lost.

He sat in the back room of the Salt & Anchor with a cup of tea going cold beside him and a leather folio open on his knees, and he read the permit schedule for the eleventh time as though repetition might soften what it said.

*Concession Grant 44-C, Upper Vane Headwaters. Exclusive survey and extraction rights, Meridian Consolidated Water & Power. Term expires end of season: October 31st. Renewal subject to review; renewal not guaranteed.*

Not guaranteed. That was the phrase the lawyers loved, because it let everyone pretend. In truth he had seen the internal correspondence — a favor called in from a man who owed him worse than money — and the renewal would not come. Meridian had already begun transferring assets out of the valley. The company had taken what it wanted from the river's paper trail and intended to seal the rest behind a new grant cycle that would not open for twenty years. Twenty years. He would be eighty-one. Tomas would be forty and would have inherited nothing but the name of a man who had bought mountains and never once stood on one long enough to be changed by it.

The tea had gone bitter. He drank it anyway.

Through the window of the back room he could see the harbor, gray under a sky the color of wet slate, and beyond the breakwater the bay where the tide had done its unprecedented work three days ago. The Tollhouse, they called the cave. A century of tourists had paid two bits to peer into its dark mouth at ordinary low tides and see nothing but wet stone and kelp. Now the sea had pulled back like a curtain drawn by an indifferent hand, and for one week — perhaps less — the thing itself was walkable.

And someone had walked it. He was nearly certain of that now.

* * *

It had happened on his second day in town, and it had happened to his hands before it happened to his eyes.

Augustin had gone down to the dock to inspect the fishing boats he'd chartered for the coastal approach — old habit, checking everything twice, because men who bought mountains learned early that the mountain always checked you back. One of the deckhands, a woman coiling line near the winch, had glanced at the climbing rope he'd left draped over a bollard and stopped mid-motion the way a musician stops when someone plays a wrong note.

"That splice is wrong," she'd said. Not rudely. Not even to him, really — to the rope, or to some standard she carried inside her like a compass needle. "Whoever tied it is going to lose the load."

"I tied it," Augustin had admitted, and felt the peculiar pleasure of a man confessing to something just to see what it cost.

She'd crossed the dock without asking permission, taken the rope out of his hands, and rebuilt the splice in ninety seconds flat — fingers moving with the blind certainty of long practice, each tuck seated and milked down, the whole thing finished with a sharpness of motion that reminded him of nothing so much as a surgeon closing. She hadn't looked up while she worked. When she did look up, her eyes went through him the way water goes through gravel — registering him, finding him shallow, moving on.

"Freya," one of the boatmen had called her. Just Freya, no surname offered, none asked for. Dockhands didn't trade surnames here. Surnames were things people with futures used.

But her hands. Her hands had a history, and histories have names.

That night Augustin had sat in his rented room above the chandlery with a glass of the local whisky — adequate, which was generous — and turned the problem over. He had interviewed eleven guides in four days. Eleven résumés of competent mediocrity: men who knew the lower gorge and lied about the upper, women who quoted insurance liability figures before they quoted route times, one earnest young fellow who suggested they might "wing it past the falls." All of them had looked at Augustin and seen exactly what he showed them: a rich old man buying an adventure, a wallet with legs, a mark.

The dockhand had looked at him and seen *nothing*, and that nothing had been more honest than anything the eleven had said.

There was more, though. There was the way she'd held the rope — the specific school of it. Augustin had climbed in enough ranges, hired enough guides, buried enough friends, to know that hands were trained by teachers, and teachers had lineages. That splice came out of the northern schools, the hard ones, the ones that produced guides whose names ended up on memorial cairns or on blacklists, and occasionally both. He had watched her fingers and thought, absurdly, of Halvard Voss.

Halvard Voss, who had walked into the upper Vane country nineteen years ago with a survey contract and come out with a report so clean, so reassuring, that Meridian had built half its concession case on it. Halvard Voss, who had died — drowned, they said, though no body had ever been recovered — before anyone thought to ask why a man of his scrupulousness had signed data that a first-year hydrologist could have torn apart.

Augustin had spent a great deal of money over the years learning not to say certain sentences aloud. He poured another finger of whisky and said this one to the empty room:

"Either that woman trained with Voss, or she *is* somebody."

* * *

She came to the Salt & Anchor at seven the next evening, because he had asked the harbormaster to ask her, and the harbormaster had reported back with a shrug that meant *she'll come, but don't think that means anything.*

Augustin had dressed carefully for the occasion, which is to say he had dressed like a man who had stopped caring about dressing carefully: good boots worn soft, a coat older than the waitress, no watch worth stealing. He'd learned decades ago that wealth worn openly bought obedience, and wealth worn carelessly bought curiosity, and curiosity was the only currency that mattered when what you wanted was a person rather than a service.

"Miss Freya." He rose, and she did not rise to meet the gesture; she simply stood there by the door of the back room with her arms loose at her sides, taking inventory of the exits, the window, the knife block visible through the kitchen pass-through. He recognized the habit. He'd seen it in soldiers and in fugitives and, once, memorably, in a cardinal. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. I've ordered food whether you eat it or not."

"I'll eat it," she said, and sat with her chair angled toward the door. "You're Vale. The one buying the concession."

"The one trying," Augustin corrected. "Meridian holds it until the thirty-first of October. After that—" he spread his hands, "—we'll see what the law leaves lying around."

"You're too old for the upper river."

The bluntness landed like a stone dropped in still water, and Augustin found himself laughing before he could arrange his face into dignity. It had been years since anyone had told him a true thing to his face without wanting something for it.

"I am," he agreed. "Sixty-one, two replaced valves, and a knee that forecasts weather better than the almanac. I don't intend to stand on the source. I intend to pay for the standing-on. There's a difference, and the difference is you."

The food arrived — fish stew, bread, a pot of tea fresh enough to steam — and she ate while he talked, which he took as a compliment. Only people who trusted their circumstances ate freely in front of strangers.

"Here is what I know," he said. "The Vane's headwater sits inside the concession boundary. Meridian has sealed the upper valley for nine years — no public access, no scientific access, no access at all except their own rangers, who are fewer every year because the company is winding down. The permits lapse at season's end. And here is what I believe: whatever comes after will not be a reopening. It will be a sealing. Twenty-year grant cycle, rolled into the regional water authority, locked past the lifetimes of everyone in this room." He leaned forward. "I have watched companies do this my whole life. They don't seal valleys because there's nothing in them."

Freya set down her spoon. For the first time she looked at him directly, and Augustin had the sensation — familiar, unwelcome, addictive — of being read. Not his words. His motives. She had the gaze of someone accustomed to weighing loads before trusting them to a rope.

"Why do you want the source?" she asked. "Nobody pays what you're paying to look at water."

"Nobody sane," he conceded. "I'm told I stopped qualifying some years ago." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, a small ritual of composure. "My brother surveyed that valley in his youth. He talked about it the way other men talk about the women they should have married. He died before he ever saw the headwater. I'm old enough now to understand that if I don't go this season, the going is never done by anyone. Not me. Not anyone." This was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and Augustin had lived long enough to know that the difference between those two things was where most sins were born. "Call it a pilgrimage with a budget."

"And the crew?"

"Small. Four, maybe five. A hydrologist — I've already engaged a Nils Ferro, recently of the Regional Water Authority, recently unemployed, reasons obscure but I suspect he asked an inconvenient question in public. A cartographer. My nephew, Tomas, who needs a river more than he needs another year in my sister's house. And a lead guide." He met her eyes. "Wages of four hundred a week plus completion bonus of five thousand. Equipment provided. Your terms on route decisions are absolute — I don't countermand a guide on the water, ever. Any member of the crew can be voted off at your sole discretion, including me, including my nephew."

Her expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, the way light shifts when a cloud moves off snowfield. Four hundred a week was triple what a dockhand made in a season. Five thousand was escape money. Reinvention money. A new name in a new country money.

"You said five," she said. "Four, maybe five."

"The fifth position is unfilled because I haven't found anyone qualified."

"You found me."

"I found your hands," Augustin said. "The person attached to them is your own affair. I'm not a curious man, Miss Freya, despite appearances. I've survived this long precisely because I don't ask questions whose answers would obligate me to act. You were somewhere before this dock. Everyone was. The river doesn't care. Water doesn't check references."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the harbor bell buoy clanked its slow count against the ebb tide, and somewhere down the street a dog was arguing with the sea. Augustin watched her do arithmetic that had nothing to do with money — he could almost see the columns: risk against reward, exposure against wages, the past against the future. He recognized the ledger. He kept one himself.

"Six weeks," she said finally. "You said the permits run out at season's end. That's six weeks."

"Thirty-nine days, if we're precise, and precision is apparently your religion." He opened the folio and turned it toward her: the concession map, the boundary lines, the upper valley rendered in the confident ink of a company that believed it owned forever. "We go in on foot from the north fork, up through the gorge, past the falls. Two hundred kilometers of country that hasn't seen an independent party in nine years. We reach the headwater spring before the thirty-first, we document it — properly, scientifically, with Ferro taking readings the whole way — and we come home with something no company can argue with. Whatever happens to the valley afterward, there will be a record. An honest one."

Something moved across her face at the word *honest* — fast, quickly mastered, but Augustin had spent a lifetime reading faces across negotiation tables, and he caught it the way you catch a fish rising: a ripple, then smooth water again.

"You've done this before," she said. "Bought expeditions."

"Seven. Three succeeded. Two failed honestly. Two failed dishonestly, which is the kind that teaches you things." He closed the folio. "I'll tell you the worst of them sometime, if we live. It's instructive."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire the least bad of the eleven and we probably drown in the gorge, and the valley gets sealed for twenty years, and I die having bought everything except the one thing I wanted." He smiled, and let the smile be tired, because it genuinely was. "I'd rather it was you. But I've been refused by better mountains than you, Miss Freya, and I'm still here."

She looked at the map a long time. Her eyes went to the headwater basin and stopped there — stopped the way a compass needle stops, or the way a man stops at a grave he wasn't expecting to find. Augustin noted it and filed it and asked nothing, because asking was the one tool he couldn't afford to use.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"I pick the cartographer. You said you had one lined up. Un-line them. I know someone."

"A teenager, I hear. The harbormaster's niece who draws charts on butcher paper."

"She's better than anyone you've lined up, and she has nothing to lose, which on a river is worth more than experience." Freya pushed back her chair and stood, and the interview was plainly over whether he liked it or not. "I'll take the job. Four hundred, the bonus, absolute route authority, and my fifth slot. We leave when I say the gear's ready, not when your calendar says."

"Agreed to all of it." Augustin rose more slowly, joints announcing themselves. At the door he paused, and here he committed the small sin he'd been rehearsing all evening — the sin of saying almost the true thing. "Miss Freya. Whoever you were before the dock — I hope they're along on this trip. Rivers like this one have a way of only letting people cross who bring their whole selves."

She looked back at him, and for one unguarded instant the mask was thin as ice in March, and beneath it he saw not fear exactly but vigilance — the exhausted, permanent vigilance of a person carrying something heavy in plain sight of everyone.

"Get your affairs in order, Mr. Vale," she said. "Six weeks goes fast on a river."

Then she was gone, and Augustin sat alone with the cold stew and the concession map, and allowed himself, at last, the sentence he'd been withholding:

*Now I only have to make sure she never learns what my brother's signature was doing on the falsified survey.*

He turned the folio face-down on the table, as if paper could be shamed, and began — methodically, the way he did everything — planning what he would burn first.
