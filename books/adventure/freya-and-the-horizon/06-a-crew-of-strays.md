## 6. A Crew of Strays

Freya interviewed them in the back room of the Salt & Anchor, a tavern that smelled of tar, old beer, and the particular despair of a coastal town that had stopped believing in its own future. She'd set up a table by the window where the light was honest — she wanted to see their hands, and she wanted them to see the water. Anyone who couldn't look at the river they were about to fight didn't belong on it.

The first three were tourists in ambition's clothing. A bodybuilder who called himself an alpinist and had never slept above sea level. A woman with a drone and a business plan. A man who kept saying *synergy* until Freya showed him the door.

Then came Nils Ostrander.

He was fifty-something and built like something the tide had given up on returning — long bones, stooped shoulders, a face that had been handsome before it decided to stop participating. He sat down across from her without being asked, set a battered leather satchel on the table, and said, "You're Whitlock."

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit.

"Freya," she said. "I'm Freya."

"Of course you are." He opened the satchel and produced a folded paper — not a résumé but a discharge notice, creased soft as cloth from years of being carried and read and refolded. "Hydrologist. Fifteen years with the Vane Authority. I flagged anomalous flow data in the upper gorge in my third season. I was told to re-measure. I re-measured. I got the same numbers. Then I got fired for insubordination, which is what they call it when you refuse to lie." He looked at her with eyes the color of slate run-off. "I hear you're hiring people the river has already chewed."

"I'm hiring people who can do the work," Freya said carefully.

"Good. Because I can't carry sixty pounds anymore, but I can read water better than anyone alive, and I know the Vane's temper better than the Authority does. They published their gauging records. I memorized mine before they confiscated them." He tapped his temple. "It's all still in here. Every reading. Every date they changed."

Freya felt the old hunger stir — the one the cave had woken, the one that wanted a self that wasn't hiding. This man knew things about the upper river that no permit-holder would ever put in print. And he'd been ruined by the same machine that had ruined her.

"Can you swim?" she asked.

"Badly."

"Then stay tied to the boats. You're hired."

Nils blinked, as if he'd prepared arguments for weeks and hadn't expected to need none of them. "That's it?"

"That's it. Dockside, dawn Tuesday. Bring your memory."

* * *

Bee arrived the way weather arrives — announced by nothing, suddenly everywhere.

Freya found her on the dock at low tide, crouched over a tide pool with a pencil stub and a sheet of paper salvaged from somewhere, drawing the coastline. Not sketching. *Surveying.* There were bearings written along the margins, depth soundings guessed from color, a scale bar drawn freehand but accurate to within a hair. The girl was maybe fifteen, all elbows and salt-stiff hair, wearing a man's oilskin coat cut down badly and boots two sizes too large, stuffed at the toe with newspaper.

"That's the Tollhouse," Bee said without looking up, pencil tapping the cave mouth on her map. "I've been trying to chart inside it since the tide dropped. Can't get the angles right. The echo lies."

"Most echoes do," Freya said. "Who are you?"

"Bee. Just Bee." Now she looked up, and her eyes were quick and dark and assessed Freya the way Freya assessed terrain — for weaknesses, for routes, for load-bearing truth. "You're the guide Vale hired. Everyone's talking. They say you're taking a crew up the Vane to the source before the permits die."

"They say right."

"I want to come."

"You're fifteen."

"I'm fourteen and eleven months, actually, and I'm the best cartographer in this town, which is a town full of fishermen who can't draw a straight line between their boat and the harbor light." She held up her tide-pool survey like a lawyer presenting evidence. "Your patron wants maps of country nobody's mapped in forty years. Who's going to make them? You?"

Freya took the paper. It was good. It was better than good — there was a discipline in it, a patience, the hand of someone who drew because stopping would be unbearable. And beneath the bravado, if you looked, there was the same thing Freya saw in every mirror: a person running.

"Where are your parents, Bee?"

The girl's chin came up. "Where are yours?"

Fair hit. Freya let the silence sit for a moment, then handed the map back. "Camp skills. Knots. Can you cook rice without burning it?"

"I can cook anything without burning it. I lived on a boat for six months."

"With whom?"

"With people who aren't relevant to your expedition." But something flickered across her face — fast, gone, like a fish under a rock. "Ask me about contour intervals instead. Ask me anything hard."

"I just did," Freya said. "Tuesday. Dockside. If you're late, you're left. If you cry about being cold, you're left. If you lie to me about anything that matters, you're left. Understood?"

Bee grinned, and it transformed her whole face into something young and fierce and briefly unguarded. "Understood. You won't regret it."

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone else is lying," Bee said, and walked off down the dock with her coat flapping, already drawing something in the air with her finger.

* * *

Tomas Vale did not apply. He simply appeared, the way money appears when it has decided to be inconvenient.

Freya came back to her rented room above the chandlery on Monday evening and found him sitting on her stairs, dressed in clothes that were trying very hard to be cheap and failing at the seams. He was twenty-three or so, with his uncle's heavy jaw and none of his uncle's armor. He looked like a man who had been arguing with someone for years and losing.

"You're the guide," he said.

"And you're trespassing."

"My uncle's name is Augustin Vale. He's funding this." He stood, and up close she could see he hadn't slept. "I want to come."

"Why?"

The question landed on him like a blow. He actually swayed. "Because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because my uncle hasn't spoken to me properly in four years, and he speaks about this river the way other men speak about dead wives, and I need to understand what it is he's actually buying. Because everyone in my family buys things instead of feeling them, and I am so tired of being purchased." His voice cracked and he hated it; she could see him hating it. "And because I can row. I grew up on sculls. I'm strong, I'm obedient, and I'll do whatever you say."

"Obedient," Freya repeated. "That's usually the last virtue to survive a river."

"Then I'll learn a better one."

She should have refused him. Everything in her training, everything in her scarred and sensible past, said: *do not take the financier's nephew, do not take the variable, do not take the man whose presence changes the mission's meaning.* But she looked at his hands — soft, yes, but steady — and she remembered Augustin Vale watching her repair his rope, that terrible moment of being seen, and she thought: *the uncle sent him, or the uncle will be furious, and either way I want eyes inside that house.*

"Medical conditions?" she asked.

"None."

"Fear of drowning?"

"Yes."

"Honest answer. Good. Tuesday. Dockside. Dawn. If you're late—"

"—I'm left," he finished, with the ghost of a smile. "Bee told me. She's terrifying, by the way."

"She is," Freya said, and found she was almost smiling herself. "Go home. Sleep. You'll need it."

* * *

They loaded at gray dawn, and the bay gave them its flattest, most indifferent face, as if to say: *I have drowned better crews than yours.*

Two wooden dories and a rubber supply raft. Rope, tarps, a week of food to start, Nils's instruments wrapped in oilcloth like relics. Bee arrived forty minutes early and had already reorganized the gear manifest by weight and frequency of use, which annoyed Freya deeply and impressed her more. Tomas arrived on time, pale, carrying a duffel that weighed too little — no tent, she noticed. He'd never bought one. He'd never needed one.

Nils arrived exactly at dawn, shook everyone's hand like a man closing a business deal, and immediately began arguing with Bee about the datum her charts used.

"Mean lower low water," Bee said.

"Based on whose benchmark? The harbor authority's benchmark shifted in the flood of '09 and nobody resurveyed—"

"It's *fine*, old man—"

"Don't call me old man, call me Nils, and it is not fine, it's off by eleven centimeters, which is nothing today and a death sentence in a narrow channel—"

Freya stood back and watched them fight and thought: *yes. This. These are my people.* Strays recognize each other. They don't sniff out pedigree; they sniff out damage, and they trust it, because damaged things have been tested.

They rowed out on the morning tide, rounded the headland, and entered the mouth of the Vane by noon.

The river at its lower reach was wide and brown-green and deceptively gentle, sliding through dairy country where cows watched them pass with bovine contempt. Bee lay in the bow of the lead dory with her chart board strapped to her chest, drawing the banks in swift confident strokes. Nils sat amidships calling depths off his memory of gauging records that were, he reminded them hourly, falsified. Tomas rowed and sweated and asked questions — too many questions, earnest ones, about eddy lines and hydraulics and what the word *strainer* meant, and why everyone went quiet after he asked.

First camp went up in the rain, because of course it did.

It went up wrong. Freya had assigned tents by lot and Tomas drew the site on the gravel bar, which looked perfect and was not — it was inside the high-water mark, marked by a ragged line of flood debris that he hadn't been taught to read yet. She made them strike it and move it uphill in the dark, in the rain, while Bee complained with the tireless precision of a fourteen-year-old and Nils offered commentary like a sportscaster ("Note the guide's expression. Note the jaw. That jaw has ended careers.") and Tomas apologized four times and meant it every time.

By the time the tents stood, everyone was soaked, cold, and furious, and Freya built the fire anyway, because a guide builds the fire, and put a pot of rice on, because Bee was busy sulking with dignity under a tarp.

They ate. The rain softened. And then Nils, wiping his bowl with bread, said to the fire:

"Kestrel Pass."

Freya's spoon stopped.

"Fifteen years ago," Nils went on, conversationally, to the flames. "A flood event in the upper Vane. A survey team caught above a rising gauge. One survivor walked out. The Authority's report blamed her judgment. The report was signed by a man named Corven, who signed a great many reports, all of them convenient." He glanced sideways at her, mild as milk. "I read everything, Ms. Freya. It's a disease."

The fire popped. Bee had gone very still. Tomas was looking at Freya with an expression she couldn't afford to decode.

"The survivor," Freya said evenly, "was my teacher."

It wasn't a lie. It also wasn't the truth. It hung in the smoke between them, half-and-half, and Nils looked at her for a long moment with those slate eyes and then nodded slowly, once, and returned to his bowl, and she understood that he had accepted the half-truth the way you accept a rope thrown across a gap — knowing it might not hold, choosing to grab it anyway.

"Well," said Bee, into the silence, "my turn. I ran away from a foster placement in March. The boat people were smugglers, kind of, but they fed me, and then they got arrested, and now I'm here. So." She shrugged fiercely. "Nobody here is clean. Good. Clean people are useless."

Tomas laughed — a surprised, barking laugh, the first sound of its kind Freya had heard from him. "My family owns most of the permits we're breaking," he said. "So I'm possibly a spy. I'm really not. But I understand why you'd think it."

"I don't think it," Bee said. "I *know* it. I just don't care. Spies can row."

Nils snorted. The rain ticked on the tarp. And Freya sat among them — the disgraced scientist, the runaway child, the rich boy in borrowed grief, and the guide with no name worth printing — and felt something in her chest that she had not felt since Kestrel Pass, since Halvard, since the world taught her that trusting people was how you buried them.

It was small. It was fragile as a stitch through wet leather. But it pulled tight when she tested it.

"Watch rotation," she said, gruffly, because the feeling was dangerous and must be managed. "Two hours each. Bee, you're with me first. Nils, show Tomas where the fire goes when it rains. We make the gorge tomorrow."

"The actual gorge?" Tomas asked, and she heard the fear in it, honest and bright.

"The actual gorge," Freya said. "Sleep while you can."

Later, in the black hours of her watch, she stood at the edge of the gravel bar and listened to the river talking to itself in the dark, gathering volume out of hidden country, already far along on a journey it had no choice about. Behind her the camp breathed — three strangers under canvas, each carrying a ruin they hadn't fully unpacked.

Ahead, upriver, past the dairy flats and the first gorges, waited the sealed headwater, Halvard's grave-country, and whatever truth he had buried there with the water.

The river ran toward it. So, starting tomorrow, would they.

And somewhere behind them, far downstream, a lantern moved along the concession road — slow, patient, official — though none of them saw it, and none of them would, until it was much too late.
