## 7. Upstream Rules

Bee had drawn maps of places she'd never seen. That was the whole trick of cartography, she'd decided at thirteen, hunched over stolen topo sheets in the back of a library in Correnvale: you could know a country better than anyone who lived in it, and it would never once ask your name. Lines on paper didn't check whether you'd run away from home. Contour intervals didn't care that she was sixteen and lying about it. A map was the only honest relationship she'd ever had.

So when Freya Whitlock — or Freya, or whatever her name actually was; Bee kept a private ledger of things people pretended not to notice — told the crew at first light that they'd be entering the gorge by way of the scree fan below the old toll bridge, Bee had already drawn it. Three nights before, by headlamp, from the survey sheets Nils had smuggled out of the concession office in his coat lining. She knew the fan's gradient. She knew where the river pinched against the north wall and where the talus would be loose as gravel in a drum. She knew this country the way you know a person you've only written letters to.

She did not know it the way Freya clearly did.

* * *

The morning came up gray and smelling of wet stone, the kind of light that made everything look like it was holding its breath. They stood at the mouth of the Vane Gorge with their packs cinched and their breath steaming, four strangers pretending to be an expedition, and Bee watched Freya do something she'd never seen a guide do: nothing.

Freya just stood there. Ten full minutes, maybe more, saying not one word, while Tomas fidgeted with his pack straps and Nils checked and rechecked the water-testing kit like it might escape. Freya stood with her weight settled low, hands loose at her sides, and read the gorge.

Not the map. The gorge itself.

Bee followed her gaze and tried to see what she saw. Rock, mostly. The two walls leaning together overhead like conspirators, the river running green-black between them, fast and silent in the way that fast water is silent right before it stops being so. A scree fan spilling down from the south rim, gray and indifferent. Birds — some kind of dipper — working the shallows.

"Okay," Freya said finally, and turned around. "Rules."

Tomas straightened like he'd been called on in class. He was twenty-three and built like someone who'd been fed well his whole life and never asked to carry anything heavier than a tennis racket, but there was something in him that leaned toward instruction, hungry for it, and Bee had already privately filed him under *harmless, probably useful later.*

"One," Freya said. "Nobody walks ahead of me. Not because I don't trust you. Because the river doesn't care how much I trust you." She held up a second finger. "Two. If I say *rock*, you freeze where you are. Don't look for the rock. Looking moves your feet. Three—" and here she paused, and something crossed her face too quick for Bee to catch it, "—if we get separated, you climb *up*. Never down. Water takes the low ground and everything on it. High ground waits for you."

"What if high ground's a cliff?" Tomas asked.

"Then you'll wish you were on a cliff instead of in the river, and you'll be right." Freya shouldered her pack. "Questions cost nothing now. Ask them now."

Nils asked about flow rates. He was the hydrologist, disgraced in some way he hadn't explained and Bee hadn't pried at, a big soft-spoken man with frostbite scars on two fingers and the habit of talking to rivers like they were colleagues who'd disappointed him. Freya answered him in numbers, clean ones, off the top of her head — cubic meters per second at this season, the gauge history going back years, the way the Vane dropped its level overnight when the snowmelt shut off in the high basin.

And that was the first tick of the pencil in Bee's private ledger. Because the official gauging record for the upper Vane — Bee had copied it, all of it, forty years of it, into her working charts — did not match those numbers. Not closely. Not in the way a river varies. Freya quoted figures that belonged to a different river entirely, a river that ran fuller and colder and truer than the one on paper.

Bee said nothing. She drew, later, a small mark in the margin of her field book. She had a system of marks. Her father had taught it to her before he taught her anything else, back when he was still a man worth learning from: *when the numbers disagree, one of them is a lie, and your job is to find out which.*

* * *

The scree fan was exactly as bad as Bee's map promised, which was to say it was worse than any map could promise, because maps don't render the sound a slope makes when it decides to move. It started under Tomas's left boot, a dry whisper that became a hiss that became a long grinding sigh, and then thirty meters of hillside let go of itself all at once.

Bee heard Freya say *rock* — sharp, flat, no fear in it, just command — and froze, arms out, heart slamming, while the world slid past her on both sides. Stones the size of loaves, then the size of tors, pouring down the fan in a brown-gray flood, and through the middle of it, impossibly, Freya moved *sideways*, three quick diagonal steps across the face of the slide like a person crossing a street in traffic, and caught Tomas by the shoulder strap and threw him — actually threw him, a hundred-and-sixty-pound grown man — behind the spine of bedrock that jutted from the fan's edge.

The slide roared itself out. Dust hung in the air. Somewhere below, the river swallowed the debris without comment.

"You okay?" Freya said to Tomas. Not breathless. Not even loud.

"I— yes. How did you—" Tomas was white to the lips. "You walked *across* it."

"It wasn't moving where I stepped." Freya looked at the slope the way other people looked at old enemies. "It was always going to move there. You could hear it. The grain of the rock changes pitch half a second before it goes. Listen long enough and the mountain tells you what it's about to do." She glanced back at Bee, and for one moment her eyes were distant, somewhere else entirely, some other slope in some other year. Then it passed. "Bee. You good?"

"Good," Bee said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "That bedrock spine isn't on my chart."

"No," Freya agreed. "It wouldn't be."

And she turned and led on, and Bee stood a moment longer looking at the spine of stone, and then wrote in her margin, beneath the first mark, a second one.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing Bee turned over all afternoon as they climbed into the narrowing throat of the gorge, as the walls rose sheer and dripping on either side and the trail became a ledge became a suggestion: *it wouldn't be.* Freya had said it lightly, but it was true in a way that went past modesty. The last published survey of the upper gorge was eleven years old. Slides, floods, and winters rewrite scree fans every single season. No map — not Bee's careful charts, not Nils's concession documents, not the aerial photos Augustin Vale had paid for — could have put that bedrock spine where it was. Only feet that had walked this fan recently. Very recently. Or a memory of it so deep it might as well have been recent.

Bee thought about the way Freya had paused before rule number three, as if choosing the words. Thought about the numbers that belonged to a different river. Thought about how, twice that morning, coming to forks in the ledge where the charts showed ambiguity, Freya had chosen a direction without opening a map at all — not hesitating like someone deciding, but hesitating like someone *remembering*, checking herself, making sure the decision looked like a decision.

Admiration sat in Bee's chest like a warm coal. Suspicion sat beside it like a cold one, and neither one would go out.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar where the gorge widened just enough to permit a fire, under a wall so tall the stars didn't reach the bottom third of it. Nils cooked — the man was a genius with lentils, which Bee found funnier than she could explain — and Tomas told a story about his uncle Augustin that involved a dead bear, a bet, and a duchess, and Bee laughed until her ribs hurt, and it occurred to her, sitting there in the firelight with flour on her sleeves and smoke in her hair, that she had not laughed like that since she'd jumped a freight out of Correnvale with a stolen compass in her pocket. This was the danger of crews. Of families, even accidental ones. They made you want to be known.

Which was exactly why the cold coal flared when Freya took the first watch and Bee, unable to sleep, lay listening to the river and watching the guide sit motionless at the fire's edge.

Freya wasn't looking at the fire. She was looking upstream, into the black throat of the gorge, and her face in the ember-light had an expression Bee had no line symbol for. It wasn't the alertness of a guide on watch. It was older than that. It was the look of someone standing outside a house they used to live in.

Bee must have shifted, or breathed wrong, because Freya turned her head — quick, precise, a hunter's turn — and for a moment their eyes met across the dying fire.

"Can't sleep?" Freya asked. Her voice was easy. Warm, even. Nothing in it but kindness.

"No," Bee said. "The river's too loud."

"It does that." Freya nodded toward the water. "First night in a gorge, everyone lies awake listening. By the fourth night you can't sleep without it." A pause. "Get some rest, Bee. Tomorrow's the narrows."

Tomorrow's the narrows. Bee rolled into her bag and stared at the underside of the tarp and added a third mark to her margin, because she'd shown Freya the route plan only once, sketched in charcoal on a split plank two nights ago, and Bee had drawn the narrows *unnamed*. Just a pinch in the lines. There was no reason — no reason at all — why Freya should know what the locals called that section of river, or that there were locals who called it anything.

Unless she'd been here before. Unless she knew this gorge the way Bee knew maps: intimately, obsessively, from the inside.

Unless the name on the blacklist — the name Nils had gone quiet and strange about whenever the Kestrel Pass report came up, the name that made Tomas's uncle's lawyers twitch — belonged to the woman keeping watch over their fire.

Bee fell asleep eventually, the way you fall asleep anywhere: because the body overrides the mind. But she dreamed of contour lines rearranging themselves into a face, and woke before dawn with her pencil already in her hand.

* * *

The narrows came at midmorning, exactly where her chart said they would, and they were worse than anything Bee had drawn. The walls closed to less than a boat's width apart, the river compressing between them into something that wasn't rapids so much as argument — white, furious, deafening — and the passage along the wall was a shelf of wet limestone barely wide enough for boots, slick with spray, dropping straight into the churn.

Freya went first. Of course Freya went first.

Bee watched her cross the worst section — a bulge of rock where the shelf thinned to nothing and the only option was three traversing steps on footholds that may or may not have existed — and she watched Freya place her feet without testing a single hold. Not recklessly. Precisely. The way you step onto stairs in your own house in the dark. Left foot here, right foot there, hand on the flake, weight through the heel, done — twelve seconds of movement so fluid it looked rehearsed, because it *was* rehearsed, because no one moves like that on terrain they're meeting for the first time.

Then Freya anchored a rope and talked each of them across, patient and unflappable, catching Tomas's elbow when he slipped, praising Nils's balance, telling Bee — quietly, with something that might have been respect — *"good feet"* as she came off the last hold.

Good feet. From her. Bee glowed for a full hour afterward and hated herself for glowing.

At noon they stopped above a bend where the river slowed and deepened, and Nils knelt at the waterline with his testing kit, and Bee sat on a boulder sketching the gorge walls, and that was when she saw it.

Across the river, ten meters up the far wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of moss: iron. A ring bolted into the limestone, rusted orange, with the frayed end of an old rope still knotted through it. And below it, faint under decades of lichen, chiseled letters in the stone itself.

Bee couldn't read them from where she sat. But she could see that someone had cut them deliberately, deep, meant to last. And she could see that Freya — filling her canteen at the water's edge, back half-turned — had seen them too. Had gone very still, canteen forgotten under the waterfall's edge, water overflowing over her hand, staring at those letters across the river with an expression that cracked her open like a fault line.

Then Freya capped the canteen, turned back, and smiled at whatever Nils was saying, and the crack sealed over so smoothly that Bee doubted her own eyes.

But she wrote it down anyway. Fourth mark. Fifth. Her margins were getting crowded.

That evening, checking her position by the sun, Bee opened Halvard's cave map — the drowned survey case's treasure, the document that had started all of this — to compare its strange, beautiful, hand-inked course of the underground river against her own charts. And she noticed, for the first time, what she should have noticed weeks ago.

The initials inked in the corner of the master sheet weren't just H.V.

There was a second set, smaller, in different ink, in a younger hand. Two letters, half-erased, scratched out by someone who'd wanted them gone but hadn't pressed hard enough.

R.W.

Bee sat very still in the blue dusk of the gorge, the map open on her knees, the river roaring its one long syllable below, and understood that tomorrow — or the day after, or the day after that — the numbers were going to stop letting her pretend.

Upstream, around the bend, something metal glinted on the far bank: a skeletal tower of rusted steel rising out of the willows, half-swallowed by the valley's green indifference.

A gauging station. Abandoned, by the look of it. And Freya, standing at the fire, had already turned to look at it — before anyone else had seen it at all.
