## 8. The Drowned Gauge

Nils had spent eleven years learning to distrust rivers, and the Vane was teaching him a twelfth lesson before breakfast.

He stood on the gravel bar where the gorge narrowed to a throat, watching the water move with the particular confidence of something that has never once been wrong. It came down from the north in a green so deep it looked solid, and it made a sound like a crowd murmuring in a language only other rivers understood. He had measured water like this for a living once. He had loved it. That was the part nobody warned you about — that the fall doesn't come from hating your work, it comes from loving it too much to check the numbers twice.

"Keep up, Nils," Bee called from ahead, her boots slapping wet stone with the cheerful recklessness of someone who had never watched a river take anything she loved.

"I'm keeping up," he said. "I'm keeping up thoughtfully."

Freya was thirty meters upstream, moving along the bank the way she moved everywhere — as if the terrain had been briefed on her arrival and had agreed to cooperate. She stopped at a bend where the cliff leaned out over the current and put up her hand, and the whole line of them stopped, because by now stopping when Freya stopped wasn't obedience, it was instinct.

"What is it?" Tomas asked, catching up, breathing hard. The boy — man, really, though he carried himself like furniture that hadn't decided what room it belonged in — was still finding his legs on the trail. Three days in and he'd stopped apologizing for slowing them down, which Nils counted as progress of a kind.

"Old iron," Freya said. "In the cliff face. See it?"

Nils saw it. A rusted bracket, half-swallowed by lichen, bolted into rock at a height that suggested whoever had put it there had been standing on something that no longer existed. Below it, another bracket. Then a third, descending toward the waterline in a staggered sequence that any hydrologist would recognize the way a musician recognizes a scale.

"That's a staff gauge," Nils said, and heard his own voice change. "Or it was."

"A what?" Bee asked.

"A measuring station." He was already wading toward the near bracket, trousers soaked to the knee, not caring. "Rivers get read. That's the whole secret of civilization, more or less — somebody stands at the same place every day and writes down how high the water is, and after enough years you can predict floods, set irrigation schedules, price the water, argue about the water, fight wars about the water. A gauging station is just a promise that somebody kept showing up."

"And this one?"

Nils touched the lowest bracket. The rust came off on his fingers in orange flakes, fine as pollen. "This one stopped."

* * *

The station itself was forty meters further on, tucked into an alcove where the cliff receded and the river slowed into a pool the color of bottle glass. Nils saw it first as a shadow — a rectangle of deeper dark in the cliff's overhang — and then his eyes resolved it into walls. Concrete, poured in panels, stained black-green with a century of spray. A flat roof sagging under moss. A door hanging open on one hinge, swinging gently in the breath of air that moved through the gorge, in and out, in and out, like the building was breathing.

"There's no record of a station here," he said. He said it mostly to himself, but Bee heard him, because Bee heard everything.

"You'd know?"

"I worked the Vane basin for six years." He kept his voice level. "Lower basin. Irrigation planning, flood modeling. I knew every active gauge on the river, every decommissioned one that appeared in the archives. This isn't in the archives."

"Maybe it predates the archive," Tomas offered.

"Maybe." But Nils was already climbing the slick apron of concrete at the alcove's edge, and the word *maybe* sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Stations didn't predate archives. Archives were how stations got remembered. A station missing from the record wasn't old — it was *removed*, and things got removed from records by people, and people removed things for reasons, and every reason he could imagine was worse than the last one.

The door scraped open under his hand. Inside, the air was cold and mineral, tasting of stone and standing water. The floor was flooded ankle-deep, clear over silt, and in the silt were the prints of their own entry, and beneath those, older prints — boot-shaped, long-heeled, pressed into mud that had dried and cracked and been rewetted so many times the edges had gone soft as old bread.

A workbench ran along the left wall. On it: a rusted tin that might once have held tea, a lamp with its glass chimney intact and blackened, a stone that had been used to sharpen something, and a ledger bound in oilcloth, tied with cord that crumbled when Nils touched it.

He untied it anyway. Some part of him had been untying forbidden cords since he was nine years old, and that part had never learned anything.

The ledger opened to a page dated forty-one years ago, in a hand that started confident and grew cramped as the years went by, the way hands do when they're writing in the cold.

Readings. Daily readings. Stage height in meters, recorded to the centimeter, in columns ruled off in faded blue ink. Temperature. Weather notes in the margins — *sleet, hard; wind NW, gusting; ice forming on the intake*. And beside each reading, in a second column, a second number. Not a correction. A second measurement, taken from somewhere else, or by some other means, and written down next to the official figure without comment.

For the first hundred pages, the two columns agreed.

Then, on a Tuesday in late autumn, they stopped agreeing.

* * *

Nils sat down on the workbench stool — which held, barely — and did what he had done every working morning for eleven years: he read data. But this time the data was reading him back.

The discrepancy began small. Two centimeters. Three. The second column running consistently below the first, as if the river being reported and the river being lived were two different rivers. Over weeks the gap widened. By spring it was half a meter. By the following year, the official column and the true column described waterways so different they might have been on separate continents — one a modest, well-behaved stream whose flows fit neatly within the concession's licensed extraction limits, and one a river running fat and wild and heavy, carrying twice the volume the paperwork allowed anyone to believe existed.

Someone was stealing a river. Or rather — someone was stealing the *record* of a river, which amounted to the same thing, because a river that officially doesn't exist can be sold, dammed, diverted, or buried without anyone having to admit they'd taken it.

"Find something?" Freya's voice came from the doorway. She stood with the light behind her, and Nils couldn't see her face, only the outline of her — shoulders squared, weight even, the stance of a woman who had already guessed what he'd found and was waiting to see if he'd say it out loud.

"Two sets of readings," he said. "Forty-one years of them. The official ones match the published record — I checked against the flow tables I memorized back when I still trusted tables. They're identical. Whoever wrote the first column wrote the river's public biography."

"And the second column?"

"The second column is the truth." His throat tightened around the words. "The real flows are nearly double the recorded figures. Someone stood in this room, day after day, year after year, in the cold, and measured the actual river, and wrote it down next to the lie, and never said a word about it. Never filed it. Never sent it anywhere. Just... kept it. Like a witness who testifies only to themselves."

Freya came inside then. She crossed the flooded floor without hurrying, crouched, and turned the ledger pages with a care that told Nils she understood exactly what she was handling. Water-stained paper, forty years of patience, a dead person's handwriting.

"Do you know whose hand this is?" she asked quietly.

"No." And then, because lying to her had become harder than telling her things: "But I know whose hand it *isn't*. Look at the signature block."

She turned to the final page. There was no final reading. Instead there was a single paragraph, written smaller than the rest, in ink that had gone brown with age:

*Record maintained in duplicate per survey contract 114-C. Official copy transmitted quarterly to concession office. True copy retained at source station per surveyor's discretion. Discrepancies noted and not reported, per instruction.*

*Per instruction.* Nils read the phrase three times. Somebody had told the surveyor to falsify the record, and the surveyor had obeyed — and had also, in the small stubborn way that some people resist, kept the truth alive in a concrete box in a gorge where nobody was supposed to look.

Below the paragraph, a signature. A name Nils didn't recognize, followed by credentials, followed by a date.

But it was the countersignature underneath that stopped his heart.

He knew that signature. He had seen it a hundred times, in the margins of reports, on the title page of the standard hydrological reference for the whole upper basin, in the acknowledgments of papers he'd studied in school. It belonged to the most respected name in the field — the man whose methods everyone used, whose integrity was proverbial, whose endorsement had legitimized the entire regulatory framework governing this river.

And it was the same signature that appeared — Nils knew this with a certainty that felt like falling — on the Kestrel Pass report. The disaster report. The one that had ended Rosalind Whitlock's career, cleared the concession company of all responsibility, and buried whatever actually happened up there under a landslide of official language.

He had reviewed that report during his own disgrace. Everyone in the field had. It was cited the way scripture is cited — as the final word, the settled matter, the thing you referenced precisely because questioning it marked you as unreliable. Nils had questioned it once, early, quietly, in a footnote of a paper nobody read. He'd noted that the flow figures in the appendix didn't reconcile with the precipitation records for the same season. The discrepancy was small. He'd assumed error. Everyone assumed error.

It wasn't error.

"It's the same hand," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, like it was coming through the station wall from outside. "The countersignature. It matches the Kestrel Pass report. Exactly — the same pressure, the same loop on the terminal letter. I've spent years staring at that signature. You don't forget a signature like that. It's on everything."

Freya had gone very still.

Not shocked-still. Nils had seen shock on people, and this wasn't it. This was the stillness of a woman standing in a room where a door she'd bricked shut had just swung open. Her eyes were fixed on the page, and something moved behind them — fast, controlled, drowned — like a fish turning under ice.

"How sure are you?" she asked. Her voice was perfectly calm. That was the frightening part.

"Signature analysis isn't my field," Nils said carefully, "but I've read that report more times than any document should be read. I cross-checked it against my own disgrace. I built spreadsheets. I was trying to save myself with it, back when I still believed saving myself was possible." He laughed, short and bitter. "I know that signature the way you know a scar."

Bee appeared in the doorway behind Freya, rain-jacket dripping, eyes going straight to the ledger with the naked hunger of a girl who loved documents the way other people loved candy. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong," Freya said, too quickly.

"Everything's wrong," Nils said, because it was, and because he was suddenly, terribly tired of the expedition's polite architecture of not-asking. "This station isn't in any archive. These readings contradict the official record of the Vane by nearly double. The surveyor was instructed to falsify the data — it says so, right here, *per instruction* — and the man who countersigned the falsification is the same man who authored the Kestrel Pass report."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Tomas had come up behind Bee, and Nils watched the boy's face do something complicated — watched knowledge arrive in it that he clearly already possessed, watched him decide, visibly, in real time, whether to pretend otherwise. It was like watching a man balance on a log in white water. For one moment Tomas looked directly at Freya, and there was something in his expression that was less surprise than appeal. *Don't,* the look said. *Please don't.*

Then he looked away and examined the rusted tea tin with great interest.

"We should keep moving," Freya said. "Weather window won't hold."

"Freya." Nils stood. His knees ached from the cold floor, and he found he didn't care. "Do you understand what this is? This isn't a gauging station. This is a grave. Somebody was killed here — not with violence, maybe, but professionally, reputationally, erased the way I was erased, the way—" He stopped himself. The way she had been erased. He almost said it. The name rose in his throat, *Whitlock*, and he swallowed it down, because saying it here, now, in front of Bee and Tomas, would be a detonation, and whatever was coming between them deserved better than a detonation in a flooded concrete box.

"This is evidence," he finished instead. "Of a fraud that shaped an entire river's legal history. Of the report that ruined—"

"I know what it is," Freya said.

Three words, delivered flat and quiet, and Nils heard everything inside them — the exhaustion, the warning, the grief. She wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth with hands that were steady and careful, and she looked at him, and what passed between them was not an argument but a treaty: *not here, not yet, not in front of them.*

He agreed to the treaty. He hated agreeing to it. Eleven years ago he had signed a different treaty, with different people, the quiet agreement to let small discrepancies stay small, and it had cost him everything, and here he was signing again because a woman with a hidden name asked him to wait.

But as they climbed out of the alcove and back onto the trail, Nils found himself doing what he always did with data: holding it up against everything else he knew, looking for the pattern.

And the pattern was this. A mentor named Halvard, initials on a waterproof case in a sea cave. A map to a sealed headwater. A patron buying his way upriver on a deadline. A guide leading them through country she shouldn't know, navigating by memory instead of maps. A nephew who flinched whenever the concession was mentioned. And now a drowned station full of falsified readings, countersigned by the same hand that had signed away Rosalind Whitlock's life.

The expedition wasn't looking for the source of the Vane.

It was digging up a body. It had been from the moment they left the coast, and everybody aboard knew it except the two who didn't — Bee, who would take it hard, and Tomas, who apparently already had.

Behind them, the station door swung on its one hinge, breathing in and out, in and out, keeping its promise to nobody.

That night at camp, while Bee sketched the gorge walls by firelight and Tomas scrubbed pots with unnecessary force, Nils sat across the flames from Freya and watched her watch the river. The ledger lay in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth, and the firelight made the pack look like it was breathing too.

"Tomorrow we pass the falls," she said, to no one in particular. "Long day. Sleep well."

"Nils." She said his name, and he looked up. Her face was unreadable in the shifting light. "Whatever you think you found today — the river will tell us the rest. Upstream. Where it starts."

"That's not an answer," he said.

"No," she agreed. "It's a route."

She rolled into her bedroll with her back to the fire, and Nils sat alone a while longer, listening to the Vane run loud and honest in the dark — carrying, he now knew, nearly twice the water the world believed it carried, toward a source that somebody had paid a great deal to keep closed.

He thought about signatures. About how a man's whole life could hang from a few loops of ink, and how the same hand that had signed the Kestrel Pass report had signed this fraud, and how both documents pointed, like compass needles, at the same patch of high country they'd be entering tomorrow.

Sleep didn't come. Around midnight he gave up on it, and lay watching the stars wheel over the gorge rim, and admitted to himself what he'd been circling since the station:

They weren't following a river anymore.

They were following a cover-up, upstream, to its headwaters — and the woman leading them there had been swimming in it her whole life.
