## 9. What Tomas Carries

Tomas Vale had learned to sleep in the mountains the way a man learns to sleep on a train — lightly, with one ear open for the sound of his own life changing. It was a skill he'd acquired young, at his uncle's estate, where the corridors filled after midnight with the particular hush of important men making decisions that would never be written down. He heard them through walls: the low murmur of Augustin's voice, the clink of decanters, the occasional laugh that meant something had been settled and someone had paid for it.

He heard nothing like that now. What he heard instead was the river, patient as a creditor, working its way down the gorge below the camp. And above it — fainter — the sound of Freya Whitlock moving around the fire pit, banking coals the way she did every night before anyone thought to ask her to.

He couldn't sleep. He hadn't really slept since the drowned gauge, since Nils had stood there in the ruined station with his glasses fogged and his hands shaking, reading the falsified numbers aloud like a man reciting evidence at a trial no one had scheduled. *The signature matches the Kestrel Pass report.* Nils had said it quietly, almost to himself, but Tomas had been standing close enough to see Freya's face when the name landed. He'd seen what crossed it — not surprise. Recognition. The look of a woman finding her own handwriting in someone else's letter.

She knew something. She had known it, he suspected, from the first night, from the moment Augustin had spread the concession maps across the table in the harbor tavern and traced the river up with one finger like a man drawing a line around something he intended to own. And now Nils knew something too, or half of it, and Bee asked questions the way teenagers ask them — relentlessly, innocently, with no idea how much damage a single honest answer could do.

Which left Tomas carrying the rest of it alone, the way he'd carried everything alone since he was nine years old and understood, without being told, that some truths in his uncle's house were load-bearing. You didn't pull them out to examine them. You built your life on top of them and hoped the structure held.

It wasn't holding.

* * *

He found her at the water's edge an hour past midnight, when the moon had cleared the eastern ridge and turned the gorge into a corridor of silver and black. She was crouched over the shallows, rinsing grit out of a cook pot, and she didn't look up when his boots crunched on the shingle. Of course she didn't. She heard everything before it arrived; he'd watched her do it for two weeks, this woman who navigated by a sense that seemed to live somewhere beneath her skin.

"You should be sleeping," she said. "We make the narrows tomorrow."

"I know."

"Then say whatever you came to say and go back to your bedroll."

There it was — the flatness in her voice that wasn't cruelty, exactly. Efficiency. She spent her words the way a miser spends coin, and Tomas had spent three weeks trying to decide whether he admired that or feared it. He'd settled on both.

He sat down on a boulder near the water, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him. The river ran past, dark and quick, carrying snowmelt down from a source none of them had seen yet. He watched it for a while. She let him. That was another thing about her — she could wait out a silence the way other people waited out weather.

"My uncle funded the original survey," he said.

The rinsing stopped. The pot went still in her hands, though she didn't turn around.

"The one from thirty years ago," Tomas went on, because now that the dam had cracked he might as well let the whole flood through. "The concession survey. The one the gauging records were built on. Augustin paid for it. Not the company — him. Personally. Before the permits were ever filed, before any of it was official, he put up the money because he wanted the valley mapped and the company wanted it mapped cheaply, and Halvard—" he stumbled on the name, saw her shoulders tighten at it, kept going anyway— "Halvard was the surveyor my uncle hired."

Freya set the pot down on the stones. Slowly. When she finally looked at him, her face was unreadable in the moonlight, which somehow made it worse.

"Go on," she said.

"He found something. I don't know what, exactly — I was a child, I only know what I've pieced together since, from letters, from things said after too much wine. But the numbers that came back were wrong. Nils is right about that. The flow data was falsified, the seasonal yields were inflated, and the whole permit structure — the twenty-year seal, the concession boundaries, all of it — sits on top of those numbers like a house on a bad foundation." He swallowed. "And my uncle knew they were wrong when he signed off on them. That's the part I need you to understand. He didn't get fooled. He got the report he paid for."

The river filled the pause. Somewhere upstream an owl called once and got no answer.

"And why," Freya said at last, and her voice was very quiet, very controlled, the voice of a woman standing on a ledge checking the rock under her feet, "is he paying five people to paddle toward that foundation now?"

Tomas made himself hold her gaze. "Because the permits expire in six weeks. Because when they lapse, the valley reopens — new surveys, new assessments, real ones this time, done by people who don't work for him. And if anyone goes up there and finds the original field notes, the true readings, the documents Halvard actually wrote before someone else rewrote them—" He stopped. Started again. "My uncle isn't trying to reach the source, Freya. He's trying to get there first. To find whatever's buried at that headwater and make sure it stays buried. He wants to reach the wonder so he can kill it."

He'd rehearsed this. On the trail, over four days of walking, he'd rehearsed it a dozen different ways, softened it, structured it, imagined her reactions and prepared answers. None of the rehearsals survived contact with her actual face. She wasn't angry. That was the terrible thing. She just watched him, still as the boulders, letting him hang himself with his own rope.

"You've known this the whole time," she said.

"Yes."

"You signed on to lead us into a box canyon for a man who wants to bury the truth, and you've known that since before we launched."

"Yes."

"Why tell me now?"

And there it was — the question he'd been walking toward for four days, the one with no rehearsed answer, because every answer he tried on felt like either a lie or an indictment. He looked at the river. He looked at the fire pit behind them, where Bee slept curled in her map case like a fox in a den, where Nils snored with the untroubled rhythm of a man who believed the world could still be fixed with better data. He looked at all of it and then at her, and he told the truth because he had nothing left that was worth more.

"Because of the gauge," he said. "Because Nils read that signature and I watched your face, and I realized you already knew more than I did — maybe more than my uncle does. And because if this comes apart, it shouldn't come apart on you. Any of you. You're the ones who'll pay for it. I'm just the one who brought the debt along in my pack." His voice cracked and he hated it. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to let me fix it. Quietly. We get to the source, we find whatever's there, and we handle it ourselves — before Augustin arrives, before the company, before any of it becomes public. Please. Don't tell them. Don't tell Nils, don't tell Bee. If Bee finds out whose money put that falsified report in the archive, she'll—" He stopped, because he couldn't finish that sentence without betraying something else, something he'd promised himself he wouldn't use.

But Freya caught it anyway. She always caught everything.

"She'll what?" she said. "Finish the thought, Tomas."

"Bee's family lives off the concession," he said quietly. "Half the lower valley does. The towns, the seasonal work, the maintenance contracts. If the original surveys come out — if it becomes public that the whole permit regime was built on cooked numbers — it won't just be my uncle who burns. It'll be everyone downstream of him. Every family that signed on in good faith with a company they thought was honest." He spread his hands. "That's what he tells himself, anyway. That burying it protects people. Maybe some part of him even believes it. But I've watched him for thirty years, and I can tell you what I know: Augustin Vale has never once confused protecting people with protecting himself. He just prefers it when the two look identical."

* * *

For a long time Freya said nothing at all. She picked the pot back up, finished rinsing it with a kind of deliberate calm, set it upside down on a stone to dry. Tomas understood that she was thinking, and that her thinking happened in her hands, and that the worst thing he could do was fill the silence.

When she finally spoke, she spoke to the river.

"Halvard Osten was my mentor," she said.

Tomas went very still.

"I don't know what he found at that source. I don't know what's buried there or whether it clears my name or damns me further. But I know the man taught me to read water when I was nineteen, and I know he didn't falsify anything, and I know that whatever my name costs to clear, it doesn't cost the valley their livelihoods. So don't stand there telling me about protecting people as if it's a reason to keep secrets. I've lived inside a secret for six years. It doesn't protect anyone. It just gets heavier."

She stood, and up close, in the moonlight, Tomas saw what he'd been refusing to see since the rope repair in the harbor — the exhaustion under the competence, the vigilance under the calm. A woman holding a great deal, and holding it well, and paying for it hourly.

"But here's what I'll give you," she said. "I won't tell them tonight. Not because you asked — you lost the right to ask for anything when you boarded that raft — but because a crew splits in the middle of white water, and we hit the narrows tomorrow. If this comes apart, it comes apart on solid ground, at a time I choose, in words I choose. Do you understand me? This isn't mercy. It's tactics."

"It's more than I deserve," Tomas said.

"Yes," she agreed, without heat. "It is."

She shouldered the pot and turned toward camp, then paused, half-turned back, and the moonlight caught something in her expression that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite pity.

"One more thing. Your uncle funded the survey. Fine. But someone silenced Halvard — Nils said it himself, the record was rewritten, and men don't rewrite reports for free. If Augustin paid for the lie, Tomas, then ask yourself what else he paid for. Ask yourself why a healthy surveyor stopped sending letters home. And ask yourself why your uncle needs *you* on this trip, specifically, when he's spent your whole life keeping you away from his business." Her eyes held his. "You're not here because he trusts you. Think about why he brought you."

Then she walked up the shingle toward the dying fire, leaving Tomas alone with the river and the enormous weight of a question he should have asked years ago.

He stayed at the water's edge until the cold came up through his boots. Above him, the gorge climbed away into darkness, mile after mile of it, toward a spring sealed inside a mountain — toward whatever Halvard had hidden there, and whatever his uncle intended to do when he reached it. Six weeks of paper, Augustin had called it. Six weeks to get there first.

Tomas looked down at his own hands, pale in the moonlight, hands that had held nothing but ledgers and luggage his entire careful life, and understood with sudden, sickening clarity that they were going to have to hold something heavier soon. Loyalty or truth. Uncle or crew. The man who raised him or the people who had, in three short weeks, become the first family he'd ever chosen.

The river ran past him in the dark, indifferent, carrying everything downstream.

Tomorrow they entered the narrows. Tomorrow the current would take the choosing out of his hands for a while — and Tomas, who had spent his whole life avoiding exactly this kind of current, lay awake until dawn listening to it, and was almost grateful for the delay.
