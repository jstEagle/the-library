## 10. White Water, Old Wounds

The river told her the night before it turned.

Rosalind had learned to read water the way other people learned scripture — not from books but from repetition, from the thousand small sermons of eddy and foam-line. So when she stood at the head of the gorge that morning and looked down at the Vane running high and brown with snowmelt, she felt the message arrive in her body before her mind could translate it: *today is a day the river collects.*

The gorge narrowed ahead into a slot of granite no wider than a wagon road, walls rising sheer on either side, and through it the water moved with the confidence of something that had never once been refused. From the rim they could see the first rapid — a long chute of standing waves, then a horizon line where the river simply ended, pouring over some drop they couldn't see past.

"We portage," Rosalind said. "Around the top section, then line the boats down the left channel."

Bee was already shaking her head. The girl had her charts spread on a flat rock, weighted with four stones and one tin cup, and she pointed at the survey marks. "There's an eddy system here. See? The old gauge notes say it's runnable at this level. If we run the chute we save half a day, and half a day matters if the permits—"

"The gauge notes are forty years old," Nils said quietly. He'd been quiet all morning, since the drowned station, since the numbers he kept copying into his notebook like a man transcribing a confession. "And the man who wrote them may have been lying about everything else."

"They're *hydrology*," Bee insisted. "He wouldn't falsify flow rates. That's not politics, that's just — data."

"Everything is politics," Nils said, "if someone's paying."

Rosalind let them argue. It was easier than admitting what she already knew, which was that Bee was right about the eddies and wrong about everything else. There *was* a runnable line through the chute — she could see it, a dark ribbon of smoother water threading between two holes that would hold a boat and drown its crew without ceremony. She could see it because she had run worse lines than that in countries whose names she no longer used, under conditions that would have made this look like a garden stream.

She could see it, and she wanted it, and that wanting frightened her more than the rapid did. Because the woman who wanted it was the woman who had gotten people killed, or so the reports said, and six weeks of leading these three — of being Freya, competent and closed, a tool rather than a person — had made her forget, in small increments, how dangerous it was to be good at something when people were watching.

"Half a day," she said finally, "isn't worth a life. We portage."

But even as she said it, she was studying the line. And Bee, who missed nothing, saw her studying it.

* * *

They portaged the upper chute by early afternoon, hauling the boats along a ledge above the roar, and put back in below the horizon line where the river gathered itself for the second act. This stretch was worse in a different way: not one big drop but a quarter mile of continuous chaos, boulder gardens and lateral waves and a hole mid-channel that local lore called the Grinder, though no one living claimed to have seen it up close.

Rosalind ran it first, alone in the lead boat, calling the water as she went — *left tongue, right of the green, stay off the wall* — her voice carrying flat and clear over the noise the way voices only carry when the person speaking has done it a hundred times in worse places. She eddied out at the bottom and watched the others come down.

Nils came through fine, pale and precise, paddling like a man defusing a bomb. Tomas came through rattled but upright, and there was a moment when his boat clipped a wave wrong and slewed toward the Grinder, and Rosalind saw him correct — late, ugly, but correctly — and she thought, *he's learning,* and felt something in her chest that she didn't have a name for and didn't want one.

Then came Bee.

The girl was the lightest of them, and light boats do what light things do on big water: they fly, and then they get thrown. She entered the rapid clean, hit the first three moves exactly as called, and Rosalind allowed herself the luxury of thinking it was going to be easy.

Then the fourth wave took her sideways.

It wasn't even the big one. It was a small lateral, the kind you shrug off a hundred times, but Bee was leaning the wrong way — weight inside the turn instead of out — and the river seized the mistake and amplified it. Her boat broached across the current, hung there for one impossible moment broadside to the push, and folded.

Rosalind heard the sound of it even over the roar — the crack of the hull, the girl's cry cut short by water.

Bee surfaced once, downstream of the wreck, arms windmilling, mouth open in a scream that the river swallowed whole. Then the current took her into the boulder field, and Rosalind watched her go under against a rock and not come up.

Later, she would not remember deciding. She would remember the cold, and the taste of the water, and the strange calm that descended on her like a lid closing — the calm she had spent years trying to unlearn, because that calm was the thing that had been in the boat at Kestrel Pass, that had made her decisions quick and certain while the world came apart. The calm was not a virtue. The calm was a symptom.

She drove her boat into the field after the girl.

What followed took ninety seconds and would live in Rosalind's body for the rest of her life. She read the water at speed — not choosing a line but *taking* the only one that existed, a thread of current between two rocks with maybe a boat's width of margin. She caught Bee's jacket strap as the girl swept past a rock, hand closing on canvas, and swung her toward the stern. Bee was limp, half-conscious, lungs full of river. Rosalind got an arm across her chest, pinned her against the hull, and then did the thing that no textbook teaches and no certification course names: she backed the boat into an eddy behind a mid-channel boulder using a reverse ferry angle that required reading the seam of the eddy line from *inside* the turbulence, feeling it through the hull, because it was invisible from above.

It was a technique. That was the problem. It was a specific, named, teachable technique, taught in exactly one place in the world, by exactly one instructor, to a handful of students in a mountain program that had burned down in scandal eight years ago. Halvard had called it *sewing the seam.* He'd invented it himself, on a river in a country that later banned his student from ever returning. He'd drilled it into her until it lived in her hands instead of her head.

Her hands did it now without asking her permission.

She ferried to the bank, hauled Bee out, and worked her until the girl vomited half the Vane onto the stones and came back to the world coughing and cursing, which was the most beautiful sound Rosalind had heard in years.

"You're all right," she said. "You're all right. Breathe."

"I lost it," Bee gasped. "I leaned in, I knew better and I leaned *in*—"

"Yes."

"I almost died."

"Yes," Rosalind said again, and held the girl's head while she shook.

Upstream, Tomas and Nils were running the bank toward them, and Rosalind had perhaps ten seconds to notice what she had done. Not the rescue — the rescue was fine, the rescue was the one uncomplicated thing. What she had done was *sew the seam*, in daylight, in front of witnesses, with her whole body signing her name in a language only one dead man had ever spoken fluently.

Nils reached them first. He dropped to his knees beside Bee, checked her pulse with hydrologist's fingers, and then looked up at Rosalind — looked at her the way he'd looked at the falsified gauge readings, with the particular attention of a man who has found a number that doesn't fit.

"That eddy turn," he said.

"She's alive," Rosalind said. "That's what matters."

"It was a reverse ferry off the seam. From inside the hole." His voice was gentle, which was worse than accusation. "I've read about that technique. In the literature. There's one source."

"Nils." She kept her voice level. "She needs dry clothes and warmth. Help me."

He helped her. But he watched her the whole time, and she felt the watching like weather coming in.

* * *

Camp that night was a gravel bar beneath walls so high the stars were a narrow river of their own overhead. Bee slept wrapped in every dry layer they owned, exhausted and alive, her charts clutched to her chest even in sleep like a child with a stuffed animal. Tomas sat up stoking the fire longer than it needed, saying nothing, doing the wordless penance of a boy who had frozen on the bank while someone else went into the water.

Rosalind sat apart, mending the cracked thwart of Bee's boat by firelight, because her hands needed work and her mind needed silence, and she got neither.

Nils came and sat across the fire from her. He didn't speak for a long time. He was a careful man, a slow man, a man who had lost his career to precision and wore the loss like a coat. When he finally spoke, he spoke quietly, so the words wouldn't carry to the sleeping girl.

"At Kestrel Pass," he said, "they said Whitlock tried a rescue on the icefield. A crevasse extraction. Improvised anchor systems, tension angles nobody taught outside that program." He turned his hands toward the fire. "I reviewed the incident report. Twice. Before I signed on to this expedition, actually — I review everything, it's my affliction. The techniques described in that report were..." He searched for the word. "*Distinctive.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream the river ground away at its boulders, patient as a debt collector.

"Say what you mean, Nils," Rosalind said.

"Rosalind." He said it softly, testing it, the way you test ice before committing your weight. Not *Freya*. "Your name is Rosalind Whitlock."

The world did not end. That was the terrible part. The fire kept burning, the river kept running, Bee kept breathing ten feet away, and the name simply hung there in the smoke between them, exposed, naked, hers.

She found that she was gripping the thwart hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She made herself let go, finger by finger, because a guide who panics is worse than a guide who lies.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since the gauging station. The signature on the falsified readings matched the Kestrel Pass report — same handwriting analysis, same era, same program. I started wondering who else came out of that world." He fed a stick to the fire. "Today removed all doubt. Nobody else sews a seam like that. I've read every account of the Pass. I've read yours."

"And?" Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Are you going to tell them?"

"Tell them what?" Nils spread his hands. "That our guide is the most qualified river rescuer any of us will ever stand next to? That the woman who pulled Bee out of the Grinder today is the woman the reports say killed three people on an icefield?" He shook his head slowly. "I've spent eight years reading that report, Dr.— Ms. Whitlock. And I've spent eight years watching real data get buried by men with better handwriting than mine. I know what official accounts are worth." He looked at her across the flames. "I'm not telling anyone anything. But you should know that I know. Because secrets rot crews, and I won't have this one rotting mine."

"*Yours*?"

"Ours," he amended, and there was something almost like a smile in it. "I've decided it's ours. All of us. Whatever this expedition turns out to be."

Rosalind sat with it. The terror was still there — the old animal terror of being known, of the name escaping into the air where it could travel, could reach the concession men, could reach Augustin Vale, who had hired a stranger precisely because she was a stranger. Being Freya had cost her everything except safety, and now the safety was leaking out through a hole a dying girl and a drowning river had torn in the hull.

But underneath the terror, unwelcome as groundwater, was something else. Nils had said the name aloud and the sky had not fallen. He had said *ours*. Eight years she had carried the name like contraband, and the first man to find it had weighed it in his careful hands and handed it back instead of spending it.

"Why?" she asked him. "Why keep it?"

Nils considered the question with the seriousness he gave everything. "Because today I watched you go into water that would have killed me before I finished thinking about it," he said. "And because whatever happened at Kestrel Pass, I don't believe the river cares about paperwork. The river judged you this afternoon." He nodded toward Bee's sleeping shape. "That's the only review that's ever meant anything to me."

He rose, brushed gravel from his knees, and paused. "One more thing. Vale hired you through intermediaries. He's never seen your face. But he's seen your file — everyone with money has seen the Kestrel Pass file. If he figures it out before you tell him..." He left the sentence unfinished, the way hydrologists leave a channel unfinished when they don't like where it goes.

"He won't figure it out," Rosalind said.

"Probably not," Nils agreed. "But Tomas might. He's been watching you too. Everyone watches the person who saves the person they love." He glanced at the sleeping girl. "Sleep, Whitlock. Tomorrow the gorge gets harder."

He walked back to his bedroll, and Rosalind sat alone with the fire and the name, turning it over like a stone she'd found in the shallows — familiar, heavier than it looked, warm on one side from her own grip.

Tomorrow she would decide what to do about Tomas, about Vale, about the tightening net of eyes. Tomorrow the gorge would demand everything again.

Tonight she sat with the terrifying, unbearable gift of having been seen — and discovered that the worst part was not the exposure.

The worst part was how much she had missed it.

Across the bar, Bee stirred in her sleep and murmured something — a fragment of a chart coordinate, a river's name — and Rosalind realized the girl's charts were spread open beside her, and that the top sheet, the one she'd drawn of today's rapid, bore a note in the margin in her small fierce handwriting:

*Guide ran a line I've never seen written anywhere. Ask her where she learned it.*

The fire burned low. Upstream, around the bend, the company's rangers were somewhere in the dark valley, and the permits were ticking down like water through a narrowing channel, and tomorrow — or the day after — someone was going to ask her the question directly, out loud, in front of everyone.

Rosalind fed the last stick to the flames and began, silently, to rehearse the truth.
