## 11. The Confession at Cold Camp

The cold came up out of the ground before the fire could argue with it. They had made camp in a hollow above the river where the wind couldn't reach them — Freya's choice, though she'd framed it as Bee's, letting the girl "find" the shelf of flat stone on her own map so she'd have the small victory to carry into sleep. The site was good. It was also, Freya knew from a map thirty years old and a man thirty years dead, the last defensible ground before the gorge narrowed and the company's rangers would find them easy as coins in a drain.

She fed the fire while the others ate. Nils had managed a passable stew from dried fish and a root he refused to name, and Tomas had actually laughed twice today, which was two more times than the whole first week. Bee sat cross-legged with her charts spread across her knees, re-inking a contour line by firelight, her tongue caught between her teeth.

It was Nils who said it. He said it quietly, the way a man sets down something heavy he's been carrying all day and pretending isn't there.

"You ran that line at the falls," he said. "The one you used to pull Bee out of the rapid." He turned his spoon over. "I've read every technique manual printed in the last twenty years. That rescue isn't in any of them."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the river spoke its one long sentence, over and over, and never finished it.

"It's not in the manuals," Freya said, "because the woman who taught it was struck from them."

Bee looked up. Tomas set his bowl down. Nobody moved, which was its own kind of answer — they had all been waiting for this without knowing they were waiting, the way a rope waits for load.

Freya put another stick on the coals and watched it catch, because it was easier to speak to fire than to faces.

* * *

"My name isn't Freya."

She let that sit. Let it do its work. She'd learned long ago that truth lands harder if you don't rush to catch it.

"Rosalind Whitlock," she said. "That's what my mother wrote on the form. Rosalind, after a great-aunt nobody liked. Whitlock, after a father who left when I was six and took the good years with him." A breath. "Freya is a name I built the way you build a shelter — quickly, out of whatever was lying around, meaning to replace it later."

"Later" got a sound out of Bee, half laugh, half something else.

"I was a mountain guide. A real one. Certified, published, the kind whose routes get other people's names attached to them anyway because I didn't care about credit, I cared about the line." She turned her hands over, palms up, the scars catching orange light. "Eleven years ago I led a commercial party through Kestrel Pass. High route, late season. Six clients, an assistant, me."

Nils had gone very still. He was the one who would know the shape of what was coming; hydrologists and guides drink from the same well of disaster literature.

"There was a storm forecast for three days out. We should have been through the pass in two. But the client who paid the most wanted a summit day, and I—" Her jaw worked. "I let money argue with weather. Not even my money. His. That's the part I've never been able to forgive, that I did it for someone else's vanity and called it professionalism."

"What happened?" Tomas asked softly. He already knew it was bad. You could hear bad coming in a story the way you hear a rockfall before you see it.

"The storm came early. Two days early, and meaner than the models said. We made the pass col with visibility at nothing and the temperature falling like it had somewhere to be. I made a call to push for the shelter hut rather than dig in." She shook her head slowly. "Wrong call. My call. The assistant — a kid named Oren, twenty-three, wanted to dig in, argued for it, and I overruled him because I had eleven years and he had two and seniority is just a word for being wrong with confidence."

The fire cracked. Bee had stopped breathing, or close enough.

"We lost him on the descent. Whiteout, cornice collapse off the lee slope — a slope I'd crossed twice before and judged stable. He went into a couloir we couldn't even see the bottom of. We found him four days later when the weather broke." Freya's voice stayed level, which cost more than breaking would have. "Two clients lost fingers. One lost a foot. And I walked out on my own legs, which is the cruelest thing a mountain can do to you — leave you intact to carry the arithmetic."

"And the blacklist," Nils said. Not a question.

"Inquest cleared me of negligence. Technically. 'Tragic confluence of factors.'" She smiled without anything behind it. "But the guiding associations in two countries don't run on technicalities. They run on insurance and reputation, and I had become a name that appeared in the same sentence as a body. Kestrel Pass became a case study. My face was on the cover of the report. There wasn't a guide service from here to the icefields that would touch me, and the ones who would, I wouldn't work for."

"So you buried yourself," Bee said.

"I drowned myself," Freya corrected. "There's a difference. Buried things can be dug up. Drowned things stay down — unless the tide goes out far enough." She glanced toward the dark mouth of the valley, toward the coast they'd left behind, and saw understanding move through all three faces at once. "You know about the cave. You know what I found there. So you know the rest."

"Halvard," Nils said.

"Halvard Voss. My mentor. The best mountaineer I ever knew and the worst liar, which is why what I found in that case doesn't make sense yet — and why I need to tell you all of it, because you've been following a map drawn by a man I loved like a father, and you deserve to know what kind of man drew it."

Tomas shifted. Freya caught it — the small flinch, the look away toward the fire. She'd seen it before, on the traverse, whenever Halvard's name came up. She filed it, the way she filed everything now, and kept going.

"He taught me everything. Reading snow the way you read a face. Rope work. The rescue line Nils asked about — Halvard invented it, after his own brother died in a rapid in this very watershed, and he taught it to exactly four people in his life, and I was the last. When Kestrel Pass happened, he was the only one who didn't treat me like a disease. Everyone else offered condolences the way you offer them to the bereaved, except I was the bereaved *and* the cause, and Halvard just showed up at my door with a pack and said, *We're going up. You'll want to quit everything else, so quit it with me watching.*"

Her voice caught, once, and she let it.

"For ten years we climbed together. Then three years ago he took a contract surveying the upper Vane — inside the concession, under permit, working for the company. He wrote me letters from the field. Good ones. Funny ones. Then the letters stopped, and then a letter came from a lawyer saying his effects had been settled and I shouldn't expect anything further. No body recovered. Officially: accident, high water, solo error." She looked up. "Officially."

"And unofficially?" Bee whispered.

"Unofficially, I found his initials burned into a waterproof case in a sea cave that opens once in forty years, containing a survey map of terrain no legal expedition has touched in a decade, routed through features only someone who'd walked them could draw. Unofficially, that map leads to the source of the Vane, and the source is where the permits expire in five weeks, and the company that holds those permits is the same company whose hydrology reports signed off on the gauging data that—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The same reports that were cited when Kestrel Pass was ruled a freak weather event instead of what it partly was: a river system that had been lying about itself for years."

Nils made a sound low in his throat. "The falsified readings. At the drowned gauge. The signature—"

"Was Halvard's," Freya said. "I know. I've known since you showed me the logbook, and I've been carrying it alone because I couldn't make it true. Halvard Voss did not falsify data. I would stake my remaining name on it, and I don't have many names left to stake. Which means either the man who taught me honesty was a fraud, or someone needed a fraud and picked the signature of a dead man who couldn't argue." She fed the fire again. "I'm going up that river to find out which. And I dragged the three of you into it under a false name and a partial truth, and that ends tonight."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fire to settle twice.

Bee spoke first, because Bee always did, because silence was a kind of cage to her. "So the maps I've been drawing — the ones I'm proud of — they're based on a dead man's secret map, drawn illegally, by a girl who doesn't exist."

"Yes."

"Okay." Bee nodded, fast, processing. "Okay. That's — honestly? That's the coolest thing anyone's ever told me. Also I'm furious at you. Both at once. Is that allowed?"

"It's required," Freya said.

"Why not tell us at the dock?" Tomas asked. His voice had an edge she hadn't heard from him before, but it wasn't anger aimed at her — it was anger on her behalf, looking for somewhere to land. "At the start. Why wait until we're five weeks deep and can't turn around?"

"Because at the dock you'd have had a choice," Freya said, "and I was afraid of what you'd choose. Because every person who's ever learned my name has used it — to pity me, to punish me, to price me. Because 'Freya' got hired and 'Whitlock' got audited." She held his gaze. "And because I told myself it was safer for you. Which was half true. The other half was that I couldn't stand to watch you look at me the way everyone looked at me after Kestrel Pass. Like a door that had killed somebody."

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Then, in a voice that had changed registers entirely: "My uncle funded the original survey. The one Halvard worked under." He swallowed. "I've been carrying that since the second camp. I was going to tell you. I keep almost telling you." He laughed, ragged. "Apparently we're all liars on this river."

"We're all people on this river," Nils said. "Lying is just what scared people do with the truth until they're ready." He looked at Freya across the flames, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't hide it. "I lost my position because I flagged anomalies in concession-funded data. Twenty-two years, and one memo ended it. When I found that gauge station, I thought — finally, proof, vindication, the whole machinery of justice will grind forward." He shook his head. "I never once thought about the man whose signature was on the forgery. What it would mean if he was innocent. What it would mean for the person who loved him." He passed a hand over his face. "You should have told us. And I understand completely why you didn't. Both things are true, and I'm staying."

"You're staying?" Bee said.

"I'm staying," Nils said. "I've been blacklisted by committee. It's a club. We have terrible meetings."

Bee laughed — a wet, cracking laugh — and wiped her nose with her sleeve, and then she crawled around the fire and did the thing Freya had no defense against at all: she leaned against her shoulder, fifteen years old and smelling of woodsmoke and ink, and said, "You're still Freya. You're both. Rosalind is just the part that survived the mountain. Freya's the part that survived everything after." A pause. "Also I'm keeping the name. It suits you better. Rosalind sounds like someone who apologizes."

"She does apologize," Tomas said. "I've heard her. Under her breath. To rivers."

"To *one* river," Freya said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

They talked until the stars wheeled. Small things, mostly — Bee asking what Kestrel Pass looked like, really, and Freya describing it honestly, the beauty and the horror sharing the same frame. Tomas telling a story about his uncle that was, for once, fond. Nils explaining why the root in the stew was safe if you boiled it twice. The confessions thinned out into ordinary talk, and the ordinary talk was the miracle, because that was how you knew the family was holding: not in the dramatic declarations but in the fact that afterward, everyone still passed the pot.

Near midnight, Bee curled up in her bag by the fire and said, drowsily, "When we get to the source — whatever's there — you're not deciding it alone anymore. That's the deal. Whatever your name is."

"That's the deal," Freya agreed.

She took first watch, because she always did, and because she wanted to sit alone with what had just happened. For eleven years her name had been a wound she kept bandaged under an alias, and these three had pulled the bandage off and pressed their palms against it and called it theirs. It didn't hurt less. It hurt differently — the way a bone hurts when it's setting straight instead of crooked.

She looked up the valley, into the dark where the gorge narrowed and the company's country began. Five weeks of permits left. Halvard's map rolled against her spine. Three sleeping people behind her who knew everything now, and had chosen the fire over the exit.

Then she saw it — or thought she did, at first, a trick of starlight on the far ridge. A light. Moving. Steady, deliberate, descending the opposite bank in the slow switchback rhythm of men who knew the trail and were in no hurry, because the trail belonged to them.

A lantern. Then a second. Then the faint, carrying sound of voices across the water, too far to parse, close enough to count.

Three lights. Company rangers, on the night shift, moving downstream toward the lower valley — toward the town, the docks, the paper records of who had bought supplies and when.

Freya rose without a sound and stood between the fire and the ridge, her shadow thrown long across her sleeping crew, and began calculating how much darkness was left, and how fast a cold camp could break, and whether the river would cover the noise of their leaving — or betray it.
