## 12. The Concession Men

Augustin Vale had spent sixty-eight years learning to read rooms, and the room he was reading now was a valley.

He stood at the mouth of their camp in the grey hour before dawn, when the mist came off the river like breath off a sleeping animal, and he watched the far ridge where the light would touch first. It touched there. And below the touch of it, moving along the treeline with the unhurried discipline of men who owned everything they walked over, went three figures in company grey.

Rangers. Concession rangers — the Vane Consolidated's private constabulary, men paid not to protect the wilderness but to protect the paperwork that fenced it. Augustin had signed cheques that bought men like these for forty years. He knew their gait the way a farmer knows weather: the weight on the outside of the boot, the rifle slung for show rather than use, the pause at intervals to glass the valley. They were not hunting. They were patrolling, which was worse. Hunting meant they knew something. Patrolling meant they owned the ground under your feet and wanted you to remember it.

"Augustin."

Nils, behind him, voice pitched low, binoculars already up. "Three of them. Maybe a fourth in the trees. They're following the old haul road along the north bank."

"I see them."

"They'll see our smoke by midmorning if we light one."

"We won't light one." Augustin lowered his own glasses. The rangers had stopped at the bend where the river narrowed against a shelf of black rock, and one of them — the tall one, the one who moved like he'd been a soldier once and been bought after — turned and looked directly across the water at their camp. Not searching. Looking. The way a landlord looks at a tenant he intends to raise the rent on.

"They know we're here," Nils said.

"Of course they know we're here. We've been leaving footprints through their garden for nine days." Augustin turned back toward the tents. Freya was already awake — she was always already awake; he suspected she slept the way cats do, in shallow installments with her ears working — and Bee sat wrapped in her map case like a dragon on a hoard, and Tomas stood apart from all of them doing his best impression of a man unconcerned, which was the least convincing performance Augustin had ever watched his nephew give.

"Gather them," Augustin said quietly. "We move tonight. And Nils — no fires from here to the falls. Cold food, cold camp. Tell them it's my order and let them hate me for it. Better they hate me than learn why."

* * *

The reason arrived at noon, as reasons did, folded inside a bird.

Not literally — though Augustin had learned, in this country, never to entirely dismiss the literal. A supply runner out of the lower valley worked the river on a floating schedule of bribes and favors, and among the dried fish and lamp oil he sold them at criminal markup was a canvas packet addressed in a hand Augustin knew better than his own signature. He took it upriver fifty meters from camp, alone, and sat on a stone that had been sitting in that river since before either of their concerns existed, and he opened it.

One page. Company letterhead, which was itself a message: *we know you can be reached.*

*Mr. Vale —*

*It has come to our attention that your expedition party is currently operating within Concession Block Seven without authorization. We note with interest that your application for scientific survey access was withdrawn by your own office on the 14th of March, four days before permits for the block were set to lapse permanently.*

*We are puzzled. A man of your resources does not withdraw applications carelessly. We conclude you withdrew them because you understood what the lapse meant, and what it sealed, and we further conclude that your present presence in the valley is connected to that understanding.*

*We invite you to withdraw your people from the block within seventy-two hours. In exchange, Vane Consolidated will decline to pursue the matter of your trespass, and will regard your long association with this company as unimpaired.*

*We would prefer not to discuss the Kestrel Pass report. We are confident you share this preference.*

*— R.M., Office of Concessions*

Augustin read it three times. Then he did what he had done perhaps five times in his life, each time at a moment when the man he had built finally collided with the man he had been: he laughed. Alone, on a rock, in a valley full of people who trusted him, he laughed until his eyes watered, and there was nothing funny anywhere in it.

*We would prefer not to discuss the Kestrel Pass report.*

They had him exactly, precisely, surgically. Because the truth — the truth beneath the truth the crew didn't yet know, the truth beneath even the truth Freya half-knew — was this:

Augustin Vale had known about the falsified survey for twenty-two years.

Not suspected. Known. He had been on the board in those years, young and hungry and flattered to be seated among the great men of the concession era, and he had seen the Kestrel Pass report pass across the table with its beautiful, impossible numbers, and he had asked one question — one — about whether the gauging data supported the flow claims, and he had been answered with a look around the table that he still felt on his skin at night. And he had withdrawn the question. Withdrawn it, and then, years later, when the permits came up for renewal and the source was to be sealed behind twenty years of legal iron, he had done something worse than ask nothing.

He had withdrawn his own survey application. Four days before the deadline. Deliberately. Because he understood — the way only a man who helped build the machine understands — that an approved application would have triggered a records review, and a records review would have surfaced the original surveys, and the original surveys were the thing the entire concession had been constructed to bury.

He had known the permits were a trap. He had known the trap was designed to snap shut on anyone who came looking for the truth of the river. And he had applied anyway — no. Worse. He had engineered the whole expedition so that it would arrive *unauthorized*, so that when it failed, it would fail as trespass, as folly, as the vanity of an old man, and the documents at the source would remain legally sealed and practically untouched and nobody would ever connect Augustin Vale to them at all.

Except the letter proved otherwise. The letter, in four paragraphs of corporate courtesy, proved that the company had always known he knew. His withdrawal hadn't been clever. It had been *noted*. Filed. Kept in a drawer for twenty years like a loaded gun, waiting for the day he came up the valley himself.

They weren't threatening to expose him. They were reminding him that they'd never needed to.

Augustin sat on the stone a long time. Below him the river made its patient argument against every obstacle in its path, and lost none of them, and won none of them either, and simply continued.

He thought about Freya. About the way she'd looked at him the day he offered her the lead-guide wage — that flat, measuring look, a woman pricing a buyer. She thought she was hiding her past from him. He had let her believe it, because a crew follows a guide who believes herself unreadable, and because — he told himself this, and mostly believed it — her past was hers to tell. But there had been a night in the second week, cold camp above the gorge, when she'd spoken of her mentor Halvard with a grief too specific for legend, and Augustin had lain awake afterward understanding that the woman leading his expedition was carrying the other end of the same rope he was. Halvard had found the falsification. Halvard had been silenced. And Augustin had sat in boardrooms while it happened, signing things, saying nothing, telling himself that a man could not fight every battle.

A man could not. That was true. It had been true for twenty-two years and it remained true and it had never once, in all that time, made him lighter.

Tomas, then. His brother's boy, who had come on this expedition — Augustin had told himself — for the river, for the wonder, for a last chance at the uncle who'd been a name more than a presence. And who had, Augustin now understood with the terrible clarity of hindsight, come because Tomas had found something in the family papers. Because Tomas knew. His own nephew had signed on to a doomed walk into a sealed valley either to stop him or to watch him or — God help them both — to help him bury it deeper.

And the crew. Nils with his ruined career and his honest instruments. Bee with her maps and her mother in a company town. They trusted him. They called him *sir* with an irony that had softened, week by week, into something almost like affection. They believed they were on an adventure purchased by an eccentric rich man, and that the eccentricity was harmless, and that whatever secrets rode in Augustin Vale's breast were old men's secrets, dry as ledger paper, weighing nothing.

The letter weighed everything.

Seventy-two hours. Withdraw, and be spared, and spend the rest of his life knowing the spring ran clean and buried and that he had chosen his comfort over it twice. Stay, and the rangers would come up the valley with warrants instead of warnings, and the crew — the strays, the children he had gathered and armed with rope and trust — would be arrested alongside him, their names dragged through the same mud that had swallowed Rosalind Whitlock.

There was a third option. There was always a third option; he had built a fortune on finding them. But every third option he turned over in his hands had someone else's blood on the handle.

Augustin struck a match.

He held the letter over the flame and watched the corner catch, the fire walking up the page with its small orange feet, eating the letterhead first — of course it ate the letterhead first, fire has priorities — and then the courteous threats, and then the sentence about Kestrel Pass, which curled and blackened and became, briefly, legible in reverse, a mirror-image ghost of the words that had ruled his life for two decades, and then ash.

He fed the whole thing to the river. The ashes went into the current and the current took them apart and distributed them, molecule by molecule, into the water that flowed down to the towns, past the gauging stations, through the turbines, into the taps of ten thousand houses. Let them drink it, he thought. Let them drink the confession. It's the only form I've got.

Then he stood, and straightened his coat, and walked back down to camp wearing the face he had worn for sixty-eight years, and began to plan how to spend his last credit well.

* * *

He found Freya re-lashing the cargo raft — of course he did; she was always touching the boats, checking knots the way other men checked their pulse — and he crouched beside her and said, without preamble:

"The rangers will have a warrant by tomorrow night. We move tonight, after full dark, and we don't stop until the siphon."

Her hands didn't pause on the lashing. "You're sure?"

"As sure as I've ever been of anything I can't prove."

She looked up at him then, and he braced himself for the question — *how sure, sir, why sure, what do you know that we don't* — because she was the sharpest instrument he'd ever hired and he deserved the question. Instead she studied his face for a long moment with those river-grey eyes, and said:

"You got a letter today."

It wasn't a question either.

"The supply runner," she said. "He doesn't carry mail. He carries messages. Everyone on this coast knows that. So either you got a message, or you got something worth burning." Her eyes flicked, almost gently, toward the river. "I noticed you came back smelling of smoke and you haven't lit anything all day."

For a moment — one long, cold, vertiginous moment — Augustin considered telling her. All of it. The boardroom, the withdrawn application, the twenty-two years, the drawer the letter had waited in. She of all people would understand the arithmetic of complicity; she had lived inside its results.

But the mask had held this long, and the crew was fragile as a frozen waterfall, and if he confessed now, tonight, hours before a night march through patrol territory, he would not be spending his credit — he would be spending *theirs*. So he smiled the smile that had closed a thousand deals, and said:

"A man my age gets letters from his lawyers. They're all bad news. This one was merely expensive."

Freya held his gaze a beat longer than politeness required. Then she nodded, once, and went back to her lashing, and Augustin walked away feeling the mask sit heavier than it ever had — not because it had slipped, but because he had just realized, watching her hands work the rope, that she had seen it. Not what was underneath. Just the fact of it. The seam.

*Being seen is dangerous*, he had written in his journal thirty years ago, in another life. He had meant it as advice to young men entering rooms full of rivals.

He had not understood until this evening that it was also a warning about the rooms you build yourself, out of other people's trust, and then have to live inside.

That night they moved. Four shadows and a raft-load of gear sliding up the north bank under a sky scoured of moon, Freya leading with a night-sense that bordered on the uncanny, Bee navigating by stars and stubbornness, Nils carrying the instruments like relics, Tomas silent in a way that had stopped looking like temperament and started looking like dread. Augustin took the rear, as befitted both his age and his guilt, and twice he turned and looked back down the dark valley they'd come through, and twice he saw — or believed he saw — lantern-light kindling far below, patient as interest accruing.

By the last hour before dawn they reached the lip of the gorge above the falls, where the river vanished under the mountain into the tunnel the maps refused to name. Freya called the halt in a whisper. The crew dropped packs and breathed.

Augustin stood apart, at the edge of the drop, and looked up-valley toward the source he had spent a fortune and a lifetime approaching, and felt nothing that a fortune should have bought. Only proximity. Only the terrible nearness of a thing you've wanted so long you no longer know what you'll do with it.

Behind him, softly, Bee said: "Mr. Vale? Why does the company want to arrest us? We haven't actually broken anything yet."

He turned. The girl was watching him with her head tilted, map-case hugged to her chest, and her eyes in the starlight were wide and clear and utterly without guile, and for one naked second Augustin Vale stood on the edge of the whole truth like a man on a high board.

"Not yet," he said. "Get some sleep, child. Tomorrow we go underground."

And far downstream, faint as a heartbeat, the lanterns kept coming.
