## 13. Bee's Map Breaks

Bee had learned to draw before she learned to trust anyone, and she had learned both the hard way — alone, at a kitchen table, with a stolen ruler and a father's old survey pencils that smelled of cedar and failure. Maps, she had discovered at thirteen, never lied. They simply waited, patient as stone, for someone to read them honestly. People lied. Charts didn't. A contour line had never once looked her in the eye and said *everything's fine, Bee, go back to sleep.*

Which was why, on the fourth morning above the falls, when her map stopped making sense, she felt the ground open under her in a way that had nothing to do with geology.

She was sitting on a granite shelf above the river with her board across her knees, the good board, the one she'd traded three months of dishwashing to buy, re-inking the morning's fixes. The Vane had narrowed overnight. That was the first wrongness. Rivers narrowed, sure, that was physics, but they narrowed *gently*, the way a sentence tapers to its end, and this one narrowed like a fist closing. On her chart the valley walls should have spread into the broad basin the concession surveys promised — the Upper Meadow, the maps called it, all gentle gradient and braided channels. Instead the canyon had pinched to a throat barely twenty meters across, walls going up and up into mist, and the river poured into that throat with a sound like a crowd deciding something.

She checked her bearings. Checked them again. The sun was where it should be. The aneroid read the elevation her contours predicted to within a few meters. And still the river was wrong — or her map was — because the Upper Meadow, the wide green room where Halvard's journal said they'd find the old portage trail, simply wasn't there.

"Bee." Freya's voice, from the rocks below. "Water's up. We moving?"

"Give me ten minutes."

"You said ten minutes an hour ago."

Bee didn't answer. Freya climbed up anyway, the way she always did, moving over wet granite like the rock had personally agreed to hold her, and crouched at the edge of the board. Bee felt the familiar prickle — the one that had been growing for days now, the small cold splinter of *she knows this country too well* — and pushed it down. There'd be time for that later. Right now there was a bigger problem, and the bigger problem was hers.

"Show me," Freya said.

Bee turned the board. "The survey says the valley opens here. Meadow, braided channels, easy walking for two days to the headwall. But it doesn't open. It closes. And the gradient—" she tapped the river's line, where her own careful hand had inked the fall of the water, "—the gradient's wrong. The river's dropping too fast for this reach. It's losing elevation like it's falling off a shelf, but there's no falls marked, no gorge, nothing. It's like the river is—" She stopped, because the word that came next was ridiculous, and she was already the youngest and the smallest and the one whose charts everyone smiled at.

"Like it's going somewhere the surface can't follow," Freya said quietly.

Bee looked up. "You've seen this before."

"I've read about it." Freya's face did the thing it did — the shutter coming down, the features arranging themselves into neutral. "Keep reading. What does your map say the water does?"

Bee traced the line down. Past the throat of the canyon, past where they stood, the river reappeared on the surveys a kilometer downstream, wider, calmer, fed and full. Between the two points, the maps drew the Vane as a confident blue thread with no interruption at all.

"It says it's continuous," Bee said. "It's not. Look at the volume going in there." She pointed at the throat, where the whole river bent itself through the gap and vanished into shadow with a roar that came up through the soles of her boots. "That's the whole Vane. And a kilometer down it comes back out bigger than it went in. There has to be—" She stopped again. Said it. "It goes underground."

Freya was silent for a long moment. Below them, Nils was crouched at the water's edge with his sample kit, and Tomas stood watch on the trail with the rifle slung wrong, the way he always slung it, like a man carrying a debt he didn't know how to hold. Mist moved through the canyon in slow rags. Somewhere upstream, a bird made a sound like tearing paper.

"Get the journal," Freya said at last. "Halvard's. Page — there's a sketch near the end. Folded page, water-stained. Get it."

* * *

They spread the journal on the flat rock with the map board beside it, and Bee felt the two documents like two people in a room who had once known each other and no longer spoke.

Her own charts were beautiful. She knew that without vanity; she'd earned the beauty, hour by hour, inking in the cold, re-drawing in the rain under a tarp, cross-checking every bearing against the sun and the stars and Nils's barometer. They were beautiful and they were useless, because they were built on the concession surveys — the company's maps, the company's truth — and the company, it turned out, had never once bothered to look into the throat of its own canyon. Why would it? The source was a legend to sell permits with. Nobody had surveyed what they'd already decided wasn't there.

Halvard's sketch was a different animal entirely. It was ugly. It was a single water-stained page, drawn in pencil gone grey with damp, lines wavering, annotations cramped sideways into every margin. It showed the throat of the canyon not as a gap but as a *lid* — a roof of stone over a void — and beneath the lid, in section, cut away like an anatomist's drawing, the river ran on: a tunnel, a flooded gallery, a long dark intestine of mountain with depths marked in Halvard's small, stubborn hand. He'd labeled it in the old surveyor's shorthand Bee had taught herself out of library books. *Siphon.* And beside it, underlined twice: *The river does not go around. The river goes through. So must we.*

"How far?" Freya asked Nils, when he'd climbed up to look.

Nils took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirt, put them back on — his ritual when the world had presented him with something he didn't want to see. He paced the throat's edge, threw a stick into the current, timed it, watched it vanish and did not come up. He came back looking older.

"Flow volume says the tunnel's mostly full. Halvard's depths — if they're right — there's an air gap for maybe two-thirds of the traverse. A hand-line traverse, in the dark, in moving water, over a drop that would—" He stopped. "It would be the hardest thing I've ever done, and I've done stupid things in flood stage."

"And if his depths are wrong?" Tomas, from the trail. His voice had an edge on it that hadn't been there at the first camp.

"Then the river eats us," Nils said simply. "All of it. Every one of us, in the dark, under a mountain, and nobody finds anything but our boots in the outflow."

The silence after that had weight. Bee looked from face to face and saw the thing she'd been half-dreading and half-hoping to see since the gorge began: doubt, arriving at last, aimed at the map. At *her* map. Because that was the unwritten law of the crew — Freya led, but Bee's charts were the truth they all walked on, and if the charts were wrong then the ground itself was a liar and there was nothing left to stand on.

"My charts aren't wrong," Bee said, and heard how young her voice came out. "They're *incomplete*. There's a difference. Nobody surveyed this reach because the company never—" 

"I know," Freya said. "I know they didn't."

And there it was again, that splinter. *How do you know what the company never did? How do you know the meadow isn't there before we've looked? How did you read that canyon wall yesterday like you'd read it a hundred times?* Bee kept her eyes on the journal and said nothing, and hated herself a little for the silence, and kept it anyway.

"The question," Freya said, "is whether we trust a dead man's pencil."

* * *

They argued it through the afternoon, and Bee, who had spent her whole short life learning that maps were the only adults who never lied, sat apart on her rock and felt the argument happen inside her own chest.

Because here was the thing nobody else seemed to grasp, the thing that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone: if Halvard's sketch was right, then her charts were not just incomplete — they were *built on a lie*. The company's surveys weren't sloppy. Sloppy was gaps, was guesswork, was honest blank space a mapmaker fills with dotted lines and a question mark. What the concession maps had was worse than a gap. They had a *confident blue thread* drawn straight across a thousand meters of underground river, a fiction rendered in the same sure hand as every true thing around it. Someone had sat at a desk and drawn a river that wasn't there, and signed it, and filed it, and the whole world downstream — the towns, the water rights, the gauging records Nils had gone grey over — had been built on top of that one smooth lie.

She had spent three years believing maps didn't lie. She had run away from home on the strength of that belief, had traded her father's world for this one, because in her father's world the maps were drawn by men who took money to make the water look clean, and she had thought — she had *needed* to think — that out here, past the edge of the concession, the lines went honest again.

And now the honest lines had led her to the throat of a canyon, and the only truth left was in a dead man's water-stained pencil.

She found Freya at dusk, at the canyon's edge, alone, looking down into the roar.

"You haven't decided," Bee said.

"I've decided. I just don't like it." Freya didn't look around. "We go through. Tomorrow, first light, before the melt raises the level. Halvard's route, his depths, his line anchors if any of his bolts are still in the rock after twenty years." A pause. "Your charts get us to the door, Bee. They've been right every day since the dock. But past that door, I'm navigating on a dead man's hand, and I want you to know what that costs me."

Bee sat down beside her. The mist came up cold off the water.

"Because you don't trust him," Bee said. "Halvard."

Freya was quiet long enough that Bee thought she wouldn't answer. Down in the throat, the river threw its sound against the walls like a prisoner who had stopped expecting anyone.

"I trusted him more than anyone alive," Freya said finally. "That's the problem. Trusting him is how I ended up—" She stopped. The shutter came down, but slower than usual, like it was heavy that evening. "A map is a promise. His map was the truest promise anyone ever made me, and it still — things went wrong, Bee. People went wrong. So when I look at that page, I'm not asking *is it accurate*. I'm asking *do I follow him one more time*, knowing where the last time left me." She turned her head, and in the last grey light her eyes were the color of the water. "You'd think the answer would be easy."

"It's not?"

"No." Almost a laugh, with no humor in it. "Because the map's still true. That's the rotten joke of it. He was true, the map was true, and it ruined us anyway. So tomorrow I follow a dead man through a mountain, and I do it knowing that being right and being safe are two different countries." She looked back down at the water. "Your charts got us here honest, Bee. Don't you dare apologize for the blank space. The blank space is the only honest thing the company ever drew."

Bee felt her eyes sting and blamed the mist.

* * *

They rigged the first anchor by headlamp before dawn, and Bee worked the rope with her hands and hated every minute of it, because her whole craft was the opposite of this — her craft was *seeing*, was standing above the country and knowing it whole, and here there was no above. Here the country was a throat, and they were going down it.

Freya went first. Of course Freya went first. She clipped into Halvard's line — the old surveyor had left bolts in the rock, two of them still true after twenty years, and Nils had wept a little, quietly, behind his glasses, at the sight of them — and she dropped over the lip of the tunnel entrance into the dark with the river's breath coming up cold around her, and Bee stood at the edge holding the spare coil and listened to the sound of her leader's breathing change as the light took her.

"Floor at three meters," Freya's voice floated up, flattened by stone. "Water to mid-shin, moving fast. Air gap's real. Halvard was right." A pause. "He's *still* right. Next anchor's forty meters on. Bee — your turn. Keep the board dry."

Bee looked back once, at the thin grey line of the canyon mouth behind her, at the honest daylight she was leaving, at Tomas's face, pale above his collar, and Nils checking his lamp a third and fourth time. Then she turned and followed the dead man's pencil down into the mountain, into the roar, into the country no chart had ever drawn.

The dark closed over her like a page turning.

Forty meters in, her lamp found the next anchor bolt — and beside it, cut into the rock by a patient hand twenty years ago, a single mark she'd seen before, on the water-stained page, in the margin, underlined twice.

Halvard's initials. And under them, fresh as the day they were cut, as if he'd been waiting:

*Keep going. It's not far now.*

Bee put her hand on the letters, cold and certain, and behind her, deep in the tunnel, she heard the river change its note — a deeper sound, a wider sound, the sound of the gallery opening into something vast and flooded and waiting — and she understood, with a cartographer's cold clarity, that Halvard's map had brought them to the one place her charts could never follow.

The siphon. And somewhere past it, the source. And somewhere behind them, faint but unmistakable even over the water, the sound of an engine — a boat motor, in the dead of the gorge, where no boat had any business being.
