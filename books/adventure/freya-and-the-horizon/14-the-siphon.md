## 14. The Siphon

Nils had spent eleven years of his life reading water, and in all that time he had never once heard it make this sound.

It was not the roar of rapids or the hush of a deep pool. It was something between breathing and swallowing — a vast, slow intake, as if the mountain itself were drawing breath through its teeth. He stood at the edge of the flooded gallery with his headlamp beam stretched out over black water and felt the sound in his sternum before he heard it properly in his ears, a low percussion that had nothing to do with anything flowing and everything to do with something *waiting*.

"Everyone stop talking," Freya said, and her voice was quiet enough that it should have been lost under the noise, but it carried anyway, the way certain voices do when they've been trained against exactly this kind of silence-breaking roar. "Listen to it for a minute. All of you. Before anyone touches anything."

They listened. Bee stood with her ruined charts rolled tight against her chest like a child clutching a blanket. Tomas had gone very still near the rear of their line, stiller than Nils had ever seen him, and Nils noticed because noticing people was the other half of what eleven years of solitary fieldwork taught you — you learn rivers, and then, if you're honest, you learn that rivers are easier than people, and you start learning both.

The gallery opened ahead of them into a chamber the size of a cathedral's nave, ceiling lost somewhere above the reach of their lamps, floor drowned beneath water so dark it seemed less like water than like a hole cut into the world. Along the left wall, a shelf of rock ran outward into the dark — sometimes wide as a walkway, sometimes narrowing to a hand's breadth, sometimes vanishing altogether where the flood had eaten through. Halvard's map, the copy Freya carried in its waterproof sleeve, showed the route as a single confident line drawn straight across the chamber and into a second tunnel mouth on the far side. Beneath it, in Halvard's cramped hand: *The Siphon. Air space varies with season. Go at low water. Do not stop.*

"Air space varies," Nils repeated aloud, mostly to himself.

"It means the roof comes down close to the water," Freya said. "In places. You'll be swimming sections with your face pressed against stone, breathing from whatever pocket of air the rock lets you keep. It also means—" she turned, and her headlamp caught each of their faces in turn, "—that I need to know right now, before we commit, whether anyone wants to turn back. Because halfway across, turning back stops being an option. There's no halfway back on a siphon. There's only through."

Nobody spoke. The river swallowed and breathed behind them.

"I'll go first," Nils said. "I'm the heaviest swimmer and the worst climber. If I can make the shelf, anyone can."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Something passed across her face — not surprise, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone recalibrating a person upward.

"All right," she said. "But you don't go first. I go first. Always. That's not ego, it's arithmetic — I'm the only one who's done this kind of thing, and if there's a bad section I need to find it before it finds any of you." She began unbuckling her pack straps, redistributing weight with quick, economical movements. "Bee, you're second. You're small, you're light, and your hands are the best on this crew — if I have to pass anything back along the shelf, it goes through you. Nils, third. Tomas, last."

Tomas flinched. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a half-inch drop of the shoulders — but Nils saw it, and he suspected Freya saw it too, and said nothing about it, which was its own kind of seeing.

"Tomas," Freya said, gentler now. "Last isn't punishment. Last is where I can watch you. Every guide worth her rope puts the person who needs watching where she can see them. You're last because I'm choosing to keep you closest. Understand?"

Tomas nodded. His voice, when it came, was thin. "Understood."

* * *

They stripped to base layers and sealed their drybags, everything they couldn't afford to lose lashed inside, everything they could afford to lose left in a pile on the stone — and looking at that pile, Nils felt the strange lightness and the strange grief of it all at once. Eleven years of instruments and notebooks, and here he was leaving most of them on a rock in the dark because the water would take them otherwise. He kept the gauge log. He kept one pencil. Everything else could drown.

Freya went over the rules twice, her voice flat and clear, a teacher's cadence. One hand on the line at all times — she'd rigged a light cord between pitons along the traversable shelf, and where the shelf vanished, the cord ran underwater and you followed it blind. Three kicks and a pull, three kicks and a pull, when swimming the submerged sections. If the air pocket closed early, you did not panic upward; you exhaled slowly and followed the cord, because the cord knew where the next pocket was even when you didn't. And above all: nobody stopped. Not for cramp, not for fear, not for anything. Stopping in a siphon was how siphons kept people.

"The water's cold enough to steal your hands in four minutes," she finished. "So we move like we mean it. Questions?"

"What's on the other side?" Bee asked.

Freya almost smiled. "Dry rock. A climb. And after that, I think, the hardest part of the whole river." She clipped herself to the cord. "Go time."

She dropped off the shelf into the water without a splash — a clean, practiced entry, like a coin slipping into a well — and was gone. Ten seconds. Twenty. Then her lamp bloomed faintly in the black ahead, a green-white star underwater, moving steadily toward the far tunnel mouth, and Nils understood she was checking the submerged route by feel before any of them touched it.

When she surfaced at the first air pocket — a gasp, a slap of palm on wet stone, a shouted *"Clear! First pocket good!"* — Bee went in after her, small and fierce, and Nils went in third.

The cold hit him like a verdict. Not pain exactly — pain would have been simpler — but a total, immediate claim on his body, every nerve rerouted to the single business of keeping his core alive. His hands went wooden around the cord. He swam the way she'd told them: three kicks and a pull, three kicks and a pull, face down, following the green star of her lamp through water that had no bottom and no edges, only the cord running taut and true through the dark like a thread through a needle's eye.

At the first air pocket he came up gasping into a space barely bigger than a coffin laid on its side, cheek against stone slick as tongue, and found Freya already hauling Bee up beside him, already counting them, already gone again before his breathing steadied. She moved through the siphon the way water moved through it — no hesitation anywhere, no negotiation, just passage.

*Do not stop,* Halvard had written. Freya had taken it as scripture.

* * *

It was on the third section — the longest swim, the one where the cord dove deep and the ceiling came down until Nils could feel the rock pressing overhead like a held breath — that things broke.

He came up into the fourth air pocket with his lungs burning and his fingers past feeling, and he counted heads out of habit. Freya, ahead, already at the lip of the next dive. Bee, beside him, shivering violently but game, jaw set. And behind them, in the black water between pockets—

Nothing.

"Tomas," Nils said. "Tomas!"

The word came back off the stone flattened and dead. Freya's head snapped around. For one full second she hung there in the pocket, listening, and Nils watched her face do something he'd never seen it do — watched the competence crack and show the fear underneath, raw and enormous, before she sealed it away again behind her teeth.

"He was right behind me," Bee whispered. "He was *right behind me*, I heard him kick—"

Then they heard it: a sound from the water behind, not a cry but worse — a rhythmic, splashing thrash, uncontrolled, the sound of a body fighting the water instead of working with it. Panic-swimming. The worst sound a siphon can give you, because panic in a siphon doesn't stay contained; it takes the panicking person and it takes whoever goes in after them, and the mountain does not care which it keeps.

Freya didn't hesitate. That was the thing Nils would carry out of that tunnel for the rest of his life, the thing he'd tell the story around a hundred fires and never once manage to make fully believable: she didn't hesitate. She took one breath, said "*Hold the line*" — meaning the cord, meaning Bee, meaning him, meaning *do not follow me* — and slid backward off the pocket's lip into the black, into the direction they had all come from, into the one direction every instinct in a human body screams against.

Going back. Into the siphon. Against the current of the whole expedition, against the current of the entire logic of survival, for a man she'd known six weeks.

Nils held the cord and held Bee and held his own terror down with both hands, and he counted. He was a scientist; counting was what he had. Thirty seconds. Forty-five. Bee was shaking so hard her teeth chattered audibly in the little stone room, and Nils pulled her against his chest and wrapped his arms around her and told her, in the calm voice he used for flood readings, that Freya Whitlock — he almost said the name, the real name, the name they'd all agreed not to say since the fire at Cold Camp — that *she* did not lose people. That he'd watched her read water for weeks now and she had never once been wrong about it.

A minute and ten seconds.

A minute and thirty.

And then the green star bloomed in the water below them, rising, and with it a second shape, limp, dragged by the collar, and Freya's free hand shot up out of the black water and seized the cord and *pulled*, arm over arm, hauling them both up into the pocket with a strength that seemed impossible for a body that size running on cold and will alone.

Tomas was conscious but barely. He'd frozen mid-swim — the ceiling coming down over his head had simply switched something off in him, he said later, in a voice like a man reporting his own drowning from a great distance — and he'd drifted off the cord, and the current had begun walking him back the way they'd come, deeper into the mountain's throat, and Freya had found him by touch in absolute darkness because she'd been counting his kicks behind her the way she counted everything.

She got him breathing. She got him angry — actually shook him, once, hard, and said, "You don't get to quit in my river," and Nils saw something change in Tomas's face when she said *my river*, saw the boy understand, maybe for the first time, that he wasn't cargo on this expedition, wasn't the patron's nephew being ferried toward some family reckoning. He was crew. Crew gets gone back for. Crew gets shaken and cursed and saved.

"Can you swim?" Freya asked him.

"Yes," Tomas said, and his voice had iron in it now, new-forged. "Yes. I can swim."

"Then we finish this. Nobody stops. Nobody freezes. We are forty meters from through, and we go *now*, while you're mad instead of scared, because mad swims better."

* * *

They went through.

The last two pockets came fast, the final swim short and merciful, and then the tunnel spat them out onto a gravel bank in a high dry chamber where the sound of the siphon fell away behind them like a door closing, and they lay on the stones gasping and laughing and, in Bee's case, crying without shame, and Nils lay on his back watching the headlamps swing across a ceiling furred with pale mineral bloom and thought: *this is what trust is made of.* Not the decision to trust. The going-back. The measured minute and thirty seconds in the dark, the arm that pulls, the voice that says *hold the line* and means it.

Freya crouched over Tomas, checking his hands, his lips, the blue at his fingernails, ordering layers back onto him, hot drink, movement, all of it brisk and unsentimental — and Tomas caught her wrist as she turned to go.

"You came back," he said. "Into that. For me."

Freya looked at him for a moment. "That's the job," she said.

But it wasn't, and everyone in the chamber knew it wasn't, and nobody said so, and the not-saying bound them tighter than any saying could have.

It was Bee, wrapped in her drybag like a moth in a cocoon, who broke the silence — pointing with a chattering hand at the far wall of the chamber, where the headlamp beams had found something none of them were prepared for.

Carved into the living rock, old and weathered and deliberate, were letters. Tall as a man. And beneath them, a date, and beneath the date, a single word that stopped Nils's heart where he knelt:

*Halvard.*

And below that, smaller, fresher, in handwriting Nils recognized from the journals they'd found at the falls camp — handwriting he had spent three nights cross-referencing against the falsified gauge reports — a final line, an instruction, an arrow pointing up and into the dark:

*The source keeps what I buried. Climb before the water rises.*

Behind them, far back through the flooded tunnel, faint but unmistakable, the engine sound was getting closer.
