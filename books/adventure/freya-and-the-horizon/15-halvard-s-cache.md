## 15. Halvard's Cache

The river above the falls was a different animal entirely.

Freya had known that from the map — Halvard's inked contour lines showed the Vane shedding its violence in one long white scream and then gathering itself, chastened, into something narrow and cold and quick. But knowing a thing from paper and standing in it were two different liturgies. The spray reached them before the sound did, a fine cold grit on the face, and then the roar arrived like weather, filling the gorge wall to wall until speech became a thing you did with your hands.

They had come up the last pitch in the grey hour before dawn, roped in pairs, Bee's chattering teeth audible even over the water. Now the sun was clearing the eastern rim of the valley and finding them one at a time: Nils first, hunched over his ruined boots; then Tomas, gray-faced and silent since the siphon, carrying more rope than he needed because carrying was easier than thinking; then Bee, who had not stopped looking at Freya since the tunnel, as if she were trying to memorize her from the inside out.

And there, on a shelf of granite forty feet above the boiling plunge pool, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging moss — a camp.

Freya saw it the way you see a face across a crowded room after ten years. A rusted tent frame, its canvas long since eaten to lace. A ring of stones with char still black in their hearts. And pinned under a flat stone, catching the light like a coin at the bottom of a well, a tin box the size of a hymnal.

Her hands stopped working before her mind caught up. She was halfway across the ledge before Nils called her name, before she remembered she was supposed to be the one who moved slowly, who checked every hold twice. The rock was wet and treacherous and she crossed it like it was the floor of her mother's kitchen.

*Halvard.*

She knew it was his before she touched anything. Guides leave signatures the way painters do — in the geometry of a fire ring, in the way a tent frame is lashed to survive wind from any quarter, in the small economy of a camp where nothing is placed that cannot be grabbed in the dark. She had learned all of it at his elbow, on the Kestrel glacier, when she was nineteen and hungry for the mountains the way other people were hungry for God. He had built this camp to last and then, apparently, walked away from it, or been made to walk away, or —

She knelt. Lifted the stone. Took the tin.

It was heavier than it looked. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: three journals, bound in waxed canvas, and beneath them a leather roll that clinked faintly when it shifted — instruments, brass and glass, a surveyor's theodolite no bigger than a fist. And folded into the lid of the tin, where only someone opening it would find it, a note in a hand she would have known blindfolded, in the dark, underwater:

*R. — If you're reading this, the tide finally told the truth. Go to the source. Everything is there. Everything I couldn't say. — H.*

*If you're reading this.* He had written it to her. Not to whoever came next, not to some future company man or curious tourist paying their admission at the Tollhouse. To *R.* Rosalind. The name she had buried in two countries and a hundred false ledgers. He had known, somehow, that she would be the one to find it — or he had hoped so hard it amounted to the same thing.

"Freya?" Bee's voice, close. "What is it?"

She hadn't heard the girl climb down. That was unlike her. That was what this place did — it unpicked the stitches she'd spent six years setting.

"Halvard's," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like sound over ice. "My teacher's."

Bee crouched beside her, dripping, eyes going wide at the journals. "He was here? At the source?"

"He was here." Freya turned the first journal over in her hands. The cover was swollen with damp, the spine cracked, but the oilcloth had held. He had always been meticulous about waterproofing. It was the first thing he'd ever taught her: *the mountain doesn't care about your gear, so your gear has to care about you.*

She opened it.

* * *

The early pages were ordinary and that ordinariness nearly undid her. Gauge readings. Weather notes. Sketches of the gorge in pencil so fine they looked etched — the falls from below, the siphon's mouth, the shelf where the camp stood, each one annotated in his cramped, patient hand. Dates from eleven years ago. She did the arithmetic without wanting to: eleven years ago she had been twenty-three, newly certified, already talking about the Vane expedition he kept promising her a place on. The expedition that never happened. The season he'd told her, over bad coffee in a station canteen, that the funding had fallen through, that these things happened, that there'd be other rivers.

There had been no other rivers. There had been Kestrel Pass instead, eight months later, and the world ending, and everything after.

She read on. The entries grew shorter, denser. His handwriting changed — tightened, pressed harder into the page, as though he were writing against something.

*May 14. Cross-checked the spring discharge against the concession's published figures. Discrepancy of 34%. Not seasonal variation. Not instrument error — I've run it four ways. Someone upstream is reporting numbers that don't exist.*

*May 22. Found the old intake gallery above the headwater. Sealed with concrete newer than the permit file claims. Someone built something here they don't want measured.*

*June 3. Wrote to the Commission. No reply. Wrote again. Acknowledgment only. I know how acknowledgments work. They are receipts for letters that will be forgotten.*

*June 19. Met with Vane's people today. They were very courteous. They offered me a consultancy. Generous. They asked what I intended to publish. I said the truth. They smiled the way men smile when they have already decided.*

Freya's breath went shallow. She was aware of the others gathering — Nils lowering himself carefully onto a boulder, Tomas standing too straight, Bee pressed against her shoulder, reading upside-down, lips moving. She should have stopped. Should have taken the journal somewhere private and rationed it out. But her hands wouldn't obey, and besides, some part of her understood that this could not be read alone. Some documents are load-bearing. You open them in company or you collapse under them by yourself.

*July 2. They have begun circulating a draft report with my name on it. My name. Figures I never took, conclusions I never drew. If it stands, the record will show that I certified the concession's water accounting. I have two weeks to sign it or be named a saboteur. I have decided what I am going to do. I am going to be faster than they are.*

*July 9. Copies made. Originals sealed. If anything happens to me, the originals go to the source — the one place their permits make sacred ground. They will never search the thing they are paid to protect. Irony is the last honest emotion left in this valley.*

*July 11. Last entry. I taught a girl once who would have known what to do with this. She'd have burned their whole ledger down and made tea while it burned. If she ever finds this: Rosalind, I'm sorry about the coffee. I'm sorry about the pass. None of it was yours. Take the originals. Clear your own name first — I mean that, stubborn girl, clear YOURS first — and let the rest fall where it falls.*

The gorge roared. The sun climbed. Freya sat on wet granite above a waterfall with a dead man's handwriting swimming in front of her eyes, and for a long moment she could not have said which century she was in.

*None of it was yours.*

She had carried Kestrel Pass for six years like a stone sewn into her chest. The collapsed cornice, the four names, the inquiry, the report — *the report*, signed by Halvard, the one that had ended her career in two countries with a single bureaucratic stroke, the one whose signature she had seen again just days ago in the drowned gauge station, matching the falsified river data, and thought: *he did this to me, he was part of it, my teacher sold the same lie twice.*

He hadn't. He had discovered the lie. And they had used his name — coerced it, forged it, bought it, she might never know which — to bury him and her in the same grave. The falsified gauge readings, the Kestrel findings, the whole architecture of her ruin: it wasn't his betrayal. It was theirs, wearing his hand like a glove.

Vindication, she had imagined, would feel like setting down weight. This felt like being cut. Six years of exile, of dock work under a borrowed name, of flinching whenever anyone said *Whitlock* within earshot — and it had all rested on a document he had never willingly touched. She had hated a ghost for a crime committed against him. She had built her whole hidden life on a foundation of someone else's forgery.

A sound escaped her that she didn't authorize. Bee's arm came around her shoulders, thin and fierce, and Freya let it, because she was too empty to pretend otherwise.

* * *

Nils read the middle journal for an hour without speaking, cross-referencing against the readings they'd found at the drowned gauge, muttering figures like prayers. When he finally looked up, his eyes were wet and furious in equal measure.

"This is it," he said. "This is the exhumation. The thirty-four percent discrepancy — it matches what I found at the station, almost exactly. He measured it first. Eleven years before I did." He tapped the page with a trembling finger. "Do you understand? The official record says the upper Vane yields X. Halvard proved it yields far less — that the company has been over-allocating water rights down-valley based on fiction. Every permit, every concession payment, every family drawing concession wages — it all sits on top of a number he proved was a lie."

"And the originals?" Tomas asked quietly. He had not sat down. He had been standing at the edge of the shelf looking at the falls for a long time, and when he turned, his face had the look of a man arriving late to a funeral he'd helped arrange.

"The source," Freya said. Her voice had come back, low and level, the guide's voice, the one that worked even when nothing else did. "He sealed them at the source. Behind whatever the intake gallery was hiding." She closed the journal. "Two days' travel. Maybe less, if the weather holds."

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. Whatever war was happening behind his face, it was losing on both fronts.

"We should talk about what this means," Nils said. "For the towns. For the permits. For—"

"For her," Bee said simply, and nodded at Freya. "It means for *her*. It clears her. Doesn't it? The Kestrel report — if his real findings contradict it, if they forged his signature once, they could have—" She stopped, doing the math aloud with her whole body. "You're not disgraced. You never were. Somebody *made* you disgraced."

The words hung in the spray-light. Freya looked out at the falls, at the white column of water falling forever and going nowhere, and felt the strangest sensation: not joy, not relief, but a kind of terrible homesickness — grief for the six years that a piece of paper had stolen, rage at men who were probably dead or comfortably retired, and underneath it all, rising like groundwater, something dangerously close to hope.

"Yes," she said. "It clears me."

"Then we get it," Bee said. "Tomorrow. Tonight. Now. We get it and we take it down the mountain and we nail it to every door in the valley."

"It's not that simple," Tomas said.

Everyone looked at him. He had gone pale in a new way — not the siphon's fear but something older, something he'd been carrying since before the Tollhouse ever drained.

"Tomas," Freya said, and her voice was gentle in a way that made him flinch harder than shouting would have. "What do you know?"

He looked at the journal in her hands. At the falls. At his uncle's name, unspoken, enormous, sitting in the air between them like weather coming.

"I know why my uncle really funded this expedition," he said. "And it isn't wonder."

The roar of the falls filled the silence he left behind, and Freya, holding her mentor's last words against her chest, understood that the mountain had one more toll to collect — and that this time, it would be taken from all of them.
