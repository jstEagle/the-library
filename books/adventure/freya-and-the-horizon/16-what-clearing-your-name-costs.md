## 16. What Clearing Your Name Costs

Tomas Vale had spent his whole life learning how men lied with their hands, and so he watched them now — his uncle's fingers drumming the table at Cold Camp, Nils turning the steel case over and over as if the truth might leak out through a seam, Bee sitting too still, the way she did when she was afraid and didn't want anyone to know it. And Freya, standing apart, her back to the fire, looking at Halvard's journals spread across a flat stone like an autopsy.

The cache had given them everything. That was the cruelty of it. Three days above the falls, in a hollow beneath an overhang that the river had been carving for ten thousand years, they had found Halvard Ostrand's final camp: the tent frame rotted to lace, the tin plates furred with lichen, and inside a dry-bag sealed with wax, the original surveys of the River Vane — the true ones, the ones that showed the headwater gauges had been falsified, the flow understated by a third so the concession company could claim scarcity, justify diversion rights, sell water it had never owned. Beside them, the journals. Forty years of a man's handwriting, growing smaller and angrier toward the end, until the last entry, dated eleven years ago:

*They will call me the forger. Let them. The paper is safe where only the river can reach it. If Rosalind ever reads this, tell her I never once doubted her.*

Tomas had read that line over Freya's shoulder and felt something crack quietly in his chest, because he knew — he alone among them fully knew — what had come next. His uncle's money. His uncle's lawyers. His uncle's name on the retainer that had buried Halvard Ostrand under an avalanche of fabricated evidence and buried Rosalind Whitlock under the rubble that slid off him.

He had confessed all of it four nights ago, kneeling in the dirt like a penitent, and Freya had not forgiven him, exactly, but she had kept him on the rope, which in her country was the same thing.

Now the steel case sat open on the flat stone, and everyone was doing arithmetic with other people's lives.

"The original surveys," Nils said, for perhaps the sixth time, as though saying it slowly enough would make the implications arrange themselves. "Signed, witnessed, stamped. This isn't a copy of a copy. This is the document the falsified record was built to replace. With this, the concession's entire water-rights portfolio is void. Every permit since the second year." He looked up, and his eyes behind the cracked spectacles were bright with a joy that made Tomas's stomach turn. "Do you understand? They stole a river. And here is the receipt."

"Who did they steal it *from*?" Bee asked.

It was the first thing she'd said in an hour, and it landed in the camp like a dropped knife.

Nils blinked at her. "From — from the public trust. From the valley."

"From the valley," Bee repeated. She pulled her knees up to her chest. She was sixteen and had the sharpest eye for terrain of anyone Tomas had ever traveled with, and right now she was using it on the people around the fire. "My mother works the intake station at Lower Mill. My aunt runs the pump house at Corren. Half the town of Ashford draws its wages from the concession — the maintenance crews, the rangers, the seasonal fishery contracts. When the permits void, the company pulls out. That's not justice, that's just a different kind of empty." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You're talking about burning down the only economy my family has ever known. To fix something that happened before I was born."

"Bee—" Nils began.

"No. You don't get to look wounded. You've been reading those gauges all week like they were scripture." She turned to Freya, whose back was still to them, whose shoulders had gone very straight. "Freya. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's a version of this where my mother keeps her job."

Freya didn't answer immediately. The fire popped. Somewhere below them the river ran loud and constant, the sound it made when it thought no one was listening, and Tomas thought: *that's the sound of everything we're arguing about.* A river doesn't care who owns it. Only people do, and people have mothers, and mothers have jobs, and jobs are the thin ice every family in the valley skates across from one season to the next.

"There is a version," Freya said finally, without turning, "where the truth comes out slow. Where the towns get ahead of it. Where the company settles instead of collapses."

"And a version where it doesn't," Bee said. "And you'd be gambling with our town either way, because you need this. Because it's *your name* in that case."

The silence after that was the worst Tomas had heard since Kestrel Pass entered his vocabulary. Freya turned then, and her face in the firelight was the face of a woman holding something heavy at arm's length, deciding whether to set it down or throw it.

"I'm going to walk," she said, and took the journal, and went up into the rocks.

* * *

Tomas found her twenty minutes later, or maybe she let herself be found; with Freya it was impossible to tell the difference, and he had learned not to flatter himself about it. She sat on a shelf of granite above the camp, the journal closed on her knee, watching the last light go copper on the snowfields across the valley. Below, the river vanished into the mountain's throat — tomorrow's problem, the siphon behind them now, the source less than a day upstream. Less than two days until the permits expired and the law came up this valley like a tide.

He sat down at the edge of her silence, not inside it.

"You should say what you came to say," she said.

"How do you know I came to say anything?"

"Because you sit like your uncle when you want something. Hands quiet. Breathing measured. Augustin Vale rehearsing."

That stung more than it should have, mostly because it was accurate. Tomas looked at his hands, which were indeed quiet, and hated them a little. "Bee's right," he said. "Partly."

"Don't stop there. Cowards always stop there."

"She's right that it's not clean. It's not a ledger where the good number wins. There are four hundred people in Ashford. Maybe six hundred counting Corren and Lower Mill. Rangers, mechanics, dock crews, the school that exists because the company pays its taxes. You burn the truth into the public record and the company doesn't just lose the permits — they lose the reason to be there, and they leave, and they leave *fast*, because companies don't grieve. And the towns don't have anywhere to go. Bee's mother is fifty-three years old with hands ruined from valve work. What does she do when the intake station goes dark?"

Freya said nothing. The light was dying out of the snow. He pressed on, because he had come here to press on, because he had been composing this argument in his head since the cache, polishing it like a stone, telling himself it was conscience when some cold honest part of him knew it was also fear — fear of what the documents would do, yes, but also fear of what they'd already done to the shape of the person sitting beside him.

"And there's another arithmetic nobody wants to do," he said. "Yours. Eleven years, Freya. Eleven years you've carried a name that wasn't yours and a crime that wasn't either. That case clears you. Not symbolically — *legally*. Whitlock walks free. You could guide again. Anywhere. You could stop being a ghost." He made himself look at her. "I watched you repair my uncle's rope that first day. I've watched you read water like other people read letters. The world took that from you and put it in a box, and the box is sitting on a rock forty meters below us, and you're telling me the cost of opening it is too high?"

"It is if the cost is paid by someone else," Freya said.

"But that's the point — it wouldn't be! It'd be paid by the company—"

"By the *company*." She laughed, short and airless, nothing in it. "Tomas. Your uncle funded the original survey. Who do you think holds the concession? Who do you think employs the rangers, signs the fishery contracts, pays the taxes that keep the school lit? It's not a monolith called The Company. It's a web, and every strand ends in a kitchen table. Some of those tables belong to people who knew. Some belong to people who suspected and looked away. Most belong to people who never knew anything except that the pay was steady and the work was honest and you don't ask questions about water." She turned to face him fully, and her eyes in the dusk were the gray-green of the river in shadow. "Halvard taught me that. Last lesson he ever gave me, on a ridge not so different from this one. He said: *before you pull a thread, find out what it's holding up.*"

"So we do nothing? We let them keep the lie? We let *him*—" Tomas heard his own voice crack and hated that too. "We let my uncle win. Again. He buried Halvard. He buried you. He funded this whole expedition to make sure the truth stays underground, and you're quoting his victim at me about threads?"

"He didn't fund this expedition to bury the truth."

"What?"

Freya's jaw tightened, and Tomas saw — with the strange clarity that comes in the last blue light before dark — that she had said more than she meant to. "Never mind."

"No. Say it. Say it right now."

She stood. And it happened fast, the way weather happens fast in high country: one moment stillness, the next the front moving through. "You want to talk about cowardice? Fine. Let's talk about it plainly, since you brought the word into camp wearing Bee's face." Her voice rose, carrying down the rock, and Tomas flinched. "You sat on this for four days. Four days you've known what your uncle wanted from this river, and you told us in whispers, at night, on your knees, so it would cost you less. And now the documents are real and suddenly you've discovered mercy — mercy for six hundred strangers you've never met, mercy for a company that destroyed two lives you know *by name* — and you dress it up as conscience. You stand there doing arithmetic with other people's suffering because doing it with your own would mean admitting what you actually want."

"What do I actually want, Freya? Tell me."

"You want your uncle to stay your uncle!" she said. "You want to love him and be clean, and you can't, and rather than carry that you'd hand the whole valley's truth to the river and call it kindness." She was breathing hard now, the journal gripped in one hand, knuckles white. "Bee gets to be afraid. Fear is honest. But you — you've made fear wear virtue's clothes, and I have watched men do that my whole life, and I am *done* being polite about it."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, savagely. "None of this is fair. That's what I've been trying to tell everyone since the cave. Fairness is a luxury of people whose names are intact." She pushed past him, boots scraping granite, heading down toward the camp with the journal still in her fist, and threw the last words over her shoulder like a dropped tool:

"Coward."

Then she was gone, down into the dark, and Tomas sat alone on the cold rock with the word ringing in his ears and the terrible discovery settling over him, slow as snowfall, that the worst part wasn't that she'd said it.

The worst part was that some chambered, hidden place in him had known it was true for four days, and had been waiting for someone brave enough to say it out loud.

Below him the fire was small now, and the figures around it small inside their shadows: Nils hunched over the steel case, Bee with her arms wrapped around her knees, and his uncle's brother-in-law-to-nothing, his patron, his blood — Augustin, apart as always, feeding the fire stick by stick, watching the flames with the expression of a man reading a letter he intends to burn.

Tomas got up. His legs were unsteady. He had maybe an hour before Freya reached the case, before the choice stopped being an argument and became a fact, and he understood suddenly — with the same clarity, the same awful gift — that whatever he did in that hour would decide not just what happened to the documents.

It would decide whether he ever got to stop being his uncle's nephew, and become his own man, named by his own choices.

He started down the rock.
