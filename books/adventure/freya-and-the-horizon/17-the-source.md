## 17. The Source

The mountain did not announce itself. That was the first thing Rosalind noticed as they climbed the last switchbacks in the blue hour before dawn — no fanfare of peaks, no white crowns catching the first light the way the postcard mountains did. The Vane's source hid inside a shoulder of grey limestone that looked, from below, like every other shoulder of grey limestone in this range: anonymous, patient, indifferent to the paper war being fought over its heart.

She had been awake for two hours when Bee shook her shoulder. Not because she'd slept badly — she'd slept better than she had any right to, curled against her pack with Nils's spare tarp pulled over them both while the wind worried at the tent fly — but because some old animal part of her had known what day it was and refused to let her body forget it.

Dawn would end the permits. Dawn was less than an hour away.

"Freya." Bee's voice was careful, the way it always was before something mattered. "Tomas says the light's coming."

Rosalind sat up. The camp was already half struck; Nils moved through the dark with the economy of a man who had spent thirty years reading water and had learned, somewhere along the way, to read people too — which meant he'd learned to know when not to talk. Tomas stood at the edge of the fire ring, feeding the coals the last of their fuel wood, his face lit orange from below so that he looked like a man standing inside a small, dying sun.

"Four hundred vertical meters," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, guide-voice, the voice she'd built like a wall around herself over six years. "Maybe five. We move fast and we don't stop. If we're not at the spring by first light, none of the rest of it matters."

Nobody asked what *the rest of it* meant. They'd been not asking for three days now, since Halvard's cache, since the journals, since the steel case entered their lives like a stone dropped into still water whose ripples hadn't finished spreading. She loved them for the silence. She was also afraid of it, because silences like that don't hold forever, and she could feel the pressure building behind Tomas's jaw every time he looked at her, the argument he was still carrying even after she'd called him a coward and walked into the trees and stood there until her hands stopped shaking.

They climbed.

The trail — if you could call it that; Halvard's map called it a route, and Halvard had been generous — went up through scree that shifted underfoot like a living thing deciding whether to keep them or shed them. Rosalind led, as she had led for five weeks, reading the slope by feel and by the faint grammar of lichen on stone, choosing each foothold the way you choose words in a language you dream in. Behind her came the others, strung out in the order they'd settled into without ever discussing it: Nils second, steady as ballast; Bee third, light-footed, chattering to herself the way she did when she was scared and didn't want anyone to know; Tomas last, carrying more weight than his share of the gear and all of it invisible.

The sky behind them went from black to iron to the color of a bruise healing. Somewhere below, in the valley towns where the concession men were waking up to telephones and ledgers, lawyers were opening files marked PERMIT 7-C, EXPIRY, and somewhere above them — here, now, under her boots — was the thing all that paper pointed at and none of it could touch.

At the top of the scree the route bent into a cleft in the rock, a slot barely wide enough for a pack, and the air changed. Rosalind felt it before she understood it: cooler, wetter, moving against the general drift of the morning like a current running the wrong way. Water breathing. She stopped and put her palm flat against the limestone and felt the mountain's pulse come up through the stone, faint and ceaseless, the way you feel a train coming through a rail laid on the ground.

"There," she said.

Bee made a sound that wasn't quite a word. Nils said nothing but took off his hat, which from Nils was a speech.

The cleft opened inward, and the darkness inside was not empty. It was full of sound — a deep, layered hush, like a cathedral after the congregation has gone, if cathedrals breathed. And threaded through the hush, growing louder with every step, was the voice of the river being born.

* * *

Halvard's map had described the final chamber in four lines of his cramped, slanted hand, and three of those lines had been measurements. The fourth said only: *You will understand why I never wrote more.*

He'd been right. There were no words for it that weren't smaller than it.

The chamber opened around them like the inside of a held breath. The walls rose beyond the reach of their headlamps, slick and ribbed with flowstone, banded in whites and rusts and the pale jade of mineral-rich seep, so that the whole space seemed veined like a leaf, like a wing, like something organic rather than geological — as though the mountain had grown this room instead of merely containing it. And at its center, rising from a floor of pale sand so fine it held their footprints like snow, the spring.

It did not gush. It did not roar. It simply *was*: a pool perhaps ten meters across, perfectly round, so clear that the eye kept trying to find its bottom and failing, not because of depth but because there seemed to be no bottom at all — only blue going down into bluer, an axis of color drilled straight through the world. From its center rose the water itself, upwelling in slow, silent columns visible only as shimmer, as distortion, as the suggestion of movement too vast to be hurried. Where the pool overflowed its rim, the River Vane began — a ribbon of water no wider than a stride, sliding away across the sand and into a crack in the far wall, beginning the journey they had followed upstream for five weeks, backward through every rapid and gorge and drowned tunnel, to this: the smallest, stillest, most enormous water any of them had ever seen.

Nils knelt at the rim and did not touch it. His hands hovered over the surface, trembling slightly, and Rosalind watched thirty years of hydrology collapse into something simpler and older — a man greeting the source of everything he'd spent his life studying downstream, the way you might finally meet the mother of someone you'd loved.

"It doesn't match," he whispered. "The gauges downstream, the seasonal curves — I've been telling myself for weeks there had to be a storage aquifer, a karst reservoir, some buffering system. Look at it. It just *is*. All my models—" He laughed, a cracked, wondering sound. "All my beautiful models, and the answer is that the river is simply honest and the paperwork isn't."

"The paperwork," Bee said quietly, "is right there."

Rosalind followed her gaze.

On a natural shelf of flowstone at the pool's edge, exactly where Halvard's journal had promised, sat the case.

Steel. Grey-green with age, furred at the hinges with mineral bloom, but intact — sealed, latched, weatherproofed by a man who had known he might not live to open it himself. Her mentor's initials were etched into the lid in letters she knew better than her own handwriting: **E.H.V.** Eirik Halvard Voss. The man who taught her to read water. The man who had walked into these mountains eleven years ago carrying the truth about the Kestrel Pass report and the falsified surveys and the concession's poisoned ledger, and who had cached that truth here, at the source, where the company's permits — the very instrument of the lie — would one day expire and leave it unguarded.

Where *she* would one day find it, if she was the one who found it at all. He had believed she would. He had written as much, in the journal they'd found at his last camp, in a passage Tomas had read aloud two nights ago while the fire burned low and nobody spoke: *If Rosalind reads this, then the tide gave back what it owed. Tell her the pass was not her fault. Tell her the map was always hers.*

Her name. Her real name, written in his hand, waiting in the dark for six years.

The others had drawn back. She realized it gradually — the soft scrape of boots on sand, the widening circle, the way headlamps swung politely elsewhere until only hers illuminated the shelf and the case upon it. They had formed, without discussion, a kind of honor guard of absence, giving her the one thing she had not had since Kestrel Pass: room to be alone with the truth.

Except she wasn't alone. That was the thing. That was the whole terrible, miraculous thing.

She crossed the sand and stood before the case, and the spring breathed beside her, and the minutes began to fall away like water off a ledge.

* * *

Forty minutes to dawn. Maybe fifty. She had learned to read light the way other people read clocks, and the grey seeping down the entrance cleft told her the sun was still below the eastern ridgeline but gathering itself, and somewhere in a company office a clerk was sharpening a pencil beside a file that would, at 6:14 or 6:15 or whenever the sun formally cleared the horizon, transform this chamber from a place they were permitted to be into a crime scene with them in it.

She thought about the file. She thought about what was in it — she'd seen enough of the concession's paperwork during the trial, all those years ago, to know how the machine worked. Permit 7-C covered the upper valley: the source, the gauging stations, the headwater corridor. When it expired, the renewal process would take, by the company's own published schedule, eighteen months of environmental review — review conducted, naturally, by consultants the company selected. Twenty years of legal seal, Augustin had called it, and Augustin had bought enough mountains in his life to know how seals worked. After dawn, no one would stand here legally again until Rosalind Whitlock was an old woman, if she lived to be one, if she was ever Rosalind Whitlock anywhere but inside her own skull.

And the case held the original surveys. Halvard's field copies. The true readings from the gauging station at the drowned gauge, next to the falsified ones, in the same ink, dated the same week, signed by the same men — proof, physical and undeniable, that the Kestrel Pass report had been doctored, that the flood that killed nine people including the man whose name she'd buried had been predicted and the prediction erased, that the disaster she'd been blamed for had been manufactured in an office by men who had never once been cold.

Proof that would clear her name. Restore her certifications. Open the borders of two countries that had closed to her. Give her back the life she'd had before, the name she'd been born with, the right to stand in a courtroom or a hiring office or her mother's kitchen and say *I told the truth* and have the world believe her.

And proof that would do something else, too. Something she'd been circling for three days the way a hawk circles a slope, unable to land.

Because the original surveys named names. Not just the engineers who'd falsified the readings — the men at the top, the concession directors, the ones who'd ordered it. And beneath them, woven through every page like thread through cloth, the valley towns. The mill leases. The water rights. The pension funds of three hundred families who lived off the concession's wages, whose town councils had signed the easements, whose grandfathers had sold the grazing land, whose children — one of whom was standing twenty feet behind her right now, wrapped in a coat two sizes too big, watching her with eyes too old for her face — grew up eating bread bought with the concession's money.

Bee's family. Bee's town. Bee's father, who drove a maintenance truck for the company and had a bad knee and a mortgage and a daughter who'd run away to follow a stranger up a river.

That was the arithmetic Halvard had never solved either. She'd read his last entries twice now, looking for an answer, and found instead a man writing in circles around the same wound: *Truth is a flood. You cannot release it partially. Ask the valley what a flood does.* And then, later, in a hand gone shakier: *Perhaps that is why they sealed the source. Not to hide the lie. To hide the choice.*

She reached out and touched the case. The steel was cold enough to burn, the way metal is cold in caves, drawing heat from her skin with the patience of geology. Her fingers left prints in the mineral dust. Underneath the dust, the latch. Underneath the latch, the documents. Underneath the documents, her whole life, waiting to be handed back to her like a coat checked at a door six years ago.

Behind her, no one spoke. But she could feel them — feel Nils's steady presence like ballast, feel Bee's breath going quick and shallow, feel Tomas standing apart from the others the way he'd stood apart since Cold Camp, carrying his uncle's secret and his own, waiting to see what kind of person she decided to be in the next forty minutes, because whatever she chose would tell them all who they had followed up this river, and who they had become by following her.

The spring paid none of them any attention. That was the other thing she understood, standing there, the understanding arriving the way the cold arrived, gradually and then completely. The water rising in the center of the pool had been doing this for ten thousand years. It had risen through ice ages and warm centuries, through the arrival of every people who ever named it, through the surveyors and the liars and the lawyers and the girl from the coast who'd once believed a river could be owned by a filing cabinet. It would go on rising after dawn, after the permits, after all of them. It owed the paper nothing. It owed the truth nothing. It simply rose, and overflowed, and began.

Everything human that happened in this room tonight happened on the sand at its edge, among creatures who had maybe sixty years apiece and spent them fighting over documents.

Her hand rested on the latch.

She thought of Halvard's handwriting. *Tell her the pass was not her fault.* She thought of her mother's kitchen, the phone call she'd never made, the name she'd set down like a heavy pack at the side of a trail six years ago because carrying it any farther would have killed her. She thought of the Tollhouse cave, the first night, the tide draining away to show her a door she hadn't known was there — the past surfacing, literally, demanding admission.

She thought of Bee, sixteen years old, who had redrawn every chart on this expedition by lamplight and never once asked what Freya's real name was, because it hadn't mattered, because what mattered was whether the rope held and the route was true and the person on the other end of it would come back for you.

The rope holds. The route is true. She comes back for you.

That was the record. That was the certification. No office issued it. No expiry date applied.

But her hand stayed on the latch, because knowing a thing and choosing it are separated by a distance wider than any gorge, and the sun was coming, and the steel was cold, and somewhere behind her Tomas took a breath as if to speak—

The light in the entrance cleft changed. Not dawn-grey. Lantern-yellow, swinging, and with it, faint but unmistakable, carried up the last of the route they'd climbed: voices. More than one. Moving fast.

The permits had seconds left, and the company had not waited for the paperwork.
