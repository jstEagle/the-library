## 18. Fire on the Water

Bee had drawn maps of things that did not exist. Coastlines invented for a school project when she was eleven, a fantasy kingdom with rivers that bent like question marks, and later — after she ran — charts copied from memory into the margins of other people's books, because paper was scarce and her head was not. She had always believed that a map was a promise: that what was written down would hold its shape, that ink was a kind of loyalty.

She had never watched anyone burn one before.

The spring came up out of the mountain like something breathing. That was the only way Bee could think to describe it afterward, and she thought about it a great deal in the days that followed, because describing things was what she did, it was how she kept herself from coming apart. The water rose through a seam in the rock floor of the cavern, pale as skimmed milk where the lamplight caught it, and spread outward in slow, glassy sheets until it found the channel that would become, miles downstream, the River Vane. It made almost no sound. That was the strangest part. A river's birth should have been loud. Instead it was quiet as a secret being told to someone who already knew.

The steel case sat on a shelf of stone at the water's edge, exactly where Halvard's journal said it would be, furred with mineral bloom, its latch crusted white. Freya knelt beside it and did not touch it for a long time.

The rest of them waited at the mouth of the passage. Nobody had said they should wait there; it had simply happened, the way a crew learns to read each other without words after weeks of shared cold and shared fear. Nils stood with his arms folded, his gauges forgotten in his pack, his face doing the thing it did when he was trying very hard to be a scientist about something that was not science. Tomas leaned against the wall with his jaw set, watching Freya's back the way you watch weather. And Bee sat down on the cold stone with her knees drawn up, her broken chart case open beside her, and drew.

That was her instinct, always. When the world got too large or too true, she drew it smaller. She sketched the cavern in quick graphite strokes — the breathing spring, the shelf, the kneeling figure — and then stopped, because she realized she was drawing the moment before something happened, and she did not know yet what the something was, and a map of an unknown country felt like a betrayal of the whole discipline.

"Freya," Nils said quietly. "Whatever you decide—"

"I know." Her voice came back off the wet stone, flattened of everything but itself. "I know what it costs. All of you do. That's why I'm deciding alone."

"It shouldn't be," Tomas said. "Alone."

"Yes," she said. "It should. That's the whole point of a name, Tomas. You carry it yourself or it isn't yours."

* * *

Later — much later, in the version of this night that Bee would tell and retell around a hundred fires — she would try to explain what it was like to watch Freya Whitlock open the case. But every telling failed in the same place, so she learned to leave out the middle and start with the fire.

Here is what actually happened, as close as Bee can get it:

Freya worked the latch free with her knife point, patiently, the way she did everything — no wasted motion, no hurry, as though the mountain had all the time in the world and she were merely borrowing some of it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, were the original survey folios. Bee saw the edges of them, the surveyor's grid faintly visible through the wrapping, the stamps of the concession company along the borders like brands on cattle. She saw the second bundle beneath them, thinner, bound in leather she recognized because she had been carrying its twin for six weeks: Halvard's field journal, the real one, the final one, the one whose existence the journal in Freya's pack had promised like a hand pointing at a hand.

Freya lifted the folios out. Held them against the light.

And Bee understood, in the way you understand avalanches — completely, and far too late to matter — what those papers were. Not just the corrected surveys. Not just proof that the river data had been falsified, that the Kestrel Pass report had been built on numbers a man had been paid to invent, that the disaster which killed nine people and erased Rosalind Whitlock's name had been, at root, an arithmetic crime. Those pages were a key. They opened the blacklist. They opened the courts. They opened every door that had been shut in Freya's face for seven years, and behind one of those doors was a life Bee had only glimpsed in fragments — the famous guide, the woman with a reputation instead of an alias, the person who existed before she became Freya.

They also opened the valley. Every town downstream. Millbrook, where Bee's mother still lived, where half the wages came from the concession company's offices and the other half from the tourists the concession brought. Where the falsified numbers had never mattered to anyone's supper table, and where the truth of them would land like a rockslide through a village square.

Tomas had argued for burning them. Freya had called him a coward and walked away from the fire, and Bee had lain awake half the night listening to the argument replay in her own head, unable to decide who was right, hating both of them a little for making her choose.

Now nobody had to choose. Now it was only Freya, kneeling at the edge of the source of the River Vane, holding her own exoneration in two hands.

"Bee." Freya didn't turn around. "Come here a minute."

Bee went. Her legs felt borrowed.

"You're the one who keeps records," Freya said. "So look. I want somebody to see it before it's gone. Not for evidence. Just — witnessed."

Bee looked. Up close the folios were beautiful, in the way old working documents are beautiful: dense, exact, annotated in three colors of ink, Halvard's handwriting marching across the margins in furious, patient corrections. *Recheck. This is wrong. They knew. Recheck.* Page after page. A man arguing with a lie for months, alone, in country like this, knowing what the lie would cost and writing it down anyway because writing it down was the only weapon he had left.

"He wasn't careful enough," Bee whispered. "He should have hidden copies."

"He did hide copies." Freya's voice was very even. "This is the copy. And careful men die in rivers, and careless companies live forever, unless—" She stopped. Bee watched her jaw work. "Unless somebody decides otherwise."

"Freya." Bee's throat hurt. "Your name. We could fix your name."

"My name isn't in trouble, Bee." For the first time she turned, and her eyes in the lamplight were the gray-green of deep water over stone. "My name is fine. It's *Rosalind* that's in trouble. And Rosalind—" she looked down at the folios, and something crossed her face that Bee had no map for, grief and relief tangled together so tightly they couldn't be told apart — "Rosalind died at Kestrel Pass anyway. Everyone says so. Maybe it's time everyone was right."

"That's not fair."

"No," Freya agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing here is. That's rather the theme."

She stood. She carried the folios to the spring itself — not the shelf, not the dry stone, but the water, the actual source, as if jurisdiction mattered, as if the river should be the one to take them. She crouched. From her pocket she took the tin of matches Tomas carried and had argued over and finally surrendered at Cold Camp, and Bee understood that Freya had taken them hours ago, that she had decided long before any of them reached this room, and that the argument in camp had been theater, or grief, or both.

"Wait," Bee said. "Please. Just — wait."

Freya waited.

Bee didn't have anything. That was the terrible thing. She had spent her whole life believing that words and drawings were the answer to everything, that if you could just chart a thing precisely enough it could be saved, and now the most important document she would ever stand in front of was about to become smoke and she had no argument, only a feeling, huge and wordless, standing in her chest with its hands out.

"Someone should copy it first," she managed. "I could copy it. Give me a day. Two days. I'm fast, you've seen me work—"

"And every line you copy becomes leverage again," Freya said gently. "Paper doesn't stay kind, Bee. You know that better than anyone. Paper gets stolen, sold, subpoenaed, burned by other people. The only safe place for a truth this heavy is inside people." She tapped her own sternum, once. "That's why the journal stays. Halvard's journal. His voice, his story, his mistakes and his courage. That's not a weapon. That's a testimony. Weapons win wars. Testimonies raise children. I'd rather raise the next twenty years of this valley than win whatever war those folios start."

"The towns need the concession," Bee said, hearing her mother's voice in it, her mother's tired hands, the rent ledger on the kitchen table. "If it falls—"

"If it falls honestly, it can be rebuilt honestly," Freya said. "If it stands on a lie, it's already fallen. It just hasn't hit the ground yet." She struck the match.

Bee flinched. She couldn't help it.

But Freya held the flame a moment longer, unlit paper in her other hand, and looked up — past Bee, past the others frozen in the passage mouth, at the spring itself, rising and rising out of the mountain with its soft indifferent breath.

"Halvard," she said. "You told me once that a river doesn't remember being lied about. I didn't understand you then." The match burned down toward her fingers. "I think I do now."

She touched flame to paper.

* * *

It burned slowly, at first. Oilcloth takes fire grudgingly, and for a few seconds Bee thought — hoped — it might gutter out, and then the old varnish on the survey covers caught with a soft blue rush and the flames stood up waist-high, orange against the milk-pale water, and the heat pushed against their faces like a living thing.

Nils made a sound. Small, involuntary, the sound of a man watching data die. He turned away and pressed the back of his hand to his mouth.

Tomas didn't move at all. He watched with an expression Bee had never seen him wear — not vindication, though he had argued for this, not relief either. Something older and sadder. As if he had wanted the burning and discovered, now that he had it, that winning felt like losing something he hadn't known he was holding.

Freya fed the folios in page by page. She did not throw the whole mass on at once. She gave each section its own fire — the falsified gauge readings, the Kestrel Pass report, the correspondence, the payment ledgers with names in them that would have toppled families like trees — and she watched each one catch and curl and go to ash, and her face never changed, and that was the most frightening thing Bee had ever seen, more frightening than the Siphon, more frightening than the rapid that had nearly taken her. Because Bee could read faces the way she read terrain, and Freya's face said: *this costs me everything, and I am paying it, and I will not make my payment cheaper by letting you watch me weep.*

The ash floated. That was the detail that would stay with Bee forever, the one she would draw and redraw until her pencil wore down. The burning pages shed flakes of gray that rose on the cavern's slow air current and drifted out over the spring, settling on the moving water, and the water carried them — carried her name, carried the lie, carried the truth, all of it together, indistinguishable now — away into the dark of the mountain, down toward the valley, toward Millbrook, toward the sea. The river taking the record where no one could ever retrieve it. The river keeping it, in the only archive that never lies and never tells.

When the last page was ash, Freya stood for a long time with her empty hands hanging at her sides.

Then she picked up Halvard's journal, wiped the stone dust from its cover with her sleeve, and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where a heartbeat could keep it company.

* * *

"You're too late."

Freya said it without turning around, and Bee spun toward the passage, heart slamming, hand going for the lamp — because the voice that answered was not one of theirs, and it came out of the dark upstream where no voice should have been.

"I've been late since 1911," Augustin Vale said. "A few more minutes hardly signifies."

He came into the lamplight looking like a man who had aged ten years in six weeks. His coat was soaked to the shoulder — he must have come up the flooded traverse alone, at sixty-three years old, without a rope team, which was either magnificent or insane and was probably both — and his breath smoked in the cold air of the cavern, and his eyes went first to the floating ash, and then to the steel case, and then to Freya's hands, which were empty.

For a long moment nobody breathed.

Bee positioned herself, without quite deciding to, between Vale and Freya. She noticed Nils do the same on the other side. Tomas stayed where he was, but his weight shifted forward onto the balls of his feet. None of them had planned it. All of them did it. Later, Bee would think that this — more than the fire, more than the spring — was the moment the crew became a family: four bodies arranging themselves around a fifth without a single word.

Vale looked at the wall of them, and something moved across his face that might have been surprise, or envy, or grief for a kind of wealth he had never managed to buy.

"So," he said. His voice was rough. "It's done."

"You knew what was in there," Freya said. It wasn't a question.

"I suspected. I funded the original expedition, Miss Whitlock. Or should I say—" he paused, and Bee braced for the name, the birth name, the blacklisted name, the end of everything — but he let it go unfinished, waving one gloved hand as if brushing away his own sentence. "It doesn't matter what I call you. You stopped answering to names some years ago, I think. I knew the permits were a trap. I knew the company wanted the source sealed before anyone honest could reach it. I told myself I was racing them to preserve access." He looked at the ash on the water. "I told myself a great many things. Old men are mostly memory management."

"Why are you here?" Tomas asked hoarsely. "Uncle Augustin. Why come all this way if you knew?"

"To stop her." Vale said it plainly. "That was the plan. Arrive ahead of the rangers, secure the documents, negotiate — I'm good at negotiating, it's the only sport I ever won — trade the company silence for the valley's water rights, everyone walks away rich and ruined in the correct proportions." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as paper. "And then I climbed up here alone in the dark, and I stood in that passage, and I watched a woman burn the only thing in the world I couldn't buy, and do you know what I felt?"

Nobody answered.

"Relief," he said. "God help me. Relief." He took off his hat, and Bee realized his hands were shaking. "I have spent forty years purchasing mountains, rivers, permissions, people. And every purchase made the world slightly smaller and slightly safer and slightly less true. And this morning I received word that the company's rangers are two valleys behind me with arrest warrants, and I understood that I had one move left on the board, and every move I owned was a move they taught me." He looked at Freya. "You just made a move none of them would ever imagine. You destroyed your own ransom. There's no negotiating with a woman who's already given the thing away. It's the freest I've ever seen anyone be." He put his hat back on. "Let it go, then. Let it all go."

"The rangers," Nils said sharply. "How long?"

"Dawn. Perhaps sooner. They have horses; you have a river." He glanced at the spring, at the channel running swift and silver out of the mountain's heart. "The Vane drops four hundred feet in the next twelve miles. No ranger in this province will follow a boat through that gorge."

"Then we run it," Freya said.

Bee felt it go through all of them at once — not fear, or not only fear, but the electric click of a plan assembling itself, the same feeling as a map suddenly matching the ground under your boots. Four hundred feet of drop. The gorge they'd bled to climb. All of it, backward, in the dark, at flood speed, with the law at their heels.

Vale was already pulling off his coat. "I'll hold them at the upper trailhead," he said. "I still have a name that opens gates. I'll spend it. All of it." He looked at Freya one last time, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Halvard would have done exactly what you did. I know because I'm the reason he couldn't."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded — once, the smallest possible motion, a debt acknowledged between two people who would never speak of it again.

"Get your people to the boats," Augustin Vale said, turning back toward the dark of the passage, toward the sound, far below and growing nearer, of the river that now carried everything. "Dawn's coming. And dawn belongs to them."
