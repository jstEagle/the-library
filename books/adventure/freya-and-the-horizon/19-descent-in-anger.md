## 19. Descent in Anger

The letter came up the valley on a mule.

Augustin Vale watched it arrive from the veranda of the Concession House at Kestrel Ford — a building he had helped finance thirty years ago and had never once been glad to see. The mule was gray and tired. The rider was not; he dismounted with the crisp economy of a man who had never carried anything heavier than paperwork, and he handed the sealed envelope to the house steward without looking around, as though the valley were merely a corridor between two offices.

Augustin knew that kind of man. He had been that kind of man for most of his life.

He did not go down to meet him. He stayed on the veranda with his coffee going cold and his hands folded on the rail, and he read the rider's posture the way he had once read balance sheets: quickly, completely, and without mercy. The man stood too straight. He waited to be acknowledged rather than introducing himself. He carried a second envelope in his breast pocket, and the shape of it said *warrant* the way a scabbard says *sword*.

So. They had tipped off the company after all.

Augustin had spent sixty-eight years learning that every bargain has a third party standing behind it, usually holding a knife. He had assumed — arrogantly, he could admit it now, alone on this veranda with the river muttering below — that his name still bought silence. That the men who signed the concession permits would let an old financier's vanity expedition die quietly in the upper gorge rather than risk what might surface if it succeeded.

He had been wrong about which of them was more afraid.

* * *

The steward found him twenty minutes later. "Mr. Vale. There's a Mr. Corven from the Vane Consolidated office. He's asking for you by name."

"Of course he is." Augustin set down the cup. "Show him to the map room. And bring the good brandy — the one I keep for funerals."

Corven was younger than Augustin expected, which meant nothing; they grew them younger every year, and harder. He had a clerk's hands and a soldier's eyes, and when Augustin entered the map room, Corven was already standing at the great framed survey of the valley, tracing the river's course with one finger — up past the gauging station, past the falls, into the blank space where the cartographers had given up and written *SOURCE SEALED BY PERMIT* in letters so confident they looked like scripture.

"Mr. Vale." Corven turned. He did not offer a hand. "You've been hard to reach."

"I've been in the mountains. It's rather the point of them."

"You have six people in the restricted headwater zone. Four of them unlicensed. One of them—" Corven paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the incision, "—is traveling under a name that appears nowhere in our guide registry."

Augustin poured two brandies anyway and pushed one across the table. Corven ignored it.

"And you know this how?" Augustin asked.

"We know it the way we know everything, sir. Someone told us."

Someone. Augustin turned the word over. A ranger who'd spotted their night movement through the upper valley, most likely — or something worse, something closer. He thought of the letter he had burned at Cold Camp, the one proving he'd known the permits were a trap from the start, and felt its absence like a pulled tooth. He had destroyed his own shield because shielding it any longer would have made him complicit in a different way. Now he stood here with nothing but his reputation, which was exactly as much protection as a reputation ever is: considerable, right up until the moment it isn't.

"The woman you're referring to," Augustin said, "is my lead guide. Her credentials are her work, and her work is currently four days ahead of your rangers and above the falls, where no warrant of yours will reach before the season closes."

"Then we'll wait for her below." Corven finally picked up the brandy, sniffed it, set it down untouched. "Mr. Vale, I'm authorized to offer you a graceful exit. You withdraw your sponsorship, surrender the survey materials recovered from the cave, and return downriver under escort. In exchange, the company declines to pursue charges against you personally. Your nephew and the others will face trespass citations. Fines. Possibly a short detention."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then everyone is arrested. Including you. Including the girl." Corven's eyes flicked up. "There's a separate matter regarding her. An old file. Two countries' worth. I imagine she'd prefer not to be processed under her real name in front of your crew — or in front of a magistrate."

There it was. Augustin kept his face still, but inside, something cold turned over. They knew. Not everything — Corven spoke like a man holding a card, not a hand — but enough. Enough to make the threat personal, which meant someone upstream had fed them specifics, and there was only one direction those specifics could have flowed from.

He thought of Halvard. Dead eleven years in a fall the company called accidental. He thought of the falsified gauge readings Nils had found, the signature matching the Kestrel Pass report. He thought of Rosalind Whitlock — Freya, she insisted, and he had begun to understand why — standing at his rope repair bench with hands that moved like water finding its level, and how he had recognized her not by her face but by her competence, and how he had hired her anyway knowing, in the wordless way old men know things, that she was running from something and that he might be the thing she was running toward or the thing she was running from, and had chosen not to ask.

He had told himself he was buying a mountain. He had actually been buying time. Six weeks of paper, and now the paper was expiring, and the only currency he had left was himself.

"Mr. Corven," Augustin said, "how long does it take your rangers to get from the ford to the lower gorge?"

"Half a day, if they push."

"And to the takeout below the falls?"

"A day and a half. Why?"

"Because," Augustin said, settling into the chair opposite him with the deliberate slowness of a man arranging his own monument, "I am going to need every minute of that day and a half, and I wanted to know how much of it I'd have to steal."

* * *

He stole the first hour with brandy.

It was almost embarrassingly easy. Corven wanted to be flattered — a junior man sent to handle a legend wants the legend to be real — and Augustin gave him forty minutes of it: the old expeditions, the passes opened, the bridges financed, the time he'd argued down a border commission over a watershed neither country properly owned. He watched Corven's resistance soften the way snowpack softens in afternoon sun, imperceptibly and then all at once, and he hated himself a little for how good he still was at this, and then stopped hating himself, because self-hatred was a luxury and the river didn't wait.

The second hour he stole with paper.

"My solicitor prepared a filing last month," he said, producing a folder he had genuinely prepared and had genuinely hoped never to use. "A formal challenge to the permit renewal, citing irregularities in the original hydrological surveys. If it enters the courts, the concession becomes public news. Every valley town learns what their water rights are actually built on. Every shareholder learns it simultaneously." He let that sit. "You may arrest me, Mr. Corven. Arrest is a local event. The filing is already lodged with three parties and releases automatically unless I countermand it by wire — and I cannot countermand it by wire from a cell."

Corven stared at him. "That's extortion."

"That's negotiation. Extortion is when I want something for myself." Augustin spread his empty hands. "I want nothing, Mr. Corven. That's what makes me difficult. A man who wants something can be paid. A man spending his last credit — what do you offer him?"

For a long moment the only sound was the river below the windows, patient as arithmetic.

"What are you actually doing?" Corven asked quietly. All the clerk had gone out of his voice. What remained was young, and almost honest. "Up there. What's worth this?"

Augustin considered lying to him. Then he considered that Corven would spend the rest of his career carrying out orders from men who had signed Halvard's silence, and that somewhere in the ledger of the world there ought to be at least one entry marked *told the truth at cost*.

"A dead man's name," Augustin said. "And a living woman's. That's all. That's the entire expedition. Everything else — the source, the legend, the wonder of it — that's just the terrain we cross to get there."

* * *

They came for him at dusk, which he had expected, and they came politely, which he had counted on. Corven had wired down-valley; the rangers at the ford would hold Augustin in the Concession House overnight and move on the takeout at first light. A day and a half, minus what Augustin could shave off in the dark.

He shaved off the whole night.

The Concession House had one telephone line, and Augustin had financed its installation, and the steward — a gray, silent man named Osk who had served three concession managers and despised all of them — owed Augustin a debt from a winter fifteen years gone that had never been spoken of and never expired. At half past midnight, Osk put through a call to the ferry operator at the lower gorge, a woman named Marta whose dock Augustin had rebuilt after the flood year, and Marta agreed, in the roundabout language of people who owe each other things, that her ferry would be unavailable for official transport until the ice report cleared, and that the ice report was likely to be slow this year, very slow, possibly glacial.

By two in the morning, the rangers' route to the takeout required a forty-mile detour on foot.

By three, Augustin sat alone in the map room with the brandy he hadn't drunk, looking at the great framed survey, at the finger-tracked line of the river running up into the sealed blankness where a spring rose inside a mountain and a steel case held a dead man's vindication and a living woman's freedom, and he permitted himself, briefly, the luxury of fear.

Not of arrest. Arrest was a room and a process and eventually a fine; he had survived boardrooms, which were worse. He was afraid of the other thing — the thing he had been circling since the cave map surfaced, since he'd recognized those hands, since Tomas had come to him white-faced at Cold Camp and said *Uncle, I know why you really funded this* and Augustin had not denied it.

He had funded the original survey. Thirty years ago, a younger Augustin Vale, hungry for the kind of legacy that gets printed in almanacs, had put money behind a hydrological study of the Vane's headwater — and when the study came back wrong, when the numbers threatened the concession that half the valley's economy hung from, he had looked away while other men fixed the numbers. He had told himself it was pragmatism. Hundreds of families, after all. A town's livelihood against a footnote of truth. He had been very good at telling himself things, right up until the year Halvard Eriksen went into the mountains with the real figures and came down as a body at the base of a cliff, and Augustin understood that his pragmatism had a signature on it too, even if the ink was invisible.

So he had spent thirty years paying interest on it. Endowments. Scholarships. This expedition, dressed up as a last adventure, actually a pilgrimage with a warrant attached.

And now the woman he'd hired to lead it was up there deciding whether to use the truth as a blade or burn it as an offering, and Augustin realized — sitting in the map room with the river talking to itself in the dark — that he genuinely did not know which choice she would make, and that either one would be right, and that this was the first problem of his long life he was not permitted to solve with money.

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold over the eastern ridge, and with it came the sound of hooves.

Augustin was on the veranda again, dressed, shaved, the folder of his last leverage squared neatly on the table beside a cold cup of coffee. The rangers came down the ford road eight strong, led by a sergeant with a face like a closed ledger, and behind them, riding a borrowed mule with the resigned posture of a man attending his own audit, came Corven.

"Mr. Vale." The sergeant dismounted. "We have a warrant for the detention of yourself and six persons on charges of trespass in restricted concession territory. You'll accompany us."

"They're not here," Augustin said pleasantly. "They ran the river yesterday evening. By now they're below the falls, probably eating breakfast, certainly laughing at some joke I won't hear until they tell it badly."

The sergeant looked at Corven. Corven looked at the river, high and brown with snowmelt, running fast and loud toward the lower gorge.

"The ferry," Corven said slowly. "Marta wouldn't—"

"Marta's ice report is pending," Augustin said. "These things take time. Rivers are unpredictable. That's rather their charm."

Something passed over Corven's face — not anger, Augustin noted, but the particular anguish of a young man watching the rules fail and not yet knowing whether to be horrified or relieved. "You stalled us. With phone calls and favors and a frozen ferry. You spent—" He stopped, recalculated. "You know what this costs you. The challenge filing dies the moment you're detained. Your influence, your name, the Vale endowments — after today, none of it protects anyone. You're spending all of it on six people who aren't even here to see it."

"Yes," Augustin said. "That is generally what credit is for."

The sergeant stepped forward with the warrant papers, and Augustin rose to meet him, straightening his back the way he had once straightened it before shareholders and border commissions and his brother's funeral, and found — to his genuine surprise — that it straightened easily, that it had perhaps been waiting sixty years for exactly this weight.

"One condition," he said. "When you catch up with them — and you won't, but when you do — the girl is processed under the name Freya. Whatever else your files say. She earned that name on this river, and I'll consider any deviation a breach of our arrangement, and I will spend whatever remains of me making the company regret it publicly."

Corven, remounting, paused. For a moment he looked less like a clerk and more like what he'd be in twenty years if the mountains got hold of him: tired, and honest, and fond of rivers.

"The permits lapse at dawn tomorrow," Corven said. "After that, the headwater reverts to open record. Whatever they found up there becomes... admissible. Public. Real." He gathered his reins. "Your six weeks of paper, Mr. Vale. I hope they were worth it."

"They weren't mine to spend," Augustin said. "That was rather the point."

They took him down the valley in a wagon, eastward, away from the river, and Augustin Vale — arrested, ruined, outmaneuvered by a ferrywoman's ice report, his fortune's last credit spent on people who would only learn of it later, if ever — watched the water running beside the road and discovered that he was smiling, and that the smile hurt, and that he had no intention whatsoever of stopping.

Behind him, far upstream, past the falls, past the siphon, past the cache of a silenced man, a spring rose inside a mountain toward a decision being made in the dark — and the first gray light of the permit's final day was already moving down the valley toward it, faster than any wagon, faster than any law, like a debt coming due.
