## 20. The Long Rapid Home

They ran the first mile in silence.

Not the silence of fear — Freya had heard that silence before, on Kestrel Pass and in a hundred smaller places since, the hush of people listening for their own deaths. This was different. This was the silence of four people who had stopped wasting breath. Bee crouched low in the bow with her charts wrapped in oilskin against her chest like an infant. Nils worked the stern line without being told where to stand. Tomas paddled with his jaw set and his eyes forward, and Freya understood that whatever he had carried up the mountain, he was carrying it down faster now, and it weighed less with every stroke.

Behind them, faint but persistent as a toothache, came the sound of engines. The company's patrol boats were still above the falls, working their way through the portage trail she'd hoped would slow them longer. An hour, maybe two. Augustin had bought them that much with whatever was left of his name, standing on the landing with his hands raised and his voice carrying that old boardroom authority even as the rangers closed around him. She hadn't looked back. Looking back was how people died in whitewater, and looking back was how people lost the nerve to do what needed doing.

The gorge narrowed ahead, walls rising sheer and wet on either side, the river compressing itself into a corridor of green water and white noise. Freya read it the way she read all rivers now — not as obstacle but as sentence, each feature a word, each rapid a clause, the whole thing saying something if you knew the grammar. Halvard had taught her the grammar. Then he'd taught her that some sentences are written to be misread, and that the men who write them count on guides like her never learning to read at all.

"Lead-out's a quarter mile," she called over the roar. "Then the Squeeze. Stay center until I say break right."

Bee gave a thumbs-up without turning. Good girl. Six weeks ago the kid couldn't read water at all; now she felt the current through the hull like a second pulse. Freya had taught her that. The thought landed strange and warm — every skill she'd hoarded in exile, every trick learned under a name that wasn't hers, poured out now into these three like water finding its level. You can't run a river alone. Halvard again, from a campfire years dead. The river doesn't care what you know. It cares what the people beside you know.

The lead-out hit them like a fist.

* * *

It was a standing wave the height of a barn door, fed by a chute no wider than their boat, and they went through it exactly as planned and exactly wrong at once — perfect line, perfect angle, and then the river changed its mind beneath them. A lateral surge off the left wall, invisible from above, caught the stern and swung them broadside into the trough. Freya felt the world tilt into that long, slow moment every guide knows, when the water stops being a surface and becomes a decision.

"High side! Now!"

Nils threw himself across the gunwale. Tomas followed, graceless and total, and Bee — Bee stayed in her seat, small hands locked on her paddle, because Freya had drilled it into her: *the chart is cargo, the crew is cargo, you are cargo last.* The wave took them anyway. The world became green and thunder and the particular cold of snowmelt reaching up to pull you under.

Freya came up gasping, one hand on the overturned hull, counting heads by instinct before she could count anything else. Bee — there, bobbing, arms windmilling. Nils — clinging to the stern line like a man holding a promise. Tomas —

Tomas was gone.

She found him ten seconds downstream, which is a lifetime in water like this, snagged against a boulder with the current folding over him in a drowning loop. She saw the mistake instantly — he was fighting toward the rock instead of away from it, burning his strength against the one thing in the river that didn't care whether he lived. Every instinct screaming *grab, hold, survive*, and the instinct was killing him.

She swam the eddy line the way only Whitlock ever swam it — angling hard upstream, letting the current do the work, spending nothing she didn't have to spend. Her shoulder hit him. "Let go of the rock," she shouted into his ear, and when he wouldn't, she pried his fingers loose herself, one by one, feeling him resist and then surrender, feeling the exact moment panic turned into trust. Then they were both in the flood together, and she rolled him onto his back, hooked her arm across his chest, and ferried him into the slack water behind the next boulder as if the river had been asked politely.

They lay on the stone, coughing, shaking, alive. Tomas stared at her with an expression she'd seen once before, on a rope team below an ice cliff, on a boy who'd just learned his life belonged to someone else's competence.

"You came back for me," he said. "In the siphon too. You always come back."

"Don't make it a habit," she said. "I'm expensive."

Bee laughed — a wild, cracked, glorious laugh from the shallows where she and Nils were dragging the flipped boat to shore — and the sound broke something open in the morning. They righted the raft, dumped it, repacked it with hands that shook and didn't stop working anyway. Freya checked Bee's oilskin bundle first. Dry. The kid looked at her, and Freya nodded, and Bee's whole face lit like a lamp.

"River owes us one," Nils said, wringing out his beard.

"The river doesn't owe anybody anything," Freya said, pushing off. "That's why I love it."

* * *

They ran the middle gorge through the afternoon, and somewhere in those miles the fear burned off and left something else behind, something Freya had no clean name for. It happened in pieces. It happened when Bee called a hidden hole thirty yards before they reached it, reading the V-shaped tongue of smooth water for the lie it was, and Freya let her call stand and it was right. It happened when Nils, soaked and half-frozen, kept his gauge notebook dry inside his coat out of pure stubborn habit and made them all laugh by reading river levels aloud like scripture. It happened when Tomas, without being asked, took the bow position that had nearly drowned him and held it steady through rapid after rapid, jaw set, eyes reading the water like a man paying a debt.

Every lesson paid forward. That was the phrase that surfaced in her, unbidden, riding the rhythm of the strokes. Halvard had spent his skills on her, and she had spent hers on these three, and now they were spending theirs on each other, and the current carried all of it downstream faster than any ranger could march.

The gorge walls began to glow red as the sun dropped behind the western rim. The river widened. The rapids came longer and more sustained, wave trains rolling for hundreds of yards, and Freya stood in the stern — when had she started standing? — calling lines in a voice gone hoarse and happy.

"Right of the pour-over — carry your speed — punch the haystack — good, good, GOOD—"

They surfed a wave by accident and Bee screamed with delight. They threaded a slot between two rocks with six inches to spare on either side and Nils swore in three languages. They ran a rapid so long that when it finally released them into flat water, nobody spoke for a full minute, just breathed, four people grinning at nothing, at everything, at the fact of being alive in the same instant.

This, Freya thought. This is what they took from me at Kestrel Pass, and this is what they can't take back. Not the name. Not the permits, not the record, not the reputation. This. The motion. The crew. The river saying yes.

Then the flat water ended, and the last gorge opened below them, and she saw the problem.

* * *

The final rapid guarded the lower river like a tollgate — a quarter-mile of churning chaos known to the locals as the Judgment, where the whole valley's water funneled through a maze of house-sized boulders. No one ran it loaded. No one ran it late in the day with a tired crew and a boat full of evidence. The standard route was a portage trail on the left, two hours of scrambling.

Two hours they did not have. Behind them, far upstream but closing, she could hear the engines again, echoing off the canyon walls, impossible to place and impossible to ignore.

"We portage," Nils said, reading her face. "Tell me we portage."

"We don't have time to portage."

"Freya." He said the name gently, and she heard what he was doing with it — offering it back to her, the false name, the safe one, a courtesy. "We're exhausted. We're lucky to be alive. Whatever's chasing us, it isn't worth—"

"It is," Bee said quietly. "It's Halvard. It's the truth. It's twenty years of sealed river." She looked up at Freya. "I'll follow your line anywhere. But you have to tell us true — can we run it?"

Freya looked at the Judgment for a long time. The light was failing. The holes were real. And yet — she read it again, feature by feature, the way Halvard had taught her, the way she'd taught Bee, the grammar of it opening up like a book she'd been reading her whole life. There was a line. Narrow, unforgiving, demanding everything precisely. But there.

"There's a thread," she said. "Left of center, past the first island, hard right at the fang rock, then hold the seam down the gut. If we're perfect, we're through. If we're sloppy, we swim. If we're very sloppy—" She didn't finish it.

"Then we're perfect," Tomas said.

They scouted from the bank, Bee sketching the line from memory into her chart, Nils marking the hazards, Tomas walking the shore to find the rescue eddy. Freya watched them work and felt her chest ache with something that wasn't grief anymore. Grief disguised as pragmatism, she'd called it once, all those gray years on the docks. This was its opposite: love disguised as competence, four strangers becoming a rope team, a family braided out of skill and trust and the shared decision to keep moving.

They launched at last light.

* * *

The first island came at them fast and she called it and they were past it, the hull singing against the current. The fang rock rose black and fanged indeed, and Tomas pulled them right on her mark with a stroke so clean she could have kissed him. Then the gut opened — the seam, the thread, the narrow lane of green water threading between chaos on both sides — and they were in it, committed, flying.

Everything after that happened in the space between heartbeats and lasted a year.

A hole yawned where the seam should have held. "Brace!" The bow plunged, the stern kicked, and for one endless second they were surfing the edge of disaster, the whole river leaning on them, and Freya leaned back — not fighting the water, arguing with it, using every ounce of technique the blacklist had tried to erase — and the boat came free. Another wave train. Another call. Bee's voice joining hers now, calling features before Freya named them, the student become a second pair of eyes. Nils bailing without being told. Tomas holding his line like a man who had decided, finally and completely, who he wanted to be.

And then — release. The gorge spat them out. The river spread wide and golden in the last light, gentle as a lake, and the walls fell away behind them, and ahead the lower valley opened under a sky going soft with evening stars.

They drifted. Nobody paddled. The current carried them, unhurried now, as if the river too had finished running.

Downstream, where the valley town's lights were beginning to kindle along the banks, a church bell began to ring. Freya counted the strokes out of old habit — the concession office rang it at season's end, she'd learned that much from the permit papers. Nine. Ten. Eleven.

Midnight. The sixth week. The deadline.

The permits were expired. The source of the River Vane was legally sealed — and they had come down out of it free, every one of them, carrying the truth in oilskin and in memory, and there was nothing on paper anywhere in the world that could take that back.

Bee started laughing first, then crying, then laughing again. Nils removed his ruined hat and held it over the side in salute to the river. Tomas put his hand briefly on Freya's shoulder — just briefly, just enough — and said nothing, which was somehow the most eloquent thing anyone had said all day.

Freya stood in the stern of the battered raft and let the current take them home, and for the first time in six years she did not feel like a woman hiding inside a borrowed name. She felt like a woman in motion, headed toward a town full of people who deserved to hear what Halvard had actually done, with a journal in the boat and a family at the oars.

The bell finished ringing. In the silence after, Bee lifted her head sharply, listening.

"Boats," she said. "Coming downriver behind us. More than one."

The engines she'd been hearing all day had never stopped.
