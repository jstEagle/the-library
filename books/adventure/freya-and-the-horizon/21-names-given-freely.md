## 21. Names Given Freely

The valley town was called Merrow's Bend, and it smelled of sawdust and river water, the way towns do when they have made their living from a single current for three generations. Freya had passed through it once before — years ago, under her other name, with Halvard's pack straps cutting grooves into her shoulders — and she remembered it as a place that kept its shutters closed against strangers. Now the shutters were open. Now every window on the main street seemed to hold a face.

They came down out of the hills on the morning after the escape run, six of them and what remained of their gear, looking like exactly what they were: people who had been in the mountains too long. Nils walked with his instruments wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like an infant. Bee carried her ruined charts bundled under one arm and Halvard's journal in a waterproof bag slung across her back, because Freya had given it to her two miles below the falls without explanation, and Bee had understood without asking. Tomas limped. Augustin Vale was not among them; he had stayed upriver with the rangers, spending himself like a man paying off a debt, and none of them knew yet whether he would walk free or be led away.

The town square held a market that day, stalls of smoked fish and wool and the flat brown bread the valley baked dense enough to survive a winter. The crew stopped at its edge, uncertain, a knot of mud and silence. It was Bee who moved first. She always moved first now.

She climbed onto the low stone wall beside the well, unslung the journal, and held it up over her head where everyone could see it.

"My name is Beatrice Okafor," she said, in a voice pitched to carry the way she'd learned to pitch it across canyons. "I'm fifteen years old, I ran away from home in March, and I've spent six weeks learning that maps lie when the people who draw them are paid to lie. This book belongs to a man named Eirik Halvard. He surveyed this river thirty years ago. And everything your town believes about him is wrong."

* * *

They told it in the guildhall that evening, because the town asked them to, because word travels faster than water in a valley and by noon half of Merrow's Bend had heard that the crew from the forbidden headwater had come down carrying papers. The guildhall was a long room of dark timber that smelled of lamp oil and old arguments, and it filled past capacity, farmers and millworkers and the concession men's own clerks standing along the walls, and Freya stood at the front with her hands at her sides and let the others speak first.

Nils spoke about the drowned gauge. He set his salvaged readings on the table beside the official record, the one stamped and sealed and filed in the capital, and he showed them, line by line, how the numbers diverged — how the river had been recorded as running thinner than it truly ran, year after year, so that the concession could claim scarcity and price it accordingly. "Someone signed that falsification," Nils said. "And the signature matched the report that buried a guide named Rosalind Whitlock after Kestrel Pass."

He said her name. Her true name, spoken aloud in a room full of strangers, and Freya felt the old cold move through her chest, the cold she had carried for years like a stone sewn into her coat. But Nils did not stop there. He turned to the room and said: "She's standing right here. She came into this valley under another name because the world took hers away. And she led us upriver anyway."

Tomas spoke about his uncle. He stood in front of the town that lived off Vale money — the concession contracts, the seasonal wages, the roofs that Augustin's paper had put over their heads — and he told them about the burned letter, or rather about what it had proved: that the permits were designed to expire, that the sealing of the source was never conservation but concealment, renewed every twenty years by men who knew precisely what was buried there. "My uncle funded the original survey," Tomas said, and his voice cracked and steadied. "I won't pretend otherwise. But he sent us upriver knowing we might find the truth, and when we found it, he let my friend burn it rather than use it. Whatever else he is, he spent his last credit on us. I'll vouch for that if no one else will."

Bee read from Halvard's journal. She read the passages where his careful hand described finding the falsification — the dates, the gauge discrepancies, the letter he wrote to the commission that was answered not with an investigation but with the revocation of his license and, eventually, the silence around his death. She read until her voice gave out, and then Freya stepped forward.

* * *

Later, Freya would not be able to reconstruct what she said. She remembered fragments: the well light through the guildhall windows going amber, then blue; the face of the miller's wife in the third row, wet-eyed and unmoving; her own voice sounding strange in her ears, unpracticed at this scale, used only for shouting over rapids and murmuring across campfires. She told them about Kestrel Pass — not the official version, the disaster with her name attached to it like a brand, but the truth: the falsified flow data that had put her party into a gorge the river had no business being in, the report that blamed the guide because blaming the guide was cheaper than blaming the signature. She told them she had let Rosalind Whitlock die on paper because a dead woman could still work docks and lead climbs under any name she chose, while a blacklisted one could not work at all.

"I didn't come here to clear my name," she said. "I came down this river to keep a promise to a dead man. What you do with what you've heard tonight is yours. That's all I know how to say."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that she heard the river itself, distant and constant, through the guildhall walls.

Then the miller's wife stood up. Then the ferryman who had charged tourists a coin apiece to gawk at the Tollhouse cave for forty years. Then the schoolteacher, then two of the concession clerks, then a boy Freya recognized as one of Bee's cousins, red-faced and furious at the world for letting his cousin vanish and return as someone remarkable. They didn't cheer. That wasn't the shape of it. They began, instead, to talk — to argue, actually, in the low urgent way of people doing arithmetic — about petitions, about signatures, about the blacklist board sitting in the provincial capital and the fact that a petition bearing four hundred names from the valley whose water Halvard had protected might carry a weight no lawyer's brief ever had.

"They owe him," the ferryman said simply, to the room, to Freya. "This river runs honest because of him. And they broke him for it. If the valley doesn't speak, who will?"

By midnight there was paper everywhere. The schoolteacher had ink and a steady hand. Bee drew the petition's header herself — a small, precise rendering of the Vane's source spring, the one she'd seen with her own eyes — and beneath it the lines filled with names, one after another, the ink still wet when the lamplight guttered toward dawn.

Freya stood at the edge of it all, watching, and felt something loosen in her chest that she had thought was scar tissue. For years she had believed that names were things taken — taken by reports, by boards, by men with stamps. She had built a life on the assumption that no document would ever give hers back. She had not imagined this: that a name could be returned by hand, passed person to person like a pail of water up a line, until it arrived whole.

Nils found her by the window near dawn. "You understand what this means," he said quietly. "If it works, you're Rosalind Whitlock again. Guides will want to hire *her*. The mountains will want her back."

Freya looked out at the river running silver in the first light, the same river that had tried to kill them all and carried them home anyway.

"Let me think about what I want to be called," she said, "when I decide who I am."

Behind them, Bee was already asleep at the table, one hand resting on the petition, guarding it even in dreams.
