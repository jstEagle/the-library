## 1. Miles, Not Years

The rain started before dawn, the way it always did this time of year, and Juniper Ravensworth was in the air before it finished starting.

She liked that about the monsoon season, if she allowed herself to like anything: the way it imposed a schedule on the world. Rain came, rivers rose, villages on the low ground needed medicine and rice and radio batteries, and she flew. There was no room in a schedule like that for thinking. Thinking was a luxury for people who had somewhere to be.

She had nowhere to be. That was the whole point.

The Cessna—nineteen years old, patched twice, faithful as a dog—climbed through the gray ceiling of cloud with a load of penicillin, antimalarials, and one crate of something the clinic at Two Rivers had radioed for in code, which meant antibiotics or birth control or both, because those were the only things anyone ever coded. Below her the country was drowning politely, the way it did every year. Rivers that were roads in the dry season had become brown arteries, swollen and slow, and the villages sat on their rises like animals standing in water up to their hocks, waiting it out.

"Ravensworth," said the radio, which was wrong, but she'd stopped correcting people years ago. "You copy?"

"Copy." Her voice came out flat and even, a voice with no history in it. She'd practiced that voice for fifteen years until it wasn't practice anymore.

"Weather's closing the eastern strips by tomorrow night. You want to get your runs done today."

"I always get my runs done."

A pause on the other end, the particular pause of a dispatcher deciding not to say what he was thinking. She knew that pause. She knew every species of silence a man could make around her—the respectful ones, the curious ones, the ones that meant *who is this woman who landed here four years ago with cash and no past*. Four years at this strip. Before that, two years at another. Before that, eighteen months somewhere she didn't let herself remember. Fifteen years of arithmetic like that, each number smaller than the last, each stay shorter, until the stays had worn down to almost nothing at all.

Her rule was simple, and she kept it the way other people kept religion: never stay anywhere longer than necessary. Necessary had a definition, and she wrote the definition herself. Fuel. Repairs. A load that couldn't wait. The moment necessity ended, so did she. Wheels up, gone, no goodbyes that couldn't be said from a moving plane.

People called it restlessness when they thought she couldn't hear. She'd heard it in trading posts and mission kitchens, said over beer, said kindly: *that pilot woman, she's got ants in her.* Let them think it. Ants were safer than roots. Roots could be dug up. Roots could be found.

* * *

Two Rivers first, because Two Rivers always came first—it sat lowest, and its clinic served six villages that couldn't be reached any other way once the water came up. The strip there wasn't a strip so much as an argument between grass and mud, and she put the Cessna down on it the way she did everything: precisely, without flourish, without a single unnecessary foot of rollout.

Sister Bernadette was waiting with a hand truck and three boys who materialized, as boys did, out of nowhere, drawn by the smell of an engine.

"You're late," the nun said.

"The river's early."

"That's what late means, in the wet season." Sister Bernadette was seventy and built like a fence post and had been at Two Rivers longer than Juniper had been alive, which made her, in Juniper's private taxonomy, the most dangerous kind of person: someone who remembered things. "Fever cases doubled this week. And the Mabutu crossing is under. Half my patients can't reach me."

"So I'm your ferry now."

"You've always been my ferry. You just charge more than the church does." The nun heaved a crate onto the hand truck and looked at her sideways, the look she probably thought was casual. "There's a bed behind the clinic. Dry. If the weather turns—"

"It won't turn before I'm gone."

"It always turns before you're gone. That's the monsoon, girl. It turns whenever it likes." Sister Bernadette wheeled the truck around, then stopped, and Juniper felt the question coming the way you feel a change in the air pressure before a storm. "You could stay for supper. Father Anselm makes—"

"Sister." Juniper kept her voice light, kept her hands moving, checking tie-downs that didn't need checking. "I've got nine stops and weather coming. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," the nun said. "That's why I ask every time."

And that was the thing about Sister Bernadette—she asked every time, and she never pushed, and somehow that was worse. Pushing you could refuse. Patience you had to keep refusing, week after week, year after year, and every refusal cost a little something you didn't get back.

Juniper finished the med drop in forty minutes, took on fuel she didn't strictly need, and was airborne again before the nun finished waving.

* * *

The middle stops blurred, the way they always did. A bag of flour at Kessel's Landing. Vaccines at the mission on the ridge. A woman in labor at Sorrow Creek who turned out not to need her after all—the baby arrived while Juniper was still circling, and the father ran out onto the strip waving both arms overhead, and she dropped a bundle of cloth diapers and a tin of condensed milk and climbed away grinning despite herself.

This was the part of flying she permitted herself to love. Not the places—never the places—but the doing. Hands on the yoke, eyes on the sky, the whole world reduced to wind and weight and the next hundred miles. In the air she was nobody's daughter, nobody's witness, nobody's ghost. She was just a pair of hands and a machine and a job that needed doing, and if there was a cleaner kind of existence than that, she hadn't found it in fifteen years of looking.

By mid-afternoon she was down to her last stop, a cluster of huts on a bend of the Dola called Meridian Flats, where the water had come up faster than anywhere else. She could see it from three miles out—the village shrunk to an island, brown water lapping at the doorposts, dugout canoes tied to the church rail like horses. She made her pass, judged the strip—half drowned, soft at the far end, doable—and set it down with the wheels kissing the water off the grass itself.

They came out to meet her wading, which told her everything. A dozen men and women, hip-deep, passing crates hand to hand toward higher ground. A boy of maybe ten took the box of water purification tablets from her and held it above his head like a trophy.

"How long?" she asked the headman, an old man named Ekalu whose English was better than hers and who used it sparingly, like money.

"Three days, maybe four. Then we go to the church hill and wait for the water to leave." He squinted up at the sky, reading clouds the way she read instruments. "You should not have come today."

"You needed the tablets today."

"Yes." He almost smiled. "So. You should not have come today, and thank you."

She was reloading the tie-down netting when the radio in the panel crackled—not her dispatcher's frequency, but the shared band, the open channel everyone in the territory monitored the way farmers watched the horizon. Static, then a voice she half recognized, one of the government district officers, flat and official:

"—repeat, all stations, all stations. Survey team Alpha-Seven, contracted geological survey, provincial works department, overdue at last check-in. Six personnel, last known position grid—" a burst of static ate the coordinates "—any station with information regarding team movements, contact the district office. Repeat—"

Juniper tuned it out the way she tuned out weather warnings for places she wasn't going. A survey team. Six men with government boots and government pay, lost somewhere in the interior. It wasn't her problem. Nothing was her problem; that was the arrangement she had with the world, and the world had honored it for fifteen years. People went missing out here every season—fell into sinkholes, drowned in flash floods, walked into the bush after a quarrel and never walked out. The interior was big and indifferent and full of ways to die, and the government losing track of its own employees was the least surprising news the monsoon had ever produced.

Ekalu was watching her face. "Bad news?" he asked.

"Some surveyors are missing."

"Ah." He said it the way old men say *ah*, containing whole histories. "The ones who came through on the boat, asking questions? Taking water samples?"

Juniper paused with her hand on the cowling latch. "Surveyors don't take water samples."

"These did." Ekalu shrugged, a small movement, a man declining to be interesting. "Weeks ago. Polite fellows. Asked about the fish kills downstream. Wrote everything in little books." He looked at her with mild, ancient eyes. "The river has been strange this year. Rust-colored, some mornings. The fish float belly-up past the bend. But the government men will find their surveyors, I am sure. The government finds whatever it wants to find."

Something cold moved through her then, brief and specific, the instinct she trusted above instruments—a pilot's sixth sense, the one that said *check that gauge again, walk the wing again, look at that cloud again*. It passed. She latched the cowling.

"Keep your people off the floodwater," she said. "It'll carry disease."

"It carries worse than disease," Ekalu said, "but yes. We will keep them off it."

* * *

She climbed out of Meridian Flats into the teeth of the evening storm front and flew home through rain so hard it sounded like gravel on the airframe. Home. The word arrived in her head uninvited, and she evicted it immediately. The strip at Kessel's Crossing wasn't home. It was a hangar with a cot in the corner, a kettle, a locked steel box bolted to the floor, and four years of accumulated nothing—which was exactly as much as she intended to accumulate anywhere, ever.

She dried off. She heated soup. She checked the weather one more time, confirmed what she already knew—that the eastern strips would close by tomorrow night, that she'd beaten the clock the way she always beat it, that she'd be gone south within the week, ahead of the worst of it, same as every year—and she lay on the cot listening to the rain hammer the tin roof and felt the particular satisfaction of a day correctly survived.

Nine stops. Nine landings, nine takeoffs, medicine delivered, nobody dead, nobody asking questions she couldn't answer smoothly. Tomorrow she'd service the plane, settle her account at the trading post, and be ready to move when the window opened. South, this time. She hadn't been south.

The rule held. The armor held. Fifteen years, and it had never once failed her: keep moving, keep light, keep your name short and your stories shorter, and the past stays where you buried it.

On the shelf above the cot, in a dented aluminum toolbox she never opened, sat a compass she'd carried through every one of those fifteen years without ever letting herself read the engraving on the back. She didn't look at it now, either. She'd gotten very good at not looking.

The radio on the workbench hissed through the storm, scanning bands, and somewhere in the static a voice repeated itself—*survey team Alpha-Seven, repeat, survey team Alpha-Seven, overdue*—and Juniper reached over and switched it off.

Not her problem. Not her name. Not her year.

Outside, the rain kept falling on a country that was quietly drowning, and somewhere out in all that water, six men with little books full of water samples had stopped answering their radios, and the first thread of a rope was already paying out across the miles, looking for something to catch.

It caught her four days later.
