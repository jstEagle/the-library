## 2. The Last Coordinates

The radio woke her before the storm did.

Juniper had been sleeping in the Cessna's shadow, a cot rigged between the wheels where the hangar's corrugated tin threw a wedge of shade across the strip at Mabui Crossing. Fifteen years of bush flying had taught her to sleep anywhere—on cargo netting, on sacks of rice, once on a crate of live chickens that complained all night like a jury deliberating. She slept light and she slept armed with the particular alertness of a woman who had learned that trouble rarely announced itself twice.

The radio crackled again. Then the thunder, rolling up the valley like furniture being dragged across heaven.

She lay still for a moment, cataloguing. Wind from the southwest, building. The air had that bruised weight it got before the monsoon committed to anything serious. Two more weeks, maybe three, and the strips would start going under one by one, drowned country by drowned country, until the whole interior became a single brown sea with islands where hills used to be. Her season was ending. She could feel it in her knees and in the barometer.

The radio crackled a third time, insistent.

She rolled off the cot, bare feet on warm concrete, and crossed to the little workbench where her set lived under a tin roof patched with tarpaulin. The frequency was one she kept for charters—the bush telegraph, every pilot and post manager and mission nurse within four hundred miles sharing weather and gossip and the occasional mayday.

"—repeat, survey team, government contract, six souls, last check-in Tuesday. Anyone in the eastern ranges copy?"

She didn't answer. That was the rule. She listened the way she did everything else: without leaving fingerprints.

*Missing survey team.* She filed it away the way she filed everything—under *not mine*, in the drawer marked *keep moving*. Government teams went missing the way tourists went missing: usually they'd taken a wrong turn, run out of fuel, gotten romantic about a shortcut. The interior ate people who thought their paperwork would protect them. It had eaten better-prepared men than six surveyors with a satellite phone and a mandate.

She made coffee on a single burner, watched the storm build over the ranges, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the weather coming.

* * *

They found her two days later at Kessara Post, which annoyed her, because Kessara was supposed to be a refueling stop and nothing more. She had a personal geography of places like Kessara—dirt strips beside brown rivers, a fuel drum, a man named Okello who weighed drums by eye and never asked questions because she paid cash and left before the questions could form. She'd been landing there for eleven years. Okello knew her as the pilot who drank her tea standing up.

The man waiting under the post's awning was not Okello.

He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, wearing field clothes too clean to have ever met actual mud. A government lanyard hung around his neck, laminated card, official blue. He stood the way men stood when they were used to rooms responding to them. Beside him, younger, quieter, a second man held a clipboard against his chest like a shield.

"Pilot Ravensworth?" The older man smiled. "Corben Hale, Regional Administration. We've been hoping to catch you."

"Nobody catches me," Juniper said. "I land, I leave. You want something flown, talk to Okello, he takes messages."

"We tried the other pilots." Hale's smile stayed fixed while his eyes did arithmetic. "Most won't fly east this time of year. You will."

"I fly where I'm paid to fly."

"And we pay well." He let that sit. "One drop. Single pass, no landing if you don't want one. Coordinates here." He produced a folded map, creased along lines that said it had been opened and shut many times. "Supply resupply for a contracted survey team working the eastern ridge country."

Juniper looked at the map and felt the first cold thread go through her, thin as wire.

She knew that country. Not from charts—from fifteen years of reading terrain the way other people read faces. The coordinates sat deep in the fold of the eastern ranges, past the last marked strip, past anything a supply convoy could reach. Country you flew into only if someone already there needed you to, or if you wanted very badly not to be seen.

"That's a long way from anywhere," she said.

"That's rather the point of a survey."

"What are they surveying?"

Hale's pause lasted half a second too long. "Water resources. Hydrology. The usual pre-development assessments."

*The usual.* Juniper had heard *the usual* enough times to know it was the word men used when the unusual was sitting in the room with them, wearing its good clothes.

"The missing team?" she said, watching his face. "Radio's been asking after six surveyors, eastern ranges."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "That's them. Communications have been intermittent. Weather interference." He gestured at the sky, where the monsoon was stacking itself into cathedral walls of cloud. "Which is why we need someone willing to fly below the weather. Someone experienced."

Flattery. Money. A problem dressed as an opportunity. She'd been sold things by better salesmen than Corben Hale, and she'd bought every one of them, back when buying things was how she'd ended up needing to disappear.

"One drop," she said. "Cash. And I don't land unless the strip's marked and clear."

"No landing necessary. Drop zone's a meadow below the ridge. They'll signal."

*They'll signal.* She took the map. His hand brushed hers as he passed it over—deliberate or accidental, impossible to say—and his fingers were cold despite the heat.

"Pleasure doing business, Pilot Ravensworth."

"You haven't done business yet," she said. "You've bought a look at my tail number."

His smile didn't waver. But she caught the younger man writing something on the clipboard, and she caught, a half-beat later, the way he angled the clipboard away when she glanced at it.

That night she checked her plane twice, which she never did, and slept badly, which she always did before a flight she couldn't explain to herself.

* * *

The eastern ranges came up out of the haze like the backs of sleeping animals.

Juniper flew the last hour low, under the cloud deck, threading valleys that the maps rendered as blank white space and her memory rendered in detail no cartographer would believe. Rivers braided and unbraided beneath her. Mist stood in the side drainages like smoke trapped under glass. The world down there was green and wet and utterly indifferent to governments, contracts, and laminated cards.

The cargo bay held twelve sealed crates, stenciled with a logistics code she'd photographed before loading and would hand to no one. Food, mostly, by the weight. Medical boxes by the shape. One crate heavier than it should have been, dense in a way that rations weren't, and she'd decided somewhere over the third river system not to wonder about it out loud.

The coordinates put the drop zone in a hanging valley below a long ridge, a meadow of pale grass bisected by a stream. She circled once, high, reading the ground. No wind sock. No marker panels. No signal fire.

"They'll signal," Hale had said.

She circled again, lower. The meadow was empty. The stream ran loud and silver. On the ridge above, the forest grew right down to the rock line, dark and close.

She should have turned around then. Later, in the long accounting she kept of her own judgment, she would mark this as the moment—the empty meadow, the missing signal—and ask herself why she hadn't. The honest answer was money, and the truer answer was that some part of her had already smelled the wrongness and wanted to see it clearly before she ran. Curiosity was the flaw she'd never managed to file smooth. It had testified against her family once. It was about to do worse.

She dropped to five hundred feet and flew the ridge line.

The fog was doing what fog does in hill country at midmorning—burning off in patches, closing again, giving the world away in torn pieces. She flew through a bank of it, instruments telling her what her eyes refused to confirm, and came out the far side into a sudden clean corridor of air along the crest.

And there they were.

Figures on the ridge. Four of them, maybe five, spaced along the rock line where the trees broke. At that distance, at that speed, they registered the way things register in a half-second of flight: upright shapes, dark clothing, one of them raising an arm—waving, signaling, *there's your signal, pilot, right on schedule.*

Her thumb was already moving toward the release toggle when the fog closed and took them.

She held her line. Counted. The mist thinned again thirty seconds later over a saddle of bare rock, and she looked down expecting the figures, expecting to make the drop.

The ridge was empty.

Not empty-empty. There were things there. Equipment, laid out with a neatness that snagged her eye even from the air—cases aligned in rows, tarps squared, gear staged the way soldiers stage gear, not the way surveyors do, scattered and dragged and half-buried in expedition chaos. She'd flown supplies to a dozen field teams. Field teams lived like raccoons. Nobody's camp looked like a parade ground.

And among the cases, catching the sun through the shredding fog, something glinted.

Not a compass face. Not a watch crystal or a cook pot or any of the hundred innocent bright things a camp produces. This was a hard, wet gleam, the specific shine of machined metal oiled and cared for, and it moved—tracked, as she passed, the way a lens tracks.

Barrel. She knew the geometry of it the way she knew the geometry of a control yoke. Rifle barrel, or the barrel of something longer, mounted or braced on the rock line, and pointed not at the valley, not at the forest—

Up. Angled at the sky corridor she had just flown through.

Juniper pulled power and dropped toward the meadow in a spiral that put the ridge behind her own shoulder, heart going hard and flat, hands steady on the yoke because fifteen years had taught her hands to be steady while everything underneath them screamed. Below her the meadow waited, pale and empty and perfect, a drop zone with no one to receive the drop.

Six surveyors. Intermittent communications. Weather interference.

Nobody camps like that. Nobody signals from a ridgeline and then isn't there. And nobody points a rifle at the approach path of their own resupply flight unless what they're actually watching for is the flight itself.

She climbed out of the valley the way she'd come, fast and low and then higher, putting ridges between herself and the glinting thing until the eastern ranges sank back into haze behind her. Only when the first river system slid underneath did she let herself think in sentences again.

The crates rode in the back, twelve of them, riding quietly.

She could turn around. She could deliver them. She could find the meadow, kick them out the door from two hundred feet, and be gone before anyone on that ridge finished deciding whether to shoot. That was the job. That was the money. That was none of her business, and her whole life was built on the architecture of *none of my business*, load-bearing walls of it, fifteen years of *not mine, keep moving*.

But the figures had waved. Hadn't they? Or had one of them raised an arm to adjust something—a scope, a sight, a strap? At speed, in fog, a wave and a sighting-in were the same gesture.

And the camp had been too neat, and the barrel had tracked her, and Corben Hale's fingers had been cold in ninety-degree heat, and the young man with the clipboard had written down her tail number before she'd ever agreed to anything.

Juniper flew west with the storm building behind her and the crates riding silent in the back, and she did not decide anything, because deciding meant committing, and committing meant staying visible in a story she didn't yet understand.

But somewhere over the fourth river system, alone in the cockpit noise, she admitted the thing she'd known since the ridge: the instinct had spoken. The old one, the animal one, the one that had kept her alive through years she never talked about. It didn't speak often anymore. When it spoke, people who ignored it ended up as case files.

*Something is wrong in those mountains,* the instinct said. *And they know a plane is coming.*

She looked at the coordinates penciled on the map's margin, small and precise in Hale's handwriting, and noticed for the first time what she should have noticed in Kessara under the awning.

The pencil marks were fresh. But beneath them, faintly, where the paper had been erased and rewritten, were older coordinates—half a mile south, in the open, in the meadow proper. The original drop zone. The one someone had moved, deliberately, to the base of a ridge with a clear field of fire over the approach.

Whoever filled out that requisition hadn't wanted the supplies delivered.

They'd wanted the plane delivered—to a place where it could be met.

Juniper's mouth went dry. She was three hours from anywhere, carrying twelve crates for men who might be surveyors or might be bait, and the monsoon was coming up behind her like a tide, and she had given Corben Hale her tail number, her route habits, and eleven years of Okello knowing exactly where she landed and when.

For the first time in fifteen years, Juniper Ravensworth understood that she was not the one choosing the coordinates.

She turned the map over, as if the back of it might hold an answer, and began—carefully, methodically, the way she did everything that mattered—to plan how a woman disappears from a story that has already written her into it.
