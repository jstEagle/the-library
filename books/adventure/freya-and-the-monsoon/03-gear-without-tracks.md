## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The rain came in sideways off the ridge like it had a grudge, and Juniper flew through it the way she did everything—alone, on schedule, and without looking back.

Three days since the supply drop at the survey coordinates. Three days of flying her med route down the river chain: antimalarials to Ketchikan Bend, insulin to the mission at Two Rivers, a woman in obstructed labor airlifted out of a village whose name changed depending on who you asked. Normal work. The kind of work that filled the hours so completely she never had to notice they were passing.

The radio had been another matter.

She'd heard it first on Tuesday, half-listening while she threaded a pass between cloud decks, the way you hear a neighbor's dog barking—background noise until it isn't. The regional frequency, the one every pilot in the territory kept dialed because it was where weather lived and where emergencies started:

*"—survey team, government contract, six personnel, last contact seventy-two hours. Any station with information—"*

Then static. Then a different voice, bored, reading a NOTAM about a closed strip.

Juniper had turned the volume down two clicks and told herself it wasn't her problem. Six people she'd never met, dropped into country that ate people for breakfast every monsoon. Surveyors were always losing radios, always missing check-ins, always turning up three days later sheepish and mosquito-bitten and full of stories. It happened. It was practically a tradition.

She'd said as much to herself over coffee at Two Rivers, and the words had sat in her stomach like ballast she couldn't jettison.

Because there was the other thing. The thing she'd seen through the fog on that ridge and hadn't reported, hadn't written down, hadn't let herself think about except in the last hour before sleep when the mind does its accounting: the glint of metal that didn't look like tripods or stakes or any survey gear she'd ever hauled. The figures standing too still, spaced too evenly, like men who'd been told how to stand by someone who understood sightlines.

She'd filed it under *not my business* and flown home.

But the check-ins kept getting missed. Wednesday: nothing. Thursday: nothing, and now the voice on the regional frequency had a different texture—not bored anymore but careful, the carefulness of people reading from a script someone else had approved. *Search coordination is being handled. Civilian traffic should avoid the grid squares listed below.*

Avoid the grid squares. As if the jungle were contagious.

Juniper stood in the open door of her hangar Friday morning and watched the rain hammer the flooded strip and thought about grid squares. She thought about the fact that nobody avoids a place unless they know exactly what's in it. She thought about the polished man at the trading post—no, that came later; right now there was only the rain and the radio and the itch between her shoulder blades that fifteen years of bush flying had taught her to treat as data, not nerves.

The itch said: *go back.*

Her rule said: *never go back.* Never return to a strip twice in one season if you can help it. Never fly the same route two days running. Never stay anywhere longer than necessary, never owe anyone anything, never leave a thread loose enough for someone to pull. Fifteen years of rules, each one purchased with something she'd rather not count.

But the rules were for protecting herself from other people's trouble. And this trouble had a shape now, and the shape was six missing people and a government suddenly very interested in keeping small aircraft away from one specific patch of drowned green nowhere.

"Damn it," she said, to no one, and went to fuel the plane.

* * *

The weather broke just after noon, the way monsoon weather does—like a door slamming open onto a room you'd forgotten existed. Sunlight hit the flooded valley and turned it into hammered brass. Juniper climbed through the clearing deck with the Cessna light, forty minutes of fuel more than she needed, a pack with a machete, a tarp, water, and the pistol she kept wrapped in an oilcloth under the spare tire and told herself she carried for snakes.

The coordinates were burned into her memory the way all her coordinates were. She didn't write things down anymore. Writing things down was how you left evidence.

The ridge appeared out of the haze exactly where it had been before, which felt almost rude, as if the landscape ought to have rearranged itself to match her mood. She made one pass low along the flank of it, wheels and floats up, eyes working the treeline the way they'd worked a thousand drop zones.

The camp was still there.

That was the first wrong thing. Six days into a missed-check-in situation, with search parties supposedly combing the area, a camp should have been torn apart—tents stripped, gear scattered, the archaeology of panic everywhere. Instead the site sat below her, neat as a diagram. The orange dome of the mess tent. The surveyor's yellow. Equipment laid out on tarps in rows.

Laid out. In rows.

Nobody lays gear out in rows unless they're leaving it for someone to find, or unless they're gone and something has arranged it.

She put the plane down on the flooded oxbow a kilometer east, where the water lay flat between two sandbars, and waded ashore with the pack high on her shoulders and the machete already in her hand. The mud took her to mid-calf and held her boots with a sucking reluctance, and the heat closed over her like a hand, and the insects found her within thirty seconds, and none of it registered because she was already reading the ground.

Reading ground was the oldest skill she had, older than flying. Her father had taught her tracking before he'd taught her anything else, back when learning from him had felt like inheritance instead of infection. *The country talks, Junie. Most folks just don't speak it.* She'd spent fifteen years trying to forget the language and failing, and now it came back unbidden, fluently, horribly.

There were no footprints.

Not few footprints. Not faded footprints. None. The mud around the camp was a page six days old—rain-erased, yes, but rain doesn't erase everything, and it doesn't erase selectively. She circled the perimeter slowly, reading. The ground recorded the comings and goings of everything: a monitor lizard's tail-drag stitching toward the water, hornbill droppings, the collapsed runnels of their own landing-day tracks from a week ago, still faintly visible near the oxbow where she'd come ashore the first time.

Six adults had lived here for weeks. They'd walked to the river for water. They'd strung lines, cut brush, moved crates. The ground should have been a ledger of them.

It was blank.

Someone had erased the page. And erasing a page takes time, and time takes men, and men take orders, and no survey team erases its own existence.

Juniper stood at the edge of the camp with the rain starting again somewhere far upstream, a sound like a crowd approaching, and made herself do the thing she least wanted to do: walk in.

* * *

The mess tent first. The flap was tied open, and inside, everything waited.

Cots made—or rather, not slept-in; the bedding was rolled with military precision, corners square. A card table with a radio set on it, antenna coiled beside it like a dead snake. The radio was the expensive kind, the kind with a battery life measured in weeks, and its case was dry despite the humidity, which meant it had been stored properly, which meant whoever packed it away had done it calmly, unhurried, indoors.

Mugs on a shelf. Six mugs, washed, inverted to drain. Six.

She counted them twice. Counting was a pilot's habit—fuel, souls, minutes—and the number stayed six both times, and somehow that was worse than five would have been. Five meant someone might have walked out. Six meant they'd all been here, together, and then they'd all been nowhere.

The supply crates she'd dropped were stacked against the tent wall, opened, inventoried. On top of the nearest crate, weighted down with a stone, someone had left a clipboard. Juniper picked it up. A manifest, in a small precise hand—her brother's kind of handwriting, she thought absurdly, and then hated herself for thinking it, because Daniel was eleven years and an ocean and a whole dead life away, and every meticulous hand in the world was not his.

*Received in good order,* the note read. *Thanks for the lift. — R. Okafor, team lead.*

Dated nine days ago. After the drop. After the glinting metal on the ridge. The handwriting was steady. No tremor, no hurry. Whatever had happened to these people had happened either very fast or very quietly, and the note sitting there under a stone like a receipt suggested they'd expected someone to come looking and had wanted to leave a good impression.

Or someone else had wanted the finder to leave with a good impression.

Juniper set the clipboard down and stepped out into the gray-green light and looked at the equipment rows again with new eyes. Theodolite in its case, latched. Sample kits, sealed. Core boxes, empty. Everything present, everything orderly, everything *staged*. This wasn't a camp. This was a display. Someone had arranged six people's lives into a tableau that said *we left voluntarily, we'll be back, nothing to see here*, and had done it well enough to satisfy a casual search party, and had then scrubbed the ground so thoroughly that even the mud agreed with the story.

Except the mud didn't quite agree. She found it on her third circuit, at the north edge, where the canopy thickened and the light went submarine: a single impression in the soft earth under a root overhang, sheltered from the rain. A boot heel. Deep, sharply defined, recent enough that the edges hadn't slumped. Size twelve, aggressive tread, the kind of boot you bought in a city and wore into country like this to make a point.

One print. One man, walking carefully everywhere else, careless only here, under cover where he thought no sky was watching.

They hadn't vanished. They'd been *taken*, and the taking had been done by professionals who cleaned up after themselves, and one of them had stood right here, under this root, probably watching the camp while his partners worked, and he'd shifted his weight, and the ground had remembered what he wanted forgotten.

Juniper crouched over the print for a long moment, rain beginning to stipple the leaf canopy above, and felt the dread change key. It had been a general dread until now—the diffuse unease of a pilot smelling smoke. Now it narrowed, sharpened, became personal in a way she couldn't yet name but could feel approaching, the way you feel a storm front before the barometer confirms it.

She checked the rest anyway. She was thorough; thoroughness was armor. The latrine trench: unused, or refilled. The fire pit: cold, ash raked flat. The water point at the creek: two aluminum panniers set neatly on the bank, waiting to be filled by hands that never came back for them.

And then, on the card table in the mess tent, where it definitely had not been on her first pass—she was certain of that, certain the way you're certain of a runway you've landed on ten times—there was a compass.

Brass-cased, old-fashioned, the lid dented from some long-ago fall. Not survey equipment. Not government issue. A personal thing, the kind of object a man carries for thirty years until it becomes part of his hand.

She knew it before she touched it. She knew it from across the tent, from the dent alone, from the weight of it in memory, from a kitchen a lifetime ago where a man had taught a girl to read the needle and told her *the country talks, Junie*—

Ravensworth. Engraved on the inside of the lid, in the flowing script her grandfather had used on everything he owned, because in his line of work ownership needed to be provable and deniable in equal measure.

Juniper stood very still. Rain drummed the canvas overhead. Somewhere in the wet dark of the forest a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

Daniel's compass. Daniel, who'd gotten it from their grandfather, who'd carried it through every bad year of their childhood like a talisman, who'd sworn—last time she'd seen him, across a courtroom aisle, eleven years and a lifetime ago—that he'd sell it before he ever spoke to her again.

Her brother was supposed to be in the provincial capital. Married, settled, respectable, a hydrologist with a government salary and a lawn. She'd tracked his career the way you track weather—from a distance, obsessively, telling yourself it was closure. He'd published papers on river contamination. He'd testified before committees. He'd built a clean life on the far side of the wreckage she'd made of their family.

And his compass was lying on a card table in a staged camp in the middle of the drowned interior, six days after six people stopped existing.

The dread bloomed all the way through her then, roots and branches, and she let it, because fighting it would cost more than feeling it. Daniel was here. Or had been. Daniel was part of this—part of the missing team, or part of whatever had happened to them, or part of the machinery that had made them disappear. Every possibility was worse than the last, and none of them changed the arithmetic: the past she'd spent fifteen years outrunning had stopped running behind her and planted itself directly in her path.

She closed the compass lid. The click of it sounded enormous in the tent.

Outside, the rain was strengthening, and beneath the rain, faint and rhythmic, she caught another sound—a boat engine, far downstream, running slow the way engines run when they're being quiet on purpose.

Juniper pocketed the compass, killed the lantern of her expression, and stepped out into the weather to become, once again, nobody in particular.

The engine sound faded upstream. Toward the ridge. Toward wherever the page had been erased from.

She had maybe twenty minutes before anyone reached the camp, and a kilometer of flooded oxbow between her and the plane, and for the first time in fifteen years, Juniper Ravensworth—whoever that name even belonged to anymore—was not sure she wanted to be invisible.

That scared her more than the boot print did.
