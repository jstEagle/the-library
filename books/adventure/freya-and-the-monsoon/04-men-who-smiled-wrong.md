## 4. Men Who Smiled Wrong

The rain came in sideways off the river, and Juniper Ravensworth watched it from the covered porch of the Kestrel Landing trading post with a cup of coffee she'd been nursing for an hour because buying one cup bought her the right to sit.

Three days since the ridge. Three days since the camp with its gear laid out like a museum exhibit and no footprints anywhere in the mud. Three days of telling herself the same story on a loop: *flash flood, they moved out fast, they'll check in from somewhere downriver and this will all be nothing.*

She didn't believe it. She'd stopped believing it somewhere over the second range, flying home through weather that would have grounded a saner pilot, running the image over and over—the compass in her pocket, brass worn smooth at the edges, *RAVENSWORTH* engraved on the back in her father's careful hand. She'd turned it over so many times on the flight back that she'd nearly had to set it down on the instrument panel to work the throttle.

It was sitting in her jacket now, against her ribs, warm as a coal.

Kestrel Landing was the kind of place that existed because the river bent wrong there and boats needed fuel. A fuel dock, a general store that sold everything from outboard oil to canned peaches, six rooms above it for people who couldn't afford to keep moving, and a porch where those people could watch the water and pretend they weren't waiting for anything. Juniper had been coming here for fifteen years, long enough that the store owner, a leathery woman named Petra, knew to leave her alone and refill her coffee without being asked.

That was the arrangement Juniper had built her whole life around: nobody asked, nobody told, everybody left each other's ghosts alone.

Which was why the two men on the porch made her skin prickle before either of them said a word.

They'd arrived an hour ago by boat—she'd heard the engine, noted that it idled too clean for a working boat—and taken the corner table inside with the view of both the dock and the airstrip road. They wore khaki. Not bush khaki, which fades and frays and takes on the color of the country, but catalog khaki, pressed, the kind you buy when you want to look like you belong outdoors without ever having been rained on. Their boots were new. Their hands were soft. One of them had a satellite phone in a leather case on his belt, which cost more than most boats at the dock.

And they were smiling. That was the thing. They were smiling at everyone—at Petra, at the deckhand hauling fuel drums, at a couple of kids fishing off the pilings—with the fixed, generous smile of men who had practiced it in a mirror.

Juniper had grown up around smiles like that. Her father could produce one mid-negotiation that made strangers feel like they'd known him twenty years, right up until they signed something they shouldn't have. She knew exactly what lived underneath a smile like that, and it wasn't warmth.

She should have left that morning. The strip at Kestrel was grass and gravel, marginal even in good weather, and the ceiling had been low all day, but she'd flown worse. She'd flown much worse. There was still fuel in the tanks and a hundred places north where a plane could disappear for a season.

But the compass sat against her ribs, and the missing team's last coordinates sat in her logbook, and some stubborn, stupid part of her—the part that had already started counting days since anyone checked in—wouldn't let her go without knowing whether anyone had come looking from the other direction.

So she stayed. And the men in catalog khaki finished their coffee, and came out onto the porch, and one of them smiled at her.

"Ma'am." He was maybe forty-five, square-jawed, hair cut short enough to be ex-military or ex-something. "Mind if we join you? Porch is getting crowded."

The porch held four empty chairs.

"Help yourself," Juniper said, and went back to her coffee.

He sat anyway, angling his chair toward hers with the easy geometry of a man who'd done this a hundred times. His partner stayed standing near the rail, hands clasped behind his back, watching the dock with the studied casualness of someone who was actually watching her reflection in the window glass.

"Name's Corliss," the seated one said. "This is my colleague Brandt. We're with the Emergency Coordination Office."

"Congratulations," Juniper said.

Corliss laughed like she'd meant it as a joke. Good instincts; he adjusted instantly, kept the smile warm. "We're coordinating the search for the survey team that went missing upriver. Six scientists, government contract. You fly this area, I understand."

"I fly a lot of areas."

"The Cessna on the strip. Tail number ending four-seven-one." He said it without checking anything. "You've been based out of here, what, ten years? Fifteen?"

"Fifteen," Juniper said. *Don't correct him. Don't give him more than he gives you.* But he already had more than she liked, which meant he'd talked to Petra, or pulled the field registry, or both. "If you're looking for someone to fly your grid, I'm not taking contracts right now. Weather's turning."

"We're not hiring pilots." Corliss leaned forward, elbows on knees, voice dropping into something confiding. "We're interviewing everyone who might have had contact with the team. Standard procedure. And your name came up."

"Whose list?"

"Pardon?"

"You said my name came up. On whose list?" She let the silence stretch just past comfortable. "Because I don't remember filing a passenger manifest with any emergency office."

Corliss's smile didn't waver, but something behind it recalibrated. "A supply drop request came through the coordination channel three weeks ago. Six cases, mixed medical and survey equipment, coordinates in the upper Serrano basin. The pilot who accepted the job was registered to this strip." He spread his hands. "That's you."

"It's a big basin."

"It's a precise coordinate set, Ms.—" he glanced at a notebook he hadn't opened until now, a prop, a stage business—"Ravensworth."

There it was. Her name in a stranger's mouth, spoken with the small satisfaction of a man laying a card face-up. Juniper kept her face still and drank her coffee. It had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

"I did a drop," she said. "Weather was closing in. I never saw anyone on the ground. Dropped the crates on the marked panel and went home."

"That's interesting," Corliss said mildly, "because the drop request specified a visual confirmation protocol. Team signals with panels, you confirm personnel on site, you note it in your log."

"My log says what I saw."

"And what did you see?"

Fog. Figures on the ridge—or shapes she'd decided were figures. The glint of something on the far slope that wasn't survey equipment, that caught the light like glass or polished metal, arranged in a line, deliberate. She'd logged *visibility poor, ground contact unconfirmed* and told herself the unease in her stomach was just turbulence.

"Visibility poor," she said. "Ground contact unconfirmed."

Brandt shifted at the rail. Just a weight change, barely audible, but Juniper heard it the way she heard a change in engine note—something off, noted, filed.

"That's unfortunate," Corliss said. "Because the team's beacon stopped transmitting the same afternoon. If you'd seen them—"

"If I'd seen them, I'd have said so." She set the cup down. "Look. I've got a plane to tie down before this wind picks up. You want my log entry, get a warrant and a records request through the aviation authority. Otherwise, I dropped crates in fog and flew home. That's the whole story."

"Of course." Corliss stood, smoothing his trousers, the smile returning full strength. "One more thing, though. Routine." He produced a photograph—not printed, on a tablet, which was itself a tell; nobody doing real field rescue carried a tablet into monsoon country. He turned the screen toward her. "Any of these faces familiar?"

Six headshots. Government ID photos, stiff and flat. Juniper looked at them the way she'd look at a chart, methodical, top left to bottom right, letting her eyes pass over each face for exactly as long as attention required and no longer.

Five strangers.

The sixth man, bottom row, third position—older than his photo, thinner, hair going gray at the temples—was her brother.

Daniel Ravensworth. Fifteen years older than the last time she'd seen him, which had been across a courtroom, on the other side, while she destroyed what was left of their family with her own sworn testimony. He'd stared at her the whole time she spoke. She'd never once looked back. She'd walked out of that courthouse, changed her surname at the first registry office past the provincial line, and never used the word *brother* again.

Now his face was on a stranger's tablet, listed among the missing, and the compass with her family name burned against her ribs like it had been heated on a stove.

"No," Juniper said.

"You're sure? Take another—"

"I'm sure." She stood, unhurried, gathering nothing because she'd brought nothing out but herself. "Bad weather coming. Good luck with your search."

"Ms. Ravensworth." Corliss's voice followed her, pleasant, unhurried. "The coordination office will be in the area for some time. If you think of anything else—anything at all—we're staying at the post. Room's under Corliss."

"Memorable name," Juniper said, and walked down the steps into the rain.

* * *

She tied down the Cessna with movements her hands knew better than her head did—double loop, tension check, pitot cover, control lock—and let the work hold the shaking off for as long as it could.

It didn't hold long.

Under the high wing, out of sight of the post, she finally let herself breathe the way you breathe after a near miss in the air: fast, shallow, hand braced on the strut. Rain drummed the fabric overhead. Down the slope, the river ran brown and swollen, carrying the season toward the coast, and somewhere upstream in that drowned green country was a camp with no footprints and a team of six who had stopped existing the same afternoon she'd flown away from them.

From Daniel.

She'd known, in the fog, on the ridge. Some animal part of her had known, the way you know a storm cell before the radar paints it. And she'd logged *ground contact unconfirmed* and flown home, because confirming meant engaging, and engaging meant the past, and the past was the one country she'd spent fifteen years mapping every route around.

*No,* she'd said. Flat, immediate, bored. It had come out of her mouth smooth as oiled cable, and the worst part—the part making her sick now, in the rain, alone with her plane—was how easy it had been. No hesitation. No heat. The lie had been sitting there fully formed, waiting, like a tool hung on a peg in a shed she'd sworn she'd burned down years ago.

She'd lied to federal investigators once before, too. Different life, different name, nineteen years old and terrified, lying about cargo manifests while her father watched from the gallery with that smile, that mirror-practiced smile, proud of her. She'd testified eventually—that was the whole catastrophe—but the lying had come first, and it had come easily then too, and she'd told herself for fifteen years that the girl who lied like that was gone, buried under a new name in a new province, dead and decomposed.

She wasn't dead. She'd felt her wake up on that porch, fluent and calm, denying her own brother's face.

Juniper pressed her forehead against the cold strut and let the rain have her for a minute.

Then she straightened up, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and did what she always did with unbearable things: broke them into tasks.

Task one: the compass. It couldn't stay on her body if those men searched her—and they would search her, eventually, because Corliss hadn't believed her any more than she'd believed him, and men like Corliss had resources and patience. She pried loose the inspection panel behind the rear baggage bulkhead, the one Petra's mechanic had never bothered to re-torque properly, and wrapped the compass in a rag from her kit, and wedged it into the airframe where only someone who knew that particular airplane would ever think to look. Her father's handwriting disappeared into the wing spar's shadow. It felt like burying him twice.

Task two: information. Corliss had said *emergency coordination office*, and Juniper had spent enough years around officialdom to know the phrase meant nothing and everything. Real rescue coordination ran on radio schedules, grid maps, volunteer rosters—chaotic, exhausted, cash-poor. It did not run on satellite phones, tablets, pressed khaki, and questions asked in precisely the right order. Those men weren't searching for the survey team. Those men were searching for whatever the survey team had found, or seen, or recorded—and for anyone who might know about it.

Which meant the team hadn't simply vanished. Which meant someone wanted them vanished.

Which meant the glinting thing on the ridge—the line of glass and metal she'd pretended was fog-shimmer—had been worth killing for, or worth disappearing people for, and she had flown away from it with a logbook full of coordinates.

Her stomach dropped the way it dropped in downdrafts, that hollow elevator lurch, except there was no stick to push forward, no altitude to trade. She was on the ground. Grounded by weather, grounded by the wrecked strip conditions upstream, grounded by the simple fact that the fastest way out of this valley was also the most visible thing in it, and visible was the last thing she could afford to be for a few days.

*Few days,* she thought. *Wait out the front, then go.*

But even as she framed the thought, she knew it for what it was—the same lie in a different coat. *Wait out the front.* She'd been waiting out fronts for fifteen years. Waiting out her father's trial, waiting out the news coverage, waiting out the anniversary articles, waiting out every rumor of her family's old associates surfacing in some port town. Always a few days, always a little longer, until the waiting became the life.

Rain hammered the wing above her. Somewhere upriver, her brother was either dead or alive, and both possibilities demanded the same thing from her: that she stop pretending she hadn't seen his face.

She secured the panel, wiped her prints off the screws out of pure habit—old-life habit, always another old-life habit—and walked back toward the post through the downpour, because there was one more task, and it was the hardest one.

Task three: find out who Corliss really was, without letting Corliss find out who she really was.

* * *

Petra was restocking shelves when Juniper came in dripping, and the old woman's eyes flicked from Juniper's face to the window, to the corner table where Brandt sat alone now, tablet face-down beside his untouched coffee, and back.

"They booked two rooms," Petra said quietly, stacking cans without looking up. "Paid cash. Cash, up here, in monsoon season. Nobody pays cash unless they don't want a trail."

"Who else have they talked to?"

"The deckhand. The fish-and-chips kid. Me." She set a can down harder than necessary. "Asked me how long you'd been based here, whether you kept to a schedule, whether you had family in the province." Her jaw tightened. "I told them you were a quiet woman who paid her bills. Which is true, and none of their business."

"Thanks, Petra."

"Don't thank me." The old woman finally looked at her, and her eyes were sharp and unafraid, the eyes of someone who'd watched thirty years of men come up this river thinking money made them invisible. "Thanked me once already, girl, first year you landed here half-frozen. I don't ask what you ran from. But I'll tell you what I see. Those two aren't looking for anybody. Looking-for-somebody men are worried men. Those two are *patient* men. There's a difference, and it matters."

Juniper nodded slowly. Patient men. Men with expense accounts and timetables and a list, and her name on it, and her brother's face on a tablet.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Room two. Corliss, at the window, watching the strip. Watching her plane.

*Let him watch it,* she thought. *It's just a plane. It's just a tired Cessna on a grass strip, and I'm just a freight dog who flies medicine and fuel and keeps to herself.*

Except the compass was hidden in its belly, and the coordinates were in her logbook, and the truth was that she was the last person to see six missing people—including the brother whose face she'd denied knowing—and two patient men in catalog khaki were between her and the whole drowned interior of the province, and the monsoon had only just begun.

She climbed the stairs to her own room, number five, end of the hall, and locked the door, and sat on the bed with the lamp off, listening to the rain and to the small sounds of the building settling around her.

Through the wall, faintly, she heard Corliss's voice, low and level, speaking into a phone. She couldn't make out words. She didn't need to. She knew the cadence—reporting, updating, reassuring someone far away that the situation was contained, that the local elements were cooperative, that the pilot had seen nothing.

*The pilot had seen nothing.*

Juniper lay back and stared at the ceiling and hated herself with a clean, cold efficiency, because tomorrow she would do it again. She would smile at them over breakfast and answer their questions with nothing shaped like something, and she would be good at it, and every hour she spent being good at it would take her further from the woman she'd promised herself she'd become and closer to the one she'd fled.

Unless.

Unless she stopped using the old skills to hide and started using them to hunt.

The thought arrived quietly, the way storm cells arrive on a clear horizon—a darkening at the edge, easy to ignore, impossible to ignore. She could play their game. She could let them believe she was fooled. She could learn their schedule, their boat, their frequency, their employer, while wearing the mask they expected: dumb bush pilot, harmless, forgettable.

It was what her father would have done. That was the problem. Every clever bone in her body was an inheritance.

Outside, thunder rolled up the valley, and the rain thickened from heavy to vertical, and the river below the post rose another inch against the pilings with a sound like slow swallowing.

In room two, the phone call ended. Silence. Then footsteps crossing to the window, pausing there a long moment—looking out, she knew, at the strip, at the small wet shape of her airplane.

Juniper closed her eyes.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow the front would hit full force, and the strip would flood, and everything would be decided by water and patience.

She just hoped she'd be gone before the water won.
