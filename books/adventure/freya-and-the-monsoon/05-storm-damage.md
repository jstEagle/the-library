## 5. Storm Damage

The sky had been lying to her all morning.

Juniper had learned to read weather the way other people read faces—quickly, suspiciously, with an eye for what was being concealed. The band of cloud sitting over the eastern ranges looked like any other monsoon buildup: tall, bruised, theatrical. But underneath it the air had gone flat and heavy, a stillness that had nothing to do with calm. It was the stillness of something holding its breath.

She'd loaded the last of the med-drop crates at dawn—antibiotics for Kessara Landing, insulin for the mission at Two Rivers, a box of sutures someone had labeled in handwriting that slanted like it was running away from itself—and she'd sat in the Cessna's pilot seat for ten full minutes with her hand on the throttle, doing the arithmetic every bush pilot does and pretends not to.

Fly now, ahead of it. Or wait it out and lose two days.

Two days meant two villages without medicine. She went.

The first hour was fine. The second hour was the sky changing its mind.

* * *

It came in from the northeast the way water comes through a failed dam—not gradually, not with warning, but all at once, as if it had simply been waiting for permission. One moment Juniper was threading between towers of cloud with the river glittering below like spilled mercury; the next, the world turned the color of wet slate and the rain hit the windshield so hard it sounded like gravel.

She'd flown in monsoons before. Fifteen years of them. She knew the drill: slow down, trust the instruments, don't fight the updrafts, find your hole. What she hadn't counted on—the thing no one counts on until it happens to them—was the wind shear coming off the ranges sideways, a wall of displaced air that took the Cessna by the wings and shoved her four hundred feet down like a hand closing over a moth.

The stall warning shrieked. She pushed the nose over, felt the wings bite again, hauled back up into air that felt less like air and more like a river she was swimming through.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the fear in its cage. "Okay. You're fine. We're fine."

Below her, the landscape she'd known for fifteen years was unrecognizing itself. Rivers she could have navigated blind were swollen past their banks, braided into brown lakes that swallowed fields, roads, the low ground where houses used to be. A village roofline became an island. A football pitch became open water. The whole interior was drowning in real time, and there was nothing anyone could do about it except get out from under it.

Kessara Landing's strip was a dirt ribbon on a levee above the flood line. In dry season it was marginal. Today it would be a coin flip.

She keyed the radio out of habit more than hope. "Kessara, Kessara, this is Charlie-November-Four-Seven-One, inbound from the north, requesting wind and strip condition."

Static. Then, faintly, a voice she didn't know—some relief coordinator three hundred miles away, relaying traffic for half the province. "—all strips south of the range reported under water, advise divert, advise—"

The transmission dissolved into static, and the static dissolved into the roar of the rain.

Divert. To where? Every strip south of the range was underwater. Behind her was the storm front, a hundred miles of rotating black. Below her was Kessara, or what was left of it, and a levee strip that might be mud, might be standing water, might be gone entirely.

Her fuel gauge said she didn't get to be choosy.

* * *

She came in low over the flooded fields to look, the way you'd test ice with one foot.

The strip was there. Barely. The levee had held along its length except for a section near the northern end where the water had found a way through and was pouring across the surface in a shallow, fast-moving sheet, turning the packed earth beneath into something between soil and soup. The southern third looked solid. Dark, soaked, but solid.

Wind out of the northeast at—she squinted at the drift, did the math—twenty-five gusting forty, quartering tailwind if she landed to the south. Landing downwind on a short, wet strip. Every instructor she'd ever had, living or dead, would have told her to go around and take her chances with the storm.

Every instructor she'd ever had had never watched a village run out of antibiotics.

"Stupid," she muttered, and brought her around anyway.

The approach was ugly. The rain came in horizontal sheets, and twice the wind picked the Cessna up by one wing and tried to set her down sideways. She fought it the way she'd fought a thousand landings—with hands and feet and a cold, narrow attention that shut out everything in the world except the strip, the speed, the sink rate. This was the part of flying she'd never stopped loving even when she'd stopped loving everything else: the total occupation of it. There was no room in a crosswind landing for the past. No room for names, or testimony, or brothers. Just the machine and the air and the ground coming up to meet her, and her job, which was to make all three agree.

She flared over the flooded section, held it, held it, felt the mains touch down on the southern third with a jolt that ran up her spine—

—and heard the sound.

Pilots know their airplanes the way they know their own bodies. There are sounds that mean nothing and sounds that mean everything, and this one—a crack, deep and structural, followed by a grinding shudder through the airframe—was the second kind. The left gear. Something in the left gear had let go.

The Cessna slewed, dug in, and Juniper stood on the right rudder pedal with everything she had, riding the skid as the plane fishtailed down the saturated strip, mud and water fountaining past the windows, the propeller chewing a rooster tail of brown spray, until finally, mercifully, the tailwheel caught and the aircraft swung around and stopped, nose pointed back the way it came, engine idling, rain hammering the roof like a fist demanding entry.

She sat there for a long moment, hands welded to the yoke, listening to her own heartbeat slow down.

Then she cut the mixture and got out into the rain to look at the damage.

* * *

The left main gear had cracked at the strut fitting—a clean break, the kind that comes from metal fatigue meeting bad luck. The wheel had folded up under the wing and plowed a furrow thirty feet long through the mud. When she crouched down and looked at the fracture, she could see the old corrosion ghosting through the metal, rust blooming along the grain like a stain spreading through cloth.

She'd been meaning to replace that strut fitting for eight months.

That was the sentence she'd been carrying around in her head for years, in one form or another. *I've been meaning to.* Meaning to overhaul the engine. Meaning to file the paperwork that would make her registration bulletproof. Meaning to leave this country before the rains, the way she always swore she would, and never once had. Fifteen years of *meaning to*, and the bill had just come due on a flooded strip at the edge of nowhere, in the middle of the worst storm of the season.

She stood in the downpour and laughed, once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it.

Because here was the truth of it, laid out in broken metal at her feet: she wasn't going anywhere. Not today, not tomorrow, probably not for weeks. The strut fitting wasn't a field repair. It needed a machinist, a parts order, a hangar—and every airstrip that had a hangar was on the far side of a hundred miles of flooded country, reachable only by boat, if the boats were running, which during the monsoon they mostly weren't.

She was grounded. Properly, structurally, undeniably grounded.

Juniper Ravensworth—who hadn't slept two consecutive nights in the same place since she was twenty-four, who measured her life in miles precisely so she wouldn't have to measure it in years, who had built an entire existence out of the space between departures—was stuck.

* * *

She got the plane chocked and tied down as best she could, though tying down felt like a joke against wind like this. The villagers came out when the worst of the rain eased—an old man named Sombath who ran the med station, two teenagers who helped her wrestle the tie-down ropes, a cluster of kids watching from the eaves of the clinic with the solemn fascination children reserve for disasters that aren't theirs.

"The medicine," Sombath said, when she handed him the crates. He held the antibiotic box against his chest like something rescued from a fire. "You came through that sky for this."

"It was on the way," Juniper said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and he smiled at her the way people smile at liars whose lies are kind.

"You'll stay? The storm—" He gestured at the horizon, where the next band of cloud was already stacking up, purple-black and patient. "There is more coming. Much more."

"I'll stay till I can't fly," she said. "Looks like that's already decided."

He studied her face, then the plane, then the bent gear hanging crooked under the wing, and he nodded slowly, the way men nod when they understand more than they've been told. "The water is rising," he said. "By tomorrow it will reach the clinic steps. By the day after, maybe the strip." He paused. "You should think about the boat."

"I don't do boats."

"No," he agreed mildly. "I have noticed this about you. But the river does not care what we prefer." He carried the crates inside, and that was the end of the conversation, and Juniper stood alone in the mud with her ruined airplane and felt the walls of the world drawing in around her like a fist closing.

* * *

That night she slept—or lay—in the Cessna's cabin, because the clinic was full of patients and she had never in her life been good at accepting shelter. The rain came and went in waves, sometimes soft, sometimes with a violence that rocked the fuselage on its gear, and each time the plane moved she woke with her heart slamming, reaching for controls that weren't there.

Grounded.

She turned the word over in the dark like a stone with a sharp edge. It wasn't just the plane. She understood that now, lying in the smell of wet canvas and avgas. It was the season. The monsoon had arrived in earnest, and the whole interior was converting itself into a single inland sea. The rivers were rising. The trails were gone. In a week, maybe less, the only way in or out of this entire region would be by water—and the water belonged to other people. Boatmen, traders, officials, whoever else was willing to move through the flood. People who asked questions. People who could be bought.

For fifteen years her safety had rested on a simple architecture: she was hard to find because she was always leaving. No fixed address, no pattern, no history visible from the ground. The cockpit had been the cleanest room in her life—up there, at altitude, she was nobody's daughter, nobody's witness, nobody's debt. Just a callsign and a heading.

Now the sky was closed, and she was a woman sleeping in a broken airplane on a drowning strip, and every mile between her and the capital had become a mile of water.

She thought about the survey team again. She'd been trying not to. The radio chatter had picked at it for days—*missing party, six persons, government contract, last contact the ridge coordinates*—and she'd filed it away the way she filed away everything that didn't fit inside a flight plan. But tonight, trapped, with nothing between her and her own thoughts except the rain, the file opened itself.

Six people, somewhere out in that drowned wilderness, past their check-in window. And those figures on the ridge she'd glimpsed through the fog on the supply drop, standing too still, beside equipment that glinted wrong. She'd told herself it was nothing. She was good at telling herself things were nothing; it was practically a professional skill.

But lying in the dark with the water audibly rising around her, she found she couldn't make herself believe it anymore.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the eye of some larger rotation passing overhead, and Juniper climbed out into the mud to take stock.

The strip was worse. The sheet of water that had crossed the northern end yesterday had widened overnight; the flood was climbing the levee in slow, patient increments, the way water does, indifferent to schedules and intentions. By afternoon, Sombath's prediction would be true. By tomorrow, the Cessna would be sitting in standing water to its hubs, and after that it wouldn't matter how good the machinist was, because the strip itself would be gone until the dry season.

Months. That was the honest number. Months, unless she moved the plane soon to whatever marginally higher ground existed, and moving it required gear she didn't have and a strip that didn't exist.

She walked the levee with her hands in her pockets and made herself look at it plainly, the way she looked at weather: this is what is true, now plan around it.

True: she was grounded indefinitely. True: the monsoon had weeks left in it, and the rivers would rise before they fell. True: the only road out was the river, and the river meant boats, and boats meant people, and people meant questions, and questions were the one cargo she had spent fifteen years refusing to carry.

And true—one more thing, small and cold, sitting at the bottom of the list like a stone in a boot: the missing survey team had gone missing near coordinates she knew. Coordinates she had personally flown supplies to. Coordinates where she had seen something glinting that wasn't survey equipment, and had flown away from, because flying away was what she did.

She stopped at the edge of the levee and looked downriver. The water was high and brown and fast, carrying branches, carrying roofs, carrying the whole disordered wreckage of the interior toward the coast. Somewhere down that river was the trading post. Somewhere beyond it, the capital, the newspapers, the world where things could still be said out loud. And somewhere upstream, in country that was now mostly water, six people were either dead or hiding or taken, and the government was sending polite men with polished shoes to ask precise questions about who had seen what.

Juniper had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of not being part of anything. It had worked. It had cost exactly what it always costs, which was everything, paid slowly.

Now the sky had closed, the water was rising, and the choice she'd spent a decade and a half avoiding was walking toward her at the speed of a river.

Down at the dock below the levee—she could see it from here, a rough timber platform half-submerged, with a couple of pirogues tied alongside and, further out, a long lean riverboat riding at anchor, dark-hulled and battered, looking like it had survived things most boats hadn't—figures were moving. Loading. Preparing.

Someone was going downriver.

Juniper stood in the mud with the rain starting again on the back of her neck, and watched the boat, and did not move, and understood perfectly well that she was already lost.
