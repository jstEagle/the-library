## 6. The Stranger at the Dock

Tomas had been living on the *Cormorant* for eleven days when the woman came down to the water arguing with three men in matching jackets.

He noticed the jackets first. That was the thing about the flooded season—nobody wore matching anything. The monsoon stripped people down to what they owned and could carry: rubber boots gone grey with river mud, shirts stiff with mildew, rain capes patched with tar and prayer. Matching jackets meant an organization. An organization meant money. Money this far upriver, in the middle of a monsoon that had drowned half the district's landing strips, meant somebody wanted something badly enough to pay for it.

The three men stood at the head of the trading post dock under a canvas awning, dry as accountants. The woman stood in the rain without a hood, which told him something too. She was tall, weathered brown, mid-thirties maybe, with the kind of stillness he'd learned to read in border towns and inspection offices—the stillness of someone who counted exits.

"—told you everything I saw," she was saying as Tomas came closer, pretending to check his mooring line. "Camp intact. Gear laid out. No tracks. That was four days ago and I've said it twice since."

"And we appreciate your cooperation, Ms.—"

"Ravensworth." A beat, half a second too long. "That's the name I fly under."

Tomas kept his eyes on the cleat. *Fly under.* Not *my name is.* He filed it away the way he used to file discrepancies in shipping manifests—one small wrongness, noted, dated, waiting for its brothers.

"Ms. Ravensworth," the lead man repeated smoothly. He was the polished one, silver at the temples, a smile that arrived on schedule like a ferry. "We just need to confirm a few details about your flight path. The exact coordinates of the drop. What you observed from the air. Whether you approached the camp on foot."

"I walked in. I told you that."

"How far did you walk?"

"Why does it matter how far?"

"It matters because—" The smile recalibrated. "Because the search grid depends on accurate information. These are trained surveyors, Ms. Ravensworth. Six of them. Families are worried."

"Families are always worried," she said. "That's not why you're here."

The silence that followed lasted two seconds and contained a whole conversation. Tomas had spent fourteen years inside government offices where silences were load-bearing structures; he knew when one was holding something up. The polished man's smile didn't move but his weight shifted back onto his heels, and the younger one by the rail—a hard-faced man with a radio clipped to his shoulder like a holstered weapon—turned his head a few degrees toward the boat.

Tomas became very interested in a knot in his mooring line.

*You're out,* he reminded himself. *Fourteen years, one signature on a termination letter, and you're out. Whatever this is, it floats past you.*

But he'd thought that at the checkpoint outside Karst Hollow too, right before the men in clean boots asked to see his papers and then photographed them anyway, and he'd learned since that *out* was a direction, not a place. You were never fully out. You were just between questions.

The woman turned away from the three men—not storming off, nothing so legible as anger, just turning, the way a person turns when a conversation has stopped being worth the calories—and came down the dock toward the boats. Toward his boat.

She stopped three meters short, which he respected. Three meters was far enough to talk and close enough to see hands.

"You're the leaky one," she said.

"That's me."

"The one who told the postmaster he was going downriver tomorrow."

"Did I tell him that?"

"He says you did."

"The postmaster also says the beer here is cold." Tomas straightened up, gave her the full measure of his attention, and watched her take her own measure of him in return. Her eyes went to his hands first—he approved of that, hands told you things faces wouldn't—then to the boat, cataloguing. He let her. The *Cormorant* was twenty-two feet of river workhorse, aluminum hull gone dull as old pewter, a canopy of blue tarp stretched over a frame welded from scrap, an outboard that started on the third pull if you spoke kindly to it and the fifth if you didn't. She was ugly and she leaked and she had never once left him stranded, which was more than he could say for any institution he'd ever worked for.

"Where downriver?" the woman asked.

"Depends who's asking."

"Nobody's asking. I'm asking."

He almost smiled. "Confluence at Serrano Bend. Maybe farther, if the water holds."

"The water won't hold. Monsoon breaks in three weeks, maybe less. After that the tributaries drop and everything above the gorge is mud and mosquitoes until next season." She said it flatly, a pilot reciting weather, but something underneath the flatness was moving fast. He could see it working behind her face like current under ice.

"So whatever you're doing," Tomas said, "you're doing it wet or not at all."

Her jaw tightened. "I'm not doing anything. My plane's cracked on the Kessel strip. Landing gear took the hit when the floodwater undercut the surface. She's grounded until it dries out and I can get parts, and parts come from Serrano, and Serrano's a week by boat."

"Two days if you know the channels."

"Do you know the channels?"

"I've been running this river since before you had a license, probably."

Something flickered across her face—not offense, exactly. Recalculation. He watched her decide whether to be insulted and then decide she couldn't afford it, and he recognized the arithmetic because he did it daily now: *what do I need, what will it cost, can I pay it without giving away more than I buy.* It was the arithmetic of people with something to hide, and he'd worn its expression in mirrors for years.

"Passage costs?" she said.

"Fuel share. Hands on the bail bucket. And you don't ask me questions I don't ask you."

"That last part free?"

"That last part's the price."

They looked at each other across the three meters and the rain. Behind them, up on the dock, the three matching jackets were watching. Not pretending otherwise anymore, either—the polished one had a phone out, angled low, and the hard-faced one hadn't taken his eyes off the boat since the woman turned away. Tomas felt their attention land on his back like a hand.

"They'll follow you," he said quietly. "Whatever you told them, they don't believe it, and they don't believe you're just waiting for parts."

"I know."

"They photograph everyone who leaves this post. Did you know that?"

"I watched them do it to the mail runner yesterday."

"And you're still standing here talking to me."

"I'm still standing here because my other option is standing there." She tipped her chin at the dock without turning around, which took a certain kind of nerve, or a certain kind of practice. "And those men asked me three times how far I walked into the bush. Nobody asks that question three times unless the answer matters more than the missing people do."

Tomas looked at her properly then. Rain ran off her jaw and dripped from the collar of a flight jacket old enough to have outlived two fashion eras. There was mud dried in the creases of her knuckles and a healing scratch along one forearm, thorn-height, bush-height. But none of that was what made him speak.

What made him speak was the look. He'd seen it before, in the faces of witnesses who came to his office to report contamination and then glanced at the door; in the face of a young clerk who'd found names in a ledger she wasn't supposed to open and resigned by lunchtime; once, memorably, in his own reflection in a darkened office window the night he decided not to sign the Karst Hollow clearance. It was the look of a person calculating the distance between themselves and the horizon and finding it insufficient. The look of someone running—not from a place, from a *fact*. Places you could leave. Facts followed.

"You saw something out there," he said. "At that camp."

"I told those men what I saw."

"You told them the truth. That's different from telling them what you saw."

For a moment he thought she'd walk away. Her weight shifted toward the balls of her feet, the way a person shifts when every instinct says *go*, and he understood that he had perhaps four seconds before she vanished into the flooded streets of the trading post and became one more rumor the river swallowed.

"I'm leaving at first light," he said. "There's room for one passenger and her gear. I run quiet, I know the cut-offs, and I owe no allegiance to anyone wearing a matching jacket. That's the whole offer. It expires when the sun does."

"Why?" she said. "Why offer? You don't know me."

"No."

"People don't do favors on this river for free."

"People don't," he agreed. "This isn't a favor. This is a bet."

"On what?"

"On the idea that whoever those men work for sent them a thousand kilometers into a monsoon to interview one bush pilot, and that a bush pilot who makes that kind of trouble worth a thousand kilometers is probably on the side I like." He shrugged. "Or I'm wrong, and you're trouble, and I'll put you ashore at the first village and forget your face. I'm good at forgetting faces. It's a professional skill."

"What profession is that?"

"The one I don't have anymore."

Something moved through her expression then, quick as a fish under the surface—recognition, maybe, of a shape she knew. Two strangers on a dock, both fluent in the grammar of not-answering, both doing the same arithmetic. He watched her weigh it: the cost of trusting a stranger against the cost of staying where the matching jackets could keep asking their three-times-asked question.

Up on the dock, the polished man closed his phone. The gesture had a finality to it, a period at the end of a sentence, and Tomas had seen enough sentences end that way to know what kind of paragraph came next.

"They're coming down," he said.

The woman didn't look. "I noticed."

"Decide."

Rain drummed on the tarp overhead. Somewhere across the flooded street a generator coughed and caught, and the trading post's single electric bulb buzzed on behind its film of moisture, throwing weak yellow light across water the color of steeped tea. The *Cormorant* knocked gently against the pilings, patient, indifferent, leaking her slow leak into the river that would carry her.

"First light," Juniper Ravensworth said. "I'll bring fuel share. And I bail my own watch."

"You'll bail double. You're getting passage and my silence."

"My silence isn't yours to sell."

"No," Tomas said. "But it's mine to give, and I'm giving it, and that costs extra."

The ghost of something crossed her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if she remembered how. Then footsteps on the dock planks, unhurried, confident, the tread of men who had never once had to hurry, and the polished voice arriving ahead of its owner like perfume:

"Ms. Ravensworth. One last question, if we could—"

"She's done answering questions today," Tomas said, without turning around. "Come back tomorrow. Or don't. The river doesn't keep office hours."

A pause. He felt the man reassessing him, reclassifying him, sliding him into some mental folder—*boat trash, irrelevant, handle later*—and he let him, because being misfiled was a survival skill too, and he'd been misfiling himself for fourteen years.

"And you are?" the polished man asked.

"Nobody," Tomas said. "Ask the postmaster."

* * *

He heard them leave an hour later—the whine of an outboard bigger than it needed to be, heading not downriver but up, toward the ridge country, and he marked that too. Men claiming to coordinate a rescue going *away* from the missing people's last known position. In his old life that would have been exhibit C in a report nobody would read.

Juniper Ravensworth came back after dark. He heard her before he saw her, a soft knock of knuckle on the hull, and she stood at the edge of the lamplight with a jerry can in each hand and a duffel over one shoulder, and she said only, "Still expiring at sunrise?"

"Sunrise," he said, and cleared a space under the tarp, and did not ask her a single question, and watched her not ask him any, and understood that he had just tied his small leaking life to a woman carrying something heavy enough that three men in matching jackets had crossed a drowned province to ask her the same question three times.

She stowed her gear with a pilot's economy—everything strapped, nothing loose—and lay back against her duffel with her eyes open, watching the rain shadow dance on the tarp.

"One rule on my boat," Tomas said, killing the lamp. "If we're boarded, you let me talk."

"Planning on being boarded?"

"Planning on being ready."

In the dark, the river breathed against the hull. He listened to her breathing slow toward sleep—or toward the imitation of sleep that runners practice—and he stared up at the underside of the tarp and thought about the look on her face, the horizon-measuring look, the fact-fleeing look, and about how the last time he'd trusted a stranger on water, the stranger had been carrying documents that ended a career and started this exile.

First light was six hours away. He intended to spend all of them awake.

And when the sun finally came up grey and dripping over the flooded trees, and Juniper Ravensworth stepped aboard without looking back at the dock, and the *Cormorant* slid out into the brown current with three men already watching from the shore, phones already raised—Tomas pushed the throttle forward and understood, with the calm of a man long past surprise, that whatever she was carrying, it had just become his to carry too.
