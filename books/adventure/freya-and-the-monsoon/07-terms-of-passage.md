## 7. Terms of Passage

The rain came down in ropes.

Juniper stood under the corrugated awning of the trading post's loading dock and watched the river eat the world. Two days ago it had been a brown ribbon below the bank. Now it was a lake with opinions, swallowing fence posts, the low field, half the mission road. A dugout canoe drifted past upside down, spinning slowly, like something dead deciding which way to fall.

Her plane sat on the flooded strip a quarter mile east, one wheel buried to the axle in standing water, its landing gear cracked like a wishbone. She'd looked at it that morning and felt something she hadn't allowed herself to feel in fifteen years: stuck.

"You're still here."

She didn't turn. She'd heard him come around the corner of the building—Tomas moved quiet for a big man, but not that quiet—but she let him think he'd surprised her. It cost nothing to let people believe small things about you.

"Planes don't swim," she said.

"No," he agreed. "They don't."

He came to stand at the edge of the awning, hands in the pockets of a canvas jacket gone dark with damp. He didn't look at her. He watched the water the way she did, like a man reading a document written in a language he mostly knew. Behind them, through the post's fly-specked window, the two men who called themselves rescue coordinators were still drinking sweet tea with the post manager, still smiling their wrong smiles. She could see Voss's reflection in the glass—Voss, the polished one, the one who said *we just want to help* with his eyes on your hands.

"They asked me about you again this morning," Tomas said.

"I know. I was there."

"They asked where you'd sleep tonight."

Juniper turned then, finally, and looked at him. He had a face built for weathering—deep lines, a jaw that had been broken once and set slightly proud of true. His eyes were the color of the river before it went bad: ordinary brown, patient, hiding things.

"And what did you tell them?" she asked.

"That I didn't know. That bush pilots sleep wherever the fuel is." He shrugged. "It's even true, probably."

"Probably."

"Is it?"

She smiled without warmth. "You've been asking questions since the day we met, Tomas. You ask them slow, like you're not asking. But you're always asking."

"Yes," he said simply. No denial, no deflection. It caught her off balance, the plainness of it. Most men lied by reflex. This one seemed to regard lying as an expense he avoided unless the purchase was necessary.

"So ask," she said.

He considered the water another moment. Then: "You found the survey camp."

It wasn't a question either, but she let him have it. "Everyone knows I made the last drop. The coordinators made sure of that. They announced it at the dock like a prize raffle."

"Everyone knows you made the drop. Not everyone knows you went back." He glanced at her sideways. "Your boots have red clay on them. There's exactly one place within walking distance of here with clay that color—the ridge above the north bend. You came back from there yesterday at dusk, soaked, carrying nothing, and you haven't told anyone where you went. And this morning when Voss mentioned the missing team, you stopped chewing."

Juniper looked down at her boots. Red clay, dried to a pale crust along the welts. She'd cleaned the compass, wrapped it in oilcloth, buried it in her duffel under a false layer of spare socks. She'd been careful. She'd thought she'd been careful.

Fifteen years of being careful, and one pair of boots.

"What do you want?" she said quietly.

"A ride isn't possible anymore. Your plane's done until the water drops, and the water won't drop for weeks." He nodded toward the dock, where a boat sat low against its moorings—a broad-beamed river workboat, forty feet of scarred steel and stubbornness, its paint peeling back to rust in long stripes. Water seeped from somewhere near its stern in a thin, constant thread. "That's the *Mariana*. She leaks, but she floats. I'm taking her downriver tomorrow at first light, before the checkpoint at Kilometer Forty gets reinforced. There's a man in the capital who needs to see some documents I'm carrying, and after the monsoon breaks, no boat gets through for six months. So it's now or never."

"The capital." She kept her voice flat. "What documents?"

"My own business." He said it without apology. "Same as yours is yours."

"I didn't say I had any."

"No," Tomas agreed. "You didn't."

* * *

They negotiated on the dock, because neither of them trusted interiors. Rain drummed the tin roof above them and ran off the edges in beaded curtains, giving them a room made of water.

"Passage costs," Juniper said. "I can pay."

"I don't want money."

"Everybody wants money."

"Not for this." He crouched, checked a tie-down on a crate lashed amidships—she noted the crate, noted how carefully he checked it, noted that he positioned himself between her and it while doing so—and stood again. "I want hands. The *Mariana* takes two to run properly in flood current. Bailing, watch shifts, the locks at the old weir if they're still manned. I can't do it alone and I don't trust hired crew this season. Half the dock workers are on somebody's payroll, and I don't know whose."

"So you want labor."

"I want someone who doesn't leak," he said. "And you—" he looked at her directly for the first time since he'd arrived, and she felt the weight of it, the assessment, "—you don't leak. Whatever else you are, you keep your mouth shut and your eyes open. I watched you lie to those two men for ten minutes without blinking. That's worth more than money right now."

"You enjoyed watching that, did you."

"No," he said. "It worried me. People who lie that well learned it somewhere expensive."

The rain filled the silence between them. Out on the drowned strip, her Cessna sat with its gear bent, looking less like an aircraft every hour and more like a grave marker for the version of her life that had worked.

"Terms," Juniper said. "One. I pick my own stops. You're going downriver; I may need to go up one tributary or another. When I say stop, you stop, no questions about why."

Tomas's face didn't change, but something behind it did—a settling, like a man confirming a suspicion he'd already priced in. "Where upriver?" he asked.

"I said no questions."

"You said no questions about *why*." His voice stayed mild. "The *where* matters, Juniper. The north bend is four days' travel against current. That's not a stop, that's a detour. And everything between here and the capital is getting watched closer every day. So yes—I need the where."

She weighed it. Give him the camp and she gave him the compass, the neat gear, the mud without footprints, all of it, because he'd already assembled most of the pieces anyway. Keep it from him and she'd be spending energy on concealment she'd need for survival—and he'd know she was spending it, which was its own kind of telling.

Compromise, then. Her old life had taught her that too: never lie when a smaller truth will carry the load.

"There's something up there I left behind," she said. "Something I need to collect before anyone else finds it. After that—straight through to the capital. Same as you."

"Something you left behind," Tomas repeated. "On a ridge where a government survey team vanished out of intact campsites without leaving footprints."

"If you already know about the camp, why am I explaining anything?"

"Because I want to hear what you choose to tell me." He said it without accusation, and somehow that was worse. "You chose 'something I left behind.' Noted."

"Two," she continued, ignoring that. "If armed men board this boat, I talk. You stay quiet. You've got an honest face; it'll get us shot. Mine works better under pressure."

A ghost of something crossed his expression—not quite a smile. "Three?"

"How do you know there's a three?"

"There's always a three."

"Three." She pulled the wet hair off her neck and tied it back, buying a second to decide how much of herself to spend. "Nobody touches my bag. Not you, not customs men, not anybody. If it goes overboard, I go overboard after it, and you can explain to whoever's paying you why your cargo jumped ship."

"Whoever's paying me," Tomas said. "Right."

"Somebody's always paying somebody on this river. You're carrying documents to a man in the capital. Documents somebody wants buried badly enough to make a survey team disappear. You're not a fisherman, Tomas. Fishermen don't check tie-downs like they're defusing something."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and the rain came down, and the river rose another invisible inch.

"All right," he said finally. "Terms accepted. All three. And mine: one—you bail when I tell you to bail, without debate. Two—if I say jump overboard, you jump, and you swim for the left bank, always the left. Three—" he paused, and she saw him choosing his words with the same economy she used, "—when you're ready to tell me the truth about what's in the bag and what's on the ridge, I'll listen. Until then, I won't dig. But I'll know you're not telling me. Don't insult both of us by pretending otherwise."

"That's not a term, that's a prophecy."

"It's a term," he said. "Honesty deferred, not denied. I can live with deferred. I've done it myself."

There it was again—that plainness, that refusal to perform. She'd spent fifteen years among people who were all surface, all angle, including herself, especially herself. Tomas stood in the rain like a thing that had been rained on for years and had stopped minding.

"When do we leave?" she asked.

"First light. Before five. The checkpoint crew at Kilometer Forty drinks rice wine after midnight and sleeps like the dead till seven. We pass at six, we're ghosts." He uncoiled a line, began coiling it again, hands busy. "There's a berth behind the engine housing. It smells like diesel and worse. You'll have it. I'll take the deck."

"You're giving me the dry spot."

"I'm giving you the spot where nobody walks past your bag in the night." He met her eyes. "Term three works both directions, Juniper. I don't touch yours. Nobody touches mine."

* * *

She spent the evening preparing, and the preparation itself was a kind of confession she made only to herself.

The compass came out of the duffel first. She unwrapped the oilcloth by lamplight in the post's back room and looked at the engraving again, as if it might have changed overnight, as if the letters might have rearranged themselves into someone else's name.

*R. RAVENSWORTH & SONS — FINE INSTRUMENTS.*

Her father's shop. Her father's brass, her father's stamp, the compasses he'd sold to surveyors and pilots and, in the back room, to men whose expeditions needed instruments that pointed wherever money wanted them to point. Daniel had carried one everywhere since he was twelve. She'd have known that compass in a pile of a thousand, the dent in the bezel from the time he dropped it off the porch—

Daniel.

She put it away before the name could finish arriving in her chest. Later. There would be time for later, or there wouldn't, and either way the work came first.

She inventoried instead, because inventory was prayer for people like her. Med kit from her drop runs—antibiotics, fever tabs, sutures, the good broad-spectrum ampoules she'd been ferrying to villages the government forgot. Three days of food compressed into foil. The flare pistol. A knife, a second knife, waterproof matches. And the photographs she'd taken at the camp with the little camera she kept for documenting drop sites—gear laid out square, bedrolls rolled, cookfire cold but orderly, and the mud. The mud with no prints in it. Six people don't levitate out of a campsite. Six people get *carried*, or they walk out barefoot on boards, or—

Or they leave the way someone wants them to leave.

She repacked the bag with the compass at its center, wrapped in clothing, wrapped again. Then she sat on the narrow bunk and listened to the rain and thought about Voss's smile, the way it sat on his face like a tool sitting in a rack, ready to be picked up and used. *We're coordinating with local assets.* Local assets. Men who photographed passengers at checkpoints. Men who asked where she slept.

They'd asked the post manager for the guest register too. She'd seen it happen through the window. The manager had handed it over with the eagerness of a man hoping to be on the winning side.

Her alias was in that book. *J. Cole.* Fifteen years old, that name, worn smooth as a river stone. If Voss traced J. Cole anywhere—and men like Voss always traced—he'd find a clean trail, because she'd built it clean. Fifteen years of clean. But clean trails end somewhere, and the place hers ended was a courthouse in a provincial capital, and a girl of twenty-two with her family's name in her mouth like a stone she couldn't swallow or spit out.

Stop, she told herself. One problem at a time. Get upriver. Get the rest of whatever Daniel's team found—or failed to survive finding. Get to Odile Vasseur at the floating relay, because Tomas wasn't the only one who'd heard rumors on this river, and the rumor Juniper had heard, three seasons ago, drunk pilot gossip at a fuel dock, was that there was a journalist living on a barge mid-channel who broadcast on frequencies the consortium couldn't buy, and that she paid for evidence in protection.

Get the truth to the light, and then disappear into the noise of a hundred other stories. That was the plan. Visible for a day, invisible forever after.

She almost believed it.

* * *

At four-thirty the rain had thinned to a gray whisper and the dock was black and slick. Juniper came down the planks with her bag on her shoulder and found Tomas already aboard, engine cover open, working by the light of a shielded lantern.

"Change of plan," he said, without looking up.

Her whole body went still. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning Voss walked the dock an hour ago. Looked at the *Mariana* for a long time. Talked to the harbormaster." He closed the engine cover and straightened. In the lantern light his face was unreadable, but his hands weren't—they were very steady, the steadiness of a man who'd decided something. "We don't leave at first light. We leave now, in the dark, running without lights past the moored patrol launch, and we hope the rain covers our sound."

"Now. In the dark. On a flooded river I've never seen, in a boat you admit leaks."

"Yes."

"That's insane."

"It is," he agreed. "But in six hours, Voss will have paperwork making the *Mariana* subject to search, and whatever's in my crate stops being my business and becomes evidence. So. Insane now, or finished later." He held out his hand, palm up—not for shaking, she realized, but for her bag. For stowing it deep, below decks, where it belonged. "Last chance to stay grounded, Juniper. Some people would call it the sensible option."

She looked at his open hand. She looked back once, down the dark dock, toward the trading post where two men who smiled wrong were sleeping behind locked doors, and beyond it toward the drowned strip where her airplane stood broken in the water like the last version of her life.

Then she swung the bag off her shoulder and put it in his hand.

"Cast off," she said. "And Tomas—when we hit the checkpoint at Kilometer Forty, I'm the one who talks."

"I counted on that," he said, and threw the line.
