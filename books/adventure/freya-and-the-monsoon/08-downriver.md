## 8. Downriver

**Juniper**

The boat was worse than Tomas had let on.

She'd seen it tied at the dock in the grey half-light before dawn—a long, flat-bottomed river skiff with a canvas awning sagging in the middle like a tired spine—and she'd told herself it would do. Boats were boats. They floated or they didn't.

By the second hour on the water, she understood that this one did both.

The leak wasn't dramatic. It didn't gush. It seeped, a slow brown insistence through a seam near the stern, pooling in the bilge beneath the supply crates until the water rose past her boot soles and reminded her, quietly, that the river was always coming aboard whether they invited it or not.

"You knew," she said. It wasn't a question.

Tomas stood at the tiller, one hand loose on the wooden handle, steering them down the flooded channel with the unhurried attention of a man who'd done it ten thousand times. The rain had eased to a fine drifting mist, and the country around them had turned into something between land and water—trees standing waist-deep, rooftops of abandoned fishing camps just breaking the surface, the whole interior gone soft and drowned.

"I said it leaked," he said.

"You said *a little*."

"It leaks a little. Constantly." He nodded toward a dented tin pail wedged under the thwart. "That's what the pail's for. Bailing isn't a repair. It's a relationship."

Juniper fished the pail out, scooped, and heaved the water over the side. Then again. Then again. The rhythm of it settled into her arms faster than she liked to admit. There was something almost honest about bailing—you couldn't lie to a leaking boat. You either kept up with it or you swam.

"Where'd you get it?" she asked.

"Bought it off a man who owed me money. That should have been my first warning about the man." A pause. "Or the boat."

She bailed twice more and set the pail down, flexing her fingers. Her shoulders ached from three nights of bad sleep in the trading post's back room, listening for boots on the boards outside. The fake rescuers were still there—she'd watched their truck from the window at dusk, two silhouettes smoking under the awning, patient as spiders. Waiting for her to run so they could follow.

So she hadn't run by road. She'd run by river, in a boat with a hole in it, with a stranger whose name might not even be Tomas.

"Keep us wide of the main channel," she said. "If those men have any sense, they'll put a boat on the water by noon."

"They don't have sense. They have money. Different problem." Tomas adjusted the tiller a degree. "There's a cut through the deadfalls about two kilometers down. Slower, but nobody watches it."

"How do you know what nobody watches?"

He glanced at her then—the first real look he'd given her since they cast off. Dark eyes, level, missing nothing. "Same way you know how to lie to men in matching jackets," he said. "Practice."

She let that sit. She scooped another pailful over the side and decided not to answer it, and decided he'd understand that the not-answering was itself an answer.

* * *

**Tomas**

She bailed like she flew, he decided. Efficient, unsentimental, every motion stripped of anything that wasn't necessary. He'd watched her load the boat before dawn—crates balanced, weight distributed fore and aft without being told why it mattered—and he'd known then that whatever name she was using, it wasn't the first one she'd worn.

Everyone on this river was running from something. Most of them were just running from debt or a wife or a warrant. She was running from something with architecture behind it. You could tell by how she checked sightlines. By how she'd positioned herself in the boat with her back never fully to the bank.

He steered them into the cut through the deadfalls, where the flooded trees closed overhead and the water went green-black and quiet. Good water. Water that swallowed engine noise.

"So," he said, because silence had its own cost and he preferred to know what he was paying for. "The trading post. Those men asked you questions and you gave them nothing. Most people would've given them something just to make the conversation end."

"Most people talk when they're nervous."

"You weren't nervous."

"I was furious." She wrung out the pail and set it down. "It's harder to hide."

He almost smiled. "What did they want to know, exactly?"

"What I saw at the drop site. Whether I went back. Whether I touched anything." She paused, and he heard her deciding how much of the truth to spend. "They wanted to know if I found coordinates written down anywhere."

"And did you?"

"That's the wrong question."

"It usually is." He let the boat drift through a gap in the deadfalls, close enough that a branch scraped the awning. "Here's mine, then. Why does a bush pilot who won't give her real destination to the man driving the boat care enough about a missing survey team to risk her neck going back to their camp?"

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere ahead, a bird made a sound like a hinge swinging.

"Because somebody has to go back," she said finally. "And because I don't trust anyone official to do it."

"That's not an answer. That's a slogan."

Her jaw tightened. For a moment he thought she'd shut down entirely, fold back into that armored quiet she wore like a flight jacket. But then she exhaled, slow, and surprised him.

"Fifteen years I've flown this country," she said. "I've carried medicine, mail, bodies, babies. I've landed on strips that didn't exist on maps. And in fifteen years, every single time something goes wrong out here—every time someone disappears or a village gets sick or a river turns the wrong color—the government sends men who ask questions and write nothing down. Or they write it down and bury it. So no. I don't trust them to find six surveyors who are probably not lost at all." She picked up the pail, bailed once, hard, as if the water had personally offended her. "Your turn. Why doesn't a boatman trust the law? Boatmen love the law. The law keeps the checkpoints predictable."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "I worked for them. Once."

"The government?"

"A bureau. Inspections." He watched the channel ahead rather than her face. "Water quality, mostly. Industrial runoff. I spent nine years taking samples and writing reports, and for eight of those years the reports went where reports go. The ninth year, I wrote one that someone important didn't want written. They offered me a transfer. Then a settlement. Then they offered me unemployment and a reputation problem, and I learned which offer comes last."

"What was in the report?"

"Rust-colored water and a lot of dead fish downstream of a mine concession." He shrugged, though it felt heavier than a shrug. "Old story. New company name every few years."

Juniper looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached down, pulled a crate toward her, and started redistributing weight without being asked—shifting the heavy med supplies aft, where the leak came in, so the stern sat higher in the water.

"You've been fighting that leak wrong," she said. "You bail it flat and it comes right back. Trim the boat nose-down and the seam rides above the waterline. You'll bail half as much."

Tomas stared at her. Then at the boat. Then he eased the tiller and let the bow settle lower, and within a minute the infernal seeping slowed to almost nothing.

"Nine years flying," he muttered. "Eight writing reports. And neither of us thought to trim the boat."

"Don't feel bad. Neither did whoever built it."

But something had shifted between them, small and structural, like weight moved to the right end of a boat. He felt it in the way she stopped watching his hands. He suspected she felt it too, and hated it a little.

* * *

**Juniper**

By midday the rain came back in earnest, a soft grey curtain that erased the horizon and turned the world down to the size of the boat. Juniper bailed on a schedule now—every twenty minutes, more out of discipline than need—and Tomas held them in the narrow channels with a certainty that unsettled her, because it meant he'd traveled these drowned backwaters often, and recently, and with reason to avoid the open river.

People who avoided the open river were people avoiding eyes. She knew the type. She was the type.

"Eat something," he said, nodding at her pack without looking away from the channel.

"I'm fine."

"You've eaten nothing since the dock. I counted."

"You were counting my food?"

"I count everything. It's a professional deformity." He reached into his own bag, produced a wrapped parcel of dried fish and hard bread, and tore it in half without ceremony. He held half out. "Eat. Fainting pilots drown slower than passengers but it's still embarrassing."

She took it mostly because refusing would take more energy than accepting. The fish was salty and terrible and the best thing she'd tasted in days. They ate in the rhythm of the river, and the rain drummed the canvas, and for a while neither of them performed anything at all.

It was Tomas who broke it, quietly, eyes on the water.

"My daughter used to hate this river," he said.

Juniper went still. He hadn't mentioned a daughter. He hadn't mentioned anything personal that wasn't a weapon laid on the table between them.

"She'd cry every time we took the boat out. Not because she was scared. Because she said the river smelled like secrets." He chewed, swallowed. "She was four. Where do you get a sentence like that at four?"

"Where is she now?"

"With her mother. In a city far enough away that the people who ended my career can't find her by finding me." He folded the wrapping paper into a neat square, precise as a man folding evidence. "That's the arithmetic I live by now. Everything I do gets measured against whether it leads anyone to her door."

Juniper understood that arithmetic better than he could know. Fifteen years of it. Every route planned backward from visibility. Every kindness rationed, every friendship left unfed, because attachments were trails and trails led home.

"That's why you're helping me," she said. "Not charity. You think the survey team's disappearance connects to your old report. To the mines."

"I think rust-colored water and vanished inspectors are the same story wearing different clothes, yes." He met her eyes. "And I think you think so too, or you'd have taken the road."

She bailed. Twenty minutes early, but her hands wanted the work.

"There's a camp," she said. Carefully. Spending truth like coins she couldn't replace. "Upstream of the falls. If the team moved, they moved deeper. I need to see it before I decide anything else."

"Before you decide whether to keep running south or start running toward something."

She didn't confirm it. She didn't have to. He read the silence the way she read weather, and nodded once, and gave the tiller a touch that swung them toward the falls country without another word spent on it.

Grudging, she thought. This whole arrangement was grudging—two people sharing a leaking boat and a language of half-truths, each waiting for the other to show the shape of what they were actually carrying. And yet when he handed her the pail without being asked, taking the tiller so she could work both hands, she caught herself thinking: *he counts everything, and he hasn't once counted me as a liability.*

It was the closest thing to trust she'd felt in fifteen years, and she distrusted it immediately, thoroughly, and without much conviction.

* * *

**Tomas**

Dusk came early under the storm clouds, the light failing in stages, green to pewter to a darkness that smelled of iron. They tied up in the lee of a drowned copse, awning rigged, stove lit low inside a windscreen. The river breathed around them, vast and patient.

Juniper took first watch without discussing it. He pretended to sleep and listened to her move—checking the tie-lines, checking the sky, checking the pail out of pure habit even though the trimmed boat had barely shipped a cupful all afternoon.

Near midnight, she woke him anyway.

"Radio," she said.

He came up out of the bedding fast, all the sleep gone. She was crouched over the little battery set she'd brought aboard, headset pressed to one ear, her face lit faintly by the dial's glow. Her expression was doing something complicated.

"What is it?"

"Relay station out of the capital. Weak signal." She turned the gain up. Static rolled and parted, and through it came a voice reading coordinates in a flat, careful cadence—numbers, grid references, repeated twice, the way you'd read them if you thought someone might be writing them down.

Then the voice said a word that wasn't a number, and Juniper's whole body changed.

Tomas leaned closer. The static surged. Through it, half-drowned, the voice finished the transmission with a phrase that sounded less like coordinates and more like a message:

*"—tell her the compass points home—"*

The signal dissolved into rain-hiss. Juniper sat motionless, headset on, staring at nothing, and Tomas watched her hand come up slowly and press flat against her sternum, over the pocket where she'd been keeping something wrapped in oilcloth since the day he met her.

"Juniper," he said quietly. "Who was that?"

She pulled the headset off. When she turned to him, her eyes were wet and furious about being wet, and her voice came out steady as a level.

"That transmission came from upstream," she said. "From the holding depot country. And whoever sent it knows my family name."

She unwrapped the oilcloth. In the stove-light, the brass compass caught fire—old brass, worn smooth, an engraving along the case that Tomas couldn't read from across the boat but that he suddenly, terribly, understood the weight of.

"That's not possible," he said. "Those men disappeared weeks ago."

"I know what's possible." She closed her fist around the compass like she was holding it shut. "Set a course for the depot, Tomas. First light. We're done being careful."

Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere far upriver, thunder answered.
