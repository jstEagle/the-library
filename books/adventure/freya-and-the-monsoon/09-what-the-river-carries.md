## 9. What the River Carries

Tomas noticed the fish before Juniper did.

He was bailing—third time that morning, the ritual already worn into his arms like an old scar—and he'd paused with the bucket half-raised because the bank had gone wrong. Not empty. Wrong. The mudflats on the eastern side of the channel were scattered with them, silver-bellied and stiff, dozens of them, then hundreds as the boat slid past a bend in the river. Catfish. Carp. A long-nosed gar that must have weighed twenty pounds when it was alive, now bloated and pale, its eye gone milky.

"Juniper."

She was at the tiller, one hand on it, the other holding a chart she'd been studying since dawn without appearing to read a word of it. She looked up at his tone. He didn't have to point. Her eyes went to the bank, tracked along it for a full hundred meters, and came back to him flat and hard.

"How far," she said, "does this go?"

"I don't know yet." He set the bucket down. "That's what worries me."

They ran another hour before the water itself told them the rest. It happened gradually enough that you could pretend not to see it—the way you can pretend not to see a storm building—but by midday there was no pretending left. The river had been tea-colored since they'd launched, tannin-stained and honest about it. Now it was rust. Orange-brown, thick-looking even where the current ran fast, and where their wake curled against the hull the foam sat on the surface like scum on old milk and didn't dissipate.

Juniper cut the engine to idle and let the boat drift.

"You've seen this before," she said. It wasn't a question.

Tomas leaned over the gunwale and dipped two fingers into the water, held them up to the light. The stain clung. He wiped them on his trousers and the mark stayed, faintly orange, like something bleeding through from underneath.

"Twice," he said. "Once in the north, at a copper operation that swore up and down it wasn't theirs. Once here, three years ago, on a tributary two hundred kilometers east of this one." He watched her face while he said it, watching for the flicker. "Different rivers. Same color."

"And the cause?"

"Tailings." He said the word the way you'd say the name of a disease. "Waste rock ground fine enough to bleed metal into everything it touches. When the water table's high—like now, like every monsoon—it leaches straight out of the containment ponds and into the watershed. Fish die first. Then the reeds. Then, if people keep drinking it, the people."

Juniper turned back to the tiller. For a moment he thought she was going to say nothing at all, just open the throttle and put distance between themselves and the evidence. But she surprised him. She killed the engine entirely.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

They beached on a gravel bar where a side channel braided away from the main current, shallow enough that the water ran almost clear over the stones—almost, but not quite. Even here there was a faint amber tint, a ghost of the poison upstream. Tomas crouched and filled a glass jar from his kit, held it up. Sediment swirled in it, fine as flour, settling slow.

"That's not silt," he said. "Silt's heavier. This is suspended particulate—ground ore, basically. It'll hang in the water column for weeks. Travel hundreds of kilometers if the flow's right." He screwed the lid on and set the jar in the bottom of the boat, next to the bail bucket and the spare fuel line. "I used to collect these for a living."

She glanced at him sideways. "You said you worked government."

"I did." He let it sit there. She didn't push, and he found he respected that more than he would have respected questions. Some debts you pay in your own time.

Juniper walked the gravel bar, boots crunching, and Tomas followed her gaze downstream. The dead fish thinned out below the confluence where clean water from a southern tributary diluted the main channel—but above it, back the way they'd come, the banks had been a graveyard. He did the math without wanting to. Whatever was killing this river sat somewhere north of them, high in the interior, close to the coordinates where a survey team had last been heard from.

"You're thinking what I'm thinking," he said.

"I'm thinking about concessions." Juniper stopped walking. She pulled the folded map from inside her jacket—she'd been carrying it since the trading post, he'd noticed, touching it like a talisman—and spread it on a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. Her finger moved across it, sure and quick, tracing watersheds the way other people traced roads. "Here. And here. And this whole drainage basin." Three areas, all upstream, all north. All marked, in the consortium's own published filings, as new mining concessions granted within the last eighteen months.

"The same direction the survey team went," Tomas said quietly.

"The same direction the survey team went." She stared at the map, and something moved behind her eyes—not fear exactly, but the cold recalculation of a pilot reading weather she doesn't like. "A government team goes in to survey that exact country. They miss four check-ins. Their gear turns up laid out neat as Sunday clothes with no tracks around it. And meanwhile the water downstream of their last known position starts running the color of a wound."

"You think they found the tailings."

"I think they found worse than tailings." She folded the map with a snap. "Containment ponds fail all the time—that's negligence, that's cost-cutting, that's the kind of thing companies pay fines for and call it weather. But this—" she nodded at the rust-colored current sliding past the gravel bar, "—this much discharge means either a catastrophic failure nobody reported, or a system running with no containment at all. Illegal. Off the books. Which means everyone from the site manager to the provincial water board has to keep it invisible. And a government survey team mapping that drainage basin is the opposite of invisible."

Tomas looked at the jar in the boat. His hands remembered the weight of a hundred jars like it, sealed and labeled and logged, and then remembered the weight of the report that had followed—the report he'd refused to bury, and the career that had been buried instead. He'd spent three years telling himself the pattern was local. Bad actors, bad luck, isolated failures. Standing on this gravel bar, watching a whole river die in real time, he felt that comfortable fiction dissolve.

"It's not one mine," he said aloud. "It's a policy."

Juniper looked at him. "Say that again."

"A single operation fouls a creek, that's an accident. But coordinated concessions across three separate drainages, all granted fast-tracked, all upstream of villages, all discharging at once during flood season when dilution hides the worst of it?" He shook his head. "Somebody planned the timing. Flood season is cover. By the time the dry season comes and the water runs clear again, the sediment's settled into the floodplain and the fish kills are attributed to heat. I've seen the reports written that way. I've seen them *approved*."

The wind shifted, coming off the water, and it carried a smell he hadn't caught from the moving current—something chemical beneath the organic rot, sharp and industrial, like a struck match left burning. Juniper's nostrils flared. She was a bush pilot; she knew what fuel spills smelled like, what swamp rot smelled like. This was neither.

"Sulphur compounds," Tomas said. "Ore processing. That smell shouldn't exist out here. There's no industry within a hundred kilometers—except whatever's happening upstream."

* * *

They ran until late afternoon, and the river got worse before it got better.

At a village landing—a cluster of stilt houses, drying racks, a church roof gone green with moss—there were no children swimming. That was the detail that lodged in Tomas's chest. Every river village he'd ever passed had children in the water, screaming, splashing, driving the adults crazy. Here the water lay empty. A woman stood on a landing platform watching them approach with an expression he recognized: not hostility, but the flat wariness of people who had learned that strangers brought paperwork and paperwork brought ruin.

Juniper brought the boat alongside and called a greeting in the trade tongue, easy and unaccented. The woman answered. The conversation was short. No, they had no fuel to sell. No, they had no food to trade. Yes, the fishing had died. When Juniper asked since when, the woman looked at her for a long moment and said, "Since the water changed," and would say nothing else.

Back on the river, Juniper was silent for a long stretch. The light was going gold and then copper, matching the water, and the drowned country slid past on both sides—treetops standing in floodwater, roofs visible where houses should have been, the monsoon having swallowed the land whole.

"My brother flew medicine into places like that," she said finally.

Tomas kept his eyes on the channel ahead. He hadn't known she had a brother. He filed it away without comment, the way you pocket something valuable you find on a path.

"He hated exactly this," she went on, as if talking to herself as much as to him. "Companies that treat rivers as sewers and villages as rounding errors. He used to write letters. Official complaints. I always thought—" She stopped. Shook her head. "Doesn't matter what I thought."

"Letters get answered or they don't," Tomas said. "Reports get buried or they don't. I wrote the best contamination assessment of my career once. It had photographs, chain-of-custody samples, signed affidavits from villagers. It was bulletproof." He laughed, short and without humor. "It took eleven days to disappear. Eleven days from submission to the file being closed as 'unsubstantiated.' I know the number because I counted. Counting is what you do when you can't do anything else."

Juniper looked over at him then, really looked, and he saw her reassessing him—the way you reassess a plane's performance when the weather turns. Not a drifter. Not a smuggler, or not only. Something with edges.

"What was in the report?" she asked.

"Cadmium. Arsenic. Levels in the drinking water forty times the legal limit, downstream of a consortium pre-processing site." He watched the channel, calling the deep water from memory of how the current bent the surface. "Same consortium whose name is on those concessions you marked. Same name on the provincial water board's list of 'industry partners.' You start pulling that thread and you find it's woven through everything."

"And when you wouldn't bury it—"

"When I wouldn't bury it, they buried me. Quietly. No charges—they didn't need any. Just a leaked personnel note, an ethics review that never ended, and a door held open." He shrugged. "I walked through it. Told myself I was done. Told myself the river would speak for itself eventually." He gestured at the orange water, the dead fish, the empty village landing sliding past. "Rivers don't testify. That's the lesson. That's the whole lesson. Everything out here dies silently unless somebody stands up and says the words out loud, in a room where it counts."

Juniper was quiet for a kilometer or more. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, stripped of the trading-post polish.

"The gear I found at that camp," she said. "There was a compass. Engraved with a family name. Mine, as it happens." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "So before you decide how much of me to trust, know that I'm not flying this river for charity. Whoever that team was, someone on it has my name in their blood. And whoever emptied that camp wants very badly for no one to ever know why."

Tomas absorbed it. A missing surveyor with her surname. A compass carried into the interior and left behind. Fifteen years of a woman who never stayed anywhere longer than necessary, suddenly anchored to a mystery at the exact center of a poisoning.

"Family," he said carefully, "is the longest river there is. You can't see the source from the mouth."

Something crossed her face—too quick to name—and then she opened the throttle and the boat climbed up onto the plane, throwing spray, running hard into the darkening east.

* * *

They made camp on high ground at dusk, in the shell of an old mission station whose roof had mostly survived, and Tomas built a small fire while Juniper checked the boat's hull by lantern light. The leak was holding. Barely. He heard her muttering to the boat the way pilots talk to aircraft, half affection, half threat.

Over the fire they ate rice and tinned fish—regular tinned fish, from his stores, and Tomas noted that Juniper eyed it with more suspicion than appetite before eating anyway. Hunger beats caution eventually. That was another lesson of the river.

"Do you hear that?" she said suddenly.

He listened. Wind in the flooded trees. Water moving. And underneath it, far off, a sound he couldn't place at first—rhythmic, mechanical, a deep irregular pounding that traveled through the ground more than the air.

"Dredges," he said slowly. "Or pumps. Big ones." He cocked his head, estimating. "Twenty, thirty kilometers north. Maybe less. Sound carries strange over floodwater."

"At night," Juniper said. "Mining operations don't run at night unless they're hiding the schedule. Daylight shifts are for inspectors. Night shifts are for things you don't want photographed."

Tomas fed the fire a stick and watched the sparks climb. Somewhere north of them, in the drowned dark, machines were chewing the interior apart and bleeding it into the water, and somewhere north of them six surveyors had walked into that country carrying instruments and clipboards and the government's authority, and had not walked out. The jar of rust-colored water sat in the bottom of the boat, catching the firelight, sediment drifting in it like a slow snowfall of evidence.

"Tomorrow we pass the mouth of the Serapi tributary," Juniper said. "If the discharge is coming down the main stem, the Serapi's clean. If the Serapi's worse—" she looked at him across the fire, and the firelight made her face look carved, older than fifteen years of running should have made anyone, "—then we follow it up. To the source."

"And toward wherever your team disappeared."

"Our team," she corrected, and then seemed to hear herself, and looked away.

Tomas said nothing. But as he rolled into his blanket and listened to the distant mechanical heartbeat pulsing through the flooded earth, he understood that the shape of the thing had changed for both of them. Yesterday it had been a missing survey team, a favor owed, a delivery to make. Tonight it was a poisoned river, a bought province, and a machine grinding north of them in the dark—and two people in a leaking boat who had each, separately, already lost everything to men who buried truth for a living.

Sleep came late. When it came, it came to the rhythm of the pumps, and Tomas dreamed of water the color of rust rising slow and patient, the way water does, toward everything that had ever tried to stand above it.

In the morning, they turned north.
