## 10. Checkpoint

The river narrowed an hour past the dead-fish banks, and Juniper felt it before she saw it—the way a pilot feels turbulence three seconds before the wings start to shudder. Something wrong in the air. Tomas felt it too. He'd stopped talking twenty minutes ago, and his hands had gone quiet on the tiller, making small corrections, keeping them close to the reed line as if the boat could shrink.

"There." He tipped his chin without turning his head.

Around the next bend, where the channel pinched between a gravel bar and a drowned forest, someone had strung a cable across the water. Two aluminum boats sat anchored midstream, and on the near bank, under a tarp strung between saplings, stood four men in matching olive jackets. Not uniforms. Uniforms had creases and insignia and the particular boredom of men paid by the hour. These jackets were too new, too identical, and the men wearing them stood the way her brother used to stand when he was watching the road for headlights that shouldn't be there.

"Checkpoint," Tomas said. "First one I've seen on this stretch in six years."

"Maybe they're looking for your surveyors," Juniper said. "That's what they'll say."

"You don't believe that."

"I believe they'll say it."

She was already moving. The duffel sat under the thwart where it always sat, and inside it, wrapped in a sock, was the compass with RAVENSWORTH engraved on the brass case. She'd told herself she was carrying it because it was evidence. That was half true. The other half was that she couldn't make herself put it down—couldn't leave it in mud or water or anyone else's hands. It was the only thing on this river that knew who she was.

She didn't wrap it deeper. Wrapping it deeper would look like hiding something. Instead she took it out, opened the case, and set it on top of the duffel like any pilot's tool among tools, then covered the whole thing with a rain jacket and a coil of rope. Visible. Ordinary. A compass is not contraband; a hidden compass is.

"Names," she said quietly. "We're delivering fuel drums to the mission at Two Rivers Bend. You're a boatman I hired at Kessely Landing. I'm a pilot, grounded, taking work where I can find it. We met two days ago. You don't know my last name because nobody on this river does."

"And yours is?"

"Vance. June Vance." It came out smooth, and she hated how smooth. Fifteen years since she'd needed a lie this size, and it fit her mouth like an old glove, still warm from the last hand that wore it.

Tomas studied her a moment longer than was comfortable. "June Vance," he repeated, filing it away somewhere behind his eyes. "And if they ask why a pilot is running a boat?"

"Because planes need dry strips and the monsoon took mine. Because it's honest work and I say yes to it." She looked at him. "You've lied to officials before. I can tell."

"I've been lied *about* by officials before," Tomas said. "Different skill. Watch how they photograph things. Photographs are how you know what they actually want."

The cable came up on a pulley as they approached, and one of the men waved them in with a flat palm—not friendly, just efficient. Up close the tarp shelter held a folding table, a generator humming under it, and a camera on a tripod pointed at the water like a bird gun. A fifth man sat behind the table with a ledger open, and he was the one Juniper marked immediately, because he wasn't looking at the boats. He was looking at faces.

"Morning," the man at the table said. "River authority. We're coordinating the search for the missing survey team." He said it the way a man reads a sign aloud—no ownership of the words. "Name, origin, destination."

"Tomas Ferro. Boatman. Out of Kessely."

"June Vance. Pilot, currently grounded. Running supplies up to the mission at Two Rivers Bend."

The pen scratched. The man's eyes came up between entries, unhurried, weighing. "Pilot. What kind?"

"Bush freight. Med drops, mostly."

"So you were flying recently." Not a question. A hook, baited and dropped.

"Until the storm cracked my gear on a flooded strip. Ask the mission at Serrano; they saw me come in."

"I'll ask everyone eventually," he said pleasantly, and wrote something that was probably not her answer. "You'd have been in the air over the interior, then. Last week. Anyone request a drop out that way? Survey team would've filed for one."

Here it was. Chapter three of a story she'd already written in her head on the walk down to the dock: *I flew a drop for a team out of the provincial office, coordinates on a ridge, fog on the approach, never saw them on the ground.* Every word true except the ones that mattered, arranged so the truth itself became the camouflage. She gave it to him plainly, bored around the edges, a working woman recounting a job.

"Fog was thick. I made the drop on instruments and guesswork. Never saw a soul." She shrugged. "If they walked out, they walked a long way."

"Mm." He turned a page. "And the camp? You went back?"

The question landed a half-second too fast, aimed at a detail she hadn't offered. They hadn't published anything about a camp being found. Which meant either they knew about the camp because they'd been there—or because they'd made it empty.

"I overflew the grid twice," she said. "Nothing worth landing for."

Something flickered across his face—disappointment, maybe, or recalculation—and she understood with cold clarity that she had just passed a test whose passing she wouldn't have known how to study for. He believed her, or believed enough, and the reason he believed her was that she had learned this grammar before she learned to drive. Her father's kitchen table, men with ledgers, her father saying: *the best lie is a boring truth with one door moved.*

"Step out of the boat, please. Both of you. Standard processing."

*Standard.* The word did a lot of lifting. On the bank, another man worked the camera while a third asked them to stand against a measuring board painted with height lines, the kind used for mug shots, though he called it a "documentation frame." The flash popped. Once for Tomas. Once for her.

"Why photographs?" Tomas asked mildly. "For a search?"

"Searches generate paperwork," the man said. "Paperwork needs attaching."

"To whom?" Tomas said. "Missing people don't need passenger files."

The photographer smiled with his mouth. "You'd be surprised what missing people need."

Juniper kept her breathing even and her face at the dull setting. But her mind was running the arithmetic it always ran: *what do they have, what do they want, what's the gap between those two things.* They wanted faces attached to names attached to movements. That wasn't a search. Searches cast nets wide and apologized for the inconvenience. This was a filter, set narrow, tuned to catch something specific moving north on this river. And whatever it was tuned to catch, it had a description, because the man at the table had looked at her exactly one beat too long after she said *pilot.*

They let Tomas back toward the boat first. As Juniper followed, the man at the table spoke without looking up.

"Vance. Uncommon name."

"It's common enough where I'm from."

"Where's that?"

"Gone now. Flooded." She let a shadow cross her face, grief-shaped, uncheckable. Whole valleys on this river had gone under reservoirs in the last decade; it was the most alibi-rich country on earth.

He almost smiled. "Lot of that going around." Then, as she stepped past him into the shallows, lightly, conversationally: "Safe travels, Ms. Vance. Weather's turning. River'll be closing soon. Be a shame to be caught out here when everything shuts down."

It wasn't a warning. It was a countdown, delivered by someone who knew the clock better than she did.

They pushed off. The cable lowered behind them, and Juniper kept her eyes forward until the checkpoint folded into the bend, and then she let out a breath she'd been rationing.

"They photographed us," Tomas said. "Both of us. Full-face, against a board."

"I noticed."

"That's not a search. That's a catalogue." He steered them back toward the channel's heart, away from the reeds now, oddly less careful than before. "Six years I ran this river and never once got photographed. Today there's a generator and a tripod and a man asking where you're from like he's filling out a warrant."

"They're not looking for six surveyors," Juniper said. "They're looking for whoever knows why the surveyors are missing. And they think that person is coming upriver or downriver, and they've narrowed it to this channel." She watched the water slide by, brown and patient. "Which means somebody told them to expect us. Or somebody like us."

Tomas was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed—flatter, more decided. "There's a side channel past the burned snag, maybe two hours on. Local traffic uses it during high water. No checkpoints because officially it doesn't exist."

"Officially."

"The map says marsh. The marsh disagrees." He glanced at her. "It adds a day. Maybe two. It's slower, narrower, and if we break the prop on a submerged log, we're walking."

"And the main channel?"

"Is a corridor with cameras at both ends and men who smile wrong."

Juniper thought about the compass under the rain jacket, warm now from sitting near her knee through all of it, as if the brass itself had a pulse. She thought about Daniel's handwriting waiting in a village she hadn't reached yet, and about a man at a folding table saying *be a shame to be caught out here,* and about fifteen years of practice at being no one, which had just bought her forty clean seconds against a professional.

Forty seconds. The margin was shrinking. She could feel it the way she felt weather building behind a ridge—pressure without wind yet, the whole sky holding its breath.

"Take the side channel," she said.

Tomas nodded once, unsurprised, and swung the bow toward the burned snag rising black out of the floodwater ahead—a tree that had died standing, arms stripped, pointing at nothing.

Behind them, far back down the channel, an engine started. One of the aluminum boats, leaving its anchor.

Not following. Just moving. Keeping pace somewhere out of sight, the way a net keeps pace with the fish it hasn't closed on yet.

Juniper settled beside the duffel and put her hand flat on the rain jacket, over the compass, and watched the burned snag come closer, and understood that from here on, every mile of river would be measured in two currencies at once: distance traveled, and time remaining before the gap between those men's questions and her answers finally closed.

The snag slid past on their left. The side channel opened like a held secret, narrow and green-shadowed, and Tomas took them into it at half throttle, quiet as a rumor.

Halfway in, Juniper heard it—carried flat across the water from the main channel, thin with distance but unmistakable.

A radio voice, reading names.

And the second name it read was *Vance*.
