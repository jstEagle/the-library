## 11. The Village That Went Quiet

The river told them before the village did.

Tomas cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the boat drift, and Juniper understood why. The current here ran slow and strange, carrying things it shouldn't—strips of bark, a child's rubber sandal, a mat of oily scum that caught the gray light and held it like a bruise. The water had gone the color of weak tea left too long, and where it lapped against the pilings of the village dock, it left a rust-colored line, like something bleeding slowly from upstream.

"Kilat," Tomas said quietly. "Or what's left of it."

Juniper had seen Kilat on her charts for fifteen years. A fuel stop. A trading village, once—a real one, with a sawmill and a school and a dock long enough for three boats abreast. Her charts still said *POP 400* in faded pencil in her own handwriting. What she saw now was maybe sixty structures, half of them standing open to the rain, roofs caved or doors gone, and not one of them looking lived-in.

Except that wasn't quite true either. Smoke rose from two chimneys, thin and careful, the way smoke rises when people don't want to be found.

"They knew we were coming," she said.

"The whole river knows we're coming." Tomas nudged the boat toward the dock with short strokes of the oar. "Word travels faster than water down here. Question is whether they decided we're the disease or just more symptoms."

He tied off at the downstream end of the dock, away from sightlines, and Juniper climbed out onto planks so soft with rot she could feel them give under her boots like old bread. She kept the canvas bag on her shoulder—the one with Daniel's compass wrapped inside it, riding against her hip like a second heartbeat. She'd stopped thinking of it as evidence days ago. It was a hand on her shoulder now. It was *ask me who I am.*

The village street ran up from the dock in a single muddy lane, and it was empty the way rooms are empty after an argument. Not abandoned—maintained, barely—but emptied. A dog watched them from under a stilt house without barking. Laundry hung on a line, stiff with mildew, forgotten mid-chore. On the wall of the shuttered trading post someone had painted over the old company sign, but the paint was thin and underneath you could still read the letters: **MERIDIAN**.

Juniper stopped walking.

"You know them," Tomas said. It wasn't a question.

"I've flown their executives into hunting lodges." She heard her own voice go flat, the pilot's voice, the voice that carried nothing. "Charter work pays the same no matter whose money it is. That's what I used to tell myself."

"And now?"

"Now I count dead fish."

They walked the lane end to end and saw four people, total. An old woman splitting kindling who never looked up. Two children who vanished behind a house with the practiced silence of kids who'd learned that strangers meant men asking questions. And finally, at the far end near the flooded rice terraces, a man mending a net who stood when he saw them—not fast, not frightened, just deliberate, like a man deciding something.

He was perhaps sixty, built like the buttresses of a rain tree, with a face that had stopped bothering to hide anything years ago. He looked at Tomas first, then at Juniper, then at the boat, then back at Juniper, and whatever equation he ran behind his eyes, he seemed to arrive at an answer he didn't love.

"You're not police," he said. His accent was river-flat, unhurried.

"No."

"You're not Meridian."

"No."

"Then you're lost, or you're trouble, and I've had enough of both this year." He shouldered his net. "The fuel store is closed. The church is closed. Everything is closed."

"We're not buying anything," Juniper said. "We're going downriver. To the capital."

Something moved in his face then—the smallest crack, a hairline fracture in all that deliberate blankness. "Everyone's going somewhere," he said. "Nobody's arriving anywhere."

* * *

His name was Eku, though it took an hour and a shared pot of bitter tea to get even that much. He led them to a house on stilts at the village's edge, set apart, and inside there were signs of a life being lived small: a cooking fire, a wall of photographs gone soft-focus with humidity, a grandson's school certificate framed in cheap wood. The old woman turned out to be his sister, Ama, and she made the tea without being asked and then sat facing the door with her arms folded, as if she expected the room itself to be raided.

"How many people lived here?" Juniper asked.

"Four hundred. Maybe more, in the wet season, when the fishing camps came down." Eku poured tea for all three of them, and his hand was steady, and that steadiness struck Juniper as the most exhausting thing she'd seen in weeks—the discipline of it. "Now? Sixty. Seventy if you count the ones who come back to check on houses they can't live in anymore."

"What happened?"

Eku looked at his sister. Ama looked at the door. Then Eku laughed, short and terrible, and said, "You know what happened. Everyone on this river knows what happened. The question is why you're asking like you don't."

So Juniper told him some of it. Not all of it—never all of it—but enough. The survey team. The camp with no tracks. The compass. The men at the trading post who smiled wrong. She watched him while she talked, and she watched the tea cool untouched in front of him, and by the time she finished, Ama had unfolded her arms.

"Six weeks ago," Eku said, "there were men here. Not the ones with guns. Worse ones—the ones with clipboards." He said the word *clipboards* the way other men said *machetes*. "They came in two boats, wearing clean shirts, and they sat right where you're sitting, and they had papers for every family in the village. Compensation agreements, they called them. For the fish die-off. For the skin sickness the children got in the dry season. Sign here, take the money, agree you won't make claims later."

"How much?" Tomas asked.

"Enough." Eku's mouth twisted. "Enough that a man with three kids and no boat couldn't say no. Enough that the ones who refused got visited twice. The second visit, the shirts weren't so clean and the smiles weren't so wide, and they didn't bring papers. They brought photographs." He paused. "Photographs of my grandson's school. Of Ama's path to the garden. Of our house, taken from the water, taken from a boat, meaning they'd been sitting out there watching us. And a message: accidents happen on rivers."

Ama spoke for the first time, her voice dry as seed husks. "My husband signed. He cried doing it. He said, *what good is my name against theirs.* He was dead within the year—not murdered, nothing you could point at. Just stopped. Old men stop, when they're made to feel small. The river took him the way rivers do, gently, and everyone knew and nobody said."

"And the ones who wouldn't sign?"

Eku was quiet a moment. "There was a teacher. Young man, came up from the coast, taught thirty children in that schoolhouse you walked past. He wrote letters. Real letters, with copies, sent to the provincial office, to the newspapers, to anyone with an address. He documented the die-offs. He kept jars of the dead fish on his windowsill like trophies, like proof." Eku stared into his cup. "One morning his boat was gone and so was he, and the schoolhouse was locked, and a week later a man from the district office came and told us the teacher had resigned and moved away for personal reasons. Personal reasons." He laughed again, that same terrible laugh. "Half the village left within two months. You don't argue with a company that can erase a man and call it paperwork."

Juniper sat very still. She could feel the fury building in her chest, and underneath the fury, worse, the recognition—because she knew how this worked. She had grown up inside a smaller version of it. Money first, then fear, then the quiet erasure, then the story everyone agreed to tell because the alternative was unbearable. Fifteen years ago she had burned a version of this machine down, and it had cost her everything, and here it was again, bigger, better funded, wearing a mining consortium's logo instead of her father's flag.

"Some people didn't sign," she said.

"Some people didn't sign." Eku nodded slowly. "And those are the sixty you see. The stubborn ones. The ones with nowhere else, or nothing left to lose, or—" he glanced at his sister "—too old to start over."

* * *

It was Ama who broke the stalemate, the way it's always the ones who've already lost everything who can afford generosity.

She stood abruptly, went to the back wall, and pried loose a board that Juniper would have sworn was structural. Behind it was a cavity, and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with fishing line, a bundle.

"My husband kept the papers," she said, coming back. "All of them. Every agreement, every refusal, every letter the teacher wrote that we could copy before they locked the school. He said someday someone would come downriver asking questions, and we'd need to be ready, because ready is the only weapon poor people get." She set the bundle on the table between them. "He's been dead two years. Nobody's come. Until you."

Juniper didn't touch it. "Why give it to us? You don't know us."

"No," Ama agreed. "But I watched you walk up that lane. The police walk fast and look at faces. Company men walk slow and look at property. You—" she pointed a knotted finger at Juniper "—you looked at the schoolhouse first. Before the dock, before the store. You looked at the schoolhouse and your face did something. So either you have children you lost, or you have a teacher's heart, and I'll gamble on either."

"That's a hell of a gamble," Tomas murmured.

"I'm seventy-three," Ama said. "Everything left is a gamble."

Juniper untied the fishing line with fingers that weren't quite steady. Inside the oilcloth: a sheaf of compensation agreements, dozens of them, each bearing a signature or a defiant blank; carbon copies of letters in a young man's precise hand, citing dates, tonnages, water readings; and beneath it all, a separate folder, thicker, protected by a layer of plastic sheeting, labeled in block capitals: **SURVEY TEAM — LEFT WITH US FOR SAFEKEEPING — IF WE DON'T RETURN, GIVE THIS TO SOMEONE GOING DOWNRIVER**.

Her breath stopped.

She opened the folder the way you open a door you're afraid of what's behind. Water sample logs. Chain-of-custody forms filled out in triplicate. Photographs of the rust-colored scum on the water, dated six weeks back. And on the cover page, in the top corner, a name written in handwriting she hadn't seen since she was nineteen years old—small, slanted, meticulous, the handwriting of a boy who'd labeled his insect collection and alphabetized his cassette tapes and once, only once, written her a letter begging her to come home.

*D. Ravensworth.*

Daniel.

The room went distant. She heard the rain starting on the tin roof, heard Ama saying something, heard Tomas shift beside her, and none of it reached her properly, because she was looking at her brother's handwriting for the first time in fifteen years and it was exactly the same, unchanged, as if the boy had been waiting in suspension all this time for her to find it.

"He was here," she said. Her voice came out wrong, scraped. "Six weeks ago."

"Five," Eku said. "Maybe five and a half. They came down from the ridge, all six of them, on foot, tired and jumpy. The leader—" he studied her face "—he looked like you, actually. Same jaw. He wouldn't tell us what they'd found, but he'd been upriver past the falls, past the old depot, and whatever he saw had scared him badly. He asked about boats. Asked which channels were patrolled. Then he gave my husband that folder and said if they didn't come back through within the month, send it downriver to anyone honest enough to carry it." Eku spread his hands. "You're the first anyone honest enough."

"They went back up," Juniper said. "Toward the depot."

"Toward the depot," Eku agreed. "And that's the last anyone saw of them. After that, the men with clipboards started coming here instead. Funny timing, isn't it. Like they knew exactly where the team had made friends."

Juniper's hands had closed around the folder hard enough to crease the plastic. Grief and fury had fused in her chest into a single hot alloy, and she couldn't have separated them if she tried—grief that Daniel had been here, *this close*, sitting in this room, drinking this same bitter tea, alive and afraid and thinking of her, maybe, or maybe hating her, and fury at the machine that had picked him up like a piece of litter and filed him away. *Transfer order,* she thought, not knowing yet why the phrase surfaced, only that it felt like a shape glimpsed under dark water.

"Take it," Ama said softly. "That's what it's for."

"There's a risk," Tomas said quietly. "If they learn this village gave us documents—"

"They already know," Eku said. "They photograph everything. Let them come. We're the ones who didn't sign, remember? We've been the ones who didn't sign for two years." He looked at Juniper, and his eyes were old and level and completely without pity. "But hear me, woman. Others have carried papers down this river. Papers get bought, papers get buried, papers get traded for safety by people who mean well. My husband's dying wish was that these reach daylight, not a drawer. If you're taking them, promise me you're not selling them, and you're not hiding them. Promise me they'll be *read.*"

Juniper looked down at the folder, at her brother's block capitals, at the compass-shaped weight against her hip.

Outside, down at the dock, an engine sound rolled up the river—distant, heavy, the low thrum of a boat much larger than theirs, moving against the current toward Kilat.

Eku and Ama exchanged a look that contained an entire conversation.

"You have maybe ten minutes," Eku said, rising. "Back door, through the gardens, there's a channel the big boats can't follow. But first—" he tapped the folder in her hands "—answer the question. Daylight, or a drawer?"

Juniper met his eyes, and the lie she'd been carrying for fifteen years—the comfortable one, the *I'm nobody, I'm just passing through*—sat on her tongue like a stone.

"Daylight," she said.

Down at the dock, the big boat's engine died.
