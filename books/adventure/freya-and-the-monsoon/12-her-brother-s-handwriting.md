## 12. Her Brother's Handwriting

The documents were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was wrapped in a woven mat, and the mat had been tucked beneath the elder's sleeping platform with the kind of care people reserve for things that might get them killed.

Juniper sat cross-legged on the floor of the elder's house while rain hammered the thatch overhead, and she unwrapped the bundle the way you'd defuse something.

Tomas watched from the doorway, arms folded, saying nothing. He'd been saying nothing for two days now, and she'd decided she preferred it to most men's talking.

Inside the oilcloth: three items. A waterproof field notebook, its cover swollen and warped from damp but intact. A zippered plastic pouch of sample vials, each labeled in tiny block capitals. And a map—topographic, government issue, folded soft as cloth from handling.

She knew the handwriting before she knew what it said.

That was the thing nobody warned you about. You could spend fifteen years building a person out of absence—out of a name you didn't say, a face you kept deliberately blurred—and then a stranger handed you a notebook and your whole body recognized the lettering before your mind caught up. The way Daniel crossed his sevens. The way he wrote his dates in the military style their father had drilled into them both: day, month, year, no ambiguity, because *ambiguity is where mistakes live, Junie*. The way his capital letters stood at attention while his lowercase slouched, like a man who'd learned discipline but never believed in it.

*12 March.* Water sample, confluence of the Serrano branch. Turbidity high. Sheen on surface — not organic. pH 4.1.

*13 March.* Second sample, same site, six hours later. Fish kill beginning downstream. Counted forty-one, mostly pacu. Locals say it started three days ago. Nobody will speak on record. One man took me aside and said the river "changed color after the trucks came." Asked him which trucks. He walked away.

*15 March.* Found the spur.

She stopped breathing.

Not dramatically—not the movie version where the world tilts. Just a small mechanical failure, her lungs declining the next instruction, her hands going still over the page. Rain on the roof. A child laughing somewhere outside, quick and bright and oblivious. Tomas shifting his weight in the doorway, the boat's hull creaking against its mooring line below.

*Found the spur.*

Daniel had found it. Weeks ago. Before the survey team went dark, before the fake rescuers arrived at the trading post with their pressed shirts and their questions like scalpels, before Juniper had stood in the mud of an abandoned camp holding a compass engraved with a name she'd buried—her family's name, his name, her own original name—Daniel had been alive and working and writing in this exact hand, and he had found the illegal pipeline spur the consortium would burn down half the province to keep hidden.

"Juniper." Tomas's voice, low.

"I'm reading."

"You've been staring at the same page for a minute."

She turned the page so he couldn't see it shake.

*16 March.* Followed the spur on foot. It branches off the main line at grid reference 44-South-117, runs northeast through concession land the consortium maps call "unallocated." It is not unallocated. It terminates at a settling pond built into a ravine above the Kesh tributary. When it rains hard enough, the pond overflows into the Kesh. The Kesh feeds every village from here to the delta. They are not leaking. They are draining. There's a difference, and the difference is intent.

Her eyes burned. She told herself it was the lamp smoke.

The elder—an old woman named Ilsa with a spine like a fishhook and hands that had clearly done decades of work the river had since claimed—sat across from her, mending a net by lamplight, pretending not to watch. She'd given up the bundle without ceremony. *"Your brother left these,"* she'd said, in the flat tone of someone who had already decided Juniper was trustworthy or wasn't and no longer cared which. *"He said if anyone came asking honest questions, they should have them."*

Honest questions. Juniper wondered what Daniel had seen in the elder's face that made him trust her with them. Wondered what he'd seen in hers, fifteen years ago, that had made him stop trusting her at all.

She turned to the map.

It was the same survey map she'd glimpsed in the abandoned camp—the one missing from the gear, the one she'd assumed the consortium's men had taken. Wrong. Daniel had kept a copy. Of course he had. Their father used to say Daniel backed up everything twice and hid the backups three ways, and their father had meant it as a criticism, back when criticism of Daniel was the family's favorite indoor sport.

The map was annotated in colored pencil. Blue for waterways. Red for the main pipeline route, running from the consortium's processing plant down toward the provincial terminal—a legal artery, permitted, inspected, signed off. And branching off it in orange, drawn in that precise draftsman's hand, the spur. Northeast through the concession land. Terminating at the settling pond. With a date beside it in the margin: *verified 16/03.*

And below that, smaller, almost apologetic:

*Photos in second pouch. Coordinates logged. If anything happens to us, this goes public. Not filed. PUBLIC. D.R.*

D.R. Daniel Ravensworth.

Juniper set the map down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"He was here." Her voice came out strange. Flattened. "Weeks ago. He sat in this house."

Ilsa didn't look up from her net. "Six of them. Four men, one woman, one boy barely old enough to shave. Your brother did the talking. The others ate like they hadn't seen food in days and apologized for it." She pulled a knot tight with her teeth. "They stayed two nights. The woman was sick—breathing trouble, river damp. Your brother sat up with her both nights. Didn't sleep himself, far as I could tell."

"Where did they go?"

"Upstream. Toward the falls country." Ilsa's needle paused. "There's nothing up there but old mining works and the depot."

"The depot."

"Closed years ago. Officially." The old woman's eyes came up then, gray and unhurried. "Unofficially, boats go up. Boats come down. Nobody gets off the boats coming down. That's all I know, and it's more than I've told anyone else, so let's not make me regret it."

Juniper looked at the vials. Twelve of them, each filled with water that ranged from clear to a faint rust-brown, each labeled with location, date, time, and a number code she didn't recognize but suspected matched entries deeper in the notebook. Daniel had catalogued a poisoned river with the methodical patience of a man building a case brick by brick. He'd known, even then, that the evidence would have to survive without him.

That was the thought that finally got under her armor. Not the poison, not the pipeline, not the machinery of cover-up she was only beginning to see the shape of. But the fact that her brother—her careful, stubborn, infuriating brother—had packed these documents for a stranger because he'd understood, weeks ago, that he might not be the one to carry them out.

He'd prepared for his own disappearance. And then he'd disappeared anyway.

* * *

Tomas waited until Ilsa had banked the lamp and gone to her sleeping platform, until the rain had settled from assault into steady drumming, until the house held nothing but the two of them and the small yellow circle of the last coal in the cookstove.

Then he said, quietly: "Your brother."

It wasn't a question. She'd told him about the compass days ago—or rather, she'd failed to lie about it convincingly, and he'd failed to press, and between those two failures something like understanding had taken root. Now there was no room left for the arrangement.

"Daniel Ravensworth," she said. Saying it aloud felt like setting a bone. It hurt in the right place. "He led the survey team. Six people. The ones everyone's looking for."

"And you didn't know."

"I didn't know." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Fifteen years, Tomas. Fifteen years I've kept myself so far away from that name I didn't know my own brother was in the province. I fly medicine to villages. I don't read newspapers past the weather. I told myself it was operational security, staying blind. It was just—" She searched for the word and hated the one that arrived. "Cowardice. Clean, well-organized cowardice."

Tomas lowered himself onto the bench across from her. In the coal-light his face was all planes and shadow, the graze on his arm already scabbing under her dressing. "You testified against your family," he said. Not cruelly. Just testing the weight of it, the way he tested everything.

"Yes."

"And he never forgave you."

"He was nineteen when I did it. I was twenty-four. Our father went to prison for eleven years. Our uncles scattered. The business—the whole life we'd grown up inside—came apart in a courtroom over four days, and I did most of the talking." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Daniel sat in the gallery every single day. Front row. He wanted me to see him. And I want to tell you he looked at me with hate, because hate would be simple, but he didn't. He looked at me like I was a math problem he couldn't solve. Like he was trying to find the error in my reasoning. And when it was over he walked past me in the corridor and he said—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it; some sentences you either finish or you carry forever. "He said, 'You could have talked to us first.'"

The stove ticked. Rain.

"What happened to him after?" Tomas asked.

"I don't know. I vanished the same year. Changed my name, got my pilot's license under it, went north, went remote. I told myself the distance protected him too—that being near me was dangerous, that anyone who touched my old life got burned. Maybe some of that was even true." She stared at the coal. "But mostly I just couldn't stand to find out what he'd become. Whether he'd turned into our father. Whether he blamed me. Both answers were unbearable, so I chose ignorance and called it mercy."

"And now?"

"Now he's writing field notes about poisoned rivers in handwriting I'd know in the dark, and he's been missing for five weeks, and there are men with government credentials photographing passengers at river checkpoints, and I'm sitting in a flooded village holding the only copies of the evidence that explains all of it." She looked up. "And the worst part—the genuinely pathetic part—is that some part of me is glad. He was alive here. He was *working* here. Which means when they took him, he was worth taking alive. People don't guard what they plan to throw away."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You said 'took.'"

She blinked. "What?"

"Earlier you said 'when they took him.' Not 'if something happened to him.'" His voice stayed level, but something behind it had sharpened. "You believe he's alive."

Did she? She turned the notebook over in her hands, feeling its water-swollen weight. "The camp was clean. Gear laid out neat, no struggle, no tracks. If they'd died out there—if the river took them—we'd have found something. A body, a boot, a bag. Rivers give things back, Tomas. Everyone who lives on one knows that. This river has given back nothing, because nothing was put in it." She heard herself accelerating, the logic spilling out like she'd been holding it underwater. "And the fake rescuers aren't searching, they're *sweeping*. You don't sweep for bodies. You sweep for witnesses and loose ends. Bodies don't ask questions at trading posts."

"So the ledgers, the samples, the map." Tomas nodded slowly. "This is why the checkpoints. Why the photographs. They're not hunting six lost surveyors. They're making sure nothing these six people touched reaches anywhere it can be read."

"Yes."

"Then we're carrying the most dangerous cargo on this river."

"We are."

"And you still mean to take it to the capital."

"I mean to take it somewhere it can't be buried." She met his eyes. "Wherever that is. Whoever will print it, broadcast it, nail it to a courthouse door—I don't care anymore. I spent fifteen years believing the safest thing was to stay invisible. Invisible people can't testify. Invisible people can't be followed. Invisible people also can't save anybody, including themselves." She tapped the notebook. "My brother understood that before I did. He wrote *public*, in capital letters, knowing what it would cost him. The least I can do is finish the sentence he started."

Something moved across Tomas's face—there and gone, too fast to name. Recognition, maybe. Or grief. Later, much later, she would learn about the reports he'd written and the superior who'd shredded them, and she'd understand that look completely. That night, she only registered that the wariness between them had shifted, settled, become something closer to alliance.

"Sleep," he said finally, rising. "We leave at first light. The falls country is three days upstream if the water cooperates, and the water hasn't cooperated once since I've owned this boat."

"Tomas." He paused at the door. "Thank you. For not asking sooner."

"Don't thank me yet." His silhouette filled the doorway, rain silvering behind him. "Everything you just said about invisible people? I've been invisible longer than you have. Ask me tomorrow whether I believe a word of it."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat by the dead stove with the notebook open on her knees and read it cover to cover, twice, until she could have recited it.

And near the end, wedged between a sketch of the settling pond and a list of village names with interview dates beside them, she found a page that wasn't data at all.

It was dated the night before the team left Ilsa's village—the last entry before the handwriting stopped. And it wasn't addressed to any agency or archive. It was just a paragraph, written fast, the letters leaning like grass in wind:

*Saw a plane today doing med drops on the lower villages. Twin otter, blue tail. For a second I thought—no. Impossible. She's a thousand miles from here and a thousand miles from wanting to be. But God, the way it flew. Low and patient, the way she used to practice over the lake when Dad wasn't watching. Whoever you are, pilot, I hope your skies stay empty and your landings stay soft. Some of us ended up measuring rivers. I hope wherever you landed, it was somewhere safe. — D.*

Juniper read it four times.

Then she closed the notebook, held it against her chest, and let herself cry—for the first time in fifteen years, quietly, thoroughly, without shame—while the rain came down on the roof of a stranger's house and the river rose in the dark, carrying its rust-colored secret toward the sea.

At first light, they would go upstream. Toward the falls. Toward the depot.

Toward whatever was left of her brother.
