## 13. Leak Below the Waterline

The storm didn't announce itself. It just arrived, the way bad news does—one moment the river was a black ribbon under a bruised sky, and the next the whole world was sideways rain.

Tomas felt it in his hands before he heard it in the sky. The tiller went heavy, the current turned against them like a living thing with an opinion, and the first real gust came off the eastern bank and flattened the water to white noise.

"Get the tarp!" he shouted over it.

Juniper was already moving. She'd been bailing for two hours without complaint, working the dented pot in a steady rhythm that had become, somewhere downriver, something close to conversation between them—her scooping, him steering, neither needing to speak. Now she threw herself at the canvas lashed behind the midships bench, dragged it forward, and got one corner hooked over the fuel drum before the wind ripped it out of her grip.

The boat took a wave broadside. Not a big wave—the river here was wide and shallow-banked—but a cold, muscular shove that lifted the port side and dropped it hard enough that Tomas heard something in the hull say *crack*.

Not creak. Crack.

He knew boats the way Juniper knew planes: from underneath, from the inside, from years of listening to them complain before they failed. That sound wasn't a plank flexing. That was a seam letting go.

"Kill the lamp," he said. "We're beaching."

"Here?" She looked at the bank—a wall of black vegetation leaning over brown water, no shape to it, no landing, nothing but rain and dark. "There's nothing here."

"There's less everywhere else."

Another gust hit, and this time the boat answered with a different sound, lower and wetter, and Tomas felt it through his boots: the deck going soft. Taking water faster than the bilge could lie about it.

Juniper didn't argue again. He'd learned that about her in six days on the river—she argued until she had the facts, then she stopped arguing and started working. She killed the lamp, grabbed the bow line, and moved to the front of the boat low, like someone who understood weight distribution in her bones, though she'd never said so and he'd never asked.

The bank came up fast. Too fast. Tomas swung the nose into the current as much as the dying engine allowed, felt the prop bite once, twice, and then they were grinding up over submerged mud and roots with a shriek of old timber, and the boat stopped moving, and the rain kept falling like it hadn't noticed.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Just breathing, and the rain hammering the tarp Juniper had finally wrestled half over the cargo, and somewhere back in the flooded trees the sound of branches tearing loose.

"The hull," Juniper said.

"Yeah."

"How bad?"

Tomas put his palm flat on the deck plates near the bench. Cold water seeped up around his fingers, steady, unhurried. "Bad enough that we're not floating tomorrow. Good enough that we're not sinking tonight." He looked up at her, a pale smudge in the dark. "Probably."

* * *

They beached her properly by feel—winched the bow line around a tree trunk thick as a church pillar, hauled until the hull rode up on the mud shelf above the waterline, and stuffed every piece of spare cloth they owned into the worst of the leak to slow the seep while the river did its work below. By the time Tomas got the small camp stove lit inside the overturned hull's lee, sheltered under the tarp and a lean-to of cut saplings, his hands were shaking from cold and effort in equal measure.

Juniper built the fire. Of course she did. She'd stripped bark with a pocketknife in the driving rain and gotten dry tinder from the heartwood of a deadfall, and now there was actual flame throwing orange light against the wet black world, and Tomas sat beside it feeling like a man who'd been rescued by competence.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm thinking that I've poled and powered boats on this river for eleven years and I have never once gotten a fire lit in weather like this."

"Lucky knife." She fed a branch to the flames. "Lucky tree."

It wasn't luck and they both knew it, but she said it anyway, deflecting, and he let her, because he'd learned that too. Six days of sharing a leaking boat taught you a person's silences before their words. Juniper Ravensworth—or whatever her name really was; he'd stopped believing *Ravensworth* around day three—deflected with jokes when she was tired, went quiet when she was afraid, and worked with her hands when she couldn't stand either one.

The boat sat on the mud above them, listing, its belly exposed. In the firelight Tomas could see the damage properly for the first time: a seam along the second strake, starboard side aft, opened like a split lip. Old caulking gone brittle, then gone entirely. The patch job he'd done at Maren's cousin's landing had held for four days of calm water. The storm had ended that conversation.

"We need pitch or tar," he said. "Or resin. There's pine stands upstream—I saw them at the last bend. Tomorrow I can cut some and we cook it down with ash and charcoal, pack the seam, bind it with canvas strips."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"It'll hold."

"That's not what I asked."

Tomas watched the fire. "If it doesn't hold, we walk the last stretch along the high bank, carry what matters, and you swim your part of the way, because I know for a fact you won't leave anything behind."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. "You've been paying attention."

"It's hard not to. You catalog everything. You sleep facing the door. You counted the rounds in my flare gun the first night and you thought I didn't see."

"I didn't count your flare gun."

"You counted everything else."

She almost laughed, then didn't, and pulled her knees up, and for a while they just listened to the storm work itself into exhaustion over the flooded forest. The rain softened from assault to insistence. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the sky like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

"Tomas," she said finally.

"Mm."

"Why were you fired?"

He'd known the question was coming since the checkpoint, maybe since the first night, when she'd watched him refuse to look at the police launch without flinching and filed it away behind those gray eyes. He'd had days to prepare an answer. He'd prepared several. All of them were lies, and all of them were smooth, and lying smoothly was apparently contagious, because he found he didn't want to do it anymore. Not sitting next to a woman who'd bailed water for six days without complaint. Not by a fire she'd built in a storm.

So he told the truth instead, and it came out rougher than he'd rehearsed.

"Because I wouldn't bury a report."

Juniper went still. She had a way of going still that most people never managed—total stillness, breath included, like a heron deciding whether the water held fish.

"Water quality survey, northern tributaries, three years ago," he said. "I was a field inspector. Environmental compliance, provincial office. It sounds grander than it was. Mostly I tested rivers and wrote reports nobody read." He turned his hands toward the fire. They were scarred, rope-burned, engine-greased hands, and he looked at them like they belonged to a man he used to know. "Except this time somebody read it. Because what I found wasn't drift contamination or a lazy operator. It was tailings. Raw mine tailings entering the watershed through an outfall that wasn't on any permit. Arsenic, mercury, suspended metals at concentrations that made my instruments think they were broken."

"The consortium."

"You already know."

"I've been connecting dots since the rust-colored water. I wanted to hear you say it."

"Yes. The consortium. Or rather, the company that became the consortium before it bought enough names to call itself that." He kept his voice level, which took work. Eleven years of work, actually. "I wrote it up. Full documentation. Chain of custody, duplicate samples sent to an independent lab, photographs, coordinates. My supervisor thanked me, said it was thorough, said he'd handle it from there. Two weeks later the report came back to me redlined. Findings revised. Contamination attributed to 'legacy natural deposits.' My name still on it."

"They made you sign your own lie."

"They tried." The fire popped. He fed it a stick because his hands needed something to do. "I refused. Loudly. Formally. I wrote to the ministry, to the ombudsman, to a newspaper that turned out to be owned by a holding company owned by—you can guess. Within four months I was dismissed for 'procedural irregularities' dating back years, none of which existed, all of which were documented beautifully. Blacklisted from every government post in the province. My wife—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My wife left before the blacklist finished the job. Took our daughter south to her mother's people. Said she couldn't raise a child on a man's principles when principles didn't pay for rice."

Juniper said nothing. She was good at that too—listening without filling silence, which was rarer than honesty, in his experience.

"So now I run a leaking boat and move cargo that nobody official asks questions about," he said. "Medicine, mostly. Letters. Sometimes people who need to be somewhere without being seen. The river people pay me in food and repairs and looking the other way, and I tell myself it's a life." He laughed, short and without humor. "Eleven years telling myself that."

"You could have signed," Juniper said. "Kept the job. Worked from inside. Fought slower."

"I could have. And every year I would have had a new reason why this year wasn't the year." He looked at her. "You of all people should understand why I didn't sign."

Her jaw tightened. "Why's that?"

"Because you're the same kind of stubborn. You just aim yours at running instead of staying."

The words landed harder than he intended. He saw it hit—saw the flinch she buried half a second too late—and he thought she'd get up, walk into the rain, shut him out the way she'd been shutting him out since the dock. Instead she stared into the fire for a long time, and when she spoke, her voice was flat and careful, like ice on a pond early in the season.

"My turn, then. Since we're doing this."

"You don't have to."

"I know." A breath. "That's why I am."

* * *

She told him in pieces, the way people tell things they've carried too long—starting in the middle, circling back, skipping the parts that hurt most and then doubling back for them when she thought he wasn't tracking.

A family operation. Smuggling, though she never used the word—*moving things*, she said, *things that needed moving*, and he let her keep the euphemism because the shape of it was clear enough. A family that ran the border country like a private railroad. A father whose word was law and whose law had no appeals. And a daughter, nineteen years old, who sat in a government hearing room fifteen years ago and answered questions under oath.

"I testified," she said. The firelight made her face flicker, older and younger at once. "Names, routes, dates. Everything I knew. My father went away for eleven years. Two of my uncles went with him. And then I changed my name and disappeared into the bush and I've been flying away from that courtroom ever since."

Tomas kept his voice gentle. "You did the right thing."

"Don't." Sharp, immediate. "Don't do that. You don't know what it cost. You don't know who paid it." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "My mother didn't speak to me again. Not once. Fifteen years. My brother—" She stopped. He watched her decide, in real time, how much truth the night could hold. "My brother was eighteen. He didn't testify. He didn't do anything except be my little brother, and when I finished talking in that room, his whole life burned down with everyone else's. He thinks I chose the law over him. Maybe he's right. I've had fifteen years to figure out if he is, and I still don't know."

"Is that why the compass stopped you?" Tomas asked quietly. "At the camp. The engraved one."

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

"The survey team," he said. "The leader."

"My brother." Her voice barely cleared the fire's crackle. "Daniel. Daniel's out there somewhere, or he isn't, and either way it's because he walked back into the family business of being near me and I led him straight into whatever's in these rivers." She shook her head. "No. That's not fair. He didn't know I was here any more than I knew he was. But it feels the same. It feels exactly the same."

The rain had thinned to a whisper. The fire burned low and gold. Down on the mud, the wounded boat leaned against the dark like a tired animal, and the river slid past, carrying its rust and its secrets toward the sea.

Tomas found that something had shifted in his chest, some load-bearing wall coming down. He'd spent eleven years believing his story was the heavy one—the ruined career, the broken home, the report that died in a drawer. Sitting next to Juniper, he understood suddenly that hers weighed more, because she'd chosen hers. Nobody had fired her from her family. She'd walked out with the truth in her mouth and paid retail for it ever since, alone, in the air, in miles.

"We're a pair," he said. "Two people the system chewed up, running cargo on a rotten boat, chasing a mining company that owns half the province."

"Not running anymore, though." She looked at him across the fire, and there was something new in her face—not softness exactly, but the absence of armor, which on her face was more striking than any softness could be. "You're going back. To the capital. With the documents. Aren't you? Even knowing what happened to you last time."

"I'm going back," he said. "This time I'm not writing a report for a drawer. This time it goes to people, not paper. Every name, every sample, every page. Let them bury that."

She nodded slowly. Then, so quietly he almost missed it: "Maybe I will too."

He didn't push it. Some doors you hold open; you don't shove people through.

They took turns sleeping after that—real turns, announced, trusting, for the first time since the dock. Tomas lay under the tarp listening to the river and the last mutterings of the storm, and he thought about his daughter, who would be fourteen now, who had his eyes and her mother's sense, and he thought: *when this is done, whatever it costs, I'm going south to find her.* The thought didn't hurt the way it usually did. It felt more like a heading.

He was nearly asleep when Juniper spoke from her watch at the fire's edge.

"Tomas. Wake up. Look at the bank."

He rolled onto his elbow, hand finding the flare gun out of habit. Across the black water, far downstream where the channel bent, a light was moving. Not a house light—houses didn't move. Not lightning. It swept slow and deliberate along the far bank, the way a man sweeps a torch when he's searching the near shore for a boat.

Then a second light joined it.

Juniper's voice came low and even, pilot-calm, and it raised the hair on his arms.

"Somebody's looking for us. And they're checking both banks."
