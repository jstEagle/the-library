## 14. Juniper's Confession

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Juniper spoke.

They'd beached the boat on a gravel bar below the second bend, hull propped against two downed logs, Tomas's patch job drying in the heat of the flames. The storm had moved off east, dragging its thunder with it, and now the night was that particular monsoon quiet—rain still falling somewhere far away, everything else holding its breath. The river slid past black and patient.

Tomas sat with his back against a log, arm bound where the fever had come from, watching her over the fire. He'd told her his story an hour ago. The inspector's badge he'd surrendered instead of the report. The daughter whose birthday he could recite but hadn't heard in six years. He'd said it plainly, without asking for anything in return, and then he'd looked at the fire and let the silence sit there like an open door.

Juniper had been staring at that door for an hour.

She fed a branch into the flames and watched the bark curl. Somewhere across the water a frog was going at it like a man trying to start an engine in winter.

"You already know half of it," she said finally. "You just don't know you know."

Tomas didn't move. He'd learned, in the days since the dock, when to let her work up to things.

"Ravensworth," she said. "That was the name. Not Coldharbour. Ravensworth."

The name landed between them and neither of them touched it for a moment. Then Tomas shifted slightly, and she saw him doing the arithmetic—the way people did when an old scandal surfaced from wherever old scandals went to drown. There had been articles. There had been a trial. There had been, for one season, a story everyone knew and then agreed to forget.

"The smuggling operation," he said quietly. "Up north. The air corridor."

"My father built it. My uncles ran it. My brother kept the books." She said it flatly, like reading a cargo manifest. "Cigarettes first. Then whatever paid better. Guns, once, which is where I got off the ride. Fifteen years ago I stood up in a courtroom with a lawyer the feds gave me and I put my family away in sentences. Eleven of them went down. My father got fourteen years. He'll be out soon, if he isn't already."

The fire popped. Downriver, the dark went on being dark.

"I was twenty-three," she said. "I'd been flying for them since I was seventeen. Bush strips at night, no lights, no flight plan, transponder off. I was good at it. That was the whole tragedy—I was *good* at it. My father used to say I had hands like a surgeon and nerves like a nun." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He meant it as a compliment. Everything he ever said to me sounded like a compliment right up until you thought about it."

"And then?"

"And then a load went wrong." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "A pilot named Callum Reyes. He was nineteen. Younger than I'd been when I started. They sent him into weather he shouldn't have been in because the schedule mattered more than the boy, and the mountain took him, and my father's response was to fine the kid's mother for the lost cargo. Out of his estate. Out of what little there was." She heard her own voice go very even, the way it went when she was flying into something ugly. "I'd swallowed a lot by then. I'd told myself a lot of stories. But that—" She stopped. Started again. "You can't tell yourself a story about a nineteen-year-old's mother getting an invoice for her son's coffin. So I walked into a federal building and I didn't leave until they believed me."

Tomas said nothing. The river said nothing. The frog had given up.

"They offered me witness protection. New name, new region, the whole kit. I took the name and threw away the rest of it. Protection means handlers, means check-ins, means someone always knowing where you are." She shook her head. "I'd spent my whole life being known by people who owned me. I wasn't going to trade one set of owners for another. So I came out here, where nobody asks questions because nobody wants answers, and I made myself into a service. Medicine in, mail in, people out. I never stay anywhere longer than the fuel stop takes. I've slept in forty different villages and I couldn't tell you a single neighbor's name."

"That's not true," Tomas said.

She looked at him.

"The village at the confluence. You knew the woman who runs the clinic. You knew her son's name. You knew which house floods first." His voice was gentle in a way that made it worse. "You know more names than you think. You just keep them somewhere you don't have to look at them."

Juniper stared at the fire until her eyes stung. "Maybe," she said, which was as close to conceding as she intended to get tonight.

* * *

The thing about armor, she thought later, was that it didn't come off clean. It came off in pieces, each one rusted to the next.

"The compass," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen her turn it over in her hands too many times, thumb finding the engraving like a tongue finding a chipped tooth.

She dug it out of her jacket and held it up so the firelight caught the letters. RAVENSWORTH. Her grandfather's initials beneath, and the year, 1961, when the world had still been something the family could pretend to own honestly.

"Daniel's," she said. "My brother. The survey team lead—the one who's missing, the one those smiling men keep asking about. Daniel Ravensworth." She turned the compass over. "It was our grandfather's. When Dad went inside, the estate got carved up, and this was supposed to go to me. I wouldn't take it. I wouldn't take anything with that name on it. So it went to Daniel, and Daniel—" Her voice caught on something and she flew through it. "Daniel wrote to me. Every year. For ten years. Letters to a post office box I abandoned after the first one. I never opened them. I don't know why I'm telling you that except that I found it in the mud at an empty camp three hundred miles from anywhere, and the only way it got there is that my brother carried it, and the only reason he'd carry it is that some part of him still thought I might—"

She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"He was alive here," she said. "Weeks ago. The elder's documents prove it. And now he's gone, and there were no tracks, and men with government letterhead are photographing every passenger on this river, and I keep thinking: fifteen years ago I testified against my family and disappeared, and the universe has apparently decided that wasn't enough. Now it wants the other one too."

"It doesn't want him," Tomas said. "Men want him. There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I'm sitting?"

"Yes." He said it without hesitation, and she hated how much steadier his voice was than hers. "The universe doesn't run mining concessions. Men do. Men can be fought."

Juniper laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "You sound like a report that refused to get buried."

"Somebody has to."

* * *

She should have stopped there. A reasonable person would have stopped there. But the armor was off now, and underneath it she could feel all the soft exposed places she'd spent fifteen years pretending didn't exist, and the fire was low and kind, and Tomas was looking at her the way no one had looked at her in fifteen years—not assessing, not calculating, just *listening*, like her words were worth the fuel it cost to burn them.

"There's a part I've never told anyone," she said. "Not the feds. Not anyone."

Tomas waited.

"When I testified, it wasn't just my father and my uncles who went down. There were other people. People who worked the docks, the fuel depots, the strip in the north. Small people. People who took envelopes because envelopes were how the whole machine worked and they didn't know any other way to eat." She pulled a stick from the ground and turned it in her fingers. "One of them was a man named Efraim Duval. He fueled our planes. He was sweet on my aunt, or she was sweet on him, I never knew which. When the indictments came down, the prosecutors wanted him. I gave them what I knew about him. What I knew was—he handled the fuel. That's all I actually knew. But the way I said it, the timing I suggested, the details I filled in because I thought that's what a good witness did—" The stick snapped in her hands. She hadn't meant to break it. "He got four years. He had a bad heart. He died in the second year, in a facility two hundred miles from anyone who loved him, because a twenty-three-year-old girl wanted to be thorough."

The silence after that was long enough to grow things in.

"After that I understood something," she went on, quieter. "Visibility kills people. Being seen, being known, being a name in a file—it kills people. So I made invisibility into a discipline. Never stay. Never owe. Never let anyone close enough that they end up in a file next to mine." She looked at him across the fire, and her voice went rough. "And then I met you, and within a week you're shot and fevered on a gravel bar because you helped me, and I'm sitting here handing you every piece of myself like it's not going to end up costing somebody. That's the pattern. That's always the pattern. Everyone who gets close to me pays for it."

"Or," Tomas said, "everyone who gets close to you gets the truth, and the truth is the only thing that's ever saved anybody on this river."

"That's not—"

"Odile would say it better. Maren would say it better." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight moving on his face. "But I'll say it anyway. You've spent fifteen years believing your testimony killed a man. Maybe it did. Maybe the system that locked up a fuel handler while the owners bought their way down to light sentences killed him, and you were just the last honest instrument it passed through. Those aren't the same crime, Juniper. One of them you can carry forever. The other one you can fight."

She wanted to argue. She could feel the argument assembled and ready, fifteen years of it, load-bearing, familiar. But she was tired down to the keel, and the fire was warm, and the truth was that she'd rehearsed this confession alone in a hundred cockpits and it had never once ended with someone telling her she could put it down.

"Efraim Duval," she said. "Say the name back to me."

"Efraim Duval."

"So somebody besides me remembers he existed." Her throat closed. She nodded, once, and looked at the river. "Okay. Okay. That's—that's the whole ugly inventory. Smuggler's daughter. Government witness. Ghost. That's what you signed onto when you offered me passage at the dock, and I let you think I was just a bush pilot running from weather."

"I didn't think that," Tomas said. "Nobody runs from weather for fifteen years."

* * *

Later, when he'd settled against the log and his breathing had gone slow and even, Juniper sat awake with the compass in her palm and did the thing she'd sworn off doing: she thought about Daniel.

Not the abstract Daniel of the missing-persons bulletin. The real one. Eight years old, teaching himself morse code with a flashlight because their father's radio room was forbidden territory. Fourteen, drawing maps of the delta in the margins of his schoolbooks, accurate down to the sandbars. Nineteen, standing in the courtroom gallery on the day she testified, and the look on his face—not hatred, though she'd deserved hatred, but something worse. Something like a boy watching the last exit door close on a burning building and realizing his sister had already gone through it and wasn't coming back for him.

She'd told herself for fifteen years that he'd chosen his side. That staying silent was a choice too. Both things were true. Both things could be true and it still cut like broken glass that the brother who'd kept writing to a dead post office box for ten years was the same brother who'd led a survey team into country where men with polished smiles made people disappear—and that the only reason she was coming for him was a compass in the mud.

No. That wasn't true either, and she was done lying to herself; she'd done her quota for one lifetime.

She was coming for him because he was hers. Whatever the name on the compass cost, whoever she'd been before she burned it all down—Daniel was the last living piece of the girl from before, the one who'd flown at night with her heart hammering and told herself it was freedom. Saving him wouldn't absolve her. Nothing would. But maybe, Tomas's voice said in her head, maybe it didn't have to.

The fire collapsed into embers. East, beyond the black line of the ridge, the sky had begun its slow bruise toward gray.

They had maybe ninety miles of river left to the camp site, and the boat leaked, and somewhere behind them men with photographs and rifles were working downstream toward upstream, patient as water. Ahead lay the ridge where her brother had vanished, and whatever had taken him, and the fresh boot prints they both knew they'd find.

Juniper closed her hand around the compass until the engraving bit into her palm.

"Fifteen years of miles," she said softly, to no one awake to hear it. "Time to find out what they were for."

Upstream, faint as a memory, something glinted on the water—and then was gone.
