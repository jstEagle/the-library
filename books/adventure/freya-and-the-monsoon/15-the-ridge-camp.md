## 15. The Ridge Camp

They left the boat at dawn, tied off to a strangler fig whose roots gripped the bank like knuckles. Juniper had argued for going up alone. Tomas had argued back with a single sentence—"You can't run and watch your own back at the same time"—and she'd hated him for being right, which was becoming a habit.

The ridge rose out of the flooded country like the spine of something sleeping. Three days downriver from the village that went quiet, then half a day inland through country where the water didn't so much fall as *decide* to leave—seeping off the hills in a hundred silver threads, gathering itself into creeks that had no names on any map Juniper owned. She'd flown over this terrain a dozen times in fifteen years. From the air it was texture. On foot it was a country with opinions.

The rain came in the way it always did here—not falling so much as *being present*, a fine gray suspension that soaked through canvas and skin and eventually, she suspected, bone. Her boots had given up any pretense of waterproofing outside of Kavet. The mud took each step and tried to keep it.

"You said three days' walk," Tomas said behind her. He'd said it twice already.

"I said two and a half if the weather held." She pushed a wet frond aside without looking back. "Weather didn't hold."

He grunted. He was good at carrying silence, Tomas. She'd noticed that about him on the boat—the way he could go hours without speaking and make it feel like company instead of absence. Most people filled quiet because they were afraid of what lived in it. Tomas treated quiet like a tool he kept oiled.

She climbed. The slope steepened, and the jungle canopy closed overhead until the gray light turned green and submarine. Somewhere above them, water dripped in slow metronome time off leaves the size of sails. Her legs burned with the good clean ache of work, and she realized—somewhere around the second hour of climbing—that this was the first time in weeks she hadn't been thinking about the compass in her pocket, or Daniel's handwriting, or the men who smiled wrong.

No. That wasn't true. She was thinking about all of it. But the walking gave the thinking somewhere to *go*, the way a river gives floodwater somewhere to go, and that made all the difference between drowning and moving.

*Daniel had been here.* That was the thought her boots beat into the mud with every step. Not dead. Not vanished into some government euphemism. Here—on this ridge, under this canopy, close enough that if she'd flown one supply drop differently, angled one approach differently, she might have seen him from the cockpit window and never known.

Fifteen years, and the first thing her brother's ghost had handed her was a compass with her family name engraved on the case. Ravensworth. The name she'd buried in a courthouse basement along with her testimony, along with her father's face when the verdict came down, along with everything else she'd decided a person could survive without.

She'd carried it anyway. She was carrying it now, against her hip, inside the oilskin wrap with Daniel's documents, and some traitorous part of her had started thinking of the whole bundle as *him*.

"Smoke," Tomas said.

She stopped. Listened. Smelled it a moment later—old smoke, days old, soaked deep into wet wood the way only a long-burning fire could manage. Someone had camped here recently enough that the smell hadn't washed out, but not last night.

"That's not our smoke," she said.

"No."

They climbed faster after that.

* * *

The camp appeared all at once, the way camps do—one moment it's jungle, and the next you're standing in a room someone built out of absence.

A clearing maybe forty meters across, cut into the shoulder of the ridge where the trees thinned to let the fog through. Six tent platforms, raised on poles against the wet, their canvas long since collapsed or dragged off. A fire ring with stones stacked in that particular careful way survey crews stacked them—engineers' hands, methodical even in wilderness. A latrine trench screened by woven palm. A drying rack for samples, empty now except for a few labeled vials someone had missed or hadn't bothered with.

And everywhere, the neatness. That was the thing that had stopped her cold the first time, and it stopped her cold again now. Gear laid out. Packs squared. A mess kit arranged beside a cold stove as though its owner had stepped away to check a gauge and meant to come back in five minutes.

Nobody walks away from a camp like this. Nobody abandons a mess kit mid-meal unless somebody with a gun told them to.

Juniper moved through the site the way she used to move through preflight checks—hands knowing the sequence before the mind caught up. Tent platforms first: six frames, and she counted bedrolls, counted again, came up with six. Six men. Daniel plus five. The documents from the village had mentioned six signatures on the water-sample chain-of-custody forms, six sets of initials in the margins. It matched.

"Someone swept this place," Tomas said. He was crouched at the edge of the clearing, studying the ground with an attention that told her his past had included more than filing reports. "Twice. Look—they went through the tents, then went through them again slower. Everything's been opened and re-closed. Sloppily, the second time. They were in a hurry, or nervous, or both."

"Looking for what Daniel found," Juniper said. "Or looking for copies of it."

"Mm." Tomas lifted a scrap of canvas, examined it, set it down exactly as he'd found it. He'd picked that habit up from her, she realized—or she'd picked it up from watching him, and neither of them had commented. Two people who understood that at a site like this, you were walking through evidence whether you meant to or not.

She crossed to the sample rack. The vials were small, glass, stoppered with wax, each bearing a strip of tape with handwriting on it in pencil. She knew the handwriting before she read a single letter. She'd have known it upside down, backwards, through thirty years of rain.

*Site 14 — confluence, E fork. Turbidity high. Sheen on surface. D. R.*

Her thumb moved over the letters without touching them. *D.R.* Daniel Ravensworth, initialing his work the way he'd initialed everything since he was nine years old—carefully, completely, as if a job half-done was a kind of lying. Their father had beaten that into him early. Some lessons a family teaches that no court can unteach.

"He was here," she heard herself say. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew how much it was costing her. "Weeks ago. Taking samples. Documenting." She held up the vial. "This is the east fork confluence. That's upstream of every village we've passed. Every dead fish we saw, every rust-colored pool—this is the man who measured it."

Tomas came and stood beside her. He looked at the vial for a long moment. "Then they took him because of what he measured."

"Not because of what he measured." Juniper turned the vial in the gray light. "Because of what he was going to do with it. There's a difference. Plenty of people measure contamination and get ignored. Get bought. Get tired. Daniel doesn't get tired. Daniel was the kid who reported his own scout troop for flogging merit badges." She almost smiled. It hurt her face. "Once they understood who they'd taken, they couldn't just scare him quiet. They had to take all of him."

The words sat in the wet air between them. Tomas didn't offer comfort and she was grateful—he'd learned that about her too, somewhere between the bailing and the checkpoint and the fever. Comfort was a currency she'd never learned to spend.

She pocketed the vial with the rest of the evidence. Then she did what she'd come three days upriver to do: she read the camp itself, the way she'd learned to read weather, reading the story the ground told.

* * *

The story the ground told had three parts.

Part one: the surveyors had stayed here a while. Weeks, probably. The latrine trench was deep and properly maintained. The fire ring showed many burnings, layered ash like tree rings. A cache pit near the largest platform still held tins—rice, condensed milk, lamp oil—buried the way bush people bury stores, against the day you come back. *When*, not *if*. They'd expected to return.

Part two: they'd left in a hurry, but not running. No scattered gear, no dropped loads, no sign of violence in the clearing itself. Whatever had happened had happened fast and controlled. Men marched out at gunpoint leave neater camps than men who scatter. She'd seen both kinds of departure in her old life, and she knew the difference in her spine before she knew it in her head.

Part three—and this was the part that put ice down the back of her shirt—

"They went deeper," she said.

Tomas looked up from the fire ring. "What?"

Juniper was standing at the far side of the clearing, where the jungle closed in again beyond the last tent platform. The vegetation there was broken—a channel through the understory, maybe a meter wide, bending away northward toward higher ground. Not a game trail. Game trails wander. This went somewhere with intent.

"Look at the ferns. They're bent back, not broken—broken means recent, days at most. Bent means walked through repeatedly, weeks of traffic. And look at the cut saplings." She pointed. Three waist-high stems had been sliced cleanly, at angles, machete work. Surveyors marked routes that way; she'd watched field teams do it from the air a hundred times, the pale scars of fresh cuts stitching across green country. "That's a marked line. Daniel's team cut a route heading north from this camp. Deeper into the interior. Toward—"

She pulled out Daniel's map. The wax paper crackled. In the gray-green light under the canopy, the ink lines swam, but she'd studied the document so many times she could have drawn it from memory. The pipeline route ran southwest to northeast across the concession block. And northeast of the ridge camp, up in the headwaters country, Daniel had penciled a small annotation beside a bend in the river. She'd read it a dozen times without understanding.

*Spur? Verify.*

Not spur of trail. Spur of pipeline. An illegal branch line, running off the main route toward somewhere it had no concession to be. And Daniel had come up here to verify it on foot, with his instruments and his meticulous, incorruptible, merit-badge-reporting soul.

"He found the spur," she said slowly. "From the air you'd never flag it—it'd read as drainage works, or seismic lines, anything. You'd need ground truth. So they grounded themselves. Set up here, cut a route north, and walked the line." She looked up at Tomas. "And whatever they found at the end of that line is why six men with government credentials got disappeared so thoroughly that even the fake rescuers don't know where they are."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. Rain dripped off the canopy in fat, deliberate drops.

"Then we follow the line," he said.

"Yeah," Juniper said. "We follow the line."

* * *

They ate cold rations in the ruined camp because building a fire felt like shouting. Juniper cached nothing and took everything portable—the vials, a field notebook wedged under the sample rack's crossbeam that the sweepers had somehow missed, a coil of good rope. The notebook was half Daniel's hand, half other men's, and she made herself stop reading it after the third page because her eyes were burning and there was work to do.

It was Tomas who found the boot prints.

He didn't say anything at first. He just went still, in that way of his, crouched at the mouth of the marked trail where it left the clearing. Juniper watched him study the ground for a full minute before he beckoned her over without looking up.

"Tell me what you see."

She crouched beside him. The trail mouth was churned—weeks of boots, six sets of them, she could pick out at least four distinct tread patterns in the dried mud, and the older prints were softened, edges slumped, half-erased by rain.

But there, pressed into the shaded mud beneath an overhanging root ball where the rain hadn't reached—fresh prints. Crisp edges. Water still standing in the deepest part of the tread.

Two sets. Large. Heavy lug soles, military pattern, the kind of boot you buy in a provincial capital and wear into the bush when you want people to know you're not from the bush.

"How fresh?" she asked, though she already knew.

"Last night. Maybe this morning, before the rain eased." Tomas touched the standing water in the print, then touched the soil beside it, comparing. "Hours. They're ahead of us on this trail, and they're not hurrying." He looked up at her, and in the dim green light his face was hard to read, but his voice wasn't. "They're not tracking the surveyors anymore, Juniper. The surveyors are gone from here. They're tracking whoever comes asking about the surveyors."

The jungle breathed around them. Water dripped. Somewhere upslope, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"They know about the village," Juniper said. "About Maren's people, the documents, all of it. Somebody talked, or somebody followed, or—" She stopped herself. Blame could wait. Arithmetic couldn't. "Two men, ahead of us, on the only route north. Walking slow. Why would hunters walk slow?"

Tomas's jaw tightened. "Because they're not hunting. They're waiting for us to catch up. Or—" he glanced back at the abandoned camp, at its neat, terrible order, "—they're herding. Keep the trail warm and obvious, and anyone following it walks straight into whatever's at the end of it."

"The pipeline spur."

"Whatever's guarding the pipeline spur."

Juniper sat back on her heels. Fifteen years of flying had taught her a specific kind of arithmetic: fuel versus distance, weather versus daylight, weight versus altitude. Never enough margin, always a choice about which risk to carry. She ran the numbers now. Go back, and Daniel's trail went cold, and the monsoon had perhaps ten days left before the rivers dropped and the boat died and the whole interior sealed itself for another year. Go forward, and they walked a marked corridor with two armed men somewhere ahead of them, possibly more, toward a secret worth killing six government surveyors to protect.

There wasn't really a choice. There never was, once you knew where the truth was buried. That was the thing nobody told you about digging things up: the dirt gets in your blood.

"We don't take the trail," she said finally. "We parallel it. Fifty meters east, in the trees, where the mud won't write our biography. We move quiet, we move slow, and we see these two before they ever see us." She stood, knees cracking, and looked north up the invisible line of Daniel's route, where the ridge climbed into cloud. "And if they're herding us toward something—"

"Then we find out what," Tomas said, "before it finds out about us."

He shouldered his pack. Juniper took one last look at the camp—six empty platforms, a cold stove, a buried cache waiting for men who weren't coming back for it—and said, silently, to the brother-shaped absence at the center of it: *Hold on. I'm coming, and I'm bringing the whole truth with me, whether you want it or not.*

They stepped off the trail into the trees.

They'd gone perhaps two hundred meters, moving in the slow, deliberate silence of people learning each other's rhythms, when Juniper heard it—faint, upslope, threading through the rain-hiss.

A voice. Human, clipped, angry. And underneath it, unmistakable even at this distance, even muffled by wet green miles:

The crackle of a radio transmission.

Someone ahead of them was reporting in.
