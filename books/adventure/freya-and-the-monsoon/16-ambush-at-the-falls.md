## 16. Ambush at the Falls

**Juniper**

She heard the falls before she saw them—a low, continuous thunder rolling up the gorge like weather that had decided to live in one place. By midmorning the sound had swallowed everything else, and the river began to move with a new kind of intent, the current leaning into the bends, the water going from brown to a churning white-brown that made her think of a horse working itself up to buck.

"That's us," Tomas said. He nodded toward a gravel shelf on the left bank where the jungle thinned into fern and fallen rock. "Portage there. Half a mile around, maybe less if we're lucky."

"And if we're not?"

"Then it's more than half a mile."

They beached the boat on the shelf and Juniper stood in the shallows for a moment, letting her legs remember land. Eleven days on the river had turned her into something half-amphibian—her hands were soft with water, her boots had never fully dried since the second week, and she'd stopped noticing the smell of bilge and wet canvas that clung to everything they owned. She'd also stopped flinching every time Tomas moved suddenly, which was its own kind of progress.

The portage was ugly. They carried what mattered: the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth inside Daniel's old survey satchel, the map tube, the medical kit, the bailing bucket, the tarp, the rifle Tomas had produced from under his bunk on day three without explanation and she'd accepted without question. The boat itself they left—there was no carrying a leaking river skiff over fallen rock—and Tomas spent twenty minutes patching her seams with resin and canvas scraps while Juniper scouted ahead.

That was when she saw them.

Not the men. Not yet. Just the signs: a cigarette butt ground into the mud beside the portage trail, fresh enough that the paper hadn't dissolved. A branch snapped at shoulder height, the break still pale and wet. And then, through the ferns, a glint of something man-made where nothing man-made should be—the corner of a rain poncho, military green, catching the light.

Her body knew before her mind did. Fifteen years of reading country from two hundred feet in the air had taught her to see patterns before she could name them, and the pattern here was wrong. The trail was too quiet. No birds working the understory. No monkeys. The jungle around the falls had gone silent the way a room goes silent when someone's already in it.

She backtracked fast and quiet, dropped the satchel behind a fallen trunk, and found Tomas crouched by the boat.

"We've got company," she breathed. "Ahead of us on the trail. At least two, probably three. They're waiting between here and the take-out."

Tomas didn't ask how she knew. That was one of the things she'd come to value about him—he spent his disbelief instead of voicing it. "The fake rescuers."

"Who else photographs passengers at a checkpoint and then lets you go?" She crouched beside him, water seeping cold through her trousers. "They let us go because we were leading them somewhere. Or because they wanted us deep in the interior where bodies don't get found."

"So we go back."

"Back is eleven days of river and a checkpoint we barely talked our way through." She looked upstream, at the white water hammering through the gorge. "And forward is an ambush."

"There's always a third option," Tomas said.

"There is." Her mouth had gone dry. "It's just a bad one."

* * *

**Tomas**

He watched her study the rapids the way he'd seen her study weather charts, and understood what she was calculating before she said it, and felt his stomach drop anyway.

"You want to run them," he said.

"The boat floats. Barely. The patches will hold or they won't." She was talking fast now, low, the words coming out flat and even the way they did when she was most afraid—he'd learned that about her too, that her calm was load-bearing. "The falls aren't a waterfall, they're a chute. Half a mile of whitewater with a couple of drops. If we hit the tongue at the top center and stay on it, we ride it through."

"If we don't stay on it, we hit rock hard enough to open this boat like a tin can."

"Yes."

"And if the patches let go halfway down—"

"Then we swim, and we lose the ledgers, and probably drown." She met his eyes. "I'm not going to pretend those aren't the odds. But I've put aircraft down on strips shorter than my wingspan in crosswinds I couldn't measure. I know how to read moving water from above, and I've been reading this river for eleven days. It's just flying with your belly dragging."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing." Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one. "You commit before you're ready, you fly the machine you have, and you don't stop making corrections until you're on the ground or you're not."

Tomas looked downstream, at the chute. The water came through the gorge in a single braided torrent, splitting around a mid-channel island, the right channel narrower and meaner, the left one wider with a standing wave at its throat that rose and collapsed and rose again like something breathing. Below the drops the river gathered itself back together and ran on, calmer, as if nothing had happened.

Three men were waiting on the portage trail with rifles. Two were visible; there would be a third covering the take-out, because anyone competent covered the take-out.

"They'll hear us coming," he said. "The engine."

"So we don't use the engine." She was already moving, pulling the paddles from under the thwart. "We paddle into the current above the chute, swing wide, and let the river do the work. They'll have maybe ninety seconds to realize what we're doing and get to the bank, and their rifles are useless against water moving that fast unless they're very lucky."

"And if they're very lucky?"

"Then we find out what happens next." She handed him a paddle. "You've been bailing this boat for eleven days. Consider this the final bail."

He took it. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Somewhere in the last two weeks—somewhere between the fever and the firelight and the night she'd told him about the courtroom and the name she'd buried—his fear had changed shape. It wasn't smaller. It had just stopped being only his.

"For the record," he said, swinging the satchel into the boat and wedging it under the thwart, "this is a terrible plan."

"Noted for the record." She pushed the bow off the gravel with one boot and climbed aboard. "Get in."

* * *

**Juniper**

The first thirty seconds were the hardest, because the first thirty seconds were silence.

They paddled hard for the middle of the channel, upstream of the chute, the boat wallowing with every stroke, the patched seams weeping thin threads of water that Tomas bailed without being told. Behind them and below, hidden by the bend, she imagined the ambushers hearing the splash of paddles and starting to run along the bank, and she kept her eyes forward, on the water, on the tongue.

There. Where the channel narrowed above the first drop, the current drew together into a smooth, glassy V pointing downstream—the tongue, the safe line, the river telling her where it wanted her to go. Everything outside the V was chaos: boils and eddy lines and the white heads of submerged rocks combing the surface.

"Left channel," she said. "Stay with me."

"I'm not going anywhere," Tomas said, and there was something in his voice she'd never heard before—not fear, exactly. Readiness. As if some part of him had been waiting fifteen years for a moment that required everything he had and no hesitation at all.

They crossed the eddy line and the river took them.

It happened the way takeoff happened—one moment resistance, the next momentum, the boat surging under them as the gradient steepened and the world accelerated. Spray came off the first wave and hit her full in the face, cold enough to make her gasp, and then she stopped gasping and started flying.

Because that was what it was. The water had texture the way air had texture, and she could feel it through the paddle the way she'd felt it through the yoke—pressure building here, dropping away there, the boat wanting to yaw left and her correcting with a sweep stroke before the yaw became a broach. A hole opened off the starboard bow where water poured over a ledge and folded back on itself, and she leaned the boat away from it with her weight alone, hip against the gunwale, and the hole slid past close enough to touch.

"Tongue!" she shouted, though Tomas was already there, already centered, already bailing with one hand while bracing with the other.

The first drop came up fast—a two-foot ledge, nothing, except that at this volume of water nothing was a lie the river told. The bow lifted, hung, and slammed down into the trough, and the whole boat flexed around them with a sound like a drum being struck, and one of the patches peeled loose with a soft tearing whisper she felt more than heard.

Water came in over the bow in a sheet.

"Bail!"

"I'm bailing!"

The second drop was bigger. Through the spray she saw the standing wave at its base, tall as a man, folding and unfolding, and beyond it the white blur of the third rapid where the channel split again around a rock the size of a house. She picked her line the way she'd pick a path through a storm cell—reading the gaps, committing early, trusting the machine.

They went over the second drop sideways.

For one long, impossible moment the world was all water—green walls rising on either side, sky gone, the roar so total it wasn't sound anymore but pressure—and then the boat slapped down flat, spun a quarter turn, and the current grabbed them again, and they were through, still floating, still bailing, the ledgers still lashed under the thwart.

A rifle cracked from the left bank.

The bullet went somewhere behind them—she heard it slap water or rock, she'd never know which—and a second shot followed, high, the shooter leading them badly or firing at a range no one should attempt. She didn't look. Looking was how people died in moving water. She dug the paddle in and pulled, and Tomas pulled, and the boat answered the way a good machine answers, imperfectly, faithfully.

The last rapid was a long brawling slide, no single drop, just half a mile of the river shaking itself apart and reassembling. They rode it out in a chaos of spray and correction, Juniper calling lines half a second before she needed them—"right, RIGHT, lean"—and Tomas moving with her, and somewhere in the middle of it she realized they weren't two people in a boat anymore. They were a crew. The word arrived whole, unbidden, and she held onto it the way she held the paddle.

Then the gorge opened, the water smoothed, the roar fell away behind them, and they were through.

* * *

**Tomas**

They drifted a mile before either of them spoke.

The boat sat low in the water, riding on the last inch of freeboard, and they bailed in shifts without discussing it, the rhythm of it almost peaceful after the noise. The sun came through a tear in the clouds and lay gold on the water, and the jungle on both banks dripped and steamed, and somewhere a bird started up again—the first birdsong Tomas had heard since the falls, the jungle exhaling.

His hands were shaking now. He noticed it with detached interest, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"You counted the shots," Juniper said finally. She was facing downstream, paddle across her knees, watching the water. "Two. Both high."

"You counted too."

"Pilots count everything. Rounds, gallons, hours." She glanced back at him. "Two shots means at least one of them didn't shoot. Means they hesitated, or they were repositioning, or—" She stopped.

"Or they weren't trying to hit us," Tomas finished.

The thought settled over the boat like the spray had, cold and fine. He worked through it aloud, because that was what they did now—they thought together, out loud, two minds circling the same problem until it gave something up.

"If they wanted us dead, the portage trail was the place. Dry ground, cover, easy shooting. Instead they let us see them—or we saw them by luck—and they fired twice from a bank we were already past." He shook his head slowly. "They wanted us in the river. They wanted to see if we'd run the rapids."

"Why?"

"Because running the rapids tells them something." He watched her profile, the set of her jaw, the way her eyes never stopped scanning the far bank. "It tells them how much the ledgers matter to us. How far we'll go. What we'll risk."

"Or it tells them we're desperate," Juniper said. "Which we are."

"Desperate people can be predicted. That's why they push." He looked down at the water seeping around his boots, at the boat that had carried them through something it had no business surviving. "They'll report that we shot the falls rather than give up the satchel. Whoever's paying them will know exactly what kind of people they're dealing with now."

"People who don't quit."

"People who are four days from Odile's relay station," Juniper said quietly, "with a boat that's dying."

* * *

**Juniper**

They beached at the first gravel bar wide enough to work on, and Juniper hauled the boat up while Tomas unpacked the repair kit, and for a while neither of them talked. There was a language in shared work that didn't need words—she held the seam tight while he laid the resin, he steadied the hull while she stitched the torn patch with waxed twine from the med kit, and the boat slowly, grudgingly, agreed to hold water again.

It was Tomas who broke the silence, and when he did, he wasn't looking at the boat. He was looking at her.

"Ninety seconds," he said.

"What?"

"On the portage. You said they'd have ninety seconds to react once they figured out what we were doing. You'd already done the math before you asked me to get in the boat." He turned the resin tin over in his hands. "You'd decided before you pitched it. You just needed me to say yes."

Juniper sat back on her heels. The river ran past, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream the way it carried everything—silt, seeds, secrets, the occasional dead fish with rust-colored gills.

"I did the math," she admitted. "But I wasn't going to run it alone. That's the difference." She heard the words leave her mouth and understood, as they went, that they were truer than she'd planned. Fifteen years ago she'd made the biggest decision of her life alone, in a courthouse hallway, and she'd been alone ever since, and she'd called the aloneness freedom because the alternative was admitting it was exile. "Before, I would have just gone. Paddled off and let the river sort it out. Now I—" She gestured vaguely, at the boat, at him, at the mended seam between them. "Now apparently I need a crew."

Tomas smiled. It was a small smile, worn at the edges, but real. "Careful," he said. "Say things like that and I might start believing I'm useful."

"You bailed a sinking boat through a Class Four rapid without dropping the ledgers. You're useful."

They worked another hour in the failing light, and when the patches had cured enough to trust—which was to say, to distrust slightly less—they ate cold rice and watched the dark come down over the gorge. The falls muttered somewhere behind them, far away now, a sound like a held breath finally released.

It was Juniper who heard it first, because Juniper heard everything now; eleven days on the river had rewired her ears. An engine. Faint, upstream, on the water. Not the putt-putt of a village longboat—something bigger, heavier, running against the current with purpose.

Tomas heard it a heartbeat later and went still.

"How far to Odile's relay?" he asked.

"Four days," Juniper said. "Maybe three and a half if the water stays high."

"And them?"

She listened again, head tilted, the way she used to listen for engines in mountain passes where a wrong guess meant a mountainside. The sound was gaining. Slowly—but gaining.

"Two days," she said. "They've got something faster than us." She looked at the mended boat, at the dark water, at the man who'd followed her over a waterfall. "So we don't sleep tonight. We paddle."
