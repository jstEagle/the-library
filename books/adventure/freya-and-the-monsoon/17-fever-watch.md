## 17. Fever Watch

The fever came for him the way the river came for the banks—slow at first, patient, then all at once.

Tomas knew it before Juniper said anything. He'd been wounded enough times to know the difference between a body that hurt and a body that was losing. The graze along his ribs from the falls had been nothing, a hot stripe he'd laughed off while they bailed the boat. But by the second morning it had gone quiet in the wrong way, and the skin around it had turned a color he didn't like thinking about, and when he pressed two fingers against it the heat came back up his arm like a wire.

"You're infected," Juniper said. She wasn't asking.

"It's fine."

"Take your shirt off."

"I'm fine."

"Tomas." She crouched beside him in the boat's shallow stern, rain drumming the tarp they'd rigged over the middle of the hull. "I've dropped medicine into villages for fifteen years. I know what sepsis looks like before it kills you. Shirt. Off."

He looked at her—this woman who measured everything in miles and never in years—and saw that she wasn't going to argue about it. So he peeled the shirt over his head, moving carefully on the left side, and heard her breath change when she saw the wound.

It wasn't pretty. The graze had scabbed, then wept, then scabbed again, and now the edges were angry red with thin lines crawling outward toward his chest like roots looking for water.

"That's lymphangitis," she said. "You know that word?"

"I know what it means."

"Then you know where those lines go if we don't stop them." She was already digging through the crate she kept lashed under the bow seat—the med-drop supplies, the ones meant for villages that had no doctor, no clinic, sometimes no road. Amoxicillin in foil strips. Gauze. A bottle of iodine half gone. Syringes wrapped in plastic like small silver fish. "I have antibiotics. Not enough for a full course, but enough to make this a fight instead of a surrender."

"Give them to someone who needs them more."

"There is no one who needs them more. You're the only other person on this boat." She tore a foil strip with her teeth. "And I need you walking when we get where we're going. So take the pills, drink the water, and let me clean this out. Those are the terms."

He almost smiled. "You always negotiate everything?"

"Only things worth having." She shook two tablets into his palm. "Swallow."

He swallowed.

* * *

The first day of the fever, he fought it the way he fought everything—quietly, stubbornly, as if refusing to be sick were the same as not being sick. He bailed water one-handed while Juniper steered through channels that had stopped resembling any map. He insisted on taking the night watch. He ate half of what she gave him and claimed the rest made him nauseous.

By the second day, the fight went out of him all at once, the way a fire goes out—not gradually but in a single collapsing moment—and Juniper found him shivering under the tarp with his teeth chattering hard enough that she could hear them over the rain.

She beached the boat on a mud bank beneath a stand of half-drowned palms and built a shelter from the tarp and a fallen trunk, and there the fever took him properly.

Time went strange after that. He remembered it later in pieces, the way you remember a storm:

Juniper's hands, cool and certain, wringing out a cloth in river water boiled over their little stove and laying it across his forehead.

The taste of the antibiotics, bitter chalk, and her voice counting: *One more. You can do one more.*

Rain on the tarp, endless, a sound so total it became silence.

Her face above him, lit by the small flame of the stove, framed by wet hair stuck to her cheeks, and the expression on it—an expression he'd never seen her wear in all the weeks since the dock. Not the flat pilot's calm. Not the wary arithmetic of a woman calculating exits. Something softer and more frightened than either.

He dreamed. He knew they were dreams even while inside them, because the dead don't walk in waking life, and his father had been dead eleven years.

*Tomas. You're letting the water rise.* His father standing at the edge of a flooded field, arms crossed, in the posture he'd used for every disappointment of Tomas's life. *I told you. I told you what happens to men who read too many reports and say too many true things.*

"The reports were true," Tomas said, or tried to say. His mouth wasn't working right. "That was the whole point of them."

*And where did truth get you? Fired. Blacklisted. Your name on a list that follows you like a smell.* His father turned away. *Your daughter doesn't even remember your face.*

"No," Tomas said, and the word came out loud, and somewhere far away a woman's voice said *shh, shh, you're dreaming,* and he couldn't stop. "No. She remembers. She has my eyes. Marisol told me—Marisol wrote me, before they cut the mail route—she said Lucía looks at photographs and points and says *papá*, she says it to strangers on the street, she says it to anyone with dark hair—"

His voice cracked apart.

"She's four," he said, to no one, to the rain, to the flooded field in the dream. "She was two when I left. Two years old. I told Marisol I'd be back before her birthday and then the ministry buried me and I thought—I thought I'd come back when I had something. When I could bring something home besides empty hands and a ruined name. And now I'm lying in a boat dying of a scratch and she's four and she probably thinks—"

He didn't finish. There was no finishing sentences like that. They just ran out of road.

When he surfaced from the dream, Juniper was holding a cup of water to his lips, and her hand was shaking slightly, and she pretended it wasn't.

"Drink," she said.

He drank.

* * *

The third day was the worst.

The infection crested like a wave, and Tomas rode it without dignity. He talked. That was the worst part—he knew he was talking, could hear himself doing it, could not stop. The fever stripped away whatever careful architecture he'd built around himself, the same way it would strip anyone, and what was underneath came pouring out in fragments.

He talked about the inspection reports. Pages of them, numbers and dates, chromium levels in the water samples, the signatures of men who had signed off on readings they'd never taken. He recited figures he hadn't consciously remembered in years—*0.8 milligrams per liter, 1.2, 2.4, the safe limit is 0.05, do you understand, do you see what they did*—his voice rising until Juniper pressed him back down against the folded life vests.

He talked about the hearing. The room with the long table and the water glasses and the director who wouldn't meet his eyes. *You can resubmit your findings with the appropriate contextual amendments,* the man had said, and Tomas had asked what context made poisoned children acceptable, and that had been the end of it.

And he talked about Lucía.

Mostly about Lucía.

"Marisol sends photographs," he murmured, in a lull between shivers. "Used to. Before. There's one I keep—in my head, not my pocket, pockets get searched—Lucía on a beach, holding a crab, terrified of it and delighted at the same time. Both at once. That's her exactly. Terrified and delighted. She gets it from—" He frowned, lost the thread, found it again. "From nobody. From me. I was never terrified of anything except being ordinary."

Juniper sat beside him through all of it, wringing cloths, measuring pills, feeding him sips of rehydration salts mixed into boiled water. She didn't tell him it would be all right. She didn't tell him to rest or hush or save his strength. She just stayed, and listened, and once—when he said *she'll be six before I can look at her without shame*—she reached out and put her hand flat on his chest, over the bandage, and left it there until he slept.

Later, much later, in a thinner part of the fever, he felt her talking to him. Low, steady, not really meant for him to hear.

"My brother used to get fevers like this," she was saying. "Daniel. Every winter, bad ones. My mother would sit up with him all night and I'd pretend to sleep in the next room and listen to her sing. She had this song about a river. About how the river takes everything eventually, so you should only give it what you can spare." A pause. The cloth wrung out. Water dripping back into the basin. "I haven't thought about that song in twenty years. Funny what comes back."

He wanted to answer her. To say *tell me the rest, tell me about Daniel, tell me why you flinch every time someone says your family name.* But his mouth wouldn't obey, and the fever pulled him back down, and the last thing he registered was her hand again on his chest, and the rain, and the small stubborn flame of the stove.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the fever broke.

It broke the way storms break—one moment the world was furnace and murk, and the next he opened his eyes and the light through the tarp was just light, gray and ordinary, and his own body belonged to him again. Weak. Wrung out. Sore in every joint, as if he'd been beaten by weather itself. But his.

Juniper was asleep sitting up, her back against the hull, her head tipped forward, the med kit open beside her with two foil strips left in it. Two. Out of a full course. She'd been rationing them like gold because they were gold, because after this there were no more drops to make, no more crates, no more supplies until the capital.

She woke the moment he moved. Pilots, he thought. They sleep like cats.

"You're back," she said. Her voice was rough with disuse.

"Apparently."

"How do you feel?"

"Like I was run over by a slow river." He tried to sit up and discovered that his abdominal muscles had filed a formal complaint. "How long?"

"Four days. Almost five." She handed him water. "You missed the worst of the storm. Lucky."

"Lucky," he repeated. He looked down at his ribs. The bandage was fresh, changed recently, the skin around it still pink but no longer angry, the red lines faded to faint traces. "You changed this."

"Every few hours. You were very unhelpful about it. Kept trying to help."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. Just don't die. It's inefficient." But she said it quietly, and she didn't quite meet his eyes, and he understood suddenly how long four days of nursing a delirious man in a leaky boat must have weighed on her—how alone she'd been with it, keeping watch over a stranger's fever in flooded country with hunters somewhere behind them and a clock ticking on the monsoon.

"Juniper." He waited until she looked at him. "What did I say? While I was under."

She considered him for a moment. Whatever she decided to tell him, he could see her deciding it, weighing it the way she weighed fuel loads and weather windows.

"You talked about your daughter," she said finally. "Lucía. Four years old. Afraid of crabs and delighted by them anyway." A pause. "And you talked about reports. Numbers. Chromium levels. And a hearing where a man offered you 'contextual amendments.'"

Tomas closed his eyes. "So. Everything."

"Pretty much everything."

"Well." He exhaled slowly. "Now you know why I don't trust the law. It had its chance with me. It chose the men with the money."

"And now I know why you're really on this river." She looked at him steadily. "Not running from something. Going toward it. You want to finish the report."

"I want to finish the report," he agreed. "Fifteen years late. With names attached. The kind of report nobody can amend."

They sat with that for a moment. Rain on the tarp, softer now. The river sliding past beyond the palms, brown and swollen and carrying, as always, its cargo of secrets.

"Two doses left," Juniper said, nodding at the med kit. "If it comes back, we're in trouble."

"It won't come back."

"You don't know that."

"No," he admitted. "But I've decided to be an optimist about my own blood. A man has to draw the line somewhere."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"Can you travel?" she asked.

"Today?"

"Tomorrow, maybe. The water's dropping. Every day we wait, the channels close a little more. After the monsoon breaks, this whole route dies until next season." She glanced upstream, the way she'd been glancing for days, as if she could hear something coming. "We need to move."

"Then tomorrow we move." He flexed his hand, testing it. It worked. Everything worked, barely. "Juniper. Thank you. For staying."

"You'd have done the same."

"I'd have done the same badly. I'd have panicked and given you the wrong dose and apologized a lot."

This time the smile actually arrived, brief and tired and real. "Yes. Probably."

She started packing the med kit, and he watched her, and something in his chest felt strange—not the infection, something else, something warmer and more dangerous. Trust, maybe. Or its first draft.

"Juniper," he said. "While I was feverish. You said something too. About a song. A river song your mother sang, when your brother was sick."

Her hands went still over the med kit.

"Daniel," he said gently. "Every winter. Bad fevers."

For a long moment she said nothing. The rain filled the silence. When she spoke, her voice was level, but he could hear the cost of keeping it that way.

"We should check the boat," she said. "See what the storm did to the hull."

And that was answer enough—that she turned away, that she reached for work instead of words—but as she climbed down into the hull to inspect the seams, she paused, one hand on the gunwale, and said without turning around:

"After the gorge. Maybe. If we live that long."

Then she was moving again, checking planks, all business, and Tomas leaned back against the hull with his fever-broken body and his four days of exposed secrets and understood two things clearly.

First: she was carrying something heavier than any of the evidence in her pack, and someday soon she was going to have to set it down.

Second: the water was dropping, the clock was running, and somewhere ahead of them—upstream, past the checkpoint country, past the rust-colored shallows—there was a ridge camp with fresh boot prints in the mud, and whoever made them was still walking the same trail.

He just hoped they'd reach it before the trail reached them.
