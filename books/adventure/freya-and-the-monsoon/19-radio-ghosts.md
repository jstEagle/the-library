## 19. Radio Ghosts

The ridge rose out of the flooded forest like the spine of some drowned animal, and Juniper climbed it because Tomas was too weak to stop her.

"You should be resting," she'd said that morning, and he'd given her the look of a man who had spent three days sweating through fever and knew exactly how much of him was left. "I mean you," she'd corrected. "You should be resting."

"And you," he said from the shade of the tarp they'd strung between two saplings, "shouldn't be climbing alone." His voice was still gravel-rough, his color wrong, but his eyes were clear for the first time in two days, and that clarity frightened her more than the fever had. A man with fever argues. A man without it has decided something.

"I'm not going far."

"You're going up." He nodded at the ridge through the mosquito netting. "You've been looking at it since dawn like it owes you money."

She hadn't realized she'd been so obvious. Fifteen years of practice at being unreadable, undone by a week on a leaking boat with a man who noticed everything and said almost none of it aloud.

The truth was simple: Daniel's map had marked this ridge. A small triangle in pencil, drawn in her brother's meticulous hand, with an elevation figure beside it and two letters she'd turned over all night until they gave up their meaning—*RR*. Radio relay. The survey team had come prepared to talk to someone. Which meant there was a chance, however thin, however drowned in static and distance, that someone could still talk back.

Or that whatever the surveyors had left behind on this ridge could tell her where they'd gone.

She climbed.

* * *

The monsoon owned the sky here. Not the building pressure of the coast or the moody build-and-break of the interior lowlands, but a full occupation—cloud stacked on cloud to a height that made noon feel like late afternoon, rain arriving not as weather but as condition, the default state of the world. It came down in shifting curtains, sometimes fine as mist, sometimes heavy enough to hurt, and between the curtains were intervals of dripping silence that felt stolen rather than earned.

Juniper moved through them the way she'd learned to move through everything: efficiently, without wasted motion, counting her steps without meaning to. Old habit. In another life, counting steps had been survival arithmetic—paces to the truck, paces to the treeline, paces between the watchman's rounds. She'd thought she'd buried that woman so deep even she couldn't find her. But the last three weeks had been an excavation. Every mile downriver, every lie told at checkpoints, every hour nursing Tomas through the fever had dug a little deeper, and now here she was on a wet ridge in the middle of nowhere, moving like a smuggler again, thinking like one, and hating how natural it felt.

The antenna mast was still standing.

She saw it before she understood what she was seeing—a vertical line where vertical lines didn't belong, guyed with wire, rising maybe eight meters out of a shelf of rock near the summit. Her heart did something complicated: lifted and dropped at once. Because a mast meant equipment, and equipment meant the surveyors had stood exactly here, and standing exactly here was the last thing anyone who vanished without footprints should have done.

The equipment box was bolted to the rock beneath the mast, green steel, government gray stenciling worn but legible: INTERIOR SURVEY DIVISION. The lock was cheap and the hinges were rusted and neither mattered, because the box had already been opened. Properly opened, recently—the cut edges of the hasp still bright. Someone had come up here after the surveyors disappeared. Someone with tools and time and no fear of being found.

Juniper crouched beside it and made herself look carefully, cataloguing the way she used to catalogue a room she'd just searched. Transceiver gone. Battery pack gone. The logbook clip empty, a few spiral shreds of paper still caught in the rings—torn out in a hurry, or taken whole. But whoever had stripped the unit had missed things, because people always missed things. A spare handset, coiled cable furred with mold, tucked behind the battery bracket. A laminated frequency card, soft-edged with damp, listing relay stations by code name. And wedged into the corner where the steel floor met the wall, small enough to overlook, a folded square of plastic laminate with a pencil rubbing inside—a tracing of a map section, done quickly, the wax catching every contour line.

She recognized the region immediately. She'd flown over it a hundred times. The upper gorge country, above the falls, where the river narrowed and the concessions began. And penciled across the tracing in handwriting she would have known anywhere—in the dark, in her sleep, across fifteen years and half a continent—was a string of numbers.

Coordinates. Four sets of them, each annotated in that same precise hand: dates. Three weeks old. Two weeks old. Ten days. Six days.

Daniel had been alive six days ago. Or someone using his radio had wanted people to think so.

* * *

She sat on the wet rock with the rubbing in her hands and let the rain hit her shoulders and tried to think like a pilot instead of a sister, because the sister was useless right now, the sister wanted to scream down the valley until the whole drowned world answered.

A pilot would ask the practical questions. Was the transmission real? Could a recording be scheduled? Yes—it could. Any decent transceiver could store audio and rebroadcast on a timer; she'd rigged worse herself, flying medicine into places where nobody stayed put long enough to answer a call. So the coordinates might be a message from a living man, or a dead man's voice on a loop, or bait set by the men who'd taken the survey team, playing her brother's voice like a lure over open water.

Every possibility was terrible. Only one of them was useful.

Because either way—and this was the thought that steadied her, that cooled the fire in her chest down to something she could work with—either way, the coordinates pointed somewhere specific. Upriver. Into the gorge country. Toward the pipeline spur she'd traced on Daniel's map, toward whatever the consortium had built in the folds of the hills where no honest surveyor was ever meant to look. If Daniel was alive, that was where they were holding him. If Daniel was dead, that was where the proof of it lay, along with everything else they'd killed to bury.

Either way, she was going. The only question was whether she went with information or went blind.

And for information, she needed the handset, the frequency card, and altitude—and luck, which she'd stopped believing in around the time she'd started believing in consequences instead.

* * *

Tomas heard her coming and had the kettle on, which was his way of saying things he didn't say. He looked better. Not good—his skin still had the waxy sheen of a body fighting itself, and the graze on his arm had scabbed into an angry red island—but his eyes tracked her steadily as she dropped onto the tarp's dry patch and laid out her findings between them like cards.

He picked up the rubbing first. Studied it for a long moment. Then the frequency card. Then he looked at her.

"You want to go up there."

"I want to know if my brother is alive."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's the only version of it I care about."

He set the rubbing down and rubbed his eyes, and she watched him weigh something—not whether to argue, she realized, but how much truth to spend. He'd been doing that all week. Spending it in increments. A name here, a scar there. Last night, half-asleep, he'd murmured a word she pretended not to hear: *Lucía.* A daughter's name, she'd guessed, though he'd never confirmed it, and she'd never asked, because there was a courtesy between people carrying wounds—you didn't press on the bruise unless invited.

"Those coordinates," he said finally. "If they're real, they're deep in concession territory. If they're fake, they're a trap in concession territory. Either way, we'd be walking into the one place on this river they don't have to pretend."

"We."

His mouth twitched. "Don't make it strange."

"Tomas, you can barely stand."

"I can stand." He proved it, slowly, holding the tarp pole, and she hated how much effort four meters of flat ground cost him, and admired how little complaint accompanied it. "What I can't do," he said, when he'd lowered himself back down, breathing harder than the climb deserved, "is sit here another day listening to you decide things alone. I did that in an office for eleven years. Signed off on other men's decisions because the alternative was my signature next to a lie. Never again. If you're going into those hills, I'm on the boat, or I'm on the ridge, or I'm in the way—but I'm there."

It wasn't heroics. That was what convinced her. There was nothing performative in him at all; it was simpler and harder than that. He'd been a man who watched, and watching had cost him everything, and he would not watch anymore. She understood that with a sympathy that felt like recognition, because she'd built fifteen years out of the opposite choice—never watch, never stay, never be anywhere long enough for watching to become complicity.

"All right," she said. "But first we find out if there's anything to walk toward."

She held up the handset.

* * *

The mast needed work. Two of the guy wires had parted, and the antenna leaned like a drunk against the grey sky, and the coax run down to the equipment box was chewed in one place—rodents, or the damp, or a knife. Juniper spliced what she could with electrical tape from her med kit and re-tensioned the guys with tent line, working with hands that remembered a hundred field repairs, and all the while the rain came and went in its slow curtains and the valley below breathed its green breath and somewhere under all that water was a world that had swallowed six surveyors without leaving a footprint.

They waited for the interval between squalls. You learned patience in the bush or you learned nothing.

"Standard relay schedule is top of the hour," Tomas said. He'd insisted on coming up after all—he'd made it halfway and refused to go back, and she'd stopped arguing because arguing cost energy they both needed. He sat propped against the rock shelf with the frequency card in his lap, calling out codes. "Station Kilo-Foxtrot is the provincial capital. Kilo-Golf is the mining district office—that one's theirs, forget it. Kilo-Sierra..." He paused. "Kilo-Sierra isn't on any list I ever saw. No location given. Just a frequency."

"Off-book relay."

"Somebody's private channel." He glanced up at her. "Survey teams don't usually get private channels."

"No," Juniper said quietly. "People running from their own employers do."

She keyed the handset anyway. What did she have to lose that hadn't already been lost? She worked the dial to Kilo-Sierra's frequency, checked her watch against the hour, and pressed transmit.

"Any station, any station, this is—" and here she hesitated, because names were dangerous, names were the whole war— "this is a friend of the survey party. Calling any station. Over."

Static. The particular static of high country in monsoon—an oceanic hiss with lightning crackle buried in it, the sound of a sky too busy drowning the earth to carry voices. She tried again. Tried the provincial relay. Tried the district. Nothing but weather and distance.

On the fourth attempt, on Kilo-Sierra, the static changed.

It was subtle at first—a rhythm underneath the hiss, a pulse that wasn't pulse. Then it resolved, the way shapes resolve out of fog, and Juniper's whole body went cold and hot at once, because the rhythm was a voice, broken and repeating, and the voice was—

"—day forty-one. Position—" burst of lightning, swallowing whole seconds— "—moving us upriver. If anyone receives, mark these. Mark these. First marker—" 

Her hand shook so badly she nearly dropped the handset. Tomas took it from her gently, held it steady, and she saw that his knuckles had gone white too, that he'd understood before she'd said a word whose voice that was, because she'd told him about Daniel on the boat, in the flat careful way she told everything now, and he'd filed it away the way he filed everything.

"Writing?" he said. "Now."

She wrote. On the back of the frequency card, in the rain-shadow of her own body, she wrote the numbers as they surfaced from the static, each set followed by that clipped, unhurried cadence she knew better than her own heartbeat—her brother reading coordinates the way he'd read grocery lists at fourteen, the way he'd read flight manifests at nineteen, methodical, complete, trusting the listener to keep up. Day forty-one. Day forty-one of what? Of captivity? Of movement? The recording—or the voice, God, let it be the voice—cycled through four coordinate sets, each with a date annotation, and then:

"—if this reaches Juniper—" 

The static surged. She lunged for the handset but Tomas held her wrist, firm, and they both leaned into the hiss like swimmers leaning into current.

"—tell her the compass points home. Tell her I kept the notes dry. Tell her—" and here the voice did something that undid her completely, that cracked her open in the rain on a ridge in the middle of nowhere— it laughed, short and rough, a laugh with exhaustion in it and something that might have been hope. "Tell her she was right. About all of it. She'll know what that means. Recording repeats in ten minutes. Out."

The static closed over the silence like water.

* * *

They sat on the wet rock while the transmission cycled twice more, confirming the numbers, filling gaps the lightning had torn. Neither spoke. There was nothing to say that the rain wasn't already saying.

*She was right about all of it.*

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of believing her brother had written her off as a traitor, and he'd spent his last free breaths on a mountaintop telling a machine to tell her she'd been right. Right about the family business. Right about what it would cost. Right to burn it all down and disappear, even if disappearing had looked, from the outside, exactly like abandonment.

"He kept the notes dry," she said at last. Her voice came out wrecked. She cleared her throat and failed to fix it. "That means the water samples. The documentation. He still has it. Wherever they're holding him, he still has the evidence."

"Or he wants whoever's listening to believe he does." Tomas said it without cruelty, the way you'd note a headwind. "Bait works both directions, Juniper."

"I know."

"Do you?" He turned to look at her fully, and the fever had burned away whatever remained of his caution. "Because I've watched you for a week. You calculate everything—wind, water, fuel, risk. But this man makes you fly blind, and the people who set that recording, if it is a recording, are counting on exactly that."

She wanted to be angry. She reached for anger and found it wouldn't catch, because he was right, and they both knew he was right, and the fact that he'd said it anyway—said it to a woman holding a radio handset with her brother's voice still ringing in it—meant he respected her more than he feared her reaction. That was new. That was terrifying. That was, she was beginning to understand, what trust looked like from the inside: someone telling you the truth when the truth was expensive.

"Then we go armed with the doubt," she said. "We assume it's a trap. We assume every coordinate is a kill zone until proven otherwise. And we go anyway." She folded the frequency card into the waterproof pouch with Daniel's map, with the compass bearing her family name, with the ledgers' copies Tomas had photographed page by page during the fever nights she'd thought he was sleeping. "Because if it's real, my brother is alive six days ago, and every day I wait is a day the river drops and the roads open and they move him somewhere I'll never find. And if it's fake—" she looked out at the drowned valley, at the curtain of rain sweeping the far ridgeline like a slow erasure— "then they went to a lot of trouble to use his voice, which means his voice is worth something to them alive."

Tomas was quiet a moment. "That's the coldest hopeful thing I've ever heard."

"I'm a bush pilot. Hope out here is a fuel gauge on E and a tailwind."

Below them, the forest steamed. Somewhere downriver, men with polished smiles and government radios were asking after a woman who matched her description. Somewhere upriver, past the falls, past the gorge, in the fold of hills where the concessions began, a recording of her brother was counting the days.

Ten minutes, the voice had said. Repeats in ten minutes.

Juniper keyed the handset one last time, knowing no one was there to hear it, needing to say it anyway.

"Hold on, Danny," she said. "I'm coming. And this time I'm not disappearing."

She broke down the antenna behind her, wiped the site clean of their presence the way she'd been taught to wipe clean a lifetime ago, and started down the ridge toward the boat, toward the falls, toward the coordinates burning in her pocket like a second compass—one that pointed not north, but home.
