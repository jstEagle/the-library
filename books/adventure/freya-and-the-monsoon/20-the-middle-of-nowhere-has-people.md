## 20. The Middle of Nowhere Has People

*Juniper*

The channel narrowed after the checkpoint until the flooded forest pressed in from both banks and the boat seemed to be threading a green corridor with no exit. Juniper kept her hand on the tiller and her eyes moving—canopy line, water surface, canopy line again—but there was nothing to fix on. No cut stumps, no tin roofs catching light, not even the blue plastic sheeting that clung to every settlement she'd ever flown over. The river here looked untouched, which was its own kind of wrong. Nothing stayed untouched this deep into the monsoon unless it wanted very badly not to be found.

Tomas stood at the bow, one hand on the gunwale, watching the water ahead the way he'd watched it all morning: patiently, like a man reading.

"You know where we're going," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know where we're not going." He turned halfway. "Anywhere with a dock. Anywhere with a generator you can hear before you see it. Anywhere the consortium has mapped."

"And that leaves what? Open water?"

"It leaves people who don't want to be on anyone's map."

She should have argued. She'd spent fifteen years perfecting the art of arguing her way out of other people's plans, and the skill hadn't rusted like everything else had. But the checkpoint was still sitting behind them like a stone in her boot—the cameras, the polite man with the clipboard photographing passengers while his colleagues pretended to search for lost surveyors—and every hour they ran the main channels, the net drew tighter. Her brother's compass rode against her sternum inside her shirt, and she'd caught herself touching it three times since dawn, the way you touch a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

"Show me," she said.

Tomas didn't smile, but something around his eyes loosened. He pointed left, toward a gap in the trees so narrow she'd taken it for shadow. "There. Kill the engine when I say, and let the current do the work. They'll hear us long before we see them if we come in loud."

"They'll hear us anyway. This boat sounds like a dying lawnmower."

"That's why we go in quiet and let them decide we're not worth the trouble."

The gap swallowed them. Branches dragged at the canopy overhead, dripping, and the light went green and dim as water through glass. Juniper killed the engine and steered by pole and paddle, feeling the current's pull, the hull scraping once over something soft—sunken brush, maybe—and then the channel opened without warning into a lagoon the size of a stadium, ringed entirely by flooded forest, and she understood that she had been expected.

Boats were drawn up along a fallen trunk in the middle distance—a dozen of them, dugouts and longboats, hulls painted dull grey, no registration numbers anywhere. Houses stood on stilts among the drowned trees, thatch and salvaged corrugated iron, connected by walkways that floated on barrels. Laundry hung between two dead kapok trees. A child sat on a walkway edge fishing with a hand line, and did not look up at their arrival, which told Juniper more than any greeting could have. Children here had been taught that strangers were weather: noticed, endured, not engaged.

People emerged onto the walkways as Tomas poled them in. Not many. Enough. Faces in doorways, a woman with a machete across her knees, an old man mending a net whose hands never stopped working even as his eyes tracked them the whole way in. Nobody called out a challenge. Nobody waved. The whole settlement watched them arrive in a silence so complete Juniper could hear the drip of water off the canopy.

"Tomas," she said quietly. "What exactly are we walking into?"

"A favor." He shipped the pole. "I testified at hearings for six years. You meet everyone eventually—the scientists who wouldn't sign, the clerks who leaked, the families who wouldn't sell. Some of them ended up out here, because out here is the only place the consortium's paperwork doesn't reach." He glanced back at her. "They took me in when I had nothing but a briefcase full of reports nobody would read. I'm asking them to take in two more."

"And if they say no?"

"Then we sleep in the boat and leave at first light, and I'll owe them the embarrassment of having asked."

He raised one arm—not a wave, some specific angle of forearm and elbow—and held it there. Ten seconds passed. Twenty. Then, from the largest house at the lagoon's center, a lamp was lit behind a window, snuffed, lit again. Two flashes.

"That's yes?" Juniper asked.

"That's *come slowly and alone*. You stay with the boat."

*Tomas*

Maren received him on the central walkway, seated in a rattan chair that had been carried out into the rain specifically so she could receive him standing in it, which was the kind of joke she'd been making for forty years and which only half the room ever caught. She was small and round and iron-grey, with forearms like mooring rope and eyes that performed the entire function of a customs inspection in under four seconds.

"You look terrible," she said.

"The river's been hard on the boat."

"The river's been hard on *you*. The boat is a separate tragedy." She nodded past him at Juniper, who stood at the gunwale with the wariness of a heron. "Your pilot doesn't like being left behind."

"She's not my pilot."

"No. And I'm not a grandmother. We're both lying comfortably." Maren rose, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and studied Juniper across thirty feet of rain. Whatever she saw made her grunt—an assessment concluded. "Bring her. Bring the boat under the big house. If she touches anything with a blade, we'll discuss it."

The big house turned out to be a hall built on rafts, its floor planks worn smooth by decades of feet, its walls papered with charts—river charts, hand-corrected, some of them older than Tomas, marked in pencil with depths and sandbars and, in red, places that were simply crossed out. He knew what the red meant. Everyone here knew what the red meant. Places where boats had gone in and not come out. Places where the enforcers kept depots, or kept people.

Word moved through the settlement faster than the rain fell off the eaves. By the time Juniper had tied off under the hall, a dozen river people had gathered on the walkways around it—not crowding, just present, the way a jury is present. An old woman brought fish stew without being asked and set it down and left. A boy of perhaps ten stared at Juniper's flight jacket from a safe distance with naked longing until his mother pulled him away by the collar.

Maren fed them first. That was the rule here and Tomas had remembered it even before she said it: nobody talks business hungry, because hungry people agree to things. The stew was catfish and wild ginger and something bitter he couldn't name, and it was the best thing he'd eaten in eleven days, and he watched Juniper eat hers with the mechanical efficiency of someone refueling rather than dining, and thought: *there's a woman who hasn't been looked after in a very long time.*

When the bowls were empty, Maren set down her spoon and folded her hands.

"Now. Tell me why the river police photographed your faces at the Kessel checkpoint, and why the men doing it wore rescue armbands over uniforms that belong to Halvorsen Mining's site security."

Tomas felt Juniper go still beside him. "You know about that?"

"I know about everything that moves on this water that shouldn't." Maren's voice didn't rise; it compressed, like a fist closing. "Halvorsen's men wear orange now. Officially they're 'community liaison.' Unofficially they're the reason the village at Bent Narrows has eleven empty houses. Unofficially they're the reason old Sefu's grandson went into the depot at Kilometer Forty-One and came out eight weeks later and won't say what happened in there, except at night, when he screams." She looked at Juniper. "You've seen them. Up close. So. Talk."

Juniper talked. She gave it to Maren straight, or straight enough—the survey team, the camp with no tracks, the compass, the documents from the quiet village, Daniel's handwriting, the pipeline spur, the ledgers waiting upriver at the depot. She did not mention her name, or the name on the compass, or fifteen years ago, and Tomas watched her elide those things with a fluency that hurt to recognize, because he'd done the same thing in a hundred meetings with officials: tell the truth with holes in it shaped exactly like yourself.

Maren listened without interrupting, which Tomas had never known her to do. When Juniper finished, the old woman was quiet for a long moment, looking at the rain coming off the roof in a silver curtain.

"Six surveyors," she said finally. "Government men."

"Government *contractors*, mostly. But government."

"Mm." Maren tapped one blunt finger on the table. "Three months ago, a family came through here heading downriver. Father, mother, two girls. They'd worked the concession camps—cooks, cleaners. The father had photographs. Payroll sheets showing Halvorsen paying men who were also on the provincial security roster. Double-dipping. Buying protection with public money." Her jaw tightened. "They asked me for the ghost route south. I gave it to them. They made it as far as the delta ferry."

Tomas heard it before she said it. "They didn't cross."

"The ferry burned that night. Officially, an accident. The family was on the north bank when it burned, and by morning they were gone, and the photographs were gone, and the man who owned the guesthouse where they slept was arrested for arson he couldn't possibly have committed, and is still in prison, and will still be in prison when we're all dead." Maren picked up her spoon and put it down again. "So understand what you're carrying, girl. Understand what happens to evidence on this river. It doesn't get destroyed. It gets *drowned*. There's always water nearby. Water explains everything."

"I understand," Juniper said.

"Do you." Maren leaned forward. "Because here is what I see. I see a woman with a dead man's compass"—Juniper's hand moved involuntarily toward her shirt, and Maren's eyes flicked down to the movement and back up, satisfied—"carrying documents that have already gotten one family erased, traveling with a fired inspector who thinks institutions can still be shamed, planning to take all of it to the capital, where the people you'd be handing it to eat breakfast with the men in those ledgers." She sat back. "That's not a plan. That's a delivery route. For them."

The rain filled the silence. On the walkway outside, someone began bailing a boat, the rhythmic slosh of it steady as a heartbeat.

"There are other routes," Maren said at last. "Routes the maps don't have. Channels that open only in flood, through country the consortium can't patrol because their boats draw too much water and their men can't read this river with their instruments, only with their eyes, and their eyes are city eyes." She spread one hand flat on the table, over the old charts. "I can put you on those routes. I can move you past every checkpoint on this water like smoke through a screen. I have done it for scientists and smugglers and one very frightened junior minister, and I have never yet lost anyone I guided."

"But," Tomas said.

"But." Maren looked at him, and then at Juniper, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from Maren was more frightening than shouting. "Nothing on this river is free, and the most expensive thing on it is trust. Before I spend mine on you, I need to know what you intend to do with what you're carrying. Because I have watched brave people trade these truths away quietly—hand them to some official who promises discretion, some lawyer who promises results—and the truth goes into a drawer and the drawer goes into a building and the poison keeps flowing and ten years later I'm guiding the next family down the same ghost route." Her finger came down on the table, once, hard. "So. What will you do with it?"

Juniper met the old woman's gaze, and Tomas saw her reach for the smooth lie, the diplomatic half-answer, the thing she'd been doing since the trading post—and saw her stop herself. Saw her sit with the question instead, uncomfortably, honestly, the way you sit with cold water.

"I don't know yet," Juniper said. "I came out here to find my brother. Somewhere on the way that stopped being the whole mission. Ask me again when I know whether he's alive."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed—one dry bark, entirely without humor—and pushed back her chair.

"Honesty. From a woman with holes in her story big enough to fly a plane through." She stood, joints cracking. "All right. You sleep under my roof tonight. Tomorrow I'll show you the routes, and we'll talk about what it costs." At the doorway she paused, silhouetted against the rain, and added, without turning:

"And girl. The compass. Whoever Daniel Ravensworth is to you—he was here too. Six weeks ago. With five others and a government seal on their papers." Now she turned, and her eyes were old and tired and certain. "He asked me the same question you just answered. He also didn't know yet."

She went out into the rain, leaving the words hanging in the lamplight like smoke.

Juniper didn't move for a long time. Then her hand came up and closed around the compass through her shirt, and Tomas watched her breathe, once, twice, and understood that the ground under her had just shifted again—that the brother she was chasing had walked this same floor, asked this same question, sat in this same chair, and gone on upriver into whatever was waiting.

"He was here," she said quietly. Not to Tomas. To herself.

"Yes," Tomas said. "And Maren knows more than she's said. She always does."

Outside, the rain kept falling, and somewhere in the flooded dark, a night bird called three times and got no answer.
