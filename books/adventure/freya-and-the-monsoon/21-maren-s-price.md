## 21. Maren's Price

Maren's house sat on stilts above the brown water like a heron standing in a stream—patient, deliberate, built to outlast whatever came up the river. Inside it smelled of woodsmoke and dried fish and something sharper underneath, camphor maybe, or the memory of sickness. Juniper sat on a bench worn smooth by generations of other people's waiting, and waited.

Tomas stood at the window with his arms crossed, watching the river people move through the flooded forest below. They traveled without engines when they could, poling through drowned trees, and they never crossed open water in daylight if a shadowed channel would do. Fifteen years of moving like that had taught Juniper to recognize the grammar of hiding. These people spoke it fluently.

"You're staring," she said quietly.

"I'm learning," Tomas said. "There's a difference."

The curtain over the inner doorway moved and Maren came through carrying a clay teapot and three cups with no handles, chipped at the rims. She was smaller than Juniper had first thought, a compact woman in her sixties with hands that looked like they'd been carved from dock pilings. She poured without asking, and the tea was bitter enough to make Juniper's eyes water.

"You flew medicine to Serrano Bend two years ago," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"I did."

"The child there. The one with the fever. You landed on a strip that was half mud, took off again in rain that should have grounded you, and went back twice more for supplies." Maren drank her tea and watched Juniper over the rim. "The mothers there still talk about it. The pilot who wouldn't stop coming."

Juniper said nothing. Compliments were hooks. She'd learned that from men who'd smiled wrong.

"They also say you never stayed," Maren continued. "Never ate with anyone. Slept in your plane. Left before dawn." She set down the cup. "I've been thinking about why a woman does that."

"Maybe I just don't like people."

"Maybe." Maren's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Or maybe you're the daughter of a smuggler who testified against her own blood and buried her name so deep even she can't find it anymore."

The room went very still. Beside the window, Tomas turned his head slowly, but he didn't look surprised, and Juniper understood that he'd already told her—or that she'd already known, because women like Maren knew everything that moved on this water, and had probably known what Juniper was since the moment the boat slid into the reeds.

"How—" Juniper started.

"There was a man named Coldharbour who ran cargo up this river twenty years ago. Guns, mostly, later fuel and untaxed ore. He had two children. The girl flew for him when she was barely old enough to reach the pedals." Maren refilled her cup as though discussing the weather. "Then one day the girl talked to the federal prosecutors, and the operation died, and the girl died too, officially. Drowned, they said. And a bush pilot named Ravensworth appeared out of nowhere a few years after, with flying skills nobody could account for and no history anybody could find."

Juniper's hands were flat on her knees. She noticed she'd pressed them there hard enough to whiten the knuckles, and made herself relax them. "You've been sitting on that information. Why?"

"Because information is only useful when it has somewhere to go." Maren leaned forward. "And now yours has somewhere to go. Those ledgers in your bag. Your brother's notes. The map of the pipeline spur. That's not information anymore, girl. That's a weapon. And weapons get used by whoever holds them last."

"I know what I'm carrying."

"Do you?" Maren's voice didn't rise, but something under it did, like a current under calm water. "Because I've seen this before. Four times, now. People come through here with proof of what Halvard Mining and its friends in the provincial government have done to this river. Good people. Angry people. And every one of them, when the pressure comes—and it always comes—they trade. Quiet settlement. Money to the family of whoever died. A promise to fix things that never gets kept. The evidence goes into a drawer, the drawer goes into an office, and the office belongs to the men who bought it." She picked up the teapot and poured again, though nobody had asked. "The river doesn't get clean. The dead stay dead. And the next person who finds out has to start from nothing."

Tomas shifted by the window. "You're talking about the inspector before me," he said. "Osei."

"I'm talking about Osei," Maren agreed. "A good man. He had photographs, water samples, internal memos. He traded them for a transfer and a pension and a quiet life in the capital. Last I heard he tends a garden." She looked at Tomas without malice. "You didn't trade. That's why they fired you instead of buying you. But you understand the arithmetic better than most."

"I do," Tomas said.

"So." Maren set the pot down and folded her hands, and Juniper saw that beneath the dock-piling texture they were trembling slightly, and understood that this old woman was afraid—not of the enforcers, not of the consortium, but of being betrayed one more time by someone she'd chosen to trust. "My guides will take you upriver. Through the channels the company boats can't follow. Past the checkpoint at Kessel Narrows without stopping. To the depot where your brother was held, and onward to Odile's station if that's where you're going. It will cost me favors I cannot replace and put my people in danger I cannot undo. I'll pay it gladly—for one price."

"Name it," Juniper said.

"You give everything to Odile. All of it. Ledgers, maps, samples, names, dates. On the record. Under your real name." Maren's eyes were pale gray and absolutely steady. "Not anonymously. Not through a lawyer. Not 'a source close to the investigation.' You stand in front of that microphone and you say: my name is Astrid Coldharbour, and here is what my family helped build, and here is what I saw, and here is what these men did."

The name hit Juniper like cold water in the face. She hadn't heard it spoken aloud in fifteen years. She'd almost convinced herself it belonged to someone else—a girl who'd died in a river that didn't exist, in a story she told herself at night so she could sleep.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can walk to the capital." Maren shrugged. "It's four hundred kilometers of flooded country, and the monsoon has five days left in it, and the men hunting you have radios and fast boats and no souls. Or you can wait here until the water drops and try your luck overland, and they'll find you before the mud dries. Or—" she spread her hands, "—you can take those documents and find some official willing to bury them for a price, and become one more ghost on this river, and I'll wish you luck and mean it, because I'm not cruel. But I won't spend my people's safety helping you hide the truth one more time."

* * *

Juniper walked out onto the platform at the top of the stairs and stood looking at the river.

The water was high and slow and the color of old tea, carrying whole trees past the stilt houses, carrying silt and secrets and, somewhere upstream, the rust-colored bleed of mine tailings that had killed the fish for sixty kilometers. Below her, two children poled a dugout through the drowned forest with the effortless competence of kids who'd never known dry land. One of them waved at her. She raised a hand back before she could decide whether to.

Fifteen years.

She tried to count what invisibility had actually bought her. A name that fit like borrowed clothes. A plane she loved more than any person. A rhythm of arrival and departure that meant no one ever got close enough to be hurt by what she was, or hurt her by leaving. It had worked. That was the terrible thing—it had genuinely worked. No Coldharbour had gone to prison because of anything she'd done since. No smuggler had come looking for the witness who'd torn the family apart. She'd paid for her testimony with her own existence, and she'd told herself for fifteen years that it was a fair price, that the girl named Astrid had gotten what she deserved.

But Maren's arithmetic kept running in her head, and it wouldn't stop. Every year she'd stayed hidden, the river had kept dying. Osei had traded his photographs for a garden, and the fish had kept floating belly-up. Some clerk in some provincial office had signed a transfer order for six surveyors—six living human beings—and the machinery had ground on, unwatched, because everyone who could have watched had chosen the drawer instead.

And Daniel. Daniel had been out here doing the opposite. He'd walked into the interior with his meticulous notebooks and his water samples and his stupid, brave, stubborn integrity, and the machinery had swallowed him alive for it. Her brother had spent fifteen years presumably hating her—she couldn't blame him; she'd put their father in prison—and all that time he'd been fighting the same fight she'd started and then abandoned. He'd finished what she began. He'd paid for it with everything.

What was her excuse?

"You don't have to answer her tonight."

Tomas had come out onto the platform without her hearing him. He leaned on the rail beside her, careful of the wound in his arm, and looked at the water.

"She gave me until morning," Juniper said.

"Maren gives everyone until morning. It's how she makes sure you mean it." He was quiet a moment. "For what it's worth—I already gave my name away once. In a hearing room, with lawyers on both sides and my career on the table. It cost me everything I had built." He turned to look at her. "And I'd do it again tomorrow. Because the version of me that stayed silent was smaller than the version that spoke. Not safer. Smaller."

"That's easy to say when your family weren't the criminals."

"It's not easy to say at all." His voice was even, but there was iron under it. "You think I don't understand wanting to disappear? I have a daughter I haven't seen in four years because I couldn't face telling her I'd failed. I know exactly what hiding costs. I've been paying it in installments."

Juniper looked at him. Fever had hollowed his face and the bandage on his arm needed changing, but his eyes were clear, and she realized that somewhere between the storm repair and the rapids and the fever watch, she had stopped performing for him—the smooth lies, the managed distance—and that he had noticed, and that neither of them had mentioned it.

"If I do this," she said slowly, "there's no going back. Not to the alias, not to the routes, not to the life. Every smuggler my father ever dealt with, every corrupt official in those ledgers, every man who ever worked for the family—they'll all know the name Astrid Coldharbour is alive and talking. I'll spend the rest of my life looking over my shoulder. So will anyone near me." She heard her own voice flatten. "So will you."

"I've been shot once already this month," Tomas said. "My standards for danger have adjusted."

"This isn't funny."

"No. It isn't." He pushed off the rail and faced her fully. "But listen to me, Juniper—Astrid—whatever you want to be called tomorrow. There are two ways to live with fear. You can run from it, and let it choose your route forever. Or you can walk straight at it and make it react to you." He nodded toward her bag, where the ledgers sat wrapped in oilcloth. "Right now, those men are reacting to you. Chasing you. Choosing your route. Maren's offer is the first time anyone's offered you the other direction."

Below them, the children in the dugout laughed at something, the sound carrying thin and bright across the water, and Juniper thought: this is what the river sounded like before the tailings. This is what it's supposed to sound like.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the corner of Maren's front room with an oil lamp turned low and read Daniel's notes one more time, cover to cover, and this time she read them not as evidence but as handwriting—as her brother's careful, upright script, the same script he'd used on birthday cards he'd signed *Danny* when they were young enough for that to matter. In the margins he'd written small annotations. *Confirm flow rate at second gauge.* *Ask the elder at Serrano about the fish die-off in '09.* *If anything happens to me, the duplicates are with M.*

With M. With Maren. He'd trusted this woman with his life's work before he'd ever trusted the government that employed him, and he'd been right to.

Near dawn, Maren appeared in the doorway of the inner room, dressed, awake, unsurprised to find Juniper sitting in the lamplight. She poured the last of the bitter tea and sat across from her, and for a long time neither woman spoke.

"My mother used to tell a story," Maren said finally. "About the monsoon bird. A woman who carried the rain's secrets inside her, and everyone told her to keep them buried, because the truth was too heavy to hold up to the light. And she carried it alone for years, and it got heavier every year, the way secrets do. Until one day she opened her hands and let the light touch all of it—and the weight didn't crush her. It turned to rain, and fell on the dry ground, and things grew." She drank her tea. "My mother said the moral was that secrets rot in the dark and grow in the light. I think the real moral is that the woman was tired, and carrying it alone was killing her either way. At least this way, something grows."

Juniper looked into her cup. "If I say yes, I need conditions."

"Name them."

"Odile broadcasts everything within hours of receiving it. Not filed away, not verified for weeks while lawyers circle. If we're doing this, it goes out before anyone can buy it, burn it, or lose it."

"Agreed. Odile's stubbornness is legendary. It's why she lives on a boat."

"Second. My brother. Whatever happens—if he's alive, if he's not—the story includes him. His name, his work, what they did to him. He didn't survive this"—her voice caught, and she pushed through it—"he didn't go through this to be a footnote in someone else's exposé."

Maren's expression softened by a degree, which from her was a flood of emotion. "Daniel's name will be in the first sentence."

"Third." Juniper set down the cup and met the old woman's eyes. "When it's done—when the broadcast is out and the officials come swarming—you help me disappear one last time. Just long enough to get Daniel out of the country if he needs to go. After that, I'm done running. But I won't hand myself to the wolves before my brother is safe."

Maren considered this for a long moment, and outside the first gray light of dawn crept across the river, and somewhere downstream a boat engine coughed and caught—the wrong hour for honest traffic, and both women noted it without comment.

"One last disappearance," Maren said. "For your brother. Then you stand still and let the world look at you." She extended her hand across the table, dock-piling palm up. "Do we have terms?"

Juniper looked at the hand. Fifteen years ago she'd raised her right one in a courtroom and sworn to tell the truth, and then she'd spent every day since making sure the truth never touched her again. Somewhere in those years she'd stopped believing she'd ever have the chance to finish what she started.

She took Maren's hand. "We have terms."

Maren nodded once, briskly, business concluded, and stood to wake her guides. But at the doorway she paused, and looked back, and for just a moment the flint left her face.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your brother wrote one more thing in those notes. Page forty-one, bottom margin. He didn't know who'd end up reading it." She tapped the oilcloth bag. "Read it before we leave."

Juniper unwrapped the ledger with fingers that had suddenly gone clumsy and turned to page forty-one. The bottom margin, cramped small, as if he'd written it in the dark:

*M. says there's a pilot on this river who flies medicine in storms and never stays. If it's who I think it is — I don't hate you, Astrid. I never did. Finish it.*

The lamp guttered. Outside, the engine sound was closer now, and Maren's voice cut sharp through the stilt house: *"Boat coming. Kill the light. Everyone up—we leave now."*

Juniper closed the ledger against her chest, over her heart, and reached for her bag.
