## 22. Halfway Water

The fever broke on the third morning, and by the fourth Tomas could sit up without the world tilting. By the fifth, he was bored, which Juniper said was the surest sign of recovery a man could show.

They'd made camp on a sandbar island in the middle of the river—a wide tongue of pale gravel and driftwood that the flood had split off from everything. Maren's guide had left them there with the repaired boat, a tarp, and instructions to wait for the second boat coming up from the delta to carry them the rest of the way. The waiting was the hard part. The river ran high and brown on both sides of them, carrying whole trees past their camp like it was nothing, like the world hadn't noticed anything wrong.

Tomas sat propped against a driftwood log with his side wrapped and his shirt off, letting the weak sun find the gap between clouds. The wound had closed into an angry red seam just below his ribs. It itched now instead of burned. He'd been shot before—once, years ago, in a stairwell in the capital by a man whose name was still redacted in the official record—and he knew the difference between a wound that was healing and one that was deciding whether to kill you. This one had decided.

Juniper was at the waterline, washing bandages in a bucket of river water she'd boiled twice over the small fire. She worked with the economy of someone who'd done field medicine out of a plane's cargo hold for fifteen years. She didn't waste motion. She didn't waste words either, most days, but today she kept glancing up at him when she thought he wasn't looking, checking him the way you check an engine gauge—not because you expect failure, but because you'd never forgive yourself if you missed it.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"Assess what?"

"Whether my nurse is going to poison me." He nodded at the bucket. "That water's been through your filter twice. Any more boiling and it'll come out as steam."

She wrung a bandage, snapped it flat. "Infection kills more bush pilots than crashes do. I've pulled men out of swamps who argued less than you."

"I believe it." He shifted, testing the pull along his ribs, and looked out at the river. Somewhere upstream, past the falls they'd shot and the checkpoint they'd lied their way through and the village that had gone quiet, there was a depot full of ledgers and a brother who might or might not be alive. Somewhere downstream, behind them, were the men who'd turned that river into a crime scene. And somewhere beyond all of it, in the capital, there were courts and ministries and newspapers, all of them staffed by people who got paid whether the truth came out or not.

He'd been thinking about it for three days, flat on his back with fever chewing at him. Thinking was about all a man could do on a sandbar.

"Can I ask you something," he said.

"You can ask."

"When this is over—the ledgers, Daniel, all of it. What do you think happens?"

Juniper carried the bandages back to the tarp and sat down across the fire from him, folding them into a neat square. "Depends what you mean by over."

"The evidence. Odile. Whatever comes after." He gestured vaguely downstream, toward the capital, toward everything. "You've seen what happens when reports get filed. You told me yourself—you flew supplies past those concessions for years. Did anyone ever stop the dredges?"

"No." She said it flatly, no drama in it. "I reported a fuel dump in a tributary once, year three or four. Sent photos, coordinates, the works. Got a letter back eight months later thanking me for my civic engagement." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Then the fuel dumps kept happening, so I stopped writing letters."

"That's the point I'm making."

"Is it?" She looked up at him then, and her eyes had that flat gray steadiness he'd learned to read on the boat—the look she got right before she said something true. "Because I've been thinking too. Three days of watching you sweat and mutter about filing systems gives a person time."

"Told you about that, did I."

"You called me 'the deputy director' twice. Very romantic."

He laughed, and it hurt, and he didn't care much. "Deputy director of contamination assessment. Fourteen years. I had a staff of nine and a filing cabinet that weighed more than your plane."

"And you buried nothing."

"I buried plenty." He said it before he could dress it up, and watched her go still. "Not deliberately, at first. That's the thing nobody tells you. You don't get asked to bury anything. You get asked to reclassify. To wait for more data. To consolidate findings into a preliminary assessment pending stakeholder consultation. Every document I ever softened had a legitimate-sounding reason attached, and every one of those reasons came from a man in a good suit who genuinely believed he was being reasonable." He picked up a stick and drew a line in the gravel between them. "The system doesn't run on villains, Juniper. It runs on procedure. Villains are just what procedure looks like when it stops pretending."

"So why refuse? If it was all so reasonable."

"Because of a fish." He turned the stick in his fingers. "There was a village—Kilometre Forty-One, we called it, nobody used real names in my reports—and the kids there had been swimming in the same water their grandfathers fished. My sampling came back with arsenic at forty times the safe level. Forty times. I wrote it up clean. No hedging, no preliminary language. And my director called me into his office, closed the door, and said, 'Tomas, you're describing a future problem, and governments don't govern futures.' Then he offered me a transfer. Promotion, actually. Out of contamination entirely. Into community liaison." He smiled without any humor in it. "They wanted to promote me away from the truth. That's how it works. Not a gun to your head. A better chair."

"What did you do?"

"I put the report in the mail anyway. All forty pages. Unsoftened." He dropped the stick. "And eleven days later I was unemployed, my security clearance was revoked, and a lawyer I'd never met sent me a letter explaining, very politely, that certain statements in my report could be construed as actionable misrepresentation of departmental position, and that litigation would follow if I repeated them publicly. I couldn't even tell my own story without being sued for telling it." He shook his head. "My wife stayed six more months. Then she took our daughter and went to her mother's in the south, and I let her go, because a man with no income and a lawsuit hanging over him isn't a husband, he's a weather system. Bad climate to raise a kid in."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. On the far bank, a heron stood in the shallows like something painted there.

"So you think the courts are bought," she said finally. "The ministries are bought. The lawyers are bought. And your answer is—" she tipped her head at the satchel where Daniel's documents rode, wrapped in oilcloth, "—what? Hand it all to Odile anyway? Put it in front of the same machine that ate your report and your career and your family?"

"Yes."

"One word, Tomas. One word, and I want the honest one. Why?"

He'd known the question was coming since the night he'd told her about Kilometre Forty-One, and he'd turned the answer over during every watch of every night since. Because it wasn't enough to say *because it's right*. She'd flown medicine into dying villages for fifteen years and gotten a form letter for her trouble; she knew exactly how much good intentions weighed.

"Because of what happened after my report," he said. "Nothing. Officially, nothing. But unofficially—I kept track, afterward, out of spite mostly. Two journalists picked it up. Small ones, hungry ones, the kind who work out of rented rooms. They couldn't print the report itself, but they printed around it. A radio station in the provincial capital ran a segment. A church group organized a water drive. And two years later—two years, mind you, long after everyone forgot—a junior minister with ambitions used it as ammunition in an internal fight, and the concession upstream of Kilometre Forty-One lost its renewal. Didn't get shut down. Just didn't get renewed. And the company moved its tailings pond twelve kilometres further from the village." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Twelve kilometres is not justice. Twelve kilometres is an insult with paperwork. But it was twelve kilometres that existed only because the truth got out, once, in daylight, and daylight is the one thing that machine cannot buy back. They can buy judges. They can buy ministers. They can buy the men who photograph passengers at checkpoints. What they cannot do is un-print a thing. Once ten thousand people have heard a name spoken on open frequency, that name belongs to everybody, and no amount of money gets it back into the dark."

Juniper fed a stick to the fire. Watched it catch.

"Maren thinks the same thing," Tomas went on. "You know that. It's why she gave us the route. Why she paid for it with—" He stopped himself, but the sentence finished itself in the silence anyway. *Why she paid for it with her freedom.* "She's watched thirty years of quiet deals. Families compensated privately. Complaints withdrawn. Evidence traded away in exchange for safety, always in exchange for safety, and every single time, the river got a little deader and the men got a little richer. She told me, that first night: *quiet justice is just injustice with better manners.*"

"She also told me quiet keeps people alive."

"She did." He met her eyes. "And she's right. Both things are true, Juniper. That's the trap. Silence protects the people in front of you and kills the ones you'll never meet. Daylight endangers the people in front of you and might—might—save the ones downstream. There's no version of this where nobody pays. There's only choosing who pays, and being honest with yourself about the fact that you're choosing."

The river slid past, brown and patient, carrying its cargo of drowned trees. Juniper looked at it for a while.

"My family ran whiskey and guns up the coast for eleven years," she said. He'd heard the shape of this story on the boat, in fragments, but she'd never told it whole, and he understood she was telling it whole now for a reason. "My father's rule was simple: pay the officials, keep the cargo boring, never make headlines. And it worked. For eleven years it worked, because we were invisible, and invisibility was the whole business model. And then I testified, and the whole thing came down in a week, and do you know what changed?" She looked up. "The ports got new inspectors. The routes moved north. Within two years, the same cargo was moving again under different names, and some of the same officials were taking the same money, and the only thing that had actually changed was that my father was in prison and my brother hated me and I had to become a different person to survive it." Her voice didn't crack. It went quieter, which was worse. "So don't tell me daylight works, Tomas. I've seen what daylight does. It burns one operation and lights the next one. I was seventeen years old and I thought I was saving the coast, and all I did was rearrange it."

"But you did it anyway," he said. "At seventeen. Knowing it would cost you everything. Why?"

"Because it was right."

"There it is." He pointed at her across the fire. "Say that again."

"It was right."

"Now listen to yourself. You didn't testify because you'd calculated the systemic outcomes. You didn't run a cost-benefit analysis on port inspections. You testified because a girl who grew up inside the machine looked at it and said *this is wrong* out loud, in a courtroom, on the record, with her name attached. And yes, the machine survived. Machines always survive. But somewhere on that coast there's a clerk, or a dockhand, or a kid, who saw a seventeen-year-old stand up and say the true thing with her name on it, and it never left them. Truth doesn't work like a bomb, Juniper. You don't detonate it once and topple the building. It works like water." He nodded at the river. "It works the way this works. Slowly, against everything, wearing the shape of the valley. You won't live to see the canyon. That's not the deal. The deal is you keep the water moving."

Juniper sat with that. He watched her turn it over the way she turned over engine parts—looking for the flaw, the loose fitting, the place where it would fail under load.

"And if it costs Daniel?" she said finally. "If we broadcast, and they decide a hostage is worth less than a headline, and they—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Maren's price. Make it public. I keep hearing her say it, and I keep hearing my own answer underneath it, which is that I've already spent one family on a principle. I'm not sure I have another one in me."

"No," Tomas said. "But that's not the choice in front of you, and you know it. The choice isn't ledgers versus Daniel. Whoever set this up wants you to believe it's binary, because binary choices are easy to control. The moment you accept *either the truth or your brother*, you've already lost both, because they own the terms." He leaned forward, wincing at his ribs, and tapped the gravel between them. "The real play—the one they can't price—is refusing the fork. Handing them a situation where the trade itself becomes the story. Where protecting their secret costs more than the secret's worth. That's not silence and it's not martyrdom. That's making daylight a weapon instead of a prayer."

Juniper looked at him for a long time. Something moved behind her face—not softening exactly, but a recalculation, a pilot reading new weather.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"For three days. On my back. With a fever." He shrugged, and immediately regretted the shrug. "Fever logic. Take it with salt."

"Fever logic." She almost smiled. Then she reached over and pulled the oilcloth bundle from the satchel, setting it between them without unwrapping it, like something that needed both of them present to be opened. "Daniel's map has a spur line on it. An illegal one. And the depot ledgers have names in them that go up past the provincial ministry. If Odile reads those names on open frequency, there's no walking it back. Not for them." She paused. "Not for me either. My name goes in that story eventually. It has to, or none of it holds together."

"You're talking about testifying."

"I'm talking about existing," she said. "Out loud. Under my real name. Fifteen years, Tomas. I built a whole person out of fuel logs and flight plans, and I'm telling you the scariest part isn't the enforcers. It's the idea that after this, I don't get to disappear anymore. Everyone I've ever avoided will know where I am. Forever."

"Sounds terrible," he said.

"It is terrible."

"Good." He said it gently. "Terrible means it matters. The things that cost nothing change nothing—that's been my professional observation across fourteen years and one destroyed career." He settled back against the log, careful of his side. "For what it's worth, I'll be standing next to you when you say it. Half-crippled, probably, but standing."

She studied him across the fire, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the river filled the silence the way it filled everything—without effort, without apology.

"Why'd you really come on this trip?" she asked suddenly. "Not the contamination. Not the reports. You could've walked away at the trading post. Nobody was holding a gun to your head. So why?"

Tomas considered lying, found he didn't have the energy, and considered the truth, found he didn't have the practice.

"Because of my daughter," he said. "She's nineteen now. I haven't seen her since she was twelve. And last year she wrote me a letter—one letter, seven years of silence, one letter—and it said, *Dad, I read your report online. Somebody scanned it and posted it. It's the only thing I have of you, and it says you were right.*" His voice caught on the last of it, and he let it. "Forty pages of contamination data. That's what my daughter has of her father. Not photographs. Not birthdays. A PDF somebody uploaded out of spite." He looked at the fire. "I came on this boat because I wanted to give her something else. Something newer. Something that proves the man in the report didn't stop at forty pages."

Juniper reached across the fire and put her hand on his wrist—briefly, firmly, the way you grip a strut to check it's sound. Then she took it back and started repacking the bandages, and the moment folded itself away into the ordinary rhythm of camp, which was its own kind of mercy.

The light was going gold when they heard it: the low cough of an outboard, upstream, running rough. Juniper was on her feet before Tomas finished sitting up, binoculars out, body dropping into that pilot's crouch he'd seen her use a hundred times now—all instinct, no wasted motion.

"Two men," she said quietly. "Maren's people. Second boat." Then she paused, and the binoculars came down half an inch, and her voice changed. "Tomas. They're alone. Maren's guide was supposed to be with them."

The boat swung toward the sandbar, and even at that distance Tomas could see the men weren't rowing easy. They were rowing scared.
