## 23. The Holding Station

The coordinates from the garbled broadcast put us two days upriver from Maren's route, in a stretch of country where the maps went vague and the water went mean. I'd flown over it a hundred times without looking down. From the air it was just green—unbroken, indifferent green, the kind of landscape that makes you believe nothing bad can happen anywhere because the world is too big to hide anything. From the water, at the speed of a leaking boat, it was different. From the water it felt like the green was watching us back.

We left at first light, running the boat with the engine at a throttle setting Tomas called "optimistic" and I called "necessary." The monsoon had settled into a rhythm now—not the first assault of weeks ago, but a steady, patient pressure, rain that fell like it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. The river had risen another half meter overnight. You could tell by the drowned fence posts, the tops of them just breaking the surface like the fingers of buried men.

"You're quiet," Tomas said.

"I'm navigating."

"You're quiet *and* navigating. I've seen you do both at once. You hum when you're just navigating."

I hadn't known that about myself. Fifteen years alone and I hadn't known I hummed. "Then I'm quiet."

He let it go, which was one of the things I'd come to trust about him. Tomas had a talent for silence—not the weaponized kind, the waiting kind, the kind that left room for you to fill it when you were ready. I wasn't ready. I'd spent the night reading Daniel's handwriting by lamplight, page after page of water samples and dates and that pipeline map, and somewhere around the third hour I'd stopped seeing the data and started seeing my brother's hand—the way he crossed his sevens, the little checkmarks he put beside measurements he trusted. I'd learned to read that handwriting across a kitchen table when we were kids, copying each other's homework, and now I was reading it in a flooded country where he might be alive or might be a line item in a ledger, and I was not ready to talk about it.

The river narrowed. The banks closed in, clay and root and hanging vines, and the current picked up like it had somewhere to be. I steered around a half-submerged log and felt the hull grind something soft beneath us—sand, silt, the river rearranging its furniture the way it did every year.

"There." Tomas pointed.

I saw it before he finished saying the word, because I'd been looking for it since dawn: a break in the green where the green had been cut and never allowed to grow back. An old clearing on the east bank, terraced into the slope. And above the treeline, the corner of a roof—corrugated iron, rust-brown, the color of everything else, invisible unless you knew where to look.

The coordinates were good.

I cut the engine and we drifted, letting the current carry us the last few hundred meters while we both looked at the shore and didn't say anything. That was the thing about the two of us by now—we'd gotten fluent in each other's silences. This one said: *once we go in, we can't unsee what's in there.*

"Could be nothing," Tomas said. "Abandoned depot. Half these sites are just rotting equipment and rats."

"Could be."

"Could be a trap."

"Could be."

He sighed and picked up the pole. "You're not going to say anything useful, are you."

"I'm going to say: tie us off upstream. If anyone's watching that bank, I don't want them watching our boat."

That, at least, was useful, and he knew it. We put in fifty meters above the clearing, in the mouth of a side channel choked with water hyacinth, and Tomas buried the anchor line in the mud while I covered the boat's gear with a tarp and the cut branches I'd been carrying for exactly this purpose since Maren's landing. Maren's landing. Maren in a depot somewhere upriver, taken because a guide sold them for passage out, and every hour I spent being careful was an hour she spent in a room with men whose names she knew. I pushed that down. Pushed it down and used it, the way you use a hot coal—carefully, but for the heat.

We went in along the high bank, single file, Tomas behind me with the machete and me ahead because I'd spent fifteen years reading ground and he'd spent his career reading paperwork, and this was ground. The rain helped—softened our sound, blurred our edges—but it also softened everything else, and twice I nearly put my boot on something that would have announced us. The second time I stopped and looked at what it was and my stomach dropped.

A tin can. Rusted through, ancient, part of the depot's first life. But beside it, half-sunk in the mud, a cigarette butt. Unrusted. Recent.

"Somebody's been here," I said quietly.

"Recent?"

"Weeks. Maybe less. Rain would've eaten it faster."

Tomas crouched, looked at the mud around the can, and I watched him learn to read ground in real time—the way his eyes moved, sorting the surface into layers the way he'd once sorted documents. "Boot prints," he said. "Heavy ones. And—" he moved a fern aside "—vehicle tracks. Tires. Two axles at least."

"A truck can't get in here. The road died years ago."

"Then they brought one in by barge and built a track." He stood. "Which means they planned to bring things in regularly."

*Things.* He said it the way you'd say it about cargo, and we both let the word sit there, because we both knew what kind of cargo needed a planned track, a planned dock, a planned depot in the middle of nowhere where the maps went vague.

We came up through the treeline at the edge of the clearing and saw the whole thing at once.

* * *

It had been a mining depot once—that was obvious from the bones of it. A long main building of timber and iron, a loading platform facing the old barge landing, a fuel tank gone to rust, the skeleton of a crane. The consortium had strip-mined this concession twenty years ago and walked away when the ore got thin, and for two decades the place had been allowed to rot in peace.

It wasn't rotting in peace anymore.

The main building's windows had been barred. Not boarded—*barred*, new steel bolted over the frames, bright metal going orange at the welds. The single door had been replaced with something heavier, and beside it someone had installed a keypad lock, the kind you'd put on an office. A generator shed stood at the building's end, its exhaust pipe running up through a sound-muffling box. Floodlights on poles, currently dark in the grey rain. A radio antenna lashed to a dead tree. And everywhere, the small tidy details that made my skin crawl worse than the bars: a boot scraper by the door. A rack for wet gear under an awning. A clothesline with nothing on it, strung at exactly the height of a uniform shirt.

Somebody had designed this. Somebody had sat at a desk with a budget and a checklist and turned a rotten mining depot into a place for keeping people, and they'd done it *neatly*, and they'd gone home at the end of the day, and somewhere in a provincial office there was probably a form with their signature on it and a line item that said *infrastructure, remote, miscellaneous.*

"That's what it is," Tomas said. His voice had gone flat, which I'd learned was his worst register. Anger he could carry; this flatness was what anger looked like when it had nowhere to go. "It's a holding station. Like the ones they used in the border provinces in the nineties. I reviewed one. On paper it was a 'transient labor facility.'"

"And off paper?"

"Off paper it had the same boot scraper."

We crossed the clearing fast and low, rain covering our sound, and reached the loading platform. The main door was locked—keypad dark, generator cold—but the building had a second life in its bones, and I knew mining depots the way Tomas knew filing systems. There was always a loading door. There was always a back. We found the loading door around the east side, a rolling steel shutter, and it was shut but not locked, the latch rusted in the open position with a padlock hanging from it that someone had cut and not replaced. Sloppy. Or confident. Or in a hurry.

I got my fingers under the shutter's lip and lifted. It screamed on its rollers—once, loud, a sound that went out across the clearing and came back—and then I had it chest-high and we were inside, and the dark closed over us like water.

* * *

The smell hit first.

I've smelled a lot of things a bush pilot smells: fuel and fish and rot and rain. This was none of those. This was disinfectant laid over damp, the chemical sweetness of chlorine fighting a losing war against mildew and bodies, and under it something else, something I couldn't name until I could, and then I wished I couldn't. It was the smell of a place where people had been kept for a long time without anywhere to put what people produce. It was the smell of a latrine bucket that had been emptied regularly, because the people running this place were tidy.

"Light," Tomas whispered, and I thumbed on the small torch we'd bought at Maren's, keeping the beam low, a hand's width off the floor.

We were in a long room. The loading bay, probably, originally—concrete floor, steel columns, a gantry rail overhead where ore buckets used to run. But the ore equipment was gone, and in its place:

Bunk frames. Rows of them, bolted to the floor, two high, made of the same bright-welded steel as the window bars. Dozens. Most were stripped, mattresses gone or never installed, but near the far end a few still had bedding—thin pads, grey blankets, folded with institutional precision. A partition wall divided the room, and through its doorway I could see a second space: tables. Benches. A serving window into what had been a kitchen. A chalkboard on the wall, wiped clean except for a ghost of writing no one had fully erased.

Tomas walked the rows slowly, his torch moving like a man reading a page. He stopped at one of the made bunks and crouched.

"Numbered," he said.

I came over. Stenciled on the bunk frame, in white paint, neat as a serial number: **S-11**. The next bunk: **S-12**. Along the wall, a painted line and more numbers, a whole sequence of them, running down the room like a ledger written in steel.

"They numbered the beds," I said. My voice came out strange, far away.

"They numbered the beds." Tomas ran his thumb under the stencil. "S for surveyor?"

"Or for something else. Something they had a lot of."

He didn't answer that. He didn't have to. I stood in the middle of that concrete floor with the rain drumming on the iron roof and I made myself look, really look, at everything, because this was the job now and flinching was a luxury. The serving window with its steel counter worn shiny where trays had slid across it, hundreds of trays, thousands. The chalkboard with its ghost-writing—I went to it and tilted the torch and made out the faint ridges of erased letters: a date, a count, and the word **TRANSFER** with an arrow. The floor near the door, where the concrete was scuffed in a wide arc, the way a floor gets scuffed when people are made to line up in the same place, over and over, facing the same direction.

Facing the door. Facing whoever came through it.

There was a room off the kitchen, smaller, and I don't know why I went into it before Tomas did, except that some instinct I'd spent fifteen years sharpening told me the small room was the important one. It had been an office once—a desk, a filing cabinet, both still there. The desk was clean. The filing cabinet was empty except for a single folder someone had left behind in a hurry, and I took it, and I didn't open it, because opening it in here felt like letting this place watch me read it.

It was on the desk that I found the thing that stopped me.

A pen. A cheap ballpoint, the giveaway kind, the kind that comes stapled to notepads from supply companies. It was lying on the desk blotter as if someone had set it down to sign something and never picked it up. And beside it, a sheet of the same notepad, mostly blank, with one line written at the top in a neat administrative hand:

*Receipt of persons: six (6). Condition on arrival: satisfactory.*

*Condition on arrival: satisfactory.*

I read it three times. The words were ordinary. That was the horror of it—the words were *ordinary*, the words of a warehouse, of a shipment of pump parts, of anything a man in a clean shirt might process before lunch. Six persons. Satisfactory. As if there were a standard. As if somewhere there was a form defining what condition a human being was supposed to arrive in, and these six had met it, and the pen had been set down, and the man had gone to lunch.

"Juniper."

Tomas was at the partition wall, at the far end, near the bunks. His voice had that flatness again, but with a crack in it now. I went to him.

He was crouched by the wall at the end of the bunk row, where the concrete met the timber sill, and his torch was on a mark low down, at the height of a kneeling hand. Someone had scratched into the concrete with something hard—a bolt, a buckle, a nail. Not a word. Letters, but hurried, half-formed, the kind of thing you cut into a wall in the dark when you didn't know how much time you had:

**D.R. — 6 — NORTH**

And under it, smaller, almost illegible, pressed so hard the concrete had crumbled:

**IF FOUND — RIVER PEOPLE — TELL J**

The rain got louder. Or I did, inside. I knelt there with the torch shaking slightly in my hand—I noticed it shaking and hated it and couldn't stop it—and I read the letters until they stopped being letters and became my brother's hand. I knew it was his. You can fake a name. You can't fake the way someone gouges the crossbar of an R, angry and exact at the same time, the way Daniel had written everything his whole life, homework and water samples and smuggling manifests, meticulous even in the dark, even in a cage.

He'd been here. Within weeks—the scratches were bright-edged, unwashed, the concrete dust still pale in the grooves. He'd been here, in a numbered bunk in a room that smelled of chlorine and defeat, and he'd known someone might come, and he'd spent his one hard thing, his one nail or bolt, on a message, and the message wasn't *help me* or *revenge* or *God*.

It was *tell J.*

He'd been thinking of me. In that room, with the trays and the numbers and the men in clean shirts, my brother had spent his only currency on me.

"Juniper." Tomas's hand was on my shoulder. I hadn't heard him move. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're not."

I was, though. It just didn't feel like it. I made myself stand. I made myself put the torch beam back on the wall and photograph it with my mind the way I photographed river bends, every groove of it, and then I said, in a voice I barely recognized as mine, "North. The transfer order said upriver. North matches. He's alive, Tomas. *Satisfactory condition.* They were keeping them alive because alive is leverage, alive is inventory—"

"Juniper."

"—and he left me a message. He *left me a message.* Fifteen years I thought—"

"Listen."

I listened. Under the rain, under my own blood, there was another sound, and it took me a second to place it because it didn't belong: an engine. Not on the river. On land. The grind of a truck in low gear, coming down something steep, getting closer.

Tomas was already moving, killing his torch, pulling me toward the loading door. "Vehicle," he breathed. "They're coming back."

* * *

We got the shutter down to knee height and went under it and made the treeline as the truck broke out of the trees at the top of the old mine track—headlights sweeping the clearing, wipers going, a canvas-covered bed. We pressed into the ferns and the mud and I put my mouth against the wet ground and breathed the rot-smell of the forest floor and watched.

Two men got out. Rain capes, rifles slung, the loose economy of men doing a job they'd done many times. One went to the keypad. The other stood in the rain and smoked, and the cherry of his cigarette was the only warm color in the whole grey world, and I lay in the mud thirty meters away and hated him with a purity I hadn't felt since the day I testified.

They went inside. The door closed. The lights in the barred windows came on, yellow and steady, and through them we watched the two men move around the office, and one of them stopped at the desk, and picked up something, and looked at it for a long time.

The pen. I knew it before he said it. I felt Tomas go rigid beside me.

"He found the notepad," Tomas whispered. "The page. *Receipt of persons.*"

"Can he trace it to us?"

"He doesn't have to trace it. He just has to know someone was here. And there's mud on the floor and the shutter's unlatched and—" He stopped. Through the window, the man with the cigarette had come back out into the rain. He wasn't looking at the building. He was looking at the treeline. At our treeline. He stood there, smoking, turning slowly, letting his eyes walk the edge of the clearing the way I'd walked it an hour ago.

Then he raised one hand and made a signal I recognized from a life I'd buried: two fingers, a circle, *sweep it.*

Tomas's hand found my arm in the dark. "Back channel," he breathed. "Now. Slow."

We began to move, belly-low, into the ferns, away from the clearing, toward the side channel and the boat, while behind us the lights of the holding station burned in the rain and two men with rifles started walking the treeline, and the last thing I saw before the green swallowed the clearing was the chalkboard through the window, and the ghost of the word on it, and the arrow pointing north.

*Tell J.*

I was telling myself, brother. I was coming.
