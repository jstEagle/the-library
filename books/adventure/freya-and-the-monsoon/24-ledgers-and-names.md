## 24. Ledgers and Names

The depot office smelled of dust and diesel and something older underneath—paper rot, the slow decay of records nobody expected anyone to read.

Juniper stood in the doorway a long moment before entering. The room was small, windowless, lit by the gray light leaking through a gap where the door no longer met its frame. A desk. A filing cabinet with one drawer hanging open like a broken jaw. A shelf of binders, their spines furred with mold. And on the wall behind the desk, a calendar from two years ago, still turned to March, as if time here had simply been switched off when the last man left.

"Someone's been back," Tomas said quietly. He was crouched by the door frame, running his thumb along the wood. "These scratches are fresh. Weeks old, maybe less."

Juniper crossed to the desk. The surface was clean in a rectangle where something had sat until recently—a ledger, or a radio, lifted away by whoever came last. But whoever it was had been in a hurry, or careless, or both. Because beneath the desk's false front, where a modesty panel should have closed off the kneehole, there was a seam that didn't match the rest of the wood.

She knelt. Ran her fingers along it. Felt the give.

"Tomas."

He came over. Together they worked the panel loose—it was hinged, she realized, badly disguised—and swung it open on a shallow compartment lined with oilcloth.

Inside: six ledgers. Bound in gray board, their corners soft with damp, but intact. Intact, and heavy, and real.

Tomas made a sound low in his throat—not quite a word. He'd spent eleven years inside the machinery of government inspection; Juniper had watched him read contamination reports the way other men read scripture. She knew what he was seeing.

"Shipping ledgers," he said. His voice had gone flat, careful, the way it did when emotion was something he was carrying rather than expressing. "Consolidated Minerals Group. CMG." He pulled the first one out and opened it across his knees. "Dates, tonnage, destinations. Tailings barges moving at night. Discharge points marked by river kilometer." He turned pages faster. "This isn't hidden accounting. This is *operational* record-keeping. They wrote it all down because they never believed anyone would ever come look."

"They were right for years," Juniper said.

"They weren't right about you."

She didn't answer that. She pulled the second ledger into her lap and began to read, and the reading was worse than the finding, because numbers have a way of becoming names if you let them. Barges loaded. Rivers listed. Villages named in a column headed *COMPENSATION DISBURSED* with figures so small beside the tonnage that the arithmetic itself felt like violence. Next to some village names, a notation: *RESOLVED.* Next to others: *OUTSTANDING.*

Outstanding. As if a poisoned river were an invoice.

"Look at this column," Tomas said. He'd moved to the filing cabinet and was pulling folders into the light. "Signatures. Counter-signatures. Every discharge permit waived, every inspection deferred—" He held up a page toward the gray light. "Provincial Water Authority. This is Assistant Director level. And this one—" his finger stopped, pressed flat against the paper, and Juniper watched his hand go still in a way she'd learned meant the ground had shifted under him. "I know this name. I *worked* under this name. He signed off on my termination review."

The rain found the roof then, a first scatter of drops on corrugated iron, building toward the steady drumming that had become the soundtrack of their lives for days. Neither of them looked up. Outside, the flooded yard lapped at the depot's stilt legs. Somewhere downriver, men with rifles were presumably still searching. In here, the past two decades of a province's quiet poisoning lay open in their laps, written in a bureaucrat's tidy hand.

"We need everything," Juniper said. "Not just the ledgers. The signatures. Cross-references. If we're going to make this stick, it has to be undeniable."

"It already is undeniable. That's not the question." Tomas stacked folders with methodical care, wrapping them in the oilcloth. "The question is whether undeniable matters. I've seen undeniable reports die in filing cabinets."

"Then we don't put it in a cabinet."

"No," he agreed. "We don't."

* * *

It was near dark when she found it.

They'd been working by flashlight, trading the beam back and forth, cataloguing. The ledgers ran four years deep. The folders held permits, memos, payment authorizations—a whole ecosystem of corruption, self-documenting, arrogant in its thoroughness. Juniper had stopped feeling sick somewhere around hour two and settled into something colder and more useful: fury with a direction now, an arrow instead of a fog.

The transfer order was in the bottom folder, misfiled—or filed exactly where someone thought no one would look, which amounted to the same thing.

She almost missed it. It was a single sheet, typed, on letterhead she didn't recognize at first: a logistics subsidiary, one of CMG's many faces. *PERSONNEL TRANSFER AUTHORIZATION.* Routine language. Routine form.

Six names.

She read the first one and the world went very quiet, the way it does just before a storm breaks open.

*Ravensworth, D.*

Her brother's name. Typed. Dated five weeks ago. Destination: a facility code upriver, beyond the gorge, in country so remote it barely appeared on maps. *Duration of assignment: indefinite. Status: retained personnel.*

Retained. Not lost. Not missing. Not drowned in the monsoon like the official bulletins said, like the fake rescuers implied with their sympathetic head-tilts and their precise questions.

Alive. All six of them—she checked each line twice, three times, her hands shaking so hard she had to press the paper flat against her knee. Six surveyors, transferred upriver five weeks ago. Alive when this was signed. And nothing since—no release order, no return authorization, nothing.

"Juniper." Tomas's voice, from far away. "Juniper, what is it?"

She couldn't speak. She held the page out. Her arm wouldn't quite extend all the way; Tomas had to come to her, take it from fingers that didn't want to let go even now, even knowing what it was.

He read it. She watched his face do what hers must have done—the flicker of hope arriving too fast to be trusted, then the colder thought landing behind it.

"Retained personnel," he said slowly. "That's what they call people they're keeping."

"I know what they call them."

"Five weeks, Juniper. If they wanted them dead, they'd be dead. Dead surveyors are a problem; disappeared ones are leverage." He set the page down on the desk with exaggerated gentleness, as if it might bruise. "This means Daniel is alive. Right now. Tonight. Possibly within fifty kilometers of us."

"And possibly being moved tomorrow." She stood. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, pilot-steady, the voice she used on the radio when the weather was turning. "We stop cataloguing. We take all of it—all of it—and we move tonight."

"The boat's patched but—"

"*Tonight*, Tomas."

He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once and start wrapping the ledgers in oilcloth without another word.

* * *

They worked by flashlight and adrenaline. Ledgers into waterproof bags. Folders divided between them so no single loss could kill the evidence. The transfer order Juniper kept on her body, inside her jacket, against her ribs, where the compass rode—her family name engraved on brass, her brother's name typed on a government form, fifteen years collapsing into the space between two objects.

At the office door she paused. Turned back. The calendar still said March, two years gone. She tore it off the wall, folded it, and put it in her pocket without quite knowing why. Maybe because someone should remember that this place had a before.

Outside, the rain had settled into its monsoon rhythm, vast and indifferent. The river had risen again overnight—she could hear it in the changed note of the current against the pilings, a half-tone higher, hungry. Tomas stood at the top of the stairs with the bags slung across his chest, watching the water with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"That gorge," he said. "If we're going upriver through the gorge in this—"

"I know what the gorge does in this water."

"Do you? Because I've been watching you calculate things for days, Juniper, and right now you're calculating whether your brother is worth a flooded gorge, and I want you to actually say the answer out loud before we commit."

She looked at the black water. At the rain marching across it in silver sheets. At the boat below, riding low, patched twice, holding.

"He waited five weeks," she said. "Every day I wait is a day he waits. So yes. The gorge. In this."

Tomas exhaled through his nose—almost a laugh, entirely without humor. "You fly planes into weather like this too?"

"Worse."

"Of course you do." He started down the stairs. Then stopped, half-turned, and said, quieter: "For what it's worth—I'm glad it's you coming for him."

She didn't have an answer for that either. She followed him down into the rain, and the river took them north, into the dark, carrying six ledgers, a hundred signatures, and one typed line that changed everything:

*Ravensworth, D. Status: retained.*

Alive, then.

Which meant the fury finally had somewhere to go.
