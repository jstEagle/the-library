## 25. Betrayal at Maren's Landing

*Tomas*

The guide's name was Pell, and Tomas had marked him the first night.

It wasn't anything he could have written in a report—those days were gone. It was the old inspector's habit, the one that had survived the firing and the disgrace and the years of drifting: watch the man who watches the exits. Pell sat with his back to the fire and his eyes on the water. He counted the boats twice a day, casually, the way a man counts his own fingers. And when Maren's people spoke of the routes north, Pell's hands went still in a way that hands don't go still unless the ears behind them are working hard.

Tomas said nothing. That was the mistake he'd carry, and he knew it even then, in the moment of not saying it. He had spent a career believing that suspicion without evidence was a kind of corruption too—that a man's reputation was not something you spent lightly. He had watched that principle cost him his job, his pension, and six years with his daughter, and he had kept it anyway, the way a man keeps a limp.

Now he watched Pell carry water up from the landing, humming, and thought: *say something.*

He didn't.

* * *

*Juniper*

Maren's landing wasn't a place so much as an agreement between the river and the people who lived on it. A shelf of packed mud, three stilt houses leaning like tired old men against each other, a smokehouse that smelled of fish and pepper. Juniper had spent fifteen years learning to read places the way other people read faces, and this place read honest. It had the particular shabbiness of a community that spent its money on children and boats and nothing else.

They'd been there four days. Long enough for Tomas's fever to break for good, long enough for Maren's granddaughters to stop staring at Juniper's scarred hands and start asking her to draw them airplanes in the dirt. Long enough that the compass on its cord around her neck had stopped feeling like a weight and started feeling like a promise.

Daniel had been here. That was the thing she couldn't put down. The elder at the quiet village had said *weeks ago*, and Maren had confirmed it the first night, turning her tea bowl slowly in her hands: "Six of them. Tired. The tall one with the notebook—he sat where you're sitting and he asked about the pipeline spur, and I told him what I tell everyone, which is nothing, because he wasn't ready to hear it. Now maybe you are."

The ledgers were wrapped in oilcloth under Juniper's bunk in the second stilt house, along with Daniel's map, copied twice, one copy hidden in the smokehouse, one sewn into the lining of Maren's own coat. Maren had insisted. "Paper burns," she'd said. "Paper drowns. Paper gets traded away by frightened people. So we make it hard to trade."

Juniper had liked her immediately, the way you like a whetstone—something that makes you sharper and doesn't care whether you enjoy the process.

On the fourth morning, Maren stood at the rail of the smokehouse and said, "You leave tomorrow. First light, before the mist burns off. Pell will take you as far as the Split, where his cousin has a boat with an engine that mostly works. After the Split, you're on your own."

"Why the Split?" Tomas asked.

"Because after the Split, the enforcers' patrol boats can't follow. Too shallow, too many snags. They know it. Everyone knows it." Maren's eyes were flat and dark as the water. "Which is why it costs me to tell you. And why it will cost someone else to tell them."

Juniper felt it then—the cold sliding up her spine, the instinct she'd trusted through fog and ridgelines and camps without tracks. "Who else knows we're here?"

Maren turned the tea bowl. "Everyone on this landing knows. That's not a leak. A leak is one person who wants something." She looked downriver, toward the mist, and Juniper watched her do a calculation she'd clearly done a thousand times before. "Pell's sister has three children in the provincial town. His cousin lost his boat license to a man named Corver—do you know that name?"

Juniper did. It was in the ledgers, twice, in the column of payments.

"Corver's people have been at Pell for a year," Maren said. "Offering him passage out. Papers. A new start somewhere the river can't find him." She set the bowl down. "I'm telling you this so you understand that if it happens, it won't be because Pell is evil. It will be because Pell is tired, and tired men take the exit they're shown."

"And if it happens," Juniper said, "what do we do?"

Maren smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman who had buried a husband, two sons, and a way of life, and had gone on feeding people anyway. "You get in the boat. You don't look back. Looking back is how they catch people."

* * *

*Tomas*

They left at first light, as planned, and the mist lay on the river like a held breath.

Tomas carried the oilcloth bundle. Juniper carried the compass and the map and the rifle Maren pressed into her hands without ceremony—"It was my eldest son's. It has killed nothing but crocodiles and I would like to keep that record, so don't miss." The boat was Maren's own, the good one, the one with the patched hull and the engine that mostly worked, and Pell stood at the tiller with his hood up, not looking at either of them.

The first hour was silence and water and the grey world between night and morning. Tomas sat in the bow with his hand on the bundle and watched Pell's shoulders.

He'd decided, somewhere around the third hour of the night, that he would say something. He'd lain awake on the woven mat listening to the river and had finally understood what had been bothering him since the first night: it wasn't that Pell watched the exits. It was that Pell had stopped. Two days ago, Pell had stopped counting the boats. A man stops counting when the answer no longer matters to him. A man stops watching the exits when he already knows which one he's taking.

"Tomas." Juniper's voice, low, from the middle of the boat. She'd gone still in the way she went still—total, animal, every part of her aimed at the same point. "Mist's burning off too fast."

He looked. She was right. The mist wasn't lifting; it was being pushed, from downriver, from behind them, a grey wall thinning into nothing as something moved through it, displacing the morning.

Then the sound reached them. Engines. More than one. The flat, purposeful growl of outboards run hard by men who didn't care who heard them coming.

Pell's shoulders didn't change. That was the worst part. Every other person on the river would have flinched, cursed, turned. Pell held the tiller in a dead man's calm and kept the boat pointed at the Split, and Tomas understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water going down his collar, that the boat was pointed exactly where it had always been going to be pointed.

"Pell," Tomas said quietly. "Turn us around."

"There's shallow water behind," Pell said. His voice was steady. Practiced. He'd rehearsed this. "The Split's two miles—"

"Pell." Juniper had the rifle out of its wrap now, low, across her knees, not aimed. "Look at me."

He looked at her. And for one moment something moved behind his face—shame, maybe, or just the animal fear of a tired man caught between two kinds of drowning—and he said, so quietly the engines almost ate it: "They have my sister's address. They have her children's school. I didn't tell them about the ledgers. I swear it on the river, I only told them where you'd be, and when, and I told them the boat would be empty by—"

The first patrol boat came out of the dying mist a quarter mile back, and there was no more time for confession or absolution or anything else.

* * *

*Juniper*

Later—much later, in the years when she would finally be counting her life in something other than miles—Juniper would replay the next ninety seconds the way pilots replay crashes: frame by frame, looking for the decision that would have saved everyone.

There wasn't one. She knew that too. But knowing didn't stop the replay.

The patrol boat was a quarter mile back and closing, and behind it, two more, spread wide, a net being cast. The Split was two miles ahead. The math was simple and it was bad: they would be caught in open water, in a boat that mostly worked, carrying the only copies in the world of the thing that could crack the consortium open.

"Maren," she said, and turned to look back at the landing.

The landing was a mile behind them now, a smudge of stilts in the grey. And coming off that smudge, fast, riding low in the water with her engine wide open, was Maren's second boat—the old one, the one with the hull Juniper had helped patch, the one that should have been dying in a backwater for another decade. Maren stood at the tiller with her coat flying open, and Juniper saw the shape of the plan the way you see a whole storm system from altitude: Maren was running straight at the lead patrol boat.

"She's drawing them," Tomas said. His voice cracked on it. "Juniper—she's drawing them off us."

"Turn the boat, Pell." Juniper's own voice came out flat and cold and pilot-calm, the voice she used in weather that killed people. "Not to the Split. The back channel, east of the Split, the snag field. Can you thread it?"

Pell's face went grey. "Nobody threads the snag field."

"I fly through canyons for a living. Thread it."

He turned the boat.

* * *

*Tomas*

The snag field was a drowned forest, dead trunks rising out of brown water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down standing. The channels between them were boat-widths wide, some of them, and the water hid stumps that could open a hull like a letter. Pell drove it weeping—actually weeping, silently, tears cutting lines down his face while he threaded the boat through gaps that Tomas's eyes refused to believe were gaps—and behind them the engines wheeled and shouted, and once, horribly, a voice came across the water with a loudspeaker, a calm bureaucratic voice saying *stop your vessel, this is a safety operation, stop your vessel,* as if the drowned forest were a traffic matter.

Then, from the open water behind, a different sound. A crash of wood. Shouting. One engine cutting out, another going to full scream.

Tomas turned in time to see it in a gap between trunks, one frame, less than a second: Maren's old boat, holed and slewing, and Maren in the water, and hands—enforcers' hands—pulling her out, and her coat, her coat with the map sewn into the lining, still on her shoulders, still on her, and then the trunk came between them and the frame was gone.

"They've got her," he said. "Juniper. They've got her, but the coat—the map's still—"

"I saw." Juniper's eyes were forward, fixed, her hands white on the gunwale. "Keep driving, Pell. Don't stop. Don't you dare stop."

"She did it on purpose." Pell's voice came apart. "She got in the old boat on purpose, she told me last night, she said *if it goes wrong, I go loud and you go quiet*—she planned it—"

"Then she planned to be taken," Juniper said, "and she planned it so we'd live. So we don't waste it. Drive."

* * *

*Juniper*

They came out the far side of the snag field into water that was calm and wide and empty, and the engines behind them had gone quiet, and that quiet was the worst sound Juniper had ever heard, because it meant the net had closed on what it had come to close on, and it wasn't them.

Pell ran the boat until the fuel gauge made the decision for him, then let it drift in the middle of a stretch of river so featureless it could have been anywhere. He sat down in the bilge water and put his face in his hands.

"They'll kill her," he said. "Or worse. They'll make an example. That's what Corver does, he makes examples, everyone knows—" He looked up at Juniper, and his eyes were the eyes of a man begging for a sentence. "Say it. You want to. Say what I am."

Juniper stood over him with the rifle in her hands and fifteen years of running in her legs, and she found—to her own surprise—that what she wanted to say was not what came out.

"You told them where we'd be," she said. "You didn't tell them about the ledgers. You didn't tell them about the smokehouse copy, or the coat, or the Split. Maren knew what you were before we ever got in this boat, and she put us in it anyway, because she'd already decided you were the risk worth taking." She crouched, level with him. "You're a tired man who took the exit he was shown. I know exactly what that is. I was one for fifteen years." She stood. "The difference is what you do in the next four days. Corver's people will move her to the depot upriver—the holding station, that's where they keep people. You know the river. You know the routes they'd use."

Pell looked up. Hope and horror fighting for the same ground.

"You're going to help us get her back," Juniper said. "Not because you owe me. Because she planned to be taken so you'd have something left to save."

*Tomas*

Tomas said nothing while Juniper talked, because there was nothing to add and because his throat had closed. He stood at the bow instead, looking back down the empty river toward a landing that was now a smudge and then not even a smudge, holding an oilcloth bundle that suddenly weighed as much as a person.

He thought: *I watched him for four days. I knew. I said nothing, because I didn't have proof, because proof is what I was trained to need, and needing proof is what let every one of those ledgers get written.*

He thought about his daughter, and about all the times he'd told himself that caution was integrity.

Never again. The words arrived without drama, the way the worst resolutions do. *Never again.*

Behind them, far off, a single engine note rose and fell and rose again—the enforcers heading upriver with their prisoner, toward the depot, toward the coordinates in Daniel's notes. Ahead, four days of water between them and a journalist who could turn paper into fire.

Juniper came and stood beside him at the bow. Neither of them spoke for a long time. The river carried them, indifferent and enormous, and the oilcloth bundle sat between their feet like a sleeping thing that had already cost too much and was going to cost more.

"We get Odile first," Juniper said finally. "That was the deal. The broadcast goes out, the names go public, and then they have nothing left to trade with and nowhere to hide her. Maren's price. Our promise."

"And if they hurt her before four days are up?"

Juniper was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had the flat calm of weather, the kind that meant everything underneath was moving.

"Then we make sure that everything she paid for comes due," she said. "All of it. Every name in those ledgers. Starting with Corver." She turned her face downriver, toward the four days, toward the light. "Get us there, Pell. And don't stop for anything."
