## 26. Running the Flooded Gorge

The first bullet hit the water a boat-length behind them and threw up a white fist of spray.

"Down!" Juniper shoved Tomas flat against the bilge boards and hauled the tiller hard to port, swinging the bow toward the gorge mouth before her mind had finished agreeing with her hands. Behind them, at Maren's landing, smoke was climbing into the rain in a gray rope, and men were running along the bank, and one of them was kneeling with a rifle braced on a fallen log, and all of it was already behind her because the river had taken them and the river did not slow down for anything.

"They've got a second boat," Tomas shouted from the deck. "Starboard side, behind the fish racks."

"I see it."

She didn't, not really. She'd seen it for half a second as they tore away from the landing—men spilling into a wide-beamed longboat with an outboard that would outrun them in open water. Which was why she wasn't going to give it open water.

Ahead, the river narrowed between walls of black basalt and turned from brown to something angrier, a churning gray-green flecked with foam, and the sound reached them before the sight did—a deep, continuous roar like the earth clearing its throat. The gorge. On her charts it was marked with a symbol and a note in some long-dead surveyor's hand: *impassable in flood.*

The monsoon had been building for three weeks. It was not impassable. It was just a question of who was flying.

"Juniper." Tomas had pulled himself up on his elbows, his face gray under the brown, the bandage on his arm already spotted dark. "That's the gorge. Maren said—"

"Maren said nobody runs it in flood." She threw her weight against the tiller, feeling the boat answer, heavy and half-full of water but still answering, still hers. "Maren also said nobody would sell her out, and look how that went."

She heard him swallow. She heard him decide. "What do you need me to do?"

"Bail. Keep us light. And when I say hold, you hold on to something that doesn't move, because everything else is going to."

The longboat's outboard snarled behind them, closer now. The men in it had stopped shooting—smart, because a dead pilot meant a dead evidence trail, and they wanted the ledgers intact. They wanted *her* intact, or at least her cargo, and that gave her exactly one advantage: they had to take her carefully, and she did not have to be careful at all.

The gorge mouth opened in front of them like a door into the planet's throat.

Fifteen years of flying went through her all at once, the way it always did at the moment of commitment—not as memory but as muscle. The first time she'd stalled a Cessna at eight hundred feet and caught it. The time she'd threaded a pass in fog with the ceiling dropping and the radio dead and her hands doing the work while her mind stood very quietly to one side and watched. Every storm landing, every short strip, every time the sky had tried to kill her and she had told it, calmly, *not today.* All of it lived in her forearms, in the base of her spine, in some pre-verbal place that did not know how to be afraid and competent at the same time, so it chose competence.

She had spent fifteen years running. Running was flying low, staying small, keeping the engine quiet and the flight plan dark. But running, she understood suddenly, with the gorge roaring in her teeth, was just another way of being chased.

This was something else. This was flying.

"Hold," she said, and drove the boat into the gorge.

* * *

The world became water.

Not water the way she'd known it for three weeks—the patient brown flood that had swallowed fields and roads and the lower verandas of houses. This was water with intent. The gorge had pinched the whole river through a gap barely three boat-lengths wide, and the flood had turned it into a single coherent animal, a mile-long muscle of gray water contracting and releasing, and they were inside it.

The bow stood up. Juniper stood with it, knees bent, tiller in both hands, riding the first standing wave the way she'd ridden thermals—reading the water's face the way she'd once read cloud streets, finding the smooth lanes in the chaos. There were lanes. There were always lanes. The river was not chaos; it was a system, and systems could be flown.

"Left!" Tomas bailed and shouted and bailed, the tin can flashing. "Rock, left!"

"I see it." She'd seen it three seconds ago, a black fang sliding out of the foam, and she was already leaning the boat away from it, using the current itself to carve the turn, letting the river do the heavy work. You didn't fight a current this size. You argued with it. You found the place where its strength became your steering.

The walls closed in. Basalt on both sides, streaming with rain, close enough to touch with an oar, and the roar became a physical thing, a pressure in the chest, so loud that thought had to shout to be heard over it. Spray came off the wave trains in sheets and hit her face like thrown gravel. The boat took a wave broadside, shipped half a ton of water, and for one lurching instant sat so low that she felt the gorge's cold breath through the planks beneath her boots.

Then Tomas bailed it out—fast, frantic, effective—and the boat shrugged back up to meet the next one.

"Second boat's at the mouth!" he yelled. "They're stopping! They're not coming in!"

Of course they weren't. The men in the longboat were professionals, and professionals knew the difference between a risk and a suicide. They'd run to the portage trail, the one Maren's people used in low water, and they'd be waiting at the gorge's far end, and they'd have radios, and somewhere downstream the net was already being set.

Juniper found she didn't care.

That was the strange part, the part she'd examine later, when there was a later. Right now, with the gorge trying to kill her and doing a professional job of it, she felt—*clear.* Cleaner than she'd felt in fifteen years. There was no past in this channel. There was no brother, no testimony, no alias, no men who smiled wrong. There was water, boat, hands, eyes. The present tense, total and absolute, the way only the sky had ever given it to her before.

The river dropped.

She saw it a heartbeat before it happened—the water ahead going smooth and green and fast, the foam lines converging, the roar changing pitch from thunder to something higher, almost a whistle—and her body understood before her mind did: *ledge. Drop. Now.*

"Down!" She threw herself flat as the bow went over the edge.

They fell. Not far—two meters, maybe less—but in a boat full of water at flood speed, two meters was a long way. The hull slammed, the whole river came aboard at once, and for a moment they were submerged, both of them, in the churning green, and the world was roar and cold and the taste of silt.

Then the boat surfaced. Because it was her boat, and she had kept it alive through three weeks of flood and one ambush and Tomas's fever, and it was not going to die here.

She came up coughing, found the tiller by feel, found the water's face by instinct. The gorge opened ahead of them, the walls falling back, the light going from stone-gray to rain-gray to something almost like sky. The last rapid took them in a long, fast slide, the boat surfing down its face, and Juniper stood in the stern with water streaming off her and laughed out loud—a short, wild, unrecognizable sound that the river took and kept.

* * *

Calm water. Actual calm water, a wide flooded valley below the gorge where the river spread out and remembered how to be a river instead of a weapon. The rain still fell, but it fell like rain again instead of like artillery.

Juniper brought the boat alongside a gravel bar, a low tongue of shingle poking out of the flood, and let the current hold them against it while they both just breathed.

Tomas lay back in the bilge, chest heaving, the bail can still clutched in one hand like a weapon he'd forgotten to put down. His arm was bleeding again. His eyes, when he turned his head to look at her, were wide and wet and full of something she hadn't seen in them before.

"You," he said, and stopped, and started again. "I've been on rivers my whole life. My father ran timber rafts. I have *never*—"

"Fifteen years of flying," she said. "It's the same sky. It's all the same sky. Water just moves slower."

"Slower." He laughed, a cracked sound. "That was not slower."

"It was, though." She looked back up the gorge, at the black walls and the standing haze of spray, at the door she'd just flown through. "In the air you get a second to decide. Down there I had three. Felt like a holiday."

He stared at her. Then he laughed again, harder, wincing when it pulled at his arm, laughing anyway, and after a while she joined him, and they sat on a gravel bar in the middle of a drowned country, two soaked, hunted, half-drowned people laughing at nothing, at everything, at the simple animal fact of being alive when they had every statistical reason not to be.

When it faded, he said, quietly: "You could have run the other way. Upstream, into the side channels. Hidden."

"Hidden is what I do." She turned the bail can over in her hands. "Hidden is all I've done for fifteen years. And look where it got Maren."

He said nothing. He didn't have to. The smoke from Maren's landing was still somewhere behind them, over the ridge, invisible and permanent.

"They'll be at the bottom of the gorge," she said. "The portage trail comes out downstream. They'll radio ahead. Every checkpoint from here to the capital will have our description."

"Probably."

"Good."

Tomas lifted his head. "Good?"

"Let them look for two people hiding." Juniper stood, steadied herself against the tiller, and looked down the long brown reach of the flooded river, toward the relay point, toward Odile's floating station, toward four days of monsoon left and a story that had to get through. "I'm done being hidden. They can watch us come the whole way. It won't matter. By the time they understand what we're doing, it'll already be on the air."

She pushed off the gravel bar. The boat swung its bow downstream, obedient, alive, and Juniper took the tiller the way she'd once taken a yoke—not gripping it, holding it, the way you hold something you've decided to keep.

For the first time in fifteen years, she wasn't running from anything.

She was flying toward it.
