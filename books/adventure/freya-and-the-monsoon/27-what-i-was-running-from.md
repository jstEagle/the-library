## 27. What I Was Running From

The gorge spat us out the way a river spits out anything it decides it doesn't want—sideways, half-drowned, and spinning.

Then, all at once, there was nothing to fight. The water widened into a long brown lake where the current barely bothered to move, and the rain thinned to a fine drifting mist, and the boat wallowed in the sudden calm like an animal that had forgotten how to stand still. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere behind us, the roar of the gorge faded into ordinary river noise.

I sat down on the thwart because my legs told me to, not because I decided to.

Tomas was bailing. Of course he was bailing. The man had taken a bullet graze three days ago, burned through a fever that should have put him flat for a week, and now he was scooping water out of a leaking boat with a dented cooking pot like it was just another chore between breakfast and lunch. His shirt was plastered to his back. His hands shook—not from fear, I'd learned, but from the effort of pretending his body was fine.

"Stop," I said.

"We're taking on water."

"We're always taking on water. It's five minutes of rest or we're both dead when the next set of rapids comes." I took the pot out of his hands and set it down. He looked at me with an expression I was beginning to know too well—the one where he weighed whether arguing with me was worth the calories and usually decided it wasn't.

He sat. The boat rocked. The mist came down soft as breath.

Ahead of us the channel bent around a drowned forest, dead trees standing hip-deep in floodwater, their branches bare and gray like the ribs of something enormous. There was no one within fifty miles of us who wasn't trying to kill us or sell us. No checkpoint ahead that we knew of. No radio. Just water, and time, and the strange hush that follows when you've survived something you had no business surviving.

I found myself thinking about my father's hands.

That was how it always started—not with the big things, the ledgers, the night flights, the men who never used last names. With his hands. Big knuckles, a scar across the left thumb from a winch cable, and the way they could be so gentle. He used to braid my hair before school because my mother worked nights at the clinic, and he braided it badly, uneven, and I wore those crooked braids like medals.

"You're doing the thing," Tomas said quietly.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go somewhere inside your own head and lock the door."

I almost laughed. "You'd know."

"I would." He pulled his knees up, wincing only slightly, and looked out at the drowned trees. "You don't have to tell me anything, Juniper. I want to say that first. Whatever you're carrying, you carried it up the gorge and through the checkpoint and past those men with the wrong smiles, and you didn't need me to carry any of it for you."

"But?"

"But," he said, "you've been circling it for two hundred miles. And I've noticed that people who circle a thing that long either plan to die with it or are waiting for someone to ask."

The mist drifted. A bird I couldn't name called somewhere in the dead trees, one note, repeated, patient.

"My name isn't Juniper Ravensworth," I said.

He didn't flinch. I'd half expected him to say *I know*, because Tomas seemed to know everything without ever saying so, but he just waited, which was worse and better at the same time.

"It's Juniper, though. That part's true. My mother picked it—she said juniper grew where nothing else would, in bad soil, on cliffs, in the cracks of parking lots. She thought it was a good name for our family." I watched my own hands, which were nothing like my father's. "She was right, actually. She just didn't mean it the way it turned out."

* * *

We were called Voss back then. Juniper Voss. It sounds like nothing now—a name from a country that doesn't exist anymore—but for fifteen years it was the name I flinched away from whenever anyone said it, the way you flinch from a sound that was playing when something terrible happened.

My father ran freight. That's what everyone said, and in a way it was true. Voss Freight & Supply: two Cessnas, later four, a hangar at a strip outside a town so small it didn't appear on some maps, contracts hauling diesel and dry goods and mail into river communities the government serviced once a year if it felt generous. We were useful. People liked us. My father paid for the school roof and the church generator and nobody asked why a bush freight outfit cleared enough money to be generous.

I started flying for him at seventeen. By nineteen I was running the interior routes alone, and that's when I stopped being able to not-know what I was carrying.

It wasn't drugs, in the beginning. That would have been simpler. It was guns going upstream, mostly—crates marked as pump parts—and cash coming back down, and then, as the mining concessions opened up along the headwaters, it became whatever the consortium needed moved that couldn't go through customs. Chemical drums with the labels soaked off. Sealed crates that hummed faintly, like they were alive. Once, a man, sedated, breathing slow, strapped into the cargo bay like freight. I flew him and told myself he was sick and being helped. I was very good at telling myself things.

"Dad," I finally said, one night in the hangar, standing under the wing of the plane I'd just landed with forty thousand dollars sewn into a tarp. "What are we?"

He looked at me for a long time. He could have lied. He was good at lying—he'd built a whole life out of it, a warm, generous, beloved life. But maybe he was tired, or maybe he wanted me in all the way, or maybe some part of him hoped I'd say no and free him from whatever he felt about it. He said, "We're the river's plumbing, June. Everything flows through us. Nothing sticks."

Nothing sticks. That was the family creed. You fly the load, you take the money, you smile at the inspector, you pay the man who needs paying, and nothing sticks. Fifteen years later I can still hear him saying it, and what kills me is that he said it with love. Like advice. Like a father teaching his daughter to survive.

"So what did you do?" Tomas asked. Not gently, exactly. Honestly. He'd earned honesty.

"I did nothing for two years," I said. "That's the part I can't forgive. Everyone thinks the hard part is the testimony. The hard part was the two years before it, when I knew, and I flew anyway, because the money was good and Dad was Dad and Mom depended on the clinic job he arranged and my brother—" My voice caught on the word and I let it sit there a second before pushing through. "My brother Daniel worshipped him. Still did, right up until recently, as far as I knew. If I blew the whistle, I didn't just burn Dad. I burned all of it. The house. The town's goodwill. Daniel's whole idea of who our family was."

"And then?"

"And then the Ochoa boy died."

Tomas went still. Even the boat seemed to stop rocking.

Rafael Ochoa was fourteen. His father ran loads for us sometimes—small stuff, a box here, a drum there, enough to keep his store open through the wet season. Rafael used to hang around the hangar asking questions about planes until my father got tired of it and told me to keep him busy. So I taught him preflight checks. Let him sit in the pilot's seat. He wanted to be a bush pilot like me. He had a notebook where he copied down every checklist in my handwriting.

The drum that killed him wasn't ours to begin with—it was a consignment we were storing, a favor for a consortium man named Ferro, and it leaked in the heat. Nobody knew what was in it. Solvent, something, a chemical that made a vapor that found the pilot light in the storeroom heater. Rafael was doing homework in the storeroom because it was warm and quiet and his house wasn't.

They told the town it was a gas cylinder fault. They told his mother it was quick, which was a lie, and painless, which was a bigger lie, because the paramedic who responded told me otherwise years later, drunk, crying, saying he'd never gotten the sound of it out of his ears.

And my father stood at the funeral with his hand on Mrs. Ochoa's shoulder, and the whole town saw it, this good man grieving with her, and I stood next to him wearing black and knowing—I *knew*, I'd read the storage manifest, I'd seen Ferro's men move the drum and say leave it till Monday—that we had killed that boy. That the family creed had killed him. Nothing sticks. Something stuck. It stuck to a fourteen-year-old doing math homework.

"That's when you went to the authorities," Tomas said.

"That's when I went to a federal prosecutor named Ilse Marchetti, who'd been sniffing around the corridor for a year and whom everyone assumed was bought, because everyone was bought. She wasn't. She was the loneliest person I have ever met. I walked into her office and said my name and my father's name and I talked for six hours." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know what she said at the end? She said, 'Do you understand what you've just done?' And I said yes. And she said, 'No. Nobody does. Not yet.'"

* * *

The mist thickened, then thinned. The dead trees slid past. I'd never told anyone the middle part—the part after the testimony—and once I started I understood why. Some stories you don't tell because they're painful. Some you don't tell because they make you look like exactly what you are.

The testimony itself took eleven minutes. Eleven minutes to end the Voss operation: the routes, the names, Ferro, the payment schedules, the inspector at the regional airfield who waved our tail number through for a monthly envelope. My father was arrested at the hangar. He didn't run. I think part of him was relieved, though he never once said so, not in the visiting room, not in the letters.

Daniel didn't come to the trial. That's the sentence I've never said out loud. My brother, who was twenty-two, who had just gotten his own commercial license, whose entire future was the family business—didn't come. He sent one letter. Four sentences. I memorized them the way other people memorize prayers. *You knew what you were doing. Mom hasn't stopped shaking since they came to the house. Dad says don't write again and for once I agree with him. I hope wherever you go, you stay there.*

So I did. I stayed wherever I went.

Here's the part nobody tells you about testifying against your own family: the state protects you right up until it doesn't. Witness relocation gave me a new surname and a stipend and a caseworker, and eighteen months later the program's funding shifted, and my caseworker's calls got shorter, and then a man I didn't recognize asked about me at a fuel stop two provinces from where I was supposed to be, and I understood that somebody had sold my file. Maybe for money. Maybe just to be helpful. Ferro's organization hadn't dissolved; it had reorganized, the way rot does.

So I finished the job the state started and then abandoned. I became Juniper Ravensworth, which was a name I took off a gravestone in a settlement nobody visited—a girl who'd died at nine in 1962, no living kin, perfect. I got a license under the new name through a licensing office in a jurisdiction where a cash donation smoothed paperwork. I flew medevac and supply runs, the honest version of the work I'd grown up doing, and I made rules for myself. Never sleep two nights in the same bed twice in a month. Never give anyone my route schedule. Never, ever testify, sign, register, or appear on paper anywhere that mattered.

Fifteen years of that. Fifteen years of being excellent at disappearing. And do you know what I told myself? I told myself I was safe, and that safety was the same thing as good. I told myself that staying invisible kept Ferro's people from finding me, and that was true, but underneath the truth was the other thing, the thing I'm saying now for the first time in my life, in the middle of a flooded nowhere to a man I've known three weeks:

Invisibility was also how I never paid anything back.

Because here's what happened after I vanished. Ferro needed someone to blame for the collapse, and the obvious candidates were the small people—the Ochoa father, who'd cooperated with investigators; the drunk paramedic who'd talked; two dockhands who'd signed statements. One by one they lost everything. The Ochoas lost the store, then the house. Old Ochoa drank himself into the river one spring—not suicide officially, just a man who fell in, except everyone knew. The paramedic moved away and died alone. The dockhands—one emigrated, one went quiet and gray and never spoke of it.

And I was gone. Safe. Flying medicine to villages, being useful, being *good*, while the people who'd helped me bury my own family were paying the bill I'd left on the table.

"They came for you?" Tomas asked. "After the file was sold?"

"Twice. Once in a border town, a car that followed me from the airstrip—I outran it, barely, and flew out that night with the tanks half-full. Once here, on the river, two years ago. Men asking about a pilot matching my description at a trading post. I changed my hair and moved my base three hundred miles the same week." I shrugged, and the shrug hurt. "Running works. That's the trap of it. Running *works*. Every time it works, it teaches you that running is the answer. Fifteen years of positive reinforcement."

"And the price others paid for your silence afterward—you mean the witnesses."

"I mean everyone," I said. "I mean that silence isn't neutral. I spent fifteen years telling myself I was keeping my head down, minding my business, doing honest work. But every year I stayed quiet, Ferro's successors got richer, and the rivers got sicker, and men like the ones at the checkpoint got bolder, and people like the Ochoas paid for it. My silence wasn't safety. It was subsidy. I was subsidizing all of it with my absence." I looked at him. "You buried contamination reports rather than sign them. You lost your career and nearly your daughter over it. I signed nothing. I said nothing. I just flew away, over and over, for fifteen years, and called it survival."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. The pot sat between us, empty, useless. Far off, thunder muttered—the monsoon clearing its throat, reminding us it wasn't done.

"Do you know what I did the day after I refused to sign?" he said. "Nothing. For a week, nothing. I sat in my apartment reading the report I wouldn't bury, and I told myself I'd been brave, and then I found out they'd reassigned the sampling team and destroyed the baseline data, and the river kept dying, and I realized that refusing to sign a document is not the same as stopping what the document was for." He met my eyes. "You're not the only one who subsidized it, Juniper. Most people do. That's how these things work. Not because everyone is evil—because everyone is quiet, and quiet adds up."

"Is that supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you accurate." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and his voice dropped into the register he used when he meant something completely. "You testified once. At nineteen, alone, against your own blood, knowing it would cost you everything. Do you understand how rare that is? Most people never do it at all. Then the system failed you, and you ran, and the running cost other people—yes. Fine. Own that. But the woman who testified is still in there. I've watched her for three weeks. She lies beautifully and hates it. She hides evidence in her boot like a woman who's done it before. She flew that gorge like the water owed her money. And yesterday, when Maren asked you for a promise, you didn't hesitate because you were afraid of the enforcers. You hesitated because you were afraid of being *seen*. Those are different fears, and only one of them gets to drive."

The mist was lifting. Ahead, the channel narrowed toward a line of green hills, and beyond them, if Odile's coordinates held, the floating relay station. Two days, maybe three, if the weather broke mercifully, which it wouldn't.

"Daniel," I said. "The letter he sent. *I hope wherever you go, you stay there.*"

"He's spent fifteen years hoping you stayed gone," Tomas said. "And he joined a survey team and started pulling water samples and writing meticulous notes, and I'd bet my boat—which is worthless, so it's a safe bet—that somewhere in those notes there's a page where he wondered whether his sister ever thought about the rivers." He paused. "People change their minds about the dead. Sometimes the dead come back and get to hear it."

I looked at my hands again. Juniper Voss's hands. Juniper Ravensworth's hands. Same hands. They'd flown guns and they'd flown insulin and they'd gripped a bail-pot through a gorge that should have killed us, and they belonged to whichever woman decided, in the next two days, what they were for.

"Tomas," I said. "When we get to Odile's station—when this goes out on the open frequency, when it's on record forever—use my real name."

He raised an eyebrow. "Voss?"

"Voss." Saying it aloud felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing. "If I'm going to be visible, I'm going to be visible as the woman who did all of it. The flying, the silence, the testimony, all of it. No more gravestone names."

He nodded slowly, and for the first time since I'd met him, he smiled with his whole face.

"Juniper Voss," he said, tasting it. "All right."

That was when the radio crackled—the little handheld I'd rigged to the boat's antenna, the one that had been dead static for two days. A voice swam up out of the noise, thin and broken, a woman's voice, clipped and urgent, repeating something. Coordinates. And then, beneath the static, a second voice, male, ragged, unmistakable even after fifteen years, even distorted, even half-drowned in interference.

A voice I'd know anywhere, because I'd spent fifteen years hearing it in my sleep.

"Junie," it said. "If anyone's listening—she'll come. Tell her it's the north spur. Tell her—"

Static swallowed the rest.

Tomas stared at me. The thunder rolled closer.

"That's him," I said. "That's Daniel. And he's alive."
