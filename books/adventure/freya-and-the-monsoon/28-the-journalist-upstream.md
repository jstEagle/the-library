## 28. The Journalist Upstream

Tomas heard the broadcast before he saw the station.

It came out of the rain like a voice from another country—a woman's voice, clipped and unhurried, reading coordinates and river depths in three languages, then repeating them. Juniper cut the engine and they drifted, both of them listening the way you listen to a sound you'd stopped believing existed.

"She's still on air," Juniper said.

"Someone is."

"No." Juniper tilted her head toward the static-wrapped voice. "That's Odile Ferran. She did a series on the diamond concessions up north, years back. Got run out of two countries for it. If that's her, she's been broadcasting from this river since before I ever flew it."

Tomas watched her face in the grey light. Fifteen years of flying these waterways and she knew the name of one stubborn journalist the way other pilots knew the names of landing strips. He filed that away with everything else he was learning about her—that her isolation had never been as total as she pretended, that she kept maps in her head of every person on this river who hadn't been bought.

The voice repeated itself. Coordinates. Depths. A warning about debris fields below the gorge. Then, almost as an afterthought, in accented but fluent river creole: *"If anyone out there is carrying something true, come upstream. The water will carry you the rest of the way."*

Juniper looked at him.

"That's not a general announcement," Tomas said.

"No," she agreed. "That's a door left open for someone specific. Or for anyone desperate enough to knock."

They were both, he thought, exactly that.

* * *

The station appeared around a bend where the river widened into a drowned forest, and for a moment Tomas thought he was looking at a wreck. It was a barge—old, flat-nosed, half its paint gone to rust—and it sat low in the flooded timber with cables running from its mast to trees on either bank, anchored against the current like a spider's web strung across the channel. Antennas bristled from the mast. Solar panels, cracked and cloudy, covered every horizontal surface. A generator shed squatted at the stern, and from somewhere inside the barge came the hum of equipment that had no business surviving out here.

Painted on the hull, peeling but legible: *THE RIVER TRUTH — 6210 kHz.*

"They can't shut it down," Juniper said, easing the boat toward the barge through the drowned saplings. "I used to wonder why. Now look at it."

Tomas looked. He understood what she meant. To raid this place, the consortium would need boats, men, gunfire—all of it visible, all of it witnessed by every fishing family within twenty miles who tuned their cheap radios to 6210 every evening. To shut down Odile Ferran quietly, they'd have to make her disappear, and a disappeared journalist became a story bigger than anything she'd ever broadcast. She'd built herself into a trap made of spectacle. The only thing protecting her was how loudly she could scream if touched.

"It's brilliant," he said.

"It's insane," Juniper said. "She's a hostage who volunteered."

A figure emerged onto the barge's deck before they reached it—a small woman, sixties maybe, silver hair cropped short, wearing a rain slicker over what looked like pajamas. She held a shotgun with the easy disinterest of someone who had carried it so long it had become furniture.

"You're late," the woman called across the water. "The man from the depot was here in March. You're four months late."

"We didn't know we were coming," Juniper called back.

"Nobody does. That's how I know you're real." The woman lowered the shotgun. "Tie off on the port cable. Mind the gap in the deck plating—I keep meaning to fix it and I never do."

* * *

Inside, the barge was a cave of paper and electronics. Transmitting equipment filled the bow. The middle was shelving floor to ceiling, and the shelves were crammed with binders, cassette tapes, folded maps, jars of pencils, evidence bags labeled in a precise hand. A wood stove fought the damp. Two cots, a table buried under documents, a kettle. The smell was mildew, solder, and strong coffee.

Odile Ferran poured three cups without asking and studied them over the rims of her glasses.

"Tomas Reyes," she said to him.

He went very still. "We haven't met."

"No. But I read your inspection reports before they buried them. The Kestrel Valley water filings, 2019. You wrote like a man who expected to be ignored and refused to write accordingly." She set his cup down in front of him. "When reports like yours vanish, I keep copies. When inspectors like you vanish, I keep names."

"I didn't vanish," Tomas said. "I was fired. There's a difference."

"There's a paperwork difference." She turned to Juniper. "And you're the pilot. Ravensworth."

Something crossed Juniper's face—quick, controlled, gone. "Juniper's fine."

"Mm." Odile sipped her coffee. "Everyone on this river has one name for the world and one for themselves. Sit. Tell me what you're carrying."

They told her. Not everything—not yet—but most of it. The camp with no tracks. The compass. Daniel's documents from the quiet village. The ledgers from the holding depot, wrapped in oilcloth inside Tomas's jacket, which he'd barely let out of his sight since Maren's landing. The transfer order. Six surveyors sent upriver, alive. The recording of Daniel's voice listing coordinates. The gorge. Maren taken.

Odile listened without interrupting, which Tomas recognized as a professional discipline rather than patience. Her eyes went to the oilcloth package twice, and both times she looked away deliberately, giving them the dignity of producing it themselves.

When they finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the deck above them.

"The ledgers," she said finally. "How many names?"

"Provincial officials, permit clerks, two judges, a police commander, and the consortium's own logistics chain," Tomas said. "Signed transfers. Payment schedules. It's not allegations. It's bookkeeping."

"And the pipeline spur?"

"Documented by my brother," Juniper said. "Water samples, dates, GPS marks. He was thorough. He was always—" She stopped. Started again. "He was thorough."

Odile nodded slowly. She got up, moved to her shelves, and pulled down a binder whose spine read *CONSORTIUM / HYDRO / 2020–*. She laid it open on the table between the coffee cups.

"I have pieces," she said. "Complaints from eleven villages. Photographs of dead fish, dated. A shipping manifest I photographed through a window in the capital two years ago. What I have never had—" she tapped the binder "—is the connective tissue. The signatures. The money moving from one hand to another. Complaints are weather. Ledgers are climate. With those, this stops being a story people argue about and becomes a story nobody can survive denying."

"So broadcast them," Juniper said.

"With pleasure." Odile's smile was thin and sharp as a blade's edge. "But understand what broadcasting means. The moment I read those names on an open frequency, three things happen. One: every corrupt official on this river knows exactly what we have and exactly where we are. Two: the consortium stops trying to be subtle, because subtlety is now worthless to them. Three—" she looked at Juniper, then at Tomas, and her voice gentled by a degree "—the information stops being yours. Once it's on the air, it belongs to everyone who heard it, and everyone who heard it becomes a target. That's the price of daylight. You don't get to hold it anymore. It holds you."

Tomas felt those words land somewhere old. He'd spent years believing reports could change things if only they were honest enough, detailed enough, brave enough. He'd learned otherwise in a windowless office with a man from legal explaining the word *liability* to him. Since then he'd trusted nothing but his own hands and his own silence.

And yet.

"Some truths only work in daylight," he said quietly. It was the argument he'd been having with himself since the depot, since the ledgers, since Maren's face going past him in the bottom of an enforcer's boat.

"Yes," Odile said. "And some people only survive in shadow. The trick is knowing which kind of truth you're carrying, and which kind of person you've decided to be." She closed the binder. "So. Before I touch a single page—why now? Why you? Why should I believe you two aren't the consortium's delivery service, bringing me the bait personally?"

"Because if we were," Juniper said, "we'd have shot you at the dock instead of drinking your terrible coffee."

"It is terrible," Odile agreed. "The beans come upriver through three smugglers. But go on."

"Because I spent fifteen years making sure nobody knew my name," Juniper said, "and I'm sitting in your kitchen anyway. Because he—" she tipped her head at Tomas "—had every reason to hand these papers to a court that would bury them and get his life back, and he carried them through a gorge instead. And because—" her voice caught, steadied "—my brother is upriver somewhere, alive as of a few weeks ago, being held as leverage against exactly this. We're not delivering bait. We're trading the only currency we have for a person we love."

Odile studied her for a long time. Then she turned to Tomas.

"And you?"

"My daughter," Tomas said. The word came out of him easier than it had in years, and stranger for it. "Her mother took her south after I lost my post. I told myself I'd go find her when the water settled. It never settles. But if I'm going to stand in front of her someday and explain what her father did with his years, I would rather explain a broadcast than a burial. I'd rather say I put the truth where they couldn't reach it."

Odile took off her glasses and cleaned them on her sleeve, which gave her face a sudden nakedness.

"Good answers," she said. "Both of you. The last man who brought me something this hot wanted payment in advance and anonymity forever. I turned him away and he sold it to a magistrate instead, and the magistrate buried it, and forty more kilometers of river died." She put the glasses back on. "You want to give it away free. That's rarer than courage."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the rain came and went in long grey curtains. Odile copied the ledgers by hand—two sets, one to transmit, one to hide—and photographed every page with an old camera she kept wrapped in wool. She made Tomas read each name aloud so she could check pronunciation against her files, and several times she stopped, tapped a name, and said, "Him. I knew it. Three years I've known it and had nothing but a feeling." Each time, something in her posture hardened and brightened at once, like metal cooling into a blade.

Juniper climbed to the mast platform with the radio log and helped Odile's technician—a silent teenage boy Odile introduced only as "my nephew, don't ask"—rig a better antenna for the night's transmission. From the deck Tomas watched them work, the pilot and the journalist, two women who had each built a life out of staying just out of reach, now standing shoulder to shoulder in the rain bolting steel to steel.

He understood, watching them, that he had already made his decision. Maybe he'd made it in the depot office, reading the transfer order. Maybe in the gorge. Maybe fifteen years ago, signing his name to a report he knew would end him. Decisions like this weren't moments; they were accumulations. You looked up one day and you were already the person who did the thing.

The kettle screamed. He went inside and poured four cups.

* * *

It was near dusk when Odile spread a chart of the river on the table and weighted the corners with mugs.

"Now the part you won't like," she said. "Timing."

She traced the river with one finger, upstream from the barge, past the falls, past the depot country, into the highlands where the tributaries braided and died.

"The monsoon breaks in four days. Maybe five. The barometer's been falling for thirty hours and the birds went quiet this morning—both mean the tail end is coming hard. After it breaks, this river drops fast. A meter a day in the upper reaches. All these channels—" her finger moved through the braided section "—become mud and gravel bars in a week. No boat gets through. Not mine, not theirs, not the devil's own. Whatever happens upriver happens in the next four days or it doesn't happen until next season."

"Next season," Juniper said flatly.

"Next season. With a consortium that now knows its ledgers are copied, its depot is burned, and its hostages are liabilities. You think Daniel survives eight months of being a liability?" She let that sit. "Neither do I."

Tomas looked at the chart. Four days. The depot was two days' hard travel upstream in good water, and the water wasn't good—it was rising, carrying whole trees, and the enforcers knew these channels better than anyone except Maren, who was gone. Behind them, downstream, the way home was closing too. They were in a corridor that was sealing at both ends.

"And your broadcast?" he asked. "If we go upriver, who transmits?"

"The boy can run the board. I've trained him for exactly this." Odile's mouth tightened. "But understand—if I'm reading names on an open frequency and the consortium decides to stop me, they'll have to do it in front of the whole river. That's my armor. It's also my death sentence with a delay on it. I've accepted that. Have you?"

"Accepted what?" Juniper asked.

"That once I start, I don't stop. Not for threats, not for negotiations, not for hostages." Odile met Juniper's eyes and held them, and Tomas saw the exact moment the words landed—the flinch, the fight against the flinch. "If they bring your brother to a microphone and tell you the broadcast stops or he dies, I will keep reading names. That's what you're buying. That's what daylight costs. Decide now, not later, whether you can live beside that."

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the porthole glass. Somewhere overhead, thunder rolled up the valley from the direction of the highlands, slow and deliberate, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Juniper looked at the chart for a long time. At the braided channels that would soon be gravel. At the small penciled X that marked the depot country. At her brother, four days away and eight months deep in the hands of men who signed transfer orders.

"Read the names," she said. "All of them. As loud as you can."

Odile nodded once, and began pulling ledgers from the oilcloth.

* * *

Tomas stood at the stern after midnight, unable to sleep, watching lightning walk the ridgelines upstream. The boy had gone to his cot. Odile was still awake in the bow, testing frequencies, her voice murmuring into the dark in three languages. Juniper sat on a crate near the mast, cleaning the compass with her sleeve, though it didn't need cleaning.

He went and stood beside her. For a while neither spoke. The river moved beneath them, black and heavy, full of everything it carried.

"Four days," he said.

"I heard."

"We'll need the boat lightened, the leak re-sealed, and a route Maren never finished drawing for us."

"I know."

"And Juniper—" he hesitated, then said it plainly, because she had earned plainness from him, "once Odile starts reading, there's no version of this where you disappear again. Even if we fail. Even if—" he didn't finish it.

She turned the compass over in her hands. In the lightning-flash he saw her face: tired, scared, and underneath it something he'd first glimpsed in the gorge and had been watching grow ever since. Not peace. Readiness.

"I know that too," she said.

Upstream, the thunder rolled again, closer now, and the first fat drops of the storm's next assault began ticking against the deck like a clock starting to count.
