## 29. Enforcers at the Relay

The rain came sideways off the river, and Odile's floating station rode it like a stubborn old bird, listing at her moorings but refusing to sink. Juniper stood in the doorway of the broadcast shack, watching the water hammer the deck, and felt the clock ticking somewhere behind her ribs. Four days. Maybe three. After that the monsoon would break and the rivers would drop and every route to this drifting scrap of truth would close like a fist.

Inside, Tomas was helping Odile string a fresh antenna wire through the leak above the transmitter. His arm was still stiff from the fever, but he moved with a quiet competence that no longer surprised her. That was the thing about trust, she thought. It didn't announce itself. It just showed up one morning as fact.

"Signal's clean," Odile said, tapping the meter. She was sixty if she was a day, gray hair cropped short, forearms like dock rope, and she ran the station the way other people ran sieges. "We go live tomorrow at dawn. I want the ledgers read on open frequency before anyone can stop us."

"They'll try," Tomas said.

"Of course they'll try." Odile grinned. "That's why we're doing it now."

Juniper said nothing. She had spent fifteen years learning when not to speak, and the habit sat deep. But she caught herself looking at the ledgers—wrapped in oilcloth, stowed under the floor panel where Daniel's compass used to sit—and feeling something she hadn't felt since she was nineteen years old and standing in a courtroom with her hand on a Bible.

She was going to be visible again. And some part of her, the part that had learned to love the fog and the empty strips and the name nobody could spell, wanted to bolt.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn.

Juniper heard them first—the way you hear things when you've spent half your life listening for engines over engine noise. Not boats running dark. Boats running loud, three of them, outboards wide open, coming upriver against the current with the arrogance of men who owned it.

"Tomas." She put a hand on his shoulder and he was awake instantly, the way soldiers wake. "Boats. Three. Coming fast."

Odile was already moving, killing the interior lights, throwing canvas over the antenna mast. "Back channel," she muttered. "There's a side slough behind the station—we slip the mooring and drift if we have to."

But there wasn't time. The first boat swung into the pool of floodlight the station's own generator threw across the water, and men came over the gunwales before it even stopped moving. Six of them. Then four more from the second boat. Rifles slung casual, the way men carry weapons when they've never had to answer for them.

The third boat held back, idling in the rain, and a man stepped onto the deck last, unhurried, like he was boarding his own property.

Juniper's whole body went cold.

He was heavier than she remembered. Gray in his beard now, a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there before. But she knew the way he stood—weight on the heels, hands loose, head tilted slightly like he was listening to a joke only he could hear. She'd seen that stance across a hundred campfires on the wrong side of the law, back when she was a girl hauling contraband up frozen rivers under a name that wasn't hers.

His name was Corrigan Salter. Her father had called him Salt. He'd carried their cargo for six years, broken a man's arm over a short payment once, and laughed about it at dinner afterward while she'd sat there with her fists clenched under the table, fourteen years old and learning exactly what kind of family she'd been born into.

He turned, scanning the deck, and his eyes passed over her face—

—and stopped.

Fifteen years is a long time. It changes faces. But it doesn't change the way recognition lands, that fractional stillness, the pupil widening a half-millimeter before the rest of the man catches up. Juniper watched it happen and knew he had her.

"Well," Salter said, and smiled, and the smile was worse than any weapon on that deck. "Small world."

Nobody else understood yet. The enforcers were busy kicking open hatches, dragging Odile's two young assistants out of the sleeping quarters, upending crates of spare parts into the bilge. One of them found the transmitter and put his boot through it with a sound like a rib cracking.

"The ledgers," Salter said pleasantly, to no one in particular. "Where are they?"

"You're too late," Odile said, chin up, rain streaming off her jaw. "Everything's already transmitted. Every name. Every date. Your paymasters are finished."

Salter looked at her with mild interest, the way a man looks at weather. "Grandmother, if anything had been transmitted, I wouldn't be standing here dry." He turned back. "So. The ledgers."

Tomas shifted, putting himself half a step between Salter and Juniper, and she saw him do the math the same way she was doing it: ten armed men, two rifles between them, a wounded ex-inspector, a pilot, an old woman, and a boat that leaked.

"We don't have them," Tomas said evenly.

"See, that's interesting," Salter said, "because I know you do. And here's what's even more interesting—" and he turned fully toward Juniper for the first time, letting the floodlight catch his face so she couldn't pretend not to see it, "—is that I know *her*."

* * *

The rain kept falling. Somewhere downriver, thunder rolled like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

"Ravensworth," Salter said softly. "Or whatever you're calling yourself these days. June? Junie? Your daddy used to say you were the sharpest knife in a drawer full of hammers." He shook his head, almost fondly. "I carried your family's freight when you were still losing baby teeth. And then one day you got up in front of a judge and burned the whole operation to the ground, and I did eighteen months because of you. Eighteen months. I had time to think about that."

"I'm sure you did," Juniper said. Her voice came out flat and steady, and she marveled distantly at how well the armor worked after all these years. "And I'm sure you've told yourself a story about it that makes you the victim."

"Oh, I'm not the victim." He spread his hands. "I'm employed. Same as always. Your family went down, so I went to work for people with deeper pockets. The consortium pays better than your father ever did, and the work's the same: make problems disappear." He tilted his head. "You'd know all about disappearing."

One of his men called out from below: "Found the floor panel!"

Juniper's stomach dropped. The oilcloth bundle came up dripping, and a man in a yellow slicker held it overhead like a trophy fish.

"No," Odile breathed.

"Careful," Salter said mildly. "That's worth more than everyone on this boat combined." He took the bundle, weighed it in one hand, and looked at Juniper with something almost like respect. "Your brother's handwriting, I hear. Family business runs deep, eh? You people were always good with paper."

"The surveyors," Tomas said. "Six of them. Where are they?"

"Alive, last I checked." Salter shrugged. "Leverage keeps better than meat."

"And Daniel Ravensworth?"

"Also alive. Also leverage." He smiled again. "In fact—" and here he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping a gift he'd been saving, "—that's why I'm glad you're here, June. Because my employers have a message for you. They want the ledgers. All of them. In exchange, your brother walks. You have until the monsoon breaks to decide. After that, the rivers close, the deal closes, and Daniel becomes a problem we make disappear. Like the others would've been, if your brother hadn't been useful."

The words landed in Juniper's chest like stones dropped down a well. She heard them hit bottom, one after another.

Her brother. Alive. Held. Being traded for the truth.

* * *

They took everything. Transmitter smashed, fuel siphoned, Odile's assistants zip-tied to the mast and left in the rain. Salter's men worked with the bored efficiency of professionals, and Salter himself stood watching Juniper the entire time, never letting her out of his sight, as if afraid she might dissolve back into the fifteen years she'd stolen from him.

At the end, he walked close—close enough that she could smell wet canvas and cigarette smoke, the exact smell of her childhood—and spoke low, under the rain-noise, where only she could hear.

"You should've stayed gone, June. I mean that kindly. There are men above me who read files, and your file has your real name in it now. Fifteen years of running, undone by one bad coincidence." He patted the oilcloth bundle against his chest. "But look at the bright side. You get to save your brother. All you have to do is hand me the copies."

"Copies?" Juniper kept her voice level. "You have the ledgers."

"I have *a* ledger book." He tapped his chest. "I don't have the copies. The photographs. The samples. Whatever else you clever Ravensworths squirreled away. My employers assume a woman who survived your family survived with backups. They're right, aren't they?"

She said nothing. He nodded slowly, reading her silence like a manifest.

"Dawn tomorrow," he said. "There's a sandbar two miles downstream. Come alone, bring the copies, and you'll get Daniel at the exchange point upriver within the week. Bring company or tricks, and we'll know. We always know. Ask Maren."

The name hit her like a slap. Maren, taken at her own landing because of a guide's betrayal. Maren, whose fate Juniper had been carrying like a stone ever since.

"What did you do to her?" Juniper asked quietly.

"Nothing she didn't survive," Salter said. "Yet. She's leverage too. Everyone's leverage these days. It's the whole economy." He stepped back, raised his voice to normal register. "Load up! We're done here."

As his boat pulled away into the rain, Juniper stood at the rail with the floodlight dying behind her and the river running black past the hull, and she felt fifteen years collapse into a single point. The past hadn't come knocking. It had kicked the door in, put its boots through the transmitter, and known her name.

Tomas came to stand beside her. He didn't ask what Salter had whispered. He just watched the boats' running lights shrink upstream, then said, "You know him."

"I grew up with him," Juniper said.

"Can he be bought?"

"He already has been. By worse people than us."

"Then we need a plan," Tomas said, "before dawn."

Behind them, Odile was already pulling splintered planking off the backup transmitter she'd hidden inside a false bulkhead, muttering curses with the calm of a woman who had been raided before and intended to be raided again. "Dawn still comes," she said. "And so does the broadcast."

Juniper looked at the black water, at the place where the boats had vanished, and felt the old instinct rise—not the instinct to run, which had ruled her for fifteen years, but something older and colder underneath it.

The instinct to fly straight at the thing hunting her.

"Dawn," she said. "Two miles downstream. He wants me alone." She turned to Tomas, and for the first time since the trading post, she let him see all of it—the fear, the fury, the family name she'd buried twice. "Then he picked the wrong Ravensworth."

Downriver, thunder rolled closer, and the monsoon leaned in like it was listening.
