## 31. Daniel

The depot smelled of rust and creosote and, underneath it, the sweet-rot stink of standing water that never quite drained. Daniel had catalogued the smell the way he catalogued everything now—methodically, obsessively, because method was the last thing they hadn't taken from him.

They had taken his boots first. That had been the worst of the early days, worse than the zip ties, worse than the hood. Not because of the boots themselves but because of what he'd had forty seconds to do with them, back on the ridge, when the men came out of the fog with their polite voices and their list of names. Forty seconds to kneel in the mud, to work the heel free of the left boot, to fold the pages small—smaller than small, down to the size of a matchbook—and press them into the hollow he'd cut weeks earlier with a heated knife, then seal it with wax and boot grease and pray.

They had given the boots back three days later. That was the thing about men like Corben's crew, the thing Daniel had written about in his reports for eleven years and never quite believed until now: they were not thorough. They were thorough about money, about ledgers, about who owed what to whom. They were not thorough about a surveyor's cracked left boot, because to them a boot was a boot. They had photographed the team, catalogued the equipment, logged the satellite phones into an evidence bag that would never see an evidence room. But the boots they'd shaken out and returned, because men who thought of everything except the inside of a heel were men who had never been poor, never been watched, never learned that the state watches the front door and the truth goes out through the floor.

Daniel Vane had been keeping notes in his boot for sixty-three days.

He knew the number because keeping count was also method. Sixty-three days since the ridge. Sixty-three days since he'd watched his four surveyors—kids, really, the oldest of them twenty-six—walk down into the fog with their hands up, and heard the engines of the boats, and understood with a clarity that felt almost physical that this was not a rescue and never had been.

They'd been moved twice. First to a river station with a corrugated roof and a man who read paperback westerns and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. Then upriver, here, to the depot—a real installation, pre-fabricated buildings on concrete piers, a crane rusted over the water, the whole place wearing the costume of abandonment like a coat. On paper, Daniel was certain, this depot did not exist. He had spent his career reading government inventories, and he knew the genre: a line item marked *decommissioned*, a structure struck from the rolls, and then, in practice, a building with new locks and a diesel generator and a radio mast that was supposed to have been torn down in the last fiscal year.

That radio mast was the reason he was still sane. Barely.

The guards were not cruel, which was somehow worse than cruelty. Cruelty you could hate. What they practiced was a kind of administrative indifference—the same tone Daniel had heard from a hundred officials signing off on a hundred permits. Food arrived twice a day. The bucket was emptied. Nobody answered questions. Nobody needed to. The message was structural: *you are a filing problem now, and filing problems are solved by filing.*

There were six of them in the long room at first. Then four—the two who'd signed the papers they were slid through the slot in the door, the ones who'd decided that a statement and a signature were worth more than their principles, and who had walked out one gray morning and not come back. Daniel didn't blame them. That was another thing he'd learned: blame was a luxury for people with options. He'd signed nothing. Not out of courage. Out of spite, mostly, and out of the stubbornness of a man who had spent his whole adult life believing that measurements mattered, that a water sample was a kind of oath, that numbers did not lie even when everyone around them did.

The water samples were in the boot. Forty-one of them, logged by site and date in his cramped, even hand. The pipeline spur, marked in red on a map he'd redrawn from memory three times until the coordinates lived behind his eyes. The names of the officials who had signed the concession, and the dates, and the amounts—amounts he'd overheard in the first week, when the guards still talked in front of them, before someone above had told the guards to stop talking.

He had one page left blank. He didn't know why he'd left it blank. Some instinct, maybe. Room for the ending, whatever the ending was.

* * *

On the sixty-third day, the rain changed.

Daniel had become a student of the monsoon the way a prisoner becomes a student of any wall. There was the ordinary rain, the drumming gray constant of it. There was the storm rain, which came in walls and made the tin roof speak in tongues. And then there was the other kind—rare, brief, a hard vertical hammering that meant the sky had given up on subtlety, that the whole season was gathering itself for the peak.

That was the sound on the morning the guard made his mistake.

The guard's name was Pell. He was maybe twenty-three, with a sunburned neck and a habit of humming when he thought no one could hear, tuneless, the same four notes over and over. He was the one who'd brought the boots back, and Daniel had decided long ago that Pell was the weak point—not weak in character, exactly, but weak in the way that young men are weak: they wanted the story to be simpler than it was. They wanted to be on the right side and hadn't fully checked which side that was.

Pell came in with the noon rice, ducking the rain off the eaves, and he was agitated. Daniel read agitation the way he read water samples—by the parts per million. Pell's hands were wrong. Pell's radio, clipped to his shoulder, was on, and turned up, which it never was, because the guards kept their channel quiet in the depot the way priests keep their voices down in a church.

And through the static, under the hammering rain, Daniel heard a woman's voice.

He heard it in fragments. The rain ate most of it. But the fragments were enough, the way a single vertebra is enough to tell you the size of the animal.

*"—broadcasting on open frequency—"*

*"—hostage exchange—"*

*"—state your name for the record, sir—"*

Pell fumbled the radio off his shoulder, thumbed the volume down, and stood there in the doorway with the rice going cold in his hands, and Daniel watched him the way you watch a man standing on ice and listen for the crack.

Because Daniel had heard that voice before. Not the words. The voice.

Fifteen years was a long time, but voices kept. Voices lived in a place below memory, below forgiveness, in the cellar of a person where the things that broke a family were stored. He had heard that voice last at a hearing he had watched from the back of a courtroom, nineteen years old, while a girl two years younger than him sat in the witness box with her hands folded and dismantled everything their parents had built, brick by brick, name by name, into a microphone, in a voice that never shook.

*Juniper.*

Not Juniper then. She'd had another name then, the family name, the name that was on the ledgers and the boats and the indictment. She'd traded it afterward, he'd heard—rumors, fragments, a cousin who swore she'd gone north into the bush, a lawyer who said she'd left the province the week the verdicts came down and no one had seen her since. Fifteen years of silence, and he had filled that silence with a hundred versions of her, most of them cruel, because it was easier to be angry at an invention than to miss a sister.

He had told himself, for fifteen years, that he hated her.

He had told himself that what she'd done was betrayal—testifying against their father, against their uncle, against the whole machinery of the family business, putting her own blood on the stand and cutting it loose in public. He had told himself that the family had been poor and hardworking and hounded, that the charges were exaggerated, that she had been turned, manipulated, that she'd done it to save herself.

He had believed it right up until the day, eleven years into his career as a government surveyor, when he'd stood on a riverbank with a sample bottle in his hand and watched the water come down the color of rust, dead fish along the banks like fallen leaves, and traced it upstream to a concession signed by men his father used to drink with—and understood, with the same physical clarity he'd felt on the ridge, that his sister had been right.

That was the shame he carried now, in the long room, in the boot. Not the captivity. The fifteen years. The invention he'd built and maintained and defended, all so he wouldn't have to sit with the simpler, harder truth: that his little sister had been braver at nineteen than he had been at thirty, and that he had hated her for it because her courage made his silence look like what it was.

* * *

The rain kept hammering. Pell stood in the doorway, and Daniel stood by the wall, and between them the radio hissed with a voice from the cellar of his life.

"Who is that?" Daniel asked. He kept his voice flat. A guard who thought you knew something became a guard who watched you; a guard who thought you were confused stayed bored. "On the radio. Who's doing a broadcast out here?"

"Nobody," Pell said. Too fast. "Weather station. Interference."

"Interference with a name," Daniel said. "I heard a name."

"You heard rain." Pell set the rice down and left, and the door shut, and the bolt went home, and Daniel stood in the gray light doing the thing he did best, the thing they could not confiscate: he worked the problem.

A broadcast on open frequency. A hostage exchange in progress. A woman's voice he knew.

He made himself go slowly. Hope, he had learned, was a contaminant; it got into everything and skewed the readings. So he treated it like a sample and ran the tests.

One: the exchange was happening now, on the river, downstream. The depot was upriver. The men here reported to the men there—Corben's crew, the polite ones, the ones who'd taken them off the ridge. If there was an exchange, it involved the surveyors, or it involved leverage, and leverage meant the ledgers, or the samples, or the map.

Two: the map. His map. The one in his boot, the one he'd redrawn three times from memory. If someone had his notes—

No. Nobody had his notes. Nobody could have his notes. They were in the heel of a boot that had walked with him through two relocations and sixty-three days, and he checked the seal every night by feel, the wax, the grease, the folded paper, like a man checking a pulse.

Three: a woman's voice, on an open frequency, doing the one thing the consortium feared more than anything else in the world. Not violence. Not theft. *Light.* Daniel had spent his career around men who would burn a building, buy a judge, bury a report—but he had watched them flinch, actually flinch, at the phrase *on the record.* Their whole architecture was built in the dark. It was not strong. It was only unlit.

And four—the fourth reading, the one he kept arriving at no matter how he tried to dilute the sample:

The voice was hers.

His sister was on the river.

* * *

That night, after the rice and the dark and the drumming rain, Daniel took off the left boot.

He did it the way he always did it, sitting with his back to the wall, angled so the door's observation slot showed nothing but a tired man tending his feet. He worked the heel loose. He drew out the folded pages, and he did not read them—he knew them, all of them, the way you know a prayer—but he checked them: dry, intact, the wax seal holding, the paper soft at the folds but whole. Forty-one samples. The spur. The names. The amounts. Sixty-three days of the only honest work being done in this whole drowned province, hidden in a heel.

Then he turned to the last page. The blank one. And for the first time, he picked up the pencil stub he'd traded two days of rice rations for, and he wrote.

Not coordinates. Not names. Just three lines, in his cramped, even hand, in the dark, by feel:

*If someone finds this: the boot is the archive. The archive is true. I was wrong about my sister, and I would like the chance to tell her so, if the river is kind.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then he folded the page in with the others, pressed the heel home, and pulled the boot back on.

Because that was the thing the shame had hidden from him for fifteen years, the thing the long gray days of captivity had slowly, painfully rinsed clean: he was not waiting to be rescued. He had never been waiting to be rescued. He was waiting to be *worth* rescuing—to have done, before the door opened, something that justified the opening of it. His sister had testified and vanished into the bush rather than be bought. He had signed nothing and hidden paper in a boot. It was a smaller courage. But it was the same kind. It was the family kind, the one thing their father had given them that was actually worth inheriting: the refusal, deep in the bone, to be lied to.

Somewhere downriver, in the peak of the monsoon, that refusal was talking on an open frequency in a voice he knew.

Daniel Vane put his back to the wall, laced his boots—the left one snug, the archive riding against his heel—and listened to the rain come down on the roof of a building that did not exist, and for the first time in sixty-three days, he let himself hope, and did not test it for contamination.

The bolt would open soon. He could feel it the way he felt weather coming.

And when it did, he intended to walk out of here on the truth, one heel-print at a time.
