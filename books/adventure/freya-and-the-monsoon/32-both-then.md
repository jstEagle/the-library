## 32. Both, Then

The lamp in Odile's station hissed like something alive, and Juniper stood over the table with the ledgers stacked between them and said, "No."

Tomas looked up. Odile's pen stopped mid-word.

"Verrane wants a trade," Juniper said. "Ledgers for Daniel. And everyone in this room is already deciding which half of me wins—the sister or the witness." She put her palm flat on the ledger's cracked cover. "I'm telling you it isn't a choice. We do both."

"Juniper." Tomas kept his voice low, the way he did when he thought she was about to do something reckless, which was most of the time lately. "The man has your brother. You don't negotiate with men like that. You pay and you grieve later."

"I've spent fifteen years paying first and grieving later." She heard her own voice come out flat and cold, the voice from before, the one she'd buried under a new name and a new country and ten thousand miles of empty sky. "It never bought anything back."

Odile leaned back in her chair until it creaked. The floating station rocked gently beneath them—old barge, older generator, a transmitter that had outlived three governments' attempts to shut it down. Rain drummed the tin roof in long rolling sheets, and somewhere below decks the river slapped against the hull like a slow hand.

"Say it properly," Odile said. "All of it. I want to hear the shape of it before I decide if I'm broadcasting a hostage exchange or an execution."

Juniper pulled the second ledger off the stack—the duplicate she'd had copied at Maren's landing, back when trust was still something she could afford to spread around—and laid it beside the original.

"We give Verrane what he asked for. Ledgers for Daniel, clean swap, neutral ground. But the ledgers he gets are decoys—real paper, real binding, real signatures where it counts on a quick look. I copy the pages that matter into this one." She tapped the original. "And while the exchange is happening, you're live."

Odile's eyebrows went up slowly.

"Open frequency. Full power. You read the names while it happens. Not after—*during*. Every official in those books, every transfer order, every payment. And you narrate the exchange itself. A government contractor trading kidnapped surveyors for evidence of their own crimes, on open channel, timestamped, witnessed by however many people still have radios working in this weather." She looked at Tomas. "He can't shoot us afterward. He can't make us disappear quietly. Because by the time he realizes the ledgers are copies, there are two hundred people who heard him trade a hostage on air."

Silence, except the rain.

"That's not a rescue," Tomas said finally. "That's a bomb with my friend's brother strapped to it."

"It's the only kind of rescue left." Juniper pulled out the chair across from Odile and sat down for the first time in hours, and felt the exhaustion hit her all at once, like turbulence she'd been flying through without acknowledging. "Think about it. If we just pay, Daniel comes back and Verrane hunts us downriver at his leisure. If we refuse, Daniel disappears upriver and we never see him again. Either way the consortium wins, because either way the story dies with us. That's how they've always worked. They don't need to beat you. They just need you quiet."

"And this way?" Odile asked.

"This way they have to beat us *loudly*." She met the old woman's eyes. "You told me yourself—your signal reaches the provincial relay at Kessel when the atmosphere cooperates. Kessel has journalists. Kessel has opposition MPs who've been starving for something like this. Once it's on the record, once it's *broadcast*, burying it means burying everyone who heard it. That's a different order of crime. Even for them."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Outside, lightning turned the river white for half a heartbeat.

"The timing would have to be exact," she said at last, and Juniper heard the shift in her voice—the reluctance draining out of it, the professional taking over. "If I go live too early, Verrane hears the frequency open and aborts. If I go live too late, the exchange finishes and you've got no leverage left. And if he searches the decoys before releasing Daniel—"

"He won't search them on site. He'll want distance. Men like Verrane don't open stolen goods in front of witnesses; they take them home and count them in private. It's pride as much as caution." She'd watched men like him her whole childhood—her father's buyers, her uncles' partners, the polite men who came to the house with briefcases and left with futures. "But even if he does check—if he flips to the middle pages and sees the dates run wrong—we're already live. He can't undo being heard."

"You're gambling your brother's life on a man's vanity," Tomas said.

"I'm gambling it on fifteen years of watching how these men operate." She didn't flinch from him. "It's the best information I have. And I am *done* letting them be the only ones who know things."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Odile produced paper, ink, a bottle of glue so old it had gone amber, and the particular ruthless competence of a woman who had forged broadcast licenses for twenty years. Juniper copied ledger entries by lamplight—names, dates, sums, the careful bureaucratic handwriting of men who moved poison and people with equal paperwork—while Tomas aged the duplicates over the stove smoke and tea-stained the edges to match.

It was strange work. Intimate, almost. Three strangers bent over a table making a lie together, and Juniper found she wasn't afraid of it. Forging documents should have felt like her old life crawling back up through her skin. Instead it felt like the opposite: her old life, finally pointed in the right direction.

Halfway through the third hour, Tomas set down his brush and looked at her across the table.

"When did you get good at this?"

"When I was nineteen." She didn't look up from the page. "My family ran cargo that didn't exist on any manifest. I learned to fake bills of lading before I learned to drive. My father used to say a good forgery isn't a copy—it's a document that tells the truth about a world that never happened." She finished the entry, blotted it. "I hated him for teaching me that. Took me a long time to understand it's just a skill. It's what you aim it at."

"And you're aiming it at Verrane."

"I'm aiming it at everything he stands on."

Odile snorted from the corner, where she was splicing cable with her teeth and a knife. "The girl talks like a manifesto now. Two weeks on my boat and everyone talks like a manifesto."

"You love it," Tomas said.

"I love ratings," Odile said. "This will be the biggest thing since the dam collapse. If we live."

"If we live," Juniper agreed, and found she could say it lightly, and was surprised by that.

* * *

Near dawn the rain slackened to a steady grey whisper, and they gathered at the table again to walk it through, piece by piece, the way she used to walk through a flight plan—every leg, every alternate, every place the weather could kill her.

The exchange site: the shingle bar below the old ferry crossing, two hours downstream, open ground, one road in and none out except the river. Verrane would insist on it because it favored his guns. Fine. Open ground also favored Odile's antenna.

The signal: Odile would run a dummy transmission at dawn—a weather bulletin, boring, legitimate—to establish the frequency as active and innocent. Then silence. Then, at the agreed hour, full power, and she would read.

The decoys: packed in the oilskin satchel, weighted right, smelling right, Tomas having done something unspeakable to the spine of the second volume to match the tear in the original. Juniper checked them three times anyway.

The recording: Odile pressed a battered portable recorder into Juniper's hands. "You carry this. Whatever happens at that shingle bar, I want it on tape—not memory, tape. Memory gets shot. Tape gets played in courtrooms."

"And if I get shot?" Juniper asked.

"Then Tomas carries it," Odile said, "and I read your name on the air so beautifully they'll build you a statue."

Tomas laughed—one short, surprised bark—and then looked almost embarrassed by it, and Juniper realized she'd rarely heard him laugh at all, and that it changed his whole face, and that she wanted, absurdly, to hear it again after all this was over. After. She was making plans that assumed an *after*. When had that started?

"One more thing," she said. "When I hand over the satchel, I say a line. On the recorder, clear, for the broadcast. 'These are the ledgers. Check them against the transfer orders from the holding depot.' Something that makes it undeniable this was a trade—that he took evidence to buy a hostage. I need his crime on tape in his own presence."

"You want him to incriminate himself by accepting a bribe," Odile said, delighted.

"I want the record to show exactly what happened. No ambiguity. No version where we simply lost the ledgers and he generously returned a hostage." She closed the satchel. "Fifteen years I let other people write what happened. Never again."

* * *

They slept in shifts, or tried to. Juniper lay on the narrow bunk in the stern cabin listening to the river and rehearsing lines she might say to Daniel, discarding each one as too angry or too soft or too much like forgiveness she hadn't earned the right to offer yet.

What she kept coming back to was the compass. Her name on the back, in her father's engraving. *Ravensworth.* She'd carried it three hundred miles to keep it away from men like Verrane, and somewhere along the way it had stopped being a relic and started being a key. Her family had built things that poisoned rivers. Her family had taught her every trick she was about to use against the people who'd learned it from them. There was no clean side of her history to stand on. There was only the choosing.

She got up before first light and found Tomas already awake on deck, drinking tea that had gone cold, watching the water.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said.

"I was thinking about my daughter." He said it simply, without the flinch the words used to carry. Fever had burned that out of him, or the gorge had, or she had. "After today, if it works—I think I'll go find her. Take a bus like a normal person. Knock on a door."

"What will you say?"

"I haven't got that far." He glanced at her. "What will you say to yours? Your brother?"

"Nothing prepared," she said. "I think that's the only honest option left."

He nodded slowly, and handed her the cold tea, and she drank it because refusing would have meant talking more, and some mornings the river says everything worth saying.

At full light, Odile ran her dummy bulletin—wind speeds, river stages, a fishing advisory—and signed off with the station's old call sign, and the frequency went quiet, and the trap sat armed and patient in the morning air.

Juniper loaded the satchel into the boat. Tomas cast off the line. The current took them downstream toward the shingle bar, toward Verrane's rifles, toward her brother, and Juniper sat in the bow with the recorder against her ribs and felt something she hadn't felt in fifteen years.

Not fear. Not exactly.

Momentum. The feeling of a plane under her hands with the runway behind her and the decision made—no more circling, no more holding pattern, no more waiting for conditions to improve. Committed. Airborne.

She keyed the recorder and spoke clearly, for the record:

"My name is Juniper Ravensworth. Today's date, the shingle bar below the old ferry crossing. What follows is a hostage exchange, witnessed and documented."

Ahead, the bar came into view through the rain-haze—grey shingle, brown water, and figures waiting with the terrible patience of men who believed the world belonged to them.

On the bank behind them, hidden in the treeline, Odile's transmitter hummed at full standby, one switch from daylight.

Juniper raised one hand, palm open, and steered for the shore.
