## 33. The Broadcast

Odile Ferreira had spent eleven years learning that silence was the most expensive commodity on the river.

She'd bought her floating station with money she'd saved from a newspaper job that ended when the consortium's lawyers arrived—three of them, smiling, sliding a settlement agreement across a desk like it was a menu. She'd refused to sign. She'd lost the job, the apartment, and for a while, most of her friends. What she'd kept was a stubbornness she'd inherited from a grandmother who'd run a printing press out of a fishing shack until the military came for it, and Odile had watched from the reeds as they burned the type. She'd been nine years old. She remembered thinking: *they're afraid of letters. Letters must be powerful.*

The station sat on a converted barge moored in the slack water behind a drowned island, forty kilometers of flooded forest from anywhere official. Its mast rose eleven meters above the deck, guyed with wire and salvaged antenna, and its transmitter—a beast Odile had rebuilt twice from parts scavenged from three dead stations upriver—could push a signal across two hundred kilometers of monsoon country on a clear night. The rain never gave her clear nights. She broadcast anyway.

For years the signal had gone out into the static like a message in a bottle. River people tuned in when their radios could catch it. Fishermen heard her read market prices and weather warnings and, once or twice a season, names—the names of men who had come to villages with clipboards and cash and left with signatures that meant surrender. She read them slowly. She spelled them. She repeated them at the top of every hour for three days running, because repetition was the only armor truth had against being swallowed.

The consortium had tried to buy her twice. The second time, the man who made the offer had done so while standing on her deck, and afterward she'd found his business card tucked under her coffee mug, weighted there deliberately so she'd know he'd touched her things. She'd framed the card and hung it by the transmitter. It hung there now, next to a photograph of her grandmother's press, as the rain hammered the barge roof and Juniper Ravensworth stood in her doorway dripping river water onto the floorboards.

"You're sure," Odile said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question since the woman had walked in an hour ago carrying a canvas satchel that clinked faintly, like it held more than paper.

"I'm sure," Juniper said. "But I want you to understand what happens after. Once those names go out, they can't be pulled back. Not ever."

"Good." Odile turned back to the mixing board, checking levels with fingers that knew every slider's position in the dark. "That's the whole point of broadcasting, my friend. Everything else is conversation."

* * *

They worked through the evening by lamplight while the storm built itself somewhere over the western hills, gathering its breath.

Tomas sat propped against the bulkhead, his infected arm bound fresh, watching Juniper copy ledger entries onto transmission sheets in her careful pilot's handwriting—block capitals, no ambiguity, each name followed by dates and amounts. Sixty-one entries. Odile had counted them twice. Provincial officials. Port authority men. Two names from the capital that made even Tomas go quiet when he saw them, one of which appeared in government broadcasts weekly, reading prepared statements about transparency.

"The frequency," Odile said, tapping the dial. "I'll go out wide first. Open band, no encryption, everything the consortium listens to will hear us coming. That's deliberate. Then I'll repeat on the relay bands so the village sets catch it."

"They'll jam you," Tomas said.

"They'll try." She smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile. "Jamming takes equipment, and equipment has to be moved, and moving equipment takes men, and men take orders from the same sixty-one names I'm about to read aloud. By the time anyone authorizes a jamming signal, this will have been heard by four hundred river receivers, two mission stations, and—if the weather holds—one very persistent shortwave hobbyist in the capital who records everything I send and mails copies to a lawyer friend who owes me favors." She tapped the dial again. "You don't kill a signal after it's loose. You can only wish you had."

Juniper looked up from her copying. "And if they come here instead? To shut you down?"

Odile gestured at the windows, at the black sheeting rain, at the drowned forest pressing close around the barge. "Look outside. They'd need boats, and boats need calm water, and the river hasn't been calm in six days. That's why I'm still alive, my friend. Inconvenience is a form of armor too."

But she'd also rigged the backup. Everyone who lived long enough on this river rigged backups. Under the deck plates near the stern, sealed in wax and oilcloth, sat a second transmitter core, a hand-crank generator, and a copy of every master tape she'd ever aired, wrapped in plastic. If the barge burned tonight, the signal would rise again tomorrow from wherever someone carried the box. She didn't tell them about the box. Some things you kept in reserve, the way a card player kept a card.

At 2100 hours, the storm arrived in earnest, and Odile decided that was perfect. Storm static buried weak signals but not strong ones, and a voice cutting clean through a monsoon sounded like what it was: something that would not be silenced by weather.

She put on the headphones. Adjusted the microphone. And pressed the switch that opened the channel.

"This is Riverlight, calling on all frequencies. This is Riverlight. I am Odile Ferreira, and what follows is a public record. If you are hearing this, you are a witness."

She glanced at Juniper, who stood beside the board with the satchel open at her hip, and nodded once.

Then Odile began to read.

* * *

She read the way her grandmother had set type: steadily, without ornament, letting the words carry their own weight.

*"Entry one. Harlan Voss, Director of Concessions, Meridian Consortium. On the fourth of March, payment authorized to the account of Deputy Minister Corren Hale. Amount: forty thousand. Purpose noted in ledger margin: 'river corridor approval.'"*

She spelled the names. She repeated the amounts. At the top of each hour she restated the provenance—"these figures are taken from shipping ledgers recovered from the Meridian Consortium's holding depot at the abandoned Kestrel Mine site"—and then continued where she'd left off, because continuity was credibility, and credibility was the only currency a broadcaster had.

By the third hour, the river was answering.

The receiver bank along the port side wall began to light up—acknowledgment signals, station identifiers, the little coded pips that river operators used to say *heard you, understood, standing by.* A mission station eighty kilometers south. A cooperative radio hut that served eleven villages. A lone operator whose call sign Odile recognized as belonging to a retired schoolteacher who ran a two-watt set from a houseboat and had never once missed her nightly sign-off. The pips accumulated like fireflies.

And threaded through them, unmistakable, came the other kind of traffic: encrypted bursts, terse and angry, on the consortium's working channels. Men asking other men questions. *Confirm location. Confirm source. Who else has copies.* Odile let those transmissions play through her monitor speaker at low volume, and she smiled at them the way you smile at distant thunder.

"They're panicking," Tomas said quietly.

"They're recalculating," Odile corrected. "Panic comes later, when they understand the arithmetic. Right now they think this is still a problem that can be located and removed." She keyed the mic between entries. "This is Riverlight. To any parties attempting to trace this transmission: your efforts are noted, and your efforts are part of the record. Every attempt to suppress this broadcast will be reported in the same manner as the payments themselves. The audience is invited to listen to both."

Beside her, Juniper had gone very still. She was holding a small recorder against her chest—not Odile's equipment; her own, a battered field unit she'd produced from her flight bag—and its red light burned steady.

"What are you doing?" Odile asked, though she'd already guessed.

"When they come," Juniper said, "and they will come, I want the negotiation on tape. Their voices. Their offers. The moment they realize the trade is dead." Her jaw tightened. "Daniel's been leverage for weeks. Tonight they find out leverage doesn't work anymore. I want to hear how a man sounds when his price drops to nothing."

Odile studied her for a moment—the mud-dried clothes, the eyes that had seen too much river in too few days, the steadiness of the hands holding the recorder. Fifteen years invisible, and now standing in a radio room volunteering to be heard. There was a story there, and Odile had learned not to ask for stories before their owners were ready to give them.

"You sound like a journalist," she said.

"I sound like a pilot," Juniper answered. "I'm just flying a different machine."

* * *

The enforcers came at 0230, which told Odile two things: they'd traced the signal faster than she'd estimated, and they were desperate enough to run the flooded river at night to do it.

She heard them before anyone—her ears trained on the water the way Juniper's were trained on sky. Engines, two of them, running heavy and low, pushing against the current with the particular roughness of overloaded gunboats. She killed the cabin lamp and keyed the mic one last time.

"This is Riverlight. To our listeners: we are about to receive visitors. Stay with us."

Then she opened the exterior speakers to full volume, so that whatever happened on this deck would go out over the open frequency, live, unedited, into the rain and the dark and the ears of everyone on two hundred kilometers of river.

Juniper moved to the doorway with the recorder raised. Tomas, gray-faced, took the spare pistol from the chart drawer and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the situation hadn't already said better.

The gunboats swung alongside in a wash of floodlight. Four men came up the ladder, and the fifth stayed below with the engines, and their leader was a broad man in a soaked slicker whose face Odile didn't know—but whose voice, when it came, made Juniper flinch as if struck.

"Ravensworth." He said the name like a blade drawn slow. "Fifteen years, and you crawl out of the bush to play radio."

The recorder's red light burned steady in Juniper's grip.

"You've got thirty seconds to explain why I shouldn't burn this barge with all three of you on it," the man went on, wading forward, boots ringing on the deck plates.

"Because it's live," Odile said pleasantly, stepping into the floodlight with her hands visible and her voice pitched for the microphone as much as for the man. "Every word you say is going out right now, unencrypted, to every receiver on this river. Your name will follow your voice within the hour—I've had eleven years to learn who hires men like you, and the ledgers I read tonight include payments to a security contractor whose initials match yours. So please. Keep talking. The audience is listening."

Something shifted in the man's face then—not fear exactly, but the cold recalculation of a man discovering the ground has changed under him. Failure was survivable; his employers had absorbed failures for decades. But exposure—exposure traveled. Exposure got quoted in hearings. Exposure made patrons calculate whether a hired man was worth the heat he carried.

"You're bluffing," he said, but his voice had dropped, and the floodlight caught sweat on his face that the rain couldn't account for.

"Am I?" Odile reached over and touched a switch, and from the barge's speakers, recorded moments earlier, his own voice played back into the night: *burn this barge with all three of you on it.* She let it play twice. "Threats against a licensed broadcast facility, issued on open frequency, witnessed by four hundred listeners. My friend, you haven't failed yet. This is the first competent thing you've done all night."

Behind him, one of his men shifted, and Juniper's voice cut across the deck, clear and hard:

"Danny's alive. Where?"

The leader turned to her, and for a moment the two of them simply looked at each other across fifteen years and a rain-slicked deck, and whatever passed between them was older and darker than anything Odile's microphones could carry.

"He's collateral," the man said finally. "He stays collateral until the ledgers come back."

"Then you have a problem," Juniper said, "because the ledgers aren't mine to give anymore. They belong to everyone who heard tonight's broadcast. You can't trade for something that belongs to everybody." She lifted the recorder slightly. "And neither can you threaten your way out of it. Your employers just became famous. Famous men don't pay for silence—they pay to make problems disappear, and you—" her voice didn't waver at all— "you just became the loudest problem on this river."

The man stared at her. Rain drummed the deck. Somewhere in the dark, the encrypted channels were screaming at him through his earpiece—she could see him listening, see the orders arriving, see the exact moment the orders stopped making sense.

Because that was the arithmetic Odile had counted on. The consortium could absorb a failed raid. It could absorb lost evidence—it had lost evidence before, and buried the loss. What it could not absorb was a public negotiation, broadcast live, in which its hired muscle threatened arson and murder on open frequency while demanding the return of documents proving bribery at the deputy-minister level. The story had stopped being a secret with a price tag. It had become a spectacle with witnesses. And spectacles, unlike secrets, could not be bought back.

"Pull back," the earpiece was saying, audible now in the quiet, tinny and frantic. "Pull back, do you hear me, pull back—"

The leader's jaw worked. His men waited. The floodlights trembled in the rain.

And then he did the thing Odile would replay in her memory for years afterward: he smiled. Slowly. At Juniper.

"Your brother sends his regards," he said. "Ask her what happened at Kestrel Mine, boys—no, wait." He raised his voice suddenly, pitching it toward the open microphone, toward the two hundred kilometers of listening river, and his grin widened into something genuinely cruel. "Ladies and gentlemen of the audience—ask Juniper Ravensworth what she did fifteen years ago. Ask her family. Oh, that's right—" he spread his arms, savoring it— "she *changed her name.*"

The rain filled the silence.

Juniper didn't move. Didn't lower the recorder. But Odile, standing three feet away, saw the color leave her face, saw the fifteen years of armor take the hit dead center—and saw, beneath it, something that hadn't been there before.

She didn't look away.

"Thank you," Juniper said quietly, into the recorder, into the open frequency, into the dark. "That saves me the trouble of saying it myself. Yes. I changed my name. And when this is over, I'll tell everyone why—myself, in my own words, under my own name. You've just guaranteed it."

The leader's smile died by degrees.

"Get off my boat," Odile said.

They went. All four of them, down the ladder, into the gunboats, engines churning away into the rain—and Odile killed the exterior feed, sagged against the mixing board, and discovered her hands were shaking for the first time in eleven years.

Tomas exhaled. Juniper stood motionless in the doorway, recorder still raised, red light burning.

"Well," Odile said, when she could trust her voice. "That, my friend, is going to be quoted in hearings."

* * *

Dawn came gray and relentless, and with it the tally.

The acknowledgment pips had doubled overnight. The schoolteacher on the houseboat had copied every entry and was rebroadcasting on her own frequency. The mission station had wired the capital. Somewhere out in the flooded country, four hundred receivers had passed sixty-one names from ear to ear, and the names were loose now in a way that no amount of money could gather back.

Odile brewed coffee on the spirit stove and poured three cups, and they drank them standing at the windows, watching the rain begin—finally, grudgingly—to thin.

"They'll move Daniel now," Juniper said. "Tonight, maybe sooner. With the ledgers public, he's worthless as leverage and dangerous as a witness. They won't keep him. They'll either disappear him permanently or cut him loose in the confusion." Her knuckles whitened around the cup. "Either way, the clock just started."

"Then we'd best finish our coffee," Odile said.

Outside, the river had begun to drop—imperceptibly, a finger's width below the mark scratched on the mooring post, but dropping. The monsoon was turning. In four days, maybe five, the waterways would close for the season, and every boat on this river would be high and dry and useless.

Until then, the signal was still live, the names were still traveling, and somewhere upriver, in a depot full of ledgers and lies, a man named Daniel Ravensworth was waiting—without knowing it—for a sister who had just introduced herself to the entire world.

Juniper set down her cup.

"We move at first light," she said. "Sooner, if the water holds."

Odile looked at her—at the woman who had spent fifteen years being no one, standing in a radio room with a recorder full of a killer's voice and a plan forming behind her eyes like weather.

"And where exactly," Odile asked, "are we moving *to*?"

Juniper smiled, and it was the first real smile Odile had seen from her.

"Upriver," she said. "To get my brother."
