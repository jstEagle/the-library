## 34. Rising Water, Falling Men

The rain came down like a verdict.

Juniper stood on the deck of Odile's floating station with the decoy ledgers in a waterproof satchel at her feet and the real ones strapped against her ribs beneath her jacket. Across the black water, lantern light wobbled on the enforcers' boat—three of them visible, rain slickers shining like oiled hide, and at the bow the man she had known for fifteen years as Cutter Voss.

He'd been younger when he worked for her family. Leaner. He'd carried crates up riverbank stairs without complaint and never once asked what was inside them. Now he was broader, harder, his face weathered into something like stone, and he stood in the rain with Daniel's life held in one hand and Juniper's past in the other.

"Bring the ledgers to the rail," Voss called over the storm. "Alone. Your man stays back."

"You bring my brother to the rail," Juniper shouted back. "Then we talk."

Behind her, inside the station's cramped cabin, Odile crouched over her transmitter, the red recording light burning steady. Tomas stood at the cabin door with a boat hook gripped in both hands, his graze wound dressed but his color still wrong, his jaw set like a man who had decided how he was going to die if it came to that and found the decision restful.

* * *

It had seemed clean an hour ago. That was the thing about plans—they always looked clean until they touched water.

Odile had gone live at dusk, reading ledger names across the open frequency while Juniper fed her pages: provincial water boards, licensing officers, two names that made even Odile pause before saying them aloud. The consortium's empire, read out like a grocery list into the monsoon dark. And layered beneath it, playing on a loop, the recorded negotiation—the voice of a hired enforcer offering to trade a kidnapped surveyor for evidence of crimes against the river.

Voss couldn't shoot the station without spectacle. Couldn't sink it without confirming everything. The math had been beautiful.

But Voss had done his own math, standing in the rain on his own boat, and somewhere between dusk and now he had concluded that a dead witness was cheaper than a live broadcast.

* * *

"Last chance," Voss said. The rain hammered the deck planks. Downriver, the gorge they'd run two days ago would be a white roar by now; the monsoon had stopped pretending to be weather and become a fact of geology. The river had risen a full meter since morning. It sucked at the station's mooring lines and dragged at the pilings, and every few minutes the whole structure groaned like something dreaming.

"The exchange first," Juniper said. "Daniel walks onto your deck where I can see him. Then the satchel comes across."

"And if I just take the station?" Voss said it almost gently, the way you tell someone the price of something they can't afford. "My paymasters are finished either way—the names are already out. You think anyone upstream cares about procedure tonight? There's no one left to report me to that isn't already bought or already running."

"Then why are you still negotiating?"

Something flickered across his face. Not doubt. Recognition, maybe—the old reflex of a man who had spent years around the Ravensworths, who knew what their blood did under pressure.

"Because I remember your family," he said. "And I want to know which one you are."

* * *

They brought Daniel out.

He shuffled to the rail of the enforcer boat with his wrists bound in front of him, thinner than the boy Juniper remembered, beard wild, eyes hollowed but—she saw it even across the black water—still moving. Still cataloguing. Even now, even after weeks in a holding depot, her brother was counting exits.

Their eyes met across fifteen years and ten meters of flooding river, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that could be said at that distance that wouldn't drown before it arrived.

"Let him step off," Juniper called. "Onto the dock plank. Alone."

Voss nodded to his men. They cut Daniel's feet free and walked him to the gangplank—a single board laid between the boats, flexing over water moving fast enough to kill a swimmer in seconds. Daniel stepped onto it. One step. Two. His bound hands out for balance, his face turned toward Juniper with an expression she couldn't read and would spend the rest of her life not trying to forget.

Three steps from safety, Voss gave the order.

She saw it before she understood it—one of the men at the enforcer boat's engine shifting his weight, the muzzle of a rifle swinging not toward her but toward the plank—and then the shot cracked through the rain and the board splintered under Daniel's feet and he dropped.

Not into the water. He caught the broken edge with his bound hands and hung there, swinging above the current, screaming something the rain tore apart before it reached her.

"It was never an exchange," Voss said, almost conversationally, over the storm. "It was leverage. And leverage is only worth keeping while there's someone to sell it to." He raised his rifle toward the station's transmitter mast. "You've got nothing left to trade, Ravensworth. So here's the new arrangement—"

Tomas hit him from behind.

* * *

Later, Juniper could never reconstruct those four minutes in order. Memory broke them into shards and handed them back one at a time, forever, like a dealer who kept the sequence for himself.

She remembered Tomas going over the rail with the boat hook and landing on the enforcers' deck in a sprawl of rain and rope, and Voss turning, surprised—not by the attack but by its source, the quiet stranger with the bad arm choosing this moment to stop being careful.

She remembered diving for the mooring line, because the station was tearing loose, the rising water ripping the old pilings out of the riverbed one by one, and someone had to keep the floating station from becoming driftwood with Odile and the transmitter aboard.

She remembered the second rifle shot and the mast light dying, and Odile's voice still going out over the frequency, calm as a lighthouse, narrating: *"They're shooting at us. You all heard it. Everyone on this river heard it."*

She remembered the fire.

One of the enforcers—panicked, or following orders, or simply done—threw a flare into the station's aft fuel locker. Flame went up the wet wood like it had been waiting years for permission. The heat hit Juniper's face from six meters away, and the real ledgers were strapped against her ribs, and the fire was between her and the water, and the choice arranged itself with terrible simplicity.

*Stay and burn, or jump and drown.*

She jumped.

* * *

The river took her like it owned her.

The current was no longer water; it was the whole flooded watershed moving at once, trees and debris and cold pressure shoving her under, spinning her, and the last thing she saw before the surface closed was the station burning above her, orange light smeared across the underside of the sky, and a shape at the rail—Tomas, back aboard somehow, bleeding, hauling the mooring line, holding the whole mad structure together with his ruined arm while the enforcer boat swung away into the dark.

The ledgers dug into her ribs. She curled around them the way you curl around a child, around a wound, around the only true thing left, and let the river do what rivers do.

She didn't fight it. Fighting was for people with somewhere to be. She had already been everywhere she needed to go; the rest was arithmetic. She thought, with strange clarity, about the compass on her plane's dashboard, engraved with a name she hadn't spoken in fifteen years, and she thought: *at least it's out of the rain.*

Then a hand closed on her collar.

* * *

Tomas had tied himself to the station with the mooring line—her own suggestion, days ago, half a joke about his leaking boat—and when she went under he paid out rope and dove, one-armed, a man with an infected graze wound and no business swimming in floodwater, and caught her by the jacket collar the way you catch something you have decided not to lose.

He hauled her along the line hand over hand, her weight and the current's weight and his own, and at some point she surfaced enough to hear him saying her name—not *Freya*, the name she'd flown under for fifteen years, but nothing, just sound, just effort—and she grabbed the rope and climbed.

They came up over the station's rail together as the fire reached the main cabin and Odile abandoned the transmitter mid-sentence, grabbing the recording cylinders instead of the equipment, because Odile understood, as Juniper understood, that the machine was replaceable and the truth was not.

The three of them stood on the burning deck as the station tore free of the last piling and began to move with the flood.

Behind them, downstream, the enforcer boat's running lights swung once, twice, and vanished. Abandoned. Voss's paymasters had cut him loose the moment the names went out over the air, and a man like Voss, alone, unpaid, named on an open frequency, was more dangerous than any of them—but he was also, for the first time in his career, nobody.

"He'll run," Tomas said, coughing river water. "Men like him always run."

"No," Juniper said. Her teeth were chattering so hard she could barely shape the words, but she shaped them. "Men like him run when there's somewhere to run *to*. He's got nothing now except the thing that ruined him." She looked at the flames, at the ledgers soaked but sealed against her body, at Odile clutching her recordings like scripture. "He'll come for the evidence. It's the only story left where he isn't the villain."

* * *

They swam for the bank—or rather, the bank came to them, the flood shoving the burning station sideways until its hull ground against a submerged levee, and they stepped off into waist-deep water and waded ashore through rain that felt like the sky emptying itself of every secret it had ever kept.

The station burned behind them until the river took it, folding the flames under the brown water one plank at a time. Odile watched it go with dry eyes. "I broadcast from worse," she said.

Daniel was gone.

The enforcer boat had taken him—no, Juniper realized, standing in the mud, wringing the river out of her hair: the shot had broken the plank, and Daniel had fallen, and the current—

"Tomas." Her voice cracked. "The plank. He fell. Did you see—"

"I saw." Tomas was scanning the water, the banks, the debris-choked eddies, with the flat competence of a man who had spent years reading rivers for a government that never thanked him. "Current runs slack along the far bank below the bend. If he caught anything—" He stopped. Pointed.

Downstream, snagged against a half-submerged fig tree, something pale moved. A hand. Bound wrists catching the lightning's light.

* * *

They ran.

Through mud that sucked at their knees, through rain, Tomas falling twice and getting up both times, Juniper out ahead because fifteen years of flying alone had built a body that did not accept its own limits. The fig tree leaned out over the flood, its trunk angled downstream, and Daniel hung against it with the water tearing at him, alive, conscious, his bound hands hooked over a root, his eyes finding hers through the rain with an expression that contained, she thought later, every unsaid word of thirty years.

She got an arm under his chest and pulled, and the root held, and Tomas anchored her belt from behind, and between the three of them and the tree and whatever gods govern flooded rivers, Daniel Ravensworth came out of the water breathing.

They lay on the bank in a heap, the three of them, gasping, while the monsoon screamed overhead and the river took the last of the burning station past them in glowing pieces.

Daniel spoke first. Of course he did. He'd always been the one who couldn't leave a silence alone.

"You're heavier than you look," he wheezed.

"You're supposed to be grateful."

"I'm working up to it." He coughed, spat brown water, and looked at her—really looked, taking in the woman she'd become the way you read a changed landscape from the air. "Freya," he said, testing it. Then, quieter, with the old family precision: "Juniper."

Her whole body flinched. Fifteen years of armor, and her brother disarmed it with one word in the rain.

"Not here," she managed. "Not yet."

"Fine." He closed his eyes. "I've got notes in my boot that say you'll want. Names, dates, transfer orders. I kept records the whole time they held me. In case." A ghost of the old smile. "In case anyone ever came."

* * *

Lightning showed them the shape of the night ahead: the flooded plain, the distant ridge, the capital four days downstream if the monsoon allowed it, and somewhere in the dark, a man named Voss with nothing left to lose and one remaining purpose.

Tomas built a fire from wet wood because Tomas could build a fire from anything, and they huddled around it—two strangers who had become something else, and a brother returned from the dead, and a journalist feeding recordings into a waterproof case like a woman packing relics.

"We need to move before dawn," Tomas said. "Flood peaks tomorrow. After that, the channels start dropping, and Voss knows these backwaters better than we do."

"He worked for my family," Juniper said. "He knows *me*."

"Then we don't go where you'd go." Tomas fed the fire another branch. "We go where I'd go."

Juniper looked at him—at the man who had tied himself to a burning station on the strength of a half-joked suggestion, who had dove one-armed into a flood for a woman whose real name he'd learned an hour ago, who asked for nothing and had nearly died twice for less than nothing—and something in her chest that had been clenched for fifteen years loosened by a single degree.

"Why?" she asked. Just that. Just the question she'd been carrying since the dock.

Tomas watched the fire. "Because I buried reports once," he said. "Clean, professional, quiet. And a village downstream drank what I helped bury." He poked the coals. "I'm not burying yours."

Above them the rain intensified, hammering the sheltering canopy, and downriver, unseen, the floodwaters rose toward their peak, and somewhere in that drowned darkness a man with a rifle and a grudge was making his own calculations about a satchel, a journalist, and a woman who refused to stay invisible.

Dawn was four hours away. The water was still rising.

And Voss was already moving.
