## 35. Daniel Free

The rain had stopped being weather and become a fact of the world, the way gravity was a fact, the way grief was. Juniper stood in the wreckage of the floating station with river water sloshing around her ankles and the ledgers pressed against her chest inside a waterproof satchel, and she listened to the silence where gunfire used to be.

Tomas was beside her. He'd earned that position twice over in the last hour—once when he pulled her off the deck as the enforcer leader's last shot tore through the space her head had occupied, and once before that, in ways she was still counting. His arm was wrapped again, fresh bandage over old infection, and his face had the gray look of a man running on something deeper than adrenaline.

Odile's station listed badly to port, half its antenna array dangling into the brown water like a drowned bird. But the transmitter light still burned green. Somewhere out there, on every open frequency for two hundred miles, the names from those ledgers were still repeating. Odile had recorded eleven hours of it before the raid came, and now the words lived outside anyone's power to recall them.

"They'll regroup," Tomas said. His voice was rough, scraped raw by fever and smoke. "The ones who ran. They won't come back here, but they'll report upriver."

"Let them." Juniper heard her own voice and didn't recognize it. It was flat. Steady. The voice of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning exactly how much noise to make and had finally, permanently, gotten it wrong. "There's nothing left for them to protect. It's all already said."

* * *

They searched the station because searching was what you did after violence—you moved through the aftermath cataloguing damage, counting costs, postponing the moment you'd have to feel any of it.

The enforcers had come fast and left faster. Tables overturned. Odile's map wall shredded by a stray burst. One of her young operators, a boy called Pell who'd never stopped grinning the whole time Juniper had known him, sat on the floor holding a rag to a scalp wound and insisting, repeatedly, that he was fine, absolutely fine, could someone please hand him the coffee pot.

Juniper checked the mooring lines. Checked the fuel drums. Checked the small armored case where Odile kept her backup recordings—it was intact, which meant the story would survive even if the station didn't.

It was Pell who found him.

She heard the shout first—a high, cracked sound from the far end of the station—and then footsteps hammering down the walkway, and then Pell's voice again, breaking apart on a single word:

"He's *here*. There's a man in the supply skiff—he was hiding under the tarp—"

Juniper's body understood before her mind did. Her feet were moving. The satchel swung against her hip. Rain hammered the tin roof and the river breathed against the pilings and none of it mattered, none of it existed, because she was walking toward the end of the station where a battered skiff was tied alongside, and under a soaked canvas tarp a figure was sitting up slowly, one hand raised—not in threat, in something older than threat, in surrender or greeting or simply the gesture of a man who had been waiting a long time to be seen.

He was thinner than she remembered. Bearded, sun-darkened, one eye swollen half-shut from an old injury going yellow-green at the edges. His clothes hung off him like they belonged to a bigger man. His wrists bore the pale rings of tape and rope, worn long enough to leave their signature in skin.

But it was Daniel.

Her brother. Six years older than her and looking twenty years older than that, alive, blinking against the gray light like a man surfacing from deep water.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on canvas, on tin, on the swollen river, on thirty-five years of shared history and fifteen years of deliberate erasure.

"You got tall," Daniel said finally. His voice was gravel and rust. "Or I shrank."

It was such a stupid, ordinary thing to say that something in Juniper's chest cracked wide open.

* * *

Later—she couldn't have said how much later; time had gone soft and unreliable—she was crouched beside the skiff with a med kit open on her knees, cleaning the rope burns on his wrists while he held very still, the way people do when they've learned that sudden movement gets punished.

"I escaped," he said. "During the raid. They moved us yesterday—all six of us, upriver, they were going to scatter us across different depots before the monsoon closed the channels. Made it harder to find us all at once. Harder to prove we ever existed." A dry, terrible almost-laugh. "Bureaucracy. Even kidnapping has paperwork."

"The others?"

"Two made it into the bush with me. Reyes and Okafor. They're heading for the mission station at Kalawe—the sisters there don't ask questions." He winced as she worked antiseptic into a raw line of skin. "The other three... they took them north. Faster boat. I don't know where."

"We'll find them," Juniper said, and heard the promise land in the air between them like something with weight.

Daniel looked at her then—really looked, for the first time since she'd knelt down. His good eye traveled over her face the way a man reads a document he's only heard about secondhand.

"You cut your hair," he said.

"Fifteen years ago."

"Yeah." Something moved behind his eyes—guilt, maybe, or just the exhaustion of a man too tired to hold anything back. "I know. I've known for a while. Since the village. The elder mentioned a pilot named Ravensworth, and I thought—no. Couldn't be. She changed her name, Dad made sure of that, made sure everyone knew she'd betrayed—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "He told everyone you were dead. Did you know that? For the first few years. Then he told everyone you were worse than dead."

"I figured."

"That's all you have to say? *I figured?*"

"What do you want me to say, Daniel?" She tied off the bandage, perhaps a little harder than necessary. "That I'm sorry? I'm not. That I regret it? Some days. Most days I just remember what was in those containers, and I'd testify again tomorrow."

"I'm not asking you to take it back." His voice cracked on the last word, and he pressed his lips together until he had control again. "I testified too, eventually. Did you know that? Year eight. After Mom died. I gave them everything I had on the shipping routes." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Nobody cared by then. The case was cold, the family was scattered, and my testimony went into a file somewhere and died there. You did it when it *cost* something. I did it when it was safe and useless. So no—I'm not here to collect an apology."

"Then why does it feel like you're angry?"

"Because you *vanished*!" It came out of him like floodwater through a broken levee, fifteen years of it, all at once, loud enough that Pell and Odile both turned to stare. "You didn't write, you didn't call, you didn't leave so much as a postcard—and I understand, I *understand*, the marshals explained the protocol, but Juniper, I was nineteen years old and I woke up one morning and my whole family was gone. Dad in custody. You in witness protection or a shallow grave, nobody would tell me which. And I spent fifteen years not knowing if my little sister was alive, and then I find out she's been forty miles away the entire time, flying medicine to villages, being a *hero*, while I—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "While I got a job. And a mortgage. And a missing persons poster with your face on it that I kept in my desk drawer for eleven years."

The rain drummed. The river breathed. Somewhere downstream, Odile's transmitter repeated the names of bought men into the storm.

"I kept the compass," Juniper said quietly.

Daniel lowered his hands.

"After I left. When they gave me the new name, the new life—I took it. From my room. I don't know why. Maybe because it was the one thing in that house that pointed somewhere true." She reached into her jacket, slow, and drew it out: brass, dented, engraved with the family name in their father's careful hand. She set it on the thwart between them. "I've carried it fifteen years. Through three identities. I told myself it was a talisman. I think it was a debt."

Daniel stared at it. His jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice was barely above the rain.

"You could have come back. After it was safe. After the trial. You could have—"

"There is no *after* for people like me, Daniel. There's before, and there's running, and there's hoping the running looks enough like a life that you stop noticing the difference." She shook her head. "And then this survey team goes missing, and I find your compass in the mud—or I thought I did, I thought the universe was playing some kind of joke—and suddenly I'm on a leaking boat with a stranger, running rapids and dodging bullets, and every mile down this river I could feel it. Fifteen years of armor coming off plate by plate. Because the truth is I wasn't hiding from Dad's enemies. I was hiding from being somebody's daughter. Somebody's sister. People are leverage, Daniel. People get hurt because of who you are. So I became no one."

"And now?"

Juniper looked at the satchel of ledgers. At the transmitter light burning green. At the swollen river carrying the evidence of poison past villages that had gone quiet, past banks lined with dead fish, past everything the consortium had tried to drown.

"Now I'm about to be the most visible woman in the province," she said. "Testimony, name, face, all of it. Odile's broadcasting it tonight. And when the officials arrive to sanitize this place, they're going to find me standing on the dock saying my real name out loud." She met her brother's eye. "So if you're going to be angry at me, Daniel, be angry fast. Because in about twelve hours I stop existing quietly forever."

Daniel was silent for a long time. Then he picked up the compass, turned it over once in his ruined hands, and set it back down between them—not taking it, not returning it, just letting it sit there in the space that belonged to both of them.

"I'm not angry," he said finally. "I'm furious. I'm heartbroken. I'm so relieved I can't stand up straight. I'm all of it at once, and I don't know which one is real yet." He exhaled, long and shaking. "But you came. Everyone else gave up—officials, colleagues, my own government filed us under 'misadventure'—and you flew into the worst monsoon in a decade and burned your whole hidden life down to find us. So." He looked at her, and his good eye was wet. "Ask me again in a year whether I forgive you. But right now, in this minute, I'm just glad you're alive, Junie."

Nobody had called her that in fifteen years. The nickname hit her like a wave hitting a breakwater, and she had to look away, out at the river, until her own eyes stopped burning.

* * *

They came ashore as the light failed, tying the skiff below the station where the bank rose steep and muddy toward the tree line. Odile had declared the station too exposed for the night—with the enforcers scattered and reporting upward, everyone agreed the smart move was dispersal: Odile and Pell to a safehouse upstream, the recordings distributed three ways so no single seizure could kill them.

Juniper helped Daniel up the bank. He moved like a convalescent, carefully, testing each foothold, and she found herself matching his pace without thinking—an old rhythm from childhood, from creek beds and game trails, from a lifetime ago when they'd been a unit of two against the world.

Tomas waited near the fire ring at the top, having built a small flame under a lean-to of palm fronds. He looked at Daniel, then at Juniper, and without a word he unrolled a third sleeping mat beside the other two and put the coffee pot on the coals. That was all. That was everything.

They ate canned rations and drank bitter coffee while the rain settled into a steady whisper overhead. Daniel told them about the depot—the holding station they'd already found, the ledgers, the transfer order. He confirmed everything, detail by detail, his memory meticulous even now, the surveyor's habit of precise observation burned into him by months of captivity.

"They kept records," he said, disgusted. "Of everything. I think they thought paperwork made it legal. Sign here, initial there, six surveyors transferred upriver for 'security processing.' Like we were crates of equipment." He stared into the fire. "You want to know the worst part? Half the guards were decent men. Doing a job. Following orders. One of them slipped me extra food when he thought no one saw. Another apologized once, quietly, like that fixed anything. That's how it works, Juniper. Not monsters. Just men who decided the paychecks mattered more than the math."

"That's how it always works," Tomas said.

Daniel nodded slowly. Then he reached into his boot—the same boot, Juniper realized, that had walked out of a holding depot, onto a prisoner barge, through a raid—and drew out a folded scrap of paper, soft as cloth from handling.

"Notes," he said. "Every day. Dates, movements, names, faces. In case I got out, or in case someone found my body and wondered why." He held it out to Juniper. "Add it to the ledgers. Make it count."

She took it. Their fingers touched on the handoff, briefly, and neither of them pulled away.

* * *

Near midnight, with Tomas asleep and the fire burned to orange coals, Juniper sat awake beneath the lean-to and listened to her brother breathe.

He was asleep on his side, facing her, and even in sleep his brow was furrowed, as if dreams were just more paperwork to process. Fifteen years of absence lay between them like the flooded country itself—too wide to cross in a night, full of drowned things and uncertain depths. She knew better than to try to wade it now. Some crossings you make in daylight, with ropes, with time.

But she watched him, and she let herself feel it—the thing she'd forbidden herself for fifteen years. Not absolution. Not yet. Maybe not ever, fully. But presence. The simple, staggering fact of him, alive, thirty feet away, breathing the same rain-washed air.

Tomorrow Odile would broadcast her testimony. Tomorrow she would say her name—her real name, the one written on his birth certificate next to hers—and become visible to every enemy her family had ever made. Tomorrow the officials would come with their sanitizing teams and their quiet settlements, and she would refuse them, publicly, on the record.

Tonight there was just the fire, and the rain, and her brother.

She was almost asleep herself when Daniel stirred, and spoke without opening his eyes, his voice thick with the edge of dreaming.

"Junie. The compass. Why'd you really keep it?"

She was quiet a long time.

"Because it was the only thing I owned that pointed home," she said.

In the dark, she heard him exhale—something between a laugh and a sob—and then the rain took the rest, and the river carried it away, and morning came up gray and certain over the water, bringing with it the sound of an outboard motor, still distant but closing, and a voice on the radio crackling through the static: officials inbound, by air and by boat, and among them, traveling under consortium escort, a provincial minister whose name appeared in the ledgers three times.
