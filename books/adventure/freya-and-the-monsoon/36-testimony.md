## 36. Testimony

The rain had stopped.

Not the way it stopped every evening—a pause between breaths—but stopped, actually stopped, for the first time in weeks. The monsoon was breaking early over the lower reaches, and the silence on the floating station's deck was so complete that Juniper could hear the river settling against the hulls, could hear Odile's transmitter cooling with a faint metallic tick somewhere below.

She stood at the rail and looked at her hands. They were steady. That surprised her more than anything else about the last two days—the ambush, the ledgers soaked but legible, Tomas's arm around her shoulders as the enforcer leader's men scattered into the flooded treeline like rats off a sinking barge. Her hands should have been shaking. Instead they were just hands, cracked at the knuckles, stained with rust-water and gun oil, holding nothing.

"Juniper." Odile's voice from the cabin doorway. "They're coming."

"I know. I can hear the engines."

Two boats, maybe three, coming upriver from the provincial seat—government launches, white paint, official markings, the kind of craft that arrived after everything was already decided. Juniper had watched them come for other people her whole life. After the fires were out. After the bodies were buried. After the story had been shaped into something safe enough to file.

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less if the current's with them."

Odile came to stand beside her at the rail. She was a small woman, sixty-something, with wire-rimmed glasses fogged permanently by the humidity and the posture of someone who had been sued, threatened, and run out of three different postings without ever once lowering her chin. She'd been broadcasting from this converted barge for eleven years because no one could shut down a radio station floating in international-adjacent waters without making headlines doing it.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic," Odile said.

"I'm deciding how much of myself goes into this."

"That's not arithmetic. That's surgery." Odile pushed her glasses up her nose. "And you've got an hour to decide where to cut."

* * *

Inside the cabin, the evidence lay spread across Odile's chart table under a tarp to keep off the drip from the ceiling seam. The ledgers from the depot—waterlogged, pages fused in places, but the ink of the important entries had been good ink, the kind consortium accountants used because they never expected anyone to read it. Daniel's field notes, recovered from Maren's people before Maren's landing burned. The transfer order with six names and a signature that would end at least one career above the provincial level. And the recording—forty minutes of Juniper's own voice negotiating with the enforcer leader while Odile read ledger names across open frequency behind her, the man's threats and offers captured clean despite the rain hammering the microphone.

Tomas sat in the corner with his bandaged arm cradled against his chest, watching her the way he always watched her—not waiting for her to speak, just present, the way a wall is present when you lean on it.

"The officials will want all of it," Odile said. "Every page, every recording. Chain of custody, they'll call it. They'll take it into a room and it'll come out the other side lighter."

"I know."

"They'll also want a statement. A formal deposition. You'll give them your account and they'll decide which parts are admissible, which parts are speculation, which parts might embarrass the ministry, and by the time it reaches a courtroom—if it reaches a courtroom—you'll be a witness to a shipping discrepancy." Odile let that sit. "Unless there's already a public record. Unless the full account exists outside their custody before their boats arrive."

"So we broadcast it again."

"We broadcast *you*. Not the documents—they can have the documents, copies anyway; I've got duplicates hidden in three places you don't need to know about. But testimony is different. Testimony has a face and a voice and a name attached." Odile looked at her over the glasses. "That's the part they can't confiscate once it's gone out on the air."

Juniper looked at the recorder on the table. It was nothing special—an old reel-to-reel Odile had kept running since before Juniper was born, it looked like, patched with tape and stubbornness.

"There's a problem," Juniper said. "The name."

"Ravensworth," Odile said. "I know. Tomas told me."

"Not Ravensworth." She heard herself say it and felt the deck tilt slightly, though the river was calm. "That's the name I was born with. It's not the name I've lived under for fifteen years. If I testify as Juniper Ravensworth, I'm testifying as the daughter of the smuggling operation—as the girl who turned state's witness and then disappeared before sentencing. Every defense lawyer in the province will spend a week digging through that trial instead of the ledgers. They'll make it about me. They'll say I sold out my own family once, who's to say I wouldn't sell out the truth for a deal now."

"That's exactly what they'll do," Odile agreed. "So what name do we use?"

Juniper had carried the alias so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in a pocket. Juniper Vale. She'd made it up in a bus station bathroom two provinces away, seventeen years old, hair still wet from crying, choosing a word that meant nothing because nothing couldn't be tracked. Fifteen years of fuel receipts and radio callsigns and med-drop manifests, all signed Vale. She had built a whole life inside that five-letter fence, and the fence had worked. Nothing had gotten in. Nothing had gotten out.

Except everything had gotten out anyway, these last weeks. The compass with her family name engraved on the lid. Daniel's handwriting. Maren's price. The gorge. The man from her father's old crew pointing a rifle at her across the water and saying her real name like a knife going in.

"Vale keeps me safe," she said slowly. "But safe isn't the point anymore, is it. That's what Maren asked me. She didn't want my promise to publish. Anyone can publish. She wanted to know if I'd stand behind it when it cost me something. Because it always costs something, and everyone before me had paid in secret and the secrets died with them."

Tomas shifted in his chair. He didn't say anything. He'd learned that about her—that she needed silences left open like doors.

"If I go in as Vale," Juniper said, "and the defense digs up Ravensworth—which they will, within a week, because that enforcer leader knows and he'll trade my name for his own skin—then the story becomes *witness lied about identity*. The ledgers become tainted. Daniel becomes tainted. Everything becomes about the woman who hid."

"And if you go in as Ravensworth," Odile said, "the story becomes *the smuggler's daughter came back to bury them*. Which is—" she smiled thinly, "—actually a better story. But it has to be yours first. It can't be theirs. If they find it, they own it. If you say it, you own it."

Juniper walked to the porthole. Outside, the river ran high and brown and endless, carrying the whole drowned country toward the sea. Somewhere upriver, in a depot or a boat or a shallow grave, was her brother—or wasn't. They hadn't found him yet. The exchange had collapsed into chaos, the enforcers had scattered, and the holding site had been empty except for a cold stove and boot prints heading toward the water. Alive, probably. Probably. She had learned to live on *probably* the way other people lived on bread.

Fifteen years ago she had stood in a courtroom and said her family's name out loud and then run from it before the echo faded. She had told herself the running was survival. Some of it was. But some of it—and she could finally admit this, here, in the smell of mildew and machine oil with the rain finally gone—some of it was shame. Shame that her blood had built its fortune moving contraband through these same rivers, poisoning nothing worse than the law until the consortium came along and showed the family how it was really done. Shame that she'd testified not only for justice but to cut herself free of them, and then discovered that cutting yourself free of a family leaves a wound shaped like a family.

Daniel had stayed. Daniel had tried to hold the name together, and when the legitimate world wouldn't have him—smuggler's son, no references, no chances—he'd taken the survey job nobody else wanted, deep interior, bad pay, and he'd done it honestly. He'd found the pipeline spur. He'd written it all down in his meticulous hand. He'd paid for the name she'd abandoned.

The least she could do was carry it back into the light.

* * *

They set up the recorder on the chart table because it was the driest surface and the most honest one—scratched varnish, coffee rings, the ghost lines of old charts still visible beneath. Odile checked the levels twice. Tomas moved his chair closer, not to be in the recording but to be in the room.

"When you're ready," Odile said. "We're live to relay in thirty seconds. Once I key it, there's no taking it back. The consortium monitors this frequency. So does half the province."

"I know."

"One more thing." Odile's voice softened by a degree, which for her was roughly equivalent to an embrace. "You don't have to tell all of it. A statement naming the ledgers' contents and your role as finder would serve."

"I'm not giving a statement." Juniper sat down in front of the microphone. It was dented, old, the foam cover gone. "I'm giving testimony. There's a difference. A statement is what you give police. Testimony is what you swear to."

"And the name?"

Juniper closed her eyes. She thought of her father's face, the last time she'd seen it across a courtroom aisle—not angry, which would have been easier, but disappointed, the way you're disappointed by weather. She thought of the compass in her jacket pocket, the needle finding north no matter how the case was shaken. She thought of the villages along the river, the dead fish, the elder who had pressed documents into her hands with fingers like tree roots and said, *You're the first one who came back.*

"My name is Juniper Elise Ravensworth," she said. "Daughter of Aldous Ravensworth. Sister of Daniel Ravensworth. Former witness for the crown in the matter of the Ravensworth smuggling operation, fifteen years past. And I'm here to finish what I started."

Odile keyed the transmitter.

* * *

The red light came on and Juniper spoke.

She spoke for fifty-one minutes. She started with the supply drop—the coordinates, the fog, the glint on the ridge that wasn't survey equipment—and she did not skip the parts that made her look foolish or frightened. She described the camp: gear laid out neat as a shop display, mud unbroken, the compass with RAVENSWORTH engraved inside the lid. She named the fake rescue coordinators and described their shoes, their questions, the way they smiled wrong. She gave dates. She gave the checkpoint location and the faces of the men who photographed passengers instead of searching for the lost. She told the village's story—the signatures bought, the complaints silenced, the fish floating belly-up for forty kilometers—and she named the elder who had trusted her, because names given freely protect; it was only names taken by force that endangered.

She told them about the ledgers. She read the six names on the transfer order aloud, slowly, twice each, and spelled the surname of the deputy minister whose signature sat at the bottom like a slug on a leaf. She described the holding depot: the bolted rooms, the cooking pots sized for a dozen men, the calendar on the wall marked in a hand she'd known since childhood.

And then she told the rest. The part that wasn't evidence. The part that was just hers.

"Fifteen years ago I testified against my own family," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she was grateful and ashamed of being grateful. "I did it because it was right, and I ran afterward because it was easier, and I have spent fifteen years telling myself those were the same act. They weren't. Justice that ends in hiding isn't finished—it's just paused. I hid under another name. I flew medicine into flood country and I told myself that was enough, that miles could substitute for years, that a person could outrun a name the way you outrun weather. You can't. Weather catches you. It caught me. And what I learned, out there, is that the consortium that poisoned this river learned everything it knows from operations like my family's—except they bought better lawyers and buried better reports. My father would have admired them. That's the ugliest sentence I know how to say, and I'm saying it on the record because the record should be ugly where the truth is ugly."

She paused. In the corner, Tomas had gone very still.

"The survey team was led by my brother. Daniel Ravensworth. He found the illegal pipeline spur. He documented contamination that the consortium's own internal reports confirm—I've read them; they're among the materials I'm surrendering tonight. Six people did not vanish into the monsoon. They were taken. There is a difference between lost and disappeared, and this government has spent years teaching this river the second word, and I am done letting it."

She leaned toward the microphone.

"My name is Juniper Ravensworth. I am not hiding anymore. Come find me. I'll be the one standing in the light."

Odile killed the transmission.

For a moment nobody moved. The reel spun down with a soft whirring sigh, and the river ticked against the hull, and somewhere far downstream a night bird called across the water.

"Well," Odile said finally, taking off her glasses and polishing them on her shirt hem, which Juniper had learned was what she did instead of crying. "That's out."

"Yes."

"They'll come for you. Not today—today they'll come with handcuffs shaped like protection, witnesses' security detail, all very courteous. But the consortium's lawyers will be drafting papers tonight, and some of the men in those ledgers still have guns."

"I know."

"You could still take the boat. Tomas's boat. Downriver, past the delta, out. By morning you'd be a rumor instead of a target."

Juniper looked at Tomas. He met her eyes and shook his head once—not *no, don't go*, but *no, she won't*. He knew. Somewhere between the fever and the gorge, he'd learned to read her the way pilots read sky.

"No," she said. "I spent fifteen years being a rumor. It's overrated."

* * *

The official boats arrived ninety minutes later, searchlights sweeping the water long before the hulls appeared, because bureaucracy loves announcing itself. Juniper stood on the deck with the ledgers stacked at her feet and her brother's field notes in a waterproof bag against her chest, and she watched the lights come, and she did not calculate escape routes. It was the strangest feeling of her adult life—standing still while something approached, letting it come.

The lead officer came up the ladder with two aides and a clipboard and the expression of a man who had been briefed on the ride over and disliked everything in the briefing.

"Juniper Vale?" he said.

"Ravensworth," she said. "Juniper Ravensworth. You'll want to write that down. Everyone else in the province has heard it by now."

He blinked, glanced at his aide, who was already scribbling, and Juniper almost laughed—at the absurdity of it, how fifteen years of invisibility ended not with thunder but with a clerk's pen scratching on a wet clipboard.

"These materials," the officer began. "You understand they'll need to be taken into custody—"

"Copies," Odile said pleasantly, appearing at Juniper's shoulder with the tarp-wrapped stack. "You're welcome to the originals. The broadcast went out on open frequency two hours ago, and duplicate sets are already beyond your reach. So let's skip the part where we negotiate what happens to them, and move directly to the part where you tell me whether your department intends to investigate the deputy minister, or whether I should schedule another broadcast for tomorrow evening."

The officer looked at Odile the way men look at weather systems they've been warned about.

Juniper picked up the top ledger and held it out to him. Her hand was steady. Her name was in the air, in a thousand receivers across the province, in kitchens and dock shanties and the barracks of men who would soon be deciding whose orders to follow. It could never be put back. She felt lighter than she had since she was seventeen years old.

"Careful," she told him. "It's heavier than it looks."

He took it. And upriver, faint under the engine noise, a single flare rose into the clearing sky—red, then green, then red again. Three colors. A signal.

Tomas was suddenly beside her, binoculars up. "That's not official," he said quietly. "That's river code. Maren's people use it."

"What does it mean?"

He lowered the binoculars. His face, in the flare-light, held something she hadn't seen on it before—something dangerously close to hope.

"It means somebody's alive on the water," he said. "And they're asking for *Ravensworth* by name."
