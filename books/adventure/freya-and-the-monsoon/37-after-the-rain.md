## 37. After the Rain

The monsoon broke on a Tuesday, the way it always did—suddenly, like a door slamming shut somewhere over the mountains. One morning the rain was a wall; the next it was a gray drizzle thinning to nothing, and the sky showed blue for the first time in weeks, so clean and enormous it hurt to look at.

Tomas stood on the deck of Odile's floating station and watched the river settle into its dry-season shape. The water had dropped a full meter overnight. Mud banks emerged along the shores like bones surfacing, and everywhere there was debris—snapped branches, a drowned canoe, half a roof from some village upstream. The flood's leavings. The season's receipts.

Behind him, inside the station, Odile was still broadcasting. She'd barely stopped in six days. The ledger names went out on open frequency every four hours, and every time she read them she added something new: an arrest confirmed here, a resignation there, a provincial minister who'd suddenly remembered he had family in the capital and needed to attend to them indefinitely.

"They can't jam us," she'd said the first night, hammering the transmitter with her fist as if daring it to fail. "Not without blowing up a civilian relay in front of witnesses. They had their chance to be quiet about this. That window's closed."

Tomas had heard that kind of certainty before—from himself, once, twenty years ago, sitting at a government desk with a contamination report in his hands and a superior telling him to lose it. He'd been certain then too. Certainty hadn't saved his career. It hadn't saved anything except, maybe, the part of him that could still look in a mirror.

He rubbed the healing wound at his shoulder. The fever was gone, but the weakness lingered in his hands, a fine tremor that made coffee cups chatter against saucers. Juniper noticed everything and said nothing, which he'd come to understand was her way of being kind.

The officials came three days after the rain stopped.

* * *

They arrived by helicopter, which struck Tomas as a small obscenity—the same machine that had spent the whole monsoon grounded now descending on their little floating island of truth, rotors flattening the water, two men in pressed khakis stepping out onto Odile's deck with expressions of grave concern that had been issued to them by a department.

Odile met them with a clipboard and a smile like a drawn blade.

Tomas hung back near the rail, watching. He knew these men. Not their faces—their type. He'd been adjacent to them his whole career, the ones who arrived after the damage was done to manage the narrative, to thank everyone for their cooperation, to promise that a thorough investigation would leave no stone unturned while quietly pocketing all the stones.

The taller one did most of the talking. "Provincial Oversight Authority," he said, showing credentials that were definitely real and possibly relevant. "We're here to secure the evidence."

"Secure it," Odile repeated. "That's an interesting word choice."

"Preserve it. For the investigation."

"Then you'll be pleased to know copies exist in four locations on three continents, and the originals are already in the hands of counsel." She handed him a list of names—journalists, lawyers, a judge or two who apparently hadn't been bought yet. Tomas watched the man read it and saw the exact moment his shoulders dropped a quarter inch. These were not names you could make disappear. These were names that would notice.

"We'll need statements," the man said. "From everyone involved."

"You'll get statements," Odile agreed. "On the record. On camera. I've scheduled them."

The shorter official glanced at Tomas. "And you are?"

"Tomas Reyes." He let the name sit there. "Formerly of the Environmental Inspection Bureau. You may remember my reports. Several of them were buried under your predecessor's signature."

Something flickered across the man's face—recognition, discomfort, the particular expression of a bureaucrat meeting a ghost from a filing cabinet. "We'll want your account as well."

"You'll have it. In writing. Notarized." He paused. "I've learned to keep receipts."

They took statements that afternoon, sitting in Odile's cramped cabin with the camera running because Odile insisted and no one had the appetite to fight her. Juniper gave hers last, and Tomas watched her do it—watched her sit straight-backed in front of the lens, hands folded, voice level, and tell the whole thing start to finish. The supply drop. The empty camp. The compass. The checkpoint. The ledgers. The exchange. Her brother.

She used her real name.

He'd heard her say it before, in the first raw hours after Daniel's rescue, but hearing it again, spoken calmly, officially, into a record that would exist forever—it landed differently. *Juniper Ravensworth.* A name she'd buried for fifteen years, dug up and set out in the light like laundry after rain.

When it was done, the officials thanked everyone, promised follow-up, and flew away. Odile watched the helicopter shrink to a speck and spat over the rail.

"Half of those arrests will evaporate," she said. "Watch. The mid-level men will hang. The ones who signed the transfer order will get 'administrative leave.' The consortium will reorganize under a new name by Christmas and be back in the concessions by spring."

"I know," Tomas said.

"And you're not angry?"

He thought about it honestly, the way he'd learned to think during the long slow years after his firing—turning things over, testing their weight. "I'm angry," he said finally. "But I'm also old enough to know what a win looks like. And this is one. An ugly, partial, leaking-hull kind of win. But the pipeline spur is shuttered. The story is out. There are forty thousand people between here and the coast who heard those names read aloud, and stories like that don't die. They wait."

Odile snorted, but she didn't disagree.

* * *

The arrests began the following week, and they were exactly as imperfect as Odile predicted.

Two of the enforcers were taken at a river checkpoint, caught flat-footed trying to move equipment upriver before the waterways closed to their traffic. Their leader—the man from Juniper's past, the one whose face had gone gray when the broadcast started reading names—was found three days later in a flophouse in the port district, alone, abandoned by paymasters who had scrubbed every trace of him from their books. He didn't run. Tomas understood why when he heard it: a man like that knows exactly how much his employers value him, and knows that running just extends the accounting.

The mid-level men scattered. Two resigned. One fled across the border with a suitcase of cash that would buy him a comfortable decade of looking over his shoulder. The provincial minister with the suddenly urgent family business retained a very good lawyer and gave a very long statement in which the words *I was unaware* appeared eleven times.

But the pipeline spur stayed shuttered. Engineers in unmarked vehicles came upriver and welded the valves closed, and this time there were cameras—not government cameras, but Odile's, and a wire-service photographer who'd paddled in on a borrowed canoe because the story wouldn't let him go. The images went everywhere. Rust-colored water. Dead fish stacked on banks. Children from the quiet village holding bottles of what came out of their taps.

The consortium issued a statement expressing deep concern and announcing an internal review. The stock dropped fourteen percent in a day. Tomas followed the numbers on Odile's battered radio with the grim satisfaction of a man watching a levee crack.

"It won't drown them," Juniper said one evening. They were sitting on the deck, sharing the last of the real coffee Odile hoarded like ammunition. Downriver, the evening light turned the water to hammered copper. "Companies like that. They'll shed skin and grow back."

"Probably," Tomas agreed. "But they'll grow back smaller. And scared. And every concession they apply for from now on, there'll be someone standing in the room holding a copy of those ledgers. That's not nothing. That's actually most of what change ever is—making the next fight easier than the last one."

She looked at him sideways. "You sound like a man who's made peace with losing most of his battles."

"I've made peace with fighting them anyway. Different thing." He turned the cup in his hands. "My father used to say justice is like bailing a boat. You never finish. You just decide each day whether the water's winning."

"That's bleak."

"He was a fisherman. We're a bleak people." He smiled, and she smiled back, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the silence was the comfortable kind—the kind two people earn by having bailed water together through a night storm, by having each other's lives in their hands and handing them back intact.

There was more between them than that. He wasn't fool enough to name it yet, and he suspected she wasn't either. Some things needed dry ground and time and a person who'd finished becoming whoever they were going to be. But it was there, steady as ballast, and both of them knew it.

* * *

Daniel left on a Thursday.

Tomas watched the goodbye from a distance, because some things weren't his to stand close to. Brother and sister on the muddy bank, the repaired Cessna gleaming behind them like a promise. They'd had three weeks together and had used them the way damaged people use reunion—carefully, circling the big things, talking about small ones. Engine hours. River levels. Whether their mother's old house still stood.

Tomas had seen Juniper cry exactly once, and it wasn't during the ambush or the fever watch or the hostage chaos. It was the evening Daniel told her, haltingly, that he'd kept notes hidden in his boot for five months because he'd believed—without evidence, without reason—that someday someone would come, and that when he'd imagined who, lately, he'd imagined her.

She'd turned away fast, but not fast enough. Some things you couldn't outrun at fifteen years per hour.

Now Daniel shook Tomas's hand with a grip that meant it. "You kept her alive."

"She kept me alive. I mostly leaked on things."

"A family talent, apparently." Daniel almost smiled. Then, quieter: "Take care of the river people's route. Maren's people will need passage out until the heat dies down. She'd want—" He stopped, jaw working. Maren was still in custody, held on manufactured charges of harboring fugitives, and her case was the bitterest entry on the ledger of imperfect outcomes. Odile was on it. Lawyers were on it. It wasn't enough yet, but it was something.

"We'll get her out," Tomas said. "Whatever it costs."

Daniel nodded once, climbed into the plane, and was gone—engine drone fading north, carrying a man home to a life that had a hole in it shaped like fifteen years.

Juniper stood on the bank a long time after the sound faded. Tomas walked out and stood beside her without speaking, because he'd learned that about her too: she didn't need words filling the space. She needed someone willing to share it.

"He asked me to fly with him," she said eventually. "North. Start over somewhere the mud doesn't know our name."

"What did you say?"

"I said I have a flight route." She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Fifteen years I built a life out of leaving places. Turns out the trick was finding one worth staying in." She looked at him then, briefly, and looked away, and Tomas felt the ballast shift.

* * *

His decision came to him on a Sunday, the way important things often did—quietly, while he was doing something mundane.

He was helping Odile restock the station's fuel drums, hauling cans across the deck, when he found it: a child's drawing, wedged behind a crate where it must have slipped months ago. Crayon on butcher paper. A stick-figure girl holding hands with a stick-figure man on a stick-figure boat, a sun in the corner with rays like a starburst, and beneath it, in wobbly letters: *PAPA COME HOME SOON.*

Lucía. Eight years old when she'd drawn it. Ten now.

He sat down right there on the deck with the paper in his hands and forgot the fuel drums entirely.

He'd sent money when he could. Letters, at first, then only money, because letters invited questions and questions invited danger—he'd been a fired inspector with enemies by then, a man whose name got quieter every year, and he'd convinced himself that distance was protection. That she was better off with a father who was a monthly envelope than a father who was a target.

Sitting in the sun with a crayon sunburst in his hands, he finally admitted what he'd known for years: that was cowardice wearing sacrifice's clothes. His enemies had never cared about him enough to find his daughter. Only he had cared about that—cared about it so much he'd made it a wall and lived behind it.

"Odile," he said that evening. "Where's the nearest place with a telegraph line?"

She looked up from her logbook. "Villa Esperanza. Two days downriver, if the water's kind." Her eyes narrowed, reading him the way she read everything. "You're leaving."

"I'm going to find my daughter."

Something softened in her face, quick and rare, like sun through storm cloud. "Good," she said. "About time. I've watched you look at that river like it owed you something for six weeks. Rivers don't owe anybody. But little girls do, sometimes, and they collect with interest."

He laughed—a real laugh, surprising him—and helped her tie off the last drum.

* * *

He told Juniper at dawn, on the bank, while mist stood on the water in white columns.

She took it better than he expected, which is to say she took it badly in the way he'd come to recognize as her version of grace: arms crossed, gaze fixed on the middle distance, jaw tight, saying "good" in a voice that meant the opposite.

"The waterways are open another month," she said. "After that, nothing moves until next season."

"I know. I'm taking the first boat out tomorrow."

"You could wait. Fly out with me—I'm running supplies to Villa Esperanza Thursday anyway. It's a smoother trip."

He looked at her. She was studying the river very intently, as though it might confess something.

"Are you offering me a ride, Ravensworth?"

"I'm offering efficient transport. Don't make it strange."

He grinned and let it stay strange for a moment longer than necessary, watching color climb her neck. Then he said, seriously: "I'll take the boat. I want to arrive the way I left—slow, and honest about it. But—" He hesitated. Fifteen years of practice at leaving had taught him exits; it had never taught him arrivals. "Come find me. When the season turns. If you want. Lucía should know there are people in the world who do the right thing at cost. God knows her father took long enough to become one."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "You'll be terrible at it. Fatherhood. You'll overexplain everything and bail water nobody asked you to bail."

"Probably."

"She'll love you anyway. That's the whole rotten miracle of kids." She stuck out her hand, formal as a treaty, and he shook it, and then she pulled him into a brief fierce hug that smelled of engine oil and river water, and was over before he could ruin it.

"Villa Esperanza," she said, stepping back, eyes bright and dry. "Thursday. I'll bring the coffee."

* * *

The boat left at first light.

Tomas stood at the stern as the floating station shrank behind him—Odile's antenna against the brightening sky, Juniper a small figure on the bank beside her plane, one hand raised. He raised his own and held it up until the bend took them all away.

The river ran calm and low, the monsoon's fury spent, carrying its season of secrets toward the sea. Somewhere upstream, engineers were welding valves shut. Somewhere in a cell, Maren waited for the world to remember her. Somewhere north, a man was learning to live in a house with his sister's name in it again. And somewhere south, in a town he'd avoided for ten years, a girl was growing up without him, drawing pictures of boats.

He put his hand in his pocket and touched the crayon drawing, folded soft as cloth now from handling.

The current carried him toward her, and for the first time in as long as he could remember, Tomas Reyes was not running from anything at all.

He was only going home—slowly, by water, the honest way—and the miles ahead felt less like distance than like a countdown.
