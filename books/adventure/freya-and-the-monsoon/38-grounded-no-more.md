## 38. Grounded No More

The mechanic's name was Ferro, and he smelled of kerosene and old cigarettes, and when he pulled the tarp off the Cessna three weeks after the monsoon broke, he made a sound in his throat like a man greeting a wounded animal.

"She'll fly," he said. "Not pretty. But she'll fly."

Juniper ran her hand along the patched landing gear, the new strut gleaming where the floodwater had crumpled the old one. The fuselage still carried a scar along the port side where a branch had hammered it during the storm—she'd have to look at that scar every time she preflighted, and she found she didn't mind. Planes earned their scars honestly. So did people.

"Take her up slow," Ferro said. "She'll tell you what she needs."

They always did. That was the thing about machines and rivers and brothers: everything told you what it needed if you were quiet enough to listen.

* * *

Daniel stood at the edge of the strip with his hands in his pockets, watching her work the way he'd watched her work since they were children—like she was doing something slightly dangerous and he was half hoping, half dreading, that she'd pull it off.

He was thinner than the man in her memory. The depot had done that, and the weeks before it, and whatever else they'd done to him upriver that he still hadn't finished telling her. There was a new hesitancy in him, a pause before he spoke, as if he'd gotten used to measuring his words for men who might use them against him.

"You don't have to come with me," she said, not looking up from the fuel cap. "I can drop you anywhere you want. Coast. Capital. A bus station in the middle of nowhere. I'm flexible."

"I know."

"So?"

"So." He came closer, scuffed his boot on the gravel. "I've been thinking about what Maren said. About how hiding is just another way of letting them win."

"Maren says a lot of things. Half of them are threats."

"The other half are true." He looked at the plane, then at her. "You're filing a flight plan. An actual one. With your actual name on it."

"It's on the registration now anyway. Odile broadcast it twice." Juniper screwed the cap down tight. "Hard to be invisible when your face has been on every frequency between here and the capital."

"You could change it again. You've done it before."

The words landed the way he'd probably meant them to—not cruel, just honest, the way Daniel had always been honest even when honesty cost them both. She let the silence sit for a moment, because she'd learned something on the river about silences: they only hurt if you tried to fill them too fast.

"Yeah," she said finally. "I could. And then in fifteen years there'd be another compass with our name on it turning up in the mud somewhere, and some other pilot would find it, and none of this would have meant anything." She wiped her hands on her trousers. "I'm tired of meaning nothing, Danny. Turns out it's exhausting work, being nobody. More exhausting than being somebody ever was."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, like it had escaped before he could check it. "Fifteen years of practice and you make it sound easy."

"It was never easy. That was the lie I told myself to keep doing it."

* * *

The flight office at Meridian Strip was a converted shipping container with a desk, a radio, and a clerk named Pell who had worked there long enough to remember when Juniper Ravensworth's family name had been worth something and worth nothing, depending on which year you asked.

She'd rehearsed it on the walk over. Not the words—the words were simple—but the feeling. The moment when the pen touched the form and the name went down in ink that couldn't be taken back.

Pell slid the flight plan across the desk. DESTINATION, it said. PILOT, it said. And under PILOT, a blank line waiting.

She wrote *Juniper Ravensworth* in block letters, pressing hard enough to emboss the carbon copy underneath.

Pell read it. Looked up. His face did something complicated and settled on professional neutrality. "First time seeing that name on my books," he said.

"It'll be on more of them."

"Rough country for names like that, historically speaking."

"Historically speaking," Juniper agreed, "the country's rough for everybody who isn't holding the ledger. That's changing." She signed the second line. "Or it's starting to. Ask me again in a year."

He stamped the form without further comment, but as she turned to go he said, quietly, "My sister lives out past the flooded districts. Water's been tasting wrong for two years. Nobody would print why." He tapped the stamp. "Good luck, Ms. Ravensworth."

Outside, the morning was rinsed clean, post-monsoon blue stacked high and cloudless, the kind of sky she'd spent fifteen years treating as an exit. Today it just looked like weather. Good flying weather. It was strange, how much smaller the sky got when you stopped using it to run.

* * *

They took off at mid-morning, Daniel in the right seat with the checklist on his knees, reading items back to her in the flat cadence of a man who'd spent six weeks memorizing the sound of his own voice so no one could take it away.

"Fuel pressure green. Flaps set. Trim—" He paused. "You always fly nose-heavy like this?"

"Always have. You always nag like this?"

"Always have." But he was smiling, and the smile cracked something open in the cockpit, some sealed compartment she hadn't known was pressurized, and for a few seconds neither of them said anything while the ground fell away and the flooded country unrolled below them, brown water braiding through green, the whole wounded landscape shining.

"There it is," Daniel said softly. "The river."

She followed it with her eyes. Somewhere down there was the falls where Tomas had taken a bullet's graze and laughed about it afterward, claiming the river owed him. Somewhere down there was Maren's landing, empty now, the guides scattered, the matriarch herself released two days ago in a prisoner transfer that Odile's reporting had made too public to bury. Somewhere down there the rust-colored water was slowly clearing, week by week, since the day the pipeline spur went dark.

"You can see the depot from here," Daniel said. "If you know where to look."

"I know where to look."

"Don't." His voice was steady but his hands weren't; she saw them tighten on his knees. "Don't look at it. Look past it. That's what I did for six weeks. Picked a point on the horizon and flew there in my head every day." He glanced over. "It works. Mostly."

"What was the point?"

"A hill. Big bare hill north of the depot, with a dead tree on top." He almost smiled. "I figured if I ever got out, I'd walk to it first. Just to prove I could pick a direction and go."

"We can detour," Juniper said. "Low pass. You can wave at it."

"That's ridiculous."

"Want me to or not?"

A pause. "...Yes. Obviously yes."

She banked north, dropped to five hundred feet, and gave him his hill. He didn't wave—he just looked, a long look, jaw tight, until the tree slid away behind the wing. Then he exhaled like a man surfacing, and said, "Okay," and nothing else, and that was enough.

* * *

The compass rode on the dash, wedged against the windscreen where it had ridden since the ridge camp, brass casing dulled, the engraving worn soft: *RAVENSWORTH*. Her father had bought four of them once, one for each of them, back when the family business was still something they called *logistics* at dinner and never anything plainer. Hers had been lost—or abandoned—in the years after the trial. This one had been Daniel's. He'd carried it into the interior because, he admitted later, he'd wanted something of the old life with him, something that predated all the choices that had broken them apart.

Now it sat on her instrument panel catching the sun, pointing its faithful needle at a magnetic north that cared nothing for smuggling or testimony or monsoons.

"You should keep it," Daniel had said, the night before, sitting on the tailgate of Ferro's truck. "It was yours before it was mine. Family property."

"It's a compass, Danny. They sell them at every trading post on the river."

"Not this one." He'd turned it over in his hands. "This one knows the way home. Took me twenty years to figure out that's rare."

So it stayed on the dash. When she leveled off at cruise, heading for the coast, for the hearing that Odile had warned her would be part theater and part ambush, she caught herself checking it out of habit—and finding, as always, that it pointed exactly where she was already going.

* * *

They stopped once, at a grass strip outside a village whose name Juniper knew from a medicine run two seasons back. Word traveled faster than planes out here; by the time she shut the engine down, a dozen kids had materialized from the tree line, and an old woman was walking toward them at the unhurried pace of someone who had decided the visitors would wait.

"You're the monsoon bird," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm a bush pilot."

"You're the one who carried the rain's secrets and gave them back to the light." The woman peered up at her, unblinking. "My granddaughter heard it on the radio. The story travels faster than you do."

Juniper opened her mouth to correct the record and found she didn't want to. The story wasn't accurate, exactly. But it was true in the way that mattered, the way stories out here had always been true—compressed, carried, kept alive by mouths that needed them.

"They say you're going to the capital to testify," the woman went on. "In front of the commission."

"I am."

"They will try to make you small. They are good at that. They made a whole river small." The woman pressed something into her hand—a woven band, dyed rust-red at one end fading to clean white at the other. "From the water's color to the water's health. So you remember which direction things move when people push."

Juniper closed her hand around it. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Fly safe. The story needs you alive more than it needs you brave."

Daniel waited until they were airborne again before he said, "Monsoon bird, huh?"

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting. I'm just saying, when we were kids you couldn't even keep a goldfish alive."

"The goldfish died of natural causes."

"The goldfish died of neglect, Juniper. Mom cried."

She laughed—really laughed, the first time in longer than she could calculate, and Daniel grinned at her across the cockpit like no time had passed at all, like the fifteen years were just weather they'd both flown through and come out the other side of, scarred but airborne.

* * *

The coast came up golden in the late afternoon, the capital's sprawl visible far to the south, a gray smudge she'd been avoiding for a decade and a half. Tomorrow there would be the commission, the lawyers, the consortium's remaining men in good suits saying reasonable-sounding things. Tomorrow there would be questions designed to make her doubt her own memory, and officials who wanted the story contained, and a brother beside her who'd insisted on testifying too, over her objection and Odile's and everyone's.

Tonight there was just a coastal airstrip, a rented room with a fan that clicked, and Daniel sitting on the bed across from her, cleaning the mud off his boots with a pocketknife the way their father used to, and the strange domestic peace of two people who had run out of running.

"Nervous?" he asked.

"Terrified."

"Good. Me too." He flicked a clump of dried mud onto the floor. "You know what I kept thinking about, in the depot? Not escape plans. Not the ledgers. I kept thinking about the day you left. Sixteen years old, backpack, and you wouldn't even look at me." He set the knife down. "I thought maybe if I ever saw you again, I'd get to yell at you properly. Fifteen years of yelling saved up."

"Do you want to?"

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Turns out I just wanted you to come back. That's all it ever was. Come back, Junie. Even angry. Even in handcuffs. Just—come back."

She crossed the small room and hugged him, awkwardly, around the shoulders, the way you hug someone when you've forgotten the mechanics and have to improvise. He held on tighter than she expected.

"I'm back," she said into his jacket.

"Yeah," he said. "I noticed. Your flight plan was public information."

She pulled back and swatted his arm, and he laughed, and outside the clicking fan the monsoon season was ending for real now, the last rain moving offshore, the rivers settling into their dry-season shapes.

* * *

She woke before dawn out of habit and lay listening to the coast: gulls, surf, the distant clank of fishing boats. On the nightstand, next to her watch, sat the woven band, rust-red fading to white. In the corner by the window, her flight bag. In the bag, copies of everything—ledgers, maps, Daniel's notes, the recording of the exchange—because Odile had taught her the first rule of daylight: never carry the only copy of anything.

On the dash of the Cessna, waiting for morning, the compass.

She got up and dressed in the dark. There was a version of her, the old version, who would already be at the plane by now, engine warm, no flight plan filed, climbing out over the water before anyone thought to ask her name. She could feel that version like a phantom limb—an itch of escape, a muscle memory of vanishing. It would probably never fully leave. Fifteen years leaves grooves.

But the phantom limb pointed the same direction as the compass now, and the compass pointed south, toward the capital, toward the hearing room where she would stand up in front of strangers and say, *My name is Juniper Ravensworth*, and mean it, and stay standing.

At the strip, the light was just coming up. She walked the plane's length in the gray-gold half-dark, checking surfaces, draining fuel samples, running her palm along the branch scar on the port side the way you'd touch an old wound to confirm it had healed into something you could live with.

Then she climbed in, started the engine, taxied out, and keyed the radio.

"Meridian Approach, Cessna niner-two-seven, Juniper Ravensworth, VFR to the capital, requesting to activate my flight plan."

Static. Then, crisp and indifferent and ordinary as sunrise:

"Cessna niner-two-seven, flight plan activated. Radar contact will be established shortly. Have a good flight."

She rolled onto the runway and pushed the throttle forward, and the ground let go of her, and the coast fell away, and ahead the capital sat in the morning haze with all its lawyers and all its lies and, somewhere inside it, a hearing room with her name on the witness list—her real name, spelled correctly, in official ink.

Behind her, on the dash, the compass needle swung and steadied and pointed the way she was going.

For the first time in fifteen years, Juniper Ravensworth was exactly where she said she'd be, going exactly where she'd declared, visible to anyone who cared to look.

She smiled all the way to the clouds.

And she was still smiling an hour later when, descending through the capital's haze toward the commission building, she keyed the mic to announce herself one final time—and heard, cutting through the frequency, a voice she recognized from a hundred broadcasts, calm and urgent at once: Odile, live on the open channel, telling the city that the hearing had been moved, that the chamber had been changed overnight, and that three of the commissioners scheduled to hear testimony had just resigned.

Someone, it seemed, was still afraid of the light.

Juniper adjusted her heading and flew straight at it.
