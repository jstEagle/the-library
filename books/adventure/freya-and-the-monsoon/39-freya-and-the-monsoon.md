## 39. Freya and the Monsoon

The dry season came the way forgiveness comes—not all at once, and not everywhere, but in patches you could learn to trust.

Juniper noticed it first from the air, three months after the broadcast, when the rivers that had swallowed half the province began to give it back. The floodplains drained brown to green. Sandbars surfaced like bones. Villages that had been rooflines and rumor reappeared whole, and people stood in their doorways looking up whenever her engine droned overhead, because by then they knew the sound the way they knew rain.

She was flying medicine again. That hadn't changed. What had changed was everything underneath it.

* * *

The name started in a village up the Serei branch, a place called Kavet Landing where she'd been dropping insulin and antibiotics since before any of it—the survey team, the compass, the ledgers, the man with his hands around her brother's throat on a muddy bank while a radio tower crackled with the sound of an empire cracking.

An old woman there, one of Maren's river cousins, told Juniper the story while she waited out a squall under the eave of the clinic hut. She spoke slowly, in the way of people who have told stories for sixty years and see no reason to hurry now.

"There was a woman," the old woman said, "before the maps were good. Before your grandmother's grandmother. The rains came wrong that year—too much, too early—and the water took the seed stores and the graves and the low fields, and nobody knew why. And there was a woman who flew." She smiled at Juniper's expression. "Not a plane. A bird-shape. A spirit-shape. It doesn't matter. She flew over the flooded country and she saw what the water was carrying, all the things people had thrown into it and buried beside it and paid to forget. She carried those secrets with her through the whole monsoon. Heavy cargo. Heavier than anyone knew."

"What happened to her?" Juniper asked.

"She gave them back," the old woman said simply. "To the light. That's the hard part of the story. Anyone can carry a secret. Carrying is easy—it's just weight. Giving it back, where everyone can see—that's the part that costs."

"And after?"

"After, the rains came right again. And when the monsoon returns each year, we watch for her. Low pass, engine-sound, wings that don't flap like a bird's should." The old woman looked at Juniper's plane sitting on the strip, rain beading on its struts. "We call her the monsoon bird. When we hear you coming in low through weather, that's what the children shout. *Monsoon bird. Monsoon bird.*" She laughed, a dry rustling sound. "They don't know they're talking about you. Or maybe they do. Children know more than we give them credit for."

Juniper didn't say anything. She looked out at the rain and felt something settle in her chest that she couldn't immediately name, and later, alone in the cockpit, she understood: it was the feeling of being seen accurately, by accident, by people who wanted nothing from her except that she keep flying.

For fifteen years she had built her life so that no one would look closely enough to name her. Now children named her every time she passed overhead, and the name was true, and she found she could bear it. More than bear it.

* * *

She measured her life in miles for fifteen years. Miles between towns. Miles between fuel stops. Miles between the person she'd been and the person she pretended to be. Distance as anesthesia. If you kept moving fast enough, nothing could catch you—not the past, not the family name, not the memory of a courtroom where a nineteen-year-old girl said words that broke her father's empire and scattered her brothers like startled birds.

Now, without deciding to, she'd begun measuring in years.

It happened in small ways. She caught herself saying *last year I dropped supplies here* instead of *I've landed here before.* She kept a calendar in the cockpit, a paper one, and marked off days not to count down to departure but just to mark them. She planted things. That was the strangest one—a row of chili plants behind the hangar at Ferro Strip, watered daily, requiring her to stay. Tomas had teased her about it once, standing there with his sleeves rolled and his scarred forearm still pink where the infection had been, and she'd said, "Shut up," and he'd said, "They're doing well," and that had been the whole conversation, and it had felt like a vow.

Years. You could only count years if you intended to stay for them.

* * *

Daniel came back to the interior in the second month of the dry season, which surprised no one who knew him and everyone who did.

He'd spent the weeks after his release giving statements—hours of them, in fluorescent rooms, to officials whose faces Juniper learned to read the way she read weather. Some of them wanted the truth. Some of them wanted the truth shaped into something harmless. Daniel, meticulous to the last, gave them both versions and kept copies, because he was their father's son even now, even after everything, and their father's sons documented everything.

But the interior pulled at him. Juniper saw it happen. He'd stand on the strip at dusk watching the storm line build over the western hills, and something in his face would go quiet and far away, and finally he said, "The survey wasn't finished. The samples from the upper tributaries were never analyzed. Somebody should finish it."

"Somebody," Juniper said.

"I'm somebody," Daniel said, and that was how it was decided.

They flew the route together sometimes, him in the right seat with his sample vials rattling in a padded case, calling out landmarks the way he used to when they were kids in their father's Cessna and neither of them knew yet what their father was carrying in the crates marked *agricultural equipment.* Those flights were hard and strange and getting less hard. They talked about water quality and landing strips and fuel prices, and once, over the drowned forest near the old holding depot, Daniel said, without preamble, "You did the right thing. Nineteen years old. Testifying against Dad. It cost you everything and you did it anyway, and I hated you for fifteen years, and I was wrong."

Juniper kept her eyes on the horizon. "It cost other people more than it cost me," she said. "That's the part I'm still paying on."

"I know," Daniel said. "Me too." And then, because they were who they were: "Your left mag's running rough. You hear that?"

"I hear it. It's been doing that since the gorge."

"You flew the gorge with a bad mag?"

"I flew the gorge with a lot worse than a bad mag."

He shook his head, but he was smiling, and below them the river ran clean for the first stretch in years, silver-brown and alive, and neither of them said anything else for a long time because some silences are the best conversation two people can have.

* * *

Tomas left in the third month.

There was a bus, eventually, when the roads dried, and then a train, and then a city two thousand kilometers north where a daughter named Ines lived in an apartment above a bakery and had not seen her father since she was nine. He packed one bag. He'd owned almost nothing on the boat—one duffel, a repair kit, a battered government-issue flashlight he refused to throw away—and he owned almost nothing now, and he stood at the edge of Ferro Strip in the early light looking like a man about to attempt the longest flight of his life with no aircraft at all.

"You could write," Juniper said. "When you find her. If it goes well or if it doesn't. Either way."

"I don't write letters," Tomas said. "I bailed water next to you for six hundred kilometers. That's a longer letter than most people ever send."

"That's not a letter, that's a chore."

"It's the same thing, done right." He shouldered the bag. For a moment the old wariness flickered across his face—the look of a man calculating exits—and then it faded, deliberately, the way a man puts down a heavy tool at the end of a shift. "Odile says the hearings start next month. Provincial commission. Open sessions."

"They'll sanitize it," Juniper said. "Half of them will. Maybe most."

"Maybe. But the pipeline spur is shuttered. Voss is in custody. Your friend Rennick—" he said the enforcer leader's name flatly, without heat, "—is singing about everyone above him to shave years off. And Odile's station rebroadcasts the ledger names every Sunday night like a church service." He shrugged. "Empires don't usually fall all at once. They leak. We opened the hull. Water does the rest."

Juniper laughed despite herself. "Did you just use our boat as a metaphor?"

"The boat taught me everything I know." He stuck out his hand; she ignored it and hugged him, brief and hard, and he endured it the way he endured most kindnesses, stiffly, and then returned it. "Keep the compass on the dash," he said into her hair.

"Always."

"And Juniper." He stepped back. His eyes were wet and he made no apology for it. "The day you testified, fifteen years ago—you weren't running from anything. You were running *toward* the truth. You just hadn't arrived yet." He picked up his bag. "You're here now."

Then he walked down the strip toward the road, getting smaller, and Juniper stood in the prop wash of her own idling engine and watched him go until the trees took him, and she did not feel abandoned. She felt like a runway after a successful departure.

* * *

The hearings came and went the way big weather systems come and go—loud, consequential, and somehow leaving the landscape mostly recognizable. Some officials fell. Some retired suddenly to consultancies. One minister resigned via a statement so carefully worded it admitted nothing and confessed everything, and Odile read it aloud on air twice, deadpan, letting its emptiness speak.

Juniper testified. Not secretly this time, not in a courtroom at nineteen with her name changed on the docket for her protection. She sat in a provincial hall with fans turning overhead and gave her account under her real name—her full real name, spoken aloud, first time in fifteen years—and afterward a clerk asked her if she wished certain portions sealed, and she said no, and the word came out of her easily, like a stone finally set down.

Her alias was gone. She'd let it go the way you let go of a boat line—hand over hand, watching it slide into the water, knowing the vessel would hold without it.

Some nights she missed the invisibility. She'd be honest about that. There had been a freedom in being no one—no history, no debts, no face anyone could attach consequences to. But it had been the freedom of a ghost, and ghosts don't plant chilies. Ghosts don't get named by children. Ghosts don't sit across a table from their brother while he grades their engine maintenance and steals rice from their plate exactly the way he did at twelve.

Visibility, it turned out, was just another kind of flying. You were exposed on all sides. You also got somewhere.

* * *

In the sixth month of the new year, she flew the Kavet run at dawn.

The strip was dry and firm, the air glassy-cold at altitude, the kind of morning that made the whole province look forgivable. Her cargo was routine: antibiotics, vaccines, a replacement part for the clinic's solar array, letters from Odile's listeners to river families who'd become minor celebrities of conscience without asking for it. On the dash, held in a clip worn smooth by her thumb, the compass turned its steady needle north. *Ravensworth,* the engraving said. She'd stopped flinching at it sometime around the fifth month.

She came in low over Kavet Landing, the way she always did, banking once over the village because the children expected it and because she had discovered, late in life, that she liked being expected.

Below, small figures spilled from the huts and the schoolhouse, waving, and the sound reached her even over the engine—thin, high, unmistakable:

*Monsoon bird! Monsoon bird!*

She rocked the wings and climbed away into the clean morning, and the old woman's story rose in her unbidden: *a woman who carried the rain's secrets and gave them back to the light.* Somewhere upstream, the tributaries ran clear where Daniel's samples confirmed they should. Somewhere south, a man was learning his daughter's laugh again. Somewhere in a records office, a shuttered pipeline spur gathered dust beside a transfer order that would haunt the consortium for a generation.

And here, at four hundred feet over flooded country gone green again, a woman flew who had spent fifteen years measuring her life in miles and had finally, quietly, without ceremony, begun to measure it in years.

The monsoon would come again. It always came again. But she knew now what the old woman knew—that the rain carries everything, and gives everything back, and the only question worth answering is what you'll be carrying when it finds you.

Juniper Ravensworth leveled out, pointed the nose downriver, and flew on into the light.
