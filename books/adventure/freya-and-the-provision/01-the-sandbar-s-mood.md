## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The Veyra had moods the way old men had opinions—loudly, often, and rarely at convenient hours—and on the fourth morning of the dry season it woke up sulking.

Ulyana Thornbury knew this before he opened his eyes. He knew it from the sound of the water against the hull, which came in a little too soft, a little too even, the way it did when the river was running shallow over its own silt and thinking about rearranging itself overnight. He lay in his bunk for another minute with his eyes closed, listening the way other men checked the weather, and then he got up, put the kettle on, and went out to have a look.

The sandbar that had been sitting politely off his port bow for three days had moved.

Not far. Not dramatically. Just enough—a new tongue of pale gravel reaching downstream like a cat stretching one paw into the current—that if he'd left an hour later, or slept deeper, or been any kind of man who trusted rivers to stay where they were put, he'd have woken to the grinding crunch of his keel plowing through six inches of fresh deposit. As it was, the *Marrow* sat in her pool of deeper water, untouched, and the bar lay there looking innocent about the whole thing.

"Good morning to you too," Ulyana said to the river.

He was forty-one years old and had spent ten of those years on the Veyra, and in that time he had learned exactly two things worth learning. The first was that the river forgave nothing. Not carelessness, not hurry, not sentiment. It took payment in hulls and cargo and occasionally people, and it never gave receipts. The second thing—the more useful one, in his experience—was that nobody upstream cared what you were running from, so long as you paid your tolls and didn't ask them the same question back.

He made tea. He ate the last of the flatbread from Tessel's Landing, toasted over the burner until it stopped being stale and started being merely stubborn. Then he ran through his morning ritual, which was less superstition than maintenance: bilge check (a slow seep, as always, gathering in the low corner by the engine mounts, patient as moss), fuel lines, rudder post, the radio.

The radio got special attention. It always did. He keyed the mic, listened to the carrier hum, checked the antenna wire where it ran up the mast, and set the dial to the channel he kept it on, which was the general traffic band for the upper-middle Veyra. He wasn't much of a talker himself. But a man alone on a river wanted to know who else was out there, and where, and whether they'd seen anything worth avoiding—officials, mostly, though also snags, fires, and the occasional ambitious fisherman who thought a channel marker was a suggestion rather than a promise.

"Traffic check," he said, because saying it aloud was part of the ritual, and rituals were how you stayed sane when your only conversation partner was a river. "Anybody awake?"

Static answered him, friendly and empty. Fine. He liked the static fine. Static meant nobody needed anything from him.

He cast off at first light proper, walking the *Marrow* off the bank with the pole, easy as easing a splinter from skin, and let the current take her around the bend. His cargo today was modest and honest, which suited him: salted fish in wax-sealed barrels, a crate of tools for the trading post at Kellum Bend, and forty sacks of rice that belonged to a widow named Ostrava who paid on time and asked no questions, which in Ulyana's private ledger made her the finest client on the entire river system.

Downriver work was the good work. Downriver you rode the current instead of fighting it, and the Veyra in dry season ran clear and green-brown and reasonably honest about its depths. He stood at the wheel with his tea going cold in its tin cup and read the water the way he'd once read other things, long ago, in another life: the smooth glassy patches that lied about being deep, the standing waves that told the truth about rocks beneath, the way driftwood collected along a seam in the current and marked it as neatly as if someone had drawn it there with chalk.

A kingfisher went past him like a thrown blue stone. He nodded to it. Conversation enough.

* * *

Kellum Bend announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke, drying fish, and the particular sourness of the tannery that nobody in town would admit to locating. Ulyana brought the *Marrow* in against the current with the unhurried precision of a man who had done it a thousand times and intended to do it a thousand more, and tied off at the third mooring post, which was the one with the loose cleat, because the loose cleat meant the harbor master's assistant never bothered checking it.

This was not laziness. This was strategy.

"Thornbury." The harbor master himself appeared, as he always did, within four minutes of any boat tying up, a round man named Pell with a clipboard and the mournful expression of a bloodhound that has been told, repeatedly, that it is not allowed to eat the mail. "You're due for inspection."

"Am I?" Ulyana said pleasantly. He was already unloading Ostrava's rice, moving with the economy of a man with somewhere to be, which was technically true; he had nowhere to be, but he liked the appearance of it. "Didn't hear anything about that."

"Quarterly hull certification."

"My hull's certified."

"It was certified. In spring. It's autumn now, Thornbury. Things happen to hulls in summer."

"Nothing happens to my hull," Ulyana said. "I don't let it."

Pell looked past him at the *Marrow*, and Ulyana watched him do it—watched the man's eyes go to the waterline, to the faint dark stain near the stern where the paint had blistered, and felt the small cold drop in his stomach that he refused to show anywhere on his face. The stain was nothing. A seep. Every old boat had a seep. He patched it monthly with pitch and canvas and it held, mostly, and it was absolutely none of Pell's business.

"That looks wet," Pell said.

"That's the river," Ulyana said. "It does that."

"I could get the inspector down here. He's in town till Thursday."

"He is," Ulyana agreed, "and I'm sure he's very busy, and I'm sure a man of your responsibilities wouldn't want to waste his time on a boat carrying rice and fish. Rice and fish, Pell. You could smell the excitement from here."

Pell sighed the sigh of a man who knew he was being managed and resented it without quite having the energy to fight it. "Next run up, you get that hull looked at. Properly. Or I write it up."

"You're a good man, Pell."

"I'm a tired man."

"Same thing, on this river." Ulyana flipped him a coin—not a bribe, exactly, just the customary mooring fee plus a little extra for the assistant's silence about the third mooring post, a system so old and so mutually understood that calling it corruption would have been like calling the tide theft—and got back to work.

By midday the cargo was delivered, the fish barrels signed for, and Ulyana had acquired, through careful and entirely noncommittal conversation at the trading post, three pieces of intelligence: one, the toll station at Weir Nine had changed shifts and the new man was greedy, so plan to arrive at dawn when greed was sleepy; two, there was a survey team working the headwaters above the gorge, some government outfit with a government budget, buying supplies in quantities that made the traders' eyes shine; and three, rain in the high country, earlier than usual, which the old-timers said meant a short dry season and a mean monsoon.

He filed all three away. That was the job, really—more than poling, more than steering. The job was knowing things slightly before they became problems, and arranging your life so that most problems happened to somebody else.

He bought pitch, canvas, a new bilge pump handle, and—after a moment of genuine internal debate, conducted with the gravity of a state decision—a paper bag of candied ginger. He told himself the ginger was for the long runs. He knew perfectly well it was for the evenings. A man alone on a boat needed one small dishonest pleasure or he'd start talking to the kingfishers, and he was already halfway there.

* * *

That evening, moored below the bend where the current slackened, Ulyana cooked rice and dried fish in the same pan because washing two pans was a tax on solitude he saw no reason to pay, and he ate on deck with his boots up on the rail, watching the light die over the ridgelines to the west. The clouds up there, he noticed, had a bruised look to them. Building. The old-timers were probably right. They usually were, which was one of the many reasons Ulyana avoided conversations with them.

At sundown, per ritual, he checked the radio.

Most nights the band was quiet. A barge captain complaining about fuel prices. The relay station at Harrow Point reading the daily notice in a bored voice. Once, memorably, a long argument between two fishermen about whose net had caught whose anchor, conducted with a fluency of profanity that Ulyana had privately admired as a regional art form.

Tonight there was something else.

"—repeat, we are holding position at the secondary site, grid reference follows, but I want it noted—" A man's voice, youngish, tight in a way that Ulyana recognized without wanting to. Tight the way voices got when a person was scared and trying to make the fear sound like procedure. "—noted that our authorization does not cover what we've found, and we are requesting immediate instruction from—"

Static swallowed the rest. Then the voice came back, thinner, farther:

"—if anyone is receiving, this is Survey Team Corvane, expedition license seven-seven-four, we are—"

And then the static again, but wrong. Not the clean empty static of an open channel. A heavy, churning static, like gravel poured into a bucket, like something deliberately laid across the frequency, and under it—if Ulyana strained, and he hated himself for straining—he thought he heard a second voice, distant, shouting, cut off mid-word.

Then nothing. Clean static. Empty. The channel sat there like a room after everyone has left.

Ulyana sat with his cup halfway to his mouth for a long time.

The river made its small sounds against the hull. A night bird called somewhere up the bank, got no answer, tried again. The bruised clouds over the western ridges had gone purple-black, and the first stars were punching through the gaps in the east.

Survey Team Corvane. He'd heard the name at the trading post that afternoon—the government outfit buying rope and tinned meat like they were provisioning a small war. Seven-seven-four. Above the gorge, the trader had said. Way above. Past Weir Nine, past the answering stations, into the country where the maps went thin and the river went mean, in the dying days of a dry season with rain already building in the headwaters.

His hand was still on the dial. He noticed this and removed it, deliberately, the way you take your fingers off a hot stove.

He keyed the mic instead. "Vessel *Marrow*, Kellum Bend anchorage. Anyone copy that transmission? Survey Team Corvane, channel four." He released the key. Listened.

Static. Then, after a while, the relay station at Harrow Point, bored as ever: "*Marrow*, be advised, no other traffic reporting. Corvane's licensed through the regional office. Suggest you log it and stand by."

Log it and stand by. The oldest words on the river. The words that meant: whatever it is, it isn't yours.

"No copy on anyone else," Ulyana said. "Logging it. *Marrow* out."

He turned the radio down to a murmur and sat there in the dark with his cold tea and his uneaten ginger, doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. The arithmetic went like this: a survey team, twelve men maybe, maybe more, holding position somewhere they weren't authorized to hold it, asking for instructions that hadn't come, and then a channel full of gravel-static and a voice cut off mid-word. And above the gorge. And rain coming early.

And on the other side of the ledger: one leaking boat, one man, one debt to Pell that was about to become official, a monsoon four weeks out at best, and a personal policy—ten years old, load-bearing, tested daily—that other people's trouble was the cheapest commodity on the Veyra because it could be had for free and cost everything to keep.

"Not my problem," Ulyana said aloud.

The river said nothing. The river never did. That was the arrangement between them, and he'd always found it restful: neither of them pretended to care about the other's affairs.

But he left the radio on that night, dialed low, its green eye glowing in the dark of the cabin like a small patient animal waiting to be fed. He told himself it was habit. Ritual. You kept the radio on; that was all; it meant nothing.

He slept badly anyway, and twice in the night he surfaced from half-dreams convinced he'd heard a voice under the static, and both times he lay in the dark listening until the listening itself became unbearable, and both times there was nothing there but the river, and the rain starting, very softly, very far away, on the high country where the maps went thin.

In the morning he would point the *Marrow* downriver, because downriver was the good direction, the honest direction, the direction a sensible invisible man went. He fell asleep repeating this to himself like a charm.

Downriver. Not his problem. Downriver.

The river, which forgave nothing, including lies, kept murmuring against the hull all night, in a tone that suggested it had heard him, and was not impressed.
