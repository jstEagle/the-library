## 2. A Stranger at the Dock

The woman was standing on my dock before I'd finished tying off, which told me two things right away: she'd been waiting a while, and she didn't know enough about the Veyra to know that waiting on my dock was like waiting for rain in dry season—technically possible, historically unwise.

I finished the knot first. A man should always finish the knot. Then I looked up.

She was city-made, no question. Boots too clean for the mud they were standing in, jacket with more pockets than sense, and hair pulled back in a way that said she'd done it that morning expecting it to still be there by evening. The river would fix that about her within a day, if the river got the chance.

"You're Thornbury," she said. Not a question, which I disliked immediately.

"Depends who's asking."

"Freya Ashcombe." She held out a hand. I looked at it. After a moment she put it away, which showed she could learn, at least. "You run cargo upriver."

"I run cargo where the cargo wants going."

"The Veyra headwaters. Past Kessel's Bend. As far as the old mining cut."

That got my attention, though I kept it off my face. Nobody ran past the Bend this late in the season unless they had a death wish or a contract written by someone else holding the pen. "I don't go past the Bend."

"You did last year. And the year before." She reached into one of her many pockets. "I checked the station ledgers at Torv's Landing. Your boat's registered under three different names, but the pilot's description doesn't change. Tall. Left-handed. Ties off with a bowline even when a cleat hitch would do."

I'll admit that stung a little. A man's knots are his own business.

"Ledgers," I said. "You read ledgers."

"I read everything." She produced a folded sheet of paper, glossy, the kind of print that costs money. "This is why I'm here."

I should have walked away then. I want that on the record, wherever records of me end up, which is nowhere, which is how I've arranged things. I should have walked away, because the paper she held out had the particular shine of trouble, and trouble on the Veyra comes upstream faster than barges do.

But I took it. That's the honest truth of it—I took it, and looking back, I think some part of me had already taken it the moment she said *left-handed*, because there is a peculiar loneliness in being known by a stranger, even a little, even wrongly.

It was a satellite photograph. The upper river in false color, blues and greens turned strange, the water rendered as black ribbon through country nobody official had bothered naming in forty years. Someone had marked a point on it in red ink. Coordinates beside it, precise to an ugly degree.

"That's where the survey team was when they stopped answering," she said. "Eleven days ago. My brother is with them. Tomas Ashcombe. Geophysicist." Her voice did something small on his name—not a crack, just a tightening, like a line coming taut. "The company that hired them has stopped asking questions. Officially the expedition is 'delayed.' Unofficially, nobody will send anyone up there during monsoon season, and monsoon season starts soon."

"So wait for it to end."

"The river won't give them eleven days of waiting." She took the photo back, careful with it, the way you handle a thing that's standing in for a person. "I need a boat. You have a boat."

"I have a leaky scow and a schedule that doesn't include suicide."

"How much?"

"No."

"You haven't heard the number."

"Don't need to. The answer's the same in any currency." I stepped past her onto the dock proper, meaning to end the conversation by the simple method of walking away from it, which works on most people. "Try the landing at Sorrel. There's a man there, Doss, runs a flat-bottom with an outboard. He'll take your money and your chances both, and he'll smile doing it."

"Doss's boat draws too much water above the Bend," she said. "I checked that too."

Of course she had.

* * *

She was still there the next morning.

I came down at first light to check my lines—the *Maren*, my boat, named for nobody living—and there she was, sitting on the dock edge with her boots dangling over the water, eating something out of a tin. She'd slept somewhere; there was a bedroll rolled tight against a piling, and her jacket showed the night's dew across the shoulders.

"There's a boarding house," I said, by way of good morning.

"It smelled of fish and opinions."

"And the dock smells of?"

"Fish and honesty." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "I prefer it."

I went about my work. This is a skill of mine, going about my work while someone watches. I checked the lines, checked the bilge—which was worse than yesterday, I'll note, though I noted it privately, the way you note a tooth that's started talking back—and began loading the day's cargo: flour, lamp oil, salt, the ordinary blood of the river towns.

"You're taking on provisions," she observed.

"I'm running freight."

"For a ten-day round trip? That's a lot of salt for Sorrel."

"Sorrel likes salt."

"Nobody likes that much salt." She stood, tucked the photo away in its pocket, and looked at my boat with an expression I recognized because I wear it myself sometimes: the look of a person reading water. Except she wasn't reading water. She was reading my boat, and worse, she seemed to be reading me. "Your hull's sitting low in the stern. You're carrying water, not cargo."

"My stern sits however it pleases."

"How much to fix it properly?"

"Nothing you can afford."

"Try me."

And here is where I made my second mistake of the week, after the mistake of taking the photograph: I named a number. A big one. A number so far beyond reasonable that it should have ended the matter like a door closing. I watched her face while I said it, waiting for the flinch, the arithmetic, the polite retreat.

She didn't blink. "Done."

"That's not—" I stopped. Started again. "That number was meant to be refused."

"I know." Something moved behind her eyes, tired and steady at once. "But I've spent three months being refused by people who wouldn't tell me why. At least you gave me a price. It's the first honest thing anyone's offered me since I landed in this country."

I stood there with a sack of salt in my arms feeling like a man who'd swung at a rope and missed it entirely.

"I haven't agreed," I said.

"You named terms. I accepted them. That's agreement where I come from."

"Where you come from, they also build cities that flood every spring, so forgive me if I don't trust your customs."

That earned me something I hadn't seen yet—a flicker at the corner of her mouth, gone almost before it arrived. "Fair," she said. "Three days. Give me three days to arrange the funds, and if you still refuse me after that, I'll find another way upriver and stop bothering you."

Three days. I said yes because saying yes to three days felt like saying nothing at all. A man can agree to anything with a horizon on it.

* * *

Day one, she was on the dock at dawn. Day two, same. She didn't plead. That was the unnerving part. Most people who want something from you on the Veyra plead, or pay, or threaten, usually in that order. Freya Ashcombe did none of these. She sat on the dock with her charts and her satellite photographs and her tin of food, and she waited the way stone waits, and occasionally she asked me a question about the river—current speeds, the depth of the channel above the Bend—and when I answered, because the answers came out of me before I could stop them, she wrote them down.

"You're keeping notes," I said on the second evening, suspicious.

"I'm a scientist. Notes are how we pray."

"What are you praying for?"

She capped her pen. Looked out at the water going copper with the sunset, at the hills upstream already turning the color of bruise where the clouds were gathering early this year.

"That the river tells me the truth," she said. "Everyone else lies."

I went home that night—it was a bunk aboard the *Maren*, home being a flexible concept—and lay awake listening to the bilge water slosh, which had developed a new note, lower and wetter, like a voice dropping to make a serious point. I'd patched that seam twice in two years. Patching is a promise you make to a hull, and hulls keep score.

I thought about the number I'd named. I thought about what that number would buy: new planking, a proper caulking, the brass fittings I'd been doing without, maybe even a season free of the low-grade dread that travels with every boatman whose vessel is older than his patience. I thought about the fact that my account at Torv's Landing held enough for fuel and not much else, and that the harbormaster there had begun greeting me with a certain smile, the smile of a man who knows your ledger better than you'd like.

I thought about a woman on a dock who read ledgers and knots and stern-lines, and who had looked at my boat—one look, one glance at how she sat in the water—and named my secret out loud.

Carrying water, not cargo.

I resented her for it. I want to be clear about that, because what happened next might otherwise look like something softer. I resented her thoroughly and specifically, the way you resent a person who finds the bruise you've been guarding and presses it gently, almost kindly, just to show you they know it's there. Ten years I'd kept the *Maren* afloat on skill and stubbornness and patch-jobs applied in the dark where no one could see. Ten years of being nobody in particular, and here came a stranger off a mail steamer who'd needed three days to see straight through to the rot.

Day three, I came down at dawn and she was already there, and this time she wasn't alone with her thoughts. She was standing at the dock edge looking down into the water alongside the *Maren*, and when she heard me she didn't turn around.

"She's shipping water," Freya said. "Starboard side, aft of the midship seam. You can see it breathing out—there, look, the slick. Oil and bilge. Every few minutes, a fresh pulse."

I looked. She was right. Of course she was right. In the flat early light the telltale rainbow spread and thinned, spread and thinned, the boat exhaling its sickness into the river for anyone patient enough to watch.

"Monsoon starts soon," she said quietly. "You told me yourself the clouds are early. Whatever you decide, decide it soon, because in a week there won't be a decision—just weather."

I stood on my own dock, on my own planks, beside my own dying boat, and felt the strange sensation of a door closing that I hadn't even known was open. Not her forcing it. Not exactly. The river forcing it, and the ledger, and the hull, and the eleven days of silence upstream where a man named Tomas Ashcombe was or wasn't, and this woman who had crossed half the world to stand on wet planks and wait for me like the answer to a question she'd already asked everywhere else.

"If we do this," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and captain-like, because a man reaches for whatever armor is nearest, "we do it my way. My boat, my rules, my route. You don't touch the tiller, you don't argue with me about water, and when I say turn back, we turn back."

"All reasonable."

"And the money's half now."

"Half now, half at the headwaters." She was already reaching for her pocket, already producing papers, and I realized with a sinking feeling that she'd had them ready since day one—that the three days hadn't been for her benefit at all. They'd been for mine. Time for the stubbornness to drown on its own.

I took the papers. They were good. I checked twice, because I count everything twice, and they were good both times.

"We load tomorrow," I said. "First light. Travel light—if it doesn't float or save a life, leave it."

For the first time in three days, Freya Ashcombe smiled all the way. It changed her face entirely, and I found I didn't trust it one bit more than the rest of her.

"One question," I said, as she turned to go. "Why me? Doss would have taken the job. Half the river would have."

She paused at the top of the dock ramp, silhouetted against the brightening sky, and considered me with those ledger-reading eyes.

"Because everyone else would have said yes," she said. "I needed someone who'd say no first. No means you've actually thought about what's up there."

Then she was gone, up the ramp toward the town, and I stood alone on the dock with a boat full of borrowed time, listening to the bilge breathe, wondering—not for the last time, though I didn't know it yet—exactly what kind of trouble I'd just agreed to carry upstream.
