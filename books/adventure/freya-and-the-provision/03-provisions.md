## 3. Provisions

The list Ulyana Thornbury gave me was written on the back of a fuel invoice, in pencil, in handwriting that slanted downhill like it was trying to escape the page. I still have it. I shouldn't, probably—it's evidence of how badly we misjudged each other at the start—but I keep it folded in the back of my field notebook, and every so often I unfold it and smile at item eleven.

*Item eleven* said: **rope. enough.**

Not a length. Not a grade. Just *enough*, as if rope were a moral quantity and he intended to purchase all of it.

"You'll want to buy your own food," he'd told me that first morning on the dock, not looking at me, looking instead at a patch of his hull the way a man looks at a wound he's decided not to treat. "I'm not a cook. I'm not a host. I'm transport."

"I can cook."

That got me a glance. Brief, assessing, gone again. "Then buy food you like. I eat anything."

It was the first of many things he'd say that sounded like nothing and turned out, later, to mean everything.

* * *

The market at Veyra Landing opened at dawn and by seven was a riot of tarps and shouting and river fish laid out on crushed ice that was already losing its argument with the heat. Dry season's last days. The air had the thick, sweet-rotten smell of a place waiting to be washed. I went through Ulyana's list like a survey, which is to say obsessively, and I learned more about him from that list than I had in three days of watching him pretend I wasn't on his dock.

He'd written *cans × 14* beside the grocer's stall. When I got there, he was already ahead of me, counting.

"One, two—" He stopped. Started over. "One, two—"

"You counted those already," I said.

"I'm counting them again."

"Why?"

"Because last year I bought twelve cans and loaded thirteen, and the extra one was bulging, and I didn't find out until week two, and the smell—" He shook his head as if the memory were a fly. "Fourteen. Counted twice. Fourteen."

I watched him do it. He counted the cans, then he counted them again, then he wrote the number on his list with a little pencil stroke of finality, like a man signing a treaty. And here was the thing I noticed, the thing I filed away the way I imagined he filed things away about me: he wasn't counting to fourteen. He was counting to *enough*. There was a number in his head—a number of days, a number of mouths, a number of things that could go wrong—and fourteen cans sat comfortably inside it with margin to spare.

This was not a man provisioning a rescue. Rescues are optimistic. Rescues assume you find people and bring them back and everything after is someone else's problem. This was a man provisioning for a longer arithmetic. For delay. For the possibility that the river would keep us, or the weather would, or something worse would, and that the only person coming would be himself.

I bought coffee. Good coffee, the expensive vacuum-sealed kind, and when I handed it to him he looked at it like I'd handed him a small animal.

"That's not on the list."

"It's on my list. I'm paying."

"I don't drink coffee."

"Everyone drinks coffee."

"I drink tea." He put the coffee in the boat anyway. I noticed that too. He argued and then he stowed it carefully, high and dry, wrapped in the oilcloth he wrapped everything in, and I thought: *there's a man who has lost cargo before, and remembers exactly how it felt.*

* * *

The boat was called the *Marrow*, which I found out later he hadn't named and didn't particularly like, and which I privately thought was perfect—a vessel that carried things through the body of the country, a little grim, a little essential. She was maybe eleven meters, steel-hulled with a wheelhouse that had been patched so many times the patches had patches, and she smelled of diesel, damp canvas, and the particular mineral coldness of a boat that has spent a decade learning one river.

I catalogued her the way I catalogue everything—glaciologist's habit. You learn a landscape by its small betrayals. The crack in the ice that's wider than it looks. The moraine that shifts. Ulyana's boat was a landscape of small betrayals: a rust bloom above the waterline shaped like a fern, a hawser frayed to its last strands, a bilge pump that he tested every morning by running it for exactly thirty seconds and listening to it with his head tilted, the way you listen to a child's breathing.

And the radio. God, the radio.

He checked it hourly. I timed him. Not roughly—hourly, on the hour, as if the radio were a patient and he were taking its pulse. He'd key the mic, call the landing station, get a crackle and a bored voice, log something in a notebook he kept chained to the console. Weather, mostly. River stage at the upstream gauges. Once, just once, I heard him ask—casual, too casual, the vocal equivalent of the downhill-slanting handwriting—whether there'd been any traffic reported above the Second Bend.

"None," the radio said.

He wrote that down too. *None.* Underlined.

"You're expecting something," I said, from the deck, where I was coiling rope badly on purpose to see if he'd correct me. He did. He always did. He came over and re-coiled it without a word, and I decided I could survive being insufferable if it kept producing this effect.

"I'm not expecting anything. I'm *noting* things."

"Noting that there's no traffic above the Second Bend."

"Absence of traffic is traffic information." He said it flatly, like a proverb he'd paid for. Then he went back to his fuel gauge, and I let it go, because I was learning that Ulyana gave up information the way a clam gives up a pearl: you could have it, but you'd have to pry, and he'd be annoyed the whole time, and he'd pretend afterward the pearl had never existed.

But I'd already worked out the shape of it. Fourteen cans, counted twice. Rope, *enough*. A man who checked the river gauges like a doctor checking vitals and underlined the word *none*. He wasn't outfitting a rescue mission. He was outfitting a *survival*, and he'd done the math on my brother's coordinates and the calendar and the clouds stacking up over the headwaters, and he had concluded—I was almost sure—that we were not coming back the way we went up. He just hadn't said so. He'd bought fourteen cans instead.

I found this, unexpectedly, reassuring. I've never trusted optimists near glaciers. Optimists underestimate the ice. Pessimists with a checklist, though—pessimists with a checklist and a pencil and a habit of counting twice—those are the people who come home.

* * *

It was on the second day of loading that we had our first real conversation, and it happened, as the important ones do, sideways, while our hands were busy and our eyes were on something else.

I was stowing the medical kit I'd brought—proper one, surgical-grade, the kind of kit that announces things about its owner—and he was re-securing the spare propeller, and he said, without any preamble at all:

"You left something."

I looked up. "What?"

"Wherever you were. Before this." He tightened the last bolt. "People who are running *toward* something pack light and forget things. People running *from* something pack like this." He nodded at my kit, at the crates I'd labeled in three languages, at the duplicate of every critical item. "You packed like you're not going back to fetch anything."

The river made a sound against the hull. Somewhere in the market a woman was arguing about the price of plantains with the conviction of a person who has never once lost that argument.

"That's a very precise diagnosis from a man who won't tell me his first name."

"Ulyana *is* my first name."

"Then what's the rest?"

"Rest of it's not for sale." He sat back on his heels and looked at me, and for the first time he looked at me properly—not the dock-side assessment, not the freight calculation, but the way you look at a piece of terrain you've decided you're actually going to have to cross. "Your brother's on that river. You want him back. That's the story you told the harbor master, the story you'll tell anyone who asks. It's even true. But it's not the whole weight, is it. Nobody comes up the Veyra in the last week of dry season carrying a surgical kit and a duplicate of everything unless they're also carrying something that doesn't fit in a crate."

I should have deflected. I'm good at deflecting; I'd had years of practice, and the practice had been expensive. But there was something about the way he said it—no accusation in it, no curiosity either, really. Just a man reading water. He wasn't asking me to confess. He was telling me he'd noted the current, and that he'd adjust his course accordingly, and that neither of us needed to make a drama of it.

"Have you ever testified?" I asked.

He went still. Not dramatically—Ulyana didn't do anything dramatically—but the kind of stillness that in a person else would have been a flinch.

"Once," I said. "Back home. A shipping company, moving contraband through legitimate freight. I was a logistics analyst. I found the pattern in the manifests—glaciology pays badly, but it teaches you to read data, and the data was screaming. I testified. The company folded. Some people went to prison." I wrapped a strap around a crate and cinched it, needing my hands busy. "And some people didn't. And the ones who didn't write letters. And after the third letter I stopped opening them and started packing."

"Where were you going?"

"Anywhere the mail was slow." I almost laughed. "I had a job offer. Research post, good one, the kind of position you spend a decade earning. I deferred it. Then I got the call about Tomas, and I—" I looked up the river, where the water ran brown and muscular between the green walls, toward the coordinates that hadn't answered their radio in eleven days. "I told myself I came for Tomas. And I did. I do. But I'd have found a reason to come up some river or other eventually. Running gets into the body. Like a cold you never fully shake. You feel it in the joints when the weather changes."

Ulyana was quiet for a while. He finished the propeller. He checked the bilge pump—thirty seconds, head tilted—and then he said, to the pump rather than to me:

"I had a boat before this one."

I didn't move. I'd learned by then that Ulyana volunteered things the way the river released logs—suddenly, without warning, and best survived by not grabbing at them.

"Different river. Different name. Different man, near enough." He wiped his hands on a rag that had achieved a texture somewhere between cloth and geology. "There was a debt. Not mine, originally, but debts don't care about original ownership the way people wish they did. I made arrangements. The arrangements required me to become difficult to find. So I found a river that's difficult to find things on, and I've been difficult for ten years." He folded the rag. "Fourteen cans. Counted twice. Now you know why."

"You're provisioning for a man who might have to run again."

"I'm provisioning for a man who *has* to be ready to run again. It's different." He stood, and something in his face closed partway, though not all the way—that was the strange thing, and I didn't understand it until much later. He'd shown me the pearl, and instead of pretending it had never existed, he'd left it sitting there between us, gleaming and inconvenient. "You should know what you've bought. You paid for transport up a river in monsoon season. What you actually bought is a boat skippered by a man with a contingency for every contingency, who counts cans twice because the world has taught him the third count is the one that saves you. If that's not the skipper you want—"

"It's exactly the skipper I want." I said it too fast, and then, because I'm a scientist and precision matters: "Optimists underestimate rivers. I'll take the pessimist with the checklist."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the geological precursor of a smile, the fault line along which a smile might one day occur, and I counted it as a triumph and pretended I hadn't noticed, because I was learning that Ulyana's dignity was load-bearing.

* * *

We finished loading at dusk. The *Marrow* sat low and steady in the brown water, fuel topped up, water tanks full, fourteen cans and their duplicates and my surgical kit and his oilcloth-wrapped everything secured under netting, because Ulyana secured everything under netting, including, I suspected, his feelings.

I sat on the dock with my feet over the edge while he did his evening ritual: radio check, gauge check, a slow walk of the whole deck with a flashlight, touching things. Rail. Cleat. Hatch. As if taking attendance.

"Tomas," I said, when he came back. "My brother. He's the one who taught me to read water. Everyone assumes it was our father, but our father was an accountant who got seasick on ferries. Tomas was the one who dragged me down every river in the county. He'd stand in the bow and call out the sandbars before the boat even felt them. He said the river tells you everything, you just have to believe it's talking." I turned the coffee tin over in my hands. "He's the reason I know how to read water. And he's the reason I'm here. It seemed right that it should be the same reason."

Ulyana stood in the wheelhouse doorway with the flashlight off and the last light behind him, and for a moment he looked less like a man and more like the river's idea of a man—something the current had shaped and kept.

"He'll be calling out sandbars," Ulyana said. "Men like that don't stop. Whatever's happened up there, whatever's gone wrong—men who read water keep reading it. It's the last thing to go."

"You can't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I've noted it." He tapped the notebook chained to the console, and I swear the man was almost, nearly, at the geological precursor of a smile again. "Get some sleep, Ashcombe. We cast off at first light, ahead of the weather. And Freya—"

It was the first time he'd used my first name. He did it so smoothly I almost missed the weight of it.

"Whatever you're running from," he said, "it's downstream. Everything downstream is a problem for the way back. Right now there's just up."

I slept aboard that night, in the narrow bunk that smelled of diesel and canvas, and I lay listening to the river talk against the hull, and I believed it was talking. And in the morning, when the light came up gray and heavy over the headwaters and the first fat drops of the season's first real rain began to stipple the deck, Ulyana stood at the wheel and looked north up the Veyra with an expression I hadn't catalogued yet, and said, quietly, mostly to himself:

"Four days. Maybe five, if the river's kind."

And then, because the river is the river:

"It won't be kind."
