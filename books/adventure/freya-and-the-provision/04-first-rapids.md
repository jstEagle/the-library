## 4. First Rapids

The Veyra changed its mind about being a river somewhere above the third bend.

Below, it had been all patience—wide, brown, unhurried, the kind of water that let a man forget it was going anywhere. But past the old mission landing the valley narrowed, the banks stood up on their hind legs and became hills, and the river remembered it had mountains to get through. It went from a road to an argument.

Ulyana knew every word of that argument by heart. He'd been having it with the upper Veyra for ten years, and he'd won most rounds not by being stronger than the river but by knowing which questions not to ask it. You didn't fight the current at the Narrows of Sable Rock; you flirted with it, gave it your stern at just the right angle, and pretended you hadn't been holding your breath. You didn't trust the green water above Kettle Falls, no matter how smooth it looked, because smooth water up here was just the river thinking.

He was thinking about none of this out loud, obviously. Out loud he said nothing at all, which was his usual conversational setting, and steered the *Maren Vey* through the morning with one hand on the wheel and one eye doing its slow arithmetic of shadows and surface.

Freya Ashcombe sat on the cargo hatch with a mug of tea she'd made herself from his galley without asking, which should have annoyed him more than it did. She had her boots off. Her socks were drying over the exhaust pipe like small surrendered flags.

"You're doing that thing," she said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you look at the water like it owes you money."

"It does."

She smiled into her mug. Three days on the boat now, and she'd already learned the shape of his silences—which ones meant *leave me alone*, which ones meant *I'm working*, and which ones, rare as frost in lowland summer, meant something closer to *stay*. This one, he suspected, she was still cataloguing.

The truth was the river owed him plenty. Repairs to the hull that he'd been promising himself since last season. A new impeller. The radio's antenna held together mostly by habit and electrical tape. And now a passenger paying triple rate to go upriver into country where sensible people were already coming down, because the dry season was dying and everyone with sense could smell the monsoon building in the headwaters like a storm deciding whether to be polite about it.

"Those clouds," Freya said, nodding upstream. "They've been sitting there two days."

"They'll sit there four more. Then they won't."

"That's not reassuring."

"Wasn't trying to reassure you. Was trying to be accurate." He eased the wheel a spoke to port. "Reassurance is for people selling tickets."

"And what are you selling?"

"Passage," Ulyana said. "The rest is extra."

* * *

The first rapids announced themselves the way they always did—a sound like distant applause, then less distant, then not applause at all but something with teeth in it.

Ulyana killed the engine noise down to a mutter and brought the boat in toward the right bank, where the eddy line ran lazy behind a shelf of rock. Above them, maybe six hundred meters of river dropped through a chute between granite shoulders, white water stacked in three long steps, each one folding into foam and then gathering itself again like something getting angrier in stages.

"Sable Steps," he said. "We walk the first one, run the second, and portage around the third if it's running high. Today it's running high."

"We walk it?"

"I walk it. With a line. You sit here and don't touch anything."

Freya looked at the chute for a long moment. "That second drop—the middle one. There's a channel left of center. Two boats wide, maybe."

Ulyana turned and looked at her properly for the first time that morning.

"There is," he said slowly. "Behind the submerged shelf. Which you can't see from here."

"I can see where the water isn't boiling. The boil line tells you where the rock is. And there's a seam—" she pointed, her finger steady, "—running downstream from the big boulder on the left. Green thread through the white. That's deeper water. That's your line, isn't it?"

It was. To the meter, it was his line, the one he'd found the hard way eleven years ago with a cracked propeller and a lesson he'd never told anyone about.

"Where did you say you were from?" he asked.

"City," Freya said, easy as pouring tea. "Big one. Pavements, trams, terrible coffee."

"Mm." He looked back at the rapids. "And where in this city did they keep the whitewater?"

She didn't answer right away. When he glanced over, she was watching the river with an expression he recognized from mirrors—not the face of someone admiring scenery, but the face of someone reading a letter from an old acquaintance and deciding how much of it to believe.

"My father ran barges," she said finally. "A long time ago. Another life."

"You said city."

"I said I'm from a city. Both things are true. Cities have rivers." She pulled her socks off the exhaust pipe and started putting her boots on. "What do you need me to do?"

"Nothing. Sit. Hold nothing. Touch nothing."

"All right." She laced the second boot. "But when you change your mind, change it fast. Rivers don't wait politely."

* * *

The first step went the way it always went: Ulyana in the shallows with a bowline wrapped twice around his waist, walking the *Maren Vey* up the tongue of calmer water along the bank while the main current thundered past close enough to wet his knees. Forty minutes of muscle and swearing. Freya, to her credit, sat exactly where he'd told her and touched exactly nothing, though she watched his footing with an attention that felt less like worry and more like study. Cataloguing him, the way she'd catalogued his cans and his radio checks. He kept waiting for it to feel invasive. It kept not feeling invasive, which was somehow worse.

The second step was the decision point.

He stood at the wheel and looked at the chute, and the chute looked back. High water changed things. High water filled the channel behind the submerged shelf fuller and faster, which was good, but it also pushed the boil line wider, which was bad, and it made the seam harder to hold because the current wanted to sweep him onto the shelf's upstream lip and fold the boat around it like paper.

Line and winch the second step too? Safe. Slow. Half a day lost, and he didn't have half a day; the monsoon had stopped being a rumor overnight, the clouds over the headwaters gone from sitting to stacking.

Or run it. His line. His river. Eleven years of knowing it.

"Freya." He heard himself say it before he'd decided to. "Come stand here. Tell me what you see."

She came forward without hurrying, which he noted. People who hurried toward danger were worse than useless. She stood where he pointed, gripped the rail, and was quiet for a good ten seconds. He counted them.

"Seam's still there," she said. "Thinner than yesterday, I'd guess—more volume pushing everything sideways. Your entry wants to be earlier than you'd normally take it, or the current will put you on the shelf before you can turn down. And the eddy behind the mid-channel boulder—" she frowned. "Is that usually there?"

"No."

"Then it's new, and new means the bar's moved. Which means the channel behind the shelf might be narrower than it looks." She glanced at him. "How narrow can it be and still fit you?"

"Boat needs four meters. Comfortably five."

"Then I'd want to know if it's four or five before I committed." She looked at the water again. "You've got a sounding pole?"

He almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a grunt, which from him was practically applause.

* * *

They ran it together, in the sense that he ran it and she called the water.

He took the *Maren Vey* up above the drop and held her against the current with the engine grumbling, and Freya stood at the bow with the sounding pole, leaning out over the water like she'd been born on some deck somewhere and had merely been misfiled into a city life. She probed the channel entrance. Four meters at the lip. Five once you were past the shelf's shoulder. Enough—but only if the entry came early, exactly as she'd said.

"Early entry," she called back. "Hard to starboard once you're in, before the seam bends. Don't chase the seam—let it come to you."

"Don't tell me how to drive my boat."

"Then stop asking me what I see."

Which was, he had to admit, fair.

He brought the throttle up. The *Maren Vey* swung her nose at the drop, and the river reached up and took her, and for eleven seconds there was no room in Ulyana's head for anything except wheel and water and the sound Freya made—not a scream, nothing so useless, but a short sharp call of *"Now—starboard—NOW"* at precisely the moment his own hands were already turning the wheel, and the boat slid past the shelf's shoulder with a hand's width of green water under the hull instead of granite, and then they were through, into the boil and out of it, into the long calm pool below where the river gathered itself for the third step and forgot about them entirely.

Silence. Engine idle. Water ticking against the hull.

"Well," Freya said. Her knuckles on the rail were white. Her voice was perfectly level. "Your boat handles nicely."

"You called my line better than I would have."

"I know," she said, and then, catching herself, "I mean—I've read about it. River mechanics. For the survey. Glaciology involves hydrology."

"Does it."

"Considerably."

Ulyana looked at her. She looked at the third step, which was running angry and high and which they would portage around, both of them, hauling gear along the trail on the left bank while the river shouted itself hoarse beside them.

"Barges," he said. "Your father."

"A long time ago." She met his eyes then, and he saw her decide something—how much to spend, how much to keep. "Another river. Another name, even. Ashcombe isn't the one I grew up with."

There it was. Not the whole lie, but the seam of it, exposed the way the sounding pole had exposed the shelf: not hidden, exactly, just waiting for someone patient enough to probe. A woman with a borrowed name who read water like scripture and testified—she'd said it herself, that night in the squall was still ahead of them, but he'd seen the flinch when he'd mentioned officials at the last landing—who had reasons to be someone else.

He could have pushed. Ten years ago he'd have pushed, because ten years ago he'd still believed that knowing a thing gave you power over it, and power was safety.

Now he just nodded and turned back to the wheel.

"Portage trail starts at the big pine," he said. "Two trips. Gear first."

"That's it? That's all you're going to say?"

"What do you want me to say?"

"I don't know. Questions. Interrogation. Something."

"Rivers don't interrogate," Ulyana said. "They just remember. Same as me."

* * *

The portage took the afternoon. The third step was running too high to argue with, so they hauled crates and line and the sounding pole along a trail the width of a goat's opinion, and by the time they'd ferried the boat itself through the slack channel on the far side using winch and rollers and a vocabulary Ulyana tried to keep family-friendly and failed, the light had gone the color of weak tea and the clouds upstream had climbed another thousand feet and turned the color of a bruise.

They camped on the shingle below the steps, boat tied with three lines because two wasn't honest anymore. Freya cooked—another thing she did without asking, another thing that should have annoyed him—and they ate beans and rice out of tin plates while the river talked to itself in the dark.

"You could have grounded us today," she said. "On the shelf. If I'd been wrong."

"If you'd been wrong, we'd have had a bad afternoon and a bent propeller."

"If *you'd* been wrong."

"I wasn't going to be wrong."

"No," she agreed, and there was something in her voice he couldn't file anywhere. "You weren't. I watched you take the entry and I thought—he's done this so many times the river's part of his hands now. That's not learning. That's becoming."

Ulyana scraped his plate and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't cost him something, and he was out of practice spending.

But later, lying in the boat with the tarp half over them because the first fat drops of the season had started arriving—early, too early, days early—and listening to the rain tick on canvas and the river keep its own counsel below the steps, he found himself doing the thing he never did: turning the day over, examining it, keeping it.

She'd called his line. Better than he would have. And she'd lied about where she learned it, and the lie had a shape he recognized, because he wore one cut to the same pattern every single day.

*Not my problem*, he told himself, the same words he'd used when the survey team's radio had gone to static. *Her name, her business, her river.*

The rain thickened. Upstream, thunder muttered in the headwaters like something clearing its throat.

In the morning, he knew, the water would be up. The bars would have moved in the night. And somewhere above the fourth bend, in country the rising river was starting to close like a fist, there was a survey camp that had stopped answering, and a woman on his boat who read water like scripture and slept with her boots where she could reach them.

*Not my problem,* he thought again.

The river, which remembered everything, said nothing at all.
