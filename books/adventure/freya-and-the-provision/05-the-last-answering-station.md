## 5. The Last Answering Station

The answering station announced itself an hour before we saw it, which was typical of the Veyra. First came the smell—wet iron and old creosote—then a scatter of rusted drums along the bank like toys dropped by a careless giant, and finally the tower itself, leaning out of the treeline at an angle that suggested it had been trying to sit down for some years and had only recently committed to the idea.

"That's it?" I asked.

Ulyana didn't answer immediately. He was doing the thing he did when approaching anything—a dock, a sandbar, a question—with his whole body angled toward the water and his eyes doing arithmetic I couldn't follow. "That's it," he said finally. "Station Nine. Or what's left of it."

"It looks like it lost an argument with the forest."

"The forest usually wins up here." He eased the throttle back and brought us in along a shelf of gravel that looked, to my untrained eye, exactly like every other shelf of gravel we'd passed in two days. To him it was apparently as legible as a parking space. The hull kissed bottom with a sound like a drawn-out sigh, and he killed the engine. "Twenty minutes," he said. "We look, we don't linger. Weather's turning."

I had learned by now that Ulyana's weather pronouncements were less forecasts than verdicts. I looked upstream. The sky over the headwaters had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom, and the air had that thick, held-breath quality I remembered from fieldwork in other mountains—the atmosphere gathering its argument before making it.

"Twenty minutes," I agreed, and went to find my boots.

* * *

The station had been built, judging by its bones, sometime in the middle of the last century: a timber tower on concrete legs, a long low shed beside it, and a clearing being patiently digested by ferns. A cable still ran from the tower's crown down into the trees, sagging in the middle like a hammock for ghosts. Someone had painted a number on the shed door once—*9*, or possibly *6*; the paint had opinions of its own now.

Ulyana stopped at the edge of the clearing and did not go further.

"You're not coming?"

"I'll check the boat." He said it lightly, but I'd spent three days learning his lightness, and this wasn't it. His eyes were moving across the clearing the way mine moved across a crevasse field—cataloguing everything that could open underfoot. "Radio posts attract visitors. Some of them stay."

I understood then that he meant bodies, or the possibility of them, and that he was offering me the choice to be the one who walked into the building where that possibility lived. It was, I was beginning to realize, a kind of courtesy. Ulyana's courtesies all looked like inconveniences.

"I'll shout if I need you," I said.

"Don't shout." He was already turning back toward the boat. "Whistle. Two short."

"What if I'm too frightened to whistle properly?"

"Then scream. It carries better anyway." And there it was—the ghost of the almost-smile, gone before it could commit to anything—and I climbed the station steps feeling, absurdly, lighter than I had in days.

* * *

Inside, the shed smelled of bat guano and pencil shavings and time. Racks of equipment stood against one wall, stripped of anything portable decades ago; whatever scavengers worked this stretch of river had been thorough about copper wire and casual about everything else. A desk sat under the one window that hadn't been boarded, its surface drifted with leaf litter and the small dry droppings of generations of mice who had clearly considered the answering station their ancestral home.

And on the wall, a log board.

I'd seen them before in old survey stations—slate or painted plywood where operators chalked transmissions received and relayed, date, origin, disposition. Most of it had faded to pale smears. But the last section, lower down where rain couldn't reach through the window, was in grease pencil, and grease pencil is stubborn stuff.

I read it twice before my hands stopped shaking enough to copy it down.

The entries were routine at first. *Relay test, Station 12, weak signal.* *Weather advisory forwarded.* Then, eleven days ago, in a hand I knew—of course I knew it, I'd grown up watching it across the dinner table, slanting left, the *y*'s with their dramatic tails:

**DAY 41 — CAMP RELOCATED. GRID REF FOLLOWS.**

And beneath it, coordinates. I copied them carefully, then checked them against the map folded in my jacket pocket, the one with Tam's official itinerary marked in the expedition office's tidy blue ink.

They didn't match. They didn't come close. The coordinates on the board put the relocated camp twelve kilometers north of anywhere the survey was authorized to be—up a side valley, toward the old mining country, toward terrain so broken the official maps simply gave up and printed contour lines like a nervous sketch.

Below the coordinates, in a different hand—heavier, pressed hard enough to score the board—someone else had written:

**PROVISION MOVED TO CACHE. DO NOT LOG AGAIN.**

And beneath that, smaller, in Tam's hand again, so faint I had to tilt the board to catch the light:

**provision = ?? ask R. NOT SURVEY EQUIP.**

I stood very still in the mouse-smelling dark and let the sentence rearrange itself in my head, because it refused to mean what it plainly said.

My brother had not been lost. My brother had been *moved*. He had radioed a relocation to a place no survey should have gone, and someone aboard—R, whoever R was—had told him to stop writing things down. And Tam, being Tam, had obeyed the letter of the instruction while breaking its spirit, chalking his questions onto a derelict wall in a dead station where nobody would ever read them except, apparently, his sister, six days too late and one river too far away.

"Not survey equipment," I said aloud, just to hear how it sounded. It sounded worse out loud.

* * *

I searched the rest of the shed because standing still had become unbearable. Under the desk, wedged behind a drawer that had long since surrendered to rot, I found the logbook itself—water-swollen, its cover furred with mold, but the final pages legible if I handled them like something newborn. The expedition's own log, or the tail end of it. Entries in several hands. Fuel tallies. Equipment inventories. And then, in the last week, a change: the neat daily entries became erratic, defensive. One page had been torn out—not fallen out; torn, ragged stubs remaining, deliberately. On the final written page, someone had recorded a fuel transfer that made no sense—forty liters of petrol issued to a boat the inventory insisted they didn't have—and beneath it, in the heavy scoring hand again, a single word: **enough**.

I put the logbook inside my jacket, against my ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the light had changed. The bruised sky had finished deciding. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat—not the theatrical summer kind but a low, grinding rumble, tectonic, patient.

Two short whistles sounded from the direction of the boat. Then, after a pause, two more, which I chose to interpret as *hurry up* rather than *come quickly*, though with Ulyana the two signals shared a vocabulary.

* * *

He met me at the gravel shelf, and I watched him read my face the way he read water—one glance, total assessment, no wasted motion.

"You found something."

"How can you possibly—" I started, and gave up. "Yes. Coordinates. They moved camp somewhere the survey never filed. Twelve kilometers north, up the Kess valley." I hesitated over the next part and decided he'd earned it; he'd carried me and my satellite photo and my terrible secrets this far without once asking for receipts. "And a note about something called 'the provision.' Cargo. Someone was moving cargo the team wasn't authorized to move, and my brother noticed, and someone told him to stop noticing."

Ulyana took the notebook from my hand and read the copied entries without expression. That was the thing about him—other men would have performed concern, or performed calm; Ulyana simply declined to perform anything, which somehow made him easier to trust than either.

"Kess valley," he said at last. He said it the way you'd say the name of a person you owed money to.

"You know it?"

"I know it's mining country. Was. The cut closed before the war—whichever war you prefer, there's always one convenient." He handed the notebook back. "I also know it adds four days to your brother's last known position. Four days there, and the monsoon closes the river behind us."

"Then we don't take four days."

He looked at me then, fully, and I braced for the lecture—the sensible-man lecture, the *this is where I turn the boat around* lecture, which I had been half-expecting since the dock. Instead he squinted upstream at the weather, chewing on something.

"Three and a half," he said, "if we run the shallows at first light and I don't sleep, which I wasn't going to anyway." He turned toward the boat, then stopped. "Freya."

It was the first time he'd used my name unprompted. I waited.

"A survey doesn't smuggle ore out of a dead mine because it's having fun. Somebody paid them. Which means somebody wanted it moved quietly, which means somebody will mind if it's found." He said it flatly, a man reading depth soundings. "Your brother may not have been lost. He may have been *discouraged*."

The word landed harder than any gentler one would have. I thought of Tam's handwriting on the board—*ask R. NOT SURVEY EQUIP.*—the small defiant curiosity of him, following the wrong thread because it was interesting. Following it straight into the teeth of whatever the provision was.

"Then we'd better find him before whoever paid does," I said.

Ulyana studied me for a moment longer, and I had the distinct sense of being weighed—my usefulness recalculated, my recklessness reclassified from liability to cargo he'd agreed to deliver. Whatever he concluded, he kept it, as he kept everything, sealed behind those river-gray eyes.

"Get aboard," he said. "Storm's coming, and we're burning daylight arguing with it."

* * *

We ran until the light failed. Ulyana drove the way he did everything—economically, precisely, with occasional bursts of profanity directed at the river in a tone that was less anger than negotiation. I worked the bow line when we shot the shallows and bailed when we didn't, and between duties I sat with the logbook open on my knees under the tarp, reading Tam's entries by flashlight, looking for the man in them.

He was there, in fragments. A joke about the cook's rice. A careful note that the new campsite had "excellent sightlines," which Tam would only write if he wanted someone to know he could see trouble coming. And on the third-to-last page, almost hidden in a margin, in letters so small they might have been meant for no one—or for anyone patient enough to look:

*F—if you ever read this, don't come. Not being noble. Being specific.*

I closed the book.

Of course I came. He'd known I'd come; that was why he'd written it. We Ashcombes had never once obeyed each other's sensible instructions, and it was oddly comforting, sitting in the pitching dark with a stranger at the tiller and a storm assembling itself over the headwaters, to know that my brother understood me well enough to predict my disobedience and love me anyway.

Ahead, lightning stitched the ridgeline, and for half a second the whole upper valley stood lit in negative—black river, white sky—and I saw, or thought I saw, the shape of the country ahead: narrow, steep-walled, leading up into the Kess cut and whatever waited there.

Behind us, the river we'd come by was already beginning to rise.

Ulyana glanced back once, at the water swallowing our wake, and I watched him do the arithmetic again—that constant, silent tallying—and this time I saw the number land on his face, and it wasn't good.

"Four days," he said quietly, to the river more than to me. "Maybe less."

The rain arrived five minutes later, all at once, like a door slamming.
