## 6. Monsoon Warning

The clouds came in the way bad news always did on the Veyra: politely at first, from a long way off, giving you every chance to pretend you hadn't noticed.

Ulyana noticed.

He'd been noticing since dawn, actually—since before dawn, when he'd woken in the grey half-light to a sky over the headwaters that looked like a bruise deciding whether to bloom. By mid-morning the bruise had made up its mind. The clouds had stacked themselves above the northern ridgelines in great slow towers, white at the tops and the color of wet slate underneath, and they were not drifting anywhere. They were *arriving*. That was the difference. Dry-season clouds passed through like travelers. These had come to stay.

He stood at the tiller with one hand on it and the other holding his tin cup of tea gone cold an hour ago, and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for two days.

Four days. Maybe four and a half if the river was kind, and the river had never once been kind to him personally, though he held no grudge about it—it wasn't personal for the river either. Four days to reach the survey site at the head of the middle gorge, find whatever there was to find of Freya Ashcombe's brother, and get back down to the narrows before the monsoon closed the upper river like a fist.

Behind him, Freya sat cross-legged on the cargo hatch with the expedition's logbook page spread out on her knee, reading it for perhaps the fortieth time, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something less troubling if she stared hard enough. She'd found a pencil somewhere and was annotating the margins now. Little neat marks, little tidy question marks. She annotated everything. He'd caught her annotating a bag of rice.

"You're staring at the sky," she said, without looking up.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring at the sky."

"The sky is part of navigation." He drank the cold tea anyway. It tasted like tannin and regret. "On this river, the sky is most of navigation."

She looked up then, followed his gaze north, and he watched her do her own version of the arithmetic—the glaciologist's version, which involved ice and meltwater and things he understood only in the way a man understands a dog by being bitten by it. Her face did something complicated and then settled into calm, which he was learning was her way of being frightened. Calm as armor. He recognized the fit of it. It was his own coat she was wearing.

"Four days?" she said.

"Give or take."

"Give or take what?"

"A day," he admitted. "Possibly my life."

She almost smiled. Almost. It got halfway up and stalled out, the way her smiles tended to when the logbook page was within eyeshot. "Then we should talk about the boat."

And there it was. The conversation he'd been steering around for three days with all the skill he brought to steering around sandbars, snags, and questions about his past—which is to say, skillfully, and only until someone asked directly.

* * *

Here was the thing about the boat: her name was the *Patient Magdalen*, she was forty-one years old, she had been built for a fishery that no longer existed, sold to a timber concern that went bankrupt, patched by three owners of increasing desperation, and finally bought by Ulyana Thornbury nine years ago for a price he still considered the best money he'd ever spent and would die before admitting aloud. She leaked. All old boats leak. A boat that doesn't leak is either new or lying, and the *Magdalen* had never lied to him in her life.

But she was leaking more than usual, and Ulyana knew why, and knowing why was the problem.

It had started as a weep in the garboard strake near the stern—a slow seep, the kind you managed with a bilge pump and a shrug. Then, two days ago, running the shallows below the Answering Station, they'd touched bottom harder than he liked. Not a grounding. A *kiss*. Boats kissed bars all the time; it was practically courtship on the Veyra. But the *Magdalen* was forty-one, and forty-one-year-old hulls did not forgive kisses the way they used to, and when he'd gone into the bilge that night with the lantern, he'd found the weep had become a seep, and the seep had company: a second seam, aft of the first, opening and closing with the flex of the hull like an old man working a sore tooth.

He'd patched it. Of course he'd patched it. He had a kit aboard that could keep a colander afloat through a hurricane, assembled over nine years of dry-season runs and monsoon seasons survived by stubbornness alone. Copper sheeting, marine epoxy of dubious provenance, canvas, tar, a bucket of pitch he'd traded a case of engine oil for in Kessel's Landing and had never once regretted. He'd driven the patch home by lantern light while Freya slept badly in the bow, and it had held, and he'd told himself it would go on holding, because the alternative thought was not one he wished to have aboard.

But patches on a flexing hull were promises, not solutions. And the river was about to start making demands.

So when Freya said *we should talk about the boat*, Ulyana did what any sensible man would do.

"How's the tea situation," he said. "We're low on tea."

"We're not."

"We're low on *good* tea."

"Ulyana." She set down the logbook. She had a way of saying his name that made it sound like a fact rather than a name, and facts, he'd learned, were very difficult to dodge. "I've watched you go into the bilge four times since the station. You come up with your sleeves rolled and your hands black and you say 'all sound' and you don't look at me when you say it. I've spent my life around people who don't look at me when they lie. My whole family does it. It's practically our national sport."

"That's a lot of accusation to hang on a man's sleeves."

"It is," she agreed placidly. "How bad is the hull?"

The river ran quiet beneath them here, a long green reach between riffles, and the clouds stacked up north, and the engine knocked its patient knock, and Ulyana Thornbury stood at his tiller and discovered that he had run out of river.

Because here was the other thing, the thing under the thing: he had not told anyone the true condition of anything in ten years. Not the boat, not his finances, not his name, not the small cold knot of history he carried in his chest where other men carried their hearts. Invisibility was a discipline. You practiced it daily. You said *all sound* and *she'll hold* and *none of your business*, and you kept every true thing sealed below the waterline where nobody could see it, and eventually—this was the part nobody warned you about—you couldn't see it yourself anymore. You forgot what your own bilge looked like.

Freya Ashcombe was sitting on his cargo hatch waiting for an answer, and she had waited three days on a dock once already, so he knew she'd wait however long this took, and God help him, some tired drowned part of him didn't want her to have to wait at all.

"She kissed a bar two days back," he said.

"I know. I felt it."

"Forty-one years old, that hull. Strakes are original. When she flexes—and she flexes, everything flexes, that's how boats stay alive—the seam opens. I've patched it. The patch will hold in calm water." He heard his own voice doing the thing voices do when they're delivering inventory instead of truth. "We are not going to be in calm water."

"No?"

"Monsoon water comes down the gorge in walls. Standing waves. Debris—you wouldn't believe what comes down this river when the headwaters let go. Whole trees. Roofs. Once, a piano." He paused. "Nobody knows whose piano."

"And the patch?"

"In monsoon water, the patch is a suggestion." He turned the cold tea out over the side, mostly for something to do with his hands. "The bilge pump can outrun the leak in calm conditions. In heavy water, it's a fair fight, and I've seen fair fights on this river, and the river wins them."

Freya was quiet for a moment. He braced for it—for the recalculation, the quick arithmetic behind her eyes, the sensible conclusion that a woman with money and connections ought to hire a better boat, a newer boat, a boat whose owner didn't flinch from eye contact. He'd watched passengers make that calculation before. He always saw the exact moment it completed. It looked like a door closing.

She didn't close the door. She looked at the bilge hatch, and then at him, and she said:

"What do you need from me?"

That was all. No flinch. No negotiation. No *how bad, exactly*, no *should we turn back*, none of the questions that were really statements wearing question-shaped coats. Just: what do you need from me.

Ulyana found that he had to look at the river for a while before he could speak, and he told himself it was to check the current, which was a lie, and both of them probably knew it was a lie, and neither of them mentioned it, which was its own kind of mercy.

"Copper," he said finally. "There's a wrecked barge at the mouth of the Sere cut—half-sunk, been there six years. Plating on her stern quarter's still good. If we stop, I can tear off enough copper to double-patch the seam properly. Riveted, not just glued. That turns the suggestion back into a promise."

"Then we stop."

"It costs us three hours. Maybe four, with the salvage."

"Ulyana." She said his name like a fact again. "You just told me the boat might sink in four days. I think we can spare four hours to prevent it."

"There's also," he said, and stopped.

"There's also."

"The pump." He rubbed the back of his neck. "She's a hand pump. Bilge pumps are supposed to be hand pumps on a boat this size, that's regulation, but regulation assumes two able crew, and—" he gestured vaguely at the entire situation, the missing expedition, the monsoon, the logbook page with its wrong coordinates, "—if we're bailing and running rapids at the same time, one of us is going to be doing both jobs badly. There's an impeller housing at the Sere wreck too, maybe. Off her engine. If it's not silted. If it's not cracked. If I can mate it to the Magdalen's shaft, which is a sentence containing more ifs than a prayer."

"So we salvage a pump and copper plating from a six-year-old wreck in the next four hours, then run four days of rising water into a gorge during the first storm of the season, to find your brother." He said it out loud and heard, fully, for the first time, what it sounded like. "I want to note that I've said all of this calmly, and that inside I am screaming in a way that would embarrass a kettle."

Something happened to Freya's face then. The calm armor shifted, and underneath it—not a smile, quite. Something warmer and more dangerous than a smile. Relief, maybe. Or recognition.

"Good," she said.

"*Good?*"

"Good that you said it. All of it. The real condition." She hugged her knees, looking north at the stacked clouds like a woman greeting an old adversary across a chessboard. "Do you know what everyone did, after my testimony? Everyone who was supposed to be protecting me? They said *don't worry*. They said *it's handled*. They said *all sound*. And then it wasn't sound, and I was on a night ferry with two suitcases, and the worrying arrived all at once with nobody around who'd practiced for it." She shrugged. "You telling me the boat might sink is the first honest inventory anyone's given me in six years. It's restful. Like a cold cloth."

Ulyana turned back to his tiller. The current leaned against the rudder, steady and conversational, and far upstream the first of the cloud towers shrugged and began, very slowly, to move south.

"Hold that thought," he said, "when you're bailing."

"Oh, I'll hold it," Freya said. "I'll hold it with both hands. It's the only thing aboard that isn't leaking."

* * *

They reached the mouth of the Sere cut by early afternoon, and the wreck was where six years and the river had left her: heeled over on a gravel tongue, stern quarter awash, her name long since rotted off her transom. Somebody's fortune, somebody's mistake. The Veyra collected both indiscriminately.

Ulyana backed the *Magdalen* in against the gravel with the tenderness he saved exclusively for docking maneuvers, killed the engine, and went over the side into shin-deep water with his pry bar and his bag of tools, and for two hours the world narrowed to the honest work of tearing metal off a dead boat to keep a living one alive. It was, he reflected, wedging the bar under a riveted plate, the entire profession in miniature. Salvage upon salvage upon salvage. Nothing on this river was new. Everything was borrowed from something else's ruin.

Freya proved unexpectedly useful, which no longer surprised him but still pleased him in a way he declined to examine. She held plates while he drilled. She hauled copper sheets up the bank and stacked them with a surveyor's precision. And when he surfaced from the flooded engine room of the wreck with the impeller housing held overhead like a diver's trophy, silt-caked but—by miracle, by luck, by whatever patron saint presided over dubious machinery—uncracked, she laughed. Actually laughed, short and startled, like the sound had escaped custody.

"It's not funny."

"It's a little funny. You look like you're presenting a crown."

"This crown keeps us afloat."

"Then I take it back. Long live the king." She took the housing from him with both hands, staggering slightly under its weight, and carried it up the bank with the solemnity the joke deserved, and Ulyana stood waist-deep beside a drowned barge and realized he was smiling, and stopped immediately, and failed completely.

* * *

By evening the copper was riveted home, doubled over the bad seam, tarred and canvas-wrapped, and the improvised pump—mated to the shaft with epoxy, prayer, and two hose clamps of heroic ambition—sat in the bilge like a sleeping animal he didn't entirely trust. He tested it until dark. It worked. Mostly. It worked the way a mule works: reluctantly, noisily, and only for someone who respected it.

They ate rice and tinned fish on the hatch while the light died out of the western hills and the eastern sky did nothing at all, because the east was full of cloud now, wall to wall, the monsoon's vanguard settled in for the night like an army that had taken the high ground and intended to keep it. The air had changed too. It lay thick and mineral-smelling, and the river had risen a hand's width since morning—he marked it against a scratch on the pilothouse post, an old habit, one of dozens—and the current had picked up a new voice, a deeper note underneath its usual murmur, like something large turning over in its sleep far upstream.

"Four days," Freya said quietly. It wasn't a question anymore. It had graduated.

"Four days," Ulyana agreed. "If the storm holds off another day, five. If it doesn't—" He shrugged, and left the sentence open, because some sentences were better left open. Doors, not walls.

She nodded slowly, and then, in the last grey light, she did something he didn't expect. She picked up the pencil and the logbook page, tore a strip from the margin, wrote something on it, and handed it to him.

It was a list. Her handwriting was small and exact. *Copper — done. Pump — done. Rice, 9 days. Tea — adequate.* And at the bottom, in letters slightly larger than the rest, as though it were simply one more line item: *Brother — pending.*

Ulyana read it twice. He folded it along its creases, carefully, and put it in the breast pocket of his shirt, over the place where a heart would be if he still kept his there.

"Pending," he said.

"Pending," Freya said.

Far upstream, beyond the black ridgelines, lightning flickered inside the cloud towers—silent from this distance, patient, a lamp switching on and off in a room where something enormous was getting ready. The river's deep note deepened. Ulyana checked the scratch-mark on the post one last time, tied off the tiller, and went to check the bilge, because checking the bilge was a form of prayer, and he was, despite everything, beginning to have things he prayed for.

The pump started on the third pull. He counted that as an answer.
