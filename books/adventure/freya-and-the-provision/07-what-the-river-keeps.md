## 7. What the River Keeps

The squall came down the valley like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy, just a wall of grey that ate the ridgelines and turned the Veyra the color of old pewter.

Ulyana had seen it coming before I did. Of course he had. He'd been watching the water the way other men watch their children, and twenty minutes before the first gust hit, he'd said only, "There's a cut in the bank ahead," and turned the wheel without further explanation. By the time the rain arrived sideways, we were tucked into a narrow side channel behind a curtain of overhanging bamboo, engine off, lines doubled, the boat breathing quietly against its moorings like an animal that had been given permission to rest.

I stood under the little canopy aft and watched the rain hammer the river flat.

"Does it always do that?" I asked. "Just arrive?"

"Dry season squalls. They're short." He was coiling a line with the particular economy of a man who never made a motion he didn't need. "An hour, maybe two. Then it'll be steamy as a laundry and the mosquitoes will hold a festival."

"You could have warned me to bring a book."

"I could have warned you not to come at all." But there was no edge in it anymore. Somewhere between the rapids and the grounding scare, the edge had worn down to something almost like habit—the shape of complaint without the substance. He nodded toward the cabin. "Get inside before you're soaked through. There's tea. Don't ask what's in it."

"What's in it?"

"Exactly."

* * *

The cabin was small enough that two people in it constituted a crowd, but Ulyana had a way of occupying space that made room for others anyway—he folded himself onto the bench by the stove and left me the dry corner, and pretended not to notice when I took it. The tea tasted of ginger, something bitter, and a third thing I chose not to investigate. Rain drummed the roof so hard it sounded like applause.

He checked the radio. Hourly, always hourly—I'd been cataloguing that since the dock. He keyed the mic, listened to the hiss, said nothing, set it down. The hiss filled the silence after, a soft static ocean.

"They were broadcasting when it happened," I said. "My brother's team. You told me that, back at the dock. Mid-broadcast, you said. One minute they're doing their routine position report and the next—" I snapped my fingers, and immediately regretted it, because snapping your fingers to indicate your brother's disappearance is the kind of gesture that feels obscene about half a second after you make it.

Ulyana didn't comment on the snap. "Mid-broadcast," he confirmed. "Position report, weather comment, then the carrier just dropped. Could be equipment. Could be a tower down. Could be nothing."

"But you don't think it's nothing."

He looked at the radio the way a man looks at a dog that has done something unforgivable but is still, regrettably, a dog. "I think rivers eat things," he said finally. "Boats. Radios. People. It doesn't usually do all three at once without leaving anything on the bank."

I wrapped my hands around the tin mug. It was too hot to hold comfortably and I held it anyway, because the alternative was holding myself, and I wasn't ready for where that would go.

"Can I tell you something?" I said. "Since we're trapped here for an hour and you've already decided I'm insane."

His eyebrows performed a small, skeptical dance. "You're going to tell me regardless."

"I am, yes."

* * *

Here is what I had rehearsed saying, in various hotel rooms and bus stations across three years: *My brother Tomas joined the Ashcombe-Veyra survey as a hydrologist. He stopped answering his radio. I'm going upriver to find him.* Every word true. Every word also a fence built around a larger field I didn't let anyone into.

But fences are for people who still have something left to protect, and three days on this man's leaking boat had taught me something about him: he counted cans twice not because he was fussy but because counting twice is how you make sure the world is still behaving. He checked the radio hourly because silence is only bearable if you measure it. And he'd refused my money for four days straight not because the money was insufficient—I'd seen him look at the hull, and I knew what repairs cost—but because taking it meant owing someone, and owing someone meant being known.

So I put the mug down and told him the rest.

"Three years ago I worked for a shipping analytics firm in Corvane. Boring name, boring work—we tracked cargo manifests for insurers. Except one quarter the numbers didn't track. Ore shipments coming out of the upper Veyra basin, weighed at origin and weighed at port, and the difference between those numbers was enough to buy a small country's worth of silence. Somebody upstream was moving ore that didn't exist on paper. I flagged it. My firm told me to unflag it. I didn't."

Ulyana had gone very still. Not surprised-still. Listening-still. There's a difference, and I was learning to read it in him the way I'd learned to read water.

"I testified," I said. "It took fourteen months. Hearings, depositions, a magistrate who kept mispronouncing my name like it was a disease. In the end two mid-level men went to prison and the company paid a fine it could afford to lose, and the operation itself—" I shrugged. "The operation itself never surfaced. Because the operation wasn't a company. It was people. People who don't go to hearings. People who send letters instead."

"What kind of letters?"

"The kind with no return address. The first one quoted my testimony back to me. Word for word. Which meant someone had been in the gallery, or someone had access to the transcripts, or both. The second one was shorter. It just gave a date and a time and said, *Be somewhere else.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bright, the laugh I use at funerals of people I barely knew. "I was somewhere else by morning. I've been somewhere else ever since. Three years, six cities, two names. When Tomas wrote to me from the survey, full of enthusiasm about the upper basin—*you'd love it up here, Frey, the ice cores, the light*—I thought, good, he's safe up there, nobody looks for anyone up there. Then the radio went quiet, and I realized I'd have to go upriver myself, and I thought—"

I stopped. The rain filled the pause.

"—I thought, whoever sent those letters knows these rivers better than I ever will. And if they found Tomas first, or if Tomas stumbled into whatever they're moving—"

"Then finding him means finding them," Ulyana said quietly.

"Finding him might mean being found." I looked at my hands. "I didn't come upriver just because I love my brother. I came because I've spent three years running from a thing I can't see, and I'm tired, and if it's going to catch me I'd rather it happened while I was doing something that mattered. That's the truth. The whole ugly, selfish, exhausted truth. I used my brother as a reason to stop running, and now he's missing, and I don't know which of us I'm actually trying to save."

The stove ticked. Outside, the squall threw another armful of rain against the canopy, and the bamboo along the bank bent and swished like a crowd murmuring.

Ulyana picked up the teapot and refilled my mug without asking, which was, I was beginning to understand, the closest he came to tenderness.

* * *

"You want to know the funny part?" I said, when I could speak normally again. "I practiced telling this story for three years. In case I ever got caught. I had a whole version prepared for customs officials and one for journalists and one for the hypothetical letter-writer himself, very dignified, very brave. And the version I actually use, the real one, I've never said out loud until right now, on a boat, to a man who won't even tell me what's in his tea."

"Ginger," Ulyana said. "And a root that grows near the sulfur springs. And bark I trade for at Kellin's Landing. If I told you the proportions I'd have to charge you."

"That's fair."

He was quiet for a while. The rain softened by degrees, the way storms do when they've said their piece. Through the cabin window the channel ran silver-grey, and somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

"When I was nineteen," he said, and then stopped, and I understood that stopping was part of the sentence, that he needed to stand at the edge of it for a moment before stepping off.

I waited. Waiting, I'd learned on this boat, was a skill like any other. You got better at it or you drowned.

"There was a man named Ostrava," he said at last. "Ran cargo out of Marrow Bend. I crewed for him at nineteen, twenty. He paid well and asked nothing, which at nineteen felt like freedom and at thirty I understood was ownership." His thumb moved along the seam of the bench, tracing it, an unconscious survey of his own territory. "One season he started carrying crates he wouldn't let me count. Sealed, heavy, cold to the touch even in summer. Same route every week, same landing, no manifest. I asked once. Just once. He smiled at me and said, *Boy, the river keeps what the river keeps, and so do I.*"

"And?"

"And I kept crewing. Three more seasons. Because the pay was good and asking nothing felt like freedom." His jaw tightened around something. "Until the night the revenue cutter came up the channel with lights off, and Ostrava put me at the wheel and told me to run it dark past the checkpoint, and I did. I ran it. Nothing happened—no shots, no chase, we slipped past clean. But a clerk at the next station had seen our running lights earlier, and there was an inquiry, and Ostrava stood in front of the magistrate and said, calm as Sunday, *the boy was at the wheel, I was below.*"

"He blamed you."

"He testified against me. I took eighteen months in Marrow Bend lockup for smuggling I hadn't agreed to and hadn't profited from. Ostrava kept running the route the whole time I was inside. When I got out, I owed debts—to the lockup canteen, to the man who'd held my tools, to everyone—and my name had a mark on it that no honest employer would touch. So I bought this boat with borrowed money and I've been running cargo and dodging questions ever since." He looked at the radio. "Ten years. The debt's nearly cleared. The name's still marked."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"I'm not sure," he said honestly, and that honesty cracked something open in the cabin, some small window I hadn't known was there. "You told me something true and expensive. It seemed rude to sit on mine."

I thought about that. *Vulnerability traded like ballast*—that was the phrase that came to me later, sitting on the bow after the rain stopped, watching the channel run clean again. You don't dump ballast all at once; you shift it, carefully, from one side to the other, to keep the boat level. He'd shifted his. I'd shifted mine. Neither of us had mentioned that the boat sat truer afterward.

"Ostrava," I said. "Is he still running?"

"Dead, I heard. Capsized in the lower gorge six years back, drunk, alone, exactly as deserved." Ulyana's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "The river keeps what the river keeps."

"Is that a proverb or a threat?"

"Both. Usually both."

* * *

The squall passed an hour later, as promised, and the mosquitoes convened their festival. We emerged into air like wet wool, everything steaming, the channel bright and quick again. Ulyana cast off the lines and brought us out into the main current with that unhurried competence I was no longer pretending not to admire.

I stood at the bow, because standing at the bow had become my job and my privilege both, calling out snags and reading the water ahead. My hair was still damp. My chest felt strangely light, the way it does after you've set down luggage you forgot you were carrying.

"Frey." Ulyana's voice from the wheelhouse, careful. "The letters you got. The ones quoting your testimony."

"Yes?"

"Did any of them mention the upper basin? The survey route? Anything specific to this stretch of river?"

I turned. He wasn't looking at me; he was looking at the water, but his whole body had the quality of a man listening with more than his ears.

"No," I said slowly. "Why?"

"Because the ore in those manifests you flagged. The weights that didn't match." He eased the wheel a degree, threading us past a submerged bar only he could see. "The origin point on those shipments was the upper Veyra basin. Mile 340 and above."

The river carried us forward into the green silence, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the dock: my brother, the missing team, the ore that existed off paper, and the letters that had chased me across three years and six cities—all of it converging somewhere upstream, in the country of ice and light that Tomas had loved.

"Ulyana," I said. "How much farther to mile 340?"

He checked the current, the sky, the leak he hadn't quite confessed to yet. "Two days," he said. "If the river lets us."
