## 8. Grounded

The sky had been lying to us all morning.

That was the thing about the Veyra in the last days of dry season—it wore a face of perfect innocence. Blue overhead, a few white clouds fat and lazy as sleeping sheep, the water running low and brown and gentle over the shallows. A man could look at that sky and believe the river would forgive him anything.

I didn't believe it. I'd stopped believing it the year a cloud the size of a country came over the headwaters and erased a fishing village I won't name, because the people who lived there didn't want to be named and the river didn't leave enough of them to argue. But I'd been watching the western horizon all day, and the sheep up there had stopped sleeping. They were standing. They were gathering. And they were the wrong color—the deep bruised violet that means the sky has stopped being weather and started being intent.

"Bar ahead," Freya said from the bow.

She'd gotten good at calling water. Three days of watching me do it, and she'd started seeing the tells—the way the current wrinkled differently over a shoal, the way driftwood piled in a lazy crescent where the deep channel wasn't. She was still wrong about one thing in three, which on the Veyra is a survivable ratio, but she was wrong in interesting ways, and I'd stopped telling her when she was right because she'd started getting insufferable about it.

"Which side?" I asked, though I could see it. A long pale tongue of sand, newly shifted, lying right across the channel where my charts—eleven years old and lying to me too—said there was six feet of water.

"Left. No—right. The current's splitting around it."

The current was not splitting around it. The current was piling against it like a crowd against a closed door. I cut the engine to a crawl and took us toward what looked like the deepest slot, and I want it on the record that I made the correct read. The water under the hull went from brown to green to that glassy, oily calm that means depth.

Then the river changed its mind.

I felt it through my boots before I heard it—a shudder, a grinding, the whole hull shivering like a dog shaking off rain. The bar had moved. Of course it had. Shifting bars do that; it's in the name. Somewhere under us, tons of sand had decided, in the last hour, to go be somewhere else, and we had arrived at the exact moment the new sand arrived at its new address.

We grounded hard. Not a kiss, not a scrape. A *thunk* that threw Freya into the rail and sent my coffee tin spinning across the deck plates.

I killed the engine and stood very still, listening. That's the first rule of grounding, before pushing, before poling, before any of the clever stuff: listen. A boat tells you what kind of trouble you're in if you shut up long enough to hear it. This one told me we were hard aground, bow high, stern in maybe two feet of moving water, and the sand under us was still settling, still groaning, still deciding.

"Well," Freya said, from the deck where she was picking herself up. "That sounded expensive."

"It wasn't expensive. It was free. Grounding's free. Getting *off* is where they charge you."

I got the pole out. I got the spare anchor out. I waded in to my thighs in water the temperature of melted snow and set the kedge astern, because the only way off a bar you've driven onto bow-first is to pull yourself backward off it, and the only way to pull yourself backward is to anchor in the deep channel and winch.

The anchor held. The winch turned. The boat groaned, moved six inches, and stopped.

I winched again. Six more inches. Then nothing. Then the winch handle kicked back and spun out of my hands and I watched it skip twice across the water and vanish downstream, and I stood there in the river with my hands empty and looked up at the western sky, where the sheep had turned the color of a dead man's lips.

"Ulyana," Freya said. "What's that smell?"

I smelled it too. Every river rat knows it. Petrichor, the books call it—the smell of rain arriving. Except this wasn't the polite drizzle smell of a coastal afternoon. This was the Veyra's monsoon announcing itself, and it smelled like wet iron and green rot and something almost electrical, like the air itself had been wound too tight.

"Get the tarp over the cargo," I said. "Get everything that can't swim up off the deck. And then get the buckets."

"What buckets?"

"The ones in the bilge locker. All of them."

She looked at me. She's got her brother's gray eyes, or he's got hers—I never met him—but there's more of their mother in them, I'd guess, because they go flat and assessing when she's frightened instead of wide. She looked at me and did the arithmetic on my face and didn't like the answer.

"How bad is the leak?"

"Bad enough that I was going to tell you tonight. Over dinner. With brandy."

"Ulyana."

"It's a seep. It's been a seep since before you came aboard. It's a seep I've been managing for two seasons, and I manage it fine in dry weather, and—" I looked at the sky again, and the first fat drops hit the river around us like the opening shots of something. "—and that is not dry weather coming."

* * *

The storm hit us the way debts hit a man who's been dodging them: all at once, and with interest.

The first ten minutes were just rain, but rain of a kind I'd only seen up here—a rain that didn't fall so much as *arrive*, a gray wall of it marching up the river and swallowing the far bank, the near bank, the sky. Then the wind came behind it and bent the rain sideways, and the river, which had been low and sleepy, stood up in ridges and started throwing itself at us.

And the bilge started filling.

I'll give Freya this: she didn't panic, and she didn't waste breath on questions. I got the hand pumps rigged—one working, one held together with hose clamps and stubbornness—and she took the working one and started pulling water like she'd been bailing boats her whole life, which she claimed she hadn't, and which I had already filed away with the other things she claimed, like *city origins* and *never handled a line before*. You don't learn to pump like that in a city. You don't learn to brace your legs and let your back do the work and count strokes so you don't burn out in the first five minutes. That's boat-raised knowledge. It's in the spine, not the head.

But there was no time to file anything. The boat was grounded on a bar that was itself adrift, the river was rising around us at a rate I could measure against the anchor line—six inches an hour, then eight, then I stopped measuring because the numbers were rude—and every gallon the pumps sent over the side was arriving back through the seams faster.

The leak was worse than I'd told her. That's the other thing I'll put on the record. I'd called it a seep. A seep is what you call a leak when you're not ready to have the conversation about what it actually is. What it actually was: two planks starboard, aft of the midships frame, whose caulking had dried and shrunk through ten dry seasons and was now being asked, by a river rising three feet an hour, to behave like caulking that had been laid last week. Water came through them in silver threads, then in pencils, then in fingers.

"Ulyana." Freya's voice came through the rain, steady, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "How much water is too much?"

"Depends how much boat is above it."

"How much boat is above it?"

"Right now? Enough." I jammed a tapered softwood plug into the worst seam and hammered it home with the flat of my palm, and the finger of water became a thread again. "Bail. Don't talk. Bail."

We bailed. I'll tell you what that night was, since I was there and the river has never corrected me: it was two people on a nine-meter boat, in a storm, on a sandbar in the middle of the Veyra, passing buckets. That's it. That's the whole of it. No heroics, no clever seamanship, nothing a ballad-writer could use. Just the bucket, and the bilge, and the counting. One for me, one for her, one for me. The rain came down like the sky was settling a grudge. The lightning walked the ridgelines to the west—actual walking, strobe by strobe, a slow luminous procession along the crest of the hills like lantern-bearers in some procession I wanted no part of—and each flash showed me the same picture: gray water, gray sky, and our little hull stuck in the middle of it like a beetle in a puddle.

Somewhere past midnight—the river doesn't care about midnight, but I kept the time anyway because a man needs to know how long he's been losing—the stern started to lift.

That should have been good news. Rising water floats boats; that's the entire arrangement. But a boat that floats off a bar in a flood current isn't rescued, it's *released*, and the current out there in the dark was running hard enough to fold us around the next bar like laundry. I knew it. I stood in the well with rain running off my chin and watched the water climb the anchor line and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do: if she floats, we drift. If we drift, we hit something. If we hit something, we open a seam that no plug will hold, and then the Veyra gets the boat, and everything the boat is.

"Ulyana!" Freya had to shout now, over the rain. "She's moving!"

"I know!"

"Is that good?"

"It's a question!"

I got the second kedge out—the spare, the one I'd set that morning and never recovered—and I did the thing that boat books tell you not to do, which is wade out into a flood current at night with an anchor over your shoulder. I got maybe eight feet off the beam before the water took my feet and I went downstream like a cork on a string, and I'll say this for the river, it's an honest negotiator: it showed me, in about four seconds of cold green chaos, exactly what it does to men who go into it, and then it spat me against the anchor line, because I'd had the sense to tie the line around my waist before I went in. I came up the line hand over hand, anchor dragging, lungs full of river, and I got the fluke to bite in the sand off the starboard quarter just as the hull lifted free.

And then we were afloat. Both anchors taut, the boat bridled between them, swinging like a pendulum in the current, bailing for our lives, and the storm walking its lanterns up and down the ridgelines like it had all night and intended to use it.

* * *

We didn't sink. I want that sentence to sit alone for a moment, because for about an hour there it was not a settled question.

The pumps and the buckets and the plugs held. The rain eased from *murder* to *merely biblical* somewhere in the small hours, and the lightning moved off to argue with some other ridge, and the river kept rising but stopped rising *angry*, and by the time the sky went the color of wet slate that passes for dawn up here, we were afloat, anchored, awash to the deck seams, and alive.

Freya sat on the engine box, wringing out her hair, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read at first. Then I could. It was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic on a man's face again, and this time she'd gotten the answer.

"You could have told me the hull was that bad."

"I told you it was a seep."

"You told me it was a seep *with brandy*, which I now understand was a negotiation tactic."

"It was a kindness."

"It was a lie with a glass attached." She wrung the hem of her shirt out over the side. "Why? Why lie about the boat? I'm not going anywhere. You've got my money."

And that was the question, wasn't it. Why. I had a ready answer, the old one, the armor one: *a captain doesn't frighten his passenger; a captain manages his own problems.* I'd said versions of it to charter clients, to inspectors, to myself, for ten years, and it had always fit like a boot fits a foot, which is to say it had always been shaped by the wearing.

But I was sitting in three inches of bilge water in a boat that had nearly died in the night, and the woman across from me had bailed beside me without one word of complaint, and had watched me go into the flood current on a rope and hadn't screamed, and had counted strokes with me in the dark like she'd done it before, and the old answer just—didn't fit. It hung off me like a coat the wrong size.

"Because it's not a boat," I said.

She waited. She's good at waiting. Three days on a dock taught me that.

"Everybody thinks a boat's a thing you own," I said. "A tool. An asset. Something with a price. And it is, on paper, to a magistrate or a bank. But I've slept on this deck for ten years. Every plank in her I've replaced with my own hands, some of them twice. I know her seams the way you know—" I stopped, because the sentence was heading somewhere I hadn't planned. "I know her seams. When she's right, I can feel it through my boots before my head knows it. When she's wrong, it's like a toothache. She's not the thing I live on. She's the only place I *live*. There's no other address. There hasn't been, in a long time."

The rain ticked on the tarp. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere upstream, thunder was having a long, slow argument with itself and losing interest.

"So last night," Freya said, "when the water was coming in—"

"Last night I wasn't afraid of drowning. I can swim; the river would've had to work for it." I looked at the bilge, at the silver thread still coming through the plugged seam, patient as anything. "I was afraid of the boat going down. Because everything I am is aboard. And I've never said that out loud to anyone, and I'd thank you to note that I'm saying it before coffee, which is against my religion."

Freya didn't laugh, exactly. But something in her face let go of a knot. "Noted," she said. "And for the record—when you went off the side with that anchor, I wasn't afraid of losing the boat either."

"No?"

"No. I was afraid of losing you." She said it the way she said everything, flat and gray-eyed and level, like reporting a measurement. "And I've only known you two weeks. So I'd say we're both keeping poor records."

I didn't have anything to say to that, which is rare for me and rarer still to admit. I got the coffee tin—I found it wedged under the rail, half full of rainwater, and decided that counted as brewed—and poured two cups, and we drank them sitting on the engine box with our feet in bilge water, watching the storm walk away upriver like a guest who'd finally had enough.

It was Freya who saw it first. She was looking north, at the far bank, where the rain had thinned to veils and the light was coming up gray-green through it. She went very still, the way she'd gone still at the answering station when she found the logbook page.

"Ulyana. Look at the bank."

I looked. The flood had rearranged the world overnight—new channels, new snags, the whole river rewritten in the dark. And there, on the rewritten bank, half-buried at the new waterline, was a line of crates. Six of them, maybe eight, dark wood, stenciled, half-swallowed by sand.

Stenciled with the survey's mark.

"That's three days' walk from the cache site," Freya said quietly. "Somebody moved them. Or the river did." She was already on her feet, already reaching for the boat hook, and the gray eyes had gone flat and assessing, which by now I knew meant she'd stopped being frightened and started being dangerous.

The current, I noticed, was still running hard. And the crates, I noticed further, were on the downstream side of a snag the size of a chapel.

"Freya," I said, and reached for her arm. "Freya, wait—"

She was already at the bow.
