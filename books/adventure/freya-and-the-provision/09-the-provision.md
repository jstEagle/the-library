## 9. The Provision

The river let us off the sandbar the way a cat lets go of a mouse it has decided not to eat—reluctantly, and only after making its point.

Ulyana worked us free at first light, when the water had risen just enough to float the hull. He did it without ceremony: engine at half-throttle, rudder feathered against the current, Freya Ashcombe on the bow line hauling when he said *haul* and pretending she wasn't holding her breath when he said nothing at all. The boat slid off the bar with a groan that I felt in my teeth, and then we were moving again, the Veyra running brown and swollen beside us, carrying us upstream toward whatever it had been keeping.

"You're bleeding," Ulyana said. It was the first full sentence either of us had managed since dawn.

I looked down. My palms were striped red where the rope had run through them during the night's bailing. "It's cosmetic," I said.

"Everything's cosmetic until it isn't." He reached into the crate behind his seat without looking—he knew where everything on this boat lived the way I knew where everything in my own kitchen used to live, back when I'd had one—and tossed me a tin of ointment. "Wrap them after."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. We've still got to find your brother, outrun a monsoon, and keep this boat above the waterline. Save your gratitude for something I actually accomplish."

It was, I was learning, how he said *you're welcome*. And also how he said *I was afraid last night too*, and *I'm glad you were here*, all folded into one dry little parcel that he handed over and immediately pretended he hadn't.

We pushed on through the morning. The country changed as we climbed—the banks narrowing, the jungle giving way to stands of grey-barked trees, the hills stepping up toward a skyline of cloud that never quite resolved into either weather or mountain. Somewhere ahead, according to the logbook page we'd found at the answering station, was a set of coordinates that matched no official campsite. Somewhere ahead was *the provision*.

I turned the phrase over in my head for the hundredth time. Provision. It sounded official. Supply drop, maybe. Equipment cache. Survey teams cached fuel and food all along these rivers; Tomas had mentioned it in his letters, offhand, the way he mentioned everything—as though his work were so routine it barely deserved describing. *Cached the second fuel drum below the gorge. Nothing here but midges and bad coffee.*

Nothing here but midges and bad coffee, and my brother, who had stopped answering his radio eleven days ago.

"Your face is doing something," Ulyana observed.

"My face is doing its job."

"It's doing arithmetic. I can tell because it looks like pain." He adjusted the throttle a fraction. "Whatever sum you're running, run it out loud. You'll steer better."

"I'm not steering."

"You're steering. Everyone on a boat is steering. Some of them just don't know it."

So I ran it out loud, or some of it. "If the cache is contraband—if they were moving ore they weren't authorized to move—then the survey was cover. Which means the expedition wasn't lost. It was *spent*." I heard myself say it and hated how clean it sounded. "Someone finished with them."

"Or someone finished with *part* of them," Ulyana said. "Crews like that don't come apart all at once. They come apart along the seams. Somebody wanted out. Somebody wanted more. Somebody got both wishes granted badly." He said it with the flat certainty of a man reciting a recipe, and I remembered—again—that I knew almost nothing about where he'd learned his recipes.

"And which somebody was Tomas?"

"That's what we're going up there to stop guessing about."

* * *

We found the cache an hour past noon, and we found it because of the smell.

Not decay—I braced for that, and the bracing itself told me things about where my mind had been living. No. What we smelled was diesel and cut timber, sharp and industrial, entirely wrong for a riverbank that should have smelled of wet leaf-rot and fish. Ulyana nosed the boat into a side channel where the water ran slow and tea-dark, and there, under a leaning screen of branches, was a landing that someone had built and then tried to unbuild. Stakes pulled. Planks scattered. But planks are honest; they remember where they've lain, and the mud beneath them was stamped with boot traffic, heavy and repeated.

We tied off. Ulyana went first with the machete, which surprised me until I realized it didn't—he moved into unknown ground the way he moved through everything, quietly, weight forward, already knowing three ways back out. I followed with the flare gun, which was the closest thing to a weapon I'd thought to pack, back when I'd still believed this trip might be a rescue rather than an archaeology.

The cache sat forty meters inland, in a natural hollow where the ground dipped between two root-slabbed trees. Someone had dug into the hollow and walled it with rough-cut timber, and then—this was the part that made the back of my neck prickle—someone had tried to hide having done it. Branches laid over the entrance. Mud smeared across fresh cuts. A concealment job done by people who understood concealment but not well enough, or not calmly enough.

"They were in a hurry," Ulyana murmured. He crouched at the entrance, reading the ground. "And they weren't hiding it from the river. Nobody polices a hollow this far off the channel." He glanced up at me. "They were hiding it from each other."

Inside, the light came down green and dim through the branch screen, and the cache opened up around us like a held breath.

Crates. A dozen of them, maybe more, stacked in two ranks with a crawl-space between. Most were stenciled with survey markings—VEYRA HYDROGRAPHIC EXPEDITION, UNIT 4—which would have been reassuring if the crates hadn't weighed wrong when Ulyana tipped the nearest one. Fuel drums don't clink. Food stores don't shift with that dense, granular slide, like gravel poured slow.

He pried one open with the flat of the machete, working the nails rather than splitting the wood, because even now—even here—he couldn't bring himself to waste sound lumber.

The crate held ore.

Grey-green stone in fist-sized chunks, shot through with veins that caught the filtered light and threw it back with a faint metallic gleam, packed in sawdust like eggs. I'd seen photographs of it once, years ago, in a courtroom in a city I no longer let myself name. I hadn't needed the photographs to know what it was. I'd known from the moment the first chunk rolled against the others with that soft mineral click.

"Veyrite," I said. My voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening underneath it. "Raw. Unrefined."

"You know it?"

"I know what it's *for*." I picked up a piece. It was heavier than it looked, cold even in the day's humidity, and beautiful in the way that expensive dangerous things are always beautiful—designed, almost, to make you forget to ask questions. "There's a smelter consortium two borders east that pays cash for this. No manifests, no provenance. It comes out of protected headwaters that nobody's supposed to be mining." I set it down carefully, the way you put down something that might be listening. "I testified about a shipment of it. Six years ago."

Ulyana looked at me for a long moment. Whatever he was doing behind his eyes, he did it privately, and then he nodded once and turned back to the crates, and I understood that he was adding this to the file he kept on me—the same file I was keeping on him, both of us writing entries neither of us would ever read aloud.

"Twelve crates visible," he said instead. "Call it four hundred kilos a crate, conservatively. That's not a side venture. That's a *contract*." He moved down the rank, checking seals. "Somebody with real reach hired a hydrographic survey team to map the river and move rock while they mapped it. The survey gave them cover, equipment, and a reason to be exactly where nobody could ask why."

"And when the crew figured out what they were carrying?"

"Then the crew became a problem." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Problems get divided. Some take the money. Some take the risk of walking away. And sometimes—" he didn't finish it.

"Sometimes they split the difference with a machete," I said, because someone had to.

There were signs, once we knew to look. A dark stain on the timber wall, old enough to have gone brown-black, scrubbed at but never scrubbed out. A torn sleeve of expedition-issue canvas caught on a nail. And near the rear of the hollow, half-tucked under the last crate, a scatter of personal effects that someone had gathered together and then abandoned gathering—a water flask, dented; a broken radio handset; a folding knife with a snapped blade.

And the jacket.

I knew it before I touched it. Of course I knew it. I'd bought it for him—dark blue canvas, the collar gone soft from years of sun, the left cuff mended with orange thread because Tomas could never be bothered to match thread and I had long ago stopped correcting him. His initials were inked inside the collar in his terrible handwriting, the T looping too far, the A squashed flat. *T.A.* He'd done it himself with a laundry marker the week he joined the survey, laughing down the phone at me: *Everyone on the team marks their kit. Makes us feel like an army.*

I knelt. The jacket lay crumpled, not folded—dropped, not shed. There was blood on the right shoulder, dried to a rust-brown crescent, and a tear beneath it that could have been made by a blade or a branch or any of the hundred jagged things this country keeps lying around for exactly such purposes.

No body.

I want to tell you that I thought clearly in that moment. I didn't. Thought arrived later, in pieces, like salvage floating down after a flood. What arrived first was simpler and older than thought: my hands on the canvas, my forehead against the collar, the smell of him still in it—engine oil and cheap soap and that particular Tomas-smell that I would have sworn, a month ago, I'd forgotten and would have known now, kneeling in a smuggler's cache in the middle of nowhere, I had never once forgotten at all.

I heard Ulyana move. Not away—closer. He crouched down an arm's length off, close enough to be present, far enough to leave me the shape of my grief undisturbed, and he said nothing, and the nothing he said was the most decent thing anyone had said to me in six years.

When I could speak, what came out was absurd, and I let it, because absurdity is sometimes the only door grief will fit through.

"He mended the cuff with orange thread. I sent him proper navy thread in his last birthday parcel and he wrote back that orange was 'more visible if he fell in a river.'" My laugh cracked in the middle. "As if visibility was ever his problem. As if the problem wasn't that he can't—"

"Can't stay out of trouble," Ulyana supplied quietly.

"Can't stay *found*," I said. "He's been drifting further every year. The letters got shorter. The coordinates in them got remoter. And I told myself it was a career, it was a phase, it was what he loved—and the whole time I was telling myself that, I was three thousand kilometers away being nobody, in a city where nobody could find *me* either." I wiped my face with the back of my wrist, which stung, which I deserved. "We're very good at the same trick, my family. Disappearing. We just call it different names."

Ulyana picked up the jacket's torn shoulder, examined the cut with two fingers, and turned it toward the green light. "Blade," he said. "Downward stroke. Whoever did it was standing over him." He paused. "But look at the blood volume. That's a wound that bleeds hard and bleeds *fast*—and stops. It soaked and dried in one event. If he'd died here, there'd be more, and there'd be flies, and there'd be—" he stopped himself, recalibrated. "There'd be evidence of staying. There isn't. He bled, and then he moved."

"You can't know that."

"No," he agreed. "I can't know it. But I've read a lot of ground, and this ground says one man walked out of here bleeding and another man let him—or couldn't stop him—and then everyone left in a hurry because the monsoon was coming and four hundred kilos of ore doesn't carry itself." He set the jacket down with a care that undid me more thoroughly than gentleness would have. "Your brother may be the most annoying kind of missing person, Ashcombe. The kind who leaves evidence he's alive."

Hope is a cruel thing. It doesn't comfort you; it just reopens the account. I sat back on my heels with the jacket bundled against my chest and felt hope and grief sit down inside me together like two passengers who refuse to acknowledge each other, and I understood that this was simply the arrangement now, possibly forever.

"We take it with us," I said.

"The jacket?"

"The jacket, the knife, the handset. All of it. If he's alive, he'll want his kit back. If he's not—" I made myself finish. "If he's not, someone should have it who knows why the orange thread matters."

Ulyana nodded and started loading the smaller items into his satchel. Then he stopped, crouched by the torn sleeve on the nail, and went very still in the particular way I'd learned meant he'd found something the rest of us would need a minute to catch up to.

"Ashcombe."

"What?"

"Come count with me." He pointed along the crate rank. "How many?"

I counted. "Twelve."

"Now look at the wall brackets." Along the back timber, iron brackets had been bolted at even intervals—fourteen of them. Two empty spaces at the end of the rank, the mud beneath them scuffed by recent boot-work, the sawdust still bright where crates had dragged across it.

"Two crates already gone," I said.

"Carried out. Recently—days, not weeks. Downhill." He rose and looked toward the hollow's lower edge, where the ground fell away toward the river, toward the channel, toward downstream. Toward us, and everything behind us.

"Somebody's still moving the provision," I said.

"Somebody's still being *paid* to," Ulyana corrected. He slung the satchel and stood there a moment, weighing something, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he saved for the river's worst moods. "Two crates is a man's load split over two trips. One man, working alone, who didn't dare come back for the rest until the crew stopped watching him." He met my eyes. "Which means whoever took them thinks the rest of the team is dead, gone, or no longer a problem. And he's coming back for these."

The green light through the branch screen seemed to dim, though I knew it was only cloud building over the headwaters, the monsoon drawing its first long breath.

"So we're standing in the middle of someone's inventory," I said.

"With his brother's blood on the wall," Ulyana said, "and his employer's product in our boat, if we choose to take it." He was already moving toward the entrance, machete in hand, checking the light. "Decide fast. Because if I were him, I'd be watching this hollow. And if I were *us*—" he glanced back, and for the first time since I'd met him there was something in his face that wasn't dry, wasn't guarded, wasn't armor at all—just a man asking another person to help him carry a very heavy thing, "—I'd want to be gone before he arrives."

I tucked the jacket under my arm and followed him out into the loud green afternoon, toward the boat, toward the river, toward the two empty brackets and everything they implied.

Behind us, deep in the trees, a branch broke.

Once. Deliberately. Like a knock.
