## 10. Tracks

The cache had a smell to it, once you got past the river-smell and the rot-smell and the particular sourness of canvas that had given up on being waterproof. Ore has a smell too—metallic, faintly sweet, like blood that's been left out in the sun—and the crates stacked in the hollow beneath the overturned skiff were breathing it into the morning air as if the mountain itself had opened a vein.

Ulyana stood at the edge of the hollow and did not go in.

Freya was already inside, of course. She'd been inside before he'd finished tying off the boat, moving through the wreckage of the expedition's last camp with her brother's name held in her mouth like a stone she couldn't swallow and wouldn't spit out. She'd found the jacket twenty minutes ago. She hadn't come out since.

He let her have the silence. There wasn't anything he could put into it that would help.

Instead he did what he always did when there was nothing else to do: he worked. He checked the bilge—the pump was losing its argument with the leak by about a bucket an hour, which meant they had maybe two days of river left in the old girl if the rain stayed polite, and the rain had shown no signs whatsoever of being polite. He re-tarred the seam above the waterline where his patch was peeling like sunburned skin. He counted the fuel cans twice, because counting them once had never once made the number larger.

Four cans. Two days of running, three if he babied the engine, and babied engines died anyway. They'd been over this on the sandbar last night, or rather he'd said it aloud to the fire while Freya sat across from him not arguing, which from Freya was its own kind of argument.

*We turn back,* he'd told her. *Today. We take what we can carry—her brother's jacket, the logbook page, whatever tells the story—and we run for the answering station before the monsoon closes the river. That's the play. That's the only play.*

She'd nodded. She'd even agreed. He'd gone to sleep almost believing it.

Then the light came up gray through the rain-fly, and she was standing at the treeline looking at something in the mud, and everything he'd won overnight was already lost.

* * *

"Ulyana."

Her voice had changed overnight. It had been raw yesterday, torn up from crying or from holding back the crying—he genuinely could not tell which, and suspected she couldn't either—but this morning it was level. Flat. The voice of someone who had cried herself empty and found something hard underneath.

He came around the fire pit. "Found something else?"

She pointed at the ground without looking at it. Her eyes were fixed upstream, toward the head of the valley, where the ridgelines went dark under cloud so low it seemed to be leaning on them.

The mud between the cache and the trees was printed with boot tracks. A lot of them—weeks' worth of comings and goings, half-erased by rain, but the freshest set was clean-edged, crisp, cut into the soft ground no more than a day ago. Maybe less. They led away from the camp in a loose, staggering line, one set deep-heeled and dragging, the other long-strided and sure, both of them heading upstream along the bank of the tributary that fed down from the high country.

Toward the mining cut. The one marked on none of the survey maps, the one the logbook's final coordinates bent toward like iron filings toward a magnet.

"One of them's limping," Freya said. "Look at the right foot. Every step, deeper on the outside edge. That's an ankle that's been turned and never set." She crouched, touched the print without quite pressing it. "And this one—" the long-strided track, "—is carrying weight. See how the toes dig? He's loaded down. Pack, or a person."

"A person," Ulyana said, because there was no point pretending otherwise. He'd seen the same thing she had and had been hoping she wouldn't say it. "Or ore. Could be ore."

"It's not ore." She stood. "Ore doesn't leave prints this far apart. Whoever's walking is tall, and whoever's following is hurt, and they're going up that valley together, and my brother's jacket is sitting in a crate fifty meters from here with no body in it." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were dry and terrible and absolutely certain. "He's alive, Ulyana. He went up that valley, and somebody took him there, and those tracks are a day old."

"The monsoon is twelve hours behind us," Ulyana said. "Maybe less. I watched the clouds all night—they're stacking up over the headwaters like a debt collector. When it breaks, this tributary goes from knee-deep to chest-deep in forty minutes. I've seen it happen. The boat is dying, the fuel is short, and every hour we spend climbing is an hour the river spends getting ready to kill us."

"I know."

"And the tracks are a day old. If your brother's up there, he's a day further up than that, in country neither of us knows, ahead of a storm that's going to close every exit out of that valley like a fist."

"I know."

"So we turn back." He heard his own voice do the thing it did when he was trying to be reasonable instead of honest—which was most of the time, historically, and he was beginning to suspect Freya could hear the difference now too. "We take the evidence down. We bring back people with helicopters and radios and the authority to actually do something. That's how this works. That's how it's supposed to work."

"That's how it works when the people you're reporting to aren't the ones who paid for the ore to move in the first place." She said it quietly, without heat, and it landed harder for being quiet. "You read the same logbook page I did. Someone official signed off on 'the provision.' We don't know who. Until we do, the only person I trust to find my brother is standing in front of me arguing with me in the rain."

"I'm not arguing in the rain yet," Ulyana said. "It hasn't started properly."

As if summoned, the first fat drops came down through the canopy and hit the tarps with a sound like slow applause.

Freya didn't smile. But something moved at the corner of her mouth, some exhausted ghost of humor, and then she shouldered her pack—the one she'd repacked sometime before dawn, he realized, while he slept, because of course she had, because she'd known how this conversation would go before he did—and started up the bank toward the treeline.

Ulyana stood in the drizzle and looked at the boat.

*The Veyra Rose*—not her real name; he'd painted over three names before her and would paint over hers too someday, probably—sat against the bank with her seams weeping, her pump chattering, her whole tired hull listing two degrees to port like a drunk leaning on a friend. Ten years. Ten years of water under that hull, and every year of it bought with runs exactly like this one: keep your head down, keep your cargo boring, keep your name out of every ledger that mattered. Turn back. Take the safe money. Live to run again.

That had been the whole architecture of his life. Load-bearing isolation. And here was this woman climbing a hill into a storm after a brother who might already be dead, and the entire structure was groaning.

"Hate this," he informed the boat, conversationally. "Want that noted for the record."

The pump chattered on, noncommittal.

He killed the engine, stowed the fuel cans under the tarp, wedged the bail bucket where he could find it blind in the dark, and covered the whole deck with the spare canvas, weighted at the corners with stones. It wouldn't save her if the river rose over the banks—but nothing would save her then, and at least the rain alone wouldn't get in. It was the closest thing to tucking her in that he knew how to do.

Then he picked up his own pack, which was heavier than it needed to be because he'd loaded it with rope and the good knife and the flare pistol he'd sworn for ten years he didn't own, and followed her uphill.

* * *

The valley narrowed the way valleys do when they're keeping secrets.

The tributary ran fast and brown beside them, already swollen with the storm's first offerings, and the trail—if you could call it that—hugged the bank in a series of muddy switchbacks under dripping canopy. Everything dripped. The leaves dripped, the vines dripped, the air itself seemed to drip. Within an hour Ulyana's shirt was plastered to his back and his boots had achieved the specific squelch of footwear that has stopped being footwear and become a lifestyle.

Freya climbed like she'd been born on a mountainside, which was interesting, because she claimed to have been born in a city, and Ulyana had been filing that contradiction away since the first rapids along with everything else she lied about cheerfully and well. She read the slope the way she read water—picking the line before her feet needed it, testing holds without seeming to test them, wasting nothing. City women did not climb like that. City women did not, for that matter, know what a turned ankle looked like from a footprint, or how to gut a fish, or any of the other things Freya Ashcombe apparently knew and had never mentioned.

He filed it away with the rest. The file was getting thick.

"You're staring at my boots," she said, without turning around.

"I'm staring at your technique. Your boots are a tragedy. Where did you learn to climb?"

"Here and there." A root handhold, a pull, up and over a fallen trunk slick with moss. "Places with hills."

"Mm."

"Don't *mm* me. You're doing the thing where you collect information and store it somewhere I can't reach it."

"I don't do a thing."

"You absolutely do a thing. You've got a whole warehouse of things about me in there. I can hear the shelves creaking." She paused at the top of the switchback, breathing hard but steady, and looked back down at him with an expression that was almost—almost—amused. "For the record, I'll trade. One truth for one truth. Anytime. You pick first whenever you're brave enough."

"I'm plenty brave."

"You're following me up a mountain in a monsoon after telling me it was suicide. That's not bravery, that's bad judgment wearing bravery's coat." She resumed climbing. "Bravery would have been staying with the boat."

"Then I'm proud to be a coward," Ulyana muttered, and hauled himself up the trunk after her, and hated every single step of it—not the climbing, which his body remembered from years he didn't talk about, but the *following*. The having-someone-ahead-of-him. For ten years the only thing he'd followed was the channel markers, and channels didn't walk into avalanches or get taken prisoner by men who moved contraband ore, and channels never made you care whether they came back down.

Every step uphill was a small invoice. *This* is what it costs, each one said. *This, and this.* His lungs kept the ledger. His knees countersigned.

By midday the rain had committed fully. It came down in gray sheets that turned the tributary from a stream into a young river with opinions, and the trail climbed away from the water onto a bench of broken rock where the trees thinned and the wind got its hands on them properly. Ulyana called a halt under an overhang the size of a dining table and they ate cold rice and the last of the dried fish, sitting hip to hip because the overhang wasn't big enough for dignity.

"Tracks are fresher," Freya said, between bites. She'd been reading the ground at every rest stop, and she'd gotten quieter with each reading, the way a person gets quieter approaching something they're afraid to find. "The limp is worse. He's stopping more often. And there—" she nodded upslope, where the trail bent around a shoulder of rock, "—there's blood on a stone. Not much. Old enough to be washed pale."

"Could be anything's blood."

"Yes," she agreed. "Could be."

They sat with that. Below them the tributary roared past, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways. Above them the cloud had eaten the summits entirely. Somewhere in that white blindness was a mining cut dug into the mountain's flank decades ago and abandoned when the ore played out—or when the ore *didn't* play out, and someone decided the playing-out should be quiet.

"Can I ask you something?" Freya said.

"You can ask."

"When we turn around—*if* we turn around, if he's not up there, if we find nothing—you'll take me back down the river. All the way. Even though the money's spent and the fuel's short."

Ulyana looked at her. Rain drummed on the overhang. Somewhere below, distantly, thunder cleared its throat.

"Yes," he said.

"Why?"

Because you bailed water next to me all night with lightning walking the ridgelines and you never once asked me to promise we'd live. Because you told me the true worst thing about yourself and then handed me the rice bag like it was nothing. Because I have spent ten years making sure nobody had a reason to rely on me and you've ruined it in nine days and I don't know how to be a person other people rely on, and I'm finding out at altitude, in a storm, and I resent it enormously.

"Because I said I would," is what he actually said.

Freya studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, the way you nod at a lock that's finally turned, and said, "One day you're going to answer one of my questions honestly, and I worry it'll give me a heart attack."

"I answered honestly just now."

"No, you gave me the smallest true thing that fit the hole. It's a skill. I almost admire it." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and shouldered her pack. "Come on. Light's going."

* * *

They found the cut at dusk, or rather they found what the tracks had been promising all day: a seam of worked stone in the hillside, timber-framed, half-swallowed by creepers, with a spoil heap fanned out below it like a dropped deck of cards. The entrance gaped maybe three meters wide, black as a throat. Water trickled out of it in a thin, constant thread, and the sound it made in the rain was almost like whispering.

The tracks went straight in. Both sets. The limping man and the man carrying his burden, up the spoil heap and into the dark, sometime within the last day.

Freya stood at the bottom of the spoil heap with the rain streaming off her chin, and Ulyana watched her gather herself the way you watch someone decide to open a door they've been afraid of for years.

"Freya."

"Not turning back," she said. "Don't waste the breath."

"I wasn't going to." He shrugged his pack higher and dug out the flashlight, the good one, and the candle stubs, and the length of candlewick he'd braided for a reason he'd explain someday or never. "I was going to say: I go first. You watch my feet. If the roof talks, we both stop. And if anyone's in there who doesn't want to be found—"

"Then they've had a very bad day," Freya said, "because they're about to meet both of us."

Despite everything—the dying boat, the closing sky, the ledger his knees were keeping—Ulyana felt something crack open in his chest that might, in better weather, have been a laugh.

He clicked the flashlight on and stepped into the dark, and the mountain closed around them like a mouth.

Ten steps in, the beam caught something that stopped them both: a wall of stacked stone, recent, mortared with mud—a barrier, built from the inside. And scratched into the wet stone with something sharp, at the height of a kneeling man, four words that made Freya's breath go out of her all at once:

*ASHCOMBE — ALIVE — BELOW.*

Behind the wall, faint but unmistakable, carried on the trickle of water out of the mountain's throat: knocking. Rhythmic. Patient. Someone down there in the dark, hammering on rock, keeping time with hope.
