## 11. The Mining Cut

The tracks went up like a sentence neither of us wanted to finish.

Ulyana had crouched over them for a long time that morning, pressing two fingers into the mud beside a boot print the size of his forearm, and said nothing at all, which from Ulyana was a paragraph. I'd learned to read him by then—the way he read water. A silence with his jaw set meant *this is a bad idea*. A silence with his shoulders dropped meant *this is a bad idea and I've already lost the argument*. This one was the second kind.

"Boots are military," he'd finally said. "Two sets. One dragging on the left side." He'd looked up at the green wall of the valley, where the old mining cut sat folded into the hillside like a scar someone had tried to hide under a bandage of vines. "And it's uphill."

"I noticed the uphill part," I said.

"Just making sure you'd made your peace with it."

I hadn't, particularly. My legs had opinions about the last three days of riverbank scrambling, and all of them were complaints. But somewhere behind us was a cache of contraband ore, my brother's jacket folded in the bottom of a crate like a shed skin, and somewhere ahead—maybe—was the man who'd worn it last. You don't get to be picky about terrain when the alternative is standing still.

So we climbed.

* * *

The Veyra's tributaries had carved this valley long before anyone thought to dig in it, and the miners had followed the cut like a wound. The path was old haulage road, mostly: crushed stone gone soft with moss, rusted rail spikes surfacing through the mud like the knuckles of something buried. Ulyana walked ahead with the machete he'd produced from some impossible pocket, not cutting so much as *parting* the vegetation, the way you'd move furniture in a house you used to live in.

"You've been up here before," I said.

"A long time ago." He didn't turn around. "Different cargo. Different reasons."

I let it go. There was an entire locked room inside Ulyana Thornbury—I'd known that since the dock, since the way he counted cans twice and checked the radio hourly as if the radio might lie to him—and I'd decided somewhere around the grounding on the sandbar that I wasn't going to pick the lock. He'd open it or he wouldn't. People always told you who they were eventually, if you could stand the waiting.

The air changed as we gained elevation. Cooler, wetter, tasting of stone and iron. The monsoon clouds had been building for two days now, stacked over the headwaters like dirty laundry, and the light came down greenish and strange, filtered through leaves that dripped even when it wasn't raining.

"There," Ulyana said, and stopped.

The mining cut opened out of the hillside ahead of us—a horizontal gash maybe four meters high, timbered at the mouth with beams that had gone silver-grey, half-swallowed by creepers. A collapsed trolley lay on its side near the entrance, wheels up, patient as a dead beetle. And beside the entrance, arranged with a care that made my chest tighten, were three stones stacked in a small cairn, and a strip of cloth tied to a stick planted in the mud.

A marker. Someone had marked their trail. Someone who wanted to be found—or wanted whoever came after them to know they'd passed this way.

"That's survey issue cloth," I said. My voice came out flat and too loud in the green quiet. "They use it for sample sites."

Ulyana drew his pistol—I'd almost forgotten he carried one, he wore it the way he wore everything, quietly, underneath—and checked it without drama. "Stay behind me until we know what's in there."

"It's a mine, Ulyana, not an ambush."

"Everything is an ambush until it isn't." But there was no fear in it, just the tired arithmetic of a man who'd survived by assuming the worst and being right often enough. He moved toward the mouth of the cut, machete in his left hand, and I followed close enough to count his breaths.

We were three steps into the darkness when a voice said, "If you're Kessler's people, I should tell you I have a flare gun and absolutely nothing left to lose."

* * *

He was wedged back against the tunnel wall twenty meters in, past the reach of the grey daylight—a big man gone small, is the only way I can describe it. Marcus Oyelaran, expedition team leader, according to the credentials I'd memorized off the survey manifest during those three days Freya-watching-Ulyana-watching-Freya on the dock. Six weeks in the bush had reduced him to a scaffolding of a person: cheekbones like tent poles, hands trembling around a flare gun pointed with more conviction than accuracy. Behind him, curled into the dark like a comma, was a second figure—young, hollow-eyed, wrapped in a space blanket that had given up its silver years ago.

"Dr. Oyelaran?" I said. "Freya Ashcombe. Tomas Ashcombe is my brother. He was on your team."

The flare gun wavered. "Ashcombe." He said the name like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "You're—" His eyes went to Ulyana, to the pistol, back to me. "You're his sister."

"I'm his sister. We're not Kessler's people. We don't even know who Kessler is." A lie of convenience; I knew more than I wanted to by then. "We found your relay post. The logbook page. The coordinates."

"The coordinates." Oyelaran laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the flare gun probably was. "Right. The coordinates. Sit down. Do you have food? Please tell me you have food."

We had food. Ulyana produced hardtack and the tinned fish he counted twice, and I watched a grown man cry over tinned fish, which rearranged something in my chest that I didn't have time to examine. The young one—Oyelaran called her Devi, tech specialist, twenty-three years old—took her ration without a word and ate it in small, precise bites, watching us the whole time with eyes that tracked like instruments but recorded nothing she was willing to share.

It took an hour, and two more rations, and the last of Ulyana's coffee boiled over a spirit stove (he winced at every gram of fuel; I pretended not to notice), before Oyelaran started talking. Once he started, he couldn't seem to stop, the words coming up like floodwater through a cracked foundation.

"We weren't supposed to be moving anything," he said. "That's the thing you have to understand. The survey was clean. Core samples, hydrology, glacier mapping. Boring as church. Then Kessler's people approached us at the resupply point—said the company wanted a favor. Move some crates downstream. Don't log them. Double pay and a quiet bonus for the crew." He rubbed his face with hands that wouldn't stay still. "Half the team said no. Half said yes. That's how it starts, you understand? Not with guns. With a vote."

"And Tomas?"

"Tomas voted no." Something moved through Oyelaran's face—shame, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Loudly. He said it was smuggling and we'd all hang for it, and he was right, and everyone hated him for being right. Then the crates came anyway, and the ones who'd voted yes started looking at each other like the crates were theirs, and—" He gestured vaguely at the tunnel, the valley, the whole catastrophe. "It came apart. Night before last of the survey window. Argument at the cache site. Then a gun, though I swear to God I never saw whose, and everyone ran in different directions, and Kessler's man—Voss, his name was Voss—he loaded what he could onto the barge and went downstream."

My heart was doing something unhelpful. "Tomas. Where is my brother?"

Oyelaran looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide whether I could survive the answer, which was its own kind of insult and also, given the state of me, possibly correct.

"He went after Voss," he said. "On foot, upstream first—they took the valley route because the river fork below the cut was already rising. Voss had a head start and a boat waiting at the fork. Tomas had a rifle he barely knew how to use and Devi's satellite phone, except the phone died the first night." He nodded at the girl, who flinched at being included. "Devi and I came up here because there was an emergency cache from the old mining company—tins, medical, maybe. Half of it was spoiled. We've been here eleven days."

Eleven days. In the dark, half-starved, listening to dripping water and their own heartbeats. I looked at Devi—at the way she held her ration tin like it might still be taken away—and understood, in a sideways flash, why she wasn't talking. Some silences aren't emptiness. They're fullness pressed down so hard it can't move.

"Eleven days," Ulyana repeated quietly. He'd been silent through most of it, crouched by the stove, but I'd seen him cataloging everything—Oyelaran's tremor, Devi's stillness, the layout of the tunnel, the cairn outside. Filing it all in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes. "The fork below the cut. How fast was the river coming up?"

"Fast. Why?"

"Because the monsoon arrives tomorrow, maybe the day after, and when it does, that fork becomes a chute." He stood, knees cracking, and looked at me. "Voss went downstream with the ore and your brother chasing him. Which means your brother is between us and the gorge, on foot, in country that's about to try to kill him."

"Then we go now."

"We," Ulyana said, "are taking two people who can't walk ten kilometers without being carried, down a river that's about to become a weapon, on a boat with a hull I've been apologizing to for a week." He said it flatly, not cruelly—just laying out the arithmetic the way he laid out charts. "But yes. Now."

* * *

Getting them down to the boat was its own small epic. Oyelaran could walk, barely, leaning on my shoulder while Ulyana carried Devi when the slope steepened—she weighed almost nothing and went rigid at being lifted, then, after a moment, gripped his collar with both hands and didn't let go. Ulyana didn't say a word about it. I was learning that this was his love language: complaints withheld.

The *Maren B*—his boat, though I'd never heard him call it anything but *the boat*, as if naming it might jinx it—sat at the cut's landing like a faithful dog that had been told to stay. She looked worse than I remembered. The patchwork along the portside strake had gone the color of old bruises, and she sat low in the water even empty.

"She'll hold," Ulyana said, before either of us asked.

"You counted the cans twice," I said. "You don't think she'll hold."

"I counted the cans twice because I'm a careful man." He handed Devi down into the cockpit with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him. "Get them settled. Bilge first."

Loading four people onto a boat built for one and a half is a lesson in humility. Oyelaran stretched out under the canopy and was asleep before Ulyana finished untying the lines—eleven days of vigilance cashing themselves out all at once. Devi sat in the corner with her knees drawn up, watching the water slide past with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone rehearsing words they may never say.

I took the bow seat, because that had become my station, and because I needed something to do with my hands besides shake.

Ulyana brought us out into the current and turned us downstream, and the valley opened ahead of us—all shadow and rushing water and the first fat drops of the coming rain stippling the surface around us.

"He's alive," I said. It wasn't a question, but I said it like one, offering it up to the river, to Ulyana, to whatever kept books on such things.

"He went after a barge on foot with a bad rifle," Ulyana said, reading the water the way he did, hands light on the wheel. "Either he's alive or he's very thoroughly not, and men like that—" he glanced at me, quick, "—men like your brother, stubborn enough to vote no in front of the whole crew, tend to be alive out of spite."

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

Devi spoke.

It was one word. I nearly missed it—nearly filed it under river sounds—but Ulyana's head turned a degree, and I followed his gaze, and the girl was looking straight at me with her instrument-eyes, and her mouth had actually moved.

"What?" I said gently. "What was it, Devi?"

Her throat worked. Whatever it cost her, she paid it.

"Downstream," she whispered. Her voice sounded like a door opening after years shut. "He said—if anyone came after him—*downstream*. He said he'd leave marks."

Marks. Like the cairn. Like the cloth. My brother, leaving breadcrumbs through a drowning valley for whoever was stubborn enough to follow.

Ulyana pushed the throttle forward, and the *Maren B* leaned into the current, and the rain began in earnest.

Downstream, then. Whatever was waiting there—Kessler, Voss, the whole rotten shipment—we'd beat it to the gorge or die explaining ourselves to the river.

I went aft to check the bilge. The water was gaining on us again, patient as ever.

I picked up the bail bucket.
