## 12. Four Mouths, One Leaking Boat

The *Maren B* had carried two people comfortably, three grudgingly, and four like a woman carrying too many parcels down a narrow stair—everything balanced on the edge of disaster and nothing you could set down.

I stood at the helm in the gray morning light and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since we'd pulled Voss and the tech out of the mining cut. Two hundred liters of freeboard lost to the extra weight. The current running maybe eight knots against us where the valley narrowed. And the bilge pump—the good one, the one I'd rebuilt three seasons ago with parts scavenged off a drowned dredge—running at a wheeze that sounded less like machinery and more like an old man climbing stairs.

Four mouths. One leaking boat. A river rising by the hour.

"Captain." Freya appeared beside me, hair plastered flat, holding two tin mugs of something she called tea and I called hot water that had once been introduced to a tea leaf. "Voss ate half a biscuit. Kept it down."

"That's the best news I've had all week," I said, and meant it, which frightened me a little.

"He's asking about the river. How fast it's rising."

"Tell him the truth. It's rising faster than we're descending, and if that doesn't change by tonight, we'll be rowing uphill."

She looked at me over her mug. "You could lie to him. He's half-delirious."

"Lying to sick men is bad luck. Lying to rivers is worse. I only do one kind, and never before breakfast."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about taking responsibility for human beings: they leak.

Not the way the boat leaks—I understood the boat's leaks intimately, had named them over the years the way farmers name their cows. There was Old Faithful, seeping through the seam below the galley. The Whisperer, which only dripped when the engine was under load. And the new one, unnamed as yet, somewhere forward near the chain locker, which had announced itself two days ago with the modest confidence of a problem intending to become serious.

But people leaked too. They leaked worry, and questions, and needs. They leaked heat from your stove and food from your stores and silence from every corner of a boat that had been, for ten years, exactly as quiet as I'd built it.

Voss lay in the shelter of the wheelhouse on the bench I'd padded with life vests, his leg splinted with a boat hook and duct tape, drifting between sleep and a feverish accounting of everything he should have done differently. The tech—the kid, I'd started thinking of her, though she was probably twenty-three—sat wedged in the corner where the hull curved, knees up, eyes fixed on the middle distance. She hadn't spoken since Freya found her. Not a word. She ate when food was put in front of her, drank when the mug touched her hand, and otherwise occupied herself with being a stone.

"She's not catatonic," Freya told me that first evening aboard, when we'd anchored in the lee of a bluff to keep from fighting the current in the dark. "It's shock. She'll come back when she's ready. You can't rush it."

"I wasn't going to rush it."

"You were looking at her like she was a mechanical fault."

"I look at everything like a mechanical fault. It's how I stay calm."

"Well," she said, "stop looking at her like one. She's a person who watched her whole team die or scatter. She's not a fault. She's a fracture."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I went and checked the bilge, which never asked philosophical questions.

* * *

By midday the rain had settled into its monsoon rhythm—not falling so much as relocating, wholesale, from sky to river. The Veyra had gone the color of strong coffee, thick with silt, carrying whole trees past us at a speed that made my professional pride sit up and take notice. Every hour the water climbed another hand's width up the banks. Every hour the *Maren B* sat lower in it.

I got out my salvage.

Ten years on this river teaches you to see junk differently than other people. Other people see a dead outboard rotting on a bank; I see a bronze impeller housing. Other people see a wrecked skiff; I see plywood, hinges, and enough sealant to keep a widow's boat alive another season. My stern locker held the accumulated archaeology of a decade's scavenging, organized with a rigor that Freya had once described, not unkindly, as "concerning."

I built the first pump from a bilge blower off a derelict patrol launch I'd been meaning to get around to for six years, a length of exhaust hose, and a wooden mounting bracket I cut while the boat pitched underneath me. It moved water. Not fast, but steadily, the way a good crew member works—without complaint, without glory.

The second pump took longer. I cannibalized the washdown hose, sacrificed a spare fuel line, and finally, with genuine grief, gave up the little hand pump I'd kept as a backup to the backup, because a man who has no backup to his backup is a man who has stopped respecting the river.

Freya watched me work, holding fittings, passing tools before I asked for them. She was getting frighteningly good at that.

"You're rigging pumps," she said. "Plural."

"The leak's ahead of one pump. Maybe ahead of two."

"And if it's ahead of two?"

"Then we bail. Buckets. In shifts."

"We're forty kilometers from the gorge."

"Forty-one," I said. "And the gorge is the easy part. It's the flats after it that'll kill us, if the water spreads out and we lose the channel."

She was quiet a moment, watching the rain hammer the deck. Then: "Teach me the bail pattern. If it comes to buckets, I want everyone already knowing their shift."

Everyone. She said it like it was the most natural word in the world. Four days ago it had been *you and me*, and even that had been a concession wrung out of me by circumstance. Now there was an *everyone*, and it lived on my boat, and ate my biscuits, and I couldn't remember exactly when I'd stopped minding.

* * *

I taught them the bail pattern that afternoon, anchored in the eddy behind a gravel point, because some lessons you don't give in moving water.

Voss propped himself against the wheelhouse door and listened with the fierce attention of a man who needed to be useful and had almost no body left to be useful with. The kid sat where she always sat. But she was watching. I noticed that. Whatever else was shut down in her, the part that watched—the surveyor's part, the technician's part—was still running.

"Water comes in low, forward, port side," I said, tapping the deck. "So the bucket line runs from there, aft, over the transom. Freya takes first position, Voss calls depth every ten minutes—if he can't call it, Freya calls it. Third position rotates."

"Rotates between whom?" Freya asked. "There are only four of us, and you need to steer."

"There are three of you and one of me, and I'll take third position whenever we anchor. Which will be often, because I'm not running this river blind in monsoon rain unless the alternative is drowning."

"You said last night we might not be able to afford to stop."

"I say a lot of things at night. Nights are for pessimism. Days are for logistics."

That earned me something rare: a short laugh from Voss, which turned into a coughing fit, which turned into Freya hovering over him with the particular ferocity she reserved for people she was afraid of losing. I pretended to check the anchor line and gave them their privacy.

When I straightened up, the kid was looking at me.

Not through me, past me, at the middle distance where she usually lived—but at me. Her eyes went from my face to the bucket in my hand to the deck seams I'd tapped, and then she did something that stopped my breath for a second: she raised one finger and pointed, deliberately, at a spot on the deck two meters forward of where I'd indicated.

Forward. Port side. Exactly where the new leak actually was.

I'd said low-forward-port as a general truth. She'd corrected me to the specific one. Which meant she'd been listening to the hull. All this time, silent as cargo, she'd been lying against the planking with her ear down, reading the water coming in the way I read it coming past the sandbars.

"That's right," I said quietly. "That's exactly right."

She looked away again. But something in the set of her shoulders had changed—a degree of angle, a millimeter of uncurling. A stone deciding, very slowly, that it might be a person after all.

* * *

We ran the afternoon in shifts of a different kind: me at the wheel reading the brown water for the deep channel, Freya on the bow with a sounding pole calling depths, the kid—her name was Sefa, Voss had told us, Sefa Lindqvist, communications tech—in the bilge with the bucket, working in steady rhythm whether the pumps kept pace or not.

She didn't speak. But she developed a language anyway. Two knocks on the deck meant the water was gaining. Three meant she'd gotten ahead of it. Once, when a log the size of a canoe came spinning out of the murk toward our bow and I slewed hard to miss it, she knocked four times, rapid, and I swear on my mother's grave it was laughter.

"She's talking to you," Freya said, coming back from the bow, soaked to the skin and grinning like the rain was a personal gift.

"She's knocking code."

"She's talking. There's a difference. Ask her what four means sometime."

"I know what four means."

"Do you?" Freya leaned against the wheelhouse frame, watching Sefa bail with the metronome patience of someone who had decided, for reasons of her own, to keep this boat afloat. "Because I've been studying glaciers for nine years, and I'll tell you what I've learned about things that look frozen. They're not frozen. They're moving. Slowly. Enormously. And if you stand still long enough, you find out they were headed somewhere the entire time."

I steered. The rain fell. Somewhere behind us, Sefa knocked twice—the water gaining—and then, unprompted, knocked three more times, which wasn't any code I knew, and which Freya apparently understood perfectly, because she laughed and went aft to relieve her.

A keeper of people. The phrase arrived in my head uninvited, wearing my grandmother's voice, from some old country proverb she used to mutter over her stew: *the river gives you passengers, not because you asked, but because you were going anyway.*

I'd spent ten years making sure I was never going anywhere anyone needed to reach.

* * *

We anchored at dusk in the mouth of a tributary, bow upstream, riding the floodwater's confusion. The rain eased to a drizzle that felt almost apologetic. I cooked rice with the last of the dried fish, and the smell did what smells do—it assembled all four of us into the wheelhouse without anyone issuing orders.

Voss ate sitting up now, color back in his face, his fever broken sometime during the day. He looked older than his fifty-odd years and lighter, too, like a man who had put down something heavy and wasn't sure yet whether to feel relieved or bereft.

"The gorge," he said, between bites. "You think Kessler's barge can make it?"

"If anyone's barge can make it, his can. He didn't get rich running boats that sink." I paused. "No offense to mine."

"Your boat is sinking," Freya pointed out.

"My boat is *taking on water*. Sinking is a state of mind."

Sefa made the four-knock sound against her mug.

We all looked at her. She looked back, and then—deliberately, her first voluntary act of communication aimed at more than one person—she set down the mug, picked up her finger, and began tracing on the deck boards: lines, angles, a widening shape.

"A map," Freya breathed.

Sefa nodded. Her finger drew the gorge, narrow, then the flats beyond, and then she tapped a spot on the far side of the flats and looked up at Voss with an expression of urgent question.

"The lower station," Voss said slowly. "The emergency relay. Below the gorge." His voice cracked. "Sefa—you sent a signal from there? Before the cut?"

She shook her head. Tapped the spot again. Then drew, with terrible economy, a small square beside it, and inside the square, a shape I recognized because I'd seen it burned into a cache crate two days ago: the mark of the provision.

"She saw barges there," Freya whispered. "At the lower station. Staged. Waiting for the calving window."

Below the gorge. Where the ice would open the road for thirty-six hours and close it after. Everything—the ore, Kessler, whatever remained of the expedition's rotten bargain—was staged at the exact door we had to pass through, timed to the exact window we had to run.

The wheelhouse went quiet except for the rain and the knock of water against the hull. Old Faithful, keeping time beneath our feet like a patient heart.

I looked around at them: the survivor with his splinted leg, the girl who spoke in knocks, the woman who had walked onto my dock with a satellite photo and refused to leave. None of them were mine to keep. All of them were on my boat. And the river, rising all night in the dark ahead of us, did not care which.

"All right," I said, and heard my own voice settle into a register I barely recognized—steady, certain, responsible for more than myself. "New plan. We run the gorge at first light tomorrow, ahead of the window, ahead of Kessler if we can. And when we come out the other side—" I tapped the little square Sefa had drawn, "—we go look at what's waiting there ourselves."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up her mug, held it slightly raised—an absurd, formal little toast—and knocked once.

One knock. I decided, on the spot, that it meant *aye*.

Outside, the rain thickened again, and the river rose in the dark, and for the first time in ten years I fell asleep listening not to the sound of water coming into my boat, but to the sound of three other people breathing, keeping watch in shifts, keeping my home afloat.
