## 13. The Man Who Took It

The fork came out of the fog the way bad news always did—suddenly, and after you'd already committed to something else.

We had been running the eastern channel for an hour, Ulyana reading the water with that half-lidded attention he wore like a uniform, when the river split ahead of us around a long gravel island furred with bamboo. To the left, the water ran wide and brown and lazy. To the right, it narrowed, quickened, and disappeared into a stand of deadfall that looked like it had been arguing with the current for decades.

"Left's the survey channel," Ulyana said, not looking up from the wheel. "Right goes down to the old logging camps. Nobody runs the right fork unless they're hauling something they don't want weighed."

I was about to ask how he knew which one we wanted when he raised one hand—not for me, but toward the left channel, upstream, where the morning haze was still burning off the water.

"Smoke," he said.

I smelled it a moment later. Woodsmoke, and under it something sharper, like coal oil. Then the sound arrived: engines. Not one engine. Two, working hard against the current, the way engines sound when they're being asked to do more than they were built for.

Ulyana cut our speed to a crawl and slid us into the shadow of the island's downstream tip, behind a curtain of overhanging cane. He did it without drama, without even straightening his shoulders, the way a man does a thing he has done so many times it has stopped being a decision. I found myself holding my breath anyway.

Around the bend, trailing a wake of yellow foam, came the barge.

It was bigger than anything I'd imagined—forty feet if it was an inch, flat-decked, patched in a dozen places with steel plates that had been painted three different colors by three different owners and never reconciled. A wheelhouse sat forward like a tin hat. Cargo netting sagged amidships over a load hidden under tarps lashed down with what looked like every rope on board. And aft, standing at the rail with a rifle slung across his back like a man who wanted it seen rather than used, was a figure I recognized from a photograph I had studied until the paper went soft.

My brother. Tomas.

He was thinner than in the photo. Bearded, sunburned, moving with the careful economy of someone who had learned that sudden movements cost him. His wrists were bare, but there was a band of paler skin where a watch used to be, and when he turned to look back at the wheelhouse—a habit, I realized, of a man checking whether he was being watched—I saw the bruise-yellow shadow along his jaw that no amount of beard could hide.

He was alive. He was alive, and he was standing on a smuggler's barge in the middle of the Veyra, and I made a sound that I would be embarrassed by for the rest of my life—a small, strangled thing, half gasp and half sob—and grabbed the gunwale with both hands because my knees had briefly resigned their post.

"Easy," Ulyana said quietly. He didn't tell me to be quiet. He just put his body between me and the sightline, casually, the way you'd shift your weight in a crowd, and let me breathe.

* * *

The barge passed us at perhaps two hundred meters. Close enough that I could hear voices carrying over the water—the flat, bored cadence of men doing a job they'd done too many times. Close enough that I counted them. Four on deck besides Tomas. One in the wheelhouse, visible through the glass as a shape and a cigarette ember.

And then the fifth man came out of the wheelhouse.

I knew him before I understood that I knew him. That's the strange arithmetic of recognition—that the body files its answer before the mind has finished reading the question. He stepped onto the deck and the light caught his face and something in my chest clenched like a fist around ten years ago.

Kessler.

Not the name—I hadn't known his name then, only his function—but the face. The heavy jaw, the gray going to white at the temples in two neat wings, the way he stood with his weight settled low, planted, like a man who expected the floor to try something. I had seen him across a courtroom corridor in Mereth Harbor, seven years ago, flanked by lawyers, watching me the way a farmer watches weather. I had testified against his operation. I had put four of his people away. I had run afterward, first to one city and then another and finally to another country entirely, telling myself the distance was sufficient, that men like him operated within borders the way fish operate within aquariums.

He had crossed the border. He was fifty meters away. And he was carrying my brother's freedom somewhere inside that barge like ballast.

"He's seen boats like ours before," Ulyana murmured. His eyes never left the barge. "Keep below the cane line."

"Ulyana." My voice came out strange to me, flattened, like sound heard underwater. "That's Kessler. That's—" I stopped, restarted. "I testified against him. Years ago. In Mereth. He knows my face."

Something shifted in Ulyana's expression. Not surprise—I don't think anything surprised him anymore, or if it did, he'd built a levee against showing it. But there was a recalculation happening behind his eyes, fast and thorough, the same look he got when the river changed its mind about a channel overnight.

"The brother," he said slowly. "Your brother. Working cargo for Kessler."

"Not working." I heard myself say it with a certainty that outran my evidence. "Look at him. Look at where he's standing—he's not crew, crew doesn't stand at the rail like luggage. They took him off the mountain. The expedition split over the ore, the ones who lost went up the valley, and Kessler's people took whoever was left to keep the rest quiet. Or to find out what the others know."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. The barge's wake reached us, rocking the boat gently, and the cane hissed against the hull.

"You're sure it's him," he said. "Kessler. After seven years."

"I'd know him anywhere." And then, because honesty between us had become a currency we'd started trading in and I couldn't bear to devalue it now: "I hoped I was wrong. When you said a smuggler took the shipment, I told myself it would be some local outfit, some small-time operator. Not him. Not the reason I've been running for seven years."

Ulyana exhaled through his nose. "Well," he said, with the driest gentleness I'd yet heard from him, "the river does enjoy a joke."

* * *

We held position behind the island while the barge's engine note faded upstream—or rather, we thought it faded. Ulyana cocked his head, listening the way he listened to everything, and frowned.

"That's wrong," he said. "They're slowing."

We crept forward along the cane line until we could see past the island's upstream tip. The barge had come about—badly, wallowing, the work of a pilot who knew the freight better than the water—and was nosing toward the left bank where a side slough entered the main channel through a screen of willows. A mooring. They were tying up.

"Why?" I whispered.

"Could be waiting for daylight on the narrows. Could be meeting someone." Ulyana's jaw worked. "Could be they spotted our smoke this morning and are being careful. We burned wood last night. It carries."

The guilt of that landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone. Our fire. Our little fire, the one we'd allowed ourselves because the rain had been cold and Ulyana had, uncharacteristically, said yes. I watched him arrive at the same conclusion and file it away without comment, and somehow that made it worse.

Tomas was still at the rail. From this distance I couldn't read his face, but I could read his posture, and his posture was a man memorizing a landscape. He looked at the slough mouth. He looked at the far bank. He looked downstream—at us, though he couldn't have known it, at the exact stretch of brown water where we lay hidden—and lingered there a beat longer than chance would allow.

Then one of the deck crew called something to him and he turned away, obedient, and the sight of it hurt worse than any bruise could have.

"We can't just sit here," I said.

"No," Ulyana agreed. "We also can't charge a forty-foot barge with five armed men aboard using a leaking riverboat, a flare gun, and my winning personality."

"I wasn't suggesting—" I stopped. Because part of me *was* suggesting exactly that, and we both knew it, and the part of me suggesting it was the part that had spent three days on a dock refusing to leave, and the part that had climbed a mining cut in the rain, and the part that had testified in Mereth Harbor knowing exactly what it would cost. "What I'm suggesting is that we follow them. At a distance. Until we understand the situation."

"And then?"

"And then I go aboard."

The words came out of me with a calm that frightened me slightly, the way a fever breaks calm right before it breaks everything else. Ulyana turned and looked at me fully for the first time since the barge had appeared. Rain was starting again, fine and needling, beading on his shoulders.

"Under a flag of talk," he said. It wasn't quite a question. He'd been expecting this, I think, since the relay station—since the logbook page, since *the provision*, since the whole rotten architecture of this trip had revealed itself to be built on cargo that moved at night. "You want to parley."

"I want my brother."

"Those aren't the same thing," he said, not unkindly. "That's the trap. You'll walk up that gangplank thinking they're the same thing, and Kessler will know the difference before your second sentence."

"Then I'll learn to say the right second sentence."

He looked at me for a long time. The rain ticked on the cabin roof. Somewhere upstream, faintly, the barge's generator changed pitch.

"You recognize him," Ulyana said at last. "Does he recognize you? Seven years is a long time. People change."

"People like him hire people to remember faces," I said. "I was the witness who didn't fold. There were maybe six of us who didn't fold. He sent flowers to my hotel room the day after the verdict. No card. Just flowers. It was the most frightening thing anyone has ever done to me, and I once fell through a crevasse bridge."

Ulyana absorbed this. I watched him absorb it—watched the information settle into him the way ballast settles, changing the boat's whole trim.

"Flowers," he repeated. "No card."

"It said everything a card would have."

"And now he has your brother." Ulyana rubbed his jaw, staring upstream at the smudge of the barge behind its willows. "Which means he either doesn't know who you are, and your brother is leverage against the surviving expedition members. Or he knows exactly who you are, and he's been leaving your brother alive as bait on a hook, waiting to see what swims upriver after him."

The second possibility sat between us in the rain, dripping.

"He wouldn't bother," I said. "Seven years. I'm old news."

"Men like him don't have old news," Ulyana said. "They have accounts. Accounts stay open until they're settled." He paused. "Believe me. I have one."

It was the closest he'd ever come to opening his own ledger, and he closed it immediately, turning back to the wheel, but I felt the door swing and shut and I filed it away next to all the other things Ulyana Thornbury almost said.

* * *

We argued about it for an hour, which is to say I argued and Ulyana deployed objections with the unhurried patience of a man building a sandbag wall one bag at a time. Too risky. Too many guns. Too little boat. The narrows ahead, the monsoon behind, the leak gaining on us by the hour. Every objection was correct. Every objection missed the point, and I told him so, and he looked at me with an expression that was equal parts exasperation and something warmer that he pretended wasn't there.

"You're asking me to help you walk onto that barge," he said.

"I'm asking you to get me close enough to choose. That's all I've ever asked of anyone on this river. A choice."

He was quiet for a while after that. The rain thickened, then thinned. Upstream, the barge sat moored and patient as a spider, its tarps darkening with water.

"There's a landing above the slough," he said finally. "Old logging ramp. I can put you ashore there and hold off in the channel, out of rifle range but close enough to make noise if things go badly. Noise is about all I'd be good for."

"That's not nothing."

"It's less than nothing if you're dead." He said it flatly, without drama, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "Ten years I've kept this boat and myself out of other people's stories. One season with you and I'm proposing landing zones for a boarding action against an ore smuggler." He shook his head, but there was no regret in it that I could see. Only a kind of rueful astonishment, like a man discovering water in a compartment he'd sealed years ago.

"Ulyana." I waited until he looked at me. "If he knows who I am—if this is the hook—then walking aboard isn't brave, it's stupid, and I know the difference. I've had seven years to study the difference. But if he doesn't know, then Tomas is a hostage in a negotiation I might actually be able to end, because I'm the only person on this river who knows what Kessler fears."

"What does he fear?"

"Paper," I said. "Testimony. The record. Men like him survive on silence, and I am a very loud archive. He can't afford for me to reach a magistrate with what I know about this shipment. Which means, if he recognizes me, I'm worth more to him talking than dead—and if he doesn't recognize me, I'm just a woman with money and a missing relative, and that's a conversation I can start."

Ulyana stared at me. Then he laughed—one short, disbelieving bark, quickly suppressed, as if the sound had escaped custody.

"You've rehearsed this," he said.

"I've had three days on a dock and two weeks on this boat," I said. "What else did you think I was doing during the night shifts?"

"Bailing."

"Multitasking."

He shook his head again, slower this time, and I saw the decision land in him before he spoke it—the way his hands settled on the wheel, the way his chin came up a degree. Ten days ago that face had been a locked door. Now I could watch the bolts draw back one by one, and it occurred to me that I might be the first person in a decade to be standing on this side of them when they did.

"All right," he said. "Logging ramp. We drift in quiet, I put you ashore, and you walk down to that barge under a flag of talk. But Freya—" and here his voice dropped into a register I hadn't heard from him before, low and absolutely level, the voice of a man stating the terms of a river, "—you carry nothing they can take off you that tells them anything. No photos, no coordinates, no names beyond yours and his. You give them nothing they can use on the people up the valley. Nothing they can use on us. Whatever happens on that deck, whatever he offers you or threatens you with—you come back to this boat and we decide together. That's the price. Not your safety. Your *return*."

"Together," I repeated.

"You've been alone longer than I have," he said. "I know how it fits. I know how it feels like the safe shape. But the safe shape is how they get you—one choice at a time, each one reasonable, none of them shared." He looked at me steadily. "So. Together, or I turn this boat around and we run for the settlement and you can hate me the rest of your life, which, frankly, I'd deserve less than you'd think."

I laughed despite everything—a wet, cracked laugh, but real. "You drive a hard bargain, Captain Thornbury."

"I'm told it's the only kind I drive."

* * *

We came up on the logging ramp with the engine at a whisper and the rain covering our sound, and the whole time the barge grew in the willow screen ahead of us—its tarps, its rust, its tin-hat wheelhouse, and the small figure at the rail who was my brother, alive, twenty minutes' walk away, and I had to stand at the gunwale with my hands flat on the planks and breathe like a glaciologist and not like a sister, because sisters would run, and running was the one thing I could not do.

Ulyana killed the engine and let the current set us in. He handed me the flare gun, which I stowed in my coat, and a length of white cloth torn from an old flour sack, which I tied to a broken oar. A flag of talk. It looked absurd. It looked like the most serious object I had ever held.

"For what it's worth," Ulyana said, steadying the boat against the ramp piling, "if he doesn't remember your face, he'll remember it after today. So make the first impression count."

"That's your advice? Make it count?"

"It's the best I've got. The rest is water depth." He hesitated, and then did the thing I would think about for a long time afterward: he reached over and gripped my shoulder, once, hard, and let go. "Come back to the boat, Freya."

"I will."

And I stepped onto the ramp, into the rain, and started walking toward the past—which was waiting for me at the rail of a rust-streaked barge, wearing a familiar face, and smiling like it had known all along that I'd come.
