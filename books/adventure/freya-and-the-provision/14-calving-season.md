## 14. Calving Season

Ulyana heard the glacier before he understood it.

They had come down from the mining cut at dawn, the two survivors lashed between them like cargo that breathed—Voss the team leader walking on legs that shook but held, the young tech carried in a sling Freya had rigged from a cargo net and pure stubbornness. The river had swallowed the sound of their descent, all its monsoon voice and fury, and so when the sound came, it came through the soles of Ulyana's boots first.

A crack. Deep, tectonic, like the riverbed itself clearing its throat.

Then a report that rolled up the valley and came back off the far ridgeline a full three seconds later, and Ulyana stopped dead on the shingle with the tech's weight half off his shoulder and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the gorge first appeared on his charts, ten years ago, inked in his own hand.

*Calving.*

"Tell me that was thunder," Freya said beside him. She had the brother's jacket folded under one arm—she hadn't let go of it since the cache, wouldn't let it out of her sight, wouldn't let it get wet, as if a jacket could be kept safe the way a person hadn't been. Her eyes were on the head of the valley, where the glacier came down between the walls like a tongue of white fire, blue-veined, enormous, and utterly wrong in the way it was moving.

"That was the glacier deciding to let go of something," Ulyana said. "That was the beginning of calving season."

"You said—" Voss started, and stopped, and started again. His voice was a ruin. "You said the gorge closes."

"I said the gorge *opens* first." Ulyana shifted the tech's weight, resettled the sling. The young man's eyes were open, tracking the glacier with a flat, unblinking attention that Ulyana had learned, in the last day, to read as the tech's whole vocabulary. "Ice dams don't form from standing ice. They form when the tongue calves off in a surge, and the bergs jam the narrows, and the river backs up behind them until there's no river at all, just a lake with ambitions. Before that jam, for a day, maybe two, the channel below the tongue runs wide and fast and *empty*, because everything that would normally choke it has been flushed or hasn't fallen yet. It's the only window. And it's closing."

"How long?" Freya asked. No tremor in it. He was beginning to understand that the tremor happened later, in private, in the small hours, and that she spent the rest of the time paying for it in advance.

Ulyana looked at the sky—low, bruised, monsoon-heavy—and at the water, which had come up another hand's width since morning and was running the color of milk-frothed slate, and at the barge.

The barge.

It sat at the river fork a kilometer downstream, moored against the cutbank where the smuggler Kessler had anchored it two days ago, and it was the ugliest, most beautiful thing Ulyana had seen in ten years of ugly beautiful things. Forty meters of river iron, a hold built for ore, a wheelhouse like a tin hat, and a beam—this was the part that mattered, this was the part he kept coming back to like a tongue to a broken tooth—a beam three times the width of his own poor leaking *Veyra's Daughter*, which was at this moment tied to a rootball upstream, bailing at a rate that had stopped being a joke sometime around the second night.

The barge held the channel. There was no other way to say it. The gorge below the glacier ran through a slot of rock, and in calving season that slot filled with ice the size of churches, and a boat with Ulyana's beam would be crushed like an egg against a wall, or swamped by the wake of a calving, or simply swallowed. The barge was heavy enough to shoulder through brash ice. The barge had a bow built to take a hit. The barge was, in every way that mattered, the only vehicle on this river that could get eight people—eight, he counted them again, Voss and the tech and Freya and her brother Daniel, who was alive, *alive*, on that deck, a prisoner of a man Freya had testified against a decade ago—out of the valley before the window shut and the river closed like a book.

And the barge belonged to Kessler.

* * *

They held a council of war on the shingle, because there was nowhere else, and because Freya insisted on it, and because Ulyana had discovered in the last two weeks that he would do a great many things for Freya Ashcombe that he would not do for anyone else, including things that made him want to lie down in the river.

Voss sat on a stone with the tech's head in his lap. Freya stood. Daniel—freed in the chaos at the narrows, hauled off the barge in the confusion, half-starved and furious about it—stood beside her, arms crossed, looking at Ulyana with the particular skepticism of a man who had just been told that his rescue involved a plan.

"Say it again," Daniel said. "Slowly."

"Run the gorge alongside Kessler," Ulyana said. "His wake, his ice-breaking, his channel. We stay close enough to use the path he cuts and far enough that when the calving comes down, we're not in his shadow. Then, at the narrows—where the gorge pinches to its tightest, where he'll have to slow to thread it—we take him."

"Take him how? You have a boat with a hole in it, a rifle with—" Daniel looked at Ulyana. "How many rounds?"

"Three."

"Three rounds. Against a barge crew of—"

"Two, that I've seen. Kessler and a deckhand. He sent the rest away or they left; a man like that travels light when he's running." Ulyana held up three fingers, then folded one down, then another, until only the middle one stood, which felt about right. "One for the wheelhouse window, one for the steering, one in case I miss twice."

"And if you miss all three?"

"Then I'll be having a very bad day on a river that's already having one with me."

Daniel turned to his sister. "This is insane."

"This is the only exit," Freya said. "You've been on that barge. You know what he intends. He'll run the gorge, he'll make the lowlands, he'll sell the ore, and he'll disappear the way he always disappears, and everyone on this shingle will spend the rest of their lives looking over their shoulders." She said it evenly, but Ulyana heard the thing underneath it, the thing she'd told him in the squall-shelter with the rain hammering the tarp: *I ran for ten years. I'm done running.* "Or we take the window, and we take him, and we come out the bottom of this valley with the evidence and the man both."

"The tech can't walk," Voss said quietly. His hand moved on the young man's hair, an absent, paternal gesture that seemed to surprise Voss himself every time he caught himself doing it. "The boat—your boat, Captain—can it even carry us to the narrows?"

Ulyana thought about lying. He had gotten very good at lying over ten years, the way a man gets good at any craft he practices daily. And then he thought about the leak, and how he'd confessed it to Freya on the second night, and how she hadn't flinched, and how the world had not, in fact, ended, and how strange and light he'd felt afterward, like a man who'd set down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing.

"The boat will carry us to the narrows," he said, "if we bail in shifts and if the river is kind, and the river has not been kind to me since 1974. But the boat is not the plan. The boat is the taxi. The barge is the bus. We get everyone aboard the barge at the narrows, we put Kessler and his deckhand over the side or into irons, and we ride her down to the first magistrate with the ore in her hold as evidence. Then I sell the barge and retire to a life of honest larceny somewhere with better weather."

The tech made a sound. Everyone turned. It took Ulyana a moment to recognize it, because he'd never heard it before from the young man: a short, dry, unmistakable huff of laughter. The first sound he'd made since the mining cut. Freya's hand went to her own mouth.

"Well," Ulyana said, "at least someone appreciates my retirement plan."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and the afternoon spent itself quickly, the way afternoons do when a glacier is counting down behind you.

Ulyana worked on the boat. There was a particular misery in caulking a seam you know is losing, and he did it anyway, with the patience of long habit, driving oakum into the planking below the waterline while the river tried to take the mallet out of his hands. Freya bailed. Daniel and Voss carried the tech down to the boat in the sling, an operation that involved more swearing than engineering and ended with the young man installed in the stern on a nest of tarpaulins, where he immediately began, without being asked, to sort Ulyana's tool roll by size.

"He does that," Voss said, watching. "Ordered things. It's how he holds on."

Ulyana nodded and said nothing, because there was a lump in his throat that had no business being there over a stranger organizing wrenches, and because the glacier chose that moment to calve again.

This one was close. The crack came first, a sound like the sky tearing along a seam, and then the whole face of the tongue seemed to lean forward, and then a section of it the size of a courthouse simply left the glacier and fell, and the fall took four full seconds, and the splash threw spray two hundred feet up the valley walls, and the wave came downriver like a gray wall with opinions.

"Boat!" Ulyana roared, and they ran, all of them, hauling lines, and he got the *Veyra's Daughter* swung bow-on to the wave with the last of his strength and rode it up and up and up, the whole river lifting them like a hand, and then down, down, the trough after it so deep he could see the stones of the shingle exposed and dripping, and then the water came back and the boat wallowed and everyone was on their knees and no one had been washed overboard, which on this river counted as a victory.

When the water settled, the channel below the glacier had changed. Where there had been a bar, there was now a hole. Where there had been a bend, there was now a straight. And downstream, at the fork, the barge's riding light had come on—Kessler, too, had felt the wave. Kessler, too, was doing his arithmetic.

"He'll run at first light," Freya said. She had appeared at Ulyana's elbow, soaked to the waist, hair plastered flat, and she had never in his life looked less like a glaciologist and more like exactly what she was, which was a woman who had decided something. "He won't wait. Every hour the calving accelerates, the window shrinks. He knows it. He'll want the narrows at slack water, which is dawn."

"Then we run at first light too," Ulyana said. "Half a kilometer behind. Close enough to read his line through the ice, far enough that we can stop when he stops."

"And at the narrows?"

Ulyana looked at the rifle, lying under its tarp. Three rounds. He looked at the tech, who had finished with the tools and was now watching the glacier with that flat attention, and at Voss, who was watching the tech, and at Daniel, who was watching his sister, and at Freya, who was watching Ulyana as if his answer were the only fixed point on a river that had stopped having any.

He thought about the man he had been two weeks ago, alone on a sandbar, telling himself the static on the radio wasn't his problem. He thought about how much of that man he still was, and how much of him had been bailed out with the bilgewater, and what was left.

"Freya," he said. "When we come alongside at the narrows—when it's time to take him—what's the plan? Your plan. Because I've seen your face when you look at that barge, and I know you have one, and I'd rather hear it from you now than be surprised by it at the worst possible moment."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. The glacier groaned upstream, settling, thinking.

"I board her," she said at last. "Under a flag of talk. I trade the cache's location for Daniel. And then—" and here her face did something complicated, something that closed like a ledger, "—and then we'll see what Kessler believes."

Ulyana looked at her for a long time. Ten years of reading water had taught him to read people too, or at least to read the ones who were trying to read him, and everything in him that had kept him alive for a decade stood up and said: *she is planning to trade herself, you fool, she is planning to pay with the only currency Kessler has ever wanted, which is her, her silence, her surrender, and she will do it because it's the fastest way to keep the rest of you safe.*

He said none of this. He filed it, the way he filed everything, in the deep water where he kept the things he wasn't ready to say.

"First light, then," was all he said. "Get some sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we run the gorge."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He stood the middle watch instead, alone in the wheelhouse with the river muttering below him and the glacier glowing upstream like a lamp behind a curtain, and he did what he always did on the middle watch, which was to talk to the boat.

"Old girl," he said, running his thumb along the wheel's worn spokes, "we're going to do something stupid tomorrow, and I need you to forgive me for it. We're going to put you in a channel built for a ship three times your beam, behind a murderer's barge, under a calving glacier, with a hull that's held together mostly by oakum and stubbornness. And if we come out the bottom, I'll re-plank you stem to stern. New name, if you want one. Something dignified. Something that isn't named after a river that's currently trying to kill everyone I—" He stopped. He heard the sentence he'd been about to say. *Everyone I love.* He let it go unfinished, the way he let most true things go unfinished, and patted the wheel instead. "Well. Everyone. You know."

The glacier calved twice more in the night, and each time the wave came down and the boat rose and fell, and each time Ulyana watched the barge's light at the fork, steady, patient, waiting for dawn the same as he was.

At grey light, the sky the color of dishwater, he woke them all, and they cast off, and the current took them toward the gorge with the gentleness of a hand on the small of the back, ushering them toward something none of them could take back.

The narrows opened ahead like a throat. And Ulyana, watching Freya check and re-check the signal lamp in her hands, understood with perfect clarity that the plan she had told him was not the plan she had—and that whatever she intended to do on that barge, she intended to do it alone, and that he had perhaps one hour to decide whether he was the kind of man who let her.

The glacier, behind them, began to calve again.
