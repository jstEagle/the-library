## 15. Parley at the Narrows

The barge sat in the narrows like a fist that had learned to float.

Freya saw it first from the bow—Ulyana had let her take the morning watch, a concession he'd made without admitting it was one—and she stood there for a long moment before calling back to the wheelhouse, because some part of her brain insisted that if she didn't say it out loud, it wouldn't be true.

"Boat," she called. "Big boat. Dead ahead."

Ulyana appeared beside her so fast she suspected he'd been standing just inside the door the whole time, the way he did when he wanted to be near her but not *near* her. He raised his binoculars, and Freya watched his face do something complicated.

"Kessler's barge," he said. "Has to be. Nothing else on this river is that ugly."

"It has a certain grandeur."

"It has a crane bolted to its deck and a tarp over what I'd bet my hull is ore. That's not grandeur, that's ambition." He lowered the binoculars. "There's your brother."

Freya took the glasses. It took her three tries to steady them enough—her hands had opinions of their own now—but then there he was: Tomas, moving crates along the deck under the eye of a man with a rifle slung loose and casual as a scarf. Thinner than the last photo she had of him, which was four years old and taken at a birthday party where he was mid-laugh. But upright. Moving. Alive.

Alive was a word she'd been rationing for weeks, spending it carefully, afraid it would run out. Now here it was, walking around on a smuggler's deck in the rain, and she found she couldn't spend it fast enough.

"He's working," Ulyana said. Not unkindly. Just laying it out flat, the way he laid everything out, like cargo to be weighed.

"I know what it looks like."

"I'm saying they kept him alive because he's useful. Useful people get fed. It's grim arithmetic, but it's arithmetic in your favor." He paused. "For now."

*For now.* The narrows were closing around them—the gorge walls leaning in from both sides, the river compressing itself between them, ice from the calving above already drifting down in ragged white plates that knocked against the hull like polite but persistent creditors. Thirty-six hours, Ulyana had said. Maybe less. After that, the channel would dam shut with ice and every boat on this stretch of river would be a very expensive sculpture.

And Kessler's barge held the only water wide enough for two vessels to pass.

They'd argued about it the night before, in the low murmur they used whenever the survivors were sleeping—which was most of the time, because half-starved people sleep like stones. Ulyana had laid out the geometry of the narrows on the galley table with salt and dried beans, and the geometry said what the geometry always said: you don't get through alongside another boat unless the other boat lets you.

"So we ask nicely," Freya had said.

"Men like Kessler interpret *nicely* as *weakly*."

"Then I'll ask firmly. With a flag of talk. It's an old custom. Even pirates honored it."

"Kessler isn't a pirate. Pirates have standards."

That had been the whole of their strategy meeting, more or less, and now here they were, and the strategy was still just a woman in a borrowed rain jacket holding up a white rag that Ulyana had torn off the bottom hem of a flour sack.

"You don't have to do this," Ulyana said. He said it quietly, so the wheelhouse microphone wouldn't catch it. Behind them, through the open hatch, they could hear Rennick—the team leader, hollowed out by weeks in the valley—snoring with the desperate commitment of a man making up for lost time. "I can go. I can talk to him."

"He doesn't want to talk to you. He wants to talk to whoever knows where the cache is."

"And how do you know that?"

"Because I know him." The words came out of her before she could decide whether to release them. She'd been carrying Kessler's name around like a stone in her boot for six years; it seemed only fair to finally set it down somewhere. "Viktor Kessler. He ran ore out of the Kolvar ranges when I was a junior researcher. I testified about his operation. It collapsed. Most of it, anyway." She looked at Ulyana. "He's been looking for me ever since. And now I'm about to walk onto his boat and introduce myself."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the wheelhouse roof. Somewhere upstream, the glacier spoke—a deep groan, like a giant turning over in bed.

"You could have mentioned this earlier," he said.

"There wasn't a good moment. There was a monsoon, and then there was a grounding, and then there was a mutiny site, and honestly the schedule's been packed."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and vanished, embarrassed to be seen. "You're going to do it anyway."

"I'm going to do it anyway."

He nodded slowly, and then he did something that undid her more than any argument could have: he went below, rummaged in the chart locker, and came back with the flare pistol, which he pressed into her hand along with two flares.

"If things go wrong, fire one straight up. I'll come in after you."

"The channel's too narrow for heroics and you know it."

"I know it." His jaw worked. "I'm coming in anyway. Fire the flare."

She put the pistol in her jacket pocket, where it sat heavy and warm against her hip like a promise. Then she stood at the rail with her white flag, and Ulyana brought the boat in slow, engine idling, letting the current carry them toward the barge's shadow until the two hulls were close enough that a man could step across—if the man on the barge allowed it.

Someone on deck shouted. A rope came over, heavy and wet, and Freya caught it and made it fast to the cleat herself, refusing the helping hand that was offered, because she had decided on the walk to the rail that she would accept nothing from these people except her brother.

The gangway was a plank. Of course it was a plank. Men like Kessler loved a plank; it told visitors exactly how welcome they were.

She crossed it with her chin up and her heart doing something less like beating and more like hammering nails.

Kessler received her on the aft deck, under an awning, seated in an actual chair—an honest-to-god wicker chair with a cushion, hauled upriver piece by piece, presumably, because a man who had once drunk tea with district commissioners could not conduct business sitting on a crate like common labor. He was older than she remembered. Grey had gotten into his beard, and there was a new stiffness in his left hand, which he kept curled around a cup of tea like a small animal he was keeping warm.

But the eyes were the same. Pale, patient, and absolutely certain that everyone in the world would eventually sell something.

"Miss Ashcombe," he said.

Her stomach dropped through the deck plates, through the river, all the way down to the silt. "You remember me."

"I remember everyone who costs me money." He gestured to the second chair—the fact that there *was* a second chair told her he'd been expecting her since the moment the white rag went up. "Sit. You're dripping on my deck."

She sat. The tea was terrible, over-steeped and bitter, and she drank it anyway because her body had stopped consulting her pride about such matters days ago.

"My brother," she said.

"Is fine. Fed, dry, mildly resentful. He has your stubbornness, I think. He refused to tell us where the cache was for eleven days, which impressed me and annoyed my associate." Kessler nodded toward the man with the rifle, who had taken up a position by the rail with the studied boredom of someone who'd heard all of Kessler's speeches before. "Eleven days. From a glaciologist. You people are tougher than your funding suggests."

"He doesn't know where the cache is."

"No. But you do." Kessler set down his cup. "Which brings us to the interesting part of the morning. Because here is my situation, Miss Ashcombe: the glacier above us is shedding ice like a dog sheds fur in summer. In thirty hours, give or take, this channel closes, and my barge—with several tons of very valuable ore aboard—becomes a very valuable paperweight. I need to move. You need to move. And the channel, as I'm sure your captain has already calculated, fits one boat comfortably or two boats nervously."

"You've been watching us."

"I've been aware of you. There's a difference. Watching implies effort." He smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the worst thing about him—that he could make menace feel like hospitality. "So. Proposals. Mine is simple. Tell me where the expedition cached the rest of the shipment. My crew retrieves it while the channel holds. Your brother walks free, today, onto your boat. Everyone runs the narrows together, nervous but alive, and goes home rich or righteous depending on temperament."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we wait for the ice, and we find out together which of us floats longer." He spread his stiff hand, conciliatory. "I'd rather not. Drowning is undignified and I've done it once already."

Freya set down her cup. Her hands were steadier than she expected. Six years of running had taught her at least one thing: the moment of the offer was the moment you learned what you were worth, and you should look at it directly, no matter what it showed.

"I have a counterproposal," she said. "The cache location—for Tomas. Now. Before we run anything."

"Now?" Kessler laughed, genuinely delighted. "Oh, you *are* your file. The investigators wrote that you negotiated like someone with nothing left to lose. They meant it as a complaint."

"What's yours, then? You clearly have terms beyond the obvious."

The delight drained out of him by degrees, replaced by something older and more careful. He leaned forward.

"My terms are these. One: the cache location. Two: your silence—not just now, not just on this river, but permanently. No testimony, no affidavit, no late-night confession to some magistrate downstream. The ore moves, the matter closes, and Viktor Kessler remains a rumor. Three—" and here he paused, and Freya felt the trap close around her ankle before he even finished the sentence, "—your captain's boat stays with me as surety. Collateral. If your silence proves imperfect, I have assets. If you try to run the narrows ahead of me, I have leverage. Call it insurance. Call it a hostage. The word doesn't matter; the boat does."

The rain picked up, drumming on the awning. Downstream, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, and a chunk of ice the size of a market stall slid past the rail, grinding.

"That's not a bargain," Freya said. "That's theft with extra steps."

"That's *business*, Miss Ashcombe. I'm sorry no one explained it to you sooner." Kessler steepled his fingers. "Consider it from my side. I cannot let a witness sail away downriver with a head full of my operation. I also cannot afford to shoot her, because shooting witnesses creates *more* witnesses, and because your brother would become unmanageable, and because—I'll admit it—it would spoil my day. So instead I keep the thing she loves. The boat. Simple, portable, and it hurts the right person."

"Not the right person. The wrong person. Ulyana has nothing to do with any of this."

"Everyone on this river has something to do with everything. That's what a river *is*." Kessler shrugged. "Besides—he strikes me as a man who's lost boats before. He'll survive it. People like that always survive. It's their one talent."

Something hot moved up through Freya's chest, and she recognized it with surprise: not fear. Anger. Clean, clarifying anger, the kind that made the world simpler rather than more complicated.

Here was the shape of it, laid out on the wicker chair's armrest: say yes, and Tomas walked free today. Say yes, and Ulyana—the man who had taught her to read water, who had bailed beside her all night in lightning, who had confessed the truth of his rotten hull because honesty was a relief he'd forgotten how to feel—lost the only home he had. Say yes, and she bought her brother's life with a stranger's, except he wasn't a stranger anymore, and that was precisely the problem.

Six years ago she had testified and run, and she had told herself the running was prudence. Sitting here, she understood it had been cowardice wearing prudence's coat. She had paid for her safety with other people's exposure ever since—the colleagues whose names she'd given up, the drivers whose routes she'd described, the whole chain of small hands that had touched Kessler's cargo and been burned for it while she'd sailed away to a new continent and a new name.

She was not going to buy Tomas with Ulyana's boat. She was not going to do it even though every rational cell in her body voted yes, even though Tomas was *Tomas*, even though the alternative might kill them all in thirty hours of collapsing ice.

The trouble was, Kessler couldn't hear any of that. Kessler needed to hear yes—or something that sounded enough like yes to keep him calm while she thought of something better.

"I need to discuss it with my captain," she said. "It's his boat. I won't sell it out from under him. He'd scuttle it first, and then we all float."

Kessler considered this. Outside, the rifleman shifted his weight, and somewhere below decks a hatch banged.

"Half an hour," Kessler said at last. "Bring me an answer. And Miss Ashcombe—" the pale eyes fixed on her, gentle as a hook, "—I know you're wondering whether you can outmaneuver me. Everyone wonders that. I'd only point out that the last person who tried is currently a rumor himself, and I am currently drinking tea. Do weigh the historical record."

She stood. Her legs held, which she considered a personal triumph. At the gangway she paused, because there was one thing she needed, one thing she would not leave this deck without seeing.

"Let me say goodbye to my brother. Properly. Or there's no discussion at all."

Kessler waved a languid hand. "Two minutes. The tall one by the crane."

She crossed the deck and Tomas turned, and for two minutes—she counted them, hoarding every second—they held each other in the rain, and he whispered against her hair, "Freya, whatever he offered, don't—" and she whispered back, "I know. I know. Trust me one more time," and he pulled back and looked at her with eyes that were thinner-faced and older than four years should have made them, and he nodded, because he was her brother, and trusting her against all evidence was the family trade.

Then she walked back across the plank, and Ulyana's hands were there to steady her at the rail, and neither of them said anything until the rope was cast off and the current had carried them back into their own stretch of gray water.

"Well?" Ulyana said.

Freya looked at him—at the man Kessler proposed to strip of his home as casually as pocketing a watch—and felt the plan assembling itself behind her ribs, reckless and half-formed and entirely necessary.

"He wants the cache, my silence, and your boat," she said. "And I'm going to tell him yes."
