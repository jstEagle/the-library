## 16. The Price of Survival

Ulyana counted the seconds the way he counted everything—by feel, by habit, by the small stubborn arithmetic that had kept him alive on this river for ten years.

Freya had been gone forty minutes.

The narrows were a half-hour's row each way in calm water, and the water was not calm. The glacier above the gorge had started talking to itself overnight—that deep, structural groaning like a cathedral settling—and every few minutes a chunk the size of a market stall calved off into the channel with a report you felt in your back teeth. The ice was opening the way downstream for maybe a day and a half, maybe less, and Kessler's barge sat fat and smug in the only stretch of water wide enough for two vessels to pass abreast, which meant the parley was happening whether anyone liked it or not.

Ulyana stood at the wheel with his hands folded behind his back so they couldn't do anything stupid, and he watched the gray water between here and there, and he counted.

Forty-one minutes.

He told himself it didn't matter. He told himself she'd be back when she was back, that Kessler liked to talk, that Freya Ashcombe was the most stubborn person he'd ever shared a leaking boat with and would no more let a smuggler get the better of her than she'd let a sandbar. He told himself all of it in the flat, reasonable voice he used for weather reports and engine diagnostics, the voice that had never once lied to him about anything except himself.

Forty-three minutes.

Behind him, the mining cut survivors were arranged along the bench seats like cargo that had learned to breathe. Pell, the team leader, sat upright out of some last shred of professional dignity, his hands wrapped around a tin mug of tea he hadn't drunk. The young tech—Sefa, Freya had gotten the name out of her somewhere around dawn, the first word the girl had offered in days—sat curled against the rail with her hood up, watching the ice come down the gorge with an expression Ulyana recognized because he'd worn it himself for years: the look of someone cataloguing exits.

"She'll be fine," Pell said, and then, hearing how it sounded, "Probably."

"Kessler doesn't kill people at parleys," Ulyana said. "Bad for business. He kills them later, somewhere with soft ground." He heard his own voice and winced. "That was meant to be reassuring."

"It wasn't," Sefa said.

It was the second thing she'd said in three days. Ulyana decided to take it as a good sign anyway.

* * *

The trouble—the real trouble, underneath the counting—was that Ulyana knew exactly what was being traded in that conversation, because he'd spent the morning watching Freya prepare for it, and he'd let himself believe she was preparing to negotiate.

She'd braided her hair tight. She'd put on the driest clothes she had left, the ones she'd been saving. She'd taken the logbook page with the cache coordinates and folded it into her inner pocket, and she'd looked at him for a long moment before she climbed into the skiff, and she'd said, "Whatever comes out of my mouth over there, wait until I'm back before you decide what it means."

And Ulyana—who had built a whole life on deciding what things meant immediately, on reading the current ahead of the boat, on knowing when a passenger's smile meant trouble and a partner's promise meant debt—had said, "Fine."

Because that was the other thing about him, the thing he kept in the same sealed compartment as his name and his record and the ten years of running: he trusted almost nobody, but the list of people he did trust was very short and very load-bearing, and sometime in the last two weeks—somewhere between her reading water like a woman raised on it and her telling him about the testimony and the running, trading her worst secret across a squall-dark cabin like ballast passed hand to hand—Freya Ashcombe had gotten onto that list without filing any paperwork whatsoever.

So he waited. And he counted. And the old machinery started turning anyway, the betrayal-logic, the thing that lived in him like bilge water you could pump but never fully clear. *Nobody gives something for nothing. Nobody walks onto a smuggler's barge with coordinates in their pocket unless they're buying their way out. She's got a brother to save. You're just the boat.* The logic was smooth and well-worn and it fit his hands like an old tiller, and it whispered: *when she comes back, she'll have traded you.*

Fifty minutes.

Fifty-two.

Then the skiff came around the point, and Ulyana's heart did something complicated and undignified, and he made himself stand still.

* * *

She came aboard alone. That was the first thing. Her brother wasn't with her, and Ulyana's stomach dropped through the hull planking before his brain caught up and reminded him that of course he wouldn't be—Kessler would keep him until the deal closed, that was the whole shape of men like Kessler, collateral first and conversation after.

The second thing was her face.

Ulyana had spent ten years learning faces the way other men learned weather, because on the Veyra a face could tell you about a rock hidden under brown water, about a passenger with a knife, about an official who'd already decided to search your hold. He read hers now in one pass, and what he saw stopped the betrayal-logic mid-sentence like a hand on a throttle.

She wasn't relieved. She wasn't guilty. She was *furious*—furious in a cold, banked, glaciological way, the fury of someone who has just been shown the exact dimensions of a problem and is already calculating where to place the charge. And under the fury, barely visible, something else: fear, yes, but fear pointed *inward*, the kind you feel when you've decided to do something dangerous and you're afraid mostly of the people who might try to stop you.

She tied off the skiff line with hands that were perfectly steady, which told him she'd been practicing steadiness the whole way back.

"Well?" Pell said.

"He wants the cache," Freya said. "All of it. The ore, the logbook, everything from the expedition. In exchange for Tam." She looked at Ulyana. "And the boat."

The word landed the way he'd known it would, right in the old compartment, right where the bilge water lived. *The boat.* His boat. The one thing he owned outright in ten years of owning nothing, the hull he'd patched with his own hands through four seasons, the deck where he slept, the only address he'd had since the year he'd stopped using his full name. He felt the old logic snap into place around it, neat as a bearing: *there it is. There's the price. She paid with your boat, and why wouldn't she—it was never going to be hers, was it, it was always just transport, just the means, and now the means are spent—*

"—and I told him yes," Freya finished.

The cabin went very quiet. Even the glacier seemed to lean in.

"You told him yes," Ulyana repeated.

"I told him yes. Tomorrow, at the narrows, when the channel opens widest. We run alongside his barge, I hand over the papers and the coordinates, he lets Tam off, and he takes the *Veyra Rose*." She said the boat's name carefully, watching him. "I signed nothing. But I agreed. Out loud. In front of his crew."

Ulyana stood very still. Ten years of instinct said: *this is the moment. This is the shape of every bad story you've ever heard on this river. The partner who smiles and signs. The fair-weather friend who trades your hull for her own skin. Pump it out or sink with it, Thornbury, but decide fast.*

He opened his mouth to say—he didn't know what. Something with edges.

And then he actually looked at her. Not at the words, at the face above them, and the face was doing something the words weren't. Her jaw was set like a woman who'd bitten down on a sentence and refused to swallow it. Her eyes went, for half a second—not to him, not to Pell—to the *Rose*'s wheel, to the little brass pump handle stowed by the engine housing, to the bail buckets nested under the bench. Inventorying. The way he inventoried. The way a person inventories when they're planning to fight for every plank of a thing, not surrender it.

"You're not giving him the boat," Ulyana said slowly.

"No," Freya said.

"You're giving him the *idea* of the boat."

"I'm giving him the idea of me," she corrected. "The woman who ran once and would run again, who'll trade anything for her brother, who can be bought with exactly one price. He remembers me from the trial, Ulyana. He thinks he knows what I am. I intend to be extremely convincing." A breath. "I go aboard tomorrow with the coordinates. Tam comes across. And the moment he's clear—" her chin came up a fraction, "—I take Kessler's steering apart with a fire axe."

Silence again. Sefa lifted her head out of her hood.

"That's insane," Pell said. "You'll be alone on that barge with six armed men."

"Yes," Freya said, in the tone of a scientist confirming a hypothesis she found personally distasteful but statistically robust.

"And if it goes wrong," Pell said, "you're alone on that barge with six armed men and no exit."

"I've been alone before," Freya said. "I'm good at it. It turns out it's my one transferable skill."

And there it was. Ulyana heard it under the dryness, plain as a knock in the engine: *alone*. She'd built her whole plan on the assumption of alone, the way he'd built his whole life on it. Alone was safe. Alone couldn't be betrayed and couldn't betray. Alone was the armor, and she was putting it on right in front of him, and she expected him—of course she expected him; everyone always expected it—to stand on his own deck and watch her row away into the ice and call it sensible.

Ten years ago, he would have called it sensible. Ten years ago he'd have helped her load the skiff, given her the good lantern, and told himself the river took what the river took.

Ulyana discovered that he was done with ten years ago.

"No," he said.

Freya blinked. "No?"

"No. You're not doing it alone." He heard his own voice come out level and final, the voice he used on passengers who wanted to overload the bow, and he let it stay that way. "Your plan has one flaw. One. Everything else is genuinely deranged but structurally sound. The flaw is the part where you think you have to be the only one standing in the fire."

"Ulyana—"

"The axe work needs two people. Steering systems on barges that size have a backup tiller below decks—you disable the wheel, somebody has to hold the backup, or Kessler's helmsman just walks downstairs and steers with his hands. You can't be in two places. I can." He was already moving as he talked, pulling the pump handle free, checking the bail lines, his hands doing the thinking while his mouth caught up. "Also, you row like a drowned cat. If the exchange goes sideways and you end up in the water, someone has to fish you out, and I'm faster than his crew."

"This is my brother. My history. My—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "You don't owe me this. You don't owe me any of it. I came onto your boat with money and a sob story and I've gotten your hull cracked and your name dragged toward daylight and now I'm asking you to sail it into a gorge full of calving ice next to a man who's hunted me for six years. Any sane person would—"

"Would hand over the keys and wave goodbye," Ulyana said. "Yes. I know. I've met him. He's been living on this boat for ten years and frankly he's terrible company." He set the pump handle down with a click, deliberately, like setting down a weight. "Here's the thing nobody tells you about running. It works. Right up until the day you're standing on solid ground with nothing chasing you, and you look around, and there's no one there. That's the whole prize. That's what you win. An empty dock." He looked at her, and found, to his mild horror, that his eyes were hot, and decided the spray off the glacier would explain it. "I'm not handing you over to him alone. Not for the boat. Not for the ore. Not for anything. So either we do it together, or you'll have to tie me to the mast, and I should warn you—I've rigged that mast myself, and I know which knots hold."

Freya stared at him. Something moved through her face the way light moves under ice—a crack, a shift, the whole gray surface reorganizing itself around new pressure.

"You're sure," she said. It wasn't quite a question. It was her giving him one last exit, because she was decent that way, and he loved her a little for it and hated her a lot for it.

"I've been sure since minute fifty-two," Ulyana said. "Everything before that was just me arguing with myself, and I lose those arguments eventually. Ask anyone."

"Nobody's ever asked you anything," Sefa said.

"*See?*" Ulyana said. "The mute child agrees with me. Motion carries."

And Freya laughed—one short, cracked, startled sound, gone almost before it arrived, but real, the first real laugh he'd heard from her since the mining cut—and she crossed the deck and put her hand flat on the wheel beside his, not taking it, just resting it there, two sets of knuckles on the same wood.

"Together, then," she said. "But we do it my way. I talk. You hold the axe."

"I always hold the axe," Ulyana said. "It's the only honest job on a boat."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the light.

Freya walked Pell and Sefa through the exchange, drawing the barge's layout in charcoal on the underside of a hatch cover—wheelhouse aft, hold amidships, the backup tiller below, the crew berthed forward. Sefa, unhooded now, studied the drawing with an intensity that made Ulyana uneasy in a way he couldn't name yet, and then she picked up the charcoal and added, in small precise strokes, a detail Freya hadn't known: a fuel shutoff, port side, behind a panel, marked with a faded red tag.

"How do you know that?" Pell asked.

Sefa looked at the drawing for a long time. Then she wrote, beneath the panel, in the same small hand: *I installed it.*

Ulyana exchanged a glance with Freya over the girl's head. The least likely places, he thought. Every time. The river keeps its best crew hidden in the quietest corners.

By dusk the plan had a skeleton: Freya aboard with the coordinates at slack water tomorrow, when the calving paused and the channel ran widest. Tam released at the gangway. Ulyana following in the skiff under the pretense of delivering the logbook, axe wrapped in sacking at his feet. Sefa and Pell holding the *Rose* ready at the narrows' mouth, engines warm, positioned to scoop swimmers. And when Tam was clear—when and only when—Freya's axe into the wheelhouse hydraulics, Ulyana below at the backup tiller, Sefa's fuel panel, and Kessler's barge suddenly a very large, very expensive piece of driftwood in a channel full of moving ice.

"It'll be chaos," Freya said, rolling up the hatch cover. "Real chaos. Not the tidy kind."

"The tidy kind isn't worth doing," Ulyana said.

He took the first watch, because someone had to, and because he wanted the deck to himself for a while. The gorge breathed its slow glacial breath overhead. Downriver, past the black shoulder of the narrows, Kessler's barge showed a single riding light, steady as a false star.

Ulyana stood at the wheel and let himself feel it—all of it, honestly, the way he was trying to learn to do. The fear, yes: tomorrow there would be ice and guns and a man who held grudges the way the river held silt. And under the fear, stranger and harder to carry: the feeling of having chosen. Of having stood at the fork—self-preservation one way, connection the other—and stepped, deliberately, onto the branch that could break.

His hands were shaking slightly on the wheel. He noticed, and let them.

Behind him, the cabin door opened. Freya came out with two mugs of tea, handed him one without asking, and leaned on the rail beside him, shoulder almost touching his shoulder, both of them facing downriver toward the light.

"For the record," she said quietly, "if you'd handed me the keys and waved goodbye, I'd have understood."

"I know," Ulyana said. "That's why I didn't."

Out in the dark, the glacier calved again—a boom like the world clearing its throat—and the single light on Kessler's barge rocked, and steadied, and waited.

Tomorrow, Ulyana thought, they were going to make that light go out.
