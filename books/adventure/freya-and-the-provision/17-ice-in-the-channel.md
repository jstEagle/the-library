## 17. Ice in the Channel

The glacier announced itself before we saw it.

We heard it first—a sound like a door slamming somewhere deep in the earth, then a long rolling groan that traveled down the gorge and up through the hull of Ulyana's boat and into the soles of my feet. Tam, the mute tech, lifted his head from where he'd been dozing against the gunwale and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to understand as fluently as speech. *That's not good,* his eyebrows said.

"No," I agreed, out loud, because talking to him had become a habit and because saying things out loud made them feel slightly more survivable. "It isn't."

Ulyana stood at the wheel with his shoulders set in that particular way I'd learned meant he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. Ahead of us the gorge narrowed to a throat of gray water, and above it—when the mist tore open for a moment—the glacier came into view: a wall of blue-white ice hanging over the channel like a held breath, seamed with crevasses, dripping in a hundred silver threads.

"Thirty-six hours," Ulyana said. "Maybe less now. It's calving early."

"You said maybe."

"I say what I know. What I don't know, I keep quiet about." He glanced at me, and there was something underneath the gruffness—not quite warmth, but its foundation stone. "Kessler's barge is holding the wide channel. We go alongside him like we agreed, you board under the flag of talk, and we take him at the narrows."

"And if he doesn't let me board?"

"He will." Ulyana's jaw worked. "Men like Kessler always want to hear themselves negotiate."

I thought about the last time I'd seen Kessler—eleven years ago, across a courtroom aisle, before I ran and kept running until running became a personality. I thought about my brother, alive on that barge, and how many times in the past weeks I'd imagined this moment and never once imagined feeling calm.

The flag of talk was a white rag Ulyana produced from a locker, along with a coil of rope and, without comment, a short iron pry bar that he pressed into my hand as though handing me a cup of tea.

"For talking," he said.

* * *

The barge loomed out of the mist like something that had been built by people who expected the world to apologize to them. Rust-streaked, diesel-blackened, with a crane arm folded along its deck and two men at the rail who watched us come alongside with the loose readiness of professionals.

Kessler stood behind them. He hadn't changed much. Heavier through the shoulders, silver at the temples now, but the face was the same face I'd testified against—the patient, reasonable face of a man who could explain why any terrible thing was actually your idea.

"Freya Ashcombe," he said, before I'd even stepped aboard. My name in his mouth sounded like a debt being called in. "Eleven years. You haven't aged a day. Guilt agrees with some people."

"Where's my brother?"

"Alive. Which puts him ahead of several former colleagues of mine." He gestured, and one of the men brought Tomas forward—my brother, thinner, bearded, wrists bound in front of him, but with his eyes sharp and fixed on me with an intensity that nearly buckled my knees. He shook his head, small and urgent. *Don't.* Eleven years gone and still trying to protect me.

"Here's my offer," Kessler said pleasantly. "You tell me where the expedition cached the ore. Your brother walks free. And in exchange for your continued silence—" his gaze slid past me, to our boat, to Ulyana standing at the rail with his arms crossed, "—your captain leaves his vessel here as surety. A man who runs cargo on this river understands collateral."

"The boat's not mine to give."

"Then it's a short conversation." Kessler smiled. "Though I notice you're already aboard, which suggests someone trusted somebody."

Behind me, I heard Ulyana's boat bump gently against the barge's hull, riding the swell, and I understood suddenly what he'd done—he'd put us close enough that the gap between vessels was a single step, close enough that everything could happen fast. I hadn't told him the whole plan. I hadn't dared. But he'd read it anyway, the way he read water.

I gave Kessler the coordinates. Most of them. Enough to make my eyes honest.

He turned to relay them, and that was when three things happened almost at once.

The glacier spoke again—but this time it didn't stop speaking. The groan became a crack became a roar, and the entire face of the ice wall shrugged forward, shedding a slab the size of a chapel into the gorge upstream. The wave came down the channel seconds later, lifting both vessels, slamming them together, tearing the fenders away with a shriek of grinding steel.

And in the chaos—Tomas moved. Bound hands or no, eleven years of my brother's stubbornness or no, he drove his shoulder into the nearest guard and went over the rail toward me in a graceless, magnificent arc. We hit the deck together, rolled, came up against the crane housing.

"Tie!" I shouted—it wasn't even a word, just intent—and Ulyana was already there, leaning across the gap, throwing the coil of rope so it landed between us. I looped it around Tomas's chest. Ulyana hauled. My brother came off the barge like a landed fish, swearing with impressive fluency for a man who'd been starved for weeks, and collapsed onto our deck.

Which left me on the wrong boat.

Kessler had recovered faster than any man should. He wasn't shouting. That was the worst part—he looked at me with that reasonable expression and said, quietly, "You were never going to trade silence, were you? You're the type. I've known you eleven years, Freya. You're the type."

"Then you know what happens next."

"I do." He raised two fingers—not a threat, a signal—and men began moving toward the barge's stern, toward the winch controls, toward the crane. Toward the idea, I realized with a cold drop in my stomach, of simply pinning our little boat against the hull and letting the river do the rest.

And then Tam stood up.

Tam, who hadn't spoken since the mining cut, since whatever he'd seen during the expedition's violent end. Tam, whom I'd fed soup to and talked at for days and who had answered only with those eloquent eyebrows. He had stowed himself aboard our boat without anyone deciding it, and now he was standing at the barge's stern rail, having crossed the gap in the confusion like a ghost, and he had found the steering linkage.

I saw it all in about a second and a half: Tam crouched at the exposed tiller mechanism, the pry bar—I'd left it on deck; he'd picked it up—the pry bar coming down once, twice, three times, precise as a surveyor's instrument, into the hydraulic line and the linkage pin. Fluid sprayed. The barge's rudder swung hard over and stayed there.

The whole vessel leaned into a turn nobody was commanding.

"What—" Kessler started, and then the barge began to answer the river instead of him.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do.

The barge, rudder jammed, wallowed broadside into the current. The current, swollen with meltwater and newly insulted by half a glacier, took the invitation. We swept downstream sideways, the crew stumbling, Kessler grabbing the rail, and our little boat—freed, light, responsive—danced clear on Ulyana's hands at the wheel.

"Jump!" Ulyana roared at me. "Now!"

I jumped. The gap was opening, widening, and for one horrible moment I thought I'd misjudged it, thought I'd spend the rest of the story in Kessler's custody—but Ulyana's hand closed on my wrist, then my arm, then my jacket, and Tomas and Tam hauled, and I came over the gunwale in a tangle of limbs and landed on deck looking up at four faces: my brother's, gray with exhaustion and triumph; Tam's, wide-eyed and shaking; Ulyana's, already elsewhere, already reading the water ahead.

"You," I gasped at Tam, "are the bravest person on this river."

Tam looked at the wrecked pry bar in his hand, and then—to my astonishment, to everyone's astonishment—said, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge: "It needed doing."

Four words. His first in weeks. Tomas laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound, and pulled the boy into a rough embrace, and I felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since the mining cut.

But the gorge was not finished with us.

Ahead, the channel bent hard against the glacier's foot, and the calving had choked it—ice floes the size of trucks rode the current down toward the narrows, grinding, colliding, building. Behind us, Kessler's barge drifted broadside, crew scrambling, and I heard Kessler's voice carry across the water, calm as ever, calling orders, already salvaging, already adapting. Men like him always did.

"We can't run the narrows with that ice," Tomas said, reading the channel with a surveyor's eye despite everything. "It's closing. Ulyana, it's closing *now*."

Ulyana didn't answer immediately. He stood at the wheel, and I watched him do his arithmetic—ice, current, leak, load, the dying engine's temperature gauge, the thirty-six hours that had become perhaps twelve. Then he looked back at Kessler's drifting barge, at the one channel-wide gap in the ice that only the barge's mass could hold open, and I saw him arrive at the unthinkable thing he'd been circling for an hour.

"Hold on to something," he said. "All of you. We're going back in."

The gorge roared its agreement, and the ice came down like a closing door.
