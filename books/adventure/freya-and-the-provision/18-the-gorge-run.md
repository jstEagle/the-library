## 18. The Gorge Run

The glacier told them before the sky did.

Ulyana heard it as a sound he had no name for—a deep, structural groan, like the whole valley clearing its throat—and then a crack that rolled down the gorge and set the water shivering against the hull. Freya's head came up from the bail bucket. The mute tech, Sef, grabbed the gunwale with both hands. Somewhere above the cloud line, a piece of the ice age the size of a chapel let go of its mountain.

"Thirty-six hours," Ulyana said, and did not believe it. "Maybe less now."

"Then we don't waste them," Freya said. She had river-mud to the elbows and a cut on her forearm she'd refused to let him look at, and she said it the way she said most things lately—as if the universe were a colleague who could be reasoned with. Ulyana had known her eleven days and had already stopped being surprised by her. That frightened him more than the ice did.

Ahead, through the gorge mouth, Kessler's barge was already moving, its engine laboring, its wake a dirty white seam down the middle of the channel. The only channel. The calving had begun in earnest, and the river that had spent a week rising was now being squeezed from both sides—floodwater from below, ice from above—and the passage between was narrowing by the hour into something only a fool or a man with no other option would attempt.

Ulyana was both.

"Everyone hold on to something that loves you back," he said, and put the wheel over.

* * *

The gorge took them the way it took everything: without negotiation.

He had run the Veyra for ten years and had never run it in calving season, because the men who had were mostly dead and the rest mostly lied about it. The water here moved at a speed that made thinking a luxury. The walls rose sheer and wet on either side, streaked with old ice scars, and the glacier above overhung them like a brow about to frown. Every few minutes the groan came again, and every few minutes Ulyana adjusted his estimate of how long he had to live.

The plan—if you could dignify it with the word—was simple in the way that getting hit by a truck is simple. Run the gorge alongside the barge. At the narrows, where the channel pinched and Kessler would be forced to slow, come alongside, take everyone off, and let the river have the ore. Ulyana had explained it twice. Both times, Freya had looked at him the way she looked at bad data.

"And Kessler?" she'd asked.

"Kessler can swim."

"Ulyana."

"He'll have a boat under him until the narrows. After the narrows, that's the river's business, not mine." He'd said it firmly, and had believed perhaps sixty percent of it, and had known even then that the other forty percent was going to cost him.

Now the gorge had them, and there was no more time for percentages.

The first ice came down a hundred meters ahead—a white splash, a boom he felt in his teeth, and then the wave. Ulyana saw it coming the way you see a sandbar in bad light, as a wrongness in the surface, and he turned the bow into it and held on. The boat leapt, slammed, shipped a ton of green water over the foredeck, and came down still afloat, which was more than he'd had any right to expect.

"Everyone breathe," he said.

"I am breathing," said Tomas, Freya's brother, from the bottom of the boat, where he'd been put with the team leader and told to stay. He was hollow-cheeked and shaky and had the specific stubbornness of a man who'd survived three weeks in a valley by deciding to. "Badly, but I'm doing it."

"Then you're ahead of the boat," Ulyana said, and put the wheel over again.

The barge was a hundred meters on, fighting the current, listing slightly to port. He could see figures on deck. One of them was moving crates toward the stern—Kessler, salvaging his cargo while the world came down around him. Ulyana felt the old contempt rise, familiar as a scar, and then the glacier groaned again and he put it away. Contempt was a luxury. So was hatred. Out here you paid for both in attention, and attention was the only currency the gorge accepted.

* * *

It went wrong at the narrows, because things do.

The channel pinched the way the charts said it would—charts Ulyana had drawn himself, ten years ago, in a different life, in ink he'd spent a decade trying to forget—and the current through the pinch ran like a spilled bucket. The barge went in first and immediately began to lose the argument. Ulyana watched its stern swing, watched the pilot overcorrect, watched the whole clumsy mass of it slide sideways toward the glacier wall with the unhurried inevitability of a thing that has already decided.

"Alongside," Ulyana said. "Freya—lines. Sef, you're with me. Tomas, hold the wheel and do exactly what I say and nothing else."

Tomas's eyes went wide. "I've never—"

"Today you have. Small corrections. The river punishes big opinions."

And then there was no more time for anything but the run.

The *Provision*—he'd never named her, and the irony of the name the expedition had left painted on a crate was not lost on him, but that was a thought for a man with a future—the boat went into the pinch at full throttle, wall to wall with a foundering barge, close enough that Ulyana could see the fear on the faces of Kessler's crew, close enough to count the crates. The glacier wall rose on the left. The barge's hull groaned against it, planks and ice singing one terrible note together.

"Line!" Freya threw it. It caught. Sef hauled. The first man came off the barge's rail and into the boat like a sack of meal—Ulyana recognized him as the young deckhand who'd flinched when Kessler spoke, and hauled him over the side without ceremony. Then another. Then a woman Ulyana didn't know, sobbing, both hands locked on Freya's wrists.

The barge was going over. Slowly, the way big things do, but going.

"Kessler!" Freya's voice cracked across the water. "Kessler, come *now*!"

And there he was at the rail, and Ulyana saw him clearly for the first time—the broad, careful face, the eyes that had hunted Freya across a decade and half a world and had found her at last on a leaking boat in a gorge full of falling ice. Kessler looked at her. He looked at the water. And then he looked down at the crate he was holding, the ore, the provision, the whole rotten weight of it, and Ulyana saw him make the calculation, saw him choose the cargo over the gap that was already closing—

The glacier chose for him.

The crack this time was not a groan. It was a gunshot, then a hundred gunshots, then a sound like the sky tearing along a seam. Above the narrows the ice face moved—a shelf of it, blue-white, house-tall—and went into the river with a boom that Ulyana would hear for the rest of his life, in quiet rooms, at night.

The wave it made was not a wave. It was a wall.

* * *

Ulyana did the only thing there was to do, which was to point the bow at it and hold on to everything he had ever loved, which was the wheel, and—this was new—the people in the boat.

The wall took them. The world went white and green and upside down. Ulyana lost the wheel, found it, lost Freya's hand, found her jacket, and came up out of the chaos with the boat still under him and half the river still inside it and Sef bailing with both hands and a look on the kid's face that Ulyana would remember longer than the wave: not fear. Fury. The mute tech bailing like the river had said something about his mother.

When the spray cleared, the barge was gone.

Not sunk—*gone*, rolled, broken, its cargo scattered across the channel like toys, its hull riding up against the glacier wall at a drunken angle with the river pouring through it. And there, in the white water below the wreck, clinging to a crate, was a man.

Kessler.

The current was taking him downstream, toward the lower gorge, toward the water that ate boats and kept them. He was not calling out. That was the thing Ulyana noticed, the thing he would think about later: the man was not calling out. He was holding on and watching them with the flat, calculating eyes of a man who already knew the answer, and was measuring how long he could make the question last.

Freya was already moving to the line. "Ulyana—"

"He left you to drown," Ulyana said. "Twice, by my count. Once just now, once ten years ago."

"I know what he did."

"He'd let this boat sink with all of us on it for one more crate. You saw him choose it."

"I saw." Her hands were on the line and steady, which was somehow the worst part. "And I'm asking you anyway. Because if we let him go, I'll be running from this too. And I'm so *tired*, Ulyana. I'm so tired of running."

The gorge groaned overhead. Somewhere upstream, another chapel's worth of ice let go.

Ulyana looked at the man in the water. He thought about the river, which forgave nothing—which had never once forgiven anything, which kept its dead and its secrets in the same cold ledger, and which was, right now, offering Ulyana Thornbury a chance to write an easy entry in his own book. *The current took him. Nothing I could do.* Every pilot on the Veyra would believe it. He would believe it, mostly, by winter.

But he'd have to say it to himself every night, and he had spent ten years saying things to himself at night, and he knew exactly what they cost.

"The river forgives nothing," he said, mostly to himself. "Neither will I."

He put the wheel over and went in after him.

* * *

Getting Kessler out of the water was the hardest navigation Ulyana had ever done, and he'd once threaded a sandbar at flood stage with a hole in his hull and a magistrate's launch behind him. The man was heavy with water and heavier with the crate, which he would not release, and there was a moment—Ulyana would remember this too, with a kind of tired wonder—when he had one hand on Kessler's collar and the crate was dragging them both down, and he understood that the choice was real, that he could not have both, that the river was asking him the question plainly at last.

He hit the crate with the boat hook. It went away downstream. Kessler came up gasping, and Ulyana hauled him over the transom, and the man lay on the deck plates coughing up the Veyra and glaring at them all with the affronted dignity of a cat that has fallen in a bath.

"You could have let go of the crate," Freya said.

Kessler said nothing. His eyes went from her to Ulyana to the boat—the boat he'd demanded as surety, the boat that had just saved his life—and something moved behind them that Ulyana chose not to name, because naming it would have meant it was true.

"You're paying for the planks you cracked climbing in," Ulyana said. "I keep a list."

* * *

They came out of the gorge in the long gold light of late afternoon, all eight of them—nine counting the boat, and Ulyana counted the boat—packed shoulder to shoulder on a deck built for two, bailing in shifts, the engine muttering its one good cylinder's complaint. Behind them the gorge mouth was already whitening. By morning the ice would dam it shut, and the river above would begin to rise behind the dam like a held breath, and none of that was their problem anymore.

Ahead, the valley opened. The water widened, slowed, went quiet in that particular way the Veyra had below the falls, as if the river too were tired of shouting.

Ulyana stood at the wheel and let his hands shake, now that shaking was affordable.

Freya came and stood beside him. She didn't say thank you. She didn't say anything about Kessler, or the crate, or the forty percent of him that had wanted, for one long moment, to steer away. She just stood there, shoulder to shoulder, watching the water, and after a while she said, "You kept a list of the planks."

"Two cracked. One sprung. He's at eleven silver and rising."

"And the man himself?"

Ulyana looked back. Kessler was asleep against the rail, exhausted, soaked, alive. Sef sat three feet away, watching him with the patience of a cat at a mousehole, and Ulyana had a feeling the kid had volunteered for that watch without anyone asking.

"The river gets to decide what he owes," Ulyana said. "I just decide where he sleeps."

Freya laughed—a short, cracked, real laugh, the first he'd heard from her in two days—and then she went still, looking forward, and her hand closed on his arm.

"Ulyana," she said. "What's that smoke?"

Ahead, where the river bent toward the lowland settlements, where the magistrate was, where the ore and the logbook and the truth were all finally headed—thin and gray against the evening, a thread of it rising from the tree line—someone was burning something.

And they were the only boat on the river to see it.
