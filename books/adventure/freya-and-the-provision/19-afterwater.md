## 19. Afterwater

Below the gorge, the Veyra forgot its anger all at once, the way a child stops crying mid-sob when something bright catches its eye. The water spread wide and brown-gold and slow, braiding itself around sandbars that looked almost apologetic after what we'd come through. From the bow I could see birds again—herons standing in the shallows with the composure of creatures who had never once had to run a gorge between calving ice.

I counted heads for perhaps the hundredth time that day. It was becoming a habit, like counting cans.

Ulyana at the tiller, one hand on it, the other holding a mug of tea he kept forgetting to drink. Myself at the bow. Tomas beside me, wrapped in the expedition blanket I'd carried in my kit for eleven days without knowing why. Halloran asleep against the cargo crates with his mouth open, looking ten years younger and twenty years more foolish than the man who had signed off on moving contraband ore down a protected river. June—the mute tech, though *mute* was already becoming the wrong word, since she'd spent the afternoon pointing at things and making small decisive noises, which was more communication than some people manage in a lifetime. The Renholt brothers, Dov and Anselm, Kessler's deckhands, who had thrown down their tools at the narrows and helped us pull people out of the water without being asked, and who now sat at the stern sharing one cigarette between them with the solemnity of men dividing a inheritance. And Kessler himself, wrists bound with the same rope we'd used for the anchor line, sitting as far from everyone as the boat's geometry allowed, watching the herons with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

Eight people on a boat built for two. Ulyana had said it flatly, back in the gorge, when I'd asked if we'd all fit: "Fit is a flexible word on this river." Then he'd made room.

* * *

The light went long and copper, and Ulyana brought us in against a sandbar that rose out of the water like the back of a sleeping animal. He ran the boat up onto it gently, deliberately—a practice I'd learned was called *drying out*, letting the hull rest in the shallows overnight so the leak could be found and argued with in daylight.

"We camp here," he said. "Nobody goes in past their knees. The current looks lazy but it's lying."

It was the most words he'd strung together in an hour, and everyone seemed to understand that this constituted a speech. There was a general movement toward shore, the careful choreography of exhausted people who had learned each other's rhythms in a single catastrophic day. Dov built a fire ring out of stones without being asked. Anselm produced, from somewhere in his jacket, a tin of dried fish that he offered around with the shy formality of a man presenting a bouquet.

Kessler sat apart, on a driftwood log, hands still bound. Nobody had decided what to do about him. Nobody wanted to be the first to decide.

I brought him a mug of tea. I'm still not entirely sure why. Perhaps because Tomas was watching, and I wanted him to see that fear and decency could occupy the same body at the same time. Perhaps because I remembered being nineteen and testifying in a courtroom while a man like this one looked at me across it, and remembering how much braver I'd felt afterward when someone whose side I wasn't on had handed me a glass of water anyway.

He took the mug. His wrists were bound, so he drank awkwardly, both hands around it like a child.

"You've gotten older," he said.

"So have you."

"That's not what I meant." He looked at the fire, not at me. "You were easier to find than you think, Ashcombe. You leave a shape in the world. People who testify always do."

"And yet it took you eleven years and a river to catch up with me."

"It took me eleven years to stop looking." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Then your name turned up on a survey manifest, and there I was."

I stood there holding nothing, having given away my only prop, and realized my hands had gone cold. Behind me, I heard Tomas shift his weight on the sand. Listening. Of course he was listening. He'd been listening since the gorge, since the narrows, since the moment I'd boarded that barge and said *hello, Viktor* in a voice I hadn't used aloud in a decade.

* * *

Tomas waited until the others had settled into their watch rotations and the fire had burned down to a red, thoughtful glow. Then he came and sat next to me on the boat's gunwale, our shoulders touching, the way we used to sit on the sea wall at Whitstable when we were young enough that sitting close didn't require an excuse.

"You knew him," he said. "Before all this."

"I testified against him. Eleven years ago. Ore smuggling, same ore, different river. I was a junior researcher who found shipping discrepancies in the data and couldn't keep my mouth shut." I watched the fire rather than him. "He got four years. Someone else took the fall for most of it. When he got out, he started sending letters. Then the letters stopped, and I started running anyway, because stopped letters are worse than letters."

"Mum never said any of this."

"Mum doesn't know. I told her I took a post in Tromsø."

Tomas absorbed this. He'd gotten thin in the valley—I could see it even by firelight, the new angles of his face—but his expression when he was thinking hadn't changed since he was nine years old. It was the look of a boy doing arithmetic he suspected would disappoint him.

"I thought you were the settled one," he said finally. "All those years. The lectureship, the flat in the city, the—" he gestured vaguely at the concept of my life, "—the *furniture*. And the whole time you were—"

"Running. Yes. While you were the one who joined a survey expedition to a river nobody's mapped properly, and I was the one who stayed home and worried." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "We're idiots, Tom. Both of us. We spent a decade being brave in opposite directions."

He put his arm around me then, and I let my head drop against his shoulder, and for a while neither of us said anything, because the fire was warm and the river was quiet and grief is a thing that needs silence the way bread needs rising time.

"He's really gone, isn't he," Tomas said at last. Not a question. "Okafor. And Priya. And little Sefu, who was twenty-three and talked about his mother every single day."

"Yes."

"I keep waiting for it to hit me properly. Like a wave. But it's not a wave. It's just—" He searched for it. "It's like ballast. Something heavy riding low in me that I have to carry now, forever, and it doesn't crash, it just *is*."

"Ballast keeps you upright," I said. "That's the whole point of it."

He was quiet a moment. "When did you get wise?"

"About four days ago, on a sandbar, bailing water next to a man who counts cans twice." I felt him smile against the top of my head. "Tom. Tell me about them. Not how they died. How they *were*. I want to carry them too, if there's room."

So he did. Okafor, the geologist who sang off-key at dawn and insisted it kept the wildlife honest. Priya, who could fix anything with wire and stubbornness and had fixed the radio three times before it finally refused. Sefu, who had never seen ice before and had cried the first time he touched glacier, and laughed at himself for crying, and touched it again. Tomas talked until his voice wore out, and I listened until the fire burned to embers, and somewhere in there the ballast in both of us shifted to a bearing we could sail on.

* * *

Morning came gray and soft, with mist standing on the water in columns like the ghosts of trees. I woke to the sound of Ulyana arguing with his boat.

Not metaphorically. He was hip-deep in the bilge with a lantern, talking to the hull in a low, continuous mutter—"no, no, you don't get to do that, I patched you in March, I *begged* you"—and when he surfaced he had the expression of a man who has lost an argument with a family member.

"How bad?" I asked.

"She'll hold." He wrung out his sleeves. "Below the gorge she'll hold. She knows we're nearly home. Boats know these things."

"Do you actually believe that?"

"No." He climbed aboard, dripping. "But it costs nothing to say, and she hears everything."

There it was—that dry flicker of humor he deployed like a man spending coins he didn't trust himself to have. Ten days ago I'd have catalogued it and moved on. Now I found myself wanting to stand in front of it, the way you stand in front of a fire.

Breakfast was rice porridge with dried fish, cooked by Anselm, who turned out to be the better cook of the brothers and accepted compliments with the pained grace of a man being praised for a crime. Halloran ate sitting up for the first time, color returning to his face along with, apparently, his conscience.

"The ore," he said, to the porridge, to all of us, to nobody. "The survey budget was cut eighteen months ago. Funding gone, sponsors gone, and Kessler's people approached me with numbers that made the whole expedition solvent twice over. All we had to do was move crates we weren't allowed to open through country nobody official ever visits." He looked up. "I want to say I resisted. I want to tell you I fought it. I said yes in under a week. Okafor said yes faster. Priya never said yes at all, and that's why—" His voice went. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "That's why she's not here and I am."

Nobody rushed to comfort him. On this boat, we had all learned that some confessions need to finish before they can be answered.

"You'll tell it all to the magistrate," Ulyana said. Not unkindly. Factly, the way he said *the current looks lazy but it's lying*. "Every number, every name. It's the only payment the dead will accept."

Halloran nodded, and June—watching him with those dark, patient eyes—reached over and took the ladle from his hand and gave him the job of stirring, because June had understood before any of us that grieving men need tasks.

She spoke her first full word that morning. We were breaking camp, and Ulyana asked her to hold the painter while he pushed us off the bar, and she took the rope and said, clear as a bell, "Got it."

Everyone froze. Ulyana looked at her. She looked back, chin up, daring anyone to make it a thing.

"Good grip," Ulyana said gravely, and pushed us off, and that was all, and it was exactly right.

* * *

We ran easy water all day, the river widening mile by mile until the far banks were suggestions rather than facts. The mood on board loosened the way knots do in warm water. Dov taught Tomas a card game played with half a deck. Halloran slept in actual peace. Kessler sat bound at the stern and watched the country go by, and once—only once—I caught him closing his eyes and turning his face up to the sun like a man memorizing something, and I understood that whatever waited for him downstream, he knew this might be the last free sky he saw for a long time, and I felt something dangerously adjacent to pity, and I put it away carefully, like a knife.

In the late afternoon Ulyana let me take the tiller.

He didn't announce it. He simply stepped back from it and looked at me, and I took it, and for an hour he stood beside me saying almost nothing—just the occasional *"ease her left"* or *"feel that? Bar below, the water folds over it"*—and the boat answered my hands as if it had decided, provisionally, to trust me.

"You're thinking about the settlement," he said, after a long silence.

"Rennick Landing. Two days, maybe three at this speed." I watched the water fold where he'd shown me. "Magistrate, garrison post, radio tower. Everything ends there, doesn't it. The evidence, the arrests, the testimony. Eleven years of running, and it ends at a dock with a clerk writing down my name."

"Frightened?"

"Terrified." I said it easily, which surprised me. "You?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "I haven't used my real name in ten years. Ulyana Thornbury is a coat I put on in a port town with two letters of debt behind me and a choice between disappearing or drowning." He looked at the river, not at me. "Tomorrow or the day after, I walk into that magistrate's office and take the coat off. And I don't know what's underneath anymore. That's the truth of it."

"What's underneath," I said, "is a man who hauled his worst enemy out of the water because the river forgives nothing and neither would he forgive himself otherwise. I've seen what's underneath, Ulyana. It's better than the coat."

He didn't answer. But his hand came to rest on the tiller beside mine—just resting there, not taking it—and we steered together through the folding water while the sun went down behind us and the mist began to rise off the bars ahead.

* * *

We made camp that night on a long gravel spit with the current murmuring against it like a house settling. Eight bedrolls in a row, closer than comfort strictly required, because after the gorge none of us could bear much distance. The fire was small. The talk was small. Small was what we had strength for.

It was Tomas who started it, drowsing against a crate: "When this is done. After the magistrate and all of it. What happens to us?"

"To us?" Dov asked.

"This collection of people. Do we just—scatter? Go back to being strangers who survived a river together?"

Anselm fed the fire a stick. "I've got a contract with Kessler. Had one. I'm thinking it's lapsed."

"Lapsed," Dov agreed solemnly. "Terminated. Void for criminal breach."

"I owe money in Corvane," Halloran said into the dark. "But I owe the dead more. I expect I'll spend a few years paying the second debt and longer paying the first."

"June?" Tomas asked.

June considered the fire for a long moment. Then she pointed at the boat—at the tired, leaking, heroic boat—and then at herself, and raised one eyebrow, and that was the entire speech, and everyone understood it completely.

"Tomas stays on as crew," I said, because it had been decided somewhere in the night watches without my knowledge, and I was only now catching up. "And I—" I glanced at Ulyana, whose face was firelit and unreadable and listening very hard. "I've been offered a research post. Far away. A proper one, with furniture."

"And?" Tomas prompted.

"And I'm going to write back and negotiate. There are glaciers at the head of this river that no one has studied properly. Someone should. Someone local." I let the word sit there. *Local.* It tasted strange and good, like water after a long thirst.

Ulyana said nothing at all. But he looked at me across the fire for a long moment, and whatever was in his face, it wasn't the guardedness I'd spent ten days learning to read past. It was something newer. Unpracticed. A man discovering that the coat might come off and underneath there might be—this.

He cleared his throat and stood, brushing sand from his knees with great efficiency. "Watches. Same rotation. Rennick Landing day after tomorrow if the water holds." He paused at the edge of the firelight. "Sleep well. All of you."

*All of you.* Two days ago it would have been *you lot*. I smiled at the fire and pretended not to notice, and I'm fairly sure everyone else pretended not to notice me smiling, and that, I was beginning to understand, was how families worked.

I was nearly asleep when Kessler spoke from the darkness beyond the embers, quietly, so that only I heard it—or so he believed.

"They'll ask about you at the landing, Ashcombe. The magistrate's clerks. They'll ask what name you testified under, eleven years ago, and they'll find the file, and they'll find the other name in it." A pause, soft as the current. "The man who took the fall for me. You never wondered why the letters stopped?"

I lay awake a long time after that, watching the stars wheel over the water, doing arithmetic I suspected would disappoint me.
