## 20. Deliveries

The settlement of Harrow's Landing announced itself the way all river towns do: first by smell—woodsmoke and fish guts and someone frying onions at an hour no reasonable person fries onions—then by sound, a church bell with a crack in it that made every noon toll sound like an apology, and finally by the sight of the dock itself, leaning into the current like a drunk leaning on a friend.

I had been past Harrow's Landing perhaps two hundred times in ten years. I knew which pilings were rotten, which clerk took bribes, which constable slept through his watch. I knew it the way you know a face you've spent a decade avoiding eye contact with.

This time I was going to walk straight up to the magistrate's office and introduce myself.

"You're doing the thing again," Freya said from the bow.

"What thing."

"The thing where your jaw does all the talking and the rest of you goes on strike." She turned around, one hand shading her eyes against the morning glare off the water. Behind her, the boat looked like something dredged up from a war—scorched tarpaulin, a jury-rigged pump still ticking over, eight people's laundry strung from the mast like flags of a nation nobody recognized. "You've been staring at that dock for ten minutes. The dock has noticed. The dock is uncomfortable."

"The dock can mind its own business."

"So can I, apparently, but here we are." She came aft, stepping over Tam—the mute tech, who was asleep in a coil of rope with his boots on, which was more initiative than he'd shown a week ago—and sat on the rail beside me. Close enough that our shoulders touched. She'd started doing that somewhere below the gorge, and I hadn't found a polite way to ask her to stop, mostly because I didn't want her to.

"Kessler talked to you last night," she said.

I kept my eyes on the channel. There was a snag off the north piling that hadn't been there in spring; I marked it, out of habit, the way other men mark debts. "He talks. It's mostly weather."

"It wasn't weather."

"No," I admitted. "It wasn't."

She waited. Freya waiting was worse than most people shouting. She had this trick of going very still, like deep water, until you felt obliged to fill the silence just to hear a human voice again.

"He says there's a file," I said finally. "From eleven years ago. My name in it. What I did, what I took the fall for, who I took the fall for." I shrugged, which hurt, because everything hurt; I'd been hauling rope for six days straight. "I always figured he'd use it someday. That's why I stayed small. Small boats, small runs, small towns. A man the size of a rumor doesn't get blackmailed easy."

"And now?"

"Now I'm about to make myself very large indeed." I nodded at the crates stacked amidships under oilcloth. Contraband ore, half a fortune, the reason four men had gone into the valley and not all come out. "Can't hand this over without handing myself over with it. Clerks love paperwork. Paperwork loves names."

Freya was quiet a moment. Then she said, "You could run."

"I could."

"Drop the ore overboard tonight, take me and Tam and the survivors downriver, disappear again. Kessler never testifies, the file stays shut, you go back to being a rumor."

"That's a very detailed plan for someone who claims to be asking."

"I'm a scientist. I model outcomes." Her mouth twitched. "It's what I do instead of sleeping."

I looked at her then. Salt-stained jacket, her brother's spare shirt because hers had been ruined in the gorge, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of pump gasket. Ten days ago she'd been a stranger with money and a photograph. Now she was—

Well. Now she was the reason I wasn't going to run.

"If I drop that ore," I said, "then the expedition died for nothing. Then Aldous—" her brother, currently asleep in the hold with a fever breaking, "—went into that valley for nothing, and the tech kid stopped speaking for nothing, and Kessler wins by losing, which is his favorite way to win." I turned back to the channel. "Besides. I'm tired, Ashcombe. Ten years is a long time to be tired."

* * *

Harrow's Landing magistrate's office occupied the ground floor of a building that had been a trading post, a brothel, and briefly a chapel, and had settled into none of those identities with any conviction. Inside it smelled of ink and mildew. A ceiling fan turned with the enthusiasm of a man stirring cold soup.

The magistrate himself was a small round woman named Ovid, per the plaque, though everyone on the river called her the Weighmaster, because she had once fined a cargo barge owner the exact weight of his undeclared cargo in flour, measured out sack by sack on her office scales while he watched.

She looked up when we walked in—me first, then Freya, then the procession behind us: Aldous Ashcombe gray but upright, the team leader Voss leaning on a crutch, Tam walking on his own feet with his eyes fixed on the floorboards, and Kessler at the rear with his wrists bound in the same line he'd used to tie off our fenders, which Freya had insisted on as a matter of principle.

Ovid looked at us for a long moment. Then she took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on, and looked again, as though we might have improved.

"Ulyana Thornbury," I said. "Master of the vessel *Provision*. I'm here to declare cargo."

"Are you." Her voice was dry as August. "That would be a novelty."

I set the logbook on her desk. Then I pulled the oilcloth off the first crate, so she could see the dull grey gleam of unrefined ore inside, stamped with a mining concern's mark that belonged to a company that officially did not operate above the gorge.

"Contraband ore, recovered from an illegal cache in the upper valley. Recovered persons, four, including two members of the missing survey expedition. One prisoner, one Aldous Kessler, wanted—I believe—for smuggling, kidnapping, and attempted murder, though the list may be longer; he's ambitious." I laid the logbook open to the final page, the coordinates, the note about *the provision*. "And I'm declaring myself. Eleven years ago I ran cargo for the man behind you. When the operation was broken up, I took a conviction under a false name and paid a debt that was never mine. The record of it exists. You'll find it if you look."

The fan creaked overhead. Somewhere outside, the cracked bell rang noon, apologizing.

Ovid looked at me the way she must have looked at sacks of flour: weighing.

"You understand," she said, "that once I open that file, you stop being able to close it. No more quiet landings. No more paying a man at the fuel dock to not write things down. Every port from here to the delta will know your name and your history."

"Yes."

"You'll owe fines. Possibly time."

"Possibly."

"And you're doing this why?" She leaned forward. "Men have lied to me for less. Men have lied to me for more, and better dressed."

I thought about how to answer that honestly, which was a novel exercise, like writing with my left hand.

"Because a woman waited three days on a dock for me," I said, "and I've spent ten years making sure nobody would ever do that again, and I'm done with it. Because the river keeps accounts whether you settle them or not. And because—" I glanced back at Kessler, who was watching me with an expression of genuine scholarly interest, like a man observing a rare bird behave unexpectedly, "—because if I don't say it first, he will, and he'll charge me for the privilege."

Kessler smiled. Even bound, even ruined, even facing whatever Ovid was about to feed him, the man smiled like a landlord.

"He's telling the truth," Kessler said pleasantly. "Every word. I can confirm the file exists. I can also confirm the debt was discharged—by me, on his behalf, in exchange for services rendered, which makes Thornbury here technically still in my employ, which I'm sure the court will find fascinating—"

"Silence," said Ovid, without looking at him, and Kessler went silent, which told me something about the Weighmaster that the flour story had only hinted at.

She opened the logbook. She read. The fan turned. Outside, Freya's brother was showing Tam how to lean against a wall properly, conserving strength, and their voices drifted in low and easy, two men who had survived the same valley learning each other's names.

"Thornbury," Ovid said at last. She wrote it down. The scratch of the pen sounded enormous. "Your boat. The *Provision*?"

"That's right."

"She's leaking."

"She's leaking," I agreed.

"Then after your fines—which will be substantial, and which you will pay in installments, because I am fair but I am not stupid—you'll need work. Declared work." She looked up, and for just a moment the dryness cracked and something almost kind showed through. "There's a survey office opening in the spring. Glacial monitoring, upper river. They'll need a pilot who knows the water above the gorge. Very few pilots do. Very few who do are willing to admit they exist."

I heard Freya inhale behind me, very slightly, the way she did when data confirmed a hypothesis.

"I'll think about it," I said, which was a lie, and I suspect Ovid knew it, and filed it away with everything else.

* * *

The fines took three hours to calculate, which Ovid informed us was fast, and the statements took the rest of the day, which she informed us was also fast, and by evening the whole strange cargo of us had been processed, fingerprinted, fed—the Weighmaster produced a pot of stew from somewhere behind the filing cabinets, and I choose not to investigate further—and assigned beds in the settlement's single guesthouse, which smelled of the chapel era and had sheets stiff enough to stand in a corner on their own.

Kessler went into the constable's lockup, a cell he inspected with a connoisseur's eye before remarking that the hinges were original and the drainage was criminal. He said it to no one in particular. He'd been saying things to no one in particular all day, little seeds of doubt planted in passing—*ask Thornbury about the fuel runs in '19*, *the file has an appendix*, *you should see what he carried before he met any of you*—and each one landed in the room and sat there like a wet dog nobody wanted to acknowledge.

It was Freya who finally dealt with him. She stood outside his cell bars in the lamplight, arms crossed, and said, "Appendix or no appendix, testimony or no testimony—he gave himself up. Voluntarily. In front of witnesses. Whatever's in that file, he walked in and opened the door himself. What did you ever do on purpose, Aldous? Besides other people?"

Kessler looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face, it made him lean back against the wall and, for the first time since I'd known him, stop talking.

That night I couldn't sleep, which was normal, but for once it wasn't the leak or the current or the arithmetic of survival keeping me up. It was the silence. Ten years of listening for footsteps, for engines, for the particular knock of men collecting debts, and now—nothing. Just the river going by in the dark, doing what rivers do, minding its own business, and my name written down in a ledger in triplicate where anyone could read it.

I went out to the dock. The boat sat low on her lines, tired as the rest of us, and I walked her length out of habit, checking lines, checking the pump, pressing my palm to the hull planks the way you'd check a fever.

"You're going to need new planking," Freya said, from the shadows at the dock's end. She had a blanket around her shoulders and two tin cups steaming beside her, which meant she'd predicted me, which meant I was becoming predictable, which meant—

Which meant I was known. Thoroughly, inconveniently known.

"Whole strake, maybe two," I said, sitting beside her. Taking the cup. It was tea, terrible tea, river-boiled tea that tasted faintly of pump gasket, and I drank it like it was the best thing I'd ever had.

"I asked Ovid's clerk about shipwrights. There's a yard two days downstream. Good reputation. They'll want a name for the registry when they re-plank her." She sipped her own cup. "A new name. For the re-christening. It's traditional."

"The *Provision*'s a fine name."

"The *Provision* was Kessler's joke. Cargo that was never declared. A promise that was never kept." She looked at me sideways. "You're not that anymore. None of us are."

I looked down the dark river, toward the delta, toward the sea, toward every place I'd spent a decade not going. My name was in a ledger. My debt was paid. My secrets were sitting in a lockup two streets away, and in the morning the whole settlement would know exactly who I was, and the day after that the next settlement, and so on, all the way to the sea.

It should have felt like drowning.

Instead it felt like ballast jettisoned. Like the boat riding higher than she had in years.

"I'll think of a name," I said.

"I have a list."

"Of course you have a list."

"It's alphabetical."

Behind us, up the street, a door banged and Aldous's laugh rolled out of the guesthouse, followed by Voss's drier echo, followed—faintly, uncertainly, but unmistakably—by Tam's, high and startled, like a boy discovering the sound still worked. Three voices in the dark, getting to know each other over bad stew, and the river sliding past below, and the tea tasting of gasket, and my name in triplicate.

Freya nudged my shoulder with hers. "So. Captain. What do we call her?"

I looked at the boat, riding low and patient on her lines, holding water the way she always had—badly, stubbornly, faithfully—and I found, to my considerable surprise, that I already knew.
