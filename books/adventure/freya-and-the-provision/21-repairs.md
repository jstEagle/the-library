## 21. Repairs

The boatyard at Sallow's Bend smelled of hot pitch and cut cedar, and for three weeks it was the only weather I trusted.

I say *the boat*, but that was already a lie we'd all agreed to stop telling. The vessel that had carried us down from the gorge—half-sunk, half-heroic, held together by Ulyana's patching, my bailing, and what I can only describe as spite—had been hauled out onto the ways within a day of our arrival, and the shipwright, a broad woman named Ottelie with forearms like dock pilings, had walked around it twice, sucked her teeth, and said, "You want her repaired, or you want her *rebuilt*? Because what you've got here is a rumor of a boat."

Ulyana had said "repaired." I had said "rebuilt." We looked at each other, and Ulyana, who had spent ten years winning every argument by refusing to have them, said, "Rebuilt," in the tone of a man signing away a kidney.

So: rebuilt. New planking below the waterline, new ribs where the old ones had gone soft as biscuit, a new sternpost because the old one had been, in Ottelie's professional assessment, "more suggestion than timber." Every morning I came down to the yard and every morning there was less of the old boat and more of something else, and every morning Ulyana stood at the edge of the ways with his arms folded, watching his past get pried off plank by plank, wearing the expression of a man at a funeral who keeps almost laughing and doesn't know why.

"You could help," I told him on the fourth day.

"I am helping," he said. "I'm supervising."

"You're hovering."

"Hovering is a form of supervision."

Ottelie, driving a chisel, said without looking up, "In thirty years I have never once seen hovering fix a hull."

He did start helping, eventually. That was the thing about Ulyana Thornbury that took me longest to learn: he never agreed to anything. He simply drifted toward it, the way driftwood drifts toward a shore, and then pretended he'd always been headed there. By the second week he was living in the yard more than at the boarding house, sleeping under a tarp beside the ways because—and he said this with a completely straight face—"the wood needs to get used to me."

"The wood," I repeated.

"Boats remember," he said. "You want her remembering the right people."

I let it go. There were stranger philosophies on the Veyra, and most of them had kept men alive.

* * *

The letter came in the third week, forwarded through three hands and looking like it had been opened by all of them out of curiosity.

It was from the Meridian Institute. A real letterhead, real ink, a real name at the bottom belonging to a director of field programs whose reputation I knew from before—from the life before, the one with the testimony and the running. They wanted me. Not vaguely, not as a courtesy: they wanted me for the Karstfeld Icefields survey, two thousand kilometers east, a three-year post, equipment budget, a team of six, my own program. The kind of offer I would have crawled across broken glass for, back when I was still crawling toward things.

I read it four times sitting on an upturned dinghy in the yard, while around me the yard went about its noise—sawing, hammering, Ottelie swearing at a stubborn bolt with real artistry—and I felt the strangest sensation, which was that the letter weighed nothing at all.

Three years ago it would have been everything. It would have been the door back into my own life, the proof that running hadn't cost me everything, the restoration of Freya Ashcombe, glaciologist, to her proper shelf.

Now I sat there holding it and thought about ice cores, and about how the Karstfeld cores would be drilled, catalogued, shipped, and studied by people who would never stand on the ice they came from. And I thought about the Veyra's headwaters, which I had seen exactly once, from a leaking boat, in the worst weather of my life—the glacier above the gorge, blue-white and groaning, calving into the river with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Nobody had ever surveyed it properly. Nobody had ever surveyed it at all, really, because it was upriver of everywhere and downriver of nothing, and the maps ended in guesswork and a note that said *impassable in monsoon.*

Impassable in monsoon. I had, I reflected, recently become an expert in exactly that.

"You've got the face," said a voice behind me.

Tam leaned against the dinghy next to mine. My brother. Three weeks of regular meals had put flesh back on him; three weeks of not being a prisoner had given him back his habit of talking, which apparently no captivity on earth could fully suppress. He was thinner than he'd been, quieter in some rooms, but the talking had come back first, and I clung to that.

"What face?"

"The face you make when you're doing arithmetic you don't like." He nodded at the letter. "That's a good letter. That's the kind of letter people frame."

"It is."

"And?"

"And I'm thinking."

"Dangerous. You get that from Mother." He squinted at the letterhead upside down. "Karstfeld. That's—east, isn't it? Far east."

"Two thousand kilometers."

"Huh." He picked up a wood shaving and turned it over in his fingers. "And here I was just getting the hang of the bail rhythm."

Something in my chest pulled tight, and I made myself look at him properly. He wasn't asking me to stay. He was twenty-six years old and he'd spent two months in a smuggler's custody and he was not going to be the reason his sister turned down a career; I could see him building the sentence already, the generous one, the *you should take it, Frey, don't be ridiculous.* He'd been rehearsing it for days. I'd watched him almost say it twice.

"Tam," I said. "What do you know about glaciers?"

He blinked. "They're... big. Cold. Made of ice, if the name's anything to go by."

"They advance and retreat. They calve. They dam rivers and then release the dams and kill everyone downstream who didn't see it coming." I folded the letter along its crease. "There's a glacier above the gorge on the Veyra that nobody has ever studied. It calved while we were running it. It will calve again, probably next season, possibly worse, and there are settlements downstream—Sallow's Bend, Lowmark, the ferry villages—that have no idea. No gauge, no warning system, no data. Nothing."

Tam looked at me. Then at the boat on the ways, gleaming with new cedar, its ribs showing pale and clean. Then back at me.

"You want to study *that* one."

"I want to study that one."

"On *that* boat."

"She's being rebuilt."

"She's being *born*, by the look of it." He grinned, and for a moment he was twelve years old again, and my chest did the tight thing again but in a better way. "You know Ulyana's going to pretend he hates this."

"Oh, absolutely. For months."

"He'll say the science gear will weigh too much."

"It will weigh too much. That's why we'll need a bigger boat, and he'll hate saying that even more."

"He'll say the river's too dangerous for instruments."

"And then he'll pilot us up it anyway, because he can't help himself." I looked down at the letter again, at the director's confident signature, and felt—not regret, exactly. More like the feeling of setting down a pack you'd forgotten you were carrying. "I'm going to write back and ask them something impossible."

"Which is?"

"A satellite post. A station on the lower Veyra, funded through Meridian, staffed locally. If they want a glaciologist of my particular talents"—I tapped the letter, which listed them, embarrassingly, in order—"they can come to where the glacier actually is."

Tam whistled softly. "Negotiating."

"I learned from watching a man count cans twice."

* * *

Ulyana found me at dusk, down at the water's edge below the ways, skipping stones—or attempting to; the Veyra at Sallow's Bend ran slow and deep and swallowed stones without comment, which felt appropriate.

He sat down beside me without asking, which was itself new. Three months ago Ulyana Thornbury would have found a reason to be ten feet away and facing the other direction. Now he sat close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, and neither of us remarked on it, because remarking on it would have ruined it.

"Ottelie says ten days," he said. "Maybe eight, if the weather holds."

"She seems pleased with herself."

"She's earned it. She found timber in the stern that I swear wasn't from this boat originally. Previous owner patched her with somebody else's fence, I think." He turned a stone over in his long fingers. "She asked me today what we're calling her."

My stomach did a small, careful maneuver. "What did you say?"

"Said I hadn't decided." He skipped the stone; the river ate it. "Said I was waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the crew to weigh in." He said it flatly, eyes on the water, but I heard it—the word *crew*, plural, offered up casually like it had always been true. "Tam wants something dramatic. The tech—"

"Ilma."

"Ilma wants something in her mother's language, which I couldn't pronounce and Tam pronounced worse. The team leader—"

"Voss."

"—Voss suggested something dignified and boring, which figures. And Ottelie says whatever we pick, she's painting it herself, so we'd better mean it." He glanced sideways at me. "You haven't said anything."

"I didn't think it was mine to say."

Something moved across his face—quick, there and gone, but I caught it. Surprise, and under the surprise something that looked almost like hurt, quickly filed away the way he filed everything.

"It's yours to say," he said quietly. "More than most."

I looked at the water. The light was going amber and long, and the boat above us on the ways threw a shadow clear across the yard, longer than the old boat had ever been.

"My mother's name was Maren," I said. "She grew up on a river. Not this one—a smaller one, further south, the kind you can wade across. She used to say the river was the only honest neighbor she ever had. It never pretended to be anything but what it was that day." I picked up a stone. "When I was running—after the testimony, after everything—I kept thinking about that. About how I'd spent years surrounded by people who were lying professionally, including me, and how the only thing I missed was something that couldn't lie."

Ulyana said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing. But he'd gone still in the way he went still when he was listening with his whole body.

"*Maren,*" I said. "If you want it. If it isn't—it doesn't have to be. You owned her for ten years. You nearly died for her. It should be your—"

"Maren," he said, testing it, and then again, softer, like checking a knot. "Maren." He nodded slowly. "Yeah. Yeah, that's right. She was a mother's boat before she was mine, way I figured it. Time she had a mother's name again."

I laughed, unexpectedly, wetly, and he pretended very hard not to notice, which was its own kindness.

"There's one condition," he added.

"Of course there is."

"Nobody but us calls her that in front of Ottelie until the paint's dry. She'll want to redo the transom. Twice."

* * *

The letter to Meridian took me two evenings to write and I rewrote the crucial paragraph eleven times. In the end it was simpler than I'd feared:

*I am prepared to commit to a multi-year program of glacial monitoring on the upper Veyra watershed, including hazard assessment for downstream settlements. I will require a seasonal field station, instrument funding, and the freedom to access terrain your last three contractors described as impassable. I have a vessel. I have a crew. I have a pilot who reads this river better than any map you could buy me. What I don't have is patience for being studied from a distance, and I assume you're hiring me because you feel the same.*

The reply came back in nine days, which for a bureaucracy was practically telepathic. The director wrote one line that I cut out and kept in my notebook: *We stopped trying to hire people who want comfort years ago. Terms enclosed.*

Terms enclosed. A station at Lowmark. Instruments by spring. My name on the program.

I read it standing in the yard, and Tam read it over my shoulder, and Ilma—who had begun speaking again in fragments, mostly single words deployed like snipers' shots—read it and said, "Good," which from Ilma was a parade.

Voss shook my hand with both of his and said he'd write up everything he remembered of the headwaters terrain before he took the magistrate's amnesty and went home to his wife, and he did, forty pages of it, in handwriting like seismograph readings, and it became the founding document of the whole program.

And Ulyana read the terms, all of them, twice, sitting on the transom of the newly named *Maren* with fresh paint drying behind him, and then he handed the pages back and said:

"They're going to want schedules."

"Probably."

"Deliverables. Reports. Dates." He said each word like it was a species of parasite. "Scientists love dates."

"I'm a scientist."

"You're an exception I've chosen to overlook." He looked out at the river, which was running high and bright with early season melt, and I watched him do the thing he did—reading it, always reading it, the way other men read faces. "Icefield work means upriver past the gorge. In calving season. Every year."

"Every year," I agreed.

"With me counting cans."

"Somebody has to."

He was quiet a moment. Then, not looking at me, in the voice of a man drifting toward a shore he'd deny aiming for: "The forward hold will need reconfiguring. Instrument crates ride different than ore. Lower center of gravity, tied off proper. I'll draw it up."

"That's a yes?"

"That's a *design consideration*."

From inside the cabin, where he was supposedly oiling hinges, Tam called out, "That's a yes! I heard it! Everybody heard it!"

"Mind your hinges," Ulyana said, and the tips of his ears went red, and I decided to treasure that sight for the rest of my life.

* * *

We launched on a morning so clear the mountains looked close enough to argue with. Ottelie had painted *MAREN* on the transom in letters deep enough to survive anything short of a direct geological event, and she stood on the dock with her arms crossed, watching us cast off, and she said, "Bring her back with her bottom intact this time, or next rebuild comes out of your hides," and Ulyana said, "No promises," and she laughed, which I suspected she did rarely and never for free.

Ilma took the bow without being assigned to it. Voss's forty pages rode in a waterproof case by the helm. Tam claimed the galley by right of conquest and immediately began organizing it worse than anyone had ever organized anything. And I stood amidships with my hand on new cedar that still smelled like the tree it used to be, and felt the river take us, patient and honest, pretending to be nothing but what it was that day.

Ulyana brought us around into the current, easy as breathing, and looked back at me once—just once, quick, checking, the way he checked the radio hourly and counted the cans twice—and whatever he saw in my face must have satisfied him, because he turned forward and didn't check again.

"Lowmark by dark," he said. "Then we'll see about the rest."

Above us, far upriver past the bend, past the rapids, past the gorge, a glacier we had never properly met waited with its blue-white patience, calving its warnings into the water. Come spring, we'd go and listen.

But that was spring. This was now, and the river was wide, and the boat was sound, and every soul aboard had chosen to be there—which, I was beginning to understand, was the entire difference between a crew and a cargo.

"Sandbar," Tam called lazily from the galley window, pointing at open water.

"That's not a sandbar," Ulyana said.

"It's a future sandbar. I'm thinking ahead."

"Get back to your hinges."

I laughed, and the *Maren* ran on, and ahead of us the whole bright river unrolled like a page that hadn't been written on yet.
