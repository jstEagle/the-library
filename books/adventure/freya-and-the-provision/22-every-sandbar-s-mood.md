## 22. Every Sandbar's Mood

The Veyra woke before he did, the way it always had.

Ulyana stood at the wheel with his first cup of coffee going lukewarm in one hand and watched the dawn come up gray-gold over the headwater hills, and for a long moment he did nothing at all. No chart. No soundings. Just him and the water, reading it the way other men read a newspaper—slowly, with pleasure, skipping nothing.

The river was low and clear this morning, the last week of dry season, which meant every sandbar in the upper reach was showing its hand early. He could see them from here: the pale seam of gravel below Widow's Bend, the long brown shoulder of the bar they called the Coffin because it killed a barge every few years and never explained why. The current bent around it in a lazy S, and where the current bent, the channel ran deep on the outside, close against the cutbank, tight past the deadfall that leaned out over the water like an old man eavesdropping.

Ten years ago he'd have taken the inside line anyway, just to prove he could.

These days he took the outside line because it was correct, and there was a difference between those two things that had cost him a decade to learn.

"Sandbar," said Freya's voice from the bow, easy as breathing. "Port side, maybe forty meters. It wasn't there Tuesday."

He looked. She was right. A fresh tongue of silt had built up overnight below the bend, barely awash, the kind of thing you'd only see if you were watching for the way the current wrinkled over it—a smooth place in a rough sentence. He hadn't spotted it yet. She had.

He drank his coffee to hide whatever his face was doing.

"Noted," he said.

"You're welcome."

"I was going to see it."

"In another hundred meters, sure." She turned back to her watch, one hand on the rail, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of survey flagging tape she'd refused to throw away since the gorge. "I'm just saying I saw it first."

Behind him, from the deck where the cargo tarps made a cave, came the sound of Tomas Ashcombe laughing at something, and then the mute tech—Mira, though she'd only started answering to the name three weeks ago—laughing too, a small startled sound like she still couldn't quite believe it belonged to her. Ulyana didn't turn around. Some things you didn't look at directly or they'd spook, like deer, like luck.

His crew. Four mouths plus his own, on a boat built for two, and somehow it worked. The re-planked hull rode sweet and high, the new planking smelling of pitch and cedar whenever the sun hit it, and the pumps hadn't run once since the refit except when Tomas ran them out of what he called professional curiosity and what Ulyana called an invitation to break something.

* * *

They'd loaded at First Settlement two days ago: survey resupply, a crate of books Freya had ordered and paid for out of her new stipend, seed potatoes for the widow at the relay station who'd fed them stew the night everything ended, and—lashed down under the tarp with more care than anything else aboard—the ore samples, sealed and tagged, bound for the magistrate's evidence room by way of the university lab. Kessler's trial was set for after monsoon. Freya would testify. So, in his own fashion, would Ulyana; the magistrate had asked him, very politely, whether the man who surrendered the cache might also care to explain the name on the boat's old registration, and Ulyana had said yes, and discovered that the word cost almost nothing anymore.

That was the strange part. He kept waiting for it to hurt.

For ten years he'd been nobody, running cargo on a boat with no name worth painting, dodging the harbormaster's questions and the debt collectors' letters and eventually, without ever deciding to, himself. Invisibility had been a skill. He'd been good at it. And now here he was with his name signed in a magistrate's ledger and a crew who knew his face and a woman at his bow who read water better than half the licensed pilots on the river, and the sky hadn't fallen. The sky, in fact, was doing that thing it did in late dry season—high and hard and blue, with the first monsoon towers stacked up far to the north like furniture under sheets.

"Ulyana." Mira had come forward, quiet as always, holding out a tin cup. "Tomas says try."

It was coffee, mostly. There was something else in it. He sipped. Cinnamon, possibly. Possibly cardamom. Possibly a mistake.

"It's terrible," he said.

Her whole face lit up. She went back aft walking taller, and he heard her report—"He says it's terrible"—and Tomas's delighted roar, and understood that he had just been initiated into something and there was no undoing it.

* * *

Widow's Bend passed under the keel at midmorning, clean and uneventful, and Ulyana found himself almost disappointed. That was the trouble with a good boat and a good crew: the river stopped being a fight and became a conversation, and some old stubborn part of him missed the fight.

Not enough to go looking for it, though.

Freya came back to the wheelhouse around noon, bringing the wind with her and a plate of rice and beans that she set on the chart table without asking if he wanted it. She'd learned that about him months ago—that asking got you a refusal on principle, but setting food down got it eaten.

"The Coffin's next," she said, settling onto the bench. "You've been quiet since the bend."

"I'm always quiet."

"You've been *quiet* quiet." She watched the water ahead with him, shoulder almost touching his. "Talk."

He considered not talking. Old habit, well-worn, comfortable as a broken-in boot. But that was the thing about armor—you got so used to the weight you forgot you were wearing it, and then one day someone handed you a plate of rice and beans and you took it off without noticing.

"When I ran this stretch alone," he said, "the Coffin was the best part of the trip. Because it was mine. Every rock in it, every shift of the bar, year to year—I knew it like my own hands. Nobody else did. That was the point." He chewed a bite of beans. "Now there's four people on my boat and a glaciologist calling sandbars from my bow, and I keep waiting to feel like I've lost something."

"And?"

"And I don't." He said it plainly, because it deserved plainness. "I keep checking. It's not there. Whatever it was I thought I'd lose—it turns out it was just the loneliness. I was guarding the loneliness like it was treasure."

Freya was quiet a moment. Then: "My brother does the same thing with the engine. Keeps checking it's still fixed."

"Tomas checks the engine because he enjoys taking things apart."

"He checks it because for six weeks he thought he was going to die on a leaking boat in a gorge, and now he likes confirming that things work." She shrugged. "We all have our rituals. Yours is checking whether you're still miserable."

"That's not—" He stopped. Considered. "Fair."

"I know," she said, and bumped his shoulder with hers, and went back to the bow.

* * *

The Coffin came up on them an hour later, and it had changed.

Ulyana saw it from eight hundred meters and felt the old alertness settle over him like a coat—the bar had shifted with the dry-season drawdown, the whole shoulder swung twenty meters downstream, and the channel that had run deep along the cutbank in spring now funneled hard over the bar's new tail. The water told him all of it, if you spoke the language: the color change, the boil lines, the way the riffle downstream had gone glassy-smooth where the deep hole used to be.

"Bar's moved," Freya called back, before he'd said a word. "Downstream. Big shift."

"I see it."

"Outside line?"

"Outside line." He eased the throttle down, brought the bow around wide, and took them through the deep channel hugging the far bank, slow and deliberate, the current leaning on the hull like a drunk friend. Behind him he heard Mira counting cans again—she'd picked the habit up from him somewhere along the way, and he'd decided not to be offended—and Tomas humming something tuneless over the pump handles he wasn't using.

They cleared the tail of the bar by thirty meters. Textbook. Boring, even.

And then, because the river forgave nothing and never had, the deadfall on the cutbank chose that moment to let go.

It had been dying for years—he'd watched it lean further every season, meaning to mention it to somebody, meaning to do something about it, the way you mean to do things about rivers. Now the bank gave a wet sigh and the whole trunk slid, roots and all, into the channel behind them, exactly where they'd have been if they'd run the inside line, exactly where any pilot running this stretch on last month's memory would have been. It hit the water with a boom that rolled up the valley, and the current grabbed it and swung it broadside across the old channel like a gate closing.

Nobody said anything for a second.

"Well," said Tomas, appearing at the wheelhouse door with a mug of Mira's terrible coffee. "Inside line would've been bad."

"Inside line would've been the end of us," Ulyana said.

Freya had come aft too, and the four of them stood looking back at the deadfall swinging slowly in the current, a tree that had waited years for its chance and missed by thirty meters and one moved sandbar.

"You spotted the shift," Freya said. "Before we even got close. You changed the plan and didn't even announce it."

"You'd have seen it too."

"Maybe. Maybe not." She looked at him sideways. "That's the thing about you, Ulyana Thornbury. You think you're just driving a boat. You're actually keeping four people alive and you make it look like nothing."

"It's not nothing," he said. "It's just mine. What I'm good at." He turned back to the wheel, embarrassed, and added, because it was true and because a year ago he couldn't have said it: "Ours, I mean. It's ours now. All of it. The boat, the route, the reading of the water. I'm done pretending otherwise."

Mira, at the rail, smiled at the river. Tomas raised his mug. Freya said nothing at all, but she stayed at the wheelhouse instead of going forward, and that was answer enough.

* * *

They camped that night on the long gravel bar below the bend, the one Ulyana had privately named years ago and never told anyone, because naming things felt like claiming them and claiming things felt like losing them. He told them now. "Heron Bar," he said, while the fire caught and the pots went on. "Because of the herons. Obviously."

"There are no herons," Tomas said.

"There are herons in season."

"It's October."

"They know when they're wanted."

But the herons came—two of them, gray ghosts wading the shallows at dusk, patient as monks—and Tomas stared at them and then at Ulyana and decided, wisely, to let it go. They ate rice and beans improved beyond recognition by whatever Mira put in them, and Freya spread the charts on a tarp and traced tomorrow's run with a stick, and argued with Ulyana about the crossing at Two Sisters, and lost, and pretended she hadn't, and he let her pretend.

Later, when the others had turned in, he sat by the coals and listened to the river.

It sounded the same as it always had. That was the mercy of rivers—they didn't change their voice for your benefit. Ten years ago this same water had carried a man running from a debt and a name, alone on a boat built for two, telling himself solitude was a discipline instead of a wound. The water hadn't cared then and didn't care now. It would drown him tomorrow as cheerfully as it would have drowned him yesterday, if he gave it the chance.

But he wouldn't give it the chance. Not alone, anyway. That was the whole difference, and it fit in a sentence.

* * *

Dawn came up rose and copper, and they took the crossing at Two Sisters exactly as argued, and it went exactly as Ulyana said it would, and Freya conceded this with a grace so thin you could see through it, and everyone laughed, including Mira, including Ulyana, who was out of practice and getting better.

By afternoon the boat was running the long home reach, the water wide and brown-green and kind-looking, which meant nothing, but today it mostly behaved. The monsoon towers to the north had grown through the day, blue-black and towering, still a day's march off, close enough to smell—the green metallic smell of rain coming, the smell that meant the season was turning and the river with it.

Ulyana stood at the wheel with the wind in his face and his crew scattered down the length of his—*their*—boat, and let himself feel it, all of it, without flinching. The fear was still there; it would always be there; the wilderness forgave nothing and neither did the water, and he'd haul Kessler out of the gorge again tomorrow rather than pretend otherwise. But underneath the fear, steady as ballast, was the other thing. The thing he'd spent ten years mistaking for weakness.

Company.

"Sandbar!" Freya's voice rang out from the bow, bright with triumph. "Starboard side, fifty meters, fresh—one you haven't spotted yet!"

He looked. She was right. She was always right, lately, and he was beginning to suspect she planned it.

"Noted," he called back, and grinned at the water, and brought the boat around.
