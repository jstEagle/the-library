## 1. The Blacklist

The pawnbroker's window on Corren Street still held her compass, three weeks after she'd left it there, and every morning Yara Amberdale walked past it the long way so she wouldn't have to look.

She looked anyway. She always did. The brass case sat behind the glass between a cracked fiddle and a child's silver cup, its needle frozen at some angle that meant nothing now, pointing at nothing, and Yara would stand in the cold with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and think: *that needle has pointed true through whiteout, through rockfall fog, through the worst night of my life, and here it is, priced at forty crowns.*

Forty crowns. She had eleven of them, folded soft as cloth in an inner pocket she checked too often, the way a man with a bad tooth checks it with his tongue. The rest went where the rest always went — to the ledger in the back room of the Waystone Tavern, where Marta Ostrow kept accounts for half the town's unfortunates, and where the name written above Yara's balance was not her own.

*Estate of Denna Amberdale.* Her sister. Dead fourteen months, and still costing her.

Yara had inherited more than grief from Denna. She'd inherited the loan Denna had taken against their mother's house to buy the outfitting gear that now sat rotting in a storage shed Yara couldn't afford to keep dry, and she'd inherited the interest, which compounded like frost heave — slow, patient, lifting everything out of true. Two hundred and sixty crowns. It might as well have been two thousand. On the wages a blacklisted guide could earn hauling gravel and mending fence line in a border town that didn't trust her, it was a horizon without a far side.

The blacklist was the other inheritance. That one came from the mountain itself, or from what people said about the mountain — the Serrin headwaters disaster, two springs gone, nine clients dead under an avalanche that the inquiry had blamed on "route judgment errors attributable to the senior guide." They'd never proved anything. They'd never needed to. Her license was revoked in one country, her entry barred in the other, and the story had traveled faster than either decree, mutating as it went. In some versions she'd led them into the slide for the insurance money. In others she'd survived by abandoning her rope team. Both were lies. Neither mattered. A reputation is a bridge, and hers had been cut behind her.

So she did what work she could get. Trail clearing below the tree line, where no client's life depended on her judgment and no one had to sign anything saying they trusted her. Fence posts along the valley pastures. One miserable week gutting and re-shingling the smokehouse for the tanner, whose wife fed her stew and whose husband paid her late and short. Her hands, which had once read rope and rock the way scholars read print, were going rough and stupid with axe work. Some nights she caught herself tracing routes on the tabletop with a blunt pencil — the Serrin gorge, the high traverse past the cirque — drawing maps of places she was forbidden to see again, for no one, for nothing, just to remember she still could.

It was on one of those nights, third week of thaw, that Tomas Vey walked into the Waystone.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him, because the tavern went quiet in that particular way rooms do when someone arrives who changes the weather. Not fear exactly. Attention. Tomas Vey had that effect in border country; he was known the way storms are known, respected the way wolves are, and disliked, mostly, the way both are.

He was broader than she remembered, or perhaps she was narrower. Grey had gotten into his beard at the jaw. He wore river clothes — good ones, waxed canvas, new rope coiled over one shoulder like a man who wanted to be seen carrying it — and his boots had the particular mud of the lower Serrin valley, which any guide would know and no farmer would track in by accident.

He found her at the corner table without asking anyone, which told her he'd asked before he came.

"Amberdale." He set a mug down across from her uninvited and sat. "You look terrible."

"You look rich."

"Good season." He turned the mug but didn't drink. Around them the tavern resumed its noise carefully, conversations bending back up out of the hush, though every ear within ten feet had tilted toward them like grass toward sun. "I need a crew."

"Then you're talking to the wrong woman. I don't crew anymore. I haul gravel."

"I know what you haul." He leaned in slightly, voice dropping to the register he used for clients, warm and level and utterly without warmth. "I'm offering triple your best season's wage. Lead rate. Your name on the manifest as senior guide."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped stone into a well — she heard herself want them before she could stop the wanting, heard the splash of it, deep down. Triple wages. Six months of triple wages and Denna's ledger closed, the compass back in her pocket, the shed rent paid through winter. She let herself feel all of that for exactly the length of one breath.

Then she said, "Where?"

"The Serrin River. Source to mouth — sorry." He almost smiled. "Mouth to source. Upriver. Full expedition."

The tavern noise seemed to thin around the word *Serrin*, the way air thins before weather. Someone at the bar laughed too loudly at nothing.

"No," Yara said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard *Serrin*. Everything after that is decoration." She pushed her chair back. "That route is sworn off, Tomas. By you as much as me. You stood at Denna's funeral and swore it. Everyone heard you."

"I stood at your sister's funeral because you wouldn't speak to me otherwise," he said, unmoved, "and yes, I swore it, and I've kept that oath for two years while eating nothing I liked. This is different. This is a contract, not a route. I'm not asking you to love the river. I'm asking you to run it once, paid beyond reason, and never look at it again."

"Who's the client?"

"A consortium man. Survey work." He said it smoothly, but she watched his eyes go flat and fixed on the middle distance for half a second — the tell he'd never known he had, the one she'd catalogued across six years of shared rope. He was lying, or hiding, or both. "Pell's his name. Harmless sort. Maps and instruments."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire someone worse than you, and I'd rather not." He stood, leaving the untouched mug. "Three days. I'm at the ferry house. Think about what forty crowns buys you these days."

He knew about the compass. Of course he knew. Word traveled in border towns the way water travels downhill, finding every low place.

She watched him go, hating him with the old clean hatred, which was at least familiar, and then hating herself a little for how much of her hatred was really just hunger wearing armor.

* * *

Two days later, the debt collector arrived.

His name was Brakke and he was not a cruel man, which made him worse. Cruel men you could hate and be done with. Brakke was polite, patient, and absolutely certain, the way winter is certain, and he sat down across from her at the same corner table — she was beginning to think that table cursed — and opened Marta Ostrow's ledger to her page as gently as a priest opening scripture.

"Two hundred and sixty," he said. "Plus the month accrued. Two seventy-one."

"I'll have it."

"When?" He didn't ask aggressively. He asked the way a man asks about weather he can already see coming over the ridge. "Mrs. Ostrow has carried this estate longer than she should have. There's talk of selling the debt. Debt buyers are less patient than we are, Miss Amberdale. And less gentle about collateral."

"What collateral? There's nothing left."

"There's the gear in the shed. There's—" he paused, delicately, "—there's you. Not that anyone sells a person anymore. But a woman with no license, no assets, and a debt she can't service finds her options narrowing in ways I'm paid to describe. Assisted passage south, indentured trail labor on the canal cuts. Legal. Common, even."

Yara looked at her hands on the table. Axe-rough, rope-scarred, a stranger's hands. Somewhere behind the pawnbroker's glass her compass pointed at nothing.

"How long," she said, "if I take the canal work, before the debt clears?"

"Four years. If rates hold."

Four years. Four years of the canal cuts, underground mostly, in country flatter than a lie, and she would come out the other end of it someplace in her forties with her eyes ruined for distance and her route-memory gone soft, and the mountains would have finished forgetting her.

Brakke closed the ledger. "I'm not your enemy, Miss Amberdale. I'd genuinely rather you cleared this honestly. But honest options require income, and income requires someone willing to hire you, and we both know the list of such someones is short." He rose, tucked the ledger under his arm, and added, almost kindly: "There was a man asking after you at the ferry house yesterday. Offering good money. I mention it only because I mention everything."

* * *

That night she walked the long way past the pawnshop again, and this time she stopped, and pressed her palm flat against the cold glass, and let herself admit the truth she'd been walking around for two days.

Pride was a luxury. She'd known it intellectually since the avalanche; she knew it in her bones now. Pride was something you could afford when you had a license and a partner and a sister alive to split the winter costs with. Pride was gear for people whose ropes held. Hers hadn't. Whatever the inquiry said, whatever the townsfolk repeated, the truth was simpler and worse: her route, her judgment, her lead, and nine people had gone into the ice. Whether the mountain betrayed her or she betrayed the mountain, the dead were equally dead, and she was equally broke, and Tomas Vey was the only man in two countries willing to put her name at the top of a manifest.

And he was lying about something. She was sure of it. Sure the way she'd been sure, once, reading a snowpack that everyone else called stable — the certainty that comes from underneath, from the grain of things.

She could refuse him and keep her pride and lose four years of her life to a canal. Or she could sign on with a liar, up a cursed river, toward whatever it was he wasn't telling her, and keep her eyes open the whole way.

Yara stood at the glass a long moment, breath fogging it, blurring the compass to a smudge of brass.

Then she turned north, toward the ferry house, and went to find out what Tomas Vey was hiding.
