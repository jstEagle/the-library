## 2. Terms of the Sworn-Off

Tomas Vey had learned long ago that the best way to read a person was to watch what they did with their hands. Yara Amberdale's hands, that morning in the Drowned Stag, were wrapped around a mug of something cheap and brown, and they were perfectly still — which told him she was furious. A woman who moved her hands was thinking about leaving. A woman who held them still was deciding how much it would cost her to stay.

He set the contract on the table between them and did not push it closer.

"Triple wages," he said. "Hazard pay on top. Outfitting covered by the client."

"I know what you said last night." Her voice was flat as river ice in late autumn. "I said no."

"You said no when I offered it like a favor." He sat down across from her without being invited, and she let him, which he noted with the small private satisfaction of a man who had studied her for fifteen years and still found new things to study. "This isn't a favor. It's a contract. You can hate me and sign it at the same time. I've seen you do harder things with less money involved."

The tavern was nearly empty at this hour — just the barkeep polishing glasses that were already clean, and an old trapper by the window who pretended to sleep but whose ears, Tomas knew, missed nothing in a town like Corren's Ford. Everything here missed nothing. That was the trouble with border towns: they were all scar tissue, and scar tissue remembered.

Yara looked at the contract the way she'd once looked at cornices — assessing the load, judging whether it would hold or come down on top of her.

"Who's the client?"

"A mining interest. Consortium-backed. They want their surveyor taken up the Serrin to the headwaters."

"The Serrin doesn't have headwaters you can reach," she said. "That's rather the point of it."

"They believe otherwise. They have maps."

"Everyone has maps." She finally lifted the mug and drank, and Tomas watched her grimace at whatever was in it. "Maps are how people die, Tomas. I've buried enough of them."

*We've buried enough of them,* he almost said. He kept it behind his teeth where it belonged, along with everything else he wasn't going to tell her.

Here is what Tomas Vey omitted, sitting there in his good coat with his ledger-clean reputation and his client's advance folded in his breast pocket: six years ago, after the avalanche on the Col de Sarrat took four lives and both their careers, there had been a second document. Not the inquiry report everyone read — the other one. The one he and Yara had drafted together at a kitchen table at three in the morning, still smelling of smoke and grief, swearing off the upper Serrin forever. *No party shall guide above the falls. No fee justifies it.* They had signed it in front of witnesses. He had kept his copy in a drawer for two years before burning it, which he had never told anyone, least of all her.

And here is what else he omitted: the client had shown him photographs of the lost survey team's last camp, recovered from a courier's body pulled out of the lower gorge in spring. In the background of one photograph, half-blurred, there was a seam of ore in the canyon wall that made the client's voice change when he talked about it. And in another photograph — this one Tomas had studied alone, late, with the lamp turned low — there was evidence that the team's own supply cache had been cut open from the inside. Sabotage. Someone had wanted that expedition to fail slowly, in the dark, far from any witness.

He had not mentioned this to the client either. The client paid him to find guides, not opinions.

"You're quiet," Yara said. "You're never quiet. Quiet is how I know you're lying."

"I'm being professional."

"You're being *paid*." She turned the contract toward herself and read it properly, top to bottom, the way she did everything — thoroughly, suspiciously, with her jaw set. "Escort services. River passage to the source of the Serrin. Provisioning for six weeks." Her finger stopped. "Six weeks? The run to the falls is ten days if the water's kind."

"The client wants thoroughness. Survey work takes time."

"The client wants—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at it, watched the color shift in her face the way light shifts on snow when a slope goes from stable to load-bearing. "There was already a team up there, wasn't there. That's why the wages are triple. That's why you came to me instead of hiring some licensed outfit that wouldn't ask questions."

"The previous engagement ended prematurely."

"That's a beautiful sentence, Tomas. Say what it means."

"It means four men went up the Serrin in the spring and none of them have come down."

The barkeep stopped polishing. The old trapper by the window shifted in his chair. In Corren's Ford, news traveled at the speed of debt, and everyone in the room had just recalculated what Tomas Vey's business was worth.

Yara pushed the contract back across the table. "No."

"Yara—"

"No. You don't get to walk in here with your clean boots and your consortium money and ask me to break the one promise I've kept since—" Her voice caught, just barely, on something. She smoothed it flat again with visible effort. "Since the worst day of my life. Which, in case you've forgotten, you also survived, so you know exactly what you're asking."

"I know precisely what I'm asking." He leaned forward. This was the part he had rehearsed, and hated himself for rehearsing, and done anyway. "And I know about the debt."

Her stillness changed quality. It went from fury to something colder and older.

"You wouldn't."

"Halden's note. Four hundred crowns, carried over from your partner's estate. It comes due at the end of the month, and Halden sold it — I checked — to a man named Brakk, who collects debts in ways that don't involve courts." Tomas held her gaze because looking away would have been worse than cruelty; it would have been pity, and she would have broken a mug over his head for pity. "I can buy that note this afternoon. Tear it up. Or I can leave it in Brakk's hands and wish you luck."

For a long moment she said nothing at all. Outside the window, the Serrin itself ran gray and swollen through the middle of town, loud even through glass, the way it was always loud, the way it had been loud the day it took everything from them. Tomas thought: *she's doing the arithmetic.* Not the money. The other arithmetic — the one where she weighed her pride against her survival and knew, as he knew, exactly which side was heavier. Pride was a luxury good. He'd learned that too. He'd learned it later than she had, and more gently, and he resented her a little for it even now.

"You planned this," she said quietly. "The tavern, the timing, the collector. All of it."

"I planned to make you an offer. The collector is just weather."

"The collector is a *lever*, and you pulled it." She picked up the pen. Held it over the signature line without touching it to paper. "Tell me one thing honestly, and I'll sign. Did the lost team go missing, or were they left?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped ice axe. Tomas kept his face level through long practice and felt it cost him something real underneath.

"They went missing," he said, which was true, and said nothing about the cut cache, which was also a choice, and knew that she could hear the shape of the thing he wasn't saying, because she always could.

She signed. The pen scratched once, twice — her name, then the date — and she shoved the contract back at him as though it were hot.

"There. Now we're both damned." She stood, drained the rest of the terrible coffee, and looked down at him with eyes that held no warmth and no forgiveness and, beneath all of it, something worse: a wary, grudging, professional respect that neither of them would ever name aloud. "One condition. My crew, my rules on the water. You stay out of my way and keep your client's leash short. If I say turn back, we turn back."

"Agreed."

"And Tomas?" She slung her pack over one shoulder. "If you're hiding something — anything — about why we swore off that river, I will find it. I always found things. It's how I got blacklisted."

She walked out into the gray morning, and Tomas sat alone with the signed contract and the sound of the river, feeling the particular hollowness of a man who has won exactly what he wanted.

He told himself the omissions were mercy. He told himself she was safer not knowing about the photographs, the sabotage, the men the client had promised were merely *insurance*. He told himself all of it, the way he'd been telling himself things for six years.

Then he folded the contract away, left coins on the table, and went to buy a dead woman's debt from a man named Brakk — and if his hand shook slightly as he counted out the crowns, well. The tavern was empty, and no one saw.

* * *

Three days later, at first light, the crew assembled at the landing below the falls. Yara arrived last, as captains do, and stood at the bow of the lead boat checking lashings while the green deckhand fumbled knots and the cook said nothing at all. The client's surveyor — Pell, narrow and quick-eyed, with instruments packed in wool — was already aboard, asking the deckhand cheerful, precise questions about the upper gorge that made Yara's hands pause on the rope.

Tomas watched from the bank. Yara looked up once, found him, and held his gaze across the water for a long, unreadable moment. Then she turned back to her crew and gave the order to cast off, and the current took them, and Tomas stood on the landing with the client's sealed instructions in his pocket, heavier than any contract, watching the river carry them all toward the thing he had sworn — once, at a kitchen table, in front of witnesses — never to do.

The water was high. The season was late. And somewhere upstream, in a gorge that ate its visitors, four men were still unaccounted for.

Tomas turned and began the long portage around the falls, following the only trail that mattered now: the one he'd chosen, and could no longer leave.
