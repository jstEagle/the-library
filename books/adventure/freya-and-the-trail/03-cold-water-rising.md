## 3. Cold Water Rising

The Serrin ran green the first morning, the color of bottle glass held up to the sun, and for a few hours Yara Amberdale let herself believe the river had forgotten her.

She stood at the stern of the lead raft with one hand on the sweep oar and watched the water braid itself around gravel bars still silvered with frost. Ten years since she'd run this stretch. Ten years, and the current hadn't changed its mind about anything — same lazy bends below the confluence, same standing waves where the valley pinched at Wolf Jaw, same smell of cold stone and pine resin rising off the banks as the sun burned the mist away. Rivers were honest that way. They kept no grudges and told no lies, which was more than she could say for anyone who'd hired her in the last decade.

Behind her, the crew was learning each other's rhythms the way crews always did: badly at first.

The deckhand — Fen, nineteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, hired off the docks at Corren's Landing because he was cheap and could tie a bowline without being shown twice — was already on his third mistake of the morning. He'd loaded the forward dry-bag wrong, lashed it crooked, then nearly gone over the side reaching for a paddle he'd stowed out of reach. Yara had fished him back by the collar of his jacket without breaking stride, and he'd mumbled thanks at his boots and not met her eyes since.

"He'll do," said the cook.

Yara glanced over her shoulder. The cook's name was Anselm, though nobody aboard seemed to know that yet; he'd signed the manifest as *A. Voss* and answered to nothing but *cook*. He was fifty, maybe older, built like a fence post left out too many winters, and he had not spoken an unnecessary word since Tomas introduced them three days ago. He cooked like a man performing surgery — precise, unhurried, faintly contemptuous of anyone watching — and when Fen had dropped a pot on the first night, Anselm had simply picked it up, set it back on the fire, and continued as if the sound had never occurred.

"You know him?" Yara asked quietly. "Vey's man?"

"I know his hands." Anselm turned a trout on the flat iron without looking up from the fire. "River hands. Old ones."

That was all she got. She'd stopped expecting more. There were people who came to the high country to leave something behind, and Anselm carried his leaving in his shoulders the way other men carried packs. Whatever name he'd buried, he'd buried it deep enough that even Tomas's money hadn't dug it up. Yara found she didn't mind. Silence, on a river, was a kind of courtesy.

The fourth member of the expedition was another matter entirely.

Pell sat amidships on the second raft with the client's instrument cases strapped around him like armor, and he had not stopped asking questions since Corren's Landing. Not the questions a surveyor ought to ask — elevations, flow rates, barometric readings — though he asked those too, diligently, scratching figures into a waterproof notebook with a pencil stub worn to half its length. It was the *other* questions that had begun to itch at her.

*How wide is the gorge at the narrows? Could a body be carried through it? How long before wreckage would surface downstream — days? Weeks? What's the water temperature above the treeline in late summer?*

He'd asked that last one over breakfast, casual as asking for salt, and Yara had caught herself answering before she understood why the question mattered. Now she did understand, or suspected she did, and the understanding sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. A man didn't ask how long the river kept what it took unless he wanted to know whether the river had given something back.

"Guide." Pell's voice carried across the water between rafts, thin and precise. "You said the portage trail above the gorge runs along the north bank. Is there a southern alternative?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the south wall is two hundred feet of wet granite with a river at the bottom of it."

"And the north trail — is it marked?"

"It's marked by the people who died finding it." She let the sweep bite and swung the raft toward the inside of the bend. "Which is why we take it slow, which is why you keep your questions until camp, and which is why you sit down and hold onto something, because Wolf Jaw is coming up and it doesn't care what's written in your notebook."

Pell sat. He even smiled — a small, apologetic thing, the smile of a man accustomed to being forgiven for his curiosity. Yara turned back to the water and tried to decide whether she disliked him. She couldn't quite manage it. He was earnest in a way that felt almost painful, like a dog that keeps bringing you things because it doesn't know any better. But earnest men following bad orders had ruined her life once already, and she watched him now the way she'd watch weather building behind a ridge.

* * *

They made good time for three days.

That was the trap of the Serrin below the gorge — it lulled you. The valley opened into meadowland gold with early autumn, elk moving along the timberline at dusk like smoke given legs, the rapids nothing worse than Class Three, generous and playful. Fen stopped flinching at every wave and started grinning at them. Anselm produced bread baked in a reflector oven that should have been impossible on a raft, and ate his own portion standing apart, facing upstream, always upstream. Even Yara caught herself humming on the second evening while she checked the lashings, some old route-song her partner Dev used to sing, and stopped immediately, embarrassed, as if the mountains might have heard.

Tomas ran sweep on the second raft and said little. That was unlike him. Tomas Vey had never in his life encountered a silence he couldn't improve upon, and his quiet now had a texture to it — watchful, weighted, the quiet of a man counting something. Yara told herself it was the contract sitting on him. Triple wages bought triple worry. She told herself a lot of things those first three days.

On the fourth morning they passed the last homestead, a roofless cabin with a stovepipe leaning like a drunk against the chimney, and after that there was nothing human ahead of them but themselves. The valley began to narrow. The water changed voice — deeper, colder, more insistent — and the peaks closed overhead until noon was a rumor and the river ran in blue shadow.

It was Fen who saw it.

He was on point raft with Yara that day, learning the sweep under her hand, and he shouted so suddenly she nearly swept them into the bank. "*There!* Down! On the gravel — that's a—"

She saw it before he finished. A pale shape wedged among the stones on the inside of the bend, half in the water, and her mind — guide's mind, the part of her that read rivers the way priests read scripture — sorted it in half a heartbeat. Not driftwood. Driftwood didn't have rivets.

"Hard left," she said, and put them ashore.

It was a canoe. Or what remained of one — aluminum, expedition-grade, the kind the consortium's survey teams used, torn open along one gunwale like something had peeled it. The hull number had been ground away, deliberately, by someone with a file and time to spare. Lashed to the thwarts were two dry-bags, split and empty, their contents long since scattered or taken. A paddle floated nearby, snapped at the throat.

Yara crouched beside it and did not touch it for a long moment.

Here was the thing she knew and the others didn't, the thing that made her hands go cold despite the sun: the lost survey team had come up this river eleven months ago. Eleven months of spring floods, of winter ice grinding the shallows, of a full year of the Serrin doing what the Serrin did to everything left on it. Wreckage from that expedition should have been pulp by now — shredded, scoured, distributed across forty miles of gravel in pieces too small to swear at. This canoe had been in the water weeks. Maybe less.

"Fen," she said. "Back to the raft. Don't touch anything else."

"But—"

"*Now.*"

He went. Anselm appeared at her shoulder without seeming to cross the intervening distance, silent as falling snow, and looked down at the hull with an expression Yara couldn't read. Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, or memory. He crouched, studied the torn aluminum, and touched the edge of the rip with one finger.

"Not ice," he said. First complete sentence she'd gotten from him in four days. "Ice tears lengthwise. This is across the grain. Rock, or—" His finger stopped. "Or a winch cable."

Yara looked at him. He looked back, and whatever lived in his past surfaced for exactly one second — long enough for her to see it was not a gentle thing — and then submerged again.

Tomas came down the bank fast, taking the stones two at a time, and Yara watched his face as he saw the canoe. She watched very carefully. And what she saw was not surprise.

"Tomas." She stood. "How fresh does this look to you?"

"How should I know? I'm not a—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard, a blade laid on a table. "Don't insult us both. You've known since Corren's Landing. Maybe longer. So tell me right now, standing here, with him watching—" she tipped her head at Pell, who had come down to the water's edge with his notebook already open, god help him, "—what are we actually floating into?"

For a moment the only sound was the river, patient and indifferent, worrying at the stones.

"The client says the survey team went missing in the upper gorge," Tomas said at last. "Avalanche season, bad weather, the usual. He wants their last camp located. Instruments recovered. That's the job, Yara. That's all I was told."

"That's what you were *told*." She looked back at the canoe, at the filed-off hull number, at the clean tear across the grain. "Then answer me this. If they went missing in the upper gorge, why is their boat down here, below the confluence, barely a month wet?"

Nobody answered. Pell wrote something in his notebook. Anselm straightened and walked back toward the fire kit without a word, resuming his silence like a coat.

Tomas met her eyes, and for the first time since the tavern in Corren's Landing, Yara saw something in his face she recognized from ten years ago — from the avalanche, from the inquiry, from the morning they'd both stood in a hearing room and watched their names get crossed off the rolls. Fear. Not of the river. Of the truth.

"We're four days from the gorge," he said quietly. "Contract's signed. Turn back now and we both eat the breach, and you know what breach clauses cost people like us."

"People like us," Yara repeated. "Right."

She walked back to the raft, checked the lashings twice, and pushed off into the current. Behind them the broken canoe slid out of sight around the bend, and ahead the valley narrowed, and narrowed, and the water beneath them turned the color of old glass, cold and green and keeping whatever secrets it had been paid to keep.

That night she did not hum.
