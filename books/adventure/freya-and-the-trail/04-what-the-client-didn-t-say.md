## 4. What the Client Didn't Say

Pell had never been a good liar, which was why he'd been chosen for this work. Liars were unpredictable. They embellished. They needed audiences. A man who simply declined to mention things — who kept his silences tidy and his answers narrow — could sit across a campfire for six days and give away nothing but the weather.

He told himself that was all he was doing out here: keeping silences. The consortium had been precise about it. *You are a surveyor, Mr. Pell. You will survey.* Nobody had said the word *lie* in his hearing, and he had clung to that distinction the way a drowning man clings to a plank that is visibly, structurally too small.

Six days on the Serrin River had made the plank feel smaller.

The river ran cold and green through country that seemed actively offended by their presence — spruce so dense the light came down in coins, banks of shale that shed stones into the water with small reproachful clicks whenever they passed. Yara Amberdale ran the crew the way she ran everything, he was learning: with an economy that looked like carelessness until you understood it was the opposite. She wasted no motion, no word, no kindness she couldn't afford. Her deckhand, Fen, was all thumbs and eagerness. The cook, whose name nobody used, moved through camp like a man paying off a sentence.

And Tomas Vey watched her. That was the thing Pell noticed first and kept noticing, the way you keep noticing a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall. Every time Yara gave an order, Tomas's eyes went to her face — not checking whether she was competent, but checking something else. Measuring her against some older version of her. Pell knew what that cost looked like on a man's face; he wore a version of it himself.

* * *

On the sixth night they made camp below the first gorge, where the river narrowed and began to speak in a lower voice. The wreckage they'd found that morning — a shattered cargo frame, a boot sole gone grey and brittle, a tent pole snapped clean — sat stacked at the edge of camp like evidence awaiting a court that would never convene. Too fresh. Everyone had seen it. Nobody had said much.

Pell sat apart, as usual, cleaning his instruments. The theodolite came apart into nine pieces and each piece demanded its cloth. It was good work for a guilty mind, repetitive and exacting, and he was deep into the third lens when Yara crossed the camp toward him without ceremony and crouched at the edge of his firelight.

"Surveyor," she said. "Question."

He set down the lens. "I've noticed you have those."

"The route we're taking tomorrow. You asked Fen about the upper gorge twice today. Asked me about flow rates once, about the portage line once more. I don't mind questions. I mind ones with a shape." She turned her hands palm-up, as if weighing something invisible between them. "You're not asking like a man mapping a river. You're asking like a man confirming something he already has on paper."

The fire popped. Downriver, the water worked at its stones.

Pell considered the plank. He considered how long it would hold. He thought about the second team — the one whose boot sole was sitting ten feet from him — and about the report he'd read before leaving, the one stamped with a classification he'd been told not to think about. He thought about the fact that he had read it eleven times, which was not the behavior of a man keeping tidy silences.

"You found the wreckage," he said. "So I'll say this much. The consortium didn't send me up here because they're curious about the river."

"No," Yara said. "Curious people ask better questions."

"They sent me because the first team stopped transmitting on a Tuesday and started again on a Thursday, and what they transmitted in those two days of silence was never entered into the record." He heard his own voice go flat, clerical, the voice he used for numbers. "Their instruments logged a magnetic anomaly in the upper gorge. Iron-rich formation, very deep, very pure. The assay samples they shipped back before they vanished came in at concentrations the smelters in Corvane pay fortunes for. Fortunes, Amberdale. Not money. Fortunes."

Yara didn't move. He watched her not move — watched stillness come over her like frost coming over water, from the edges inward.

"And your orders," she said quietly.

"My orders are to confirm the seam. To fix its extent, grade it, mark it for extraction." He made himself look at her. "Nobody has said the word *rescue* since I signed on. I asked, early on. Once. The answer I got was a schedule."

The frost reached her eyes. "There were people on that first team."

"There were nine people on that first team."

"And the consortium believes they're dead."

"The consortium believes nothing," Pell said. "That's the trick of it. Belief requires you to hold something in your mind. They simply proceed. The claim gets staked whether anyone comes back or not. The paperwork doesn't care." He picked up the lens and put it down again, unable to hold anything. "I've been telling myself I'm just a man with instruments. That what happens after my numbers leave my hands isn't mine. But I've watched you run this crew for six days, and I've watched you count heads at every camp, every crossing, every portage — you count them like a woman who lost count once and paid for it — and I find I can't do it anymore. I can't be the instrument."

Yara rose slowly. For a moment she stood at the edge of his firelight looking down at him, and Pell felt the full weight of what he'd handed her settle between them like a stone dropped into a well.

"Treaty land," she said. It wasn't a question. "The headwaters are protected. High Serrin was closed by the Kestrel Accord — both governments signed. No claims, no extraction, no roads. That's why there's no legal route up here. That's why the route is *forbidden*, not just hard."

"Yes."

"So this isn't a survey." Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "It's a claim-staking operation dressed as one, on land two countries promised never to sell. And if the seam is confirmed — if my instruments, my crew, my name on the manifest put it on record — then everything that follows is legal. Sanctioned. The theft gets a permit number."

"That's the design of it," Pell said. "Yes."

She was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — quieter, aimed somewhere past him, at the dark gorge where the river ran.

"I took this job because I'm broke and my name is mud in two countries. I told myself it was guiding. Escort work. Get a client up a river, get everyone home." She laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "And now I'm the escort for a robbery. My rope, my boat, my crew — complicity with a hull around it."

"I'm sorry," Pell said, and meant it, and knew how little it weighed.

"Don't be sorry. Be precise." She turned back to him, and the frost in her eyes had become something harder and more useful. "How many men does the consortium have within a week of this river?"

Pell hesitated. This was the last plank, and he knew it, and he broke it anyway. "I wasn't told. But the client's manifest lists security equipment I flagged as abnormal — climbing gear rated for military loads, radios on restricted bands, ammunition quantities no survey team carries. I assumed escort. Given what you've just told me about the treaty—" He stopped. Started again. "Given the treaty, Ms. Amberdale, I no longer assume escort."

Yara stood very still. Then she said, "Say nothing to Tomas. Say nothing to anyone. Tomorrow we run the gorge, and after that you and I are going to have another conversation — one where I decide what kind of surveyor you get to be."

She walked away across the camp, and Pell watched her go, and understood that he had not unburdened himself tonight so much as transferred the burden — that guilt, like cargo, only changes shoulders.

Above the gorge, far up the black spine of the ridge, a light appeared. Small, steady, wrong — no trapper burned a lamp that high, that late, that still.

It blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again.

Pell looked at it for a long time before he went to find Yara.
