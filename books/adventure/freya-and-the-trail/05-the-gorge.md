## 5. The Gorge

The river told them before the maps did.

Yara had learned to read water the way other people read faces, and by midmorning of the fifth day the Serrin had stopped speaking in its usual low, patient voice and started to snarl. The current bunched against a wall of granite ahead, turned white where it should have run green, and dropped away into a roar that swallowed everything else — even Pell's questions, which was perhaps the only mercy the gorge offered.

"End of the line for the boats," she called back over her shoulder. "From here we carry."

The gorge country began without ceremony. One moment they were running the Serrin through forested shallows, the deckhand — Fen, all elbows and eagerness — whooping at every riffle; the next, the canyon walls closed in like a book snapping shut, sheer grey stone streaked with the mineral stains of ten thousand years of runoff. The river compressed itself between them until it seemed less like water than like a living thing being forced through a wound.

Yara brought the lead boat in against an eddy below a shelf of rock, and the others followed. She stood in the stern a long moment, looking up at where the walls pinched together maybe two hundred yards ahead, and felt the old familiar weight settle across her shoulders. She had run this stretch once, years ago, in another life, with another crew. She remembered it as beautiful. She remembered wrong.

"Portage route's on the left," she said. "Slick stone most of the way. Single file, three points of contact, nobody carries more than their share. We move slow and we arrive."

Fen looked up at the walls with something like worship. The cook — Oskar, though he'd offered the name the way a man hands over a forged passport — said nothing at all, which was his way, and began breaking down the galley box with quick, economical hands. Pell stood at the bow of the surveyor's boat, consulting a leather case of instruments he guarded like relics, and asked, "How far to the source from here?"

"Four days if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then longer." Yara swung her pack onto her shoulders. "Weather in the gorge doesn't negotiate, Mr. Pell. It just decides."

* * *

They carried for six hours under a sky that curdled from white to bruise-grey while they watched.

The portage trail was barely worthy of the word — a seam of broken stone angling along the left wall, polished by floodwater in the spring, glazed now with a fine mist that blew up from the rapids below. Yara went first, testing each hold, calling back the bad spots: *loose flake on your left, don't trust the root, step wide of the seep.* Behind her came the long chain of them, bent under loads, moving with the careful, shuffling gait of people who understood that the stone forgave nothing.

Tomas brought up the rear. He always did. It was the position of either the most responsible man or the least trusted one, and Yara hadn't decided which she believed, which bothered her more than she let show. Five days on the river and he'd been competent, quiet, almost pleasant — and pleasant was the problem. The Tomas Vey she'd sworn off after the avalanche had been many things, but never pleasant. Pleasant men were hiding something.

By early afternoon the first rain arrived, fat and cold, spotting the stone dark around them. By late afternoon it was coming sideways off the canyon walls, and the mist off the rapids had thickened until the far side of the gorge was a rumor.

"We should camp," Tomas shouted up the line.

"One more hour. There's a bench above the narrows — flat ground, out of spray. If we stop here we're sleeping on wet rock in the open."

"If someone goes in the water—"

"Nobody goes in the water." She heard how it sounded even as she said it, the guide's catechism repeated so often it became true by incantation. *Nobody goes in the water.* She'd said it on other rivers, other years. Once, it had been a lie.

The rope failed an hour later, at the worst place, because ropes fail at the worst places or not at all.

It happened fast, the way disasters do — no drama leading up to it, just physics. They were traversing a slab above a churning side-channel where the flooded river had cut behind the main wall, and Oskar, carrying the galley box lashed to a gear sling, reached for a hold that wasn't there. His boots skated on wet stone. The safety line snapped taut, held for one full second — Yara would remember that second for the rest of her life, the way the rope hummed, the way everyone froze — and then the anchor, a piton driven into a crack that turned out to be hollow, tore free with a sound like a tooth coming out.

Oskar went down the slab on his back, silent, which was worse than screaming, and hit the channel.

The water took him instantly. One glimpse of his face, eyes wide and perfectly lucid, and then the brown flood folded over him and he was gone, the galley box bobbing once and vanishing downstream.

Yara didn't think. Thinking was for afterward. She clipped into the good rope, the one anchored to a living tree forty feet back, and went down the slab on her heels, letting the rope run through her brake hand until friction burned the glove away, and dove.

Cold water rising — that was what they called this river in the old stories, and they were right. The cold hit her like a blow to the chest, squeezed the air out of her, and the current seized her the way it had seized him, tumbling her against stone, filling her world with roar and brown violence. She caught fabric — his jacket — lost it, caught again, got a fist into his collar and locked her arm through the strap of his pack, and then it was a war of small advantages: her boots finding purchase, her free hand clawing along the channel bottom, dragging them both inch by inch toward an eddy behind a boulder where the water gave up its grip as suddenly as it had taken it.

She hauled him onto the stones and knelt over him, and for a long moment there was only the rain and her own heartbeat slamming in her ears.

Then Oskar coughed, rolled, and vomited half the Serrin River onto the rocks, and Yara sat back on her heels and laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all.

He was alive. That was the arithmetic of it. And the cost came due within the hour, when they inventoried what the river had kept: the galley box, gone. Two weeks of provisions, gone. A dry bag with spare rope and the repair kit, gone. Half their supplies, traded for one silent cook's life, and no one — not Fen, white-faced and shaking; not Pell, clutching his instrument case as if he'd considered throwing it as ballast and thought better of it; not Tomas, standing at the top of the slab with rain streaming off his jaw — no one said a word about whether it had been worth it, because they all knew better than to ask.

But Yara saw the look that passed between Tomas and Pell at the top of the slab. Quick. Practiced. The look of two men checking each other's arithmetic.

She filed it away with everything else.

* * *

They made the bench above the narrows at dusk, soaked through, and got a fire going under an overhang with the last dry tinder. Dinner was barley and rendered fat, stretched thin, cooked in the single pot they had left. Nobody complained. Hunger made people honest, and honesty tonight would have been unbearable.

Yara took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep. Her forearms burned. Her shoulder, where the pack strap had wrenched it, throbbed in time with her pulse. Across the fire, Oskar sat wrapped in every blanket they could spare, staring into the flames, and when he caught her looking he said — the longest sentence she'd ever heard from him:

"You didn't hesitate."

"It's the job."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Blacklisted guides don't do that. Blacklisted guides calculate." He looked at her a moment longer, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — gratitude braided with something darker, maybe pity. Then he lay down and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way exhausted men are.

The storm settled in for the night, rain drumming on the overhang, the river below talking to itself in the dark. Yara sat with her back to the stone and watched the fire and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding: eleven days of food for five people, four days to the source, four days back if the weather broke, and no resupply, no exit, no law within a hundred miles. The mountain was closing its hand.

Near midnight, she saw the light.

Not firelight — smaller, furtive, a shielded flame down at the edge of the camp where the supply tarp was rigged. She moved toward it without deciding to, soft-footed on wet stone, twenty years of trail craft carrying her.

Tomas crouched at the tarp with the client's manifest open on his knee — the sealed document list Yara had signed for without reading, because Tomas had vouched for it, because pride had made her careless. He had a page torn free and was holding it to a small flame, feeding it in carefully, watching it burn the way a man watches something he has done before.

Paper curled to black. Ash dropped into the mud. He fed the rest in and ground it under his boot heel, and only then did he sense her, straightening too fast, his face arranging itself into surprise a half-second too late.

"Couldn't sleep?" he said.

"What was on the page?"

"Inventory correction. Client's paperwork is sloppy."

"Tomas." His name in her mouth tasted like the old years, the bad ones. "I pulled a man out of that river today. I gave up half our food doing it. So you're going to stand there in the rain and tell me what you just burned, or you're going to tell me why I shouldn't wake the others and turn us all around right now."

For a moment — one long moment — she thought he might actually tell her. Something moved behind his eyes, some calculation reaching its final term, and his mouth opened—

And then, faint but unmistakable, threading through the drum of the rain, came a sound from the ridge above the gorge. High, thin, deliberate.

A whistle. Answered, a few seconds later, by another, farther off.

Tomas went very still. Yara stared up into the black walls where the sound had come from, and felt the last warm thing between herself and her old rival go cold.

"Get your rifle," she said quietly. "And tomorrow, Vey, you and I are going to have that conversation — whether you want it or not."
