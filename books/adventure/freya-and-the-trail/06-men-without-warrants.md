## 6. Men Without Warrants

Tomas Vey saw the riders before anyone else, because he had spent the last three days looking for them.

They came down the western ridge in the grey hour after dawn, when the mist still clung to the scree like wool and the river below was a low, constant mutter. Five of them, then a sixth, moving single file along the skyline with the unhurried confidence of men who knew no one would challenge them. They did not ride like patrolmen or like bandits. They rode like landowners. That was what turned Tomas's stomach — the ease of it, the proprietary slouch in the saddles, rifles carried across their thighs the way a farmer carries a fence post.

He crouched behind a granite shoulder at the edge of camp and counted them twice. Six. He had budgeted for four.

Below him, the camp was waking. The cook, Sef, had the fire down to coals and a pot of barley steaming over them. Denny, the deckhand, was hauling the canoes above the waterline, chattering to himself about the day's portage. Pell sat apart with his instruments, as always, recording something in that small careful hand. And Yara —

Yara stood at the river's edge with her boots in the shallows, testing the current with a stick, her hair tied back and her sleeves rolled, looking for all the world like a woman who believed she was guiding an honest survey. Watching her, Tomas felt the lie he had signed his name to settle over him like wet canvas.

*You knew,* he told himself. *You knew when you took the money.*

He had known the shape of it without knowing the details. A client who paid triple and asked no questions about losses. A route chosen not despite its dangers but because of them. A manifest with pages he wasn't meant to read — one of which he'd pulled from the fire two nights ago, though Yara thought he hadn't. He had told himself the same story the whole way upriver: that he was a middleman, that ignorance was a kind of innocence, that whatever the consortium wanted buried in the high country would stay buried without his hands on the shovel.

The riders reached the saddle above the camp and stopped. One of them raised a hand, and the others fanned out along the ridgeline with a discipline that made Tomas's mouth go dry. Not six angry men. Six positions.

He walked into camp without hurrying. Hurrying would tell Yara everything, and he needed thirty more seconds than panic would give him.

"Break camp," he said quietly. "Now. Leave the canoes."

Sef looked up first. The cook said little, but his eyes were old in a young face, and they went from Tomas to the ridge and back again, and he began dousing the coals without a word. Denny started to ask why, saw Tomas's face, and shut his mouth.

Yara straightened from the water's edge. She read him the way she read weather — fast, total, no appeal. "How many?"

"Six. Ridgeline, west saddle. They've split into firing positions."

"Bandits don't take firing positions." She was already moving, pulling her pack tight, sliding her knife free of its sheath to check the edge. "Patrols don't either. Who are they, Tomas?"

He could have lied. There was probably a version of the lie that would have held for another hour, maybe two — long enough to get them into the trees, long enough for him to decide later what to confess. But she was looking at him with those flat grey eyes that had watched him across a dozen summits and one catastrophe, eyes that had already written him off once, and he found that he could not add this stone to the pile.

"They're the client's," he said. "Security. Contract security — no warrants, no commission, nothing on paper. They're here to make sure nothing comes back downriver."

The words landed in the camp like stones in still water. Denny went pale. Sef didn't move at all, which was worse.

"Nothing," Yara repeated. "Meaning the survey data."

"Meaning the survey team." Tomas heard his own voice come out level and hated how steady it sounded, how practiced. "Yara — the first expedition didn't vanish. They were sent up and written off. Pell's crew is the confirmation run. You and I and these two—" he gestured, and his hand betrayed him by shaking, just slightly, "—we're cover. Expendables. If we die on the river, it reads as another accident on a cursed route. Nobody investigates a route everyone knows is cursed. I know because I helped make sure everyone knows it's cursed."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, gravel ticked down the ridge — small, natural, terrifying.

"You swore off this route," Yara said softly. "We both did. After the avalanche. After Marek." Her voice didn't rise; it got quieter, which from Yara was the sound of a blade being drawn. "And you came to me in that tavern with triple wages because you needed a crew nobody would miss. Because you needed people whose deaths would close the file instead of opening it."

"Yes."

"And you let me sign anyway."

"I needed the money too." It was true and it was unforgivable and he said it plainly because anything softer would have been worse. "I told myself I'd keep you alive. I told myself I'd turn us around before the source if it came to it. I told myself a lot of things, Yara. None of them were worth what I sold."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned away, and somehow that was the verdict — not rage, not a blow, just the turning away, the assessment completed and filed.

"Sef," she said. "How far to the tree line?"

"Twenty minutes, if the scree holds."

"It won't hold. They'll have the saddle cut off inside ten." She was scanning the gorge walls now, reading the rock the way other people read print. "River's unrunnable from here — you said so yourself at the last bend. So it's uphill, east side, through the talus, and we move before they finish setting their line."

Tomas blinked. He had braced for fury, for refusal, for the moment she told him to go stand on the ridgeline himself. Instead she was doing the thing she had always done better than anyone he'd known: converting betrayal into route-finding.

"You're taking my orders?" he couldn't help asking.

"No," she said. "I'm ignoring yours entirely. Yours got us here." She jerked her chin at Pell, who had risen from his instruments with his ledger clutched to his chest like a breviary. "Surveyor. Can your legs carry that pack uphill at altitude?"

Pell swallowed. "They'll have to."

"Denny — leave everything but food, rope, and the medical kit. Sef, take point with me; you know these slopes better than you admit. Tomas." She finally looked at him again, and there was something in her face worse than anger: arithmetic. "You're in the middle of the line where I can see you. If this is a trap — if those men are yours in any way I haven't been told — I'll put you between me and their rifles. Understood?"

"Understood," he said, and meant it, and deserved it.

They moved.

The scree took them grudgingly, each step a small avalanche of its own, and Tomas climbed with his ears straining backward. The riders had seen them — of course they had; that was never in doubt. What he heard next confirmed everything he'd confessed: no shout, no hail, no demand to halt. Just the flat crack of a rifle rolling down the gorge, and the spurt of stone dust a hand's width from Denny's shoulder.

No warning. No warrant. Men who shot first weren't men enforcing a claim; they were men erasing witnesses, and every step of retreat was itself the confession of what the consortium feared.

"Run!" Yara's voice cracked across the slope like a whip. "Trees! Go!"

They ran. The second shot came high and wide — ranging, not aiming yet, the shooters still finding their feet on loose ground. Tomas caught Denny by the collar as the boy veered toward a gully that looked like cover and was actually a shooting gallery, hauled him left, and shoved him up the slope. Ahead, Sef moved through the boulders with uncanny sureness, choosing lines that didn't exist until he was on them, and Yara brought up the rear-facing position, walking backward half the time, rifle out, making herself the target so the others wouldn't be.

A third shot. Then a fourth, closer, and Tomas felt the wind of it pass his ear and understood with sudden clarity that the next hour of his life would be decided by gravity, angle, and luck, and that he had spent the last month spending all three as if they were infinite.

They gained the tree line as the sun cleared the ridge, plunging into shadow and spruce. Below, the riders were coming down — dismounted now, moving in pairs, methodical as surveyors themselves. Of course. These were men used to hunting in broken country.

Yara crouched beside a fallen trunk, breathing hard, and looked back the way they'd come. The river glinted far below, indifferent, already forgetting them.

"They'll push us uphill all day," she said. "Funnel us toward the snowline, pick us off when we tire. Unless we stop being prey." She glanced at Sef. "There's a way, isn't there. Off the mapped routes."

Sef was silent a long moment. "There is a way," he said.

"Then we take it." Yara stood, and when she looked at Tomas there was no forgiveness in her face, and no hatred either — only the cold clarity of a woman who had decided that survival was the only law left worth obeying, and that he would live or die by whether he could obey it too.

"Stay close," she said. "And Tomas — from here on, you tell me everything. Before I find it out. Or I leave you for them."

Above them, faint on the wind, came the sound of the riders entering the trees.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the forest grew older around them — thicker-trunked, darker, unlogged and unpatrolled, country that existed on no consortium map because no consortium had ever bothered to look at anything it couldn't dig up. Twice they heard shots behind them, aimed at movement rather than at men, herding shots, and twice Yara changed their line sharply, refusing the funnel.

It was near midday, on a bench of ground above a waterfall they could hear but not see, that Tomas finally spoke the rest of it.

"The page I burned," he said. "From the manifest."

Yara stopped. Sef and Denny halted ahead without turning, listening.

"There was a schedule on it," Tomas went on. "Dates. A resupply window at the headwaters — and a line item for 'site remediation.' Remediation dated four days after our expected arrival at the source. Whatever the first team found, the plan was never just to confirm it. The plan was to confirm it, record it, and then make sure the only copies that existed were the ones the client wanted — along with the only people who'd seen the originals." He exhaled. "I burned it because I told myself burning it destroyed the evidence of what I'd agreed to. It didn't. It just destroyed my copy."

Yara studied him for a long time. High overhead, a bird of prey turned on the wind, and he thought absurdly that it was watching them the way the riders watched — patiently, professionally, waiting for the weak one to fall behind.

"Pell has instruments," she said at last. "Recording instruments. Field documentation, sealed and dated."

"He records everything. It's compulsive."

"Then the evidence isn't in your burned page." Something shifted in her expression — not warmth, nothing so generous, but a recalculation, a door unbarred a few inches. "It's in his ledger, and it's been in his ledger since the first wreck we found. Which means the client isn't chasing us to hide a seam of ore. He's chasing us to hide a witness with good handwriting."

Ahead, Sef raised a fist. Everyone froze.

Through the trees below them, visible in patches between the trunks, came the riders — three of them now, spread wide, climbing steadily. And beyond them, higher on the mountain, faint but unmistakable, a whistle passed from one position to another. The net was drawing closed from both sides.

Yara looked up the slope, toward the snowline and the grey ramparts beyond it, toward the forbidden headwaters that every instinct she owned told her to flee and some newer, harder instinct told her she would reach regardless.

"There's a trail," she said quietly, almost to herself. "Unofficial. Poachers' line, above the falls, past the treeline. It's on no map. It's the reason my name is on two countries' exclusion lists." She shouldered her pack. "Time it earned its keep."

She started climbing, and Tomas Vey, ruined man, followed her into the high country with the riflesounds fading below and the snowline bright and merciless above — knowing that whatever waited at the source, the men behind them had already told him the truth better than any confession could:

Nothing that came back down this river was ever meant to be believed.
