## 7. Upstream

The river gave them one hour of grace, and Yara spent it running.

They came out of the gorge country at dusk with the riders' torches flickering on the ridge behind them, and Yara made the call she'd been dreading since the first shot cracked across the water: leave the boats. Everything they'd hauled upriver — the gear, the food, the client's precious crates — stayed lashed to the bank like an offering. She took what her arms could carry: a coil of rope, a tarp, two days of rations stuffed into a flour sack, and Pell's instrument case, which he clutched to his chest like a child.

The deckhand — Fen, though he'd only told her his name on the third day, shy as a confession — carried the rifle from the supply locker and said nothing about it, which told Yara he understood better than any of them what kind of hunt this had become.

"They're not following the river," she said, crouched in the alder brush where the trail died against a wall of granite. Below them the Serrin churned white through its last narrows, loud enough to swallow voices. Above, on the skyline, three riders moved along the ridge with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the country would do their work for them. "They know every boat on this water. They'll let us run ourselves ragged along the banks and pick us off at the next rapids."

"Then where?" Pell whispered.

Yara looked up. The mountain rose black against the last grey light, and somewhere on its flanks, half-buried under slide rock and twenty years of official forgetting, was a trail that did not exist. A trail that had never been surveyed, never been mapped, never been admitted to by anyone who valued their license. A trail she had walked with her late partner in the years before the avalanche, when they were young and arrogant enough to believe the high country belonged to whoever was willing to suffer for it.

It was the same class of route that had gotten her name struck from two registries. Unofficial. Unsanctioned. In the commission's language: *reckless*.

"Up," she said.

* * *

They climbed through the night.

Yara led by feel more than sight, her boots finding holds her eyes had stopped needing years ago. The unofficial trails didn't announce themselves. They were suggestions — a game trail that widened where sheep had worn it, a seam of scree that funneled toward a ledge, a shelf of turf above the treeline that held a walker's weight if you trusted it. She'd learned them in the years when she and Marta were mapping the Serrin headwaters for no one but themselves, back when the mountains were still a romance instead of a debt.

Marta. Even now, with riders' lanterns swinging somewhere below, the name arrived uninvited. *You always find the ways nobody wants you to walk,* Marta used to say, laughing, roped in beside her on some face the guides' guild wouldn't certify. It hadn't sounded like a warning then. It sounded like praise.

By midnight they were above the trees, into the snowline, and the cold turned from discomfort into a fact of the world. Fen's breathing went ragged. Pell, who had the soft hands of a man who read maps rather than walked them, fell twice on the ice-glazed turf and got up both times without complaint, which Yara noted and filed away. There were worse things than a man who fell well.

At the first grey hint of dawn she called a halt in the lee of a boulder field and rationed out hardtack and frozen cheese. Below them, small as toys, the riders had reached the abandoned boats. One dismounted. Even at this distance Yara could read the body language: the sweep of the arm, the pointing up-valley, then — after a long moment — the pointing up-mountain.

"They've seen our line," Pell said.

"No." Yara chewed, watching. "They've seen *a* line. They think we're making for the pass everyone knows about." She nodded west, toward the notch of Col Vane, the sanctioned crossing, the one printed in every guidebook. "Which is exactly where we won't be."

"And if they figure that out?"

"Then we'll already be somewhere they can't take horses." She stood, knees cracking, and looked at the two of them — the surveyor with his precious instruments, the deckhand with his borrowed rifle and his too-young face. "Listen to me. From here on, everything I tell you will sound like a way to die. Walk the cornice edge. Cross the icefield at noon, not morning. Trust the rotten-looking turf over the clean-looking rock. Every one of those rules is why I'm banned from guiding in this country. They are also the only reason we're alive right now. So you'll follow them, or you can go down and negotiate with those gentlemen."

Fen almost smiled. Pell just tightened his grip on the instrument case and said, "Lead."

* * *

The second day was the icefield.

It lay in a shallow basin below the true crest, four hundred meters of glacier remnant crusted with winter's last snow, crevassed in the old pattern Yara remembered — and glaciers, unlike governments, kept their promises. She crossed first, probing with the axe she'd taken from the wrecked expedition's cache, calling holds back to the others in a low, even voice. Noon light flattened the surface so the cracks showed as blue shadows instead of black mouths. By the book, you crossed such a thing roped and at dawn. By the book, they'd all be dead by now.

They were halfway across when the first bullet came.

It hit the ice thirty meters left of Fen with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the crack ran outward in a white star. Yara's whole body understood before her mind did: they weren't shooting to warn. The second round kicked powder off the slope above them, and then the reports reached her ears, flat little snaps riding up the basin from the col they'd pretended not to take.

"Off the ice!" she shouted. "Fen — the far lip, the blue seam, run it *now*—"

He ran. He was fast, the boy, faster than she'd credited, and for six strides he was going to make it. Then the seventh step found the bridge over a hidden crevasse and the bridge found its honest opinion of his weight, and the snow simply opened beneath him.

He didn't scream. That was the thing Yara would remember afterward — he dropped without a sound, the rifle flying free, and the crevasse took him to the waist and held him there, wedged in blue dark, his arms spread across the fragile lip.

The shooting stopped. Of course it did. Men who shoot rather than question still prefer their targets standing still.

Yara was moving before she'd decided to. Twenty meters of glacier, axe biting, the world narrowed to rope and ice and the boy's white face between her boots. She anchored the line around a serac the way Marta had taught her — the way that had been in no manual, the way the inquiry had cited as evidence of her recklessness — and went down over the lip headfirst, into the cold that climbed her sleeves like water.

Fen's fingers were blue. His boot was wedged, his hip screaming, and below him the crevasse went down into a darkness that had swallowed better climbers than either of them.

"Eyes on me," she said. "Not down. Me."

"I can't feel my leg—"

"You don't need to feel it. You need to pull with your arms when I say. On my count." She set the sling under his chest, checked it twice, and looked up at Pell's terrified silhouette against the sky. "Pell! Haul!"

Three pulls. Four. The ice groaned, shrugged, and gave him up all at once, and they rolled together onto the solid lip while snow hissed down into the hole they'd escaped. Behind them, across the basin, a rider's voice carried thin and clear on the wind, giving orders.

They couldn't stay on the ice. They couldn't go back. Which left, as it had left her once before, the way nobody wanted her to walk.

* * *

She found the ledge at dusk.

It wasn't on any map because she had never put it on one. She and Marta had cut it themselves one desperate autumn — a horizontal seam in the cliff face below the eastern crest, barely wide enough for a body, running two hundred meters to a chimney that climbed to the high shoulder. The guild had called routes like it suicidal. The inquiry had called her judgment criminal. And here it was, holding her crew the way it had held her before, faithful in the exact manner that respectability never was.

Fen couldn't climb the chimney. Not with the leg — she'd bound it above the knee with the tarp strips and splinted it with an axe handle, and the wound beneath was ugly, a bullet graze from the icefield she'd missed in the chaos, seeping through the wrapping. He'd taken the round covering their retreat off the glacier, firing back down the basin to pin the riders while she dragged Pell into cover. He hadn't mentioned it until she saw the blood.

"You should have said," she told him, tight-wrapped in anger she couldn't aim anywhere useful.

"You'd have stopped," he said simply.

So she rigged a harness, and they raised him up the chimney hand over hand, Pell hauling above and Yara pushing below, an hour of grinding, cursing effort while the wind tried to peel them off the stone. When they finally flopped onto the high shoulder, all three of them shaking, the sun was gone and the riders' fires glimmered far below, patient as ticks.

Pell sat with his back against a boulder, the instrument case open on his knees. Inside, nested in wool, the recording drums had survived — the barographs, the survey plates, the sealed log cylinder. He touched them the way a man touches a sleeping animal he's afraid to wake.

"It's real, isn't it," Yara said quietly. "What they found at the source. What your consortium is willing to kill for."

Pell was silent a long moment. Above them the stars came out hard and cold, and below them the enemy burned their fires, and between those two facts the boy from the city weighed his life against his orders.

"There's a cirque," he said at last. "Headwaters of the Serrin. The treaty line runs through it — that's why it's closed. My instructions were to confirm the seam and stake the claim before the commission's next session." He looked up at her, and in the starlight his face had lost whatever softness it had carried onto the river. "But there were eight men on the lost team. The consortium's manifest lists seven dead."

Yara felt the number land in her chest like a stone dropped down a shaft.

"One unaccounted for," Pell said. "Maybe more. If anyone survived up there — if they've been living in that cirque since spring, guarding whatever's left of their camp—" He closed the case. "Then the truth is still up there. Waiting for someone to carry it down."

Yara looked north, where the shoulder climbed toward the high country and the forbidden headwaters beyond. Somewhere past that final wall of ice and rock was the place her old life had ended and Marta's life had ended, the source of the Serrin, the reason for every ban and blacklist and buried report. She had sworn never to go back.

Behind them, a rider's whistle shrilled across the dark — closer than the fires, much closer. They'd found the ledge.

Yara shouldered the rope.

"Then we'd better get there first," she said.
