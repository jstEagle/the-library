## 8. The Source

Pell had spent eleven years learning to read stone, and nothing in that education had prepared him for the cirque.

They came over the last shoulder of the ridge at dawn, when the light came sideways and gold and turned the snowfield into a field of hammered copper. Yara went first, as she always did, her boots finding purchase on ice that offered none, and Pell followed in the prints she left, one hand on the rope, the other pressed flat against the rock as if he could take its pulse. Behind them, the deckhand — Fen, whose arm Yara had bound twice since the gorge with strips of shirt and pine pitch — climbed without complaint, though his face had gone the color of old tallow.

Then the wall opened.

The glacier had carved out a bowl half a mile wide, ringed by walls of granite so sheer they seemed less like mountains than like the shoulders of something sleeping. At the bowl's head, the ice fell away in a blue-white curtain two hundred feet tall, and from beneath it, out of a darkness that had no business existing in daylight, came the Serrin River itself — born whole, wide as a wagon road, cold enough to kill in a minute. The water ran clear as glass over pale stone, and where it pooled in the shallows it was the color of nothing on any map Pell had ever drawn: a green so pure it looked invented.

"Headwaters," Yara said quietly. She said it the way other people said prayers.

Pell knelt at the margin of the pool and put his hand in the water, and the cold went up his arm like a wire. He had imagined this moment for weeks — through the wrecked camp they'd found downstream, through the burning page, through the nights of frozen ground and riders' gunfire cracking off the scree. He had imagined kneeling here with a confirmed seam value in his notebook and a bonus waiting at the end of it, enough to buy back his father's farm, enough to stop being a man who took orders.

He had not imagined that the rock itself would look like this.

The ore vein cut across the cirque's eastern wall in a band thirty feet thick, exposed where some ancient slide had sheared the mountain open. It glittered. That was the obscene thing about it — it *glittered*, threads of native silver braided through galena and something darker, denser, that made his instruments sing before he even uncased them. The seam ran the length of the wall and disappeared into the glacier's root, which meant it didn't end. Which meant it was worth more than the consortium's entire shipping fleet. Which meant everything the men on the ridges behind them had done — every burned manifest page, every bullet — had been arithmetic.

"It's real," Fen whispered behind him, and there was no wonder in it, only dread.

"It was always real," Pell said. "That's what nobody wanted us to know."

Yara wasn't looking at the ore. She was looking at the far side of the pool, at the talus slope beneath the wall, and her whole body had gone still in the way Pell had learned to fear.

"Smoke," she said.

* * *

There had been smoke, once. Now there were only the bones of a fire, a driftwood lean-to sagging under months of weather, and two people who should have been dead.

They heard Yara before they saw her — or rather, they heard the bell. She carried a small brass bell on her pack strap, an old guide's habit she'd never explained, and its single bright note rang across the cirque, and from the rocks above the ruined camp a voice cracked out like a rifle shot:

"That's far enough."

A woman stood up out of the stones. She was gaunt past recognition, wrapped in a survey tarp gone gray with ash and weather, and she held a broken ice axe in both hands like a spear. Beside her, levering himself up with visible agony, came a man with a leg bound in splints made of tent poles. His beard had gone white though he couldn't have been older than forty. On the collar of his ruined jacket, faded but legible, Pell read the consortium crest — the same crest stitched inside his own coat.

"Halden," Pell said. "Marcus Halden." The name surfaced from three years of briefing documents. "You're the team lead. You're supposed to be dead."

"We noticed," the woman said. Her eyes never left Yara. "Who are you? Say it plain, because we've killed for less than a lie up here."

"Yara Amberdale. This is Pell Marrow, the client's surveyor. Fen Oduya, deckhand." Yara spread her empty hands. "We came upriver because your expedition vanished. We didn't come to hurt you."

"You came because Vey sold you the same lie he sold everyone." Halden laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as gravel. "Triple wages and a cursed route. Did he tell you why it's cursed?"

"He told me nothing," Yara said. "I've been finding out the hard way."

Halden looked at her for a long moment. Then at Pell. And whatever he saw in Pell's face — the consortium stitching, the surveyor's instruments, the man's whole careful, compromised architecture — made him lower the ice axe an inch.

"Show them, Ilsa," he said.

* * *

What Ilsa showed them was a cache.

She led them — slowly, Halden hobbling between them — to a cleft in the talus where a waterproof case had been buried under stone. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were notebooks, photographic plates, and a section of cable with a coupling Pell recognized instantly, because he'd specified its twin in his own equipment order six weeks ago.

"Blasting line," Ilsa said. "Detonator-grade. Consortium issue, lot-numbered, signed out under a requisition that doesn't exist in any file." She unwrapped a photographic plate with hands that trembled from hunger, not fear. "On the fourth of last October, our own support crew detonated charges above our high camp. Not to open a trail. To close one. To bury the route down and seal us in with the seam until winter finished the job."

Pell stared at the plate. It showed the chute above their old camp, the fresh scar of the blast, the rubble fan across the only descent. He thought of the avalanche three years ago — the one that had ended Yara's career, killed her partner, put her name on two countries' exclusion lists. He thought of Tomas Vey standing in a tavern doorway offering triple wages, omitting things.

"The avalanche," Yara said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Downriver. Three years ago. Was that—"

"We don't know," Halden said. "But the requisition officer who signed ours is the same man who provisioned your expedition. Corven. Ask Vey about Corven."

Pell felt the ground tilt under him, though the ground was perfectly still. He had told himself a story all the way up the river — that he was a professional doing a professional's work, that confirming the seam was neutral science, that what the consortium did with knowledge was not his to carry. He had even, in his weakest hours, told himself that finding survivors would be a rescue, a redemption written into his contract somewhere in fine print.

There was no fine print. There had never been fine print. There was a seam worth a fortune on treaty land, a dead expedition that refused to die, and orders in his breast pocket instructing him to confirm the deposit and signal the coordinates — after which, the orders did not say, but the riders on the ridges had made it plain — nothing else needed to come down off this mountain.

"Pell." Yara was watching him. She had been watching him for days, he realized, the way you watch a rope under load, waiting for the strand that gives. "Your instruments record. Voice, readings, plates. Everything."

"Yes."

"And your orders say to transmit the seam coordinates."

"Yes."

"Then transmit." She nodded at the ore wall, at the glacier, at the two starving ghosts of the lost expedition. "Transmit all of it. The seam, the sabotage, the lot numbers, Halden's testimony, Ilsa's plates. Send the whole truth up to whatever relay your gear can reach, and let the consortium choke on it."

"They'll know exactly where we are," Fen said. "The relay pings. They'll come."

"They're already coming," Yara said. "At least this way they arrive to witnesses instead of a grave."

Pell took out the instrument case. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake. He set the recorder on a flat stone beside the headwater pool, where the river's first breath moved cold over the microphone, and he began to speak.

He gave his name, his employer, his contract number. He described the seam in the language of his profession, precise and unadorned, and it was the most honest survey he had ever conducted. Then he set the case before Halden and Ilsa, and the woman who had survived a winter on melted snow and resentment looked into the lens and told the mountain what had been done to her.

When it was done, Pell keyed the relay. The unit hummed, thought, and sent its small defiant pulse up through the thin air toward a satellite neither of them would ever see.

"There," he said. "It's done. I'm either a dead man or a free one."

"Those aren't different things up here," Yara said, but she put her hand briefly on his shoulder, and it was the first time she had touched him since the river.

* * *

They were making camp — if two tarps and a fire of wet scrub could be called a camp — when Fen, on watch at the cirque mouth, came down the talus at a run.

"Riders," he said. "Six of them. Maybe eight. They've reached the moraine."

Yara rose without hurry, and Pell watched her turn her face toward the cirque walls, reading them the way she read rivers — not as obstacles but as instruments, waiting to be played. Above the eastern col, the afternoon sun was striking a slope of fractured rock that hung above the only pass, a slope she had studied, he now understood, since the moment they'd arrived.

"How much charge do you have left?" she asked Pell. "In the blasting kit. Be exact."

And far below, faint on the wind, came the sound of hooves on stone.
