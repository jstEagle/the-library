## 9. Justice Above the Treeline

The cirque held the morning like a cupped hand, all pale granite and blue ice, and Yara stood at its rim and understood, finally, why the old maps left this place blank. Not because no one had surveyed it. Because anyone who saw it understood at once that it was not meant for them.

The headwaters spilled from the tongue of a glacier in threads of meltwater that braided and rejoined, braided and rejoined, silver on stone so white it burned the eye. And above the waterline, where the ice had retreated within living memory, the mountain had peeled itself open. The seam ran across the cirque wall like a vein exposed in a wrist — a ribbon of ore that caught the sun and threw it back in dull, bloody flashes. Copper, Pell had said, and something rarer beneath it, something the consortium's assay reports valued at more than the treaty that protected this valley had ever been worth to the men who signed it.

Two figures moved down from the ruin of the lost expedition's camp, slow as herons. Sera Voss and Ilya Brandt — Yara had learned their names the evening before, when Pell's shout of recognition had cracked across the ice and the two survivors had come out of their rock shelter with a rifle and a flare gun and nine months of vigilance etched into their faces. Half-starved, frost-scarred, and guarding, with the stubbornness of the condemned, a waterproof case of documents: survey logs, radio transcripts, and the photographs that proved the consortium had cut their own team loose, had triggered the avalanche that sealed the lower valley behind them, had written off nine lives to buy silence.

Sera stopped at the meltwater's edge and looked up at the cirque rim, at the notch in the eastern wall where the riders would come.

"They'll be through the pass by midmorning," she said. Her voice was flat with a weariness that had gone past feeling. "Four of them. Maybe five. They've been watching us since spring."

Yara turned and studied the notch. A saddle of broken scree, maybe forty meters across, walled on the far side by a hanging face of fractured rock — the kind of face she'd spent twenty years reading the way other people read weather. She had looked at a thousand such faces and known which ones were honest and which were liars, which would hold and which were waiting, patiently, for an excuse to come down.

This one was a liar. She could see the seam of weakness running through it, the old fault filled with ice that the summer sun was already softening. It wanted to fall. It had wanted to fall for ten thousand years.

All it needed was a reason.

"You're thinking something," Pell said beside her. He had his instruments cradled against his chest like a child — the magnetic recorder, the barograph, the little brass-cased camera that had already photographed every page of the survivors' evidence. "I can hear it from here. It sounds like a bad idea."

"It's a terrible idea," Yara said. "But running is worse. There's no line down the valley they don't control, and we've got two people who can't move fast and a case of paper that can't take a bullet." She looked at the notch again, measuring, and felt something in her chest that she hadn't felt since before the avalanche, before the blacklist, before the debt and the shame — not fear, exactly, and not calm either, but the cold bright clarity that came just before a commitment. "We don't run. We close the door behind us."

Pell followed her gaze up to the hanging face and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with altitude. "You want to bring that down. On purpose."

"I want to bring down the part of it that seals the saddle. Nothing more." She was already counting in her head — the spare rope, the pitons, the cook's black powder they'd carried for signaling, the two flares in Sera's kit. Enough. It would be enough, if the rock cooperated, and rock always cooperated eventually; that was the first lesson of the mountains and the last. "The riders come through the notch, we drop the face, and they're on the wrong side of forty meters of rubble. Stranded. No way down but past us."

"And then what? You're going to negotiate with armed men?"

"No," Yara said. "I'm going to make them an audience."

* * *

They worked through the morning while the light climbed and the glacier hissed its slow melt, and Yara found her hands remembered everything her heart had tried to forget. The placement of pitons in rotten stone. The arithmetic of charge and fall line. She had learned this work on routes with no names, the unofficial trails, the smugglers' paths and poachers' cut-offs that had gotten her name onto two countries' exclusion lists — and here, at the top of the world, that disgrace turned out to be the only education that mattered.

The deckhand, Fen, whose arm was still bound from the gorge, hauled rope without complaint. Ilya, who had not spoken a full sentence in the two days Yara had known him, watched her set the last charge above the saddle and said, quietly, "You've done this before. Not for saving. For burying."

"Once," Yara said. "I was on the wrong side of it." She did not explain. The avalanche that had ruined her, that had killed her partner and blackened her name, had been no accident of weather — she had learned that much from the documents in Pell's case, and the rest of it, the whole rotten architecture of that old disaster, was in there too, waiting for a tribunal. "This time the mountain and I agree on the target."

By noon the sky had gone that hard, high blue that means weather is holding its breath, and the first rider came through the notch.

Then the second. The third. Four in all, leading a fifth horse, rifles slung with the casual menace of men who had never once worried about a warrant. They spread out along the saddle with the confidence of people who believed the world below them was already settled — and Yara, crouched behind a spur of granite two hundred meters away, watched them and felt the old fear rise in her throat and, for the first time in three years, felt herself refuse it.

"Fen," she said. "The wire."

Fen unspooled the line to her hand. She wrapped it twice around her palm, looked up one last time at the hanging face, at the sun-softened ice in its throat, and said, to the mountain rather than to anyone present: "Forgive the liberty."

She pulled.

The crack came first — a sound like the world's largest knuckle — and then the face let go. It did not fall so much as decide, finally, after ten thousand years of waiting, to sit down. The whole hanging wall slid as one mass, gathering speed and stone, and crossed the saddle in a single grinding roar that Yara felt in her teeth and her sternum and the soles of her boots. The dust rose in a wall, white and gold in the noon light, and when it settled, the notch was gone. In its place: a rampart of rubble forty meters high and a quarter mile wide, and four men on the wrong side of it with five horses, no route down, and a very sudden understanding of their situation.

The silence afterward rang. Somewhere below, the meltwater kept up its indifferent silver threading, and a raven lifted off the glacier tongue and wheeled once, commenting.

Yara stood up into the open, in full view, and walked down toward the saddle with Pell at her shoulder and Sera and Ilya behind, and she felt the fear drain out of her and leave something harder and quieter in its place.

The riders had dismounted. They stood among their horses with their rifles half-raised and no target worth the shot, because the woman walking toward them was not running and was not alone and was, they could now plainly see, standing directly in front of the only way off this mountain.

The lead rider was a heavy man with a sunburned neck and the patient eyes of a hired problem-solver. "You've made a mistake," he said.

"I've made a living out of them," Yara said. "But not this one." She gestured, and Pell stepped forward and raised the magnetic recorder so the man could see it, its reels turning, its little brass needle trembling with every word. "This is Pell Ostrew, licensed surveyor, and everything he's recorded in the last six days is going to a treaty commission. The sabotage of the first expedition. The avalanche you people set. The orders you're carrying. It's all on tape, and it's already copied — the duplicates went downriver with our cook two days ago, so shooting us now buys you nothing but a longer report."

It was a bluff. The cook had gone downriver with supplies, yes, but with no duplicates, nothing but a signed statement and a prayer. Yara kept her face still and let the man do the arithmetic himself, and watched him arrive at the answer she wanted: that the story was already loose in the world, and killing its tellers would only make it louder.

"What do you want?" the rider said at last.

"Your names, your employer's name, and your rifles on the ground. In that order. Then you wait here for the commission's men, who will be along within the week, because I'm going to make very sure they know exactly where to dig you out." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "Consider it a rescue. It's what I do."

* * *

It was nearly dusk when the sound came down the valley — boots on scree, unhurried, and Yara turned with her hand on her knife and saw Tomas Vey come around the shoulder of the moraine with his rifle slung and his face unreadable in the failing light.

She had not seen him since the night she'd caught him burning the client's manifest page. Since the riders appeared on the ridge and the truth of the contract came out and the crew scattered upstream, and Tomas had gone — where? She had assumed the worst. She had assumed the worst about Tomas Vey for eleven years, and he had usually earned it.

He stopped ten paces off. His eyes went to the new rampart of rock sealing the notch, to the riders sitting disarmed among their horses, to the survivors, to Pell and his turning reels. He took it all in the way he took in a river before running it: fast, total, and without any expression he'd consent to share.

"The client sent me up to confirm you were handled," he said. "His words."

"And?"

"And I've been walking for two days deciding what 'handled' means." He unslung his rifle, and for one long breath the whole cirque went still — the glacier hissing, the riders frozen, Yara's heart a closed fist — and then he turned and drove the rifle's bolt open and dropped the firing pin into his palm and held it out to her. "Handled," he said. "I'm standing with you. God help me."

Yara took the firing pin. It was warm from his pocket, small and stupid and heavy as a verdict.

"Eleven years," she said.

"I know."

"You let them hang the avalanche on me."

"I know." His voice cracked on it, the first honest sound she'd ever heard him make. "And I'll say so to the commission, on the record, if it costs me everything. It should cost me everything."

Above them the last light left the ore seam, and the vein went dark in the cirque wall, and the first stars came out over the saddle of stone they'd raised in an afternoon. Yara closed her hand around the firing pin and looked down the long valley, toward the low country, the tribunals, the world — and saw, on the far ridge against the last of the light, a line of figures with survey poles and commission banners, already descending.

They had come faster than any of them dared hope. And they were not alone.
