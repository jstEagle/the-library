## 10. Downriver

The letter came in the last week of the thaw, carried up from the valley by a boy on a mule too small for him, and Yara read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something she could believe.

*The Treaty Commission has ruled. The Serrin headwaters and the surrounding cirque are confirmed protected land under Article Nine. The consortium's claim is void. Proceedings regarding individual principals are ongoing.*

Ongoing. She set the letter down on the rough plank table of the waystation — her waystation now, though the deed was a handshake and a debt to no one — and looked out the window at the river running high and brown with snowmelt, the same river that had tried to kill her twice and failed both times.

*Ongoing.* She knew what that word meant. It meant Aldous Crane and whatever men sat behind him had already crossed a border, or bought a magistrate, or simply dissolved one company and reconstituted it under another name, the way water finds a new channel when you dam the old one. The claim was dead. The men who'd staked it were alive, comfortable, and somewhere far below the treeline where commissions could not follow.

Pell had warned her it would end this way. "We can sink the ship," he'd said, sitting in the treaty office in his borrowed coat, his instruments wrapped in oilcloth like relics. "We don't get to drown the sailors."

She'd thought about that for a long time afterward, walking home through country that still wanted her dead on paper. And what she'd finally understood, standing at this window with the letter going soft in her hands, was that partial justice was not a failure. It was just weather. You didn't refuse to travel because the sky wasn't clear. You dressed for it, and you went anyway.

The two survivors from the lost expedition — Marat and Ilsa Voss, brother and sister, half-starved and half-mad by the time Yara's party stumbled into their cirque — had testified for eleven hours. Their account of the sabotage, the cut supply lines, the deliberate abandonment, had been read aloud in a hall Yara would never see, in a language of procedure she'd never learned. Her own testimony had been taken by deposition, in a border-town clerk's office that smelled of ink and damp plaster, while a stenographer's pen scratched like a small animal trying to escape a trap.

The clerk had looked up at the end of it and said, almost apologetically, "Your exclusion orders have been rescinded. Both countries. Effective—" he checked the date, "—last month."

Just like that. Two years of exile, undone by a signature she never saw applied. She'd walked out of that office expecting to feel something enormous — vindication, maybe, or grief, or the strange vertigo of being handed back a life. What she'd felt instead was the wind off the ridge, cold and honest, and the realization that the name they'd returned to her fit differently now. Like a coat she'd outgrown, or grown past needing.

* * *

Tomas came up in June, when the trail was finally dry enough for mules.

He stood in the doorway of the waystation with his hat in his hands, looking older than he had on the river, thinner, the arrogance burned off him like frost off stone in morning sun. He'd spent the winter giving his own testimony, and it had cost him — contracts, reputation, the careful architecture of a career built on knowing which clients to serve and which questions not to ask.

"They offered me a position," he said, over the stew she put in front of him without asking if he wanted any. "Consulting. Route assessment for the consortium's successor company." He turned the spoon over in his fingers. "I told them I'd rather guide funerals."

"Is that a joke?"

"It's a profession. Someone has to carry people up mountains they shouldn't climb and bring them back down." He ate then, in silence, the way men eat after a winter of eating badly. When the bowl was empty he said, without looking at her: "I knew. Before I hired you. About the riders, about what the client intended. Not all of it. Enough."

"I know."

"You should hate me."

"I did." She took his bowl. "It wore out. Hate's heavy gear, Tomas. I stopped carrying things I didn't need somewhere around the fourth day of the gorge."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody — and something between them settled, not into friendship exactly, but into the quieter thing that comes after, which is harder won and lasts longer. He stayed three days, helped her patch the roof, argued with her about the best line through the lower narrows, and left a coil of new rope on the table when he went, because he'd never learned to say anything directly that could be said with equipment.

* * *

The waystation had been a ruin when she found it — an old patrol hut above the confluence, abandoned since before the disaster, since before the blacklist, since before everything. She'd rebuilt it with the deckhand, Fen, whose shoulder had healed crooked from the bullet wound and who had decided, with the absolute certainty of the young, that wherever Yara went next, he was going too. The cook — Ovid, who had spoken perhaps forty words on the entire expedition and none of them wasted — arrived unannounced in May with a sack of flour and a look that asked no permission.

They didn't advertise. There was nothing to advertise. But word travels in the high country the way water does — finding every crack, seeping through everything — and word got out that there was a station above the confluence where someone would come for you.

Not guide you. That was the distinction Yara held to, the one that mattered. Guides led people up mountains for money, for glory, for reasons that usually had more to do with the person climbing than the mountain itself. What Yara did was different. When a prospector went missing above the falls, she went. When two teenagers tried the gorge on a dare and the river took their raft, she went. When a shepherd's flock scattered in a whiteout and the shepherd went after them, she went, and found him, and carried what was left of him down on her back through a night that would have killed anyone less stubborn.

She kept no ledger. People paid what they could — a lamb, a length of rope, sometimes only thanks, which fed no one but kept the fire lit in some way she couldn't explain and didn't try to. Fen kept a board by the door where he chalked the routes they'd run that season, and slowly the board filled with unofficial trails, cutoffs, game paths, the smugglers' ways and herdsmen's ways and desperate ways that existed on no map and appeared in no license examination. The routes that had gotten her banned. The knowledge that had been called recklessness by every licensing board in two countries was suddenly, quietly, the most valuable thing in the valley.

Her old compass came home to her in July. A trapper who'd heard the story tracked down the pawnbroker in Kessel's Crossing and bought it back, and left it on the waystation table while she was out on a call, with no note. She hung it by the door, where it caught the light in the mornings. She didn't need it anymore — she knew these mountains the way she knew her own hands — but some instruments you keep not for use but for witness. It had been with her at the beginning. It could watch what she became.

* * *

On the first hard night of autumn, with the first snow already falling past the window in fat, silent flakes, a knock came at the door.

Fen answered it. Outside stood a woman Yara didn't know — young, soaked to the waist, lips blue, a child wrapped in a blanket on her back and terror written across her face in a language older than any commission's. Behind her, downvalley, the river roared in the dark, and somewhere out in that roar was a man on a raft that should never have launched, and a second child.

Yara was moving before the woman finished speaking. Rope from its pegs, lantern lit, the good boots, Fen already pulling his coat on with his bad shoulder working stiffly and no complaint, because there were no complaints anymore, there was only the work.

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back once at the warm room, the chalked board, the compass turning slightly in the draft from the open door. Somewhere far downstream, in halls she would never enter, men were deciding whether justice was complete. Let them decide. Justice lived in rooms like that one, with paper and procedure, and it did what it could.

But mercy lived here. Mercy lived at eleven thousand feet in a snowstorm, on the water at midnight, on the trails no map admitted to. And mercy, unlike justice, never waited for a ruling.

Yara stepped out into the falling snow, and the mountain closed around her like a hand that had finally learned to hold instead of crush — and ahead, faint through the roar of the river, she thought she heard a voice calling, thin and human and impossibly far away, and she went toward it.
