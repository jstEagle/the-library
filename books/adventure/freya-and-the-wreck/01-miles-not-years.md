## 1. Miles, Not Years

The dust came first, always the dust. It rose off the road in a brown haze that turned the afternoon sun into a smear of copper, and it settled on everything — the tarps, the crates, the teeth. Lena Dunmore had been in the province eleven days and she had stopped tasting anything but grit.

She stood at the edge of the cut, where the highway crew had sliced a trench through a hillside that had been hiding a Roman waystation for two thousand years, and she watched her crew work with the particular attention of a woman counting something other than artifacts.

Tomas was under the water truck again, coaxing the pump with a wrench and a vocabulary he pretended she couldn't hear. Priya was down in the trench, troweling around a mosaic floor with hands so steady they looked machine-made. Bo sat cross-legged on a folding table in what passed for shade, cataloging potsherds into a ledger with columns so precise it could have been typeset. And Kit — sixteen years old, all elbows and appetite — ran water bottles along the trench line like a soldier resupplying a front nobody had declared.

Four people. That was the whole expedition. That was, if you counted honestly, the whole life.

Lena measured time differently than other people did. Not in years. Years were an abstraction, a thing that happened to calendars. She measured in miles — the distance from wherever she was standing to a rocky headland on a coast she had not seen since she was twenty-three years old. Eleven days in this province meant roughly four thousand miles from that coast, and four thousand miles was a distance she had spent five years building, one contract at a time, like a wall made of work.

Her father had died at sea, and Lena had answered by refusing to stand still. There was a logic to it that she had never said aloud: grief is a current, and if you keep moving you can pretend you're swimming rather than being dragged. Five years of salvage digs across three continents. Five years of sites chosen precisely because they were far away. She had gotten very good at choosing them.

"Boss." Kit appeared at her elbow, holding out a canteen. "Tomas says the pump's got maybe two days left in it before it eats itself. He says to tell you that in those exact words because he wants it on record."

"Put it on record," Lena said, taking the canteen. The water inside was warm enough to be tea that had given up. "Tell him if it dies, we hand-screen the last grid squares and I'll buy him a new pump out of my own pocket."

"He'll want that in writing."

"He can have it carved in stone."

Kit grinned and jogged back toward the truck, and Lena drank the warm water and let her eyes drift, as they always drifted, to the horizon east of the site. East was the direction of the sea. Any sea. Her internal compass didn't distinguish; it only pointed at salt water and then flinched.

The province was dying of thirst around them. She'd seen it from the convoy window on the drive in — fields gone the color of old bone, riverbeds cracked into mosaics of their own, villages where the wells had been hauled up on chains and padlocked by municipal order. The dig had been permitted as emergency salvage ahead of a pipeline route, which meant the money was thin, the deadline was brutal, and the archaeology was the kind you did with your heart in your boots: strip it, record it, bury it under gravel forever.

It suited her. Emergency salvage suited her the way shallow water suits a fish that doesn't want to be seen. You went in, you did the work cleanly, you left nothing behind but paperwork, and no one asked you what you were running from, because everyone on an emergency dig was running from something.

Except that lately the running had started to feel less like motion and more like circling. She'd noticed it in the small things. She'd caught herself reading tide tables for coasts she had no contracts near. She'd woken twice this week with the taste of salt in her mouth that wasn't there. And three nights ago, in the dark of her tent, she had taken out the one object she carried that never went into the field crates — a photograph, soft-cornered, of a man standing on the deck of a survey vessel with his arm around a girl who looked exactly like Lena at fifteen — and she had held it for a long time without letting herself think anything at all.

Thinking was the one dive she hadn't been able to schedule.

* * *

The wire came at dusk, which was when wires always seemed to come, delivered by the same boy on the same motorbike who brought the provincial mail twice a week. He gunned his engine up the access track, cut it, and held out a yellow envelope with the solemnity of a man handing over a verdict.

Lena signed, slit it open with her thumbnail, and read it standing in the last light.

*DUNMORE SALVAGE CONSULTANCY. PROPOSED CONTRACT. DEEP-WATER SITE SURVEY AND RECOVERY. NINETY DAYS. TERMS NEGOTIABLE IN PERSON. FEE SUBSTANTIALLY EXCEEDS YOUR CURRENT ENGAGEMENT. REPLY BY WIRE. — M. VANE.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you'd look at a snake to confirm it was a snake.

Marcus Vane. The name arrived in her body before it arrived in her mind — a cold drop behind the sternum, a tightening along the jaw. Vane. Her father's rival, his shadow, the man whose company flag had flown from vessels that worked the same waters, whose name had been spoken at the funeral in a voice carefully sanded of all expression. *Mr. Vane sends his condolences.* As if condolences were cargo he could ship anywhere in the world at a day's notice.

And the site. The wire didn't name it — of course it didn't, Vane would never put a location on paper before he had a signature — but ninety days, deep-water recovery, and the dry season deadline that any coastal operator would know by heart told her everything her mind refused to say. There was one wreck Vane would spend this kind of money on. One wreck with his company's history welded into its hull.

*Their* wreck. The one she had sworn, at nineteen, over a grave with no body in it, never to touch. Never to dive, never to survey, never to take a contract within a hundred miles of. She had kept that vow the way you keep a scar — not through discipline but through structure. Every job she'd accepted for five years had been vetted against a single question she never wrote down: *is this far enough?*

Now the farthest thing from far had found her anyway, in a yellow envelope, in a drought-stricken province, at dusk.

"Lena?" Bo's voice came from the direction of the mess table. "Mail's here? Anything good?"

She looked down at the wire in her hand and became aware that she had been standing motionless long enough for the light to change. The crew was gathering for the evening meal — rice and tinned fish and the flat bread Tomas bought from a woman in the village who had decided the whole crew needed feeding whether they paid or not. Four people, waiting, under a sky going the color of a bruise.

"Business," Lena said. "I'll tell you after dinner."

She put the wire in her breast pocket, over the heartbeat, which was stupid and she knew it, and walked to the mess table, and ate, and watched her people eat, and understood with terrible clarity that whatever she decided about Marcus Vane's contract, she was not going to decide it alone. That was the trap of a found family: you stopped being able to make choices that only ruined yourself.

* * *

Afterward, while Kit washed the pans and Priya cleaned her trowels and Tomas argued quietly with the pump in the dark, Lena sat with Bo at the tailgate of the supply truck, where the camp lantern threw a circle of light just wide enough for two people and a secret.

Bo was sixty-one, ex-university, exiled from academia by a scandal Lena had never asked about and Bo had never offered, and possessed of the finest archival mind Lena had ever seen working in the dirt. Bo read people the way Bo read manifests — patiently, completely, and with an unnerving habit of arriving at conclusions before anyone else knew there was a question.

"You got a wire," Bo said. It wasn't a question either.

"I got a wire."

"From someone whose name made you stop chewing."

Lena almost smiled. "You watch me eat?"

"I watch everyone eat. It's where people are honest." Bo folded their hands. "Well?"

Lena took out the envelope and handed it over. She watched Bo's face do what Bo's face always did — nothing dramatic, just a series of small recalibrations, like instruments finding level.

"Ninety days," Bo said. "Deep-water recovery. No site named." A pause, precise as a ledger entry. "Vane."

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him. Everyone in this trade knows of him. Marine recovery, insurance salvage, a shipping concern with fingers in more ports than most navies." Bo looked up. "I also know that you have spent five years declining contracts in a manner that suggests a map with one hole burned in it. And I know that a man like Vane does not send wires to people he expects to refuse."

"No," Lena agreed. "He doesn't."

"So the fee must be obscene."

"The fee will be whatever keeps a crew of four fed and equipped for a year after this drought dries up every salvage contract on three continents." She heard herself say it and hated how ready the arithmetic was, how quickly her mind had already run the numbers, how pragmatism had dressed itself and reported for duty before grief even got out of bed. "Emergency permits are frozen until the rains come back. We've got maybe six weeks of paid work left here. After that—"

"After that, nothing," Bo finished. "I've checked. So has Priya, though she pretends she hasn't. Tomas owes money to a pump manufacturer and possibly a person. And Kit—" Bo glanced toward the sound of splashing dishwater, and lowered their voice with genuine gentleness, "—Kit has nowhere else that would have them."

"There it is," Lena said quietly. "There's the whole case for saying yes, laid out by someone who isn't even asking why I'm staring at that envelope like it's loaded."

"Isn't it?"

Lena didn't answer. Out past the lantern ring, the dark was absolute. Somewhere east, beyond hills she couldn't see, there was ocean, and in that ocean there was a wreck, and in that wreck there was a hole in the world shaped exactly like her father, and she had built five years of distance over that hole the way you build a road over a sinkhole — fast, and hoping the ground holds.

"Here's what I know about Marcus Vane," Bo said, into the silence. "He buys things. Companies, wrecks, silence, people. And the people he buys, he buys politely, with terms that seem generous right up until you understand they were designed." Bo tapped the wire once. "A man like that doesn't offer you this contract because he needs your crew. He offers it because he needs *you* — your name, your methods, your clean reputation — attached to whatever comes up off that seabed. Certified legitimate. Your signature makes his history true."

"And if I say no?"

"Then he finds another crew, pays them half as much, and gets a sloppier version of the same legitimacy. And we spend the wet season eating each other's rations." Bo shrugged, an eloquent, terrible shrug. "I'm not advising you, Lena. I'm showing you the board."

Lena looked at the board. Four people. Six weeks of funding. A drought with no end date. And at the center of it, a wreck she had promised a dead man she'd never touch, offered to her by the living man she suspected — had always, somewhere beneath the years and the miles, suspected — had something to do with why there was a grave with no body in it.

Restless unease moved through her like weather. She named it pragmatism, because naming it anything else would mean sitting still, and sitting still was the one thing she had trained herself out of for five years.

"We'd need terms," she said finally. "In writing. Full crew veto on anything unsafe. No findings clause — I don't sign away publication, not ever, not for any fee. And I meet him in person before anything is countersigned. I want to look at his face when he talks about that site."

Bo nodded slowly. "Those are good terms."

"They're not good terms. They're the terms of a woman pretending she has leverage." Lena stood, pocketed the wire, and felt the familiar itch in her feet, the old engine of departure turning over. Except this time the destination wasn't away. This time every road pointed back.

"Get some sleep," she told Bo. "Tomorrow I'll put it to the crew. All of it. They vote."

"They'll vote yes."

"I know they'll vote yes. That's what frightens me."

Bo tilted their head. "Then maybe ask yourself what you were hoping they'd say."

Lena walked to her tent through the cold dark, past Kit's bedroll, past the generator ticking as it cooled, and lay down on her cot with the wire on her chest and the photograph in its oilcloth sleeve beneath her pillow, and she did not sleep. She lay measuring, the way she always measured: four thousand miles, give or take, from this dust to that water. Ninety days of contract. Five years of distance.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a hull breach taking on sea, the thought she had been outrunning since dusk:

*If I go back, I'll finally have to know.*

By morning she had written the reply. By noon the boy on the motorbike had carried it out along the cracked road toward the provincial telegraph office, and Lena stood at the edge of the trench watching the dust of him fade, feeling the miles begin, for the first time in five years, to shorten instead of grow.

Priya came up beside her, squinting after the bike. "That the answer?"

"That's the answer."

"To Vane?"

"To Vane."

Priya studied her for a moment with the flat, assessing gaze of a diver reading conditions before a descent. "You look like someone who just volunteered to go somewhere she swore she'd never go."

Lena kept her eyes on the road. "I look like someone who read a fee schedule."

"Sure," Priya said. "That too."

Below them, in the trench, Tomas's repaired pump coughed, caught, and began to run — steady, reluctant, alive — and the sound followed Lena all the rest of that day, like a countdown that had already started.
