## 2. The Crew That Chose Her

The telegram reached her in the third week of the drought, but by then Lena had already read it a hundred times in her head. She'd carried it folded in her breast pocket through two days of screening sediment at the roadside dig, feeling its weight like a coin she hadn't decided to spend.

*VANE MARITIME RECOVERY. CONTRACT OFFER. SITE OF YOUR SELECTION. TERMS GENEROUS. REPLY BY MONTH'S END. — M.V.*

Site of your selection. As if Vane didn't know exactly which site she'd choose, which site there was no choosing around. He'd written it that way deliberately — a door left open so she could pretend she'd walked through it herself.

She burned the telegram that night in the camp stove, but not before memorizing every word.

Now, three weeks later, she stood at the edge of the firelight watching her crew argue about beans, and the lie sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The camp was a lean-to of canvas and truck tarps pitched in the lee of a dry riverbed, four hundred kilometers from any coast that mattered. The province had gone the color of old bone — cracked earth, thorn scrub, a road that dissolved into dust behind them each morning. They'd been here six weeks on a contract surveying an ancient caravan route for a university that kept delaying payment, and everyone knew what that meant: another season of thin rice and thinner patience.

Tomas had the cook fire going, which meant Tomas had opinions about the beans. He always did. He crouched over the pot with a wooden spoon held like a surgeon's instrument, a broad-shouldered man of fifty whose hands were a map of everything he'd ever fixed — burns from engine blocks, scars from winch cable, a missing half-finger he refused to discuss and everyone else refused to ask about.

"More salt," Priya said from her perch on an ammunition crate.

"It has enough salt."

"It has *ambition* of salt. It hasn't achieved it."

"You want salt, dive to the bottom of the sea and lick a rock."

Priya grinned and threw a pebble at him. She was twenty-nine and built like a whip — narrow, coiled, deceptively strong — with close-cropped black hair going silver early at the temples and eyes that went flat and calm the moment she hit water. Lena had hired her five years ago off a pearl boat where the captain beat his divers, and in five years Priya had never once asked for anything except more bottom time. She could hold her breath long enough to worry people. She could read a wreck's bones through two meters of silt. She was, as far as Lena knew, fearless in every way except one: she hated being lied to, and she always knew when it was happening.

Bo sat cross-legged near the lantern with a ledger open across her knees, cataloging the day's finds by lantern light because she claimed daylight made her sloppy. Bo was the oldest of them — sixty-one, maybe older, she'd stopped correcting people years ago — a former university archivist who'd left academia after some scandal she described only as "a disagreement about who owns the past." She wore three sweaters even in the drought heat and drank tea boiled down to tar. Her handwriting was so precise it looked printed. When she looked up at you over her spectacles, you felt audited.

And Kit — Kit came skidding into the firelight out of the dark with dust to the eyebrows, sixteen years old and all elbows, carrying the mail sack from the crossroads forty minutes' run away.

"Nothing good," Kit announced, dropping onto the ground between Tomas and Priya like this was an assigned seat, which after eight months it effectively was. "University says payment's delayed again. There's a letter for Bo. And—" Kit paused, and Lena saw the kid clock her face, saw the pause stretch a half-second too long, "—that's it."

Kit was a terrible liar. Lena filed that away and felt the stone in her chest shift.

They ate. This was the part of the day Lena trusted most, the hour when the work was done and the fire did its old animal work of pulling everyone inward. Tomas told a story about an engine he'd rebuilt in a monsoon with the parts of three dead trucks, growing less plausible with each telling. Priya heckled him with the ease of long practice. Bo opened her letter, read it twice, and set it aside with an expression Lena couldn't read — though later she'd learn it was a letter from a former student offering her a position back at a university, a door home that Bo would quietly decline without telling anyone why.

Kit ate like someone who'd learned young that food could disappear, and watched Lena eat like someone keeping count.

It was Kit who finally said it, because Kit had no gift for waiting either.

"So are you going to tell us, or do we have to guess?"

The fire popped. Three faces turned toward her. Lena set down her tin plate.

"Tell you what."

"That you got an offer." Kit's chin came up, defiant and scared at once. "I ran the mail both ways. I know you sent a reply to the harbor office two weeks ago. I know you've been carrying something in your pocket and reading it at night. I'm not stupid."

"No one said you were stupid," Lena said quietly.

"You're doing the thing," Kit pressed on, voice cracking slightly and hating itself for it. "Where you decide things for everybody and tell us after. Like the Marrow Point job. Like when we lost the barge contract and you didn't eat for three days so we wouldn't notice the money was gone."

Lena looked at the fire. Tomas had stopped stirring. Bo had closed her ledger.

"I got a contract offer," Lena said. "A big one. Salvage excavation, full funding, equipment, hazard pay. Enough to clear what the university owes us and put a year's cushion under all of us besides."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"What's the catch?" Priya asked. Not *is* there a catch. Priya had been in this trade long enough to know there was always a catch.

"The client is Marcus Vane."

The name landed on the camp like a dropped anchor. Tomas swore softly in his mother's language. Bo took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, which was what she did instead of shouting.

"Vane Maritime," Priya said slowly. "The Vane. The one who—"

"Yes."

"And he wants to hire *us*?" Tomas set the spoon down. "He's got forty crews on retainer. He's got government contracts. Why does a man like that need four nobodies in a dry riverbed?"

"Because he needs a crew that works cheap, works fast, and doesn't ask questions," Bo said. "We're famous for all three."

"He named the site?" Priya asked.

Lena felt the stone turn over. "No. Site of my selection."

Priya stared at her. Of all of them, Priya understood first — Lena could see it happen behind her eyes, the diver's instinct for pressure changes, for the moment the water goes from clear to black. Her gaze flicked to the west, toward the coast none of them had ever heard Lena mention, and then back.

"Lena," she said carefully. "What site are you going to select?"

* * *

Later, when the others had turned in, Lena sat by the coals with Bo, who had a way of staying present without demanding conversation. The old woman poured tea from the dented pot and handed Lena a cup, and for a while they just watched the last light die over the broken hills.

"My father worked for Vane once," Lena said. It came out flatter than she intended. "Before he had his own boat. Before me, mostly."

"I know," Bo said.

Lena looked at her.

"You talk in your sleep," Bo said mildly. "Not words. Numbers. Coordinates, I'd guess. And a name or two." She sipped her tea. "I've known you seven years, girl. You think I haven't done the arithmetic?"

Lena wrapped her hands around the cup. "The wreck is real. Off the Verren coast. My father went down with it — or near enough. They never recovered him. The official finding was storm damage, hull failure, bad luck. His logbook disappeared the week after. Vane's company handled the insurance settlement." She heard her own voice go distant, clinical, the voice she used for site reports. "Twenty-two years ago."

"And now Vane wants to excavate it."

"He wants it excavated before the dry season ends and the coastal authority closes the licensing window. He wants it done quiet, done fast, and done by someone with a reason not to gossip." She laughed, short and ugly. "He thinks my grief makes me discreet."

"Does it?"

Lena didn't answer. Bo refilled her cup without being asked.

"You're not going to tell them the whole of it," Bo said. It wasn't a question.

"They'll say yes anyway. They need the money. Kit needs—" She stopped.

"Kit needs a family," Bo said, "and found one, and will follow you into the sea if you ask. Which is precisely why you should tell them everything. People forgive hard truths. They don't forgive being managed." The old woman's eyes caught the coal-light. "I managed people once, in my other life. It cost me everything I loved about the work."

"There's a clause in the contract," Lena said, barely above the fire's whisper. "No publication of findings. Everything we recover belongs to Vane. Everything we *learn* belongs to Vane."

"And what is it you expect to learn?"

Lena looked into the coals and saw, as she always did, black water and the shape of a hull going down and a radio call that cut off mid-word.

"That my father wasn't unlucky," she said.

Bo nodded slowly, as if Lena had confirmed something long suspected, and said nothing else, which was Bo's way of saying everything.

* * *

In the morning they voted. It was how Lena ran the crew — she'd established it in their first month together, half as principle and half as armor: no one signed onto a job they hadn't chosen. She laid it out plainly around the breakfast fire. The terms, the money, the deadline, the secrecy clause, the client. She gave them everything except the one thing that mattered, and she hated herself with a clean, efficient hatred while she did it.

Tomas went first, as he always did, because he believed leadership meant absorbing risk before anyone else had to.

"We're owed eleven weeks' pay by that university and we both know we'll see half of it," he said. "My daughter's tuition comes due in the spring. If the money's real, I'm in. If the money's a story, I want to know before we're standing on his dock."

"It's real," Lena said. "I've seen the draft."

Priya was next. She looked at Lena for a long moment — that flat-water look, the one that meant she was reading depths — and Lena braced for the question she couldn't answer honestly.

"Full diving budget," Priya said. "New regs, new lines, my pick of the gear manifest. And nobody tells me what I can and can't bring up."

"Agreed."

"Then I'm in." A pause. "For now."

Bo folded her hands. "I'll come," she said, "on one condition. Every artifact, every document, every measurement — I keep a complete duplicate archive. For the record. Unofficially."

Lena's heart stumbled. "That violates the secrecy clause."

"The secrecy clause violates history," Bo said serenely. "I've lived longer than you, girl. I know which one holds."

Something passed between them — Lena couldn't name it, some current beneath the words, the sense that Bo was building a lifeboat and inviting her aboard without saying so aloud. She agreed before she could think better of it.

Then all of them looked at Kit.

Kit had been very quiet through the whole discussion, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, wearing the expression of someone doing arithmetic that keeps coming out wrong. Finally the kid unfolded.

"How far is it?" Kit asked. "The site. From here."

"Nine days by road. Ten with the trucks loaded."

"To the coast." Kit's voice was careful. "Your coast?"

The fire crackled. Lena met the kid's eyes and couldn't make herself lie outright, not to that face. "Near it," she said.

Kit studied her for a long moment — and Lena watched the kid decide, visibly, deliberately, to accept the answer. It was the most adult thing Lena had ever seen Kit do, and it frightened her more than any storm.

"Okay," Kit said. "I'm in. Somebody's got to run your mail."

Tomas raised his tin mug. "Motion carries?"

"Carries," Lena said. "Four to nothing. We leave tomorrow at first light."

They cheered, actually cheered, and Priya threw another pebble at Tomas, and Bo began immediately listing supplies in her precise hand, and Kit was already talking about the route, the roads, the maps — and Lena sat in the middle of it with the warmth of them washing over her like sun on cold skin, and thought: *this is the moment I should tell them.* 

She opened her mouth.

"—and I'll need to requisition extra lamp oil," Bo was saying, "if we're working the archive at night—"

She closed it again.

There would be time, she told herself. On the road. At the coast. When they could see the water and understand. There would be time.

But that night, checking the trucks by lantern light, she found Kit awake in the bed of the second lorry, curled against a spare tire with the regional map spread out under the lantern glass, tracing the western roads with one finger — past the pass, past the salt flats, down and down toward a coastline marked in faded ink.

Toward a small bay with no name on the map at all.

Kit looked up, caught tracing, and didn't apologize. Just held her gaze across the lantern light with those too-old eyes, and said, "Ten days," in a voice that wasn't asking about the drive.

Lena said nothing. Kit rolled up the map, and the silence between them rode all the way to morning.
