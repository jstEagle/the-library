## 3. Terms Written in Salt

Marcus Vane had learned long ago that the best place to break a person was a room with a view of water. Not because water was beautiful — though this harbor, at seven in the morning, with the light coming off it like hammered tin, was beautiful enough — but because people who worked the sea could never quite look at it without measuring themselves against it. And measurement was leverage.

He had chosen the harbor office himself. The Port Authority's administrative annex on the north quay: a room of scarred teak and salt-stained glass, smelling of diesel and old paper, with a window that framed the fishing fleet going out in a ragged line against the dawn. It was not his office. That was the point. A man's own office was a fortress; borrowed ground made the other party feel they'd been granted something. Vane understood the architecture of small advantages the way other men understood scripture.

He arrived forty minutes early. He always did. In forty minutes he drank one cup of coffee — black, from the machine in the corridor that tasted of rust and cost nothing — and read through the contract a final time, though he could have recited it. He'd drafted most of it himself, in longhand, three weeks ago, sitting in a hotel room in a city whose name no longer mattered to him, while rain came down the window like something trying to get in.

The contract was eleven pages. Nine of them were ordinary: scope of work, salvage rights, indemnification schedules, the dry-season deadline expressed in dates so precise they looked almost tender. The tenth page held the secrecy clause. The eleventh held the publication ban. Those two pages were the contract; everything else was packaging.

And beneath all of it, unspoken, unwritten, lay the true term — the one Vane had built the entire document around and would never say aloud:

*You will come back to her.*

*You will come back to the wreck that killed your father, and you will do it for money, and every day you are down there you will know I am the reason you came.*

He set the pages square on the table, aligned their edges with two fingers, and waited.

* * *

Lena Dunmore arrived at 7:04. Four minutes late — deliberate, he guessed, or at least he chose to believe so, which amounted to the same thing when you were reading a person. She came through the door without knocking, which told him she'd already located the room yesterday, walked past it once, maybe stood outside it. Careful people did that. His father had been careful too, right up until the sea ate him.

She looked older than the photographs. Five years of salvage digs will do that to a face — sun-cracked the skin, put a certain flatness in the eyes, the look of someone who has stopped expecting the world to be kind and started treating kindness as a possible scam. But she carried herself the same way she had at twenty-four, the last time Vane had seen her in the flesh: shoulders level, chin down slightly, weight balanced like a woman perpetually ready to change direction. Her father's daughter. He'd hated how much she resembled him. He hated it now, standing, extending his hand.

"Miss Dunmore."

"Mr. Vane." Her grip was dry and brief. "You're punctual."

"You're nearly not."

"Traffic." She sat across from him without being invited, which he noted and filed. "Your driver circled the quay twice. I assume that was your idea."

It had been. A small test of patience, and she'd named it inside four seconds. Vane smiled and let the smile reach his eyes, because a smile that didn't reach the eyes was wasted on someone like her. "I wanted to see if you'd wait."

"I don't wait." She pulled the contract toward her without asking permission. "I read fast. You'll want to watch me do it anyway, so let's not pretend otherwise."

"I wouldn't dream of rushing you."

"Good." She uncapped a pen — her own pen, cheap plastic, chewed at the end, an affectation or a habit — and began to turn pages.

Vane watched her read the way a fisherman watches a line. He had prepared for this meeting the way he prepared for everything: exhaustively, obsessively, with contingency plans nested inside contingency plans like the compartments of a dive case. He knew her crew by name and salary history. He knew that Tomas Reyes had a daughter in a hospital in Cartagena and a debt that grew faster than his paychecks. He knew Priya Nair had been blacklisted from two national maritime institutes for refusing to falsify survey data, which meant she was either incorruptible or unemployable, and Vane had bet his operation on the second. He knew Bo Okonkwo kept meticulous records and Kit — the runner, the child, really — had no legal standing whatsoever, which made the whole enterprise beautifully deniable.

He knew Lena Dunmore took jobs that paid well and stayed gone. He knew she had refused, twice, publicly, to consult on any project touching the *Meridian* wreck site. And he knew — this was the load-bearing fact, the keel of everything — that her father's final dive log had never been recovered. That she believed, or half-believed, or feared believing, that the official account was wrong. He had counted on that hunger the way you count on a tide. You don't have to pull a woman toward a thing she already wants. You just have to stop standing in front of the door.

"Page six," she said, not looking up. "Indemnification. Your company holds harmless if my diver dies?"

"Standard for deep salvage."

"It's standard for companies that expect divers to die." She turned the page. "I'm crossing out the waiver on equipment failure caused by your advance team's modifications to the site."

"There have been no—"

"There's a base camp half-built on my beach, Mr. Vane. Your people poured concrete footings into sand that hasn't held a structure since before I was born. If your platform fails under my winch, I'm not signing away my engineer's life to save your lawyers a lawsuit." She drew a line through the clause, initialed the margin, kept reading.

Vane felt something flicker behind his sternum that he refused, on principle, to call admiration. He had expected negotiation on price. Price was easy; price was a language everyone spoke. He had not expected her to go straight for the liability clauses with a pen already uncapped, as though she'd read the contract before she ever walked in — and perhaps she had; Bo Okonkwo's reputation suggested the archivist could reconstruct a document from a photograph taken through a window.

"Reasonable," he said, and meant: *noted, countered later.* "Continue."

* * *

She reached page ten at 7:31 and slowed.

He watched her slow. It was subtle — a fractional stillness in the hand holding the page, the pen hovering a millimeter above the paper — but Vane had spent thirty years watching people encounter terms they hadn't expected, and he could read hesitation the way sonar reads a hull.

"Absolute secrecy," she read aloud, flatly. "'The Contractor shall not disclose the location, nature, contents, or existence of the salvage operation to any party not named herein, during the term of this agreement and in perpetuity thereafter.'" She looked up. "In perpetuity."

"The site is commercially sensitive."

"The site is a grave."

"The site," Vane said gently, "is a wreck that has lain untouched for five years precisely because people treated it as a grave. I'm offering to treat it as what it actually is. Cargo. Steel. Value." He let a beat pass. "Grief is expensive, Miss Dunmore. I'm simply asking you not to bill me for it."

Her eyes came up and held his, and for a moment neither of them moved, and the harbor sounds came through the glass — gulls, a winch somewhere, the low bell of a buoy — and Vane thought: *there it is.* There was the flinch. Small, controlled, immediately mastered, but present. She had heard the word *grief* come out of his mouth and something behind her face had gone white and cold as ballast.

"Page eleven," she said.

"'The Contractor shall assign all publication, exhibition, academic, and media rights in any findings to the Company, irrevocably,'" she read. "Irrevocably. So whatever we find down there — whatever is down there — it belongs to you. The story belongs to you. Even if the story is about my father."

"Even then." He said it plainly because softness would have insulted them both. "You'll be compensated beyond anything your crew has seen. Every member of your team, paid in full, plus completion bonuses that will set them up for years. In exchange, the findings are mine. All of them. Forever."

"And if I refuse the clause?"

"Then there's no contract, and some other crew dives the site by August, and you spend the rest of your life wondering what they brought up." He folded his hands on the table. "I've watched you work, Miss Dunmore. From a distance. You don't strike me as a woman who can live with that."

* * *

What he did not say — what he would never say, what he had rehearsed not saying in hotel rooms on three continents — was this:

*Five years ago I stood on that cliff road and watched the smoke go up off the water and I knew, before anyone told me, exactly what had happened down there. I knew because I signed the order. I knew because the cargo had to disappear and your father was the only man honest enough to make its disappearance impossible.*

*I have spent five years making sure the story stayed buried, and I have spent those same five years unable to stop checking whether it had surfaced. And now the survey permits have lapsed and the dry season opens a window and I need that wreck gutted and documented by someone competent enough to find nothing — and there is no one alive more competent than you, and no one alive with more reason to keep quiet about whatever she finds.*

*So I am hiring the one person on earth who might destroy me, because she is also the one person on earth whose destruction I can guarantee. Guilt is the strongest adhesive known to man, Miss Dunmore. You sealed your father's coffin with it. I intend to seal yours with mine.*

Instead he said: "Take your time."

She took her time. She went back to page six and renegotiated the equipment-liability carve-out, and he conceded it because it cost him nothing. She struck a clause requiring weekly reports directly to his office and replaced it with monthly summaries, and he conceded that too, smiling, because weekly reports were for clients who needed information, and Vane needed only compliance. She asked for a hardship premium for the runner — "the kid eats like a horse and gets hazard pay or doesn't come" — and he approved it instantly, because generosity toward the small members of a crew bought loyalty cheaper than any retainer.

Then she came back to page ten and page eleven, and read them again, slowly, and Vane watched her do the arithmetic he had designed her to do: the numbers against the names. Tomas's hospital bills. Priya's blacklisting. Bo's aging mother. The kid's future. A payday like this one didn't set people up; it freed them. And freedom, Vane knew, was the one commodity salvagers could never buy for themselves, because they kept spending everything they earned buying each other out of trouble.

"You've priced this knowing I can't walk away," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I've priced this knowing you're a professional," Vane said. "Professionals don't walk away from work. Amateurs walk away. Professionals finish."

"My father was a professional."

"Yes," Vane said. "He was."

The silence that followed lasted four seconds, and Vane let every one of them stand, because the sentence had done exactly what he'd built it to do. He watched her jaw tighten. He watched her look at the window — at the water, at the fleet coming home now with the morning behind them, nets dripping silver — and he knew she was seeing a different sea, a different morning, five years back. He knew because he saw it too. They were looking at the same ghost through the same glass, and only one of them knew it.

"He died on that site," Lena said quietly. "The official report called it a squall. Out-of-season. Freak."

"Squalls are freaks," Vane said. "That's what makes them squalls."

"Mm." She turned back to the contract. "One amendment."

"Name it."

"If I find human remains, they come up. All of them. Named, documented, returned to families. Publication rights or no publication rights, that happens, and if your lawyers object, tell them I'll leave the strongboxes where they lie and let the site silt over for another fifty years."

Human remains. Vane kept his face pleasant and his pulse even and thought: *she has no idea how precisely she has just cut.* There were no remains. He had made sure of that five years ago, in ways the sea had been generous enough to help with. Her amendment was a blade aimed at a wound that wasn't there — and agreeing to it would cost him nothing and make him look humane, and refusing it would raise questions he could not afford.

"Agreed," he said. "Remains come up. Write it in."

She wrote it in. Her handwriting was small and hard, like her father's.

* * *

She signed at 7:52.

Vane watched the pen move and felt the particular satisfaction he imagined other men got from prayer — the sense of a vast mechanism accepting your input and beginning, silently, to turn. She signed the secrecy clause. She signed the publication ban. She initialed the perpetual confidentiality term without further comment, and when she finished she blotted the signature with the heel of her hand, an oddly old-fashioned gesture, and slid the contract back across the table.

"One copy for me."

"Naturally." He passed her the duplicate, already countersigned. Their fingers did not touch. He had planned for them not to touch.

She stood, tucked the contract inside her jacket — inside the jacket, against the body, not in any bag; she'd carry it like a weapon or a wound — and looked at him one last time.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I think you know more about that wreck than any contract says. And one day, Mr. Vane, somebody's going to ask you what."

For a moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — Marcus Vane felt the room tilt, the way a deck tilts when a swell comes under you from a direction the forecast never mentioned. Then it passed. She was at the door. She had been bluffing, or probing, or simply saying the kind of thing salvagers said to men like him, the ritual defiance of the hired against the hirer. Old guilt would keep her compliant. Old guilt was the strongest chain he owned, and he had just welded it to her wrist with her own signature.

"Miss Dunmore," he said pleasantly. "Dive safe."

The door closed behind her. Vane remained at the table a while, listening to the harbor work, watching the light move on the water. Then he took out his phone and sent a single message to the man he kept on the coast:

*She's signed. Watch the crew. Especially the diver.*

Outside, the tide was turning. It always was. That was the thing about the sea that Vane had never managed to learn, in thirty years of profiting from it: it kept every account, and it settled them on its own schedule, and no contract ever written had yet survived a change of tide.

He gathered his papers, aligned their edges, and went out to meet his lawyers — certain, perfectly certain, that he had just bought the truth along with the silence.

Certain, and wrong.
