## 4. The Road Back

Kit had three maps of the same country, and none of them agreed.

The first was the official one — a gas-station road atlas with a coffee ring stamped across the coastal counties like a brand. The second was the one Bo had drawn by hand on the back of an equipment manifest, all careful ink and little annotations: *bad bridge here, water at km 40, don't trust the sign to Ferrow's Crossing.* The third map Kit kept folded in the inside pocket of a jacket two sizes too big, and it wasn't drawn in ink at all. It was made of things overheard.

*Black rocks like teeth,* Lena had said once, half-asleep against a crate of dive tanks outside a dig in the northern province, talking to no one. *You could hear it from the cliff road. Like something chewing.*

*Tide comes in sideways there,* she'd told Priya another time, when they thought Kit was asleep in the truck bed. *Doesn't roll. Sideways. Like it's coming for you.*

Kit had never seen the coast. Had never seen the ocean at all, if you didn't count the ferry crossing two years ago, which Kit did count, but Tomas said didn't count because "you were below decks throwing up, kid." So the coast lived in Kit's head as a country built entirely out of Lena's nightmares — and now the convoy was driving straight into it, and nobody seemed to think that was strange except Kit.

Four vehicles. Tomas drove the lead rig with the compressor trailer groaning behind it like something unhappy to be alive. Priya had the dive truck. Bo rode shotgun in the supply van with every document the crew owned in waterproof sleeves across his lap, cataloging even the landscape, muttering elevations. And Lena drove the last vehicle alone, which everyone pretended was about weight distribution and everyone knew was not.

Kit rode with Bo, officially as navigator, actually because Bo let you ask questions without making them feel like debts.

"Left at the junction," Kit said, finger on the atlas. "Unless the bridge is still out."

"The bridge is out," Bo said, not looking up from his sleeves. "It's been out since before you were born, probably. There's a ford."

"There's always a ford."

"There's always a ford," Bo agreed comfortably. He was a round, patient man who moved through the world like paper moves through a filing system — slowly, precisely, everything ending up where it belonged. Kit trusted him the way you trust gravity.

The country changed around them mile by mile. They'd left the drought province two days ago, the cracked earth and the skeletal irrigation rigs, and climbed into hills that were green in a tired, scrubbed-out way, like old upholstery. Now the road bent east and downhill, and the air through the vents started tasting different. Salt-adjacent. Kit rolled the window down and got a face full of it — brine and kelp and cold stone — and something in Kit's chest went tight and eager, because this was it, this was the edge of the world, the place the maps ran out of land.

Then Kit looked in the side mirror and saw the fourth vehicle fall back another fifty meters, and the eagerness curdled into something else.

* * *

They stopped at noon at a crossroads store that sold fuel, ice, and religious candles, which Kit felt said a lot about the local understanding of what travelers needed. While Tomas argued diesel prices with a woman who'd clearly won arguments like this for forty years, Kit walked the lot, stretching legs gone stiff, and found Lena standing at the edge of the gravel looking east.

There was nothing to look at. Just the road continuing, a fence line, a hill with a radio mast. But Lena stood there the way people stand at gravesides, hands loose, face doing absolutely nothing, which is how Kit had learned to recognize the times her face was doing the most.

Kit came and stood beside her. Said nothing. This was a skill — knowing when silence was a door you could open and when it was a wall. With Lena it was usually a wall, but walls had doors in them if you waited long enough.

"Crossroads of Marren," Lena said finally, to the horizon. "That's what this used to be called. Before they paved it."

"You've been here before."

It wasn't really a question, and Lena didn't treat it like one. She looked at the hill with the mast. "There was a chapel up there. Probably still is." A pause. "We stopped once. Coming home. I threw up from the winding road and my father bought me a lemonade at the store and told me the sea would settle my stomach better than any drink made on land."

Something moved through her voice — not grief exactly, or not only. Something older and more worn, like a coin rubbed smooth.

"And did it?" Kit asked.

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "I never found out." She turned back toward the trucks. "Bo's driving next stretch. Tell him the ford's flooded — I can see the willows standing in water."

She walked away, and Kit stood there holding the lemonade story like something hot, turning it over. *Coming home.* From where? From the coast? From the wreck?

Kit went and told Bo about the ford, and then, casually, the way you ask about weather, asked: "How long has Lena been running digs?"

Bo's pen paused over his manifest for exactly one second. One second was a long time for Bo. "Since she was younger than you," he said. "Longer, maybe. She grew up on the water somewhere along this stretch. She doesn't talk about it much."

"Why not?"

Bo capped his pen. Looked at Kit with those mild eyes that saw everything and filed it all. "Because some places you leave, and some places you get left, and she's never told anyone which one this was. Not even me." He patted the seat beside him. "Ride. We've got a ford to drown a van in."

* * *

The ford nearly did drown the van, which Bo took with the serenity of a man being proven right about something. They crossed in low gear, water climbing the doorsills, and on the far bank the road began its long descent toward the coast proper, switchbacking down through pine forest that smelled like resin and cold.

Kit navigated, which mostly meant calling out landmarks and watching the mirror.

That was the thing Kit couldn't stop doing now. Watching the mirror. Because every landmark did something to Lena's lead vehicle. Not much. That was the eerie part. Most people, you'd see it — a flinch, a slowdown, a hand moving on the wheel. Lena gave nothing. What happened instead was a kind of subtraction. At the chapel hill, her truck got quieter — Kit couldn't have explained how you heard quietness through an engine and thirty meters of road, but you could; the whole rhythm of it flattened. At the burned-out shell of a roadside inn, her brake lights never came on at the curve that badly needed braking. At a rusted sign reading WELCOME TO HOLLOWTIDE BAY — POP. 1,140, letters shot out and salt-eaten, she passed it at exactly the speed limit, neither faster nor slower, and somehow that was the worst one. Like someone reciting a prayer they hated in a perfectly level voice.

Hollowtide Bay. Kit wrote it in the margin of the atlas and underlined it twice.

At the next stop — a one-pump garage where a dog slept in the shade of the ice machine — Kit cornered Priya at the air hose, checking pressures with the focused calm she brought to everything that floated or dived.

"Priya. Do you know why Vane wants this wreck?"

Priya glanced up. She was small and wiry and had hands that looked like they'd been assembled from spare anchor parts, and she considered questions seriously before answering them, always, even easy ones. Which meant when she answered fast, it mattered.

"No," she said. "And that bothers me. Salvage men want wrecks for cargo or for salvage rights or for ego. Vane's paying us like it's all three, and I can't find the angle."

"Do you know where we're going?"

"Hollowtide. Why?"

Kit hesitated. Telling on Lena felt wrong. But not telling felt worse, in a different direction. "Have you noticed how she drives back there? Through this stretch?"

Priya straightened up, hose hissing. She looked down the road, back the way they'd come, then at Kit, and something shifted in her face — the diver's expression, the one that meant she'd spotted something under the surface that the surface hadn't announced. "I noticed she hasn't eaten since yesterday morning," she said quietly. "I noticed she checked the weather report four times for a coast she claims she's never worked." She coiled the hose. "What are you asking me, Kit?"

"I'm asking if it's just me."

"It's not just you." Priya said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it heavier. She looked toward Lena's truck, parked at the pumps, engine off, and even from here Kit could see Lena sitting very still behind the wheel, both hands on the rim at ten and two, staring at nothing. "But here's the thing," Priya went on. "People get to carry their own reasons until they can't anymore. If she can't, she'll tell us. Or she'll crack, and we'll catch her. Either way — we don't pick the lock. We wait at the door. Understood?"

Kit understood, and hated it a little, and nodded anyway.

"One more thing." Priya slung the hose back on its hook. "You noticing things is useful. You noticing things *out loud* at the wrong moment could cost us this job, and this job costs money some of us need pretty badly. So map it. Write it down. Keep it between us until it turns into something real." A ghost of a smile. "Congratulations, archivist-in-training. Bo will be furious and proud."

* * *

They crested the last ridge an hour before sunset, and the ocean appeared all at once, the way it does — no warning, just the world ending in a line of hammered silver, enormous beyond any preparation. Kit pressed against the window and forgot Lena, forgot Vane, forgot the missing pieces, forgot everything except the sheer fact of it. It went on forever. It went on past forever. It breathed.

Then the road turned north along the clifftop, and Kit remembered everything again.

Because the cliffs here were black. Not gray, not brown — black, wet-black rock stacked in jagged shelves, and the surf hit them and came up white and angry, and the sound carried up to the road even through closed windows: a deep, rhythmic percussion, like something enormous chewing slowly.

Like something chewing.

Kit's skin prickled. *Black rocks like teeth. You could hear it from the cliff road.* This was it. This was the road from the nightmare, the actual road, and Lena's truck ahead had slowed for the first time all day — barely, barely, five kilometers under the limit now — and Kit watched the mirror and watched the back of the lead rig and understood, with the cold certainty of a kid who'd learned to read adults the way other kids read tide tables, that Lena Dunmore was driving toward the worst day of her life on purpose, and had told no one why.

The convoy dropped down off the clifftop onto a shelf of coastal grassland, and there — visible even from the road, floodlit against the failing light — was the advance camp. Generators humming. Canvas structures. A crane already erected on a timber pad near the shore. Vane's people had been busy. Flags with the Vane Maritime mark snapped in the sea wind, neat and proprietary, like the company owned the wind too.

Tomas pulled the convoy up in a loose crescent and killed the engines, and the sudden silence was filled entirely by the sea, that slow grinding chew against the black rocks.

Everyone piled out, stretching, joking, loud with the relief of arrival. Bo began immediately inventorying what the advance team had and hadn't done, lips pursed. Priya walked straight to the cliff edge and stood studying the water with the naked hunger she usually reserved for wrecks. Tomas was already arguing with a Vane site manager about crane load ratings.

Lena stepped out of her truck last. She stood beside it, one hand still on the door, and looked down the slope at the beach — at the black sand, the tideline, the rocks offshore where something old and broken lay under the swell — and Kit, watching from the shadow of the supply van, saw her mouth move. Just once. Two words, maybe three, too far away to hear, spoken to the sea or to someone in it.

Then she shouldered her pack and walked down toward camp, spine straight, face shut, and became Captain Dunmore again so completely that Kit might have imagined the whole thing.

Might have. Except Kit had written it all down.

That night, in the canvas shelter shared with Bo's document crates, Kit opened the atlas by flashlight and added the day's entries to the margin in small careful print: *chapel hill. burned inn. Hollowtide sign. black rocks = teeth (confirmed). She said "coming home." She knows the ford, the roads, the names. She's counting something — landmarks? years?*

Kit stared at the list a long time. Then, underneath, wrote the question that had been forming all day like sediment settling:

*What happened to her father here?*

The flashlight flickered. Outside, the sea kept up its patient chewing, and somewhere down the beach, a single set of footsteps paced the tideline, back and forth, long after everyone else was asleep — and Kit lay listening to them, wide awake, worried, and certain that whatever answers waited at the bottom of Hollowtide Bay, Lena intended to go down and get them alone if she had to.

Tomorrow, Kit decided, the mapping would continue. But the territory had changed. This wasn't route-finding anymore.

This was digging — and Kit hadn't even gotten wet yet.
