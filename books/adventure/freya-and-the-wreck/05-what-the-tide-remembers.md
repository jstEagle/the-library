## 5. What the Tide Remembers

The cliff road came out of the hills the way a blade comes out of a sheath — slow, then all at once — and when it opened onto the coast, Lena Dunmore pulled the convoy to a stop without deciding to.

She heard Tomas's engine idling behind her, heard Kit ask something through the radio about fuel stops, and none of it reached her. Because there it was. The water. The same gray-green water she had spent five years not looking at, laid out below the cliffs like something patient that had never once stopped waiting for her.

She had told herself the lie so many times it had worn smooth: *it's just geography.* A coastline is a coastline. Rock is rock. There are ten thousand miles of shore on this earth and grief does not own any of them. She had said it to herself in hotel rooms in three countries, in the cab of trucks crossing borders, in the blue hour before dives when the sea looked like nothing at all — just work, just depth, just bottom time.

But the black rocks were still there. That was the thing no one warned you about geography: it kept everything. It forgot nothing. The basalt shelf ran down from the cliff base into the surf in the same broken staircase pattern, dark as iron slag, and where it met the water the undertow drew its long pale seam of foam, pulling back and back and back, the way it always had, the way it had on the morning that had split her life into before and after.

"Boss?" Kit's voice, tinny through the radio. "You want us to hold here?"

Lena keyed the mic because her hand was already on it. "Hold," she said. Her voice came out level. She'd had five years of practice making it come out level. "Five minutes."

She got out of the truck and stood at the guardrail, and the wind off the water hit her with a smell she hadn't consented to remember — salt and kelp and cold stone, and underneath it something faintly diesel-adjacent, the ghost of harbor towns, of her father's boat, of a life that had ended at nineteen in a single phone call.

*It's just geography.*

The undertow drew its seam of foam along the rocks, pulled back, pulled back, pulled back.

Below, far out past the break, she could see the swell moving over the site — a slight wrongness in the water's texture, a place where the surface stood up differently than around it, the way it did over shallow wreckage. Divers called it reading the water. Fishermen called it bad ground. Lena's father had called it *the house*, because he'd claimed you could see the roofline of a wreck in the swell if you knew how to look, and he'd taught her to look when she was eleven years old, standing exactly here, his hand heavy and warm on her shoulder.

*See it, Lena? Right there. She's lying on her side, bow to the north. You'll know her your whole life.*

He'd been talking about a different wreck then. A trawler lost in '61, a training site, a classroom. But the words had stayed, and now they applied to the other wreck, the one that mattered, the one that had taken him — and she could see it, God help her, she could see the house. The swell stood up over it gently, again and again, like breathing.

"Lena." Priya had come up beside her without sound, the way she did everything, and stood at the rail with her hands in her jacket pockets. She didn't look at Lena. She looked at the water, and Lena was grateful, because being looked at right now felt dangerous, like a hull with a hairline crack that any pressure would finish.

"That's the site?" Priya asked.

"You already know it is."

"I wanted to hear you say it." A pause. The wind filled it. "You okay?"

"I'm fine."

Priya nodded slowly, accepting the lie the way you accept a counterfeit bill from someone you love — taking it, pocketing it, saying nothing. "Tomas wants to push another hour before dark. Says we can make the beach access road tonight if we don't stop again."

"The advance team will have the camp started," Lena said. "Vane's people. They've been on-site nine days."

"Nine days." Priya turned her head at last, and her eyes were sharp and kind at once, which was a trick Lena had never learned. "And you've known this coast was the destination for how long?"

"Since the contract."

Another nod. Another counterfeit accepted. Then Priya pushed off the rail and walked back toward the trucks, calling to Kit in a voice pitched to carry, easy, ordinary, and Lena stayed at the guardrail alone for another full minute, watching the swell breathe over the house.

*You'll know her your whole life.*

Her father had been wrong about a lot of things at the end. She was beginning to suspect he hadn't been wrong about that.

* * *

She couldn't sleep.

That wasn't remarkable — she rarely slept well the first night anywhere, and less well near water — but this was different in kind, not degree. This was her body refusing the cot in the survey tent the way a compass needle refuses south, swinging, swinging, finding no rest in any direction. Around midnight she gave up on it, pulled on her boots and her jacket, and stepped out into the night.

The camp Vane's advance team had built sat on the shelf of flat ground above the beach: four canvas structures, a generator shed, a staging rack for dive bottles, a winch anchor point already bolted into the rock with hardware better than anything Tomas owned. Professional. Expensive. Quietly menacing in its competence, the way everything Vane touched was quietly menacing. Floodlights on telescoping stands stood folded like sleeping birds. Someone had strung guide rope along the path to the beach, and small amber markers glowed every few meters, leading down toward the water like a lit fuse.

Lena followed them.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. A good expedition lead walks her ground at night; she checks the tide line, the landing conditions, the sightlines. She has reasons. She has procedures. She has all the same smooth-worn lies she carried up the cliff road, and she took them down the path with her, one by one, and left every one of them at the top of the sand.

Because the moment her boots hit the beach, the truth arrived ahead of any thought, the way it does — not as memory but as weather. A pressure change. A weight.

The sand here was coarse and dark, mixed with crushed shell that glittered faintly under a half-moon. The surf came in long and low, unhurried, each wave spending itself with a sound like a long exhale. And the beach ran north and south exactly as it always had, and the black rocks stood at the north end exactly as they always had, and twenty years ago a girl had sat against those rocks with her knees drawn up while adults in orange suits walked the tideline with flashlights, calling a name into the dark.

They never found him. That was the part the ocean kept. Not the body — the certainty. For five years Lena had lived in the country of *probably*, and probably was a place with no graves and no closure, only weather.

She walked north along the tideline, hands in pockets, and the waves kept pace with her like something accompanying rather than following. Halfway to the rocks she stopped, because there was a piece of driftwood there — a rib of some old boat, bleached silver, half-buried — and her father had once pulled her out from behind a log exactly like it when a sneaker wave came through, laughing, soaked to the waist, saying *the sea doesn't hate you, Lena, but it isn't your friend either, respect is the whole relationship.*

She had respected it her whole life since. She had made a career of respecting it. Five years of salvage digs across three continents, and every job, every contract, every border crossing had been, if she was honest — and she was trying, tonight, alone, to be honest — a way of keeping moving. Miles, not years. Distance as anesthesia. As long as she was somewhere else, the wreck was somewhere else. As long as she never dove it, it stayed what the official report said it was: an accident. Storm damage. Tragic, unexplained, closed.

And now she was standing on its beach at midnight, hired by the man whose name was tangled through the whole story like wire through flesh, and the official version lay out there under the swell, breathing, waiting for her lights.

The grief ambushed her the way it always did — not gradually but all at once, a door blowing open. One moment she was walking and thinking procedurally about tide tables, and the next she was nineteen years old and on her knees in wet sand that wasn't metaphorical, wasn't geographical, was *this sand*, and her chest had closed like a fist around something too large to hold.

She didn't cry. She had mostly lost the ability, or trained it out of herself, or maybe the sea had taken that too, along with everything else. What happened instead was worse in a way: she just stood there and shook, silently, while the waves went on exhaling, and the moon moved, and the black rocks said nothing, having said nothing for five years, being rock.

*It's just geography.*

The lie floated up one more time and broke apart on the shore of the obvious. Geography remembered everything. That was the whole problem. That was why she'd run — not from the place but from the fact that the place would always be here, faithful as a dog, holding every detail: the exact pitch of the surf, the exact angle of the light at 4 p.m. in October, the exact spot where the police tape had fluttered between two stakes until the wind took it.

When her legs steadied, she noticed the light.

Down at the south end of the beach, where the advance team's gear cache stood under a tarp, a flashlight was moving. Low, careful, sweeping the sand near the cache. Then it clicked off. Then, after a long moment, it clicked on again, briefly, aimed not at the ground but at the water — out toward the site, held steady for three seconds, four, like someone taking a bearing. Or signaling. Or checking.

Lena went still among the rocks, and the old instincts came up through the grief like bedrock under loose soil. She watched the dark shape of a man move along the cache tarp, pause at the edge of the surf, look out at the swell over the house one more time, and walk back up toward the camp with the light hooded.

Not a member of her crew. She knew their walks the way she knew their voices. This man moved like the two Vane men who had met them at the harbor office — economical, watchful, owned.

She waited until the beach was empty again, then stood a long time looking out at the breathing water.

Nine days, the advance team had been here. Nine days of surveys, of soundings, of whatever else Vane's people did in the dark on a beach they had no sentimental history with. And tomorrow her crew would start diving a wreck that had killed her father, under contract to a man who forbade publication, guarded by strangers who photographed things at midnight — and she had signed it all with a steady hand, telling herself she had reasons she wouldn't say aloud even to herself.

But she had come for one reason. She had always come for one reason. The site log was missing — her father's final logbook, vanished between the wreck and the coroner's office, the one document that could tell her what he saw down there in his last hour. And the only witness left, the only record that couldn't be stolen or edited, was lying on the bottom in the house her father had taught her to read.

Tomorrow they would dive it.

Lena turned back up the beach, and behind her the undertow drew its long seam of foam along the black rocks, pulling back, pulling back, patient as an unpaid debt.
