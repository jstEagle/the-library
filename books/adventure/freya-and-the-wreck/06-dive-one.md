## 6. Dive One

The sea off Vane's Point was the color of hammered tin that morning, and Priya Chandra loved it the way she loved most things that could kill her.

She stood at the rail of the *Marrow*, their dive boat — a converted fishing trawler Tomas had bought for scrap price and rebuilt with the tenderness he usually reserved for engines and nothing else — and watched the swell move through the gap in the black rocks. The water there ran deep and cold even in dry season, fed by a channel that cut between the headland and the offshore reef like a wound that never closed. Down at the bottom of it, according to the sonar sweeps Bo had run for three nights straight, lay the wreck.

The *Corvane Star*. Lena's father's ship. The reason they were all standing on this particular stretch of cursed coastline instead of anywhere else on earth.

Priya had known Lena Dunmore for four years, since the bad season in the Java Sea when Lena's old crew dissolved mid-contract and Priya needed work badly enough to lie about her certification depth. She'd never once seen Lena look the way she looked this morning: not nervous, exactly. Lena didn't do nervous. It was more like watching someone stand very still in front of a door they'd nailed shut years ago and were now considering opening.

"You're up first," Lena said, coming to stand beside her. She held two mugs of the terrible coffee Kit made from grounds boiled twice, which was the only way to make the local supply drinkable. "You and me. Tomas runs the line, Bo logs, Kit stays topside."

"Kit's going to sulk."

"Kit sulks professionally. Let him." Lena handed over the mug but didn't drink from hers. She just wrapped both hands around it and stared down at the water like it owed her an answer. "Bottom's at forty-one meters. Cold below the thermocline, maybe eight degrees. Current through the gap picks up after ten hundred. We surface by then or we don't surface."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a cheerful person."

Priya snorted into her coffee. Below them, the swell slid through the rocks with a sound like slow breathing. She'd dived wrecks on five coasts — freighters, trawlers, one ferry that still gave her dreams — and she knew every diver's superstition worth knowing. You didn't dive a grave without acknowledging whose it was. You didn't say the dead man's name unless you meant respect. And you especially didn't dive a wreck that belonged to your captain's father without understanding that whatever came back up in your slate notes might break something none of you could fix.

"Lena." She kept her voice low. "Before we go down. What do we know about how she sank?"

Lena's jaw did the thing it did when she was choosing words carefully, which was rare enough that Priya paid attention. "Official finding was storm damage. She went down in a squall eleven years ago, hull breached amidships, took six crew with her. My father—" A pause. A recalculation. "My father survived. He was the only one who made it to shore. He died three years later."

Priya heard what wasn't said, the way she'd learned to hear engine trouble before it announced itself. There was a whole second sentence living inside that pause, wearing the first one as a coat.

"So: storm damage. Amidships breach. That's what I'm expecting to see?"

"That's what the record says."

*That's what the record says.* Not *that's what happened.* Priya filed it away next to all the other small discrepancies she'd been collecting since the harbor office — the secrecy clause, the deadline, the way Vane's advance team had already half-built the base camp before the crew ever arrived, as if they'd known exactly where everything would go. She'd been diving long enough to know that the ocean didn't hide things. People did. The ocean just kept what people gave it.

"Okay," she said, and drained her coffee. "Let's go meet her."

* * *

Forty-one meters is a country with no sun in it.

Priya felt the thermocline hit at thirty meters, a cold hand closing around her chest, and then the water went from green-gray to black-blue and the visibility opened up like a cathedral door. Her light cut a cone through the dark. Below her, rising out of the seafloor silt at a drunken list to port, lay the *Corvane Star*.

Her breath caught behind the regulator. She'd been doing this for twelve years and it still caught, every time — that moment when a shape on sonar became a thing, a real thing, with history in its planks and silence in its holds.

"She's intact," Priya said into her comm, and heard her own voice come out hushed even through the transmission distortion. "Lena, she's *intact.*"

"I see it." Lena hung in the water beside her, ten feet up and slightly seaward, her own light sweeping the hull. Even through the mask Priya could read her face: the professional assessment running underneath something rawer. "Rigging's collapsed but the mast step looks whole. Cargo hatch covers are still seated. Eleven years and she looks like she went down last season."

That was the first wrong note, though Priya didn't hear it as one yet. Wrecks in cold, deep, low-oxygen water preserved well — she'd seen wooden-hulled vessels from the 1800s looking better than this. But something about the *Star*'s condition nagged at the edge of her attention the way a faint rattle nags at an engineer, unplaceable, patient.

They descended along the starboard side, fins kicking up ghost-plumes of silt. Priya ran her gloved hand along the hull plating where she could, reading it the way Bo read documents — texture, seams, repairs. Steel hull, riveted, older construction than she'd expected from the manifest dates. Marine growth in layers: the soft pale stuff that came first, then the hard calcified colonies, then the darker encrustations. A normal succession. An honest timeline.

Then they rounded the bow section and reached the break.

The stern and the forward two-thirds of the ship had separated almost cleanly, lying twenty meters apart on the seabed like a snapped bone. And the break itself —

Priya stopped finning. She hovered, letting her buoyancy settle, and put her light full on the fracture face.

Metal doesn't tear evenly. That was the first thing they taught you when you started reading wreckage. Storm stress, grounding impact, wave action — all of it produced characteristic failures: jagged propagation lines, tearing along rivet seams, deformation where the structure bent before it broke. She'd catalogued dozens of failure patterns across her career. She knew what a hull looked like when the sea killed it.

This wasn't that.

The edges of the fracture were sharp. Outward-canted, in places — petals of steel peeled *away* from the interior, bent back toward open water. She swam closer, close enough that her glove nearly touched the metal, and traced the pattern with her light. The deformation radiated outward from a point low on the hull, below the waterline, in a roughly circular distribution. At the center of that distribution, the steel hadn't torn at all. It had been pushed. Thinned, stretched, blown through from the inside out.

"Priya?" Lena's voice in her ear. "You've gone static. Talk to me."

Priya looked at the fracture. Then she looked up at Lena's silhouette against the dim ceiling of the water, and something cold moved through her that had nothing to do with the thermocline.

"Taking measurements," she said. "Give me a minute."

She worked. That was the discipline — when the water tells you something frightening, you work, because working keeps your hands honest and your mind from running ahead of your data. She measured the fracture span. She photographed the deformation pattern from three angles, flash strobing in the dark like silent lightning. She checked the interior-facing side of the break where she could reach it, and found what she expected and dreaded in equal measure: no corresponding inward deformation. No sign of external impact. No rock scoring, no reef strike marks, nothing that any storm account or grounding report would predict.

A storm breaks ships by crushing them. This ship had been broken from within.

She'd seen it exactly once before, early in her career, on a salvage training wreck in the Gulf that the instructors used precisely because its damage was textbook: scuttling charges. Placed low, placed tight against the frame, detonated to flood the compartments fast and drop the ship before anyone could argue. The petal pattern. The outward cant. The clean separation along structural weak points rather than random tearing.

The *Corvane Star* hadn't been sunk by weather.

Priya hung there in the black water, forty-one meters above nothing, breathing recycled air through a machine, and felt the thrill of the morning curdle into something else entirely. Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't avoid: if the hull said murder, then every official account was wrong, and everyone who signed those accounts either hadn't looked or had been paid not to. And if someone had scuttled a ship with six crew aboard—

Six crew. Only one survivor. One survivor who spent the rest of his life being called the lucky one, until he wasn't alive anymore to be called anything.

And Lena Dunmore had brought them here saying *that's what the record says.*

"Priya." Lena's voice again, closer now. She'd drifted down alongside the fracture, her light joining Priya's, the two beams overlapping on the wounded steel. "Show me what you're seeing."

Priya hesitated. Three seconds, maybe four — an eternity underwater, where time is measured in air. She thought about saying *nothing conclusive, we'll review the photos topside.* She thought about the crew, about fragile trust and the secrets already stacked like crates in a hold too small for them. She thought about the way Lena had said *my father* and then swallowed the rest of the sentence.

But she also thought about the fact that they were currently hovering over evidence, and that Lena deserved to see it with her own eyes before Priya decided for her what it meant.

"Look at the fracture edges," Priya said. "Tell me what kind of force does that."

Lena studied the steel. Priya watched her read it — watched the assessment move through her the way it moved through Priya, the professional layer first, the pattern recognition, the comparison against every wreck manual ever written. Then she watched the second layer arrive, the personal one, and saw Lena go very still in the water. Divers learn to control their movement; stillness underwater is safety. But this was a different kind of stillness. This was the stillness of someone hearing a sound in another room.

"The deformation's radial," Lena said finally. Her voice was flat, controlled, each word set down like a stone. "Outward from a point source. Low on the frame."

"Yes."

"There's no external impact signature."

"No."

Lena was quiet for a long moment. Bubbles rose from her regulator in small, disciplined strings. When she spoke again, her voice had retreated somewhere Priya couldn't follow. "Photograph everything. Both faces of the break. Wide shots and detail. Mark the position on your slate."

"Priya to Lena translation: we'll discuss it topside?"

"We'll discuss it topside." A beat. "Or we won't."

They finished the survey in near silence. The rest of the wreck offered up its wonders — cargo hold doors sealed with their original lashing wire, the wheelhouse collapsed but recognizable, a ship's bell fused to its bracket with eleven years of mineral deposit. Any other day, any other wreck, Priya would have been drunk on it. A preservation state like this meant recovery potential beyond anything their crew had touched; it meant artifacts, records, maybe even the manifest strongbox if it had been stored aft. It meant the payday that would carry all of them through two lean seasons.

Instead she kept thinking about petals of steel bent toward the sky that wasn't there.

At thirty-five minutes bottom time, Lena signaled ascent. They rose through the thermocline together, the cold releasing them by degrees, the water warming and brightening around them like a slow forgiveness. At the fifteen-meter decompression stop they hung on the line, weightless, and Priya watched Lena stare down into the blue-dark where the ship waited, and understood that some part of Lena had already decided what the steel meant, and had probably decided it years ago, on land, in the dark, alone.

* * *

Tomas pulled them up the ladder himself, grinning under his beard. "Well? Is she rich?"

"She's beautiful," Priya said, peeling her hood. "Preservation's extraordinary. Cold water, deep anchorage, minimal oxygen turnover. We could recover clean for weeks."

Bo was already reaching for her camera, eyes bright behind salt-cracked glasses. "The photos? Can I start logging? If the hull's readable we can date every repair, build a maintenance history—"

"Photos need processing first," Priya said. "I want to review them properly before anyone starts building narratives."

It wasn't a lie. But it was a wall, and Bo — who noticed everything, who was paid in curiosity the way others were paid in wages — tilted her head a degree and let it go. For now.

Kit materialized with towels and the thermos, seventeen years old and vibrating with the specific agony of having been kept topside. "Was it creepy? It looked creepy from here. All the birds just circle the gap and never land on the water, did you notice that? Bo says it's because of upwelling but I think it's creepier if it isn't."

"It's upwelling, Kit," Priya said, and took the towel, and let the ordinary noise of the deck wash over her while she toweled her hair and tried to decide what she was holding and how heavy it was.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. She had a camera full of images that contradicted the official sinking account of the *Corvane Star*. She had a captain who had hired onto Marcus Vane's contract for reasons that visibly did not include money. She had a fracture pattern she could explain in a lecture hall in ninety seconds flat, and a growing certainty that explaining it aloud would detonate something in this crew that none of them were ready to survive.

Tomas trusted Lena with the simple, total faith of a man who'd been bailed out of ruin once and never forgot it. Bo trusted the work, and extended trust to Lena through it. Kit trusted Lena the way kids trust gravity. And Priya herself — Priya trusted Lena with her life, daily, professionally, in the water where trust is a technical requirement. But there was trust and there was trust, and the kind you extend to someone's competence is not the same kind you extend to their honesty, and Priya had just spent forty minutes at the bottom of the sea learning the difference.

She carried her camera below decks, to the cramped cabin she shared with the equipment lockers, and sat on her bunk with the image viewer glowing in her hands.

There it was. Frame after frame. The radial deformation. The outward-canted petals. The clean absence of any external trauma. In the photo lighting, stripped of the cathedral gloom, the fracture looked almost elegant — a flower of failure blooming from a single point, precise as engineering, because it *was* engineering. Someone had done math to make this break. Someone had chosen the placement, calculated the charge, timed the flood. Ships don't sink themselves. Storms don't plant explosives.

Eleven years ago, six men went into the water off this coast and did not come out. The record said weather. The steel said otherwise. And the only person alive who might have known the truth had been Lena's father, and he was three years dead, and Marcus Vane — who owned the company whose mark was on the charter, whose money was in their accounts, whose advance team had built their camp before they arrived — had been his rival.

Priya set the viewer down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

She thought about telling Lena right then. She got as far as the ladder. But through the cabin skylight she could hear the sounds of the evening camp assembling itself above — Tomas arguing cheerfully with the generator, Kit's laugh carrying across the water, Bo reading coordinates aloud in her careful archivist's voice — and she heard, underneath it, how thin the whole thing sounded. How much like a tent in wind. These people were held together by the contract and by Lena, in proportions nobody had ever audited, and Priya had just discovered that the ground under both might be made of something worse than anyone suspected.

Not tonight. Tonight the photos stayed hers. Tomorrow she'd process the rest of the survey, cross-check the deformation against reference patterns, be certain — certain the way you have to be certain before you tell a woman that her father's death was written in the hull of the ship that killed him.

She was still sitting in the dark with the viewer dark beside her when boots came down the companionway, unhurried, deliberate, and stopped outside her door. Two knocks. Lena's knock — she'd know it anywhere, that patient double-tap that never asked permission so much as announced intent.

Priya slid the viewer under her blanket and opened the door.

Lena stood in the passage with a lantern in one hand and her father's old compass — the one she wore on a cord and never let anyone touch — hanging loose from the other. Her face in the lantern light was calm the way deep water is calm, and she looked at Priya for a long moment before speaking, as if measuring the depth of her.

"You saw it down there," Lena said quietly. "On the hull. I watched your face change." She glanced down the empty passage, then back. "So before you decide what to do with it — I think it's time you knew why I really signed Vane's contract."
