## 7. Bo's Ledger

The mess tent smelled of diesel and instant coffee, and Bo loved it the way other people loved cathedrals.

He had claimed the folding table nearest the generator because the vibration kept his tea warm without a stove, and because from there he could see everyone come and go — Priya dripping salt water onto the planks after each dive, Tomas arguing with the compressor like it owed him money, Kit appearing and vanishing with the restless energy of someone who hadn't yet learned that waiting was most of the job. And Lena. Lena moving through camp like a woman walking a tightrope she'd strung herself, careful not to look down.

Bo had spent thirty-one years among paper. University archives in two countries, then a decade cataloging shipwreck recoveries for insurers who wanted numbers and museums that wanted stories. He had learned to read people the way he read manifests: by what was listed, by what was omitted, and by the weight of the ink where someone had pressed too hard.

Lena's ink was pressing very hard these days.

But that was later. That morning, there was only the work.

* * *

The recovery crates had come up from Dive Three the evening before, twelve of them, each one logged by Priya at depth and countersigned on the surface by Tomas. Bo's ritual never changed. He cleared the table, laid down clean canvas, set out his tools in their fixed order — soft brushes, polyethylene sleeves, the acid-free tags he still wrote by hand because his handwriting was the only thing about him that had improved with age. Then he opened the first crate and let the wreck speak.

It spoke in cargo this time. The *Meridian Star* had been a coastal freighter, forty years dead, and her hold was giving up its secrets in the patient way of drowned things: ceramic jars crusted into anonymity, ingots of tin fused into a single gray mass, bales of something organic long since eaten to lace. Ordinary salvage. The kind of material that filled museum basements and made funders nod politely.

Bo cataloged for six hours. He was good at disappearing into it — the world narrowing to object, tag, entry, photograph. His hands knew the motions. His mind, freed from driving them, wandered where it pleased.

Which is why it took him until mid-afternoon to notice the discrepancy.

He had pulled the original manifest from his document case three days ago — a certified copy from the regional maritime registry, obtained through channels so tedious that Kit had begged him never to describe them again. Standard procedure. You couldn't interpret a wreck without knowing what she carried. He'd entered the manifest summary into his working ledger on the first night, before they'd even seen the site, and had not looked at it since.

Now, cross-referencing crate nine against his own earlier entries, he stopped.

Crate nine held tin. Forty-one ingots, give or take the ones the sea had welded together, recovered from hold section two. The manifest — the official, registered, sworn manifest — listed tin in hold section two as well. Four hundred ingots of it.

They had recovered forty-one.

Bo sat back. He did this when thinking, a habit his students had once found comical, chair creaking, fingers laced over his stomach like a man settling in for a long story. Outside, the wind worried the tent fabric. Somewhere near the water, Tomas was losing an argument with a fuel pump.

Forty-one out of four hundred could mean many things. It could mean the rest lay deeper in the silt, unrecovered. It could mean the aft section had broken away — though Priya's survey maps showed no such break. It could mean simple loss, spillage, the ordinary arithmetic of a ship going down.

Or it could mean the tin had never been aboard when the ship sank.

Bo reached for the manifest copy and read it properly this time, line by line, the way he'd taught three generations of graduate students to read and then watched them fail to do under deadline pressure. And the pattern emerged the way patterns always did for him — slowly, then all at once, like a photograph developing.

Hold one: cotton, listed lost. Hold one, per Priya's survey: empty. Not collapsed, not silted — empty, swept, the dunnage removed.

Hold two: tin, four hundred ingots. Recovered: forty-one.

Hold three: copper sheeting, listed lost. Survey showed the hold doors — the *interior* hold doors — open.

A ship sinks, and her holds drown with her. That was the whole tragedy of wrecks; the sea took everything at once, indiscriminately. But these holds had been emptied *before* the water came in. Cargo listed as lost at sea had been offloaded — carefully, selectively, through interior passages that required time and knowledge and calm conditions.

Someone had stripped the *Meridian Star* and then sunk her around the theft.

Bo set down the manifest with hands that were not quite steady. Thirty-one years among paper, and paper had never frightened him before. Paper was honest. It was people who lied, and only sometimes with documents.

He thought about the insurance claim — he'd have to pull it next, if the registry would release it. Someone had collected on that cargo. Someone had stood at a claims desk, or sent a lawyer, and sworn that four hundred ingots of tin and a hold full of copper rested at the bottom of the sea, and been paid for the grief of it.

And someone else — the thought arrived cold, unwelcome — someone else had died proving otherwise.

Bo looked across the camp toward the dive boat, where Lena stood at the rail talking to Priya, her back to him, her posture carrying that particular rigidity he'd begun to catalogue alongside the artifacts. Item: captain, one. Condition: bearing weight unknown. Provenance: refused to say.

He liked Lena Dunmore more than he had liked most people in his long and largely solitary life. He trusted her with his work, which was to say with himself. Which was exactly why the question forming in his chest hurt in a way he hadn't anticipated.

Not *what happened to the cargo.* He could answer that now.

*Who knew?*

* * *

He waited until evening, because some conversations should not be had over artifact tables where Kit might wander in mid-sentence. He waited through dinner, through Tomas's running commentary on the fuel pump's moral failures, through Kit's retelling of a race against the mail truck that had clearly grown two kilometers in the telling. He waited until the others drifted toward their tents and the camp settled into the low electric hum of the generator and the deeper hum of the sea beyond the rocks.

Then he found Lena at the survey table, alone, lamplight pooling around the charts she wasn't really reading.

"Captain." He used the title deliberately, formally, the way you knock on a door before entering a room. "Have you ten minutes?"

She looked up, and something flickered across her face — quick, guarded, gone. "For you? Always."

He sat across from her. He had rehearsed nothing, which was unlike him, but he had learned long ago that rehearsals made confrontations sound like accusations, and he did not want this to be an accusation. He wanted it to be a hand extended in the dark.

"I've been working the manifest against the recovery inventory," he said. "Routine. Cross-referencing. The kind of thing I do instead of sleeping, as you know."

"I know." A small smile. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes, but then, little had lately.

"Hold one came up empty. Not damaged — emptied. Dunnage gone, doors open from the inside. Hold three the same. Hold two retained perhaps a tenth of its listed tin, scattered in a way that suggests it was left behind rather than lost." He kept his voice level, gentle, the voice he'd used with donors and grieving widows donating collections. "Lena. The *Meridian Star* was offloaded before she went down. Deliberately. By people who knew the ship, and had time, and had calm water."

The lamp hissed softly between them. Out past the rocks, the undertow made its low constant sound, the sound this coast had been making since before any of them were born.

Lena didn't move. That was the thing Bo noticed most — not shock, not confusion, none of the natural weather of surprise. Just stillness, total and practiced, like a diver hanging neutral in the water column, expending nothing, revealing nothing.

"The holds flooded," she said. "Storm damage. Things shift."

"Some things shift," Bo agreed. "Dunnage doesn't walk itself ashore. Interior hold doors don't unlatch themselves." He paused. "I'm not asking you to explain the wreck. I'm telling you what the wreck says. And I find myself wondering—" he chose the words the way he chose archival boxes, for protection as much as fit, "—whether you already knew what it would say. Before we ever came here."

For a moment he thought she would tell him. Something moved behind her eyes — the same flicker as before, but longer this time, a door opening a crack onto a lit room. He leaned forward slightly, not pushing, just present, the way you sit with a manuscript you hope will be opened to you.

Then the door closed.

"It's a forty-year-old wreck, Bo," she said, and her voice was warm, and easy, and wrong. "Whatever game the owners were playing with their cargo books, they're dead or rich or both, and none of it changes our scope of work. Vane wants the site documented and the artifacts recovered. That's what we're doing. That's all we're doing."

"That is what we're doing," Bo said quietly. "It may not be all that's happening."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning nothing. Meaning an old man reads too much into empty holds." He rose, gathered his coat, and paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Lena. Whatever you're carrying — and you are carrying something, you have been since the wire arrived, you carry it like ballast — you should know that this crew does not require you to be unburdened. Only unbowed. There's a difference, and we can tell."

She smiled at him then, and it was the worst smile he'd ever received from her, because it was kind. "Get some sleep, Bo. Big day tomorrow. Tide turns early."

"Yes," he said. "It usually does."

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in his tent with the manifest spread on his cot and his reading glasses low on his nose, and he did what he had always done with unbearable information: he organized it.

A new page in the ledger. *Discrepancies, cargo versus recovery.* Hold one, hold two, hold three. The offloading pattern. The implication, written in his smallest, most controlled hand: *Insurance fraud predicated on deliberate sinking. Beneficiaries unknown. Investigation warranted.*

And beneath that, after a long pause, a second heading. *What the captain knows.*

He wrote nothing under it. He drew a single line, the way archivists marked a lacuna — a gap in the record, a place where text had been lost or destroyed or never written at all. Lacunae, he had told his students, are not silence. They are evidence of absence. They tell you that something *was* there.

He closed the ledger and listened to the sea.

He thought about the day, five years ago now, when Lena had hired him — a reference call from a colleague, a phone interview conducted from what sounded like a moving vehicle, her voice flat with exhaustion and absolutely certain. *I need someone who cares about the record more than the reward,* she'd said. *Can you be that?* He had said yes, and meant it, and had been that, through three continents and a hundred digs, and he had never once regretted it.

He also understood, now, that he had been hired partly for this exact moment. That somewhere inside her, Lena Dunmore had known that a man who cared about the record would eventually find whatever she was hiding — and had hired him anyway. Perhaps because of it. Perhaps because part of her wanted to be found out by someone safe.

That was the ache of it, Bo thought, turning off his lamp. Not the fraud, not the sunken tin, not even the dead men. Those were problems, and problems yielded to method. The ache was simpler and older: loving someone who lies kindly. Who lies to protect you, or to protect the work, or to protect some private wound she believes is hers alone to carry. Who looks at you with warmth while closing the door.

Paper never did that. Paper just waited, patient as the sea, for someone willing to read it.

Outside, footsteps crossed the camp — light ones, quick ones, stopping briefly outside his tent as if their owner considered speaking. Bo held his breath. The footsteps moved on, toward the water, and did not return for a long time.

In the morning, he decided, he would pull the insurance claim records. In the morning, he would ask Priya, casually, what the hull survey showed about the breach point. In the morning, he would keep being the man Lena had hired him to be, even if it cost him the easy warmth between them.

Because the manifest had told him what the wreck said.

And tonight, watching Lena's silhouette stand alone at the tideline under a sky full of indifferent stars, Bo began to suspect that the wreck was not the only thing on this coast keeping secrets — and that the deepest one of all was buried not in the hold of the *Meridian Star*, but in the woman who had brought them here to open it.

The tide turned early, just as she said it would.

By dawn, everything on this beach would be different.
