## 8. Vane's Shadow

Tomas Reyes had spent twenty years learning to read machines, and in that time he'd learned something else: you could tell everything about a man by how he treated equipment. Men who kicked generators were liars. Men who wiped down tools at the end of a shift were the ones who'd stay when things went wrong. And men who watched other people's gear a little too closely — those were the ones you counted twice.

The man from Vane's advance team was a watcher. He'd introduced himself on the first morning as Corben, all handshake and easy grin, said he was liaison between the crew and Vane's head office, here to "smooth logistics." Smooth logistics. In Tomas's experience, liaisons smoothed nothing. They recorded.

Corben wore his usefulness like a costume — always carrying something, always offering something. A spare fuel filter when the pump sputtered. Cold drinks hauled up from the harbor town in his own truck. He learned everyone's name by the second day, remembered Kit liked ginger beer, complimented Priya's dive tables, asked Bo intelligent questions about cataloging systems. By the third evening he'd been absorbed into the camp's rhythm so completely that most of them had stopped noticing him.

Tomas noticed. Noticing was his job. The compressor, the winch hydraulics, the dive boat's temperamental bilge pump — these announced their failures early, if you listened. People did too, if you watched. And Corben watched wrong. A man genuinely interested in operations looked at the work. Corben looked at the paperwork.

* * *

It happened on the fourth night, or rather, Tomas made it happen on the fourth night, because he'd suspected it would happen eventually and preferred to choose the hour himself.

He left the equipment log open on the folding table outside the supply tent — deliberately, for the first time in his career. Twenty years of habit said lock it away; tonight he broke the habit and set a trap instead. The log sat beside the lantern, its pages listing every serial number that mattered: the winch motor they'd rebuilt in two provinces back, the compressor with the patched regulator, the dive boat's engine block stamped with a salvage yard code from a country none of them would admit to working in. Equipment with histories. Equipment whose paper trails could hang a crew if the wrong eyes read them closely enough.

Then he walked to the generator shed, opened the panel, and gave himself a reason to be there — a fuse to inspect that didn't need inspecting. From inside the shed, through the gap where the vent slat had rusted out years ago, he could see the table. Not well. Well enough.

He waited forty minutes. The camp settled around him: Priya rinsing her regs in the freshwater barrel, Bo's lamp burning late in the archive tent, Kit's laughter carrying from the fire pit where the kid was teaching himself card tricks from a book with a cracked spine. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who trusted the dark.

Corben came at 11:40, moving quiet along the tent line like a man who'd memorized which boards creaked. He didn't go to the fire pit. He didn't glance at the archive tent. He went straight to the table, and he took out his phone, and he photographed the log — page after page, tilting it toward the lantern light, thumbing through with the practiced efficiency of someone who'd done this before, more than once, probably on more crews than theirs.

Tomas let him finish. Let him get every page. It mattered that Corben believed the theft was clean.

Then Tomas stepped out of the shed.

"Careful with that," he said pleasantly. "Pages are brittle."

Corben froze with the phone still raised, and to his credit recovered fast — a laugh, a shrug, the phone disappearing into his jacket. "Couldn't sleep. Saw the log sitting out. Figured I'd flip through, see what I could learn about your setup. Impressive gear, honestly. Some of it's older than me."

"Some of it's older than both of us," Tomas agreed. He crossed to the table, closed the log, and held out his hand. "Phone."

The grin flickered. "Come again?"

"You photographed our equipment records without asking. On a restricted site. Under a contract that says absolute secrecy runs both directions." Tomas kept his voice level, almost friendly, the way he talked to a machine right before he shut it down. "So either you hand me the phone and delete them while I watch, or we wake up Lena Dunmore and let her decide what Vane's head office hears about it. Your call. But make it quick, because my patience has a shorter service life than that compressor."

For a long moment Corben weighed him. Tomas watched the calculation move behind his eyes — the odds, the angles, whether the big silent engineer was bluffing. Then Corben exhaled through his nose, produced the phone, and deleted the photos one by one, slow, theatrical, holding the screen up like a man surrendering contraband at a checkpoint.

"All of them," Tomas said.

"That's all of them."

"There's a cloud backup. Delete that too."

Something shifted in Corben's face — not fear, exactly. Recalculation. He thumbed through a few more screens, tapped, done. "Happy now?"

"Delighted." Tomas took the phone, scrolled to confirm, handed it back. Then he smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile. "One more thing. You'll want to check your camera before you use it again. These coastal nights are hell on electronics. Condensation gets everywhere."

Corben frowned. "What's that supposed to—"

"Goodnight," Tomas said, and picked up the log, and walked away.

What Corben wouldn't discover until tomorrow, maybe the day after, was that the phone had already begun to die. While the man had slept, Tomas had slipped into the liaison's tent — easy, because Corben's tent zipper was cheap and loud and Tomas had oiled it silent two days ago, filing the detail away the way he filed everything — and swapped the charging cable for one of his own making. Beautiful little piece of work: a hairline fault in the ground line, invisible to any inspection, delivering just enough surge over repeated charges to cook a battery cell slowly, gently, over a week. No dramatic failure. No suspicion. Just a phone that grew unreliable, lost photos, corrupted files, until Vane's office wrote it off as hardware and sent a replacement — which Tomas would also kill, in time, if it came to that.

You couldn't stop a man from spying. But you could make sure everything he stole rotted in his hands.

* * *

He found Lena at the waterline past midnight.

She stood at the edge of the surf in her boots, trousers rolled, staring out at the black water where the site buoys blinked their slow red rhythm. She did this most nights, though she probably thought nobody knew. Tomas had watched her from the workshop window often enough: ten minutes, sometimes fifteen, motionless, like a woman reading a letter written in tide.

He stopped a few paces off and let her hear his boots on the wet sand. She didn't turn around.

"Tell me you're not about to say something comforting," she said.

"I'm about to say something useful."

"Even better."

He came up beside her. Up close he could see the tiredness she hid from the others — the new lines around her eyes that hadn't been there three weeks ago, before the convoy, before the coast, before whatever lived under this water started pulling at her. Tomas had worked for her five years and had never once asked about the wreck. Some questions you didn't ask a captain. You just made sure her ship held together.

"Your friend Corben," he said. "He photographed the equipment log tonight. Every page. Serial numbers, service histories, the lot."

She turned then. In the buoy-light her face went very still — not surprised, he realized, but confirming something she'd already feared. "Did you take the phone?"

"Took the photos. Took the phone apart, metaphorically speaking. He'll be shooting blanks for a week before he figures it out." Tomas shrugged. "But that's not the point. The point is what he was after. You don't photograph serial numbers to steal equipment. You photograph them to build a file. Ownership records, customs histories, import codes — half our gear has paperwork that wouldn't survive a lawyer's squint. Whoever holds that file holds us."

Lena looked back out at the water. When she spoke, her voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant she was choosing her words like footing on scree. "Vane's contract covers the dig. Not the crew."

"No," Tomas agreed. "It doesn't. That's rather the point."

"He's done this before," she said quietly. It wasn't a question either. "You know something."

"I know talk. Harbor talk, yard talk, fifteen years of it." He turned to face her fully. "Crews that worked for Vane and came home different. Came home owing. A welder in Port Sarno who signed a 'logistics extension' and found his house mortgaged against it. A survey outfit up north that took Vane money once and never worked an independent job again — every permit they applied for afterward got challenged, every client got a quiet letter. Vane doesn't hire crews, Lena." He let it land. "He owns them. The money's the hook. The paperwork's the line. And men like Corben are the reel."

The surf filled the silence, patient, indifferent. Lena's jaw worked.

"Why didn't you say something sooner?"

"Because talk is talk, and I don't scare captains with rumors." He paused. "Tonight stopped being rumor."

She nodded slowly, and he watched her do the thing she did — the arithmetic behind her eyes, weighing the crew's payday against the shape of the trap, arriving at some number she didn't share. Five years, and he still couldn't read that final figure. But he saw the cost of it on her face.

"The others don't know yet," she said.

"Not yet."

"Keep it that way. For now." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Priya's mid-cycle on the deep surveys, Bo's buried in the manifest cross-references, Kit finally sleeps through the night. If I put Vane paranoia into the camp now, it spreads. Fear's worse than salt water — it gets into everything."

"It already will," Tomas said. "They're not blind. They've seen Corben watching. What they haven't seen is you deciding what to do about it. That's the part that spreads."

Lena was quiet a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled — a thin, tired thing, but real. "Twenty years turning wrenches and you give leadership advice."

"Twenty years turning wrenches teaches you that most failures are predictable. Machines and crews both." He shouldered past the moment gently. "One condition. From tonight, the real logs live with me. Locked box, welded seams, sleeps under my cot. The table copy stays out where Corben can find it — and I'll keep it full of numbers that are true but useless. Right equipment, wrong histories. Let him build his file out of fiction."

"And when Vane's office checks those numbers against reality?"

"Then they learn their liaison's intel is garbage, and Corben stops being trusted. Either way, we win." He glanced at her sideways. "Unless you'd rather confront Vane directly. Tell him we know his man's a spy. Watch him apologize beautifully and send someone subtler."

"No," Lena said, too fast, then slower: "No. We play it quiet. If Vane thinks his shadow's working, he'll relax. Relaxed men make mistakes." She turned from the water at last, and for a second the buoy-light caught something raw underneath the captain's calm. "Thank you, Tomas."

"Don't thank me. Fix your captain problem instead."

"What captain problem?"

"The one where you carry all of this alone and think nobody notices." He said it without softness, because softness would have let her deflect. "Whatever you're not telling us — and you're not telling us plenty, Lena, don't bother with the face — it's getting heavier. I can see it in how you stand at this water every night. Crews forgive a captain a lot of things. The one thing they don't forgive is drowning alone when there's a whole boat of hands reaching for you."

She didn't answer. She looked at him for a long time, and he thought — hoped — something might crack open. Then a gull screamed somewhere down the beach and the moment folded itself away, and she was the captain again, sealed and steady.

"Get some sleep, Tomas," she said. "Big lift tomorrow."

"Aye, Captain." He turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth — whatever's down there, whatever you're carrying — the vote stands. All of us. Same as the first night. You don't have to ask twice."

He walked back up the sand and didn't look to see whether she stayed at the waterline. He knew she would. Some reckonings couldn't be rushed; you could only make sure the person having them knew they weren't having it alone.

* * *

Morning brought the lift — the big one, a section of hull plating coming up on the crane barge, and the whole crew turned out for it. Tomas ran the winch, Priya spotter below, Kit on the tag lines, Bo logging, and Corben hovering at the rail with his coffee and his helpfulness and his dying phone in his pocket.

Halfway up, the plating swung wide in a gust and kissed the barge rail harder than anyone liked. Kit stumbled on the tag line, caught it, held — the kid was getting stronger, filling out around the shoulders, losing the last of the city softness. Tomas watched all of it from the winch housing with his hands moving on their own and his mind elsewhere, running the night's conversation on a loop.

Vane doesn't hire crews. He owns them.

And Lena had gone still when he said it. Not shocked-still. Recognizing-still. Which meant she already knew something about Marcus Vane that Tomas didn't, something older than this contract, something that lived in the same place as the nightmares and the midnight water-staring and the way she flinched whenever anyone said the wreck's old name.

By noon the plating was secured and lashed, and the crew gathered for the midday meal, and for ten minutes everything was ordinary — rice and canned fish and Kit doing the card trick badly for the fourth time. Corben drifted past the fire pit, offered around cold drinks from his cooler, lingered near Bo longer than necessary, asking cheerful questions about the cataloging system, the duplicate copies, the backup procedures.

Bo answered politely. Too politely. And when Corben moved on, Bo caught Tomas's eye across the fire, and gave the smallest shake of the head — *I see him too* — and Tomas understood that the camp was waking up around its secret, one pair of eyes at a time.

That was when Priya surfaced from the equipment tent with her slate in hand, moving fast, face pale beneath the sunburn, scanning the beach until she found Lena at the survey table. Tomas watched her cross the sand at a pace that had nothing casual in it, watched her lean close, watch Lena's expression change as she read whatever was on that slate.

Watched Lena go absolutely still — the same stillness as last night at the waterline, but deeper now, colder, the stillness of a woman looking at something she had hoped never to see.

Priya said something low and urgent. Lena looked up, out toward the water, toward the wreck, and Tomas saw her hand close slowly around the edge of the survey table until her knuckles went white.

Whatever the sea had shown Priya today, it hadn't been storm damage.
