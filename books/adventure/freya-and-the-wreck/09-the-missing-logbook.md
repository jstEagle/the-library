## 9. The Missing Logbook

The generator coughed twice and settled into its low diesel growl, and the camp went quiet the way it always did after dark — not silent, but hushed, alive with small sounds. Rope creaking against the drying rack. The hiss of Priya's regulator hanging from the rinse tank. Somewhere down the beach, Kit's radio murmuring a station that came and went with the wind.

Lena sat outside her tent with a mug of coffee she'd let go cold an hour ago, watching the water.

It was different at night. That was the thing nobody warned you about — that a sea could be a hundred seas depending on the hour. By day this stretch was work: gray-green, choppy, marked by buoys and dive lines and the flat professional language of coordinates. But after midnight it turned black and enormous, breathing against the rocks in long slow swells, and it sounded like it had something to say if you were fool enough to listen.

She'd been listening for five years. It had never once said anything kind.

Boots on gravel behind her. She knew the stride before she turned — Priya walked like she swam, economical, no wasted motion, weight forward. She dropped onto the sand beside Lena's chair without being asked, a towel around her neck and her hair still damp from the freshwater shower she hoarded like a dragon's treasure, one careful bucket at a time.

"You're doing the thing," Priya said.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-water thing. You've done it every night since we anchored." She pulled the cold mug out of Lena's hands, tasted it, grimaced, and set it in the sand. "Tomas thinks it's the site. Bo thinks it's Vane. Kit thinks you're part mermaid and it's a homecoming."

"And what do you think?"

Priya looked out at the black water for a while. The moon was three days off full and laid a broken silver road across the swell, all the way to the horizon, like something inviting them to walk out on it.

"I think," she said, "that I've dived with you for four years, and you've never once asked me to run a survey grid this tight on a wreck this shallow. There's no reason for it. A hundred-foot site in open water, good visibility, no overhead hazards worth the name. We could map this in six days with our eyes closed. You've got us on day eleven." She turned her head. "You're not excavating, Lena. You're searching. And I don't know what for yet."

The swell hit the black rocks below the bluff, and even from here Lena felt it through the soles of her boots — that deep percussive thud, patient as a heartbeat.

"Your hull damage," she said.

Priya went very still. Of course she did. Lena had watched her surface from Dive One with that look on her face, the one divers got when they found something beautiful and wrong in the same breath, and she'd known then that Priya had seen it too and was carrying it around the way people carried shrapnel — quietly, close to the bone, waiting to see if the body would reject it or absorb it.

"You noticed that," Priya said carefully.

"I read your notes. You log everything twice, once for the record and once honest. The second set's in your head, but your face does it for you." Lena almost smiled. "The plating on the starboard bow. Torn inward."

"Torn inward," Priya agreed softly. "From the inside. That's not storm damage. That's not grounding. That's—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "That's a hole punched out from within, which means either the cargo shifted in a way cargo doesn't shift, or something detonated, or someone opened her up deliberately. And before you say it — yes, I checked the historical record Bo pulled. Every account says groundswell, rogue wave, driven onto the shoal. Clean tragedy. Neat as a newspaper obituary."

"Neat as an obituary," Lena repeated. "Yeah. That's exactly how they wrote it."

The word *they* hung between them. Priya heard it. Lena watched her hear it — saw the diver's instinct for reading pressure changes kick in, the way she adjusted without flinching.

"Lena." Priya's voice was gentle now, and that gentleness was worse than any interrogation. "Whose boat was it?"

Lena had rehearsed this conversation maybe four hundred times over eleven nights, lying in her tent listening to the same swell, running versions of it the way Tomas ran load calculations. In most of them she deflected. In some she lied outright. In one or two of the worst ones she said nothing at all and let the secret calcify until it became part of the ship's keel, invisible and structural, and they all sailed on it unknowing.

She was so tired of rehearsing.

"My father's," she said.

The word came out smaller than she intended. She cleared her throat and started again.

* * *

"The *Meridian Star*. Ninety feet of converted trawler, salvage-rated, my father's whole world and eventually ours too. He ran her out of this coast for nineteen years. My mother did the books. I grew up on her decks — learned to swim off the stern ladder, learned to read tide tables before I learned cursive." Lena wrapped her hands around her knees, making herself small in the camp chair, making herself into the girl she'd been instead of the woman sitting in it. "He wasn't a treasure hunter. He hated that word. He called himself a recovery man — things lost that deserved finding, returned to whoever had the right to grieve them properly. He had rules. Never dive past your training. Never trust a fair-weather forecast. And never, ever take a contract where the client cares more about what's aboard than who lost it."

"And he broke one," Priya said quietly.

"He broke the last one." Lena stared at the silver road on the water. "Five years ago. A consortium hired him through a broker — deep pockets, vague paperwork, a manifest for a wreck two hundred miles south of here. He took it because we were drowning. The *Meridian Star* needed a new engine block, and there were bills, and my mother was sick that year, though he didn't tell me how sick until later. He took the job because it was money, and money was oxygen." Her laugh came out cracked. "I've taken jobs for the same reason since. So I can't even judge him for it. That's the joke of it, Priya. I've spent five years being angry at him for doing exactly what I do."

The swell boomed against the rocks. Down the beach, Kit's radio drifted through a burst of static and found its signal again.

"What happened?" Priya asked. Not *what do you think happened*. What happened. As if Lena's version of events were already admissible evidence.

"That's the thing. Nobody knows. The official account is the one Bo found — storm, shoal, lost with all hands. Four men. My father, two deckhands, and the broker's representative, a man named Corliss who'd ridden along to supervise the recovery." Lena's voice flattened out, going clinical, because the alternative was shaking. "The wreck was found three weeks later on the shoal, torn open, half-buried. The maritime board ruled it weather. Case closed. Insurance paid out to the families. Everyone was very sorry."

"But."

"But the weather that night was rated moderate by two separate stations. My father had forty years of sea sense and would have turned back for anything less than a monster. And when they recovered the bodies — when they recovered *him* — his personal effects bag was missing. Charts, logbook, the sealed dive journal he kept on every contract since before I was born. Everything paper. Everything that would have said what he was doing and why and for whom." She finally turned to look at Priya directly. "His logbook vanished, Priya. Out of a dead man's effects, before the body ever reached shore. Somebody removed it. And somebody wanted it gone badly enough to commit a crime over a dead salvager's handwriting."

Priya had gone pale in the firelight. "You think the sinking wasn't weather."

"I think the hull was opened from the inside." Lena let that sit. "I think whatever he found on that last job — or whatever he refused to do — got four men killed. And I think the person who arranged it has spent five years being very comfortable and very rich, and last month he wrote me a check big enough to buy my silence without either of us having to say the words out loud."

"Vane."

"Marcus Vane." Saying the name aloud felt like pulling a splinter that had been in too deep to reach. "The broker who fronted my father's final contract. The company on the letterhead dissolved itself eighteen months later, clean as a whistle. And now he's hired my crew — my family — to excavate this exact wreck, under secrecy clauses, on a deadline, with a publication ban, paying triple market rate for a site that doesn't need triple anything." She shook her head slowly. "He wants something specific off this wreck, Priya. Something that's still down there after five years, something that proves or buries what really happened, and he needs it found before the dry season closes the legal window. He hired me because I'm the best, and because he thinks grief makes me predictable."

"Does it?"

"It made me say yes." Lena's mouth twisted. "That's what it made me do. Everything else is still up for grabs."

* * *

For a long moment Priya said nothing. She picked up the abandoned mug, considered it, and poured the cold coffee out into the sand — a small ritual execution, making room, Lena thought, for whatever came next.

Then: "Why tell me? Why tonight?"

Because the crew deserves better than a captain with a hidden hold. Because Bo already suspects and loves me anyway, which is worse. Because Tomas would burn the contract tonight and we'd all be broke and I'm not sure that's right either. Because Kit is seventeen and trusts me with their whole entire heart and I can't carry that and this too.

What she said was: "Because you saw the hull damage and kept it to yourself. Not to protect yourself. To protect us — you thought the truth might crack the crew before we understood it. That was the wrong call, made for the right reason, and I recognized it, because I've been making it for eleven days." She exhaled. "I figured the least I owed you was the reason I've been making it."

Priya nodded slowly. Then she stood, walked to the edge of the firelight, and stood looking at the water with her arms crossed — thinking, Lena knew, in the way she thought underwater, total and unhurried. When she came back she crouched in front of Lena's chair so they were eye to eye, close enough that Lena could see the salt dried in the fine lines at the corners of her eyes.

"Here's what happens now," Priya said. "One. I keep this. All of it. Not because you asked — because I choose to, and you should know the difference, because the difference matters when the next hard choice comes. Two. From this night forward, no more secrets between you and me. Not protective ones, not kind ones, not strategic ones. If you find something on this wreck that scares you, I hear it the same day. If Vane offers you something private, I hear it the same hour. Three—" and here her voice roughened, just slightly, the first fracture in all that composure, "—when we find your father's logbook, or whatever's left of what he knew, I want to be on that dive. Not assigned. Asked."

Lena's throat closed. She managed, "Asked. Yeah. Okay."

"And Lena." Priya held her gaze. "If it turns out Vane killed him — if the proof comes up off that bottom in our hands — I need you to promise me we decide together what to do with it. Not you alone in a tent at night deciding what your family can bear. Together. That's the price of my silence. That's the whole truth requirement, starting right now, applying retroactively."

The swell boomed. The silver road on the water trembled and reassembled itself.

"Together," Lena said. And found, to her surprise, that saying it lifted something — not the grief, nothing would ever lift that, but the weight of carrying it alone, which turned out to be a different and heavier thing entirely. Five years of solo hauling, and it had never once occurred to her that the load could be shared without being diminished.

Priya squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way she did — everything important communicated through grip — and stood, and gathered her towel, and paused at the flap of her tent.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your father raised a hell of a recovery man."

She was gone inside before Lena could answer, which was probably the point.

* * *

Lena stayed up another hour. She should have slept; dawn dive briefing waited at six, and Tomas would give her the look if she came to it hollow-eyed. But the confession had opened something, and things that opened tended to keep opening whether you scheduled them or not.

She dug out the old photograph she kept in the false bottom of her equipment case — the only object she'd saved from the house, the only one that mattered. Her father on the deck of the *Meridian Star*, squinting into the sun, one arm around a ten-year-old Lena, both of them holding up the day's first find like a trophy: a brass porthole, crusted green, worthless, priceless. He was laughing in the picture. She'd forgotten he laughed that much.

"Dad," she said quietly, to the photo, to the water, to the wreck lying patient on the shoal a quarter mile out with its secrets silted into its ribs. "I'm here. I'm going to find what you found. And then I'm going to make sure everybody knows."

The sea breathed in, breathed out. It still hadn't said anything kind.

But somewhere out in that darkness, her answer was waiting — and tomorrow, or the day after, or on the last dive before the dry season shut the door forever, she was going down to get it.

Behind her, unnoticed in the shadow of the equipment lockers, a figure detached itself from the dark and moved silently away along the tree line, toward the lights of the compound where Marcus Vane's men kept watch.

Someone had been listening.
