## 10. Storm Out of Season

The sky had been lying all morning.

Kit knew weather the way other kids knew homework — by necessity, by hours spent reading cloud lines over the salvage yards where they'd grown up. And what Kit saw building over the eastern ridge wasn't the dry season's flat white glare. It was a bruise-colored wall, stacked and slow, with that greenish tint underneath that meant the air itself was loading up with weight.

Nobody else seemed to notice. The camp moved through its rhythms: Tomas at the compressor, coaxing it through its cough; Priya checking her gauges twice because she always checked them twice; Bo hunched over the artifact table under the tarp, cataloging shards with a jeweler's patience. Lena stood at the water's edge with the site map rolled in one hand, staring out at the buoy line like she was reading something written just beneath the surface.

Kit had learned to read Lena too. Five months on the crew — first as a runner, then as a rigger, then as whatever the day demanded — and Kit had cataloged her moods like dive sites. This morning's mood was wrong. Too still. Too quiet.

"You see it?" Kit asked, coming up beside her.

Lena didn't look away from the water. "See what?"

"The east. That's not dry-season cloud."

Lena glanced up, and for a second something flickered across her face — not surprise. Recognition. Like she'd been watching it build for an hour and hadn't let herself believe it. "Tomas," she called, without raising her voice much, which somehow made it worse. "Kill the compressor."

Tomas looked up from the engine, wrench frozen mid-turn. "We've got two down, boss. Priya and Dev are at forty minutes bottom time. They'll want their hang—"

"Kill it anyway. We're pulling them early."

The word *early* did something to the camp. It moved through everyone like current through wire. Bo lifted his head from the ledger. Priya's spare set of gear went still in her hands. Because *early* meant *wrong*, and *wrong* on this coast — this coast, with its black rocks and its undertow and everything it had already taken from Lena Dunmore — *wrong* had a specific weight nobody needed explained.

The compressor died with a shudder. And then, as if the sky had only been waiting for permission, the wind arrived.

It came off the ridge in one long exhale, flattening the grass, snapping the tarps, dragging a curtain of rain behind it that Kit could actually *see* advancing across the water toward the dive boat — a gray wall moving fast, maybe three hundred meters out, maybe less. The swell changed pitch. The buoy line dipped and vanished.

"Line's live," Kit said, hands already on the winch controls. "I can feel it through the deck."

"They'll have felt it too," Lena said. "They'll be coming up the anchor line." She turned, and now her voice carried the way it did when there was no time left for anything but orders. "Bo — kill the generator, secure the tables. Tomas, you're with me on the boat. Kit—"

She stopped. Looked at Kit. And Kit watched her do the math she clearly hated doing: two divers below, a squall inbound, one winch operator needed on shore to run the recovery line, and exactly four pairs of hands above water.

"I can run it," Kit said. "I've run it a hundred times."

"You've run it in practice."

"I've run it every time you weren't looking. Which is most times."

Something almost like a smile crossed Lena's face and drowned. "Slack side. Keep tension steady, never let it go slack, never jerk it. If the load fights you, you *give* to it — you don't win against the sea, you negotiate. Tomas will call the rhythm from the boat. You listen to his voice and nothing else. Not the wind. Not your own head. His voice."

"Yes, boss."

"And Kit." She gripped Kit's shoulder, hard, once. "Everyone comes home today. That's the whole job. Everything else is decoration."

Then she was running for the skiff, Tomas pounding behind her, and Kit was alone at the winch with the storm coming in like a debt collector.

* * *

The first ten minutes were almost manageable.

Kit fed the line out as the skiff bucked its way to the buoy, keeping tension the way Lena had said — steady, alive, a conversation rather than a command. Rain hit in fat, stinging drops. The tarps tore loose from the artifact table and Bo wrestled them down alone, shouting things the wind ate whole. The generator coughed and caught and coughed again.

Then the radio crackled, and Tomas's voice came through shredded by static:

"—line's got weight— both of them on it— coming up slow, one's cramping, I think— Kit, take up slack, *gentle*, quarter turn—"

Quarter turn. Kit gave it a quarter turn. The drum took the strain and the cable hummed a note Kit felt in the teeth.

Through the rain, the buoy appeared and disappeared between swells — and once, horribly, Kit saw the surface boil where a diver should have been and didn't see anyone at all.

"Where are they?" Kit shouted into the radio. "I can't see them!"

"Below the swell line — they're *there*, kid, keep the line honest—" A wave slammed the skiff and the transmission dissolved into roar. When Tomas came back, his voice had dropped into the flat register he used for engines that were about to fail. "Priya's got Dev. She's got him on the line. But the swell's picked up and if we try to lift them onto the boat in this, we lose the boat. So we're running them to you on the line. Shore recovery. You're going to bring them up the beach slope, Kit. Both of them. On your own."

The words landed like cold water down the back of the neck.

On my own.

"No pressure," Bo said, suddenly beside Kit, soaked through, one hand braced on the winch frame. He'd abandoned the tarps entirely. "None at all. Just the lives of two human beings and the reputation of our entire operation."

"*Bo.*"

"That was a joke. Badly timed. I'm aware." He wedged himself against the frame. "I can't work the drum — my shoulder's shot, you know that. But I can talk. I'm told I'm good at talking."

"Talk about literally anything else."

"Fine. Did you know that in 1743 a storm off this exact coast sank eleven ships in one night? Historians call it the Night of the Black Water. Very dramatic. Terrible for morale." The drum trembled under Kit's hands as weight came onto the line — real weight now, living weight, two bodies and their gear riding a cable through a drowning world. "Steady. Quarter turn. There you go."

The cable sang. Kit's arms burned. Rain drove sideways, and every swell dragged the line sideways with it, and somewhere below the gray chaos were Priya and Dev, trusting a sixteen-year-old's grip on a brake lever with their lungs.

"Weight's increasing," Kit shouted. "Coming up!"

"I see them!" Bo's voice cracked open with relief. "There — off the port side of the buoy — *there!*"

A hand broke the surface. Then a mask. Then Priya, one arm locked around Dev's vest, his head lolling, her other fist wrapped around the recovery line like it was the last true thing in the world.

"Take them slow," Tomas's voice ground out of the radio. "Slow as sin, Kit. Let the line carry them, don't fight the surge. When the next wave lifts them — *that's* when you take up. Ride the lift. Don't chase it."

Ride the lift. Don't chase it.

Wave came. Lifted them. Kit took up — one quarter turn, two — and the sea tried to slam them back down and the brake screamed and Kit's palms burned inside wet gloves and Kit *gave* to it, the way Lena said, negotiated, yielded a foot to save a yard.

Again. Another wave. Another lift. Another quarter turn.

It went on forever. It lasted ninety seconds. Both were true.

And then Dev's boots scraped sand, and Priya's knees hit the shallows, and Kit dropped the brake and ran into the surf and caught Dev under the arms as Priya shoved him forward, and the three of them went down together in the foam, gasping, alive, tangled, *alive*.

* * *

Afterward, the camp was wreckage and silence.

Dev sat wrapped in every blanket the expedition owned, shivering out the last of the cramp, swearing weakly that he was fine, fine, *fine*. Priya crouched beside him, still in her suit, hair plastered flat, hands trembling in a way she kept trying to hide by holding a thermos. The squall had passed as fast as it came — unseasonal, unnatural, gone inland to bully someone else — leaving behind a beach strewn with debris and a site plan in ruins.

Three days of bottom time, gone. The deadline hadn't moved. Dry season's end sat on the horizon like a wall, closer than yesterday, closer every hour.

"We lost the afternoon grid," Bo said quietly, pencil hovering over the schedule. "Maybe tomorrow too, if the ground's churned. That puts us—" He did the math and visibly hated it. "That puts us tight. Tighter than tight."

"Then we tighten back," Lena said.

But her voice had an edge in it, and tempers were already fraying along old seams. Dev snapped at Tomas about compressor maintenance. Tomas snapped back about divers who ignored surface conditions. Priya said nothing at all, which was worse than either of them, until finally she stood, walked to the tideline, and stood looking at the water with her arms crossed like it owed her money.

Kit found her there.

"You okay?"

Priya didn't answer right away. Then: "He cramped at depth. Forty meters, in a rising sea, and his leg locked up like a rusted gate." She shook her head slowly. "One more minute down there and we'd have missed the window. One more minute."

"But you didn't miss it."

"No." She looked down at Kit, and something in her face shifted — the professional distance dropping away, just for a moment. "You ran that line like you'd done it your whole life. Tomas told me. Riding the lift, giving to the surge." She huffed a small, disbelieving laugh. "Lena trained you well."

"Lena wasn't even watching."

"No." Priya's eyes went to the water again. "But she built you anyway. That's what she does. She builds people and pretends she isn't doing it."

Kit wanted to say something back — something grateful, something big — but the words stuck, so Kit just nodded and looked out at the sea instead, which had gone calm and glassy and innocent, like it hadn't tried to kill anybody an hour ago.

From up the beach, Lena's voice cut through the dusk: "Full crew. Now. We're reworking the schedule tonight, all of it, and I need everyone sharp."

They gathered around the camp light — six people, salt-crusted, exhausted, bound together by a cable and a storm. Lena laid the charts out on the crate-table and pinned them with stones, and her finger traced the remaining days like a surgeon counting incisions.

"Dry season ends in nine days. We've got half the wreck left and Vane's deadline doesn't care about squalls." Her jaw tightened. "So we go deeper, faster, longer rotations. I know the risks. I'm asking anyway."

Silence. Then Tomas, gruff, arms crossed: "Deeper means the lower hull sections. Nobody's surveyed those yet."

"Which is why we survey them," Lena said.

And Kit, standing at the edge of the light, felt something settle into place — not fear, not anymore. Something steadier. An hour ago Kit had held two lives on a steel drum and hadn't dropped either one. Whatever Kit had been before today — runner, kid, extra pair of hands — that person had stayed out in the surf with the storm.

What walked back up the beach belonged to this crew. Fully. Permanently.

Priya squeezed Kit's shoulder on her way to the charts. "Welcome to the deep end, kid."

But as the crew bent over the maps, arguing rotation depths in low, frayed voices, Kit caught Lena looking east again — past the charts, past the firelight, out at the darkening water where the lower hull waited. And the look on her face wasn't about schedules or deadlines at all.

It was the look of someone who knew exactly what was down there.

And who was afraid of finding it.
