## 11. Hull Breach

The wreck opened for Priya the way old houses do at night — reluctantly, with a groan of settling timber and a breath of cold water that smelled of iron and time.

She hung at the lip of the breach in the starboard bow, thirty-one meters down, and let the current decide her for a moment. Below her, the *Corvane's Grace* lay on its beam ends in the gray-green murk, half-swallowed by sand, its ribs fanned out like the carcass of some leviathan the sea had picked clean. The visibility was better than the forecast had promised — six meters, maybe seven — and the light came down in long, wavering columns that turned the interior into a cathedral of drifting silt.

"Approaching the hold breach," she said into her regulator mic, and heard Tomas's voice come back thin and crackling through the comm unit. "Copy. You're at forty minutes bottom time. Don't make me come down there and drag you up by your fins."

"You'd have to catch me first."

She finned through the gap. It had been torn open — that was the first thing she noticed, the first wrongness, though she didn't yet have the shape of it. The plating was peeled outward, not inward. She'd dived enough wrecks to know the difference the way you know a door that was broken down versus one that was broken open from inside. Storm damage crushed. It folded a hull like a fist closing. This — this was a flower blooming in reverse, metal curled back on itself, edges sharp and bright even under two decades of growth.

She logged it without saying it aloud. Her slate took the note in her own shorthand: *breach — outward. not weather.*

The interior was a museum of the ordinary. Cargo netting dissolved to lace. A ship's bell furred green with a century — no, two decades — of patience. A child's shoe. She'd learned early in her career that wrecks were not about treasure. They were about Tuesdays. About the last ordinary day of people who didn't know it was their last. She moved through the hold with the reverence she always felt and never quite got used to, her lights sweeping the dark, and she told herself the tightness in her chest was just the depth.

It wasn't. It was the hull.

Because the deeper she went, the more she saw, and the more she saw, the less the official story held. Lena had told them the account — everyone in the salvage world knew the account. The *Corvane's Grace* had gone down in a sudden squall, swamped, driven onto the rocks. A tragedy. Her father's tragedy. Lena said the words flat, the way you'd read someone else's obituary, and Priya had never pressed.

But squalls didn't peel hulls outward.

She worked her way aft, following the line of the keel, her light probing into the black seams between frames. The wreck was preserved almost unnaturally well — cold, deep, silted — and that preservation was a gift and a trap, because it meant everything down here was legible. Everything down here could still testify.

At the junction of hold three, she found the first residue.

It stopped her cold. She actually stopped finning, hung dead in the water, and made herself look again, because the first look had felt like a hallucination. On the inner face of a bulkhead, in a patch the size of a dinner plate, the steel was cratered — pitted in a radial starburst, metal melted and refrozen in droplets, the corrosion pattern all wrong for simple seawater. She'd seen this before. Not on wrecks. On a demolition certification course in Mumbai, eight years ago, where they'd shown the class samples of what shaped charges did to marine-grade steel.

*Scuttling charge. Placed against the bulkhead. Fired.*

Her heart was going hard now, and she made herself run through the alternatives like a professional. Ballast shifting? No — ballast doesn't detonate. Boiler explosion? Wrong location, wrong signature, and boilers don't leave radial cratering on a bulkhead four frames aft of where any boiler ever sat. She photographed it from three angles, her strobes turning the silt to snow, and wrote on her slate in block letters because her hands wanted to shake: *DELIBERATE. CONFIRM WITH TOMAS.*

Tomas would know. Tomas had spent twelve years in naval demolition before he'd traded it for salvage and a quieter life, and he'd taken one look at the first day's sonar and muttered something in Portuguese that Bo had translated as "that hull's telling stories."

She pushed deeper. The charge residue was the kind of thing you could almost explain away — a ship carrying its own demolition stores, an accident of storage, a war relic. People explained away a lot, given a reason to want to. She knew that too. She'd done it herself, once, with a brother who'd promised he'd stop.

But then her light caught the glint.

It was wedged under a collapsed section of the cargo deck, in the aft-most corner of hold three, half-buried in silt that had drifted in through the breach like slow snow. A box. Steel, not wood. She cleared it with careful sweeps of her glove, and the silt bloomed and drifted and settled, and the stamp on the lid came up clean through the murk as if it had been waiting twenty years to be read.

A company mark. Vane Maritime Salvage — the stylized V over the wave, the same mark that was stamped on the contract Lena had signed, on the crates in the supply tent, on the side of the survey boat Vane's people ran out of the northern anchorage.

Priya hung there in the dark of a dead ship and felt the cold come through her suit in a way that had nothing to do with the water.

*Vane's mark. On a strongbox. Inside a wreck Vane hired them to excavate. A wreck that had been scuttled.*

The math was not complicated. She wished to God it were complicated, because complicated might have meant innocent. But it was simple: someone had put this box aboard, or put this ship down, and the same name was on both the crime and the excavation. You didn't hire people to dig up your own buried secrets unless you were certain they'd never be found — or unless you could control what they did when they were.

She thought of Lena's face at the contract signing, which Priya hadn't witnessed but could reconstruct from the way Lena had come back to camp that night and drunk half a bottle of rum in silence. She thought of the missing site log, the thing Bo had mentioned once, the hole in the paper trail that had made Lena's jaw go tight for a week. She thought of the man from Vane's advance team who photographed everything and smiled at nothing.

And she thought of the crew. Tomas with his bad knee and his daughter's tuition. Bo with his archive and his gentle, stubborn decency. Kit, who was seventeen and thought this job was an adventure. All of them balanced on a payday that a single word from Vane could revoke.

*The crew's fragile trust can't bear another secret yet.* She'd been telling herself that for two days about the hull breach. Now it was a litany, and the litany was starting to sound like an excuse.

She photographed the strongbox from every angle. She photographed the stamp close, macro settings, the V and the wave sharp as an accusation. She photographed its position in the wreck with a reference scale in frame, because she was a professional and professionals knew that evidence without context was just a story. She did not open it. Something in her gut said that opening it down here, alone, thirty-one meters deep, was a decision she wasn't authorized to make — and a deeper something said that once it was opened, it could never be unopened, and the crew would be in the blast radius of whatever came out.

She checked her gauge. Twenty-two minutes left of bottom time. Enough to confirm one more thing.

She swam aft along the scuttling charge's blast line, following the cratering, and found the second one. And then, in the engine room, the third. Three charges. Placed at the waterline junctions, exactly where a demolitions man would put them to sink a ship fast and quiet and make it look like storm damage. She photographed each one. Her strobe flashes lit the interior like lightning inside a skull.

On her way back through the hold, she paused at the child's shoe. It sat in the silt, small and patient, toe pointing toward the breach as if it had been walking toward the light.

*Somebody's Tuesday,* she thought. *Somebody's whole life, and somebody got paid for it.*

She ascended slow, watching her computer, doing her stops, and the whole time the wreck fell away beneath her and the evidence stayed down there in the dark like a loaded gun on a table.

* * *

The surface was too bright.

Priya came up the ladder of the dive boat with her arms shaking — not from the swim, she told herself, from the swim — and Tomas caught her tank valve and steadied her, and she let him, because for one second she needed something solid that wasn't a secret.

"Good dive?" he asked, pulling her hood back.

"Productive."

"Productive how? You were down there an extra six minutes."

"Mapping." She peeled her gloves. Her fingers were white and pruned and would not quite obey. "The hold's more intact than the survey suggested. We should re-prioritize."

Tomas looked at her the way he looked at engines that were running slightly wrong — not broken, but about to be. He'd known her four years. He knew what her face did when it was holding something. But the deck was crowded, and Vane's survey boat was a kilometer off the northern anchorage, and there were eyes, and so he only said, "Debrief at camp. Lena wants everything on the slate tonight."

"Everything," Priya said, and heard how the word came out of her mouth like a coin she was deciding whether or not to spend.

She toweled off. She drank the terrible sweet tea Kit pressed into her hands — Kit, who was bouncing on their heels asking what it was like down there, was it creepy, did she see any skeletons, and Priya managed to smile and say it was just a ship, kid, just an old ship, and hated how easily the lie came, because she was getting practice.

The sun was dropping toward the water, turning the sea to hammered copper. The cliffs behind camp went black and gold. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it — that the world could be this beautiful while she carried a photograph of a murder weapon in a waterproof case against her chest.

She was coiling the tag line when she saw him.

He was on the point — the black rock promontory north of the beach, the one that guarded the anchorage — and he was too far away to identify by face, but not by silhouette. Broad shoulders, hands in pockets, the particular stillness of a man who is not looking at a sunset. He stood at the edge of the rocks where the Vane survey boat had moored that morning, and he was facing the dive boat. Facing her.

Priya went still, coiled rope forgotten in her hands.

Through the binoculars she'd left on the crate — she raised them without letting herself think about it, casual, a woman checking the horizon — she resolved him into detail. One of Vane's men. The quiet one, the one who'd introduced himself the first week as a "logistics coordinator" and had never once coordinated anything she'd seen. He had a camera with a long lens. It was pointed at the dive boat. At her.

He wasn't even hiding it. That was the part that turned her blood to ice water. He saw her looking. He lifted one hand — not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *I see you seeing me.* Then he turned and walked back over the point, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be and all the leverage in the world.

Priya set down the binoculars. Her hands were steady now, which surprised her. Fear had passed through her and out the other side into something colder and more useful.

They knew. Or they suspected. Either way, the clock she'd been pretending didn't exist had just started ticking. She had photographs of scuttling charges and a strongbox with Vane's name on it, and Vane's man had watched her come up from the wreck with those photographs against her chest, and the only question left was what he'd do about it — and what Vane would do when the report reached him tonight, over whatever encrypted channel logistics coordinators used.

She looked down the beach. Kit was helping Bo stake out the new trench grid, their laughter carrying thin across the water. Tomas was checking the compressor, moving with the half-limping efficiency that was his whole personality. And Lena stood at the waterline, boots in the surf, looking out at the wreck site with that expression she wore — the one that wasn't about salvage at all, that had never been about salvage, that Priya had finally, tonight, begun to understand.

*She knows something,* Priya thought. *She's known since before we ever got here. And she didn't tell us either.*

The evidence in her case was heavy as a stone. The safety of the camp, of the crew, of the whole fragile arrangement they'd built on Vane's money — that was gone now, or going, and every hour they kept diving was an hour they spent inside the blast radius.

Priya zipped the waterproof case into her dry bag, and made a decision: tonight, at the debrief, in front of everyone or in front of Lena alone, the photographs were coming out. Whatever it cost.

She was still deciding which when she saw Lena turn from the waterline and start walking toward her, and the look on Lena's face said she already knew the dive had changed everything.

"Priya," Lena said, when she reached the boat. "What did you find down there?"
