## 12. The Price of Silence

Marcus Vane had learned long ago that the sea kept no secrets worth keeping. It was people who kept them, and people could be priced.

He stood at the window of the harbor office — his harbor office, leased under a name that would never appear on paper connected to him — and watched the dive boat come in through the late-afternoon chop. *Meridian's Daughter*, Tomas Reyes's refitted trawler, riding low with the day's recoveries. Even from this distance he could read the crew's body language the way he'd once read tide tables: Priya Anand moving with the coiled energy of a woman carrying something she hadn't yet decided to say aloud. The boy, Kit, hauling lines with a competence that hadn't existed three weeks ago. Bo Kowalczyk hunched over a crate like a priest over scripture.

And Lena Dunmore at the wheel, last to leave it, as her father had been last to leave every helm he ever held.

Vane turned back to the desk and let his fingers rest on the object lying there in its oilcloth wrapping. He had carried it for five years. There were nights — few now, but they came — when he told himself that was sentiment. Most nights he knew better. It was leverage. Everything was leverage, if you held it long enough.

The knock came at six exactly. Of course it did. Lena Dunmore was her father's daughter down to the punctuation.

"Come in."

She filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon — not large, but inevitable. Salt-stained jacket, hair tied back without ceremony, eyes that went first to the window, then to the desk, then to him, cataloguing exits. She'd done that since she was nineteen years old, standing in a courthouse corridor while men in suits explained to her what an "act of God" meant in maritime law.

"You wanted to see me." Not a question. She didn't sit when he gestured to the chair. Good. Sitting was surrender; he needed her standing, needing something.

"I did." He poured two fingers of whisky into a second glass without asking whether she drank. Her father had. He set it on the edge of the desk between them, a small offering, a small insult. "How is the dig progressing?"

"You're paying for daily reports. You have them."

"I have reports." He smiled and moved around the desk, unhurried, letting the oilcloth stay where it lay. "Reports tell me what you want me to know. I'm interested in what you don't."

Something flickered behind her eyes — quick, controlled, gone. But Vane had spent thirty years reading faces across negotiating tables on four continents, and he had never once lost money to a face he couldn't read. Hers told him everything: she'd found it. Or Priya had, and Priya had told her, and the two of them had been circling each other for days like dogs deciding whether the thing between them was trust or a wound.

"Section nine," Vane said pleasantly. "Aft cargo hold, beneath the collapsed plating your engineer has been so careful to shore up. A strongbox. Company-stamped."

The silence stretched three full seconds. To her credit, she didn't glance at the door.

"Your advance team mapped the wreck," she said. "You knew the layout."

"My advance team mapped the wreck in 2019, before the storm season shifted the sandbar forty meters east." He let that land. Let her do the arithmetic herself — that he had known the site longer than she'd known about the contract, that every choice she thought she'd made had been made inside walls he'd built. "They found the box. They also found what was around it. Residue patterns in the hull plating. Do you know what I mean by residue patterns, Lena?"

"I know what scuttling charges look like."

"Then we can skip the part where we pretend this is a conversation about archaeology." He returned to the desk, opened the drawer, and laid out a folder beside the oilcloth bundle. Two documents. One thick, one thin. "Sit down. Please. This will go better seated, and I'd rather neither of us left this room angry."

She sat. On the edge of the chair, spine straight, hands loose on her knees — a diver's posture, someone ready to move. He almost admired it.

"The thin document," he said, tapping it, "is an addendum to your contract. One page. You'll find the language simple because I wrote it simply: upon signature, all findings from section nine — photographic, physical, or otherwise — transfer to my sole ownership. Your crew signs nothing. Their obligations don't change. They finish the season, they get paid in full, plus a completion bonus I've already funded. Everyone goes home whole."

"And the thick one?"

"Payment schedule. Doubled." He slid it toward her. "Not for the crew. For you. A personal retainer, payable on signature, that would retire every debt your operation carries — and I know to the dollar what those are, Lena, because I checked before I ever sent that wire. You've been financing digs with salvage advances for three years. You're one bad season from losing your equipment, which means losing your crew, which means—" He stopped himself from finishing it. Kindness was more effective unfinished; it let the listener complete the cruelty themselves.

Lena looked at the papers and did not touch them. "And the logbook."

There it was. He'd wondered how long she'd hold the word behind her teeth. Five years, apparently. Longer than he'd estimated. He revised his assessment of her upward, and reminded himself that respect was a luxury the situation didn't permit.

"Yes," he said quietly. "The logbook."

He unwrapped the oilcloth.

Elias Dunmore's final dive log sat on the desk between them, salt-crusted at the corners, the elastic band perished to a black smear, the cover warped from five years in a dry safe that had once been a wet locker. Vane watched her hands. They stayed flat on her knees, but the knuckles went white, one finger at a time, like a tide going out over stones.

"You've had it," she said. Her voice was level. Almost. "Five years. You've had it the whole time."

"I acquired it eleven days after the sinking, from the harbormaster's clerk who inventoried his effects." Vane kept his tone flat, factual, a man discussing freight rates. "I've read it. Every page. Including the last three."

Her head came up sharply. "The last pages are—"

"Water-damaged. Yes. Partially." He folded his hands. "But Elias Dunmore wrote in pencil, and pencil doesn't dissolve, it only blurs. Given time and patience, graphite is legible again. I've had five years and very good people." He paused, and gave her the truth plainly, because the plainness was the weapon: "He knew about the cargo fraud. The manifest discrepancies. He'd documented offloads at two ports before the final leg, and he intended to report them to the maritime authority when he made landfall. That's why the ship went down where it went down, when it went down. That's what the charges were for. And that is what the box in section nine will confirm, because your father kept duplicates, and Elias Dunmore was many things, but he was never careless."

Lena stood up so fast the chair skidded. She stood at the window with her back to him, shoulders rigid, looking out at water that had taken her father and given back nothing, and Vane let her stand there. Grief was not the enemy here. Grief was the current he intended to sail on.

"You killed him," she said to the glass.

"I arranged for a ship to be lost." His own voice surprised him with its steadiness. "I won't insult you by denying it, and I won't insult him by pretending it was anything other than what it was. Men have drowned ships for less than what Elias threatened to cost me. What I am offering you now is the only honest transaction anyone has ever put in front of you about that night. Closure, priced fairly. Take it or don't."

She turned. Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear, and Vane understood in that moment that he had miscalculated one thing — not the offer, not the price, but the timing. He should have waited until the deadline pressure had softened her. Instead he saw her father looking back at him from a nineteen-year-old's face, and he pressed on anyway, because pressing on was the only way he knew.

"Sign the addendum, hand over the box and whatever copies exist, and the logbook is yours tonight. Original. Unconditionally. No clause attaches to it — it's yours to burn, publish, or bury with your own hands. Double pay clears within the week. Your crew finishes the season paid and praised, and twenty years from now nobody remembers there was ever a question about the *Corvane*'s last voyage except the people in this room." He nudged the pen across the desk. "That's not a threat, Lena. It's arithmetic. Go to the authorities and you lose — my counsel owns three of the offices you'd file in, and the fourth is mine. Go to a journalist and you spend a decade in depositions while your crew scatters to other paychecks. Sign, and everyone you love walks away rich and free. It's the kindest deal you'll ever be offered, and I need you to understand that I am not offering it twice."

The room was quiet except for the harbor sounds filtering through the glass — a winch somewhere, gulls arguing over the fish market scraps, the ordinary music of a coast that didn't care what was being traded above it.

Lena picked up the pen.

Vane allowed himself one slow breath. Thirty years of deals, and the moment never dulled — the instant the other party's hand moved, the world reorganizing itself around his terms.

She clicked the pen open. Held it over the thin document. And then she set it down on the desk, uncapped, the nib pointing at him like a small black accusation.

"No," she said.

"Lena—"

"You said the last three entries. In his voice. In *his* handwriting." Her composure had cracked clean through, and what showed underneath wasn't rage. It was worse. It was grief with a direction. "You've read my father's last words to the world, and your offer is that I buy them back and promise to pretend you didn't drown him for writing them." She shook her head slowly. "You think this is arithmetic. You think everything is arithmetic. But you just made a mistake, Marcus."

"Did I."

"You told me the box confirms the logbook." She was moving toward the door now, unhurried, and somehow that was the most frightening thing in the room. "Which means you don't have what's in it. Which means you need me to sign more than I need your money. Sleep on that."

The door closed behind her with a sound no louder than a wave folding over.

* * *

Vane stood alone in the harbor office for a long time.

The whisky sat untouched in both glasses. He poured hers back into the bottle — waste offended him — and drank his own standing at the window, watching the lights of the *Meridian's Daughter* come on one by one across the dark water.

She was right. That was the intolerable part. He had shown his hand to buy her signature, and instead he'd shown her the one card he couldn't take back: that the strongbox's contents existed only in her custody now, and that his entire architecture — the forged complaint filed against their permits, the man photographing their equipment logs, the advance team's old surveys — rested on recovering it intact. If she'd already copied it, already shipped it, already buried it in some archive under a false name, then the addendum was worthless and the doubled payment was charity.

He considered his options the way he considered weather. The permits angle remained live — young Kit had been seen riding out toward the regional records office two nights ago, which meant the crew suspected the paperwork threat and was checking it. Fine. The paperwork was clean; the complaint was his own forgery, deniable, disposable. He could withdraw it and lose nothing.

But the deadline was real. Four days until the dry season ended and the dig license lapsed with it. After that, the site reverted to protected status, and any further recovery became theft — his theft, provable, public. Vane did not survive on provable things.

He opened the drawer and took out a second document, one he'd drafted weeks ago and hoped never to use. It was not an offer. It was a list of names — Tomas, Priya, Bo, Kit — with dates of birth, prior incidents, expired certifications, and the specific regulatory violations each could be charged with if a thorough inspector happened to visit a certain beach camp in the next seventy-two hours. None of it fatal. All of it expensive. Enough to make four people understand that loyalty to Lena Dunmore had a price tag attached, and that Marcus Vane could raise it daily.

He read the names twice. Then he picked up the telephone and dialed the number of the man watching the dive boat.

"She declined," Vane said. "Move to phase two. Start with the boy — he's the soft point. And Harlan?" He looked out at the lit windows of the trawler, at the small stubborn constellation of a family that had chosen each other over the world. "Be visible. I want her to watch it happen."

Across the harbor, in the galley of the *Meridian's Daughter*, Lena Dunmore was at that moment laying her father's photograph face-down on the table and telling her crew, for the first time, everything.

Vane didn't know that yet.

By morning, he would.
