## 13. What Loyalty Weighs

Vane's boat was still a smudge on the horizon when Lena finally let her hands shake.

She'd held them steady through the whole negotiation — steady when he slid the folder across the chart table, steady when he named the figure, steady when he set the logbook down beside it like a man laying a winning card on felt. Steady when she said, *I'll think about it*, and meant it as neither yes nor no. Only now, alone in her bunk with the canvas flap buttoned against the wind, did the tremor come, running up from her wrists into her forearms until she had to press her palms flat against her knees to make it stop.

The logbook sat on the shelf above her cot where she'd left it. She hadn't opened it again. She couldn't. Not yet. Opening it would mean reading her father's handwriting for the first time in five years, and reading it while Vane's offer hung over her head would taint every word of it. She wanted to read it clean or not at all.

Outside, the camp was settling into its evening rhythms. The generator's stutter and settle. Tomas swearing softly at the compressor in Spanish, then English, then that hybrid language he used only for machines he loved. Kit's laugh, high and bright, carrying across the sand from the mess tent. Priya rinsing dive gear, the rhythmic slosh of freshwater through regulators. Bo's lamp burning late in the records tent, always last to sleep, cataloguing the day's finds by lamplight like a monk at vespers.

Her people. Her crew. The only family she had left, assembled one stray at a time.

And Vane was offering enough money to buy every one of them a new life.

She turned the number over in her mind the way she'd turn an artifact in her hands, looking for the flaw, the fracture line, the place where it would break if she squeezed. Double the contract price. Paid on signature. No publication clause enforced — no, *waived*, he'd said, which was worse, because a waived clause could be un-waived, but money paid was money spent. Enough to clear Tomas's debts back in Cádiz, the ones he never talked about but that Lena knew weighed on him every time a wire transfer came in. Enough to fund Priya's certification program, the school for working-class divers she sketched plans for on napkins when she thought no one was watching. Enough for Bo to stop living out of a duffel bag and rent an actual archive space, climate-controlled, with real shelving. Enough to put Kit through whatever Kit wanted to be put through — university, trade school, a boat of their own.

Enough to set them all free.

That was the poison in it. That was what made this offer different from every dirty deal she'd ever walked away from. It wasn't money for herself. She didn't need anything. She'd been living out of salvage camps so long that her possessions fit in two crates and her wants had atrophied down to coffee, dry socks, and the truth about her father. But them — they needed things. They deserved things. And she was the only one standing between them and getting those things.

Except the price wasn't hers to pay either. That was the trap inside the trap.

Vane had been very clear, in his soft, patient way. The strongbox. Everything recovered from the aft hold, sealed and surrendered. And her signature on a confidentiality instrument broad enough to bury the scuttling evidence under legal concrete forever. In exchange: double pay, the logbook — already delivered, sitting on her shelf like bait already swallowed — and his word that the crew would never hear his name again after settlement.

*Your father's logbook returned,* he'd said, watching her face with those pale, patient eyes. *I imagine there are things in it you've waited a long time to read.*

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He'd known before he ever wired that first offer to the drought province, before the harbor office, before any of it. He hadn't hired her crew to excavate a wreck. He'd hired them to excavate his own crime, and hand him the shovel.

Lena rolled onto her back and stared at the underside of the canvas, where the lamplight made shifting shadows like water overhead.

Here was the arithmetic she kept refusing to finish:

If she took the deal, the crew got their lives. All of them. Every debt cleared, every dream funded, every future unlocked. And the cost was a lie — but not a shared lie. A private one. Hers alone. The crew would take their money and never know what it bought or what it buried. They'd never have to carry it. She would carry it for all of them, the way she carried everything, because that was the job, that was what a captain was —

No. Stop. She heard her father's voice in the phrasing, and her father had carried secrets too, had gone down to that wreck with what he knew and told no one, and look where carrying it alone had gotten him. Forty fathoms down, in a hull someone had blown open from the inside.

If she refused the deal — if she took the strongbox evidence public, dragged Vane into whatever light the courts and the press could manage — then what? She knew how these things went. Vane's influence ran through the regional permit offices; Kit had proven that much when he'd ridden overnight and found the fraudulent complaint in Vane's own hand. Lawyers would tie the findings up for years. The museum angle might work eventually, but eventually didn't pay Tomas's creditors, didn't keep Priya's school dream alive, didn't feed a crew through the dead season between contracts. They'd finish honest and broke, exactly as Tomas had framed it on the beach during the argument, and honest-and-broke had a body count in this business. She'd seen crews dissolve over less. She'd built this one out of the wreckage of others.

So: their freedom, bought with buried truth. Or the truth, paid for with their futures.

And the third option, the one whispering at the edge of the thought like the undertow below the black rocks: take the money, take the signatures, and then betray him anyway. Keep the duplicates Bo had made. Ship the archive out anyway. Let Vane pay for his own exposure.

She flinched away from that one fastest of all, and hated herself for the flinch, because she knew why. Not because it was wrong. Because it was what *he* would expect her to consider. Because once she started telling herself that a signed agreement was just another hull to breach, she became a person who breached hulls — and the whole difference between her and Marcus Vane collapsed into a matter of style.

Or maybe that was vanity. Maybe that was just the story she told herself so she could keep liking the woman in the mirror. Vane probably had a mirror-story too. Probably polished it every morning.

The generator coughed and died. Footsteps in the sand outside, unhurried, familiar. A knock on her bunk frame.

"Lena?" Bo's voice. "You missed dinner. I saved you a plate."

"Come in."

Bo ducked through the flap, gray-haired and stoop-shouldered, holding a covered tin plate like an offering. They set it on the crate that served as her table and stood there a moment, hands clasped behind their back, studying her with those archivist's eyes that missed nothing and judged gently.

"You've been in here since sundown," Bo said. "Priya says you didn't come check the evening's finds. Kit says you barely talked at lunch. I say you've got the look of a woman doing sums she doesn't want to finish."

"Is there a plate under this interrogation?"

"There is." Bo sat on the edge of the cot, uninvited, which was its own kind of message. "I won't ask what the sums are. But I'll tell you something I've learned from thirty years of ledgers, if you want it."

Lena lifted the tin foil. Stew, rice, half a fried plantain. She ate a bite so she wouldn't have to answer immediately.

"Every fraud I've ever documented," Bo said, "started with somebody deciding that one lie, held privately, was manageable. Just one. Just theirs. Just for a little while." They paused. "Ledgers don't work that way. Lies compound. Interest accrues. And the person holding the private lie starts keeping two sets of books — one for everyone else, one for themselves — and after a while they can't remember which numbers are real."

"I'm not lying to anyone."

"No," Bo agreed mildly. "Not yet. That's rather my point." They rose, patted her shoulder once — Bo was not a toucher, so the single pat landed like a medal pinned to her chest — and moved toward the flap. "Eat your dinner, Captain. Whatever you decide, decide it as someone who's fed."

The flap fell shut behind them. Lena sat with the cooling plate in her lap and understood that Bo knew. Not the specifics — Bo couldn't know the specifics — but the shape of it. The weight of it. Her archivist had looked at her tonight and recognized a woman being offered a private lie, and had come not to lecture but to leave a warning marker on the chart, the way you buoy a reef you can't remove.

She ate the stew. It tasted like nothing.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so around two in the morning she gave up and walked the beach.

The tide was out, exposing the black rocks below the cliff road, wet and gleaming like the backs of sleeping animals. The wreck lay somewhere past the second shoal, forty fathoms down, lit now by nothing but her memory. Somewhere down there was the rest of the proof. Somewhere down there were her father's personal effects, still waiting after five years for someone to come collect them.

She thought about the moment on Vane's boat when he'd pushed the logbook toward her. He'd handled it carefully, she'd noticed. Respectfully, even. As though it were an artifact and not a weapon. *I'm not a monster, Ms. Dunmore,* he'd said, and the terrible thing was that she'd almost believed he believed it. Men like Vane weren't monsters. Monsters were simple. Vane was an accountant of other people's disasters, a man who had arranged her father's death the way you'd arrange cargo — efficiently, profitably, with full documentation — and who could sit across a table from the daughter five years later and offer her a fair price for her silence without his pulse moving a single beat.

And the worst part, the part she kept circling like a diver circling a hazard: part of her wanted to say yes.

Not for the money. For the ending. Take the deal, sign the paper, burn the contract, walk away with the logbook and the crew's futures secured and the whole ugly story closed like a hatch. No years of lawyers. No watching her people go hungry on principle. No risk that the truth, once loose, would hurt the innocent bystanders Vane's fall would scatter — the dockworkers, the junior partners, whoever else stood in the blast radius. A contained detonation. Controlled demolition. Wasn't that what she did for a living?

But her father hadn't gotten a controlled demolition. He'd gotten scuttling charges in the dark and a missing logbook and a family that shattered along the fault lines of not-knowing. And somewhere in the deep water off this coast, his bones — or what the sea had left of them — were waiting for the record to be set straight, not settled.

*The sea keeps accounts.* She'd written that on the note she'd left in the decoy strongbox — no. Not yet. That was ahead of her, maybe. She caught herself thinking in futures that hadn't happened, planning moves she hadn't decided to make, and stopped hard. That was the danger of nights like this: your mind started playing both sides of the board, and whichever side you leaned toward, it showed you a winning line.

She picked up a piece of driftwood and threw it at the water, harder than she needed to. It vanished into the dark beyond the foam line.

The truth was simpler and uglier than any strategy. She didn't know what she was going to do. She was the captain, and she didn't know, and the crew was asleep twenty meters behind her trusting her to know, and tomorrow Priya would ask her — Priya had promised silence, but Priya had also demanded the whole truth next time, and next time was coming like weather on the horizon — and she would have to stand in front of people who had voted to follow her and either spend their trust or hoard it, and both options felt like drowning.

Leadership, she thought, was mostly this. Not the winch lines and the shouted orders and the dramatic rescues in squalls. This. Standing alone on a cold beach at two in the morning, weighing the people you loved against the thing that was right, knowing that whichever way you set it down, some part of the load would land on someone who never agreed to carry it.

Her father had faced his version of this choice and chosen silence, and silence had killed him. She would not choose silence. But she might have to choose delay — and delay, she knew with an archivist's certainty, was just silence wearing a watch.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old rope. Lena was at the mess table before anyone else, coffee going, the camp quiet except for gulls and the far-off slap of water on the hulls.

One by one, they emerged. Kit first, hair wild, stealing bread from the loaf before it was even cut. Then Priya, dive bag over her shoulder, checking the morning's schedule board. Then Tomas, then Bo, blinking into the light, assembling around the table with the unconscious choreography of people who had eaten a thousand meals together.

They talked about the day's plan. Priya wanted to run the aft hold survey — the aft hold, where the strongbox had come from, where the rest of it waited. Tomas argued for re-rigging the crane instead. Kit had opinions about the produce truck's axle. Ordinary talk. Morning talk. The sound of a functioning family.

Lena sat among them with her hands wrapped around her mug and said nothing about the folder in her footlocker, the number written on it, the signature line waiting at the bottom like an open mouth.

Bo glanced at her once, briefly, over the rim of a cup. Said nothing. Buoy on the reef.

Priya caught her eye on the way out to the boats, and held it a half-second longer than casual. *Next time,* that look said. *You promised.*

"I'll need two divers on the aft survey," Lena heard herself say, captain-voiced, steady, the voice that had held through every negotiation and every storm. "Priya, take Kit on the line. We'll want the rest of the hold mapped before the week's out."

"Aye, Captain." Priya shouldered her bag, and paused. "Anything else?"

There it was. The open door, offered plainly, in front of everyone. One sentence — *Vane made me an offer* — and the loneliest hour would end right there, on a beach at dawn, with the whole weight shared out before the day began.

Lena looked at her crew — at Kit's waiting face, at Tomas's narrowed eyes, at Bo's careful stillness, at Priya's steady, wounded patience — and she heard herself say:

"No. That's everything. Get moving — tide turns at nine."

They went. Down the beach, onto the boats, engines coughing alive in the gray morning. And Lena stood at the waterline with her untouched coffee going cold in her hand, hating herself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional, because she had just done the one thing her father had done, the one thing Bo had warned her about, the one thing she had sworn on a cold beach at two in the morning she would never do.

She had kept the books separate. She had told them the tide turned at nine, and not that it was turning on her.

Behind her, up the slope by the records tent, Bo stood watching the boats go, and did not look at her, and did not need to.

In her footlocker, the folder waited. And out past the shoals, in forty fathoms of green water, the sea kept its accounts — patient, complete, and indifferent to how long she took to balance them.
