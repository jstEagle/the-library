## 14. Kit Runs

Kit had learned to sleep with one ear open. It was a skill the streets taught before Tomas ever did — the difference between a door closing and a door opening, between footsteps passing and footsteps stopping. So when voices rose in the equipment tent at half past midnight, Kit was awake before the second sentence.

The camp was quiet otherwise. The generators hummed their low lullaby. Out past the black rocks, the sea worked at the shore like a patient animal. Kit lay in the narrow cot in the supply lean-to, which was technically not a sleeping space and therefore nobody's business, and listened.

Two voices. One belonged to Halloran — Vane's man, the one with the creased shirts and the way of standing like he was always waiting for a better room. The other voice took Kit longer to place, because it was low and careful and it didn't belong to anyone on the crew.

"You understand the position," Halloran said. "Mr. Vane is reasonable. He'd prefer this stay reasonable."

"I've told you what I can do," said the stranger. "What I can't do—"

"What you *can* do," Halloran cut in, smooth as oil on water, "is look again at those permits. Because from where I'm sitting, they're irregular. Provisional dig authorization issued to an entity that doesn't appear in the regional registry. Salvage rights transferred through a holding company with no physical address. And a site survey signed by an inspector who, I'm told, retired two years ago."

Kit's heart went cold and then hot, all in the same breath.

"That's not—" The stranger's voice wavered. A clerk's voice. A tired man reading paperwork by lamplight. "The transfer documentation came through the provincial office. If there's an error, it's an error of filing, not fraud."

"Errors are such slippery things," Halloran said. "They can be corrected. Or they can be prosecuted. Depends entirely on how cooperative everyone feels come morning." A pause, long enough for Kit to hear the sea twice. "Mr. Vane would hate to see this crew lose its authorization. All that work. All those paychecks. Gone over a filing mistake. But he will if he has to. You have until Friday."

Chairs scraped. Boots on gravel. Kit counted to sixty after the sound of the camp truck pulling away, then slid out of the cot and stood barefoot on the cold ground, pulse hammering in the ears.

Friday. That was four days out. And the dry season deadline was six.

Kit knew what this was. Not the details — but the shape of it. Kit had spent enough years around people who used paperwork the way other people used fists. This was a fist wrapped in a folder. Vane couldn't buy Lena — Kit had watched him try, had watched her face go stone when she came back from that meeting with the strongbox offer she still hadn't told them everything about (Kit saw things; Kit always saw things) — so now he was going to take the crew's legal footing out from under them and let gravity do the rest.

Unless the permits were actually clean.

*If* they were clean.

That was the question, wasn't it. Bo kept the expedition files in the archive tent, triple-copied, cross-referenced, annotated in that tiny handwriting like scripture margins. But Bo could verify what they *had*. What Kit needed to know was whether what they had would survive someone leaning on the registry. And there was exactly one place to find that out: the regional records office, three hours' ride inland, in the town of Corren's Ferry.

Kit dressed in the dark. Boots, jacket, the canvas satchel with the water bottle and the roll of bills from the emergency tin — forty-some dollars that Lena pretended not to know Kit skimmed a share of for exactly this kind of thing, though she'd never once said so. Kit left a note on the crate by the lean-to: *Went to check something. Back tomorrow night. Don't wait up. — K.*

Then Kit stood in the dark for a moment, looking at the note, and understood that leaving it meant Lena would know within minutes of waking, and might say no, or worse, might say yes and send someone along, and either way the thing would stop being Kit's to do.

Kit picked up the note, folded it small, and put it in a pocket.

Sorry, Lena. Some things you run alone first.

* * *

The motorcycle was Tomas's third love, after engines generally and coffee specifically, and he kept it under a tarp behind the fuel drums with a cover so thorough it was practically an apology. Getting it started without waking the camp took eleven minutes, most of them spent breathing through the nose and cursing silently in three languages Kit had learned from listening to Tomas swear.

The engine caught on a low cough, and Kit walked it a hundred meters down the track before opening the throttle, letting the coast fall away behind in the dark.

The road inland was washboard and moon-shadow. Kit rode with the headlamp dimmed until the camp was surely out of sight, then let it burn full and hunched over the tank against the wind. The country unrolled in pieces Kit had only ever heard about at night, in the bad weeks, when Lena talked in her sleep or sat up smoking by the fire: the ridge where the road narrowed to one lane; the dry riverbed crossing with the rusted bridge marker; the stretch of eucalyptus where, once, years ago, a truck had gone off the shoulder and—

Kit made himself count fence posts instead. One. Two. Three. Numbers were solid. Numbers didn't bleed.

It was strange, knowing a person's nightmares better than the person knew you knew. Kit had been fourteen when they'd joined the crew, all elbows and hunger and a talent for being wherever they weren't supposed to be, and Lena had taken one look and said, *You eat, you run messages, you don't dive.* Five years later the terms hadn't changed, but the meaning of them had. Somewhere between the storm winch line and the argument on the beach — the fracture line, Tomas called it, though not to Lena's face — Kit had stopped being the kid the crew protected and started being the kid the crew *trusted*, which was a heavier thing entirely. Trusted people carried weight. Trusted people rode three hours into the dark because a clerk's voice shook at the wrong moment.

Corren's Ferry materialized out of the dawn like something remembered rather than seen: a water tower, a grain elevator, a main street with awnings the color of faded flags. The records office was a stone building with brass letters gone green, wedged between a pharmacy and a shuttered cinema, and it opened at eight, which gave Kit ninety minutes to become the kind of person who belonged in a records office at eight in the morning.

Kit bought coffee and a sweet roll from the bakery cart and ate sitting on the office steps, watching the clerks arrive. There were three: an older woman with keys on her belt, a young man who moved like his shoes hurt, and — Kit's stomach dropped and then steadied — a heavyset man in his fifties whose face Kit catalogued against the voice from the tent. Yes. Same voice. Lower here, daytime-normal, but the cadence matched. The tired clerk. The one Halloran had cornered.

So Halloran hadn't even bothered coming back today. He'd made his call last night and left the machinery to grind. Which meant whatever happened in this office today would happen as if it were ordinary procedure — a complaint processed, a flag raised, a file pulled — and no one would ever say Vane's name out loud.

Kit dumped the coffee, brushed the crumbs off, and walked in behind the older woman like a shadow with a purpose.

"Morning," Kit said, cheerful, sixteen-going-on-thirty, satchel slung professional. "I'm hoping you can help me. I'm with the Dunmore salvage survey, coastal site, and our lead archivist sent me to pull certified copies of our permit filings. We've got a compliance review coming up." A lie built of true things — there *was* a review coming, just not the kind Bo had planned for.

The woman with the keys looked at the young man. The young man looked at the heavyset man, whose jaw tightened almost imperceptibly.

"Filing requests go through the front counter," the heavyset man said. "Form R-11. I can help you."

*Of course you can,* Kit thought. *You're going to help me whether I want it or not.*

Form R-11 wanted a name, an affiliation, a stated purpose, and a signature. Kit printed LENA DUNMORE EXPEDITIONS in careful block letters, wrote *records verification — insurance audit* in the purpose line, and signed with a version of Bo's tidy hand that Kit had practiced for exactly this kind of errand, because Bo's signature opened more doors than Lena's temper ever had. The heavyset man read the form twice, looked at Kit a beat too long, and disappeared into the stacks.

While he was gone, Kit did what Kit did best: paid attention. The public terminal by the window ran the registry index — slow, green-texted, ancient. Kit typed DUNMORE and waited through the wheel-spinning silence while the machine thought about it.

Three entries. Provisional Excavation Authorization 4471-C, issued fourteen months ago, status: ACTIVE. Salvage Rights Transfer, instrument filed through Meridian Holdings — status: RECORDED. Site Survey Certification, inspector's stamp verified, status: VALID.

Everything clean. Everything *clean*, filed properly, stamped properly, recorded in the right offices by the right hands. Bo's work, and it showed — every document chained to the next like links in a cable.

Which meant the threat wasn't real. Which meant—

"Here we are." The heavyset man returned with a folder and set it on the counter, and Kit saw the color of the tab before he saw anything else. Red. Complaints went in red folders. Kit had learned that much from Bo, who organized everything, including other people's bureaucracies.

"Complaint of irregularity," the man said, flat, rehearsed, eyes anywhere but Kit's face. "Filed against your authorization. Standard review process. Copies of filings are available, but the underlying record is flagged pending review."

"Filed by whom?" Kit asked.

"The complainant's identity is—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, like a man stepping off a ledge he'd already fallen from. "The complainant's identity is confidential during review."

"Sure," Kit said. "But the form has a date, right? And a receiving officer?"

Something flickered across the man's face — guilt, maybe, or relief at being given permission to be honest. He turned the folder toward himself, angled it away, and said, very softly, "Check the date against your transfer filing. That's all I'll say. Take your copies and go, kid. And tell whoever sent you—" His voice caught. "Tell them some of us hate this part of the job."

He photocopied the red-tabbed pages himself, stamped them, and slid them across the counter without meeting Kit's eyes.

Kit read them on the steps outside, in the strengthening sun, hands not quite steady.

The complaint was dated nine days ago. Nine days ago, the crew hadn't yet found the strongbox. Nine days ago, Vane had no reason to squeeze — unless he'd already known what the wreck held, which he had, which he'd *always* known, and the complaint wasn't a reaction at all. It was a leash, prepared in advance, waiting for the moment the crew pulled too hard.

And the receiving officer's line — the name of the clerk who'd accepted the filing — was signed in a hand Kit recognized from the counter inside. The heavyset man's own name, dated nine days back.

Except Kit had heard him, last night, talking to Halloran like a man seeing the complaint for the first time. Like a man being *shown* it.

Kit read the date again. Then the signature. Then, at the bottom, in the complainant field, a typed name that made the morning go very still:

MERIDIAN HOLDINGS, per M.V., authorized representative.

M.V. Marcus Vane. Filed through his own shell company, received by a clerk he'd since leaned on to pretend it wasn't his, aimed at permits that the registry itself showed were valid in every particular. He hadn't found dirt. He'd manufactured the accusation and buried it in procedure, and if the crew missed Friday's review, the flag would stand, the authorization would freeze, and every hour of diving past the dry season would become evidence against them instead of against him.

Kit sat on the stone steps with the copies trembling slightly in hand and felt the fear turn over, slowly, into something harder and cleaner. They'd threatened the crew with paper. Fine. Paper could be beaten with paper. Bo would know what to do — Bo always knew — but Bo needed these pages, needed them tonight, needed the dates and the signatures and the beautiful, damning fact that Vane had forged his own trap and dated it.

Kit folded the copies into the inner pocket, zipped it, and stood up.

Three hours back. Fuel enough, barely. Daylight enough, barely. Kit swung a leg over Tomas's bike, kicked it alive, and pointed it east toward the sea with the sun climbing hot on the back of the neck and the whole fragile architecture of the crew's future riding in a canvas satchel against one racing heart.

Behind, Corren's Ferry shrank to a smudge. Ahead, somewhere past the ridge and the riverbed and the eucalyptus, the tents waited — Lena carrying her secret, Priya carrying hers, the whole camp balanced on fault lines nobody had mapped.

By tonight, Kit thought, twisting the throttle open, they'd have a map of Vane's.

And by tomorrow, if Kit had any say in it, he'd wish he'd never written their name in a red folder — because the Dunmore crew didn't just survive threats.

They filed them.
