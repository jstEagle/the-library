## 15. Fracture Line

Tomas heard it before he saw it — the particular silence of a camp where something had already gone wrong.

He'd spent twenty years listening to machines, and machines never lied to him. A diesel engine with a cracked head still turned over; it just sounded tired doing it. People were different. People could sound perfectly fine right up until the moment they seized solid. And tonight the camp by the black rocks sounded fine — Priya rinsing gear at the water point, Kit stacking crates with too much energy, Bo's lantern glowing in the archive tent — but underneath it ran a frequency he recognized from bad jobs in worse places. The hum of a secret being carried by more than one person and dropped by none of them.

He found the crack in it at dinner.

Lena sat apart, as she often did, but tonight she wasn't reading charts or checking tide tables. She was just sitting, hands wrapped around a tin mug she hadn't drunk from, watching the fire like it owed her an explanation. And across the flames, Priya kept glancing at her — not the way a dive partner checks on a diver, but the way someone checks on a wound they know about and have been told not to touch.

Tomas set down his fork. "All right," he said. "Somebody tell me what I'm missing."

The fire popped. Kit looked up. Bo's pen stopped mid-line in his ledger.

"Nothing to miss," Lena said.

"See, that's the thing about this crew." He kept his voice level, the way he talked to engines. "We've been out here six weeks. I've rebuilt the compressor twice, I've watched Kit grow three centimeters, I've listened to Bo name every fish we've accidentally caught. There is nothing about this camp I don't hear. And for four days there's been a note in the air I can't place. So either my ears are going, or somebody's carrying something heavy."

Priya set down her cup. She looked at Lena, and something passed between them — a question, a permission, a refusal. Then Priya said, quietly, "It's not mine to say."

"It's nobody's to say," Lena said. "Because there's nothing."

And that — that was the sound of the head gasket letting go.

Tomas had followed Lena Dunmore through three countries and two near-bankruptcies. He had watched her lie to customs officers with a smile and lie to clients with a straight face, and he had never once minded, because she always lied *outward*, toward the world that wanted to eat them, never inward, toward the five people who ate her cooking and slept ten feet from her tent. That was the whole architecture of the crew. That was the load-bearing wall.

She had just put a crack in it herself.

"Strongbox," Bo said softly. He hadn't looked up from his ledger. "Dive eleven. You came up early. Your air was at two hundred bar and you lied about it at debrief — you said one-fifty. Nobody lies about having *more* air unless they want the debrief short." He finally raised his eyes. "I catalog everything, Lena. Including people."

The beach went very quiet. Somewhere past the rocks, the undertow breathed against the stones, patient as ever.

"Is that true?" Tomas asked.

Lena looked at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic she always did — risk versus reward, who needed protecting, what could be controlled. He watched her arrive at the answer she always arrived at: *me, alone.*

"Yes," she said. "There was a strongbox. Stamped with Vane's company mark. And there was residue inside the hull consistent with scuttling charges. Deliberate. The ship was sunk on purpose." She paused. "I photographed everything. The photos are hidden."

Kit's crate slipped from his hands and hit the sand with a hollow knock. "Sunk on purpose? Like — somebody—"

"Somebody wanted it on the bottom," Priya said. "With whatever was in it."

"And you were going to tell us when?" Tomas asked. It came out flatter than he intended, and he didn't take it back.

"When I knew what it meant. When I knew what Vane would do. When I knew how to keep us safe—"

"We're not cargo," Tomas said. The words came up from somewhere older than this job, older than the drought province, older than the day he'd first signed onto one of her digs with nothing but a toolbox and a bad knee. "You keep treating us like cargo. Pack us well, stack us low, don't let us shift in transit. Lena. We are not freight."

"I'm trying to keep you *alive*—"

"You're trying to keep us *ignorant*," Bo said, and there was no softness in it now, only the ache underneath, the ache of a man who loved someone lying kindly and had run out of room to pretend otherwise. "There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, because you've been on the other side of it. Someone decided what your father should and shouldn't know. Look how that ended."

The words landed like a dropped wrench. Lena went white. Kit made a small sound and Bo immediately looked stricken — "That was — I shouldn't have—" — but the damage was done, and everyone knew it, because everyone knew exactly what had happened to Lena's father on this coast, even if no one said it aloud, even if it lived in the silences at every landmark between here and the interior.

Lena stood up. "I hid it because Vane has a man photographing our equipment logs. Because he offered me money before we ever found anything worth finding. Because every hour I spend knowing something he wants buried is an hour our permits, our boat, and our necks are at risk, and I thought — I thought if I carried it alone, it would stay contained." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and Tomas hated how much it cost him to feel no pity at all in that moment. "I was wrong. Fine. I was wrong."

"No," Tomas said. "Being wrong isn't the problem. We've all been wrong. The problem is you decided we don't get to be wrong *with* you."

* * *

It fell apart after that, the way families fall apart — along old seams, saying old things.

Priya accused her of putting divers at risk without informed consent: "If I'd known there might be deliberate scuttling, I'd have rigged the penetration differently. You made tactical decisions for me with my own body." Kit, voice shaking, asked whether the crew had ever once been told the truth about why Lena took this job, whether the wreck was really just a contract, and Lena couldn't answer fast enough, and the not-answering was its own answer. Bo simply sat with his ledger open on his knees like a man at a trial, recording nothing, which from Bo was the loudest testimony there was.

Tomas let it burn for a while. Some fires you fight and some fires you stand back from, because they're clearing ground that needs clearing. But when Kit stormed off toward the tideline and Priya threw her gloves into the sand and Bo closed the ledger with a snap like a door, Tomas stood up and planted himself between the fire and all of them.

"Enough." He didn't shout. He never shouted; shouting was for people whose authority needed volume. "Sit down. All of you. Kit, I can see you at the waterline, sit anyway."

They sat. Even Lena, last, stiffly, at the edge of the circle, outside the warmth.

"Now here's where we are," Tomas said. "In four days, Vane's man comes back with the payment schedule. In nine days, the dry season ends and this dig becomes illegal. We have a strongbox full of evidence that our employer sank a ship and killed at least one man — maybe more — and we have a captain who found it and sat on it. Those are facts. Everything else is weather." He looked around the circle. "So we settle it the way we settle everything. We vote."

"There's no vote," Lena said. "This is my expedition—"

"This stopped being your expedition the day we voted to come," Tomas said. "Chapter two. Your tent, your map, your terrible stew. Four votes to one. You took ours then. You'll take ours now."

For a long moment she stared at him, and he braced for the version of her that walked away from crews, from clients, from continents. But then her shoulders came down half an inch, and she nodded once, and something in his chest unclenched.

"The question," he said, "is simple. Option one: we finish honest. We document everything, we surface the strongbox openly, we hand it to whoever has clean hands, and we walk away from Vane's money with nothing but our names and each other. Option two: we take his payout. We give him the box, we sign his paper, we bury it, and every one of us gets paid enough to disappear for a year. Maybe forever, if we're careful." He held up a hand before anyone spoke. "Nobody argues yet. Nobody persuades. We each say our piece, short, and then we vote. Kit?"

Kit had crept back to the edge of the firelight, sand-dusted and red-eyed. "Option one," Kit said immediately. "But — I want to say why. My mom used to say you can be poor honest or poor scared, and scared costs more." A shaky breath. "I'm not scared. Not of him. Not anymore. One."

"Priya."

Priya looked at her gloves in the sand, then at Lena. "I want option one. But I need to say — I almost picked two. Because I've got debts, and because trust that breaks once breaks easier the second time." She let that sit, aimed and unhidden. "Then I remembered I got into this work because the sea doesn't lie. It just shows you what's there. Somebody on this crew should hold to that standard. One."

"Bo."

Bo opened his ledger, then closed it again without looking at it. "I've been keeping accounts since I was nine years old. Money, artifacts, hours, promises. And I'll tell you what twenty years of ledgers taught me: debt paid in silence compounds. If we take Vane's money, we don't stop owing — we just owe someone worse." He looked at Lena, and his voice gentled for the first time all night. "One. And Lena — I'm voting one *because* of you, not despite you. Because the woman who hired us wouldn't have taken his deal either. I'd like her back."

Tomas felt the circle shift, all of them angled now toward the one person outside the light.

"Lena," he said. "Your vote counts same as everyone's. But I'll ask you to say it facing us."

She stood. She stepped into the firelight, into the middle of them, and Tomas saw her properly for the first time in weeks — not the expedition lead, not the grieving daughter running miles from a memory, but a tired woman on a cold beach who had been carrying a hull's weight alone and had finally, finally let it crack her open where they could reach her.

"Five years ago I swore I'd never come back to this coast," she said. "Then a wire came offering more money than we've ever seen, for this exact wreck, and I said yes, and I told myself it was pragmatism. It wasn't. I came because my father's logbook disappeared the day he died, and the only witness left is sitting under forty feet of water out there." She gestured at the dark line of the reef, black against blacker sky. "I hid the strongbox because I've spent five years learning that the truth about this ship gets people hurt, and I couldn't stand to watch it happen to any of you. That was arrogance. It was also love, badly built. I'm asking — I'm not ordering, I'm asking — for one more chance to build it better."

"That's not a vote," Tomas said gently.

"No," she said. "Here's my vote. Option one. Finish honest and broke." A breath that shuddered only slightly. "Vane offered me a private deal yesterday. Double pay. My father's logbook, returned, in exchange for the box and my signature. I haven't answered him yet." Murmurs went around the circle — Priya's sharp inhale, Kit's whispered *"yesterday?"* — but Lena lifted her chin. "I'm answering him now. To all of you first. No."

Tomas let the silence hold for three full breaths. Then he raised his hand. "One. Carried, five to nothing." He looked around at them — the engineer, the diver, the archivist, the kid, the captain, all of them singed and standing. "Then here's what happens tomorrow. Bo starts duplicating the entire archive, triple copies, separate locations. Priya plans the recovery of that scuttling mechanism — legally documented, witnesses present, no more solo finds. Kit keeps driving that produce route and learns every back road off this coast. And I—" he tapped his own chest "—am going to make sure that when Vane realizes what we're doing, the only thing he can take from us is time. Which he doesn't have."

He crouched and fed another branch to the fire, and the sparks climbed straight up into the windless dark.

"One more thing," he said. "What happened tonight stays tonight. We argue like family behind closed doors and sail like a crew in front of cameras. Agreed?" Nods all around, grudging and real. "Good. Now somebody make tea. I've earned it."

It was later — much later, the fire burned to embers, the others asleep — that Tomas noticed Bo still awake at the archive tent, lantern low, writing something in the back pages of the ledger. Curious, and too old to pretend he wasn't, Tomas wandered over.

"What are you logging at midnight?"

Bo didn't look up. His pen moved steadily. "An entry I've never had cause to write before." He turned the ledger so Tomas could see it. Neat columns, dated, itemized — and beside tonight's date, a single line:

*Fracture identified. Repaired. Load rating unknown.*

Below it, fresh ink, still drying:

*Note: Vane's launch sighted at anchor beyond the reef at 2300 hours. Holding position. Watching.*

Tomas looked out past the black rocks, to where a white running light bobbed gently in the darkness, patient as the undertow.

"He knows," Tomas said quietly.

"He knows," Bo agreed, and closed the ledger.
