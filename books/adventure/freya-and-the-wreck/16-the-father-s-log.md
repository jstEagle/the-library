## 16. The Father's Log

The logbook sat on the table between us like something that had been dredged up from the deep — which, in every way that mattered, it had.

Lena hadn't touched it since Vane's man had delivered it with the contract addendum two nights ago. It had come in a padded envelope, no note, no return address, just her father's initials embossed in faded gold on the cover: **E.D.** — Elias Dunmore. She'd carried it from the mess tent to her bunk and back three times without opening it, the way you carry a live charge, careful not to jolt it.

Now, past midnight, with the camp generator humming its tired lullaby and Priya asleep thirty feet away, she finally put her hands on it.

The leather was stiff with salt. Twenty years of salt, worked into the grain like a second skin. She turned it over. The spine was cracked at the same place it had always cracked — he'd had a habit of shoving it into his back pocket before a dive, and she'd teased him about it a hundred times. *One day you'll break it in half, Dad.* *Then I'll write shorter entries,* he'd say.

She opened it to the first page and the smell hit her — old paper and diesel and something faintly organic, the ghost of a man long dissolved into current and sand.

*March 14.* The handwriting was quick, slanted left, letters crowded together like men sheltering from wind. She knew this hand better than her own. She'd learned her letters copying it.

She started at the beginning, because there was no other way to do it. You didn't skip to the end of a man's life. You walked it.

* * *

The early entries were almost unbearable in their ordinariness. Fuel receipts noted in the margins. Tide tables copied out in his cramped shorthand. A sketch of the harbor at Port Calloway with a joke in the corner about the harbormaster's hat. Small complaints about a leaking valve on the *Meridian*, patched twice, cursed daily.

Her mother appeared in the margins. *R. wants me home by June. Told her the sea doesn't keep schedules. She says neither does she.* Lena could hear him laughing as he wrote it. She was eleven years old in these pages. She existed here only as a smudge of crayon on one entry — *L. drew me a picture of the boat. Better likeness than the insurance photos.*

She read for an hour, maybe two, letting the days accumulate. And then, four months before the end, the tone shifted. Not dramatically. Vane hadn't become a villain overnight; he'd become one the way rust happens, invisibly, entry by entry.

*June 2. M.V. wants the Meridian running the southern route again next month. Says the cargo manifest is "handled." I don't like how he said it. I don't like what handled means when the numbers don't match the hold.*

Lena stopped. Read it again. The lamp flickered — the generator surging as the night chill settled — and in the stuttering light her father's words seemed to move, alive, alarmed.

*June 9. Weighed in at Calloway. Manifest says forty tons of machine parts. The draft marks say we're carrying closer to sixty. Either the sea got heavier or someone's counting wrong on purpose. Asked M.V. about it. He laughed. He laughed, and then he bought me dinner, and somehow I ended up apologizing to him. That man could sell you the shirt off your back and have you thanking him for the breeze.*

A sound escaped her — half laugh, half sob — because it was so exactly him, so exactly Vane, that for a moment the twenty years collapsed and she was sitting across the galley table from her father while he told stories with his hands.

She kept reading.

* * *

*July 18. Opened a crate I wasn't supposed to find. Wasn't looking for it — hatch cover was seated wrong after the storm, went to reseat it. Inside: sealed drums, no markings, heavier than they should be. Manifest lists them as agricultural equipment. Agricultural equipment doesn't need lead lining. I've seen this kind of cargo once before, on a tramp freighter out of Singapore, and that captain disappeared the following spring.*

*M.V. was on the dock when we unloaded. Watched me too closely. Smiled too easily. Told me the Meridian's charter is renewed through winter. Generous terms. Very generous. I know what generous means now. It means: stop asking questions.*

Lena's hands were cold. She realized she'd been holding her breath and let it out slowly, deliberately, the way she did before a descent. Four more months. He had four more months, and he already knew — not the shape of it, not yet, but the weight of it. He was carrying sixty tons of something that would kill him, and he knew it, and he wrote it down anyway in his cramped left-slanting hand because writing things down was the only weapon a salvage captain had.

*August 3. Copied the manifests. All of them. Three years' worth. The pattern is clean once you see it: heavy cargo declared light, insured high, routed through waters M.V.'s competitors avoid. The Meridian isn't a freighter. She's an actuarial table with a hull. If she sinks, someone collects six times what the cargo cost. I don't think if anymore. I think when.*

There it was. The sentence she'd been diving toward for five years, across three continents, through every wreck site and roadside dig and drought-stricken province. *I think when.*

He had known. Not suspected — known. And he had done what Elias Dunmore always did with knowledge: he'd written it down, dated it, signed it, and tucked it where the sea couldn't take it from him.

Except the sea had taken it anyway. Or someone had.

* * *

The final entries were fewer. Shorter. As though he were rationing his time even on paper.

*September 22. Made copies of everything. One set goes to the maritime authority in the capital. One set stays with me. R. keeps asking why I'm distracted. I tell her it's the season. It's always the season. When this is done I'll take her and L. somewhere with no water in sight. Desert, maybe. L. would hate it. She'd love hating it.*

Lena pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. *Somewhere with no water in sight.* He'd been planning their escape from the very life that would kill him, and instead the life had followed them onto land, into the years after, into every silence at every dinner table until her mother stopped setting his place and Lena stopped coming home at all.

*October 5. Told M.V. today. Straight out, over coffee, man to man: I know what's in the holds, I know what the insurance game is, and I'm reporting it. Gave him until the end of the month to pull the Meridian off the route himself. He didn't argue. That frightened me more than shouting would have. He just looked at me the way a man looks at weather he's decided to sail into anyway. Then he shook my hand. His hand was cold. October, and his hand was cold as ballast.*

*October 11. Filed my report with the authority office. Clerk took it without reading it, stamped it, filed it under pending review. Pending review. Three years of fraud, pending review. Maybe I'm naive. Maybe the review is the point. But it's on record now. Dated. Signed. Whatever happens next, it happened after.*

*Whatever happens next, it happened after.*

She read the line five times. Her father had built a wall of paper between himself and whatever was coming, and the wall had held — the report existed, somewhere, in some filing cabinet in the capital, unless Vane's reach ran that deep too. Probably it did. Probably the report had been pulled within the week, the clerk promoted or paid or quietly retired. But the logbook proved the report had been made. And the logbook had survived.

Because Vane had kept it. All these years. Locked away like a trophy or a threat, until he'd needed it as bait.

*October 17. Final entry before the run. Weather looks dirty but the window closes after this and M.V. is pushing. I've stowed my copy of the manifests in the strongbox in my cabin — the one with the company stamp, his own stamp, irony being the one cargo that never runs short. If anything happens to me, whoever finds that box will find everything they need. Tell L. her drawing of the boat hangs in my cabin. Best likeness I ever had.*

That was the last entry. No farewell. No fear dressed up as wisdom. Just a drawing of a boat and a strongbox and a man who believed that paper could protect him.

Three days later, the *Meridian* went down off the black rocks with all hands.

* * *

Lena closed the book and sat in the dark for a long time.

The generator cycled down to its lowest hum. Outside, the surf kept its ancient arithmetic against the rocks — the same rocks, the same undertow, the same coast that had taken him. She had spent five years telling herself a story about that night: a storm out of season, a hull pushed too hard, a captain who miscalculated. Tragedy, not crime. The sea's indifference, not a man's design.

But the entries dismantled it piece by piece, the way her crew dismantled a wreck: methodically, layer by layer, until the true structure stood exposed.

Vane had known Elias was going to report him. Vane had scheduled the final run himself, pushed the window, sent the *Meridian* into dirty weather with a hold full of lead-lined drums and a captain who'd just shaken his cold hand. The storm account had never fit the hull damage — Priya had seen that on the first dive, had carried that wrongness alone for weeks. Now Lena understood what Priya had been carrying. The damage wasn't from the storm. It was from charges placed where they'd do the most work, in a hull already insured six times over, carrying evidence in a strongbox stamped with the owner's own mark.

And the strongbox — the real one, the one Priya had photographed in the wreck — was still down there. Still holding the manifests, if the seals had held, if twenty years of saltwater hadn't eaten the paper to pulp. Vane had returned the logbook to buy her silence, believing the box itself was unrecoverable or unreadable, believing grief was a currency he could spend.

He'd made one mistake. He'd given the logbook back.

Because the logbook wasn't closure. It was a map. Every entry pointed downward — to the box, to the manifests, to the mechanism that had torn the hull open on a calm-enough night in October. Her father had left her instructions the way he'd left her everything else: indirectly, trusting her to read between the lines, trusting her to dive.

Grief had been her engine for five years, but it had been an engine running hot and blind, burning fuel just to move. This was different. This was grief with a heading.

Lena stood, took the lamp, and walked to the equipment tent where the expedition's master archive lived in Bo's meticulously labeled crates. She woke Bo with a hand on his shoulder, and he came up out of sleep the way he always did — instantly, completely, glasses already in hand.

"It's late," he said. Then he saw her face, and the logbook under her arm, and he said nothing else.

"I need you to make a duplicate of everything," she said. "Tonight. Before dawn. And Bo — starting tomorrow, we work like the deadline is tomorrow. Because it might be."

Bo looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face, he nodded once and reached for his lantern.

Outside, the tide was turning. Lena stood at the edge of the camp and looked down the black slope toward the water, toward the wreck lying quiet in its grave of sand and rock, holding her father's last testimony in its iron ribs.

"Hold on, Dad," she said to the dark. "I'm coming down. And this time I'm bringing everything back up."

Behind her, unnoticed, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the fuel store and moved quietly toward the radio tent.
