## 17. Dry Season Countdown

Bo counted the way other people breathed — automatically, constantly, and with a private satisfaction when the numbers came out clean. Four days. That was what remained of the legal dig window, four days before the dry season's official end converted their permits into ash and every crate on this beach into evidence of trespass. Four days, one half-excavated wreck, a strongbox hidden in a false-bottomed equipment case under Tomas's cot, and a crew that hadn't looked each other in the eye since the argument on the sand.

Bo had spent thirty-one years among paper. Archives in three countries, a scandal in a fourth that Bo did not discuss, and finally this: cataloging other people's discoveries for wages that would embarrass a church mouse. Paper was honest in a way people weren't. Paper could be forged, yes — Bo had seen Vane's forged complaint with Kit's own wide eyes testifying to it — but paper left seams. Forgeries always left seams, because forgers were in a hurry and truth was not.

The problem, as Bo laid it out against the inside of both eyelids that night, was that they were the ones in a hurry now.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the sea lying flat as poured metal. Bo rose before anyone else, as always, and set up at the folding table with the ledger open, the recovered manifest photocopied twice and annotated in three colors of ink. The camp was quiet except for the generator's mutter and Priya's dive gear dripping somewhere behind the equipment tent. Lena's cot was empty. It was usually empty by now; she slept less than any human Bo had ever catalogued, and Bo had once worked with a monastery archivist who claimed to sleep two hours a night.

Lena appeared at the edge of the fire ring just as the sun cleared the headland, carrying two tin mugs. She handed one to Bo without asking, which was either trust or habit, and sat down heavily on an upturned crate.

"Four days," Lena said.

"Three and a half, if you count the tide tables, which I do." Bo turned the ledger around so Lena could see the columns. "Which is why I've been awake since four."

Lena drank. Waited. She had learned, over five years and eleven digs together, that Bo's silences were load-bearing.

"Here's what we have," Bo said. "Photographs of the scuttling residue. Photographs of the strongbox and Vane's company mark. The manifest cross-reference showing cargo offloaded before sinking. Your father's logbook — the original, which I have photographed page by page at high resolution, including the final entries. And my own working notes, which tie all of it together with dates, chain of custody, and enough boring detail that no lawyer alive will call it fabricated. Fabricators are never boring, Lena. It's our tell."

"You've been busy."

"I've been terrified," Bo said mildly. "It improves my output. Now. Here's the problem. Everything we have exists in exactly two places: this camp, and the copies I hid in the fuel drum liner. If Vane decides to stop pretending he's a businessman, he takes one match to this beach and five years of your life burns faster than the tents will."

Lena's jaw tightened. Bo watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Bo had been doing since four in the morning, but from the other side. Lena was thinking about her father. Bo was thinking about Kit's face at dinner last night, watching the adults argue, and about Tomas's hands shaking while he re-coiled a winch line that didn't need re-coiling.

"So we make more copies," Lena said slowly. "And get them off-site."

"We make more copies, we get them off-site, and — this is the part you're going to hate — we let him win first."

The mug stopped halfway to Lena's mouth.

"Explain that."

"Vane is not a patient man wearing patience like a coat. He's a man who has waited twenty years and believes the waiting is over. He thinks he bought us. He thinks the offer, the logbook, the threat to the permits — he thinks it all worked. And the moment he believes that, he relaxes. He inspects the site, he sees crates going onto trucks, he pays out, and he goes home satisfied. A satisfied man doesn't search a produce truck."

"And if he does search it?"

"Then he finds what I want him to find." Bo tapped the ledger. "Decoys. Broken amphora shards, ballast stones, the mundane sediment samples we're contractually obligated to deliver anyway. Crates labeled exactly the way his advance team photographed them last week — yes, I know about the photographs, Tomas told me after he killed the camera, and yes, I've adjusted my labeling accordingly. The real archive leaves in something so boring that boredom becomes camouflage. Duplicated three ways. One set ships to a locker I rented under a name that isn't mine. One goes with Kit. One stays on my person, because I am apparently too old to die and too stubborn to be robbed."

Lena stared out at the flat sea for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was rougher than usual.

"You've been planning this since before I decided anything."

"I've been planning since the day I found the manifest discrepancy," Bo said. "Planning is what I'm for. Deciding — that was always going to be yours. But Lena." Bo turned in the crate seat to face her fully, and let the gentleness show, the kind that lived underneath the pedantry like a keel under a hull. "You don't have to decide alone anymore. That was the mistake. Yours and his, both — carrying the whole weight in one skull until it cracks. We vote. Today. All of us. And whatever we decide, we execute together, or not at all."

* * *

They gathered at noon around the fire ring, the whole strange family of them: Tomas with his arms crossed like a man guarding a door, Priya still damp from the morning's survey dive, Kit sitting close enough to Bo that their shoulders touched, and Lena standing rather than sitting, which Bo noted and understood — captains stand when the news is bad.

Lena told them everything. The strongbox. Vane's private offer — double pay, the logbook, silence. Her father's final entries, read aloud in a voice that only cracked once, on the word *arranged*. And then her own failure: that she'd carried it alone for two days, weighing their paychecks against the truth like a woman counting coins in the dark.

"I should have brought it to you the same night," she finished. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking for a vote."

The silence went on long enough that Bo heard the generator change pitch.

Tomas spoke first. "Finish honest and broke, or take his money and live with it. Those were the options I gave you on the beach." He unfolded his arms. "I was wrong. There's a third option. Take his money *and* the truth. Let him pay us to bury it, and then refuse to bury it. That's not stealing. That's invoicing."

Priya laughed — a short, sharp sound with no humor in it, only relief. "He contracts crews to own them. Fine. We contract ourselves to deceive him. Same terms, reversed direction."

Kit leaned forward. "I drive the produce truck."

Everyone turned.

"You said it last night," Kit said to Bo, chin up, daring anyone to call it reckless. "Boring wins. Nobody searches a seventeen-year-old in a truck full of yams. And I already ran the route to the records office once. I know the checkpoints. I know which guard waves you through if you complain about the heat."

Bo felt something in the chest that wasn't quite pride and wasn't quite fear and was probably both wearing each other's clothes. "You'll have company," Bo said. "I ride shotgun. If they stop us, I do the talking. I've been boring authority figures into submission since before you were born."

Lena looked around the circle — at each of them, one by one, the way a diver checks gauges — and Bo saw the loneliness go out of her shoulders like water finding its level.

"All in favor of Bo's plan," Lena said. "Document everything, duplicate the archive, let Vane believe he's won until the last truck leaves."

Five hands. No hesitation, not even from Tomas, who hesitated professionally, on principle.

"Then we work," Lena said. "Three and a half days. Bo runs the archive. Tomas runs the decoys. Priya and I run the last dives. Kit—" she paused, and something almost like a smile touched her face, "—Kit learns to drive a produce truck like a man who wants to get home before the yams spoil."

* * *

The next two days passed in a rhythm Bo could only describe as liturgical. Dive at dawn, log at midmorning, photograph through the heat of the afternoon, pack through the dusk. Bo documented with a fervor that surprised even Bo — every artifact numbered, every number photographed beside a dated slate, every slate cross-referenced in triplicate. The archive grew fat and orderly, a fortress built of receipts.

Vane's man came by twice, both times smiling, both times looking at the crates stacked for loading. Both times Bo made a point of being visible at the table, annotating manifests with theatrical tedium, letting the man's eyes slide across pages of sediment analysis and broken pottery counts. Boredom, deployed correctly, was a smoke screen no camera could cut through.

On the second evening, Priya surfaced from the last recovery dive with a sealed waterproof case held against her chest like an infant, and Lena surfaced sixty seconds later looking like a woman who had argued with the sea and won on points. Bo met them at the ladder with towels and, in the case, the final pieces: her father's personal effects, and the scuttling mechanism itself, corroded but legible, its origin stamped in characters that needed no translation.

Bo photographed it all before the sun left the sky. Then, by lamplight, made the third full duplicate, wrapped it in oilcloth, and sealed it into the false bottom of a chart tube that would never leave Bo's shoulder until it reached the museum's hands.

"Three sets," Bo said to the lamplight, to the sea, to a dead diver who had once shared a mess table with Bo on a different coast, in a different life. "One for the record. One for the road. One for whoever's brave enough to print it."

* * *

The last night, the camp was packed and silent, trucks fueled and staged, and Bo sat outside the command tent with tea going cold, watching Kit check the produce truck's tie-downs for the fourth time.

"Nervous?" Bo asked.

"No," Kit said, which was the most nervous possible answer, and they both knew it, and both let it stand.

Bo looked down the darkened beach at the black rocks, at the water keeping its secrets, at the camp that had been grief and fracture and argument and was now, improbably, a machine pointed at a single purpose. Tomorrow at dawn, Vane would walk this site and see exactly what he expected to see: a beaten crew, compliant crates, his money well spent. He would toast Lena's professionalism. He would pay out. And he would never notice that the most dangerous thing on this beach was a folding table, a ledger, and a person everyone had underestimated for thirty-one years.

"Sleep," Bo told Kit. "Tomorrow we let a very powerful man believe a lie. It's the kindest thing anyone's done for him in years."

Across the water, far out where the black rocks broke the swell, something glinted once in the moonlight — a boat, maybe, or a buoy, or nothing at all. Bo watched it for a long time, and did not like the shape of it, and wrote the sighting in the margin of the ledger anyway, dated and timed, because margins were where trouble announced itself first.

Dawn was four hours away. The countdown had begun.
