## 18. The Last Dive

Priya had logged four hundred and eleven dives in her life, and she could tell you the number of the one that mattered before she ever hit the water.

Four hundred and twelve.

She said it to herself like a talisman while she checked her rig for the third time, because numbers were what she trusted when her hands wanted to shake. Numbers didn't lie. Pressure gauges didn't lie. The depth sounder reading thirty-eight meters over the wreck site didn't lie, even though everything else about this coast seemed built out of them — Lena's silences, Vane's smiles, the whole architecture of secrets they'd been stacking since the day the contract was signed.

"You're checking that reg again," Kit said from the gunwale, holding out a thermos cap of coffee like an offering. "It worked on dive nine. It'll work now."

"It worked on dive nine because I checked it four times." Priya drank the coffee anyway. It was terrible. Kit made it with too much powder and not enough patience, and it tasted like every morning of the last six weeks, and she was going to miss it terribly if any of them lived through today.

Below them, the sea lay flat and gray-green, deceptively calm, the way it always went quiet before it asked something of you. The *Meridian Star* — no, that wasn't right anymore; none of them called the wreck by its name except Bo, who insisted names were evidence — waited under thirty-eight meters of cold water with its broken spine and its cargo of lies, and somewhere inside it, in a cabin that had been Lena's father's, were the last two things that mattered: his personal effects, and the scuttling mechanism itself, the physical proof that the ship hadn't died in a storm but had been murdered.

"Current's slack," Tomas reported from the wheelhouse, squinting at the water. "You've got maybe ninety minutes of good visibility before the tide turns. After that, don't be down there."

"After that we won't be down there," Priya said.

Lena came up from below decks already half-suited, her wetsuit rolled to the waist, her hair tied back so tight it looked like it hurt. She'd been like this for three days — since the logbook, since the night Priya had found her in the archive tent at two in the morning with her father's handwriting spread out around her like a crime scene. Since grief had finished transmuting into whatever it was now, this hard bright thing behind her eyes that scared Priya more than the wreck did.

Lena had stopped being careful. That was the problem. Careful divers lived long lives. Lena was diving like a woman settling accounts.

"Buddy check," Priya said, and made herself say it again when Lena started to wave her off. "*Buddy check.*"

Lena submitted. Priya ran the line down her: tank, valve, BCD, weights, releases, regulator, alternate. Lena's gear was immaculate. Lena's gear was always immaculate. It was everything above the waterline that was coming apart.

"Your turn," Priya said, and stood still while Lena's hands moved over her rig, brisk and impersonal, and tried not to think about how the last time two people on this crew had run a buddy check together, it had been during the storm, with Kit screaming into the radio and the winch groaning and forty meters of black water trying to keep what it had taken.

"We go down the anchor line," Lena said. "Straight to the cargo hold breach. I marked the mechanism housing yesterday — frame forty-two, portside rib. Then the captain's cabin."

"The cabin first," Priya said. "Shallowest point, easiest exit. We do the hard thing second, when we're still fresh."

For a moment Priya thought Lena would argue. She watched the argument assemble itself behind Lena's eyes — the need to get to the proof, to get to the truth, to get to him, all of it compressed into a woman who'd been holding her breath for five years — and then, slowly, it let go.

"Cabin first," Lena agreed.

* * *

The descent was a slow fall through green light, the anchor line humming faintly under their grip, and Priya let the water take her the way she always did — the pressure settling on her chest like a hand, the world narrowing to the circle of her mask and the shape of Lena beside her, kicking steady, exhaling in long silver threads.

At twenty meters the wreck rose up to meet them.

She'd been down eleven times now and it still caught her, the sheer *presence* of it — a hundred and sixty feet of steel lying on its side in the sand, masts snapped, hull furred with soft white coral, the whole dead animal of it colonized by life. Fish moved through the corridors where sailors had walked. Light fell through the torn deck plating in slanted columns, dust and plankton turning in it like snow.

Priya had dived wrecks that felt like graves and wrecks that felt like museums. This one felt like a courtroom. Everything here was testimony. Every rivet, every fracture, every object resting where it had fallen or been placed — the sea had kept the record meticulously, patiently, for five years, waiting for someone to come read it.

They swam the length of the hull to the superstructure, and there it was: the doorway to the captain's cabin, warped open by the sinking, framed in coral like a mouth telling a story.

Lena paused at the threshold.

Priya saw her hand rise — not toward her gauge, not toward her light, but flat against the doorframe, the way you touch a wall of a house you grew up in. She held there for three full breaths. Bubbles rose from her reg in a thin, unsteady stream.

Then she went in.

The cabin was small, silted, intimate. A desk bolted to the floor, drawers long since emptied by time or salvage. A bunk frame. A shelf with nothing on it but a film of sediment and one unbroken glass tumbler, impossibly, standing upright, as if the sea had decided some things deserved dignity.

And there — wedged behind the desk where the advance survey photos had shown a shadow — a canvas satchel, rotted nearly through, its contents fused into a single mass of leather and corrosion.

Lena knelt in the silt in front of it and did not move.

Priya gave her a minute. She checked her own gauge — 210 bar, fine — and Lena's, and the exit, and the light, which was beginning its long afternoon lean through the deck plates. Then she touched Lena's shoulder.

Lena lifted the satchel with both hands, gently, the way you lift something sleeping.

Inside, visible through the split canvas: a waterproof case, military-grade, latched. And spilling from the case's corner, caught on the hinge, a laminated card — a diver's certification card, the photo faded to sepia, the name still legible after five years in salt water.

*Dunmore.*

Priya watched Lena read her father's name underwater, in his own cabin, in the place where he had died, and she understood suddenly that everything Lena had done since the wire arrived — every mile, every signature, every secret — had been a long approach toward this exact square meter of ocean floor.

Lena tucked the case into her mesh bag. Her movements were precise. Her eyes behind the mask were wet, and the water made it impossible to tell, and both of them pretended that was true.

* * *

Frame forty-two, portside rib. The mechanism housing.

Getting to it meant going *into* the wreck — through the cargo hold breach, along a corridor of collapsed decking, into a section where the hull plates had folded inward like a fist closing. Priya went first, running the guideline off her reel, clipping it every two meters because this was the part of the dive where the sea started collecting debts.

The space beyond the breach was a cathedral of twisted steel. Their lights swept across rib frames arched overhead, across the pale drift of silt, across — there — the housing.

It was smaller than Priya had expected. A bracket assembly bolted to the inner hull, and mounted in it, fused by five years of corrosion into the surrounding steel, the remains of a device about the size of a shoebox. Scuttling charge. Military pattern. She could see the stamped serial plate even through the marine growth, and next to it, unmistakable, deliberate, the placement marks — drill holes, mounting bolts, work done by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and had done it *before* the ship ever left harbor.

Not storm damage. Not accident. Murder, bolted to the hull with machine screws.

"Photograph everything," Lena's voice said in her earpiece, tight through the comm. "Every angle. Serial plate first."

Priya shot the plate, the mountings, the drill holes, wide shots and macro, her strobes firing silent white pulses into the dark. Then she pulled the contingency kit from her thigh pocket — the cutting tools, the sample vials — and began the delicate, horrible work of freeing a section of the mechanism from its cradle of rust.

That was when her regulator coughed.

It was a small sound. A hitch in the airflow, a stutter of resistance on the inhale, gone almost before she registered it. Four hundred and eleven dives taught you to hear your own equipment the way you hear your own heartbeat, and hers had just skipped.

She exhaled, inhaled again. The air came — but wrong. Thin. Grainy. Like breathing through gauze.

She checked her gauge. Two hundred bar. Full flow indicated. The needle lied serenely.

"Priya?" Lena's voice. "Status?"

"Reg's failing," Priya said, and heard how calm she sounded, and was proud of that, distantly, the way you're proud of a stranger. "Intermittent. I can breathe but it's degrading."

"Switch to your octopus."

"My octo is on the same first stage." Which meant the same failure, waiting. "I'm switching to my backup bottle."

The pony bottle hung at her hip — three liters of grace, maybe fifteen minutes of working time at this depth, less if she exerted. She clipped to the guideline, shed her primary reg, and took the first breath off the backup.

Clean air. Cold and honest. She almost laughed.

"Lena. Listen to me. We have maybe twelve minutes. You take the samples and the plate. I'll shoot the last angles. Then we are *leaving*, and we are leaving on schedule, and if you argue with me I will physically sit on you at forty meters."

A pause on the comm. Then, quietly: "Copy."

They worked. Priya photographed with one hand and steadied herself with the other while Lena cut free the sample section and sealed it in a vial, and the whole time Priya counted her breaths off the pony bottle like coins from a purse that was emptying. Eight minutes. Six. The light through the deck plates had gone amber and long.

They were ten meters from the breach when the silt moved.

It wasn't an explosion, nothing so dramatic. Just a shift — a groan through the hull structure as the tide turned outside and the wreck flexed on its bed of sand, and a section of collapsed decking overhead let go a sheet of plating that had been balanced there for five years, waiting.

It came down across the corridor behind them. Not on them — Priya would replay that mercy for weeks — but across their exit, a wall of steel and stirred silt, and the guideline pinned beneath it.

The world went brown. Total, boiling brown, visibility zero, their lights useless, reflecting back at them like headlights in fog.

Priya's heart slammed. She forced three slow breaths off the pony bottle. Five minutes, maybe. Less.

"Lena." She keyed the comm. "Talk to me. Where are you?"

"Right side of the corridor. I've got the line." A pause, the sound of breathing, of metal shifting. "It's pinned. I can't clear it. Priya, I can't clear it and I can't see."

"Then we go without the line. Hand on hand. I know this corridor — eleven dives, I know every rib. Stay on my fin, follow my shoulder, and do not let go."

"I'm not letting go."

"No," Priya said. "You're not. That's the whole deal."

She found Lena's wrist in the brown dark and pressed it against her own shoulder strap, and Lena locked on, and Priya began to swim.

Blind navigation is a faith-based activity. You commit your body to a memory of space and you trust the memory more than the panic. Left past the rib cluster. Down through the gap where the deck plate had buckled — she remembered it scraping her tank on dive seven, remembered cursing it, blessed it now. The silt was thick as smoke, and her air was counting down, and somewhere behind her Lena breathed in the rhythm Priya set, matching her, trusting her completely, and Priya thought: *this is what she couldn't do alone. This is why you don't dive grief solo.*

Her gauge read fifty bar on the pony when her glove touched the breach frame.

Thirty when they cleared the hull into open water.

Open water — blue-gray, real, moving — and the anchor line rising above them like a rope thrown down a well, and Priya grabbed it and looked back for Lena and Lena wasn't there.

The silt cloud was billowing out of the breach behind her, and Lena was at its edge, stopped, turned back toward the wreck, toward the cabin, toward the dark, and her hands were empty at her sides and her body was angled *inward*, and Priya understood with a cold drop through her stomach that some part of Lena Dunmore was still in that corridor, still kneeling in front of a rotted satchel, still not ready to leave her father twice.

"*Lena.*" Priya's voice cracked across the comm. "Up. Now. That's an order from your dive master and your friend and the only person between you and becoming a story Bo tells for the rest of her life."

Lena turned. For a moment her face behind the mask was the loneliest thing Priya had ever seen — five years of distance traveled collapsing into a single moment of choice — and then she kicked, once, twice, and her hand closed on the anchor line, and they ascended.

Slowly. Three-meter stops, safety stop at five, the whole liturgy of survival performed correctly because Priya's voice never stopped giving the steps, and Lena followed each one like a penitent, like a woman being walked out of a burning building she'd wanted to stay in.

The surface broke over them at 4:47 in the afternoon, gold and loud and unbearably alive.

Kit's face appeared over the gunwale, white with fear, hauling at their tanks before they'd even reached the ladder. Tomas lifted Lena aboard like she weighed nothing. Priya came up last, spat her reg, and lay on the deck breathing the ordinary, miraculous, unfiltered air.

Lena sat hunched over the mesh bag, the waterproof case in her lap, her father's name under her thumb. She was shaking. She didn't try to hide it.

"He was certified advanced open water," she said, to no one, to everyone. "He never told me. He used to say the sea was my thing. He was *certified*, and he never—" Her voice failed.

Priya crawled over and put her forehead against Lena's shoulder, the way you comfort a sister, and stayed there until the shaking slowed.

Above them, the sun leaned west. Below them, thirty-eight meters down, the wreck held what remained — the sample vial, the photographs, the mechanism with Vane's company stamped into its bones.

And on the horizon, small and getting smaller, a boat flying Vane's colors was heading out to inspect the site at first light.

Tomas saw it too. He said nothing. He just turned the wheel and pointed the bow home, fast.
