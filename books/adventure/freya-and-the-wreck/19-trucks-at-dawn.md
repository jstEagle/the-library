## 19. Trucks at Dawn

Tomas had built engines in the dark before. He'd rebuilt a generator by lantern light in the Atacama with sand grinding between his teeth and Priya swearing at him in two languages. But he had never loaded out an entire expedition while lying to a man's face, and he found that the lying took more engineering than anything with moving parts.

Four-thirty in the morning, and the camp was already awake without being lit. That had been Bo's first rule: no floodlights tonight. Work by headlamp and work slow. Light draws eyes, and Vane's eyes were everywhere — the man himself would arrive at seven to inspect the site, but his watchers had been rotating through the ridge line all week, and Tomas had stopped pretending not to see them.

He stood at the tailgate of the first flatbed and ran his hands along a crate that weighed almost nothing and looked like it weighed everything. Bo's masterpiece. Pine planks, salt-stained on purpose, stenciled with the site designation in Bo's careful archival hand: *FRW-041 / ARTIFACTS / FRAGILE*. Inside, wrapped in real conservation foam they could ill afford to waste, sat forty kilos of beach rock and broken ballast stone. It even smelled right — Bo had soaked the packing straw in seawater for three days so the crates would carry that low-tide reek of iron and kelp that clung to everything they'd pulled off the wreck. The devil, Tomas thought, was not in the details. The devil *was* the details, and Bo had made a career of shaking his hand.

"You're smiling," Kit said, materializing out of the dark with a crate strap over one shoulder like a bandolier.

"I'm worried."

"Same thing on your face." Kit dropped the strap into the truck bed and started ratcheting it down with hands that had gotten strong over these months, calloused across the palms. Seventeen years old and already knew how to read a tension gauge better than some engineers twice that age. "Priya's up. Lena's up. Bo hasn't slept."

"Bo never sleeps before a load-out. It's how we know Bo's still alive."

Kit laughed — too loud, then caught it, hand over mouth, glancing at the ridge. The laugh died there. That was the thing about this morning that kept sitting wrong in Tomas's chest: they'd all become quiet people overnight. A crew that argued about everything, that sang badly around the fire, that filled silence like it was a hole in a hull — now they moved through their own camp like thieves in someone else's house.

Because they were, he supposed. Thieves in Vane's house. Except nothing here was Vane's, not really, not honestly — which was the whole point, and the whole problem.

* * *

The shell game had been Bo's design, laid out three nights ago on the mess table with salt shakers and tin cups, and it was beautiful in the way only desperate plans are beautiful.

Three vehicles left this morning. The first flatbed carried twelve decoy crates — rock, slag, and a handful of genuinely worthless artifacts, all documented in a manifest that matched, item for item, what Vane expected them to have recovered. The second truck carried the real equipment: winches, compressors, dive gear, tools. Boring. Uninteresting. Trucks full of machinery make men like Vane sleepy.

And the third vehicle —

Tomas looked over at it now, parked at the edge of camp where the track met the coast road. A produce truck. An actual produce truck, borrowed from the fisherman two coves down whose nets Vane's advance team had fouled last month and who owed the crew a favor he insisted on repaying. It smelled of onions and diesel and the sea, and its bed was lined with empty vegetable crates, and beneath a false floor that Tomas had built yesterday afternoon while pretending to service the suspension, it held every scrap of the true archive. Priya's photographs. Bo's ledgers and cross-references. The hull surveys. The scuttling-charge residue analysis. And the sealed strongbox itself, stamped with Vane's company mark, packed in foam like the most valuable onion in the southern hemisphere.

Behind its wheel, Kit was checking tire pressure for the fourth time.

"You know I can drive," Kit said, when Tomas came over. Not defensive. Just stating it, the way you state a load rating.

"I know you can drive."

"And you know I'm the only one who can do this. If any of you get pulled over, Vane's people know your faces. They don't know mine. I'm the runner. This is literally my job description."

Tomas leaned against the cab and looked down the coast road, gray-blue in the pre-dawn, running north toward the regional highway and, eleven hours from now, a bus depot where a shipping clerk with loose morals and a prepaid envelope would send a certain crate to a certain address that existed on no manifest anywhere. "I know all of it," he said. "Knowing it doesn't make standing here easier."

Kit studied him. In the half-dark the kid's face was all angles and stubbornness. "You're going to give me the speech."

"What speech?"

"The one where you say 'if anything goes wrong, don't be a hero, just let them take it and drive away.' And then I'm going to nod, and then we're both going to know I'm lying."

Tomas laughed despite everything — a short, rough sound. "Something like that."

"So skip it." Kit held out a hand. "Tell me something true instead."

Tomas took the hand. Small, calloused, steady as a torque wrench. "You've been family since the day you showed up stealing our fuel," he said. "Come back. That's the whole speech. Come back."

Kit's throat worked once. "Yeah," the kid said. "Okay. Yeah."

* * *

By six, the loading was done, and it was done well, which was the only prayer Tomas had. Every strap true, every weight distributed, every crate logged in duplicate — one set for Vane's inspector, one set that lived inside Bo's shirt against his ribs. The decoys sat exactly where a suspicious man would look for them: visible, accessible, properly labeled. Nothing hidden except the one thing that mattered, hidden in the most boring vehicle on the coast.

Lena walked the line of trucks the way she walked a wreck site — slow, methodical, touching things. She'd slept maybe two hours; Tomas could see it in her, could see the whole weight of the last weeks riding her shoulders. She stopped at the produce truck last, and for a moment she and Kit just looked at each other through the open window, and something passed between them that Tomas didn't try to read. Some debts didn't need witnesses.

"Roads are clear till the junction," Lena said. "After that you're on your own. Radio silence after the turnoff — we burn the channel schedule tonight."

"Burn them," Kit said. "I'll be eating lunch like a normal person hauling vegetables like a normal person."

"You hate vegetables."

"I'll suffer."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. Then she stepped back and did the thing Tomas had watched her do at every dig since he'd joined her: she raised one hand, flat, and brought it down once — the diver's signal, *descend*, repurposed somewhere along the way into *go*, into *it's time*, into whatever word people like them used instead of goodbye.

The flatbeds went first, rumbling out onto the coast road in the gray light. Then the produce truck, Kit's arm out the window, fingers lifted in a lazy wave that fooled absolutely no one who knew the kid, least of all Tomas, who watched until the taillights swallowed the curve and felt something in his chest unclench and immediately re-clench tighter.

Now came the hard part. Now came the waiting.

* * *

Marcus Vane arrived at seven-ten, twenty minutes early, because men like him were always early — punctuality as a weapon, a way of catching you mid-sentence. His black SUV crunched up the track trailing dust, and behind it a second vehicle with two of his people, including the photographer, the one whose camera Tomas had quietly sabotaged weeks ago. The man gave Tomas a flat look as he passed. Tomas gave him back the pleasant, vacant expression of a man thinking entirely about torque specifications.

Vane stepped out in linen the color of bone, pressed despite the road, and surveyed the camp like a landlord at the end of a tenancy. Tomas watched Lena go to meet him and marveled, again, at her spine. She stood in front of the man who had — if the logbook told the truth, and every shard of evidence said it did — murdered her father, and she shook his hand.

"Mr. Vane. You're ahead of schedule."

"Time is the one asset I refuse to waste, Ms. Dunmore." Vane's voice carried easily; he was a man accustomed to being heard. "Dry season ends in four days. I trust the site is closed out properly?"

"Closed out. Logged. Manifested." Lena handed him the folder without being asked. "Everything's cataloged per the contract. Your inspector can verify against our duplicates."

"Oh, I intend to." Vane opened the folder right there, standing in the trampled grass, and began to read.

Tomas drifted toward the equipment truck and started coiling hose that was already coiled. It gave him a reason to be near, ears open, hands busy. Around him the camp performed its final act: Priya breaking down the dive station with brisk efficiency, Bo hovering near the artifact crates with the anxious fussiness of a curator, all of it staged, all of it real enough to pass any inspection because — and this was Bo's genius — almost none of it was false. The work had been done. The artifacts in those crates had come out of that wreck. Only the meaning had been stolen away in the night, hidden under onions, rolling north at fifty kilometers an hour.

Vane read for a long time. Then he walked the crates. He ran a gloved finger along the stenciling on FRW-041. He asked to see the strongbox recovery location marked on the site plan, and Lena showed him, and her finger did not tremble, and Tomas fell a little bit further under the spell of a woman he'd followed across three continents without ever quite being able to explain why.

"The condition is remarkable," Vane said. "Better than the survey suggested."

"Deep water, cold, low oxygen," Priya said from the dive station, not looking up. "She preserved herself."

"Mmm." Vane turned a page. "And the personal effects? The crew effects?"

"Recovered. Cataloged. Crate six." Lena nodded toward it. "Mostly degraded. Leather and paper don't survive thirty years in salt water, whatever the novels say."

"No," Vane agreed softly. "They don't."

For one second — one long, arterial second — the whole morning balanced on that sentence. Tomas's hands went still on the hose. Beside him, Bo became very interested in a clipboard. Because they both knew what Vane couldn't: that the papers *had* survived, or enough of them, sealed in the strongbox's dry core, and that the most important of them was at this moment doing eighty on a highway in a false floor under a teenager and forty kilos of onions.

But Vane only smiled — thin, satisfied, a man confirming his own expectations — and closed the folder. "Your paperwork is immaculate, Ms. Dunmore. I confess I wondered." He let that hang, the old accusation dressed as a compliment. "My office will release final payment on delivery of the cargo to the port warehouse. Standard terms."

"Standard terms," Lena echoed.

Vane looked out at the wreck site one last time — the black rocks, the flat sea, the mooring buoy they'd leave behind — and something moved across his face that wasn't satisfaction. Something older. Tomas had spent enough years around guilty men to recognize the weather. Then it was gone, banked like a fire, and Vane was all business again, shaking hands all around, praising the crew's professionalism in a tone that managed to insult everyone equally.

At the SUV door he paused. "Ms. Dunmore. A pleasure doing honest business with you at last."

Lena's face did nothing at all. "Drive safe, Mr. Vane. These roads are unforgiving."

The convoy pulled away south, toward the port, toward the warehouse where decoy crates full of beach rock would sit under guard until Vane's people opened them — tomorrow, next week, and found exactly the nothing they'd been promised, and never think to ask what else had driven out of this camp at dawn.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a full minute after the dust settled.

Then Priya threw a wrench into the tool box — deliberately, loudly, the way she celebrated — and Bo sat down abruptly on a crate and put both hands over his face, and Lena stood very still looking north down the empty road, and Tomas felt it hit him all at once, the way relief always did, sideways and hard: they'd done it. They'd actually done it. The archive was out. The kid was out. Vane had inspected the site and found precisely the obedient, broken, bought-and-paid-for crew he'd expected, and he'd driven away satisfied, and satisfaction was the most dangerous thing anyone had ever handed him.

"He bought it," Priya said. "He actually bought it."

"He bought what we sold him," Bo said, lowering the hands. "Which is not the same thing, and we should not celebrate until—"

"We're celebrating," Tomas said. "We can be terrified and celebrating. Both engines running. That's how we got here."

Lena finally turned from the road. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet, and she was smiling like a woman walking out of deep water into sunlight. "Radio silence starts now," she said, voice rough. "We break camp. Everything goes on the trucks. We're gone from this coast within the hour, and nobody looks back."

"You look back," Priya said gently.

"Yeah," Lena said. "Once."

So they broke camp fast and clean, and when the last truck rolled onto the coast road, Tomas drove, and Lena rode shotgun, and at the curve where the wreck site dropped out of the mirrors she looked back, once, the way she'd promised — a long look, a settling of accounts — and then faced forward and didn't again.

They were forty minutes north, windows down, salt wind in the cab, when the first radio crackled — a public band, a fisherman's chatter channel, nothing scheduled, nothing theirs. Tomas almost shut it off. Then the voice resolved, young and breathless and trying very hard to sound bored:

*"—anyone on this channel, this is Green Basket Produce outbound, we've got a situation at the junction checkpoint, over—"*

Tomas's hands went white on the wheel. Beside him, Lena came forward slowly out of her seat like a diver rising to something on the surface, and neither of them said a word, because Kit was not supposed to be on the radio, Kit was supposed to be invisible, and something at the junction had gone wrong.
