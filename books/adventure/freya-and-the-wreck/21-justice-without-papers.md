## 21. Justice Without Papers

The shipping office in Port Calloway smelled of diesel and old paper, and Lena stood at the counter with a parcel under her arm that weighed more than any cargo she'd ever moved.

It was a wooden crate, forty centimeters square, wrapped in brown paper and tied with twine, and inside it was five years of her father's silence and three weeks of her crew's sleepless work: the duplicated archive. Bo's photographs of the scuttling residue, catalogued and cross-referenced. The manifest pages showing the offloaded cargo. Priya's dive logs with depth readings and hull-damage diagrams that no storm could explain. Copies of her father's final logbook entries, transcribed in Bo's careful hand beside the originals. And the note from the strongbox — *the sea keeps accounts* — photographed before Vane ever saw it, because Kit had thought to do that, because Kit thought of everything now.

The clerk, a woman with reading glasses on a chain and the patient eyes of someone who'd processed ten thousand parcels without asking what was in them, looked up.

"Destination?"

Lena slid the address across the counter. She'd written it out four times the night before, on four sheets, and burned three. The Maritime Heritage Museum in Greywater Bay — a salt-bleached building she'd visited once as a girl, when her father was still alive, when he'd pointed at a case of salvaged ship's bells and told her that objects didn't lie but people did. The curator there, an old man named Aldous Wren who had once tried to buy her father's compass and been refused, would know what to do with the truth when it arrived. He'd spent thirty years writing letters to maritime boards about wrecks nobody wanted examined. He was exactly the kind of stubborn, unfashionable, incorruptible old man that Marcus Vane had never bothered to buy, because men like Vane only bought things they considered valuable.

And a second parcel, already sealed, addressed to a journalist named Cora Ives at the *Coastal Ledger* — a woman who'd lost her column for two years after reporting on Vane's harbor interests and come back meaner and more careful, publishing now from a smaller paper where nobody owned her byline.

"Two parcels," Lena said. "Separate shipments. Different couriers if you have them."

The clerk raised an eyebrow over her glasses. "Paranoid?"

"Careful."

"Same thing, most days." She stamped the first label. "Return address?"

"No return address."

The clerk's pen hovered. Then she shrugged, wrote *anonymous* in the space, and stamped it anyway. "Happens more than you'd think. People sending truth around like contraband." She glanced up. "You want a receipt?"

"No."

"Most of them don't." She pushed the parcels toward the back room. "They'll go out on tomorrow's coastal run. Weather permitting."

Weather permitting. Lena almost laughed. Everything in her life had been weather permitting, tide permitting, season permitting. But this — this was done. Whatever happened next, the truth was already packed in paper and twine, and no one could sign it away or bury it or buy it back. Not Vane. Not her. It belonged to the mail now, and the mail was more honest than any court she'd ever heard of.

She walked out into the grey morning light and stood on the office steps, breathing like a woman who'd just set down something heavy. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake.

* * *

The diner on the coast road had a name painted on the window — *Marta's* — and a neon coffee pot in the glass that buzzed and flickered whether it was lit or not. They'd been coming here every evening since the trucks left the site, eating fried food off laminate tables while the money question hung over all of them like weather.

Because there wasn't any money. That was the thing none of them said out loud. Vane had paid out — Tomas had watched the transfer clear, watched it land in the expedition account — and then Lena had emptied it. Every credit. She'd wired it, in careful installments over two days, to a string of shell accounts Bo had built years ago for exactly this kind of contingency, and from those accounts it had gone out again in payments that looked like equipment leases, insurance premiums, port fees owed to entities that existed mostly on paper. By the time Vane's accountants noticed anything strange, the money would be indistinguishable from ordinary expedition costs, scattered across jurisdictions like sand in a current. And what remained after the scattering — enough, barely, to cover their debts and one month of wages — sat in an account under a name that wasn't theirs.

Vane thought he'd paid them. On paper, he had. In practice, they were walking away unpaid, unowned, and invisible, which was worth more than the money. Bo called it *negative profit*. Kit called it *the best bad deal ever*. Tomas hadn't called it anything; he'd just checked the math three times and then cooked everyone dinner without being asked, which was how Tomas voted.

Lena came through the door at seven, shaking rain off her jacket, and found the booth in the back already claimed. Kit had spread maps across half the table even though there was nothing left to plan. Priya sat with her boots up on the opposite bench, turning a water glass slowly, watching the light bend through it. Bo had a newspaper — an actual paper, bought from the rack by the register — folded to the shipping news, because Bo read the shipping news the way other people read horoscopes. Tomas occupied the end of the booth like furniture, a permanent installation, a plate of something fried in front of him that he was defending from Kit's fork by pure positional advantage.

"You're late," Kit said. "We ordered you the soup."

"I didn't order soup."

"Tomas ordered you the soup. We're a democracy about everything except your dinner."

Lena slid in next to Priya. The booth was warm and close and smelled like grease and rain, and she felt the tension of the shipping office drain out of her shoulders one degree at a time. "Mail went out," she said quietly.

Nobody reacted visibly. That was the discipline they'd learned — three weeks of performing normalcy under Vane's cameras had made them fluent in the language of small signals. Priya set her glass down. Bo turned a page. Kit reached for the ketchup.

"Both parcels?" Bo murmured, not looking up from the shipping news.

"Both. Coastal run leaves tomorrow morning."

"Then we eat soup," Bo said, "and wait."

Tomas pushed the bowl across the table to Lena. It was some kind of fish chowder, thick and unglamorous, and she ate it while the others argued about route options for the drive north — the coast road versus the inland pass, the inland pass being longer but drier, the coast road being shorter but subject to exactly the kind of unseasonal squalls that had nearly killed two of them a month ago. Kit argued for the coast road with the fervor of someone who'd earned the right to an opinion the hard way, standing alone at a winch line in a storm. Priya backed the inland route. Tomas, as always, cared only that whatever they chose, the truck's axle could survive it.

Lena let them argue. She listened to the voices of her people fill the warm little room, and she thought: *this is what I bought with his money.* Not the payday. This. The fact that they were all still here, still arguing about roads, still stealing each other's fries, still a crew.

The bell over the door jingled. A wet man in a delivery uniform came in, shook himself like a dog, and took a stool at the counter. Behind him, through the streaming glass, the coastal road gleamed black under the streetlights.

Nobody looked up. Nobody needed to. They all knew what was coming on the coastal run, and they all knew it wouldn't come fast enough to suit the waiting.

* * *

It took nine days.

Nine days of driving north in a loose convoy, of cheap motels and shared rooms, of Kit teaching Priya a card game that was either invented or stolen depending on which of them you asked, of Tomas rebuilding the winch motor in a parking lot because he couldn't stand idle machinery the way other men couldn't stand silence. Nine days of Lena checking the radio in the truck cab every morning, scanning frequencies, listening to news bulletins that talked about fish quotas and a bridge repair and nothing else.

On the ninth day, in a roadside diner two hundred miles up the coast that might as well have been Marta's, the world changed.

Bo saw it first. Bo always saw it first — that was the job, the gift, the curse. Bo had gone out to the wire rack by the register where the local papers hung in clips, and Lena watched from the booth as Bo's hands stopped moving. Watched Bo stand very still, the way Bo did when the archive spoke, when a manifest contradicted itself, when history left its fingerprints somewhere unexpected.

Bo carried the paper back to the booth like it was made of eggshell and laid it flat between the plates.

Front page. Above the fold. The headline ran in heavy black type:

**MARITIME MUSEUM UNVEILS EVIDENCE IN DECADES-OLD WRECK SCANDAL — CARGO FRAUD, DELIBERATE SINKING ALLEGED**

Below it, a photograph of the museum at Greywater Bay, and below that, the beginning of a story credited to Cora Ives of the *Coastal Ledger*, a name that meant nothing to the waitress refilling their coffees and everything to the four people frozen around a laminate table.

"Read it," Kit whispered. "Somebody read it out loud. I can't— my eyes are doing a weird thing."

So Bo read it. Bo's voice, level and archival, carried the words across the table while the diner went on around them — the grill hissing, a baby crying two booths down, the rain starting up against the windows again.

*Documents received anonymously last week indicate that the wreck of the freighter Meridian — lost with one life in waters off the southern coast eleven years ago — was not sunk by storm damage as officially recorded, but deliberately scuttled using commercial demolition charges after its insured cargo had been secretly offloaded. The evidence includes photographic documentation of charge residue recovered from the hull, cargo manifests demonstrating the fraudulent offload, and the personal dive log of the vessel's captain, whose death during the sinking has been ruled accidental three times...*

Three times. Three inquiries, three whitewashes, three official versions of her father's death, each one signed off by men whose names appeared, Bo had found, in Vane's orbit — port authorities, insurance assessors, a maritime board chairman who'd retired onto Vane's own company board within the year. Courts and commissions, all of them entangled, all of them reachable, all of them bought or frightened or simply tired. Which was why the archive hadn't gone to any of them. Documents sent to a corrupt process die in it. Documents sent to a museum and a reporter become public property, and public property is harder to sink than a freighter.

*The museum's curator, Aldous Wren, confirmed the materials' provenance has been independently verified by two marine archaeologists not affiliated with the original salvage operation. "I've been waiting thirty years for someone to send me the truth in the mail," Mr. Wren told this paper. "Whoever they are, they did it properly. Chain of custody, full documentation, duplicates. This is how it should have been done the first time."*

Priya's hand found Lena's under the table and gripped it hard.

*Marcus Vane, whose company held the insurance underwriting on the Meridian's cargo and whose advance team conducted the recent salvage excavation at the site, declined repeated requests for comment. Through a spokesperson, Vane Marine Holdings stated that it "welcomes any legitimate inquiry" and is "confident the historical record will be vindicated." However, the Ledger has learned that shares in Vane Marine fell fourteen percent on regional exchanges yesterday, and that the regional maritime board has announced an emergency session to revisit the Meridian findings.*

*Sources close to the investigation say further documents are expected.*

Kit made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed both hands over their mouth. Tomas put down his fork with exaggerated care, as though sudden movements might shatter the moment. Priya was crying openly and pretending she wasn't, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand and glaring at anyone who might comment.

Lena stared at the photograph of the museum. At the small figure standing on its steps — white-haired, straight-backed, holding the door open behind him like a man welcoming something home. Her father had never met Aldous Wren. But Wren had wanted his compass once, and her father had kept it, and now the museum had the log instead, and somehow that closed a circle her father had started eleven years ago on a black-rock coast with water coming in fast.

"He knew," Lena said quietly. "Dad knew what Vane was doing, and he was going to report it, and Vane sank him for it. And then Vane spent eleven years making sure the story stayed drowned." She looked around the table. "And we just undrowned it. With paperwork. No guns, no courts, no permission from anybody. Just — mail."

"Justice without papers," Bo said. "Well. Without *his* papers."

"The sea keeps accounts," Priya said softly. "He wrote that on the box, Kit. You photographed it before Vane saw it. That photograph is in the archive. Somewhere a curator is looking at it right now and smiling."

Tomas finally spoke, his voice low and gravel-sure. "Fourteen percent. In one day. By the time the board session finishes, there won't be a Vane Marine to hide behind. Men like him don't survive exposure. They survive silence. We ended the silence."

"And we're broke," Kit added, with unmistakable pride.

That broke the table open. Laughter, sudden and helpless, the kind that comes after long fear — Priya laughing into her coffee, Tomas's shoulders shaking, Bo chuckling in that dry rusted-hinge way, Kit cackling loud enough to turn heads. The waitress paused by their booth, took in the scene with professional indifference, and said, "Refills are free if you're celebrating something."

"We are," Lena said. "But we can't tell you what."

"Fine by me. Happens more than you'd think." And she topped them all up and moved on, and Lena thought: *that's twice someone's said that to me this month.* Maybe anonymous truth was more common than she'd believed. Maybe the world was full of people quietly mailing evidence to museums, and the difference was that most of them never got a booth full of misfits to sit with afterward.

She looked at her crew — her ragged, unpaid, unowned, impossible family — and felt something settle in her chest that she recognized, distantly, as peace. Not the peace of an ending. The peace of a heading.

"So," she said. "We've got maybe a month of wages, a truck with a rebuilt winch motor, and a reputation that's about to be either legendary or radioactive, depending on whether Vane figures out who mailed the packages."

"He won't," Bo said. "The chain has no links. I built it that way."

"He'll suspect," Priya said. "He'll suspect forever. That's better than proof. Let him lie awake wondering which one of us did it."

"He'll never suspect Kit rode the produce truck," Kit said. "I look completely innocent. It's my best quality."

"It's your only quality," Tomas said, and stole a fry off Kit's plate in retaliation for the soup incident, and the argument that followed was loud and stupid and wonderful.

Outside, the rain eased. The coastal road shone. Somewhere south of here, an old man in a salt-bleached museum was opening a case to arrange a dead captain's logbook under glass, and somewhere else a reporter was chasing the second source, and somewhere a man who had owned everything was learning what it cost to be owned by the truth.

Lena finished her coffee and looked at the map Kit had spread across the table, its edges soft from handling, its routes leading everywhere they hadn't been yet.

"North," she said. "I'm thinking north. Cold water. Nobody's secrets."

Kit grinned and uncapped a pen.
