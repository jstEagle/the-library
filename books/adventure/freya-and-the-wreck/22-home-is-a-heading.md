## 22. Home Is a Heading

Kit had learned to read maps before learning to read much else, and in the weeks since the trucks left the coast, Kit had come to understand that maps were never really about land. They were about people — where they'd been, what they were running from or toward, which roads they'd worn smooth with use and which ones they'd sworn off forever.

The map spread across the diner table now was one Kit had drawn by hand, because no printed chart showed what needed showing: not roads, but offers. Four contracts, four directions, each one inked in a different color borrowed from Bo's archive pens.

Blue: a survey job up north, cold water, good pay, six weeks.
Green: a coastal restoration contract two provinces over, unglamorous, steady.
Red: the long haul south, a rumored wreck in deep water that would take everything they had and maybe more.
And in plain pencil, at the bottom of the page, a fifth option Kit had added without being asked: *Stay put. Fix the truck. Breathe.*

The diner sat on the inland edge of a town none of them could name on a map three months ago, the kind of place with a ceiling fan that turned like it was thinking about it and coffee that tasted like it had been poured sometime last week. The morning news played on a mounted television above the counter, muted, captions crawling. Every few minutes the screen cut to footage of a maritime museum's new exhibit hall, a banner across its doors reading *THE VANE COLLECTION: RECOVERED AND RESTORED*. A journalist's byline had been on the front pages for eleven days straight. The word *fraud* appeared in headlines the way weather appears over mountains — inevitable, once you knew to look for it.

Marcus Vane had not been arrested. Not yet. But his company's stock had fallen off a cliff, two board members had resigned, and a parliamentary committee had announced an inquiry with the solemn tone of people who had just discovered a scandal was good for their careers. It wasn't justice with handcuffs. It was something slower and possibly stronger: justice with paperwork. Justice that couldn't be bought back, because it lived now in a thousand duplicate photographs and a museum's climate-controlled vaults and the public record, where even men like Vane eventually drowned.

Kit watched Lena watch the screen.

She sat at the head of the table with her hands wrapped around a mug she hadn't drunk from, and her face did the thing it always did now when the wreck came up — not flinching, exactly. Settling. Like a house adjusting to new foundations. Five years of running had ended on that black coast, and whatever Lena had carried away from it weighed less than what she'd arrived with, though Kit suspected she'd only figure out how much less somewhere down a road that hadn't been built yet.

"Turn it off," Tomas said, not unkindly. "We've seen it."

Priya reached up with the remote and the screen went dark mid-caption. In the sudden quiet, the fan creaked through another revolution.

"So," Lena said. She set the mug down and looked at the map, really looked at it, and Kit felt the moment arrive the way you feel a tide change — nothing visible moving, everything different. "Four contracts. One truck. Five of us." She slid the map to the center of the table. "I've picked every heading since I was nineteen years old. Every dig, every road, every yes and every no. That was fine when it was just me outrunning something." Her eyes came up, moved around the table — Tomas, Priya, Bo, Kit — and stayed. "It's not just me anymore. So we vote. All five votes count the same. Including mine. Especially mine."

Bo adjusted her glasses, which she did when she was pleased but didn't want anyone to know it. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain I'm tired of being the only compass," Lena said. "A crew that only goes where one person points isn't a crew. It's a convoy."

Tomas laughed, low and rough, and leaned forward to study the blue line. "Cold water means dry suits. Dry suits mean maintenance. Maintenance means me, happy, in a heated shop for a week instead of freezing in a wetsuit."

"You'd be miserable either way," Priya said. "You're happiest when you have something to complain about."

"I contain multitudes," Tomas said, "and all of them complain."

Kit traced the red line south with one finger. Deep water. Everything they had. The kind of job that made legends or made graves, and sometimes both in the same season. Kit's stomach did the thing it did around big risks — not fear, exactly. Hunger wearing fear's coat.

* * *

They argued for two hours, and it was the best argument of Kit's life.

Not because anyone won — nobody won, that was the point — but because of how it went. When Lena used to decide things, the crew shaped their opinions around hers like water finding the low ground. Now the opinions came from everywhere. Priya wanted the southern wreck with a ferocity that surprised everyone; she talked about depth records and preservation conditions and a hull type nobody had surveyed intact, and her hands drew shapes in the air like she was already swimming it. Bo made the case for the green contract with a spreadsheet she'd apparently prepared in advance, because Bo prepared spreadsheets in advance for arguments she hadn't been invited to yet. Tomas wanted anywhere with a hardware store and a mechanic who spoke his language, which narrowed it to approximately nowhere.

And Kit — Kit argued for the north. Cold water, six weeks, good pay. Not because it was the best job. Because it was close to the regional records office where Kit had ridden through the night three months ago with forged-permit dread chewing a hole in the dark, and because there was a woman at that office named Aldous who had stamped their papers clean and told Kit, in a voice like gravel and kindness, *come back anytime, kid, the filing here is criminal.* Kit had thought about that offer more than Kit wanted to admit. There was something in the idea of a place that expected nothing from you except showing up.

"It's not about the filing," Kit said, and heard how young the voice sounded, and kept going anyway. "It's that I know the road. I've never known a road before. Everywhere we go, someone else has history with it. That stretch of highway, it'd be mine."

The table went quiet in the way tables do when somebody says something truer than they meant to.

Lena was looking at Kit with an expression Kit had never seen on her before and couldn't name until later, turning it over in the dark of the truck cab like a coin found on a beach. It was pride, Kit decided finally. Pride with grief dissolved into it, the way salt dissolves into water and stops being two things.

"That's a real reason," Lena said. "Better than most of mine ever were."

* * *

The vote took ninety seconds.

Red: two. Priya first, then — after a long pause and a look at the map that seemed to include distances no one else could see — Lena herself. Green: one, Bo, who shrugged and said stability built better archives. Blue: one, Kit. Stay put: Tomas, who raised his hand with great ceremony and said, "I want it noted I voted for happiness and was overruled by ambition, again, as is tradition."

Deadlock between red and everything else. Two rounds of runoff got them nowhere. Finally Bo, with the air of a judge delivering a sentence she'd rehearsed, proposed the compromise that had probably been sitting in her spreadsheet all along: the northern contract first — six weeks, funded, near the records office — and then south in the spring, if the rumor held and the crew still wanted it. Everyone got something. Nobody got everything. Which, Bo observed, was the definition of a family decision as opposed to a captain's order.

Lena called the vote. Five hands went up for Bo's plan, including Lena's own, and Kit watched something leave her shoulders — some old weight, some captain's burden she'd been carrying so long it had grown into the bone. She didn't smile, exactly. But the lines around her mouth rearranged themselves into something adjacent to smiling, and she said, "North, then. Somebody tell the truck."

"The truck," Tomas said gravely, "has opinions."

"The truck can file a grievance."

The truck, as it happened, was parked outside under a cottonwood, freshly repaired, running smoother than it had in two years. Kit had helped Tomas rebuild the carburetor last week, and there was a specific pride in machine-work that Kit was beginning to understand ran deeper than salvage pride: a wrecked ship gave up its secrets reluctantly, but an engine gave them up gladly, if you listened. Maybe that was the difference between the work they'd done and the work they were choosing. Some things you raised from the dead because you had to. Some things you fixed because you could.

* * *

They left the next morning, early, in the gray hour when the diner's sign still buzzed and the road east smelled like dust and rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Kit rode shotgun, which had become permanent somewhere in the last hundred miles of the escape from the coast and nobody had contested it. The map — the real one, the road atlas, dog-eared and coffee-ringed — lay open on Kit's knees, and Kit had already drawn the route in pencil: north and east, six hundred miles, past the records office, into country where the water ran cold and clear and held nothing but fish and honest mud.

Lena drove the way she always did, one wrist on the wheel, eyes doing that constant sweep — mirrors, horizon, mirrors — that Kit had once thought was paranoia and now understood was just how she saw the world. But the sweep was slower these days. Softer. She checked the mirrors like a habit instead of a hunt.

"You voted red," Kit said, somewhere past the second fuel stop. Not a question.

"I did."

"Why? You hate deep water right now. You told Priya the southern site was a coffin with delusions of grandeur."

Lena was quiet for a while. The road unspooled. Somewhere behind them, in the truck bed, Bo was cataloging the last of the duplicate archive — the copies they'd kept, the ones that belonged to no museum and no journalist, the private record of what five people had done when the law wouldn't. Kit could hear her humming.

"When my father died," Lena said finally, "I started measuring my life in miles. Distance from the wreck. Distance from him. Every dig was a way to put ocean between me and that coast, and every ocean failed, so I dug farther. Three continents of farther." Her thumb tapped the wheel. "Then we went back. And it turns out miles don't measure anything. You can drive ten thousand of them and be exactly where you started. You know what measures?"

"What?"

"People." She said it simply, like reporting a depth sounding. "How many you'd turn the truck around for. How many would turn it around for you. That's the whole instrument panel, Kit. Everything else is scenery."

Kit looked down at the atlas, at the penciled route, and understood suddenly why this trip felt different from every trip before it. All the other routes had been drawn in ink before the journey started — Lena's ink, Lena's heading, the whole crew rolling along inside a decision that had already been made. This one was pencil. This one could be erased, redrawn, argued over at every crossroads. This one belonged to all of them.

"You could have just voted us north," Kit said. "Saved us two hours of arguing."

"And stolen your reason?" Lena glanced over, and there it was again, that unnamed expression, settling into place. "You found a road that's yours. I wasn't going to be the one who paved over it."

* * *

Late afternoon, the coast came into view.

Not their coast — a different one, gentler, the cliffs lower and gold-green instead of black, the sea flat and bright as hammered tin. But it was the same ocean. It was always the same ocean; that was the thing about oceans, they kept all their accounts in one ledger. For a mile or two the highway ran along the top of the bluffs, and the whole crew went quiet in the way people go quiet in churches, except this church was older and asked for no donations.

Priya rolled down her window and let the salt air in. Bo stopped humming and looked out at the water with her glasses pushed up into her hair, seeing it, maybe, as the greatest archive there was — everything written down, nothing lost, nothing forgotten, patient past all human impatience. Tomas muttered something about corrosion and salt spray and the criminal negligence of driving a restored truck along a coastline, and everyone ignored him fondly.

And Lena — Lena looked at the sea the way Kit imagined you'd look at a grave you'd finally finished grieving. Not away. Not through. At. Full weight, full light, no flinch.

"Grieved, avenged, released," Bo said softly, from the back, as if reading it off a manifest.

"That's three words too many," Lena said. But she didn't argue the list.

The highway bent inland and the coast slid away behind them, unhurried, the way coasts do — staying on the horizon long after you'd swear it should be gone, keeping itself in the mirrors until the road finally, mercifully, turned. Kit watched it shrink in the side mirror: the gold-green bluffs, the bright tin sea, the whole geography of the worst year and the best one, folding itself down to a line, then a shimmer, then nothing but sky.

Nobody said anything. Nothing needed saying. The truck ran smooth, the map lay open to a page they'd chosen together, and six hundred miles north a cold clean coast waited with a contract and a records office and a road that belonged, at last, to somebody.

Kit picked up the pencil and wrote a single word at the top of the route, small, in the margin where the compass rose would go on a proper chart:

*Home.*

Not a place. A heading. And for the first time in five years — in Kit's five years, in Lena's fifteen, in however many it had been for the others — everybody aboard was pointed the same direction, and it was the direction they'd picked themselves.

The road rose gently ahead, curving north out of sight, and the truck climbed into it, and the ocean fell away behind them like a hand letting go.

Somewhere far south, in a museum with a banner over its doors, the truth sat in the light where anyone could read it. Somewhere farther than that, a man was learning what the sea keeps accounts of. And here, in a truck full of misfits on a road nobody owned, the future was pencil, not ink — erasable, arguable, theirs.

Kit smiled at the windshield, at the wide open nothing ahead, and realized that arrival didn't always look like stopping.

Sometimes it looked like this: motion, chosen; company, chosen; a heading, chosen — going home at seventy miles an hour, without arriving anywhere at all.
