## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The Gideon rose out of the dark like a drowned cathedral.

Marguerite Yarrow hung in the green water forty feet above her rail and let the current decide whether she'd descend today. It was a ritual she'd never admit to: the hanging, the waiting, the long moment when the wreck's silhouette sharpened from suggestion into certainty and her heart did something complicated that had nothing to do with fear of drowning.

Dawn came down through the water in slanted copper bars. Below her, the frigate lay heeled over on the sand shelf where she'd settled eleven years ago, masts snapped at the fighting tops, hull furred with weed, gunports gaping like the mouths of men who'd died mid-shout. The navy charts called it Wreck 7-Kilo. The harbor charts didn't call it anything at all. On the islands, people said *the one off Serrit Point* if they said anything, and then changed the subject.

Nobody named it. That was the arrangement.

Marguerite spat into her regulator's purge valve, checked her gauges one last time—air at two-forty bar, good—and kicked down.

* * *

She'd been diving the Gideon for six years, and in six years she'd learned its body the way you learn a lover's or an enemy's, which by now were much the same thing to her. She knew the quarterdeck rail still held its brass fittings because the coral had grown over them like a bandage. She knew the galley skylight had collapsed inward and you could drop through it into the pantry if you exhaled slow and turned your shoulders just so. She knew the stern cabin—the captain's country, the sealed door with the navy's wax stamp still ghosting under the corrosion—was not to be touched.

That was the other part of the arrangement. Brass yes. Truth no.

Today's work was amidships, port side, where a storm two winters back had peeled open a section of the lower deck like a tin of sardines. Storms were generous that way. The sea did the demolition; she did the collection. There were lamp mounts down there, and a signal lantern's housing, and if the tide had shifted the sand since last spring there might be a whole rack of instrument fittings from the chart room, all of it worth more per pound than the fish her crew could catch in a week.

She swam the length of the hull with her torch beam skating along planking gone soft as old bread, and she did not look at the sealed door, and she looked at it anyway, the way you press a bruise.

*Not today,* she told herself, and meant it, and hated how relieved she was to mean it.

The breach opened ahead of her, black against black. She clipped her lift bag to her harness, drew her pry bar, and went in.

* * *

Inside, the ship kept its own weather. Silt hung in the water like breath held for eleven years. Her torch found the chart room rack exactly where she'd hoped—brass fittings green with age but sound beneath, the kind of metal that cleaned up beautiful and sold honest. She worked methodically, wrenching each fitting free and stowing it in the mesh bag at her hip, and the work was good the way only tired work is good: hands busy, mind quiet, nothing required of her but the next bolt and the one after that.

This was the part she loved, if love was the word. Down here nobody owed anybody anything. Down here there was no ledger.

Her father had taught her to dive on a reef off Calloway Island when she was nine, his hand steadying her tank valve, his voice in her head even through the water: *Slow is smooth, Maggie. Smooth is safe.* He'd been a navy man then, before the navy stopped saying his name either. Eleven years ago he'd sailed on the Gideon as her sailing master, and eleven years ago the Gideon had come apart off Serrit Point in circumstances the inquiry described as *loss of hull integrity due to uncharted shoal water*, and her father had been listed among the dead, and the navy had paid her mother a pension for four years and then quietly stopped, citing administrative error, and no amount of letters had ever resurrected the error or the pension or the man.

Marguerite had been seventeen. She'd spent the years since learning wrecks the way other people learned scripture.

She filled the bag until it pulled at her hip, then paused, breathing hard through the regulator, and let herself drift up through the broken deck toward the light. From here she could see most of the gundeck stretching away into gloom, cannons still run out, still loaded maybe, pointed at an enemy who'd never existed. Somewhere aft, the sealed door. Somewhere below that, whatever the door was for.

The strongroom. Everyone who worked this coast knew the rumor: the Gideon carried a pay chest, a heavy one, bound for a fleet anchorage that never happened. The navy had searched for it twice with proper equipment and proper divers and had come away with nothing, and then had declared the site closed, and had posted a patrol schedule that any fool with a spyglass could read, and had made it very clear through harbormasters and customs men and once, memorably, through a pair of shore leave marines with truncheons, that the closure was permanent.

Which meant the strongroom was still down there. Which meant it was worth a fortune. Which meant Marguerite thought about it more often than she admitted, usually at night, usually when the boat's accounts were bad.

She pushed the thought down with the bag of brass and started up.

* * *

Halfway through her ascent hang, drifting at fifteen feet while the nitrogen bled out of her, she felt it.

The compass.

It lived in a oilskin pouch inside her suit, against her chest, where it had lived on every dive for six years—not for navigation, she had a proper wrist compass for that, but because taking it down felt like bringing him along. It had been her father's. It had come home to her in a box of personal effects the navy sent eight months after the sinking, wrapped in his spare shirt, and the accompanying letter said it had been recovered from the wreckage, though nobody could explain how a pocket compass survived a hull explosion, or why the letter's signature belonged to an officer whose name didn't appear in any fleet roster she could ever find.

She'd dived with it so long she'd stopped thinking about it. But some mornings, on the surface, checking their heading out of harbor, she'd notice it sitting wrong. Not broken-wrong. *Pointed*-wrong. Steady, deliberate, wrong.

She brought it out now, there in the green half-light, and held it flat on her palm.

The needle swung.

Not trembling. Not confused. It swept past north, past east, and settled pointing down-and-along, down toward the wreck beneath her fins, along the line of the hull, as if the needle were a finger laying itself against the ship's spine. As if it knew the way a body knows its own street.

Then, slowly, it swung back to north, innocent as anything.

Marguerite hung in the water with her heart going too fast for a woman at fifteen feet, watching a two-ounce piece of brass behave like something alive. She'd told herself for years it was tilt, or the iron in the wreck below, or her own eyes playing tricks in low light. All reasonable explanations. She was a salvage diver; she dealt in reasonable explanations the way a priest deals in forgiveness, professionally and without always believing.

But the needle hadn't wavered. It had *chosen*.

Above her, the surface rippled silver. Below her, the Gideon waited with its door shut. Between them, her father's compass pointed at both, and told her nothing except that everything she'd decided about the last eleven years might be built on sand.

She put it away. She finished her hang. She went up.

* * *

The *Kestrel* sat her mooring a cable's length off the wreck site, riding easy on the morning swell. Marguerite broke the surface beside the boarding ladder and Nell's face appeared over the rail, fourteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who had not been holding her breath for the last hour.

"Anything?" the girl asked.

"Brass." Marguerite handed up the mesh bag, heard the satisfying clatter of it landing on deck. "Good haul. Chart room fittings, mostly."

"That's not what I asked."

Marguerite grinned around her mouthpiece and climbed the ladder. "No boats," she said, answering the real question. "Nothing all morning?"

"Not a sail. Ilya says the patrol ran the northern channel at first light, same as always, and went on toward Cape Ferren." Nell lowered her voice with the gravity of a girl quoting scripture. "He says we have until the tide turns."

Ilya was never wrong about tides or patrols, which was fortunate, because he was wrong about almost everything else, chiefly the correct way to cook rice. Marguerite stripped her gear on deck with the economy of long habit while Nell hovered, wanting to be useful, and Tam emerged from the engine hatch wearing the expression of a man who has bad news and is deciding whether to eat it or serve it.

Tam served it. Tam always served it; it was why they kept him.

"We got a problem," Tam said.

"We have several," Marguerite said. "Rank them."

"The seawater pump's eating its own impeller. I can keep it alive another week, maybe ten days, if I baby it. After that we're diving on breath-hold and bailing with hats." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than Nell had been alive. "And the port fuel line's weeping. And—" he glanced at Nell, edited himself, "—and the other thing."

The other thing was money. The other thing was always money.

"How bad?" Marguerite asked, though she'd done the sums herself at midnight and knew.

"Bad enough that Harl Voss came by the harbor office yesterday asking after us. Asking friendly." Tam's mouth twisted. "Voss being friendly is worse than Voss being otherwise."

Harl Voss lent money the way the sea took ships: patiently, impersonally, and eventually. They owed him four months' interest on the pump they were currently losing, and he'd begun sending regards instead of notices, which among harbor people was the equivalent of a drawn knife laid politely on the table.

"I'll sell the brass in Calloway tomorrow," Marguerite said. "Chart room fittings clean up well. Should cover the interest and the impeller."

"And after that?"

"After that we find another wreck."

"There aren't any other wrecks, Marguerite. Not ones that pay. You know what's on this coast." He didn't say the name. Nobody said the name. "You know what's on this coast, and it's closed, and we've been picking its pockets for six years like it wouldn't notice."

"It hasn't noticed yet."

"It will." Tam looked out at the water, at the pale shape of the shoal beneath the morning glitter, and his voice dropped. "Every time you go down there I think about Denny."

The name landed the way it always landed, like a dropped tool on a steel deck. Nell went still. Even the wind seemed to lean away.

Denny Okafor had been theirs, once. Bosun's mate, laugh like a gull, hands that could splice wire cable in the dark. Four years ago, before Marguerite's time as master, before the vow, Denny had gone down the Gideon's forward hatch on a bet and a dare and a hunger for the pay chest everyone whispered about, alone, on air he shouldn't have trusted, in a corridor the charts didn't show. He'd come back up in pieces, three days later, when the tide shifted the silt. The crew had buried what they could on Calloway Island and sworn, all of them, hands on the rail, never to touch the Gideon again.

They'd lasted a year before hunger broke the vow down to a technicality: *we don't go inside.* Hull salvage only. Brass from the outside of the body, never the organs. Marguerite had ratified the compromise her first month as master, and had told herself it was pragmatism, and had known even then that someday the technicality would kill them too.

"Denny's not down there anymore," Marguerite said quietly. "We saw to that."

"He's down there," Tam said. "They're all down there. Every soul that ship took. And every time we scrape her rails I feel like we're robbing a grave to pay rent, and one day the grave's going to object."

Marguerite didn't answer. She looked at the water instead, at the place where the wreck slept, and felt the compass against her ribs inside her drying suit, cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket.

*I'll sell the brass,* she thought. *Pay Voss. Fix the pump. Keep going. Keep going. Keep—*

"Maggie." Nell touched her sleeve. "Boat. Coming out of the harbor channel. Under power, white hull, flying—" the girl squinted, then went pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear of the navy. "Flying private colors. Fancy ones."

Marguerite raised the glass.

The vessel coming up the channel was a fast launch, varnished and immaculate, the kind of boat that cost more than the *Kestrel* would earn in a decade. At her wheel stood a woman in a white linen jacket, dark hair cropped short, one hand raised in a salute that managed to be both mocking and perfectly correct.

Even at half a mile, even across eleven years and one ruined friendship, Marguerite knew the set of those shoulders.

"Odile Vasseur," she said.

Tam made a sound like a man swallowing a fishbone. "Vasseur? *Your* Odile?"

"She was never my Odile." Marguerite lowered the glass slowly. Her heart was doing the complicated thing again, and this time it had everything to do with dread. "Odile Vasseur doesn't come to a harbor unless there's money in it. And she doesn't come to *me* unless the money is dirty."

The launch curved toward the *Kestrel*, graceful as a knife entering water, and behind Marguerite the tide began, patient and indifferent, to turn.
