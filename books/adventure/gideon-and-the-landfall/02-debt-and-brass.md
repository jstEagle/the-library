## 2. Debt and Brass

Tam Voss counted the boat's sins by lamplight, and the lamplight was generous.

He lay flat on his back in the bilge of the *Kestrel's Grace*, cheek against cold iron, a stub of pencil behind his ear and a grease rag going gray in his fist. Above him the hull plating ticked as it cooled, that small metallic breathing every boat did after a hard day, and Tam had learned to read it the way other men read weather. Tonight it said: *you are in trouble.*

The list sat in his head, ordered by urgency, which was how Tam ordered everything. He was not a man for speeches or sentiment. He was a man for lists.

One. The port shaft bearing was shot. Not worn — *shot*, the babbitt gone to powder, the journal scoring scored itself. He could nurse it another week if he babied the throttle, and Marguerite would baby the throttle because Marguerite always listened to him about engines even when she ignored him about everything else. Two weeks if they were lucky. Three if the sea took pity, and the sea had never once taken pity on Tam Voss.

Two. The condenser wept. A slow leak, salt crusting green along the seam like the boat was growing lichen from the inside. Patchable. Barely. With solder he didn't have.

Three. The fuel pump gaskets were perished to the consistency of old cheese, and he'd been making new ones out of leather off an old boot, which worked, in the way that prayer works — you couldn't prove it did anything, but you felt better doing it.

Four. The hull zincs were eaten to lace. Sacrificial metal, the manuals called it. The *Kestrel's Grace* had sacrificed all she had and was now eating herself instead.

Tam slid out from under the engine, sat up too fast, and cracked his skull on the manifold. He swore with feeling, in three languages, none of them fluently, and pressed the rag to his scalp while the pain rang like a bell.

That was the state of things. That was the whole state of things, and there wasn't coin in the crew box to buy the zincs alone, let alone a bearing, let alone the sheet copper for the condenser. He knew the number without looking. He always knew the number. It was the one figure in his life that never moved in the right direction.

Forty-one crowns. Forty-one crowns and some coppers in a biscuit tin bolted under the chart table, because Tam trusted bolts more than banks.

He climbed out of the bilge into the engine room's amber gloom, wiped his hands on trousers that had passed beyond help years ago, and stood a moment with his palm flat against the engine block. People thought he loved machines because they were simple. They weren't simple. They were honest, which was different, and rarer. An engine never told you it would stay and then left on the morning tide.

His father had done that. Left on the morning tide. But that was not a thing Tam kept on any list.

* * *

Up top, the harbor was doing its evening arithmetic.

Saltcross Harbor at dusk was a bowl of black water and yellow lamps, masts scribbled against a bruised sky, and everywhere the sound of money changing hands reluctantly. Fish carts clattered over cobblestones. Gulls screamed their eternal grievance. And along the quay, moving from boat to boat like gulls with ledgers, came the creditors.

Tam watched them from the *Grace*'s deck while pretending to inspect a mooring line. There was Quill Harrow first, factor for the Chandlery Cooperative, a soft-bellied man with a voice like warm oil and eyes like a fishmonger's scale — everything weighed, nothing forgiven. Harrow stopped at the oyster boat two slips down, spoke briefly, patted its owner on the shoulder in a way that made the man flinch, and moved on.

Then there was the other one. The one Tam didn't have a name for yet, which bothered him more than he showed. Gray coat, gray hat, boots too good for Saltcross. He carried no ledger. He carried a walking stick he didn't seem to need, and he walked the quay like a man taking inventory of the whole harbor rather than any single debt. Twice today he'd paused near the *Kestrel's Grace*. Twice he'd looked at her lines the way a butcher looks at a beast — not at the animal, but at the cuts.

"Tam." Nell dropped onto the deck from the quay wall, all elbows and momentum, fourteen years old and built out of rope and appetite. She had a paper bag of fried something clutched to her chest like contraband. "There's a man asking about us."

"I saw him."

"He asked the bait-seller where we berth. He asked how many crew. He asked—" she paused, catching breath, "—he asked if the boat was mortgaged."

Tam looked at the gray coat, now drifting toward the harbor office. "What did the bait-seller say?"

"That we're poor and mean and nobody lends to us twice." Nell grinned. "Best review we've had all season."

Despite himself, Tam's mouth twitched. He held out a hand; she surrendered the bag; they split the fried whatever-it-was standing up, the way the crew ate most meals, food as fuel rather than occasion. Below them the water slapped the hull around the weeping condenser seam, patient as a creditor.

"Bearing's worse," Nell said, mouth full. She was sharp about sounds, Nell. She could hear a bad bearing through two decks. "She's singing."

"She's screaming. Singing is what boats do when they're happy." Tam crumpled the bag. "Don't tell Marguerite yet."

"You always say don't tell Marguerite yet."

"And I'm always right to say it. There's a sequence to these things. First I find out what it costs. Then I find out what we can sell. Then I tell her the difference between those numbers, gently, ideally after she's eaten."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a quartermaster. "We could sell the compass."

The words landed between them and neither moved.

Everyone on the *Grace* knew about the compass. It lived in Marguerite's pocket the way a heart lives in a chest — you didn't see it often, but you knew it was there, and you knew the boat didn't turn without it. It had come down to her from her father, brass-cased, heavy as sin, older than the navy that forbade anyone to speak the name of the wreck they all dove anyway. It was the last thing Elias Yarrow had owned that hadn't gone down with him.

"It would fetch," Tam said carefully. "A piece like that, with the provenance—"

"With the *tragedy*, you mean." Nell's grin had gone. "People pay extra for drowned men's things. Harrow told me. He said it like it was a good price."

"Harrow says everything like it's a good price. That's what makes him dangerous." Tam looked back toward the harbor office. The gray coat had gone inside. "Nobody's selling the compass. I just needed to know what we're not selling it for. Now I do. Put it away, Nell."

"It's already away. I only said it out loud."

"That's how it starts." He said it lightly, but he meant it. Debts started as sentences spoken out loud. *We could sell the compass.* Then it was *we should consider.* Then it was done, and someone had sold the last true thing aboard to pay for a bearing, and the boat ran smooth and meant nothing.

Footsteps on the gangplank. Bosun Ilya came aboard the way he did everything — heavily, deliberately, like a man setting each foot down on ground he intended to keep. He carried a crate of scrap brass on one shoulder, salvaged that morning, and set it down with a clang that made Nell wince.

"Quill Harrow was here," Ilya said, without greeting. Ilya skipped greetings the way other men skipped stones.

"When?"

"While you were under your engine being happy. He wants his forty crowns by month's end or he takes the winch."

"The winch is worth sixty."

"He knows. That's why he wants the winch." Ilya rubbed his beard, which contained, as far as anyone had determined, at least one gull feather permanently. "Also there's a man in a gray coat asking questions at the harbormaster's. Paid the clerk to look at our registry."

Tam felt the day settle onto his shoulders like wet canvas. "Did he get it?"

"Clerk's a drunk, not a traitor. Told him the registry page was water-damaged." Ilya almost smiled. "It isn't. I checked."

"So we've got a stranger pricing our boat, a factor calling our debts, a dead bearing, a leaking condenser, and forty-one crowns." Tam held up fingers as he counted, then folded them down again because the math was insulting. "I'll talk to Marguerite tonight."

"Tonight," Ilya agreed, and went forward to stow the brass, because grief and poverty both responded well to manual labor, and Ilya had never been sure which one he was treating.

* * *

They gathered in the cabin after dark, which was less a room than a claim the boat made on warmth. Marguerite sat at the chart table with the lamp turned low, charts rolled and tied beside her like sleeping eels. Odile Vasseur was four days from arriving in Saltcross, though nobody aboard knew it yet, and the future sat at the table with them anyway, invisible, patient, wearing its gray coat.

Marguerite looked tired in the specific way she always looked tired after diving the Gideon — wrung out and wired at once, like a wire pulled too tight on both ends. Tam had watched her do it for years: go down to that forbidden hulk at dawn, strip brass off a drowned frigate, come up with her pockets full and her face empty. Weary competence, Nell called it. Tam called it what it was. She dove that wreck the way you visit a grave you also rob.

"Report," Marguerite said.

Tam gave it straight, because she'd earned straight. Bearing, condenser, gaskets, zincs. He gave the numbers in order and then gave the total, and watched her take it the way she took a wave broadside — no flinch, just absorption.

"Total," she repeated.

"Total. And that's my labor free, which is a joke, because my labor is the only reason the total isn't double."

"How long does the bearing give us?"

"Nursed? Two weeks. Maybe three. After that it seizes, and when it seizes it takes the shaft with it, and then we're not a salvage boat anymore, we're a very slow houseboat with opinions."

Silence. The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the companionway, righted itself. Out on the water a bell buoy counted the swell, patient, indifferent.

"Sell the compass."

Nell said it. Of course Nell said it; Nell was the youngest and therefore the only one who could afford to say the true thing first. She sat with her knees drawn up, chin on them, eyes steady on Marguerite, and Tam admired her and feared for her in equal measure, because the world punished people who said true things first, and Nell kept saying them anyway.

"No," Marguerite said.

"It would cover the bearing and the condenser and the zincs and leave us float for a month—"

"It's not for sale."

"It's Dad's," Nell said, softer, and Tam winced, because Nell still said *Dad* about Elias Yarrow, having joined the crew young enough that the word had stuck to her like paint. "I know. I know it's his. But he's not coming back for it, Marguerite. And the boat is *here*."

The lamp flame leaned again. Marguerite's hand had gone, without Tam noticing when, into her pocket, around the shape of the compass. He'd seen her do it a hundred times. He'd never once seen her take it out and look at it below decks. Whatever she checked it for, she checked it privately.

"I've been thinking about selling it since before you were crew," Marguerite said. "Every winter, when the box goes thin. Every time Harrow smiles at me. You want to know why I haven't?"

Nobody wanted to know, and everybody waited to know.

"Because it's the only thing I own that the creditors can't put a number on. The moment it has a price, this whole life has a price, and then somebody pays it, and then it's over." She took her hand out of the pocket, empty. "The compass stays. Find me another answer, Tam."

"There isn't another answer," Tam said. "That's the report. The answer is: we're finished in three weeks unless a miracle berths at this quay."

As if summoned — and Tam would think about this later, the timing of it, the way the universe sometimes telegraphs its punches — footsteps crossed the gangplank. Measured steps. Good boots.

Ilya appeared in the companionway, filling it, and behind him, framed by lamplight and harbor fog, stood a woman in a traveling coat cut finer than anything Saltcross had floated in a decade. Dark hair, darker eyes, a smile like a knife being shown politely.

"Well," said Odile Vasseur, in a voice that had won arguments in three ports Tam could name and lost friends in two of them. "I heard the *Kestrel's Grace* was still afloat. I had to come see for myself. Hello, Marguerite."

The silence that followed had weight and history and, Tam noticed, temperature — the cabin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with drafts.

Marguerite rose slowly from the chart table, and her hand, Tam saw, drifted once more toward her pocket.

"Odile," she said. "You're a long way from anywhere welcome."

"I'm exactly where I'm paid to be." Odile stepped down into the cabin, uninvited, unhurried, and set a document tube on the chart table with a click that sounded, to Tam's ear tuned to machinery, exactly like a bearing beginning to fail. "And I've brought something you're going to want to hear. Though fair warning—" that knife-smile again, aimed around the room, collecting each of them, "—it concerns a wreck nobody names, a strongroom nobody's opened, and six tides that started counting the moment I docked."

Tam caught Nell's eye across the cabin. The girl's face was lit up with terror and delight in equal parts, and Tam understood, with the resignation of a man who has read this gauge before, that whatever came next, the numbers were finally about to move.

He only wished he knew in which direction.
