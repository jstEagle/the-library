## 3. Odile at the Door

Marguerite heard the engine before she saw the boat—a clean, expensive engine note, the kind that idled like a purring animal instead of coughing like theirs did. She was on the *Kestrel*'s aft deck with a rag and a tin of brass polish, working the salvage from the morning dive into something a broker might pay for, when the sound came around the breakwater.

She knew the boat before she knew the flag. A Vasseur hull, thirty feet of lacquered mahogany and white canvas, sliding through the harbor mouth with the insolent grace of something that had never once taken water over its rails. It came alongside their berth with a fender-perfect kiss, and Marguerite set down her rag very slowly, because some things you wanted your hands steady for.

"Odile," she said, to no one. To the harbor. To whatever god arranged these things.

Nell poked her head out of the cabin, took one look at Marguerite's face, and withdrew again like a crab backing into its hole.

The woman who stepped onto the dock was older than the last time Marguerite had seen her—five years, maybe six—but she wore the years the way she wore everything: deliberately. Grey-shot black hair cut sharp at the jaw. A coat that cost more than the *Kestrel*'s annual haul. And in one gloved hand, a document tube with a government seal on it, held out slightly from her body like a lantern.

"Yarrow." Odile Vasseur smiled the way a knife smiles. "You look terrible."

"You look rich."

"One of us improved." She glanced up at the *Kestrel*—at the patched rail, the rust weeping down the stack, the laundry Nell had strung between the mast and the winch because they couldn't afford to run the dryer coil—and Marguerite watched her catalog it all with those quick grey eyes, pricing the boat, pricing the crew, pricing her. "May I come aboard? I'd rather not conduct business on your dock. Your dock has opinions about me."

"My dock has good taste."

But she stepped aside anyway, and hated herself for it, and Odile came up the ladder light as a cat and settled into the cockpit bench as if she'd been there yesterday instead of half a decade ago.

The last time had been in a claims office in Corvane, both of them twenty-six and furious, fighting over the salvage rights to a wrecked collier. Marguerite had won by four days and lost something else entirely—the friendship underneath the rivalry, which had been real once, back when they were two broke divers sharing a tank compressor and dreaming out loud. She remembered Odile's face when the ruling came down. Not angry. Worse. *Disappointed*, like Marguerite had confirmed something she'd always suspected about her.

"You've heard," Odile said now.

"Heard what?"

"Don't." Odile's smile thinned. "Don't do the thing where you pretend the whole coast doesn't talk. The Gideon. You dove it this morning. Dawn tide, north channel, forty minutes on the wreck. I could tell you what brass you brought up, but I'd rather hear you say it."

Marguerite kept her face still. Inside, something cold turned over. They'd been careful. Ilya had run watch the whole dive; no patrol boat had come within a mile. But Odile Vasseur had never needed patrol boats. She needed money, and money bought eyes.

"That wreck is forbidden ground," Marguerite said. "You know it better than anyone. The navy doesn't name it. Permits don't exist for it. So whatever's in that tube, it isn't a concession for the Gideon, because there's no such thing."

Odile unrolled the document anyway. Laid it flat on the chart table between them, weighting the corners with two brass fittings off Marguerite's own polishing pile, which was either carelessness or a message.

It was a concession permit. Genuine seal, genuine watermark, genuine signature of a harbormaster whose name Marguerite recognized from a dozen legitimate wrecks. And under the vessel-and-coordinates section, in typed characters that looked obscene against the official paper: *FRIGATE GIDEON, POSITION AS FILED.*

"There's no such thing," Odile agreed. "And yet."

Marguerite didn't touch it. Touching it would mean something. "Who signed off?"

"A friend."

"Nobody has friends in the Admiralty. People have debts."

"Then call him a creditor." Odile leaned back, crossing her ankles, and for just a moment the polish cracked—something flickered behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice, there and gone. "The permit is valid for eight tides. At the end of it, the navy's own salvage fleet arrives to do what the navy does with things it forbids other people to name. Which means, Yarrow, that for eight tides, the Gideon is not forbidden. It is merely *contested*. There's a difference. I've read the maritime code three times this month and I can show you the footnotes."

"What's down there that's worth footnotes?"

"The strongroom."

The word landed in the cabin and sat there. Marguerite had spent eleven years diving the Gideon's outer decks, stripping brass and copper and staying religiously clear of the central trunk, the sealed section below the old captain's country, where the hull plates ran double-thick and the corrosion patterns said *locked from inside*. Every salvager on the coast knew about the strongroom the way sailors know about a reef. You marked it. You went around.

"It's sealed," Marguerite said. "Blast-sealed, probably. Nobody's cracked it in eleven years."

"Nobody's had a permit, a schedule, and a diver good enough." Odile's eyes came up and fixed on hers, and there it was—the real offer, arriving under the paperwork like cargo under ballast. "I have the first two. I need the third. I need you, Marguerite."

"No."

"I haven't made an offer yet."

"You don't have to. Whatever it is, the answer's shaped the same."

"Two thousand crowns. Each. On delivery of strongroom contents to my patron's representative. Plus refit costs for this—" she gestured elegantly at the entire dying boat— "whatever she needs. Paid up front. Engine, hull, rigging, whatever your engineer cries about at night."

Marguerite laughed, and it came out ugly. "Two thousand. For the Gideon. For the wreck nobody names. You think I'm stupid enough to believe a number like that comes without teeth?"

"I think you're exactly smart enough to know what your alternatives are." Odile's voice dropped, gentled, which was worse. "I know about the bank note. I know Tam's been stretching your fuel line with jury-rigged parts since spring. I know you sold the stern chocks in March to cover wages. I'm not saying any of this to wound you. I'm saying it because I did the arithmetic before I ever left port, and the arithmetic says the *Kestrel* dies within the year, and crews die with boats. Where's your bosun going to go? The girl?" She paused. "You can be proud or you can be solvent. In my experience you can't afford both for long."

The cabin felt smaller. Marguerite became aware of sounds from below—the clink of Tam's tools, Nell's footsteps overhead on the deck plates, Ilya's low voice singing something tuneless in the galley. Her people. Her debt, in the shape of people.

"You swore too," Marguerite said quietly. "After Pellam. We all stood on the dock at Corvane and swore off her. You were the one who said it loudest. *That ship eats people.* Your words."

Something moved across Odile's face then—not the flicker from before but a long, slow shadow, and for a moment Marguerite saw the girl she'd shared a compressor with, the one who'd held her hair back over a rail in Port Sallen, who'd taught her the knot she still used every day of her life.

"Pellam drowned because he panicked at depth alone with no backup," Odile said. "Not because the ship cursed him. I've had eleven years to think about it, and I've decided grief makes terrible theology." She stood, smoothing her coat. "The permit runs eight tides. My representative expects an answer by tomorrow's noon bell. If it's yes, I'll bring the advance and my gear. If it's no—" she shrugged, and the shrug was flawless, and that was how Marguerite knew it wasn't— "then I hire another crew, and in six tides the navy blows the doors off that room and takes everything in it, and neither of us ever learns why our fathers' generation lied about that ship. Because yours was aboard her, Yarrow. Don't tell me you haven't wondered."

She went down the ladder without waiting for an answer. The mahogany boat pulled away with its purring engine, and Marguerite stood at the rail holding her rag of brass polish, watching until it cleared the breakwater.

*Your father was aboard her.* As if Odile hadn't known that since before the swearing-off. As if she hadn't saved it, all these years, for exactly this moment.

Marguerite's hand went to her pocket, without her permission, and closed around the compass. Through the brass casing she could feel it ticking faintly against her fingers—or imagined she could—the needle swinging toward something that was not north.

Below, Tam's tools had gone quiet. He appeared at the cabin door, wiping his hands, Nell behind him, Ilya filling the frame behind Nell. They'd heard. Of course they'd heard; the boat had a hull like a drum.

"Two thousand each," Tam said. Not a question. His voice was carefully, terribly neutral.

"And a full refit," Marguerite said. "Paid up front."

Ilya spat over the rail, an old habit, slow and deliberate. "Pellam's dead eleven years and she shows up waving paper. That permit's got a hook in it, skipper. You can see the barb from here."

"I know."

"So we're not taking it."

Marguerite looked at the document still lying on the chart table, weighted down with her own brass. She thought about the compass in her pocket, pointing wrong. About her father, who had walked up a gangway onto the Gideon twenty-three years ago and become a name on a list and nothing else. About the refit. About Nell, seventeen years old, who deserved a boat that didn't leak.

"We vote tonight," she said. "All hands. Like last time."

Nobody moved. Then Nell, from the back, in a voice too small for the size of the question:

"What did she mean, your father was aboard her?"

Marguerite closed her hand tighter around the compass, and the harbor wind came off the water cold, smelling of salt and old iron, like the inside of a drowned ship.

"She meant," Marguerite said, "that the Gideon's been calling longer than any of us has been alive. Tonight we decide whether we answer."

* * *

They voted at dusk, in the galley, over stew nobody tasted.

Tam laid out the numbers first, because numbers were his way of praying. The refit figure alone would keep the *Kestrel* alive five more years. Two thousand crowns was three seasons of honest salvage. Against it: a navy fleet, a forbidden wreck, a patron nobody could name, and a dead diver's ghost that none of them had ever really stopped hearing.

Ilya voted no, and said so in four words, and sat back with his arms crossed like a man braced for impact.

Nell voted yes, and flushed scarlet, and didn't take it back even when Ilya looked at her.

Tam voted yes, quietly, hating it, his eyes on the ledger the whole time as if the numbers might change if he stared hard enough.

Four votes. It came down to Marguerite, and she stood there with the compass heavy in her pocket and five faces' worth of futures stacked against one dead man's memory, and understood that pride was a luxury priced beyond anything in the strongroom.

"For the record," she said, "I think this is a mistake."

"Noted," said Tam.

"But I've been making mistakes since the day my father boarded that ship, and I'd like to stop paying interest on them." She pulled the permit across the table. "We sail on the morning tide."

From the corner, Ilya said softly, almost to himself: "Pellam said something like that once. Right before the dive."

The galley went silent. Outside, the evening water slapped against the hull, patient as a clock, counting down tides none of them could see.
