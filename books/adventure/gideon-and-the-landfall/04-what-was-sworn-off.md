## 4. What Was Sworn Off

Ilya had voted last, which was the tradition, and he hated it.

The galley of the *Kestrel's Mercy* was too small for six people and one concession permit. The paper sat on the table between the salt crock and Tam's cold tea like something that had washed ashore dead — glossy, official, stamped with a seal Ilya didn't recognize and Odile Vasseur clearly wanted them to stare at. She'd laid it flat herself, smoothed it with two fingers, and now she stood with her back against the bulkhead, arms crossed, letting the document do her talking for her.

"Six tides," she said again, because apparently once hadn't been enough. "The navy's salvage fleet makes its final run in six tides. After that, the Gideon goes to the bottom of the ledger forever. This is the only window anyone will ever get."

Marguerite sat across from her, elbows on the table, and said nothing. That was worse than shouting. Ilya had known Marguerite Yarrow since she was nineteen and all elbows and noise, and he knew every silence she owned. This one was the heavy kind. The kind with a body in it.

"I'll say my piece first," Ilya said, "since nobody else has the stomach to."

Odile raised an eyebrow. Marguerite didn't move.

"We swore off her." He kept his voice level, the way you do when the deck is pitching. Not because he felt level. Because if he let it crack, everything behind it would come out, and there were things behind it that had no business being aired in front of Vasseur. "Seven years ago. All of us. We put our hands on the mast and we said the Gideon stays drowned. And now you're sitting here with a permit like a signed pardon, asking us to forget we ever said it."

"The dead don't care who dives their wreck," Odile said.

"No," Ilya agreed. "But the living remember."

* * *

They'd been younger then, all of them, and poorer in the way that made poor feel temporary. It was Tam who'd found the Gideon first — not Marguerite, whatever the harbor stories said — running a magnetometer sweep along the shelf edge for a client who'd stiffed them on payment. The needle had jumped so hard Tam thought the instrument broke. Three days later they anchored over her: a frigate down forty fathoms on her beam ends, hull split along the starboard side like a fruit, masts still standing in the way drowned ships sometimes keep their dignity.

Forty fathoms was deep work. Deep work meant money, and money meant they got greedy, and greed meant they stayed past the season's good weather into the gray weeks when the water went cold enough to kill a man's judgment before it killed the man.

His name was Piet. Twenty-three years old. Nell's older brother, though Nell was only eleven then and mostly remembered him as the boy who taught her knots. Ilya had certified him himself — shallow-water work only, never below thirty fathoms, never alone. Ilya had written those words in the crew book in his own square hand, and Ilya had been the one who broke them, because it was Ilya who sent him down.

Not alone. Never alone. But the line tendering Piet that day was Ilya's own hands on the rope, and when the current shifted — a cold tongue of water sliding off the shelf, the kind they'd been warned about by the old woman in the drowned town, the kind they'd laughed about over breakfast — Piet was forty-one fathoms down inside the gun deck, and the line went slack, and then it went heavy in the wrong way, the way lines go when they're attached to something that isn't coming up anymore.

They brought him up. Of course they brought him up. Ilya hauled until his shoulders tore and Tam dove the last ten fathoms without gear he should have used, and they laid Piet on the deck of the *Mercy* blue as harbor ice, and Marguerite did compressions until her arms gave out, and none of it mattered. Forty fathoms forgives nothing.

The navy came three days later. Not with sympathy — with a survey team and a sealed order. The wreck was closed. Concession revoked. Any vessel found working the site would be seized. They didn't say why a frigate that had been common knowledge for a season was suddenly a state secret. They didn't have to. Men who seal wrecks are men hiding something, and Ilya had been around long enough to know the shape of that particular shadow.

Before they scattered for the winter, Marguerite called the vote. All hands on the mast, the old salvage custom, each person saying it aloud so the sea could hear: *the Gideon stays drowned.* Piet's mother stood on the dock and watched them do it. Ilya had looked at her while he said his words, because he owed her that much — to say them looking at the face of the woman whose son he'd spent.

He'd meant it. That was the thing Odile Vasseur couldn't understand, standing there with her permit. He'd meant it with his whole chest, and he'd slept better for seven years believing the vow had held.

* * *

"It wasn't your fault."

Tam said it quietly, not looking up from the permit, and Ilya realized he'd been staring at the table through the whole of his own memory. The galley had gone very still.

"I didn't ask," Ilya said.

"You're going to sit there wearing it anyway, so somebody may as well say it. The current took him. Currents take divers. You ran the line by the book until the book stopped mattering."

"The book is what keeps people alive."

"And you've kept everyone alive since," Tam said. "Every dive since Piet, you've run it tighter than any bosun on this coast. So either you learned, or you're doing penance, and either way the job gets done safer with you than without you. That's my vote. Yes."

"That's not how penance works," Ilya said.

"No," Tam agreed. "Penance doesn't work. That's rather the point of it."

Nell spoke from the doorway, where she'd been standing long enough that Ilya was ashamed not to have noticed. She was nineteen now — Piet's age exactly, a coincidence the whole crew carried like a stone in a pocket and never mentioned.

"Piet loved that wreck," Nell said. "He used to say it was the best thing we'd ever find. He'd be first down the ladder if we gave him the chance."

"He was twenty-three," Ilya said. "He'd have been first down the ladder for anything."

"So am I," Nell said. "And I'm asking you not to decide for me what I'm allowed to risk. You didn't get to decide for him either, in the end. The sea did."

That landed harder than Tam's argument, because it was true, and truths spoken by the young in front of witnesses don't fade politely. Ilya looked at Marguerite, hoping for an ally in refusal, and found her watching Nell with an expression he recognized — the look of a woman seeing a ghost stand up and volunteer.

* * *

Odile let the silence run long, which was a tactic, and Ilya respected tactics even when they were being used on him.

"For what it's worth," she said finally, "I know about the diver. Everyone on this coast knows about the diver. It's why I came to you and not to some harbor outfit that would strip the wreck for scrap and sell the story to whoever pays."

"Comforting," Ilya said.

"I need people who are careful. Careful costs more than brave. I'm paying for careful." She pushed off the bulkhead and set both palms on the table, leaning in. "Here's the shape of it. My patron wants what's in the strongroom. I don't know why and I've stopped pretending I do. I get paid on delivery, not before, not after — delivery. Your boat, your expertise, your history with the site. In exchange: the debt on this boat clears. All of it. Every creditor on that dock stops circling, and the *Mercy* belongs to you free and clear."

The number she named made the galley feel smaller. Ilya watched Tam's face do arithmetic and grief at the same time. The engine needed a new fuel pump, the hull needed caulking, the air compressor was held together with prayer and wire — Tam had listed it all that morning in a voice gone flat with resignation, and now here was a stranger offering to erase the list with one word.

"And if we say no?" Marguerite asked.

"Then I hire someone less careful, they die or they fail, and the strongroom opens anyway. My patron doesn't accept no. He accepts delays." Odile straightened. "You're not voting on whether this happens. You're voting on whether it happens well."

"That's a slaver's argument," Ilya said.

"It's a realist's," Odile said. "I've been both, so I'd know."

* * *

The vote went around the mast at dusk, because Ilya insisted — if they were going to break a vow, they'd break it properly, with the sea listening.

Tam voted yes, and said it plainly, and put a hand on Ilya's shoulder on the way past, which was its own apology and its own argument. Nell voted yes with her chin up and her eyes wet, and Ilya understood that for her this wasn't betrayal but inheritance — her brother's wreck, her brother's risk, hers to finish. Old Wren, the cook, who never voted anything but the weather, voted yes and muttered that storms come whether you invite them or not, better to be underway when they arrive.

Four votes. Then Marguerite.

She stood at the mast with her hand flat against the wood, and Ilya saw her other hand go to her coat pocket — the compass, her father's compass, the one that had been swinging wrong for a month now, pointing at things compasses shouldn't point at. She'd shown him once, drunk on plum brandy at midwinter, whispering that it pulled toward the Gideon like a fish on a line. He'd told her she was tired. He'd wanted it to be tiredness.

"Yes," Marguerite said, and her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "For Piet. Not despite him. For him."

Then everyone looked at Ilya, and the sea listened, and the guilt he'd carried for seven years turned over in its sleep like something waking.

He thought about Piet's mother on the dock. He thought about the slack line, the heavy line, the wrong-heavy line. He thought about Tam's hand on his shoulder and Nell's wet chin and the fact that Odile Vasseur was right, which was the most hateful part: the strongroom would open with or without them, and if it opened without them, it would open badly, and someone else's boy would go down a ladder and not come up.

"No," Ilya said.

The word fell into the dusk and nobody moved.

"Not no forever," he said, because he owed them honesty the way he owed Piet's mother a face. "No, unless — we run it my way. My rules, my line protocols, my veto on any dive I call unsafe. Nobody goes below thirty fathoms without me on the tender. And when it's done, we swear off her again, proper, and this time I hold us to it."

Marguerite met his eyes across the deck. Something passed between them that wasn't agreement, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Two people carrying the same corpse in different arms.

"That's a yes with conditions," Odile said.

"It's a no with conditions," Ilya corrected. "Learn the difference. It'll matter."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone below and the anchor light swung its slow arc over the water, Ilya stood at the rail and looked south, toward the shelf edge where the Gideon lay keeping her secrets.

The water out there was black and patient. Somewhere under it, a frigate held a strongroom full of whatever the navy had killed to protect, and a boy named Piet who'd never come up, and now — because of a permit and a debt and a vote Ilya had lost four to one — Ilya was going to sail back to him.

He touched the rail the way you touch a grave marker.

"Hold on," he said to the dark water, to the boy, to the wreck nobody names. "We're coming. And this time nobody stays down."

Below decks, faintly, he heard Marguerite's compass case click shut — a small sound, metal on metal, like a decision being locked in.
