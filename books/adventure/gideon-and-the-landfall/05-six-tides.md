## 5. Six Tides

Odile Vasseur had learned long ago that the best lies were told with a ledger open in front of you. Numbers made people honest, or at least made them feel they'd caught you being honest, which amounted to the same thing. So she spread the accounts across the Gull's mess table—her own neat columns in violet ink beside Tam's cramped pencil figures—and let the crew of the *Kestrel* lean over them like crows over a gut pile.

"Six tides," she said. "The concession expires at slack water on the sixth. After that the navy's salvage fleet has exclusive rights to the wreck site, and anyone inside the exclusion buoy gets impounded, fined, and very politely ruined."

"And your backer?" Marguerite asked. She sat across from Odile with her arms folded, the way she'd sat across from her since they were nineteen and competing for the same salvage contract off Cape Merren. Some habits outlived friendship, and some outlived its absence too.

"Pays on delivery," Odile said. "Half on reaching the strongroom intact, half on opening it. Not before."

"That's not how concessions work." Tam didn't look up from his figures. He was a big man who moved like he was trying to take up less space than he occupied, and he had the particular stillness of someone doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Concessions are front-loaded. Mobilization fees, equipment advances. Whoever wrote this contract wants you carrying all the risk."

"Then whoever wrote it knows I'm good for it." Odile smiled. It was a real smile, mostly. She'd practiced it against enough harbor mirrors to know which parts showed.

Tam did look up then, and held her gaze for a moment longer than was comfortable, before returning to his numbers.

The truth—which she kept folded away beneath the smile, beneath the violet ink, beneath even her own attention when she could manage it—was that she had paid for this expedition down to the last coil of rope herself. Her patron had provided the permit, the charts, and a deadline. Everything else came out of Odile Vasseur's own pocket, and her pocket had a hole in it shaped exactly like a debt she could not name aloud in this room.

She watched Marguerite watch her, and wondered—not for the first time—whether Yarrow could see straight through her the way she always had. Even at nineteen. Especially at nineteen.

* * *

They provisioned through the afternoon, and Odile made herself useful, because usefulness was a currency she could spend without explaining where she'd earned it.

The Kestrel was smaller than her own vessel—the *Coriander*, sold eight months ago to a buyer whose name she'd signed without reading twice—and she felt the difference in every companionway. The Coriander had been built for show as much as for work. The Kestrel was built by people who expected to die aboard her and wanted to be comfortable about it. Every surface was worn smooth by hands. Every locker closed with the particular stubbornness of hardware that had been repaired rather than replaced.

Nell, the deckhand, hauled crates up the gangway two at a time and stacked them in the hold with a precision that suggested either discipline or fear of Bosun Ilya. Probably both. Ilya directed the loading without raising his voice, which somehow carried further than shouting would have. When Nell misjudged the center of gravity on a crate of preserved fish and sent it sliding toward the rail, Ilya simply said, "Again," and the girl caught it, reset it, and did it right.

Odile recognized the dynamic. She'd served under bosuns who taught by humiliation and bosuns who taught by patience, and she'd never once served under one who taught by expectation until she met Ilya Voss, and by then she'd already learned everything wrong.

"You're staring," Marguerite said, appearing at her elbow with two tin cups of something that smelled like tar sweetened with regret.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring." Marguerite handed her a cup. "Drink that. It'll put hair on your chest and take ten years off your life, so it evens out."

Odile drank. It was terrible. It was also warm, and her hands stopped shaking, which she hadn't realized they'd been doing.

"The manifest," Marguerite said. "Your backer provided it?"

"Provided the framework. I filled in the quantities."

"Mm." Marguerite sipped her own cup and looked out at the harbor, where the light was going bronze and the fishing boats were coming home in loose, tired lines. "You've ordered diving suits for four. We dive in pairs. You've ordered lamp oil enough for thirty hours below. We've never needed more than twelve. You've ordered—" she paused, and Odile watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing all day, "—enough shot and powder to breach a bulkhead, which is interesting, because the strongroom door opens inward."

Odile kept her face pleasant. "I plan for contingencies."

"You plan for something else." Marguerite turned to look at her directly, and there it was, the old scrutiny, the look that had once made Odile feel transparent and now made her feel like a locked box being weighed for what it might contain. "Whoever your patron is, they don't want gold, Marguerite said. Gold doesn't need lamp oil or breaching charges. Gold needs a crane and a strong back. Your patron wants access. They want the room opened and held open."

"Maybe they want options."

"Nobody pays for options six tides out from a navy fleet." Marguerite set down her cup. "I'm not asking you to break confidence, Odile. I'm telling you that my crew will walk into that water knowing what we know, and right now we know you're lying about something, and the shape of the lie matters."

*It's not my lie,* Odile almost said. *I'm just the envelope it came in.*

What she said instead was: "When I can tell you, I will. That's the best I've got."

Marguerite studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and picked up her cup again, and Odile understood that she had just been granted something rare: time. An extension. A loan against a truth she wasn't sure she could ever repay.

She thought about her brother, and drank the rest of the terrible coffee.

* * *

Evening brought the whole crew into the mess, and Odile unrolled the charts.

The Gideon lay forty miles northeast, in water deep enough to discourage casual salvage but shallow enough to make the navy's heavy equipment viable. Between the harbor and the wreck ran a shipping lane, two fishing grounds, and—beginning on the fourth tide, if the schedules Odile had memorized were correct—the forward elements of the navy salvage fleet, coming up from the south.

"We can't run the lane in daylight," she said, marking the chart in violet. "Not with a permit this fresh. We go north around the shoals, here, then cut east along the old patrol route—"

"No," Ilya said.

The word landed flat and final. Odile looked up. The bosun stood by the door with his arms crossed, and behind him Tam had gone very still, and Nell was suddenly fascinated by the grain of the table.

"The old patrol route," Ilya said, "is where the Gideon went down. It's where Denny Okafor went down with her. We swore off that water. All of us. There was a vote."

"There was a vote five years ago," Odile said carefully. "Circumstances have changed."

"Circumstances always change. Dead men stay dead." Ilya's voice didn't rise. "You want to thread our boat through the graveyard of the man we buried empty-handed, to crack a room the navy sealed itself, on the word of a rival who won't name her paymaster. And you want us to do it in six tides, racing a fleet."

"Yes," Odile said. "That's the shape of it."

Silence. Somewhere above them, footsteps crossed the deck; the harbor bells rang the second watch. Odile became aware of her own heartbeat, steady and fast, and of the violet ink drying on her fingers, and of the fact that she had rehearsed this conversation for three days and none of her rehearsals had included Ilya looking at her with something worse than anger, which was recognition.

He knew what it was to owe someone. She could see it in his face. And she could see him deciding, in real time, whether that knowledge bought her mercy or condemned her.

"It's a vote," Marguerite said quietly. "Same as before. Everyone speaks, everyone counts."

"I say no," Ilya said.

"I say yes," Nell said, immediately, then flushed when everyone looked at her. "I mean—I say we hear the rest first. But yes. If we go, I'm in."

"I say," Tam began, and stopped, and rubbed his eyes. "I say the boat needs three hundred crowns of work we don't have, and this job pays more than we've seen in two years, and I hate that those are the reasons I'm giving." He looked at Odile. "I say yes, and I say I don't trust you, and both things are true."

"I say yes," Marguerite said.

Everyone looked at Ilya.

The bosun uncrossed his arms slowly. He walked to the chart, leaned over it, and put one blunt finger on the marked route—not on the wreck, but on the water between here and there, the long gray stretch of the old patrol line.

"Denny Okafor dove that wreck eleven times," he said. "Twelve, counting the last one. He used to say the Gideon gave up her secrets slow, like a miser paying out coin one piece at a time, and every piece cost a diver something." His finger moved to the strongroom's marked position. "If we do this, we do it my way on deck. My rigging, my signals, my call on when the water's too rough. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Odile said.

"Then I say yes. But I'm saying it for Denny, not for you. Somebody owes that wreck an honest ending, and it isn't going to be the navy."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone, Odile stayed at the table with the chart and a cup of tea she had no intention of drinking.

Marguerite came back down. Of course she did. Odile had counted on it, actually—had left the chart half-rolled, the cabin lamp low, the whole scene arranged with the same care she applied to ledgers. Some invitations were better extended by accident.

"You voted yes," Odile said.

"I voted yes because we're broke and because I've spent five years telling myself I'm done with that wreck while diving other wrecks that look exactly like it." Marguerite dropped into the opposite seat. "Now tell me why you voted yes. The real reason. Not the permit, not the money."

Odile turned her cup slowly on the table. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear it in the slap of water against the hull, patient as a clock.

"My patron," she said, "gave me six tides and a permit and nothing else. No advance. No equipment. Payment on delivery only." She looked up. "Do you understand what kind of person structures a contract that way? Someone who wants the work done desperate. Someone who wants me unable to walk away, because walking away means I've spent everything I have and gotten nothing. That's not a business arrangement, Marguerite. That's a leash."

"Then cut it."

"With what?" The words came out sharper than she intended, and she smoothed them flat again, restored the charm, the easy smile. "You of all people should recognize the design. A debt you can't refuse. Terms you didn't write. I learned salvage from watching you, remember? I learned that whoever holds the paper holds the diver."

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. "Your patron's name."

"Not yet."

"When?"

"Soon." Odile rolled the chart closed and stood, gathering her papers, needing to move, needing to not be looked at like that—with concern, which was infinitely worse than suspicion. "Six tides, Yarrow. Ask me again when the strongroom's open and I've been paid. By then it won't matter whose leash it was."

She climbed the ladder to the deck, where the night air hit her cold and clean, and she stood at the rail watching the harbor lights blur and steadied her breathing.

Three days ago, a letter had arrived under her door in a hand she'd never seen, containing a permit, a schedule, and a single line: *We both want the same thing, Miss Vasseur. Deliver the strongroom, and your brother comes home.* Attached had been a photograph of Lucien, taken recently, sitting in a room with a window too high to reach.

She had told no one. She had told herself that was prudence.

But tonight, standing on a stranger's deck among strangers who voted and argued and poured each other terrible coffee, Odile Vasseur admitted the thing she'd been refusing to admit since the letter came: she was afraid. Not of the navy, not of the wreck, not even of drowning. She was afraid of what she would do when the choice came—because it would come, sooner or later, between the crew and the payment, between these people who had given her a vote and the man who held her brother's life in a high-windowed room.

And she was afraid, most of all, that she already knew which way a woman like her would jump.

Below her, faint through the deck planks, she heard Tam's voice, low and urgent, and the scratch of a pencil moving fast across paper—as if someone aboard were already doing sums of their own about her, and the numbers weren't coming out clean.
