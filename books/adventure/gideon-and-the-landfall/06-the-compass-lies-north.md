## 6. The Compass Lies North

The Gideon fell astern by midmorning, and Marguerite watched her go the way you watch a door close on a room where something of yours is still burning.

Not that she'd say so. Odile stood at the rail with her arms folded and her permit folded inside them like a knife she hadn't yet needed to open, and Marguerite kept her face set to the horizon instead, because the horizon didn't ask questions.

*Fairweather*, Tam's boat — Tam's pride, Tam's rusted heart — made six knots with a following swell and a patched boiler that knocked every fourth stroke like a polite cough. Nell had claimed the bow pulpit within an hour of clearing the breakwater and refused all suggestions to come aft, on the grounds that someone had to be eyes for wreckage. Ilya had the wheel. Odile had the chart table in the wheelhouse, which was a small territorial war nobody had declared yet.

Marguerite had the compass.

It rode in her coat pocket the way it had ridden there for eleven years, since the day a navy clerk handed it across a counter with a form and a sentence: *Effects of the deceased, next of kin sign here.* Brass case gone soft at the edges from handling, glass scratched in one corner, the needle under that glass as steady as anything in her life had ever been.

Except lately it wasn't.

She'd told herself it was the wreck. Iron mass, that much of it — a frigate carries tons of iron in her bones, and compasses near iron lie. She'd told herself it was the cold snap, the solar weather Tam muttered about, the age of the thing. She'd told herself a lot of things over eleven years, mostly about her father, and most of those had turned out to be stories too.

But they were two miles clear of the Gideon now, and the needle still wasn't pointing north.

She took it out on the afterdeck, out of the wheelhouse, away from the engine's iron and the tools in their boxes. Held it flat on her palm. The morning was clean and gray-gold, wind light from the southwest, sea running long and easy behind them. Good conditions. Honest conditions.

The needle pointed east-northeast. It had been pointing east-northeast since the harbor.

"Tam," she called through the companionway. "How much iron are we carrying?"

Tam appeared in the hatch wiping grease off her hands with a rag that had once been a shirt. "Boiler, keel shoe, winch, the anchor chain, your entire personality. Why?"

"Enough to pull a needle off north?"

"Not from back there." Tam came down the steps, squinting. "You're too far aft. And we've been steering south-southeast since the point anyway." She stopped. Looked at the compass. Looked at the wake. "Huh."

"Huh," Marguerite agreed.

"That's not deviation."

"No."

"That's not *anything*. That's—" Tam crouched, brought her eye level with the card, and went very still in the way she did when a machine said something impossible and she wanted to hear it again. "It's steady. It's not wandering, it's not sticking, it's just... wrong. Consistently wrong."

"I know."

"How long?"

Marguerite turned the compass over in her hand. The brass was warm from her pocket. "Since the first dive this season. Maybe before. I didn't want to look."

Tam didn't say *that's stupid*, which was one of the reasons Marguerite would follow her into a burning engine room. She said, "Show me," and led the way up to the wheelhouse.

Ilya had the wheel and the horizon in equal measure, his big hands resting on the spokes like they were sleeping animals he'd learned to handle. Odile looked up from the chart when they crowded in, and Marguerite saw her catalog the compass, Tam's expression, Marguerite's face, and file all three somewhere behind those cool gray eyes.

"Bearings," Tam said to Ilya. "Give me a sight on the point lighthouse, then tell me what the ship's compass says."

Ilya took the sight without question. "Lighthouse bears one-six-eight true. Ship's compass reads one-seven-two. Four degrees of westerly error, about right for this heading."

"And this?" Tam held out her hand, and Marguerite put the brass compass into it.

They stood in a triangle around it, four of them counting Odile, who had drifted closer without appearing to move. The needle lay quiet under its scratched glass, pointing its private direction.

"East-northeast," Tam said. "Sixty-some degrees. There is no configuration of iron on this boat that produces sixty degrees of error while the binnacle sits at four."

"Then it's broken," Odile said.

"It's not broken." Marguerite heard her own voice come out flatter than she intended. "It's been doing this since the Gideon. Closer we got, the further off north it swung. Like it was—" She stopped herself. Saying the next part aloud, in front of Odile Vasseur, felt like handing over something she couldn't get back.

"Like it was what?" Odile asked, and her voice was gentle in a way that was worse than mockery.

"Like it was looking at it."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part. On any other boat, any other crew, somebody would have laughed, and the laugh would have been a rope to climb back to solid ground. Instead Ilya just kept steering, and Tam kept staring at the needle, and Odile kept looking at Marguerite with the particular attention she'd always paid to other people's weaknesses, except that this time it didn't feel like aim. It felt like recognition.

"My father carried that compass," Marguerite said. "Twenty years in the patrol service. They gave him a new one twice and he kept carrying this one. Said a compass you've trusted is worth more than one that's accurate." Her thumb moved over the hinge of the case, the worn spot where his thumb had worn it first. "It was in his kit when the Gideon went down. The clerk gave it back to me with his effects. Everything else was waterlogged. This wasn't even wet."

"Sealed case?" Tam offered.

"It's not sealed. Look at the hinge. There's gaps everywhere. It should have drowned." She turned it over. Underneath, stamped small and old into the base plate, was the maker's mark and a serial number she'd read ten thousand times without ever reading: *V. Halloway & Sons, No. 114.* "And it points wherever it wants."

Odile leaned in. "May I?"

Marguerite hesitated, then handed it over. Watching Odile hold her father's compass did something complicated to her chest that she declined to examine.

Odile turned it slowly, watching the needle. It stayed fixed on its bearing regardless of how the case rotated — normal; the needle finds north, not the case. But then Odile walked it toward the stern windows, and the needle trembled, and settled again. Same bearing. She walked it forward. Same bearing. She carried it out onto the wing of the bridge, into open air, no iron within twenty feet.

Same bearing. East-northeast. Sixty-five degrees, maybe sixty-six.

"That's not possible," Tam said, following her out. "A magnetic needle responds to the field. If it's ignoring rotation it should still find north. Unless—"

"Unless it's not responding to Earth's field at all," Odile said quietly. "Unless it's responding to something stronger and closer."

"Nothing's closer. We're in open water."

"We're in *his* water." Odile said it without drama, which somehow made it land harder. She handed the compass back. "Your father patrolled these waters for two decades. If anyone ever mapped a route, marked stations, buried anything along a line—"

"He wouldn't have buried anything. He was a patrol officer, not a smuggler."

"Everyone buries something eventually." Odile's mouth curved, but her eyes didn't join it. "The question is whether he knew he was doing it."

Marguerite took the compass back and held it flat, and for a long moment the only sound was the knock of the boiler and the long hiss of the wake.

Then she went to the chart table.

* * *

The chart was Odile's — a survey chart of the whole reach, the Gideon's loss position marked in red pencil with a neat little cross that Marguerite wanted to scratch out on principle. She spread it flat and weighted the corners with the dividers, a brass weight, and her own palm.

"Now," she said. "Bearing from here to the wreck."

Odile drew it without being asked. "Zero-nine-three. Due east, a shade south."

The needle pointed zero-six-five.

"Off by nearly thirty degrees," Tam said. "So it's not pointing at the wreck either. So much for the ghost story."

"Wait." Marguerite bent over the chart. Something was itching at her, some shape half-seen through dirty glass. "His patrol route. I told you he ran the same circuit for twenty years. Harbor, the shoals, the light, the islands, home. He used to draw it for me when I was small. On the backs of envelopes. I thought it was a game." She could see it suddenly, absurdly vivid: her father's blunt pencil, the little loop he put at the turning points, *here we come about, Maggie, mind the shoal.* "It was a closed circuit. Out along the coast, around the islands, back. Day after day. Year after year."

"So?"

"So show me the bearing." She put the compass on the chart, on their present position, and let the needle speak. Zero-six-five. She laid a pencil rule along it and drew the line.

The line ran east-northeast across the reach, crossed the old shipping lane, touched the northern tip of the Serrat Islands—

—and bent, if you followed the coast, exactly the way a man would bend if he were sailing the patrol circuit. Not to the wreck. Along the *route*. The needle wasn't pointing at a place. It was pointing along a path, the way a compass in a car doesn't point at your destination, just tells you which way to turn the wheel.

"It's not a pointer," she heard herself say. "It's a follower. It doesn't say *go there.* It says *this way.*"

Silence in the wheelhouse. Even the boiler seemed to knock more softly.

"That's not how compasses work," Tam said, but her voice had gone careful, the voice she used for engines making sounds they shouldn't.

"No," Marguerite agreed. "It isn't."

Odile had gone very still. Marguerite watched her watch the needle, and understood, with a small cold shock, that Odile knew something. Had perhaps known it before she ever knocked on the harbor office door with her permit and her offer. The realization sat in Marguerite's stomach like swallowed ballast.

"What do you know?" she asked.

Odile looked up. For once she didn't perform surprise or offense. "My patron sent me to the Gideon's strongroom. That much is true. But the approach instructions weren't naval charts. They were a list of bearings." She tapped the chart, east-northeast of their position. "Starting from a point out there. I assumed they were navigation fixes. Now I'm wondering whether my patron has a compass like yours."

"Why would he?"

"Because," Odile said slowly, "you don't give a diver a list of bearings to a strongroom unless the strongroom can't be found any other way. And you don't hide a strongroom so well that only a compass can find it—" she paused, and finished the thought like stepping off a ledge, "—unless the man who hid it expected someone specific to come looking. With the key already in his pocket."

The wheelhouse felt smaller. Outside, the sea went on being beautiful and indifferent, gold-gray, endless.

Marguerite looked down at the compass in her hand. Her father's compass. The one thing of his that came back dry. The one thing the navy clerk had handed over without a second glance, because it was just a compass, just brass and glass, just an old man's keepsake.

*Effects of the deceased.*

Or effects of the missing. Because they'd never found him, had they? Never found the body, never found the boat, never found anything but questions wrapped in a flag. Eleven years of *lost with all hands* and a pension and a form to sign.

And all that time, in her pocket, the answer had been swinging gently east-northeast, waiting for her to stop assuming it was broken.

"If this is a key," she said, "what does it open?"

"The strongroom," Odile said. "Maybe. Or maybe the way to it. Your patron's bearings start forty miles short of the wreck, in open water. I thought it was paranoia. Now I think it's a lock. A route only a certain compass can walk, ending at a door only a certain person can open." Her gray eyes came up. "Your father didn't just serve on the Gideon, Marguerite. I'm starting to think he built its secrets. And your patron knows it. Which means he didn't hire me for my diving."

The name of it rose in Marguerite like floodwater. "He hired you for me."

"He hired me to bring the compass aboard a boat headed for the Gideon. Whether I knew it or not." Odile's jaw tightened — the first honest expression Marguerite had seen on her in eleven years of rivalry. "I signed papers I didn't read closely enough. I intend to remedy that."

Marguerite closed her hand around the compass. The brass was warm. It had always been warm, she realized, warmer than pocket-warmth explained, and she had filed that away with everything else she hadn't wanted to look at.

Ahead, dead ahead on the needle's bearing, the Serrat Islands sat low and blue on the horizon, and beyond them, past the edge of the chart's detail, the empty water where her father's route ended.

"Nell!" she called forward. "Come take a bearing. You've the best eyes on this boat."

Nell scrambled aft, salt-crusted and grinning, and Marguerite put the compass in her hands and watched the girl frown at it, tilt it, frown harder.

"It's broke," Nell announced. "Doesn't point north at all. Points—" she squinted along the needle, along the penciled line, out at the islands, and her grin faded into something older. "Points at the Serrats. My gran used to say nobody sails the inside passage past the Serrats anymore. Navy closed it after the sinking. Says it's restricted waters." She looked up. "Why would your compass want to go somewhere restricted?"

Marguerite looked at Odile. Odile looked at the chart, at the red-crossed wreck, at the blank space beyond the islands where the navy's silence began.

"Because," Marguerite said, "that's where the answers are. All hands on deck. We're changing course."

The needle swung, gentle and sure, as *Fairweather* came about — east-northeast, into waters the navy had closed for eleven years, following a dead man's compass toward whatever he'd died keeping.
