## 7. Fleet Smoke on the Horizon

Nell had the morning watch alone, which was either a compliment or a punishment, and she'd decided it was a compliment because the alternative made her hands shake.

The *Fairweather* ran east-northeast under a sky the color of unpolished tin. Everyone else was below — Marguerite sleeping off her watch, Ilya and Tam arguing in low voices about the engine, Odile wherever Odile went when nobody wanted her around, which Nell suspected was nowhere, since there wasn't anywhere on a boat this small. Just Nell at the wheel, the compass on the binnacle, and eleven years of closed water opening up ahead of them like a door nobody had locked properly.

She was sixteen and had been aboard eight months, long enough to know that being trusted with the dawn watch meant Marguerite had decided something about her. She just didn't know what yet.

The compass needle held steady on east-northeast. Not north. Nell had noticed that the first night out and hadn't said anything, because noticing things and saying things were different skills, and she'd learned fast which one kept you aboard.

The sea was empty. That was the thing about closed water — it was *too* empty. No fishing boats. No freighter smoke on the horizon. No gulls following them hoping for scraps, because gulls knew what she was only beginning to learn: ships avoided this stretch of sea the way people avoided a house where somebody had died.

Then she saw the smoke.

It came up over the horizon line the way a bruise comes up under skin — slow, then all at once. A gray smudge where there should have been nothing, sitting low against the water about four points off their starboard bow. Nell watched it for a full minute, telling herself it was weather, it was a squall building, it was anything other than what it looked like.

But she'd grown up in a harbor town. She knew funnel smoke from cloud. Cloud moved. Smoke *drove*.

"Nell to anyone," she said into the voice pipe, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her. "Smoke bearing two-two-oh. Closing."

A pause. Then Tam's voice, sleep-rough: "Say again?"

"Funnel smoke. It's driving. It's not weather."

Boots on the companionway ladder. Tam first, hair sticking up, spectacles pushed crooked onto his face. Then Ilya, who didn't run anywhere but arrived everywhere first anyway. Then Marguerite, pulling her coat on over her shirt, already awake in the way that meant she'd never really been asleep.

Marguerite took the glasses off their hook and stood at the rail. For a long moment she didn't move at all, and Nell learned something else: that stillness could be louder than shouting.

"Tender," Marguerite said. "Single stack, low freeboard. Navy hull if I ever saw one." She swung the glasses a few degrees. "And behind her—" The glasses stopped. "Damn it. Damn it, damn it, *damn* it."

"What?" Tam asked.

"Second smoke. Further back. They're not patrolling." Marguerite lowered the glasses. Her face had gone very controlled, the way it did when the control was costing her something. "They're *sweeping*. Line abreast, working the closed water. Which means they're not looking for smugglers or poachers." She looked at Odile's cabin door, shut tight. "They're looking for us. Or they're early. Either way—"

"Either way we're a rowboat in front of a warship," Ilya finished. His voice was flat as ballast stone.

"How long do we have?" Tam asked.

Nell answered before she thought better of it. "Tender's making maybe twelve knots. We're making seven with the current. Wind's light out of the southwest." She did the arithmetic in her head, the way her father had taught her, counting fathoms between knuckles. "Two hours. Less if they've seen our smoke. We don't make smoke, but they'll be running a proper search pattern, so it won't matter."

Everyone looked at her. She felt her ears go hot.

"She's right," Tam said.

"I know she's right," Marguerite said. "That's why I hired her." But she was smiling, just barely, and Nell held onto that smile like a handhold in rough water. "Options. Someone give me options."

"Run for the islands," Tam said. "We might make the channel before they close the gap."

"They'll have the channels watched. If the fleet's coming, the fleet's already posted pickets on every pass worth sailing." Marguerite shook her head. "Running is dying slower."

"Then we fight," Ilya said, and everyone looked at him, because Ilya suggesting fighting was like a church suggesting dancing. He shrugged. "I said options. Not good ones."

"We hide," Nell said.

Again the looks. This time she almost wished she'd kept her mouth shut, except Marguerite's eyebrows had gone up instead of down, so she made herself keep going.

"Fog bank. Off the port bow — I've been watching it build since before dawn. Sea smoke, warm water, cold air. It's thickening." She pointed. "If we can get into it before they get a fix on us, we're a ghost. Fog eats everything. Radar, eyes, smoke signals, all of it."

"That fog bank is three miles off our track," Tam said. "And the tender will cut the corner."

"Then we sail a bad course fast," Nell said, and heard herself say it, and couldn't stop now. "Better a bad course than a dead one."

There was a silence. Somewhere below, the engine knocked its steady knock. Marguerite looked at the fog bank, then at the smoke, then at Nell — a long look, measuring, the kind Nell had seen her give a doubtful mooring cleat before trusting it with the whole boat.

"You heard the deckhand," Marguerite said. "Ilya, give me everything the old girl has. Tam, kill anything that makes noise or light — galley fire, lamps, the radio stays cold unless I say otherwise. Nell—"

Nell straightened.

"—you have the wheel. Take us into that fog like you were born in it."

* * *

The next hour was the longest of Nell's life, and also the fastest, and afterward she would never quite be able to explain how both things were true.

She steered by feel and by the compass and by the little corrections Ilya fed her without stopping his work on deck — "two points starboard, easy, easy, hold her there" — while the *Fairweather* heeled and drove through a sea gone suddenly restless, as if the water itself knew what was coming. Behind them the tender's smoke grew from a smudge to a smear to a dirty gray pillar, and once, when Nell glanced back, she caught the flash of light off glass. Binoculars. Somebody on that ship was looking at them right now, and every second that passed was a second closer to being a name on a navy list.

Her hands wanted to shake. She let them want it and didn't let them do it. She thought about her father's voice: *the sea doesn't care if you're scared, girl, it only cares if you're steady.* He'd been wrong about plenty of things, her father, drowned himself off a trawler when she was twelve, but he'd been right about that.

The fog bank rose ahead of them like a wall built out of nothing. It didn't look like weather. It looked like a decision the sea had made — here, and no further.

"Hold your course," Marguerite said quietly, beside her. When had she come to stand there? "Steady as she goes. Don't flinch at it. Fog's only frightening until you're inside; then it's just the world, smaller."

Nell held her course. The wall came on. And then the *Fairweather* went into the fog, and the world ended.

It wasn't like anything Nell had imagined. One breath she could see the whole gray sweep of sea and sky; the next, the world was twenty feet wide. Water dripped somewhere above. The rigging wept. The bow wave went quiet, muffled, as though the boat had been wrapped in wool and lowered into a cellar. The compass needle glowed faintly green in the murk, holding east-northeast, holding true, and Nell gripped the wheel and steered into the green light like it was the last honest thing left.

"Slow her," Marguerite ordered. "Half ahead. We don't want to run down anything in here, including ourselves."

Tam appeared out of the white with the engine-room telegraph disconnected and a look of profound grievance. "Galley's cold. Lamps are boxed. Radio's dark. We are now, officially, the quietest illegal vessel on this coast."

"Good," Marguerite said.

"There's a problem, though." Tam pushed his spectacles up. "Fog works both ways. We can't see them, but sound carries terribly in sea smoke — everything gets smeared. If they come in after us, we won't hear them until they're alongside."

"Then we listen differently." Ilya had come forward, moving through the fog like something the fog had decided to permit. He cocked his head. "Listen for the water, not the engines. A hull pushes a bow wave even at slow speed. You hear the sea change before you hear the ship."

So they listened. All of them, spread along the rail, silent as the drowned. Nell had never heard anything like it — a whole crew of people doing nothing but listening, the fog pressing in, the drip of the rigging marking time like a slow clock. Somewhere far off, muffled to nothing, a ship's horn sounded. Once. Twice. Then silence, or what passes for silence at sea, which is never silence at all.

The horn had come from the south. From open water. From the direction of the track they'd abandoned.

"They lost us," Tam whispered.

"Don't whisper," Ilya murmured. "Whispering carries worse than talking low. And don't celebrate. They haven't given up. They're calling to each other. There'll be another horn soon, further east. They're sweeping the fog's edge, waiting for us to blunder out."

He was right. Twenty minutes later, a horn sounded again — east now, faint, patient. Then, some time after that, north. The navy was walking its fingers around the fog bank like a man feeling for a coin he'd dropped in the grass.

And inside the fog, the *Fairweather* crept.

* * *

They lost the tide at noon.

Nell understood it in pieces, from fragments of conversation she probably wasn't meant to hear, standing her post at the wheel while the adults argued in low voices by the chart table under a shaded lamp that threw more shadow than light.

Six tides. That was the window Odile had bought them — six tides before the permits expired, six tides before whatever was going to happen at the Gideon happened. They'd spent one getting clear of the harbor. Now the fog was eating a second, and there was nothing anyone could do about it, because the alternative was steaming out of the fog into the arms of a navy tender, and nobody suggested that twice.

"The tide turns at half past twelve," Tam said. "If we're not through the shoal passage by then, we lose the set entirely. We'll be punching a foul current for six hours. That's fuel we don't have."

"So we spend it," Marguerite said.

"And if the fog lifts?"

"Then we're a rowboat in front of a warship," Ilya said, "and we die slower."

Nobody laughed. Nell didn't either, but she noticed that Ilya had used her joke, or Marguerite's version of it, and that it had become theirs now, a thing the crew said, and something in her chest went warm and terrified at once. She was inside the joke now. She was inside the crew. People said things around her that got people hanged, and they said them in front of her, because she belonged here.

Odile came up on deck for the first time since dawn.

Nell watched her the way you watch a cat decide whether to cross a room. Odile stood at the rail, looking into the fog with an expression Nell couldn't read — not fear exactly, and not calm either. Something being calculated, continuously, behind those eyes, like an abacus that never stopped clicking.

"Your patron's fleet is early," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't friendly.

"My patron's fleet is *on schedule*," Odile said. "Which is not the same thing, and I suspect you know it. This tender isn't ours. Ours will announce itself." She paused. "Probably."

"*Probably.*"

"I'm being honest with you, Yarrow. Enjoy it. It's expensive."

"The tide's turning in forty minutes and we're blind in a fog bank with the navy feeling around the edges of us like a pickpocket. Forgive me if honesty feels cheap just now."

For a moment the two women looked at each other across the chart lamp, and Nell felt the temperature of the deck drop. Old history moved between them, dense as the fog itself. Then Odile turned away and looked at the compass, at its needle pointing faithfully east-northeast toward nothing any map admitted to.

"Your father's compass," she said softly. "Still pointing its own way, I see."

Nell saw Marguerite go still. Saw her hand move, just slightly, toward her pocket, and stop.

"You know about the compass," Marguerite said.

"I know about a great many things." Odile smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "That's my job. Knowing things. Sometimes it's even my choice."

The horn sounded again — west now, close, too close. Everyone froze. Through the fog came the sound Nell had been dreading and training for all morning without knowing it: the wash of a bow wave. Slow. Deliberate. A big hull moving carefully through fog, feeling its way, no more than a few hundred yards off their beam.

Ilya raised one hand, and the whole boat became a held breath.

Nell stood at the wheel and did the hardest thing she had ever done, which was nothing. She held the wheel steady. She breathed shallow. She listened to the navy's bow wave slide past through the fog, close enough that she could imagine the lookout on the tender's bridge, young like her, scanning white nothing for a silhouette that wasn't there. The wash swelled, passed, faded. The horn sounded again, further off, moving away, patient as arithmetic.

The tender went east, hunting the fog's far edge.

Nell discovered she was gripping the wheel hard enough to hurt, and made herself let go finger by finger. Her heart was going like a drum in a parade. And underneath the terror — underneath it, tangled up with it so she couldn't tell them apart — was something bright and shameless and enormous.

She had *done* that. She'd called the fog. She'd held the wheel. The *Fairweather* was still afloat and still unseen and it was partly, undeniably, hers.

"Deckhand," Marguerite said quietly.

"Ma'am?"

"Where does the fog thin? Think before you answer."

Nell thought. She thought about sea smoke and cold air and the islands beyond, about the way fog banks died against warmer shallows, about the chart she'd memorized during the night watches because there was nothing else to do. "Northeast," she said. "The water shoals toward the drowned town. Warmer shallows. The fog will break up over them like breath on a window." She hesitated. "It'll cost us the rest of this tide to get there slow and quiet. Maybe more."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she was weighing, it wasn't just the course.

"Then we lose the tide," Marguerite said. "All of it. We sit in this fog until it dies naturally, and we come out northeast, over the drowned town, on the next set." She turned to the others. "Tam, we run the engine at bare steerage way, nothing more. Ilya, rotate the watches — two hours on, and nobody sleeps deeper than a cat. We are a hole in the ocean until dark."

"A hole in the ocean," Tam repeated. "I'll put it in the log. The navy will never think to look for a hole."

* * *

Dusk came down through the fog like ash through water, and with it the fog began to die, thinning in rags, opening windows onto a sea gone violet and calm. The navy horns had stopped hours ago. Whether that meant the search had shifted or the trap had simply been reset somewhere ahead, nobody would say aloud.

Nell had the wheel again for the evening watch, and this time she wasn't alone. Marguerite stood beside her, arms folded, watching the fog unravel.

"You did well today," Marguerite said.

Nell's throat went tight. "I just — I saw the smoke."

"You saw the smoke, and you called it clean, and then you saw the fog, and you spoke up when speaking up was the harder thing. Most people wait to be sure. The sea doesn't wait." Marguerite's eyes stayed on the horizon. "My father used to say a crew isn't a list of names. It's whoever's standing next to you when the smoke shows up. Today that was you."

Nell looked down at the wheel, at her own hands on the spokes, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't crack her open.

Ahead, the last rags of fog tore away, and the sea opened northeastward — dark water, rising stars, and far off, where the shallows warmed, a faint broken glitter where the drowned town's rooftops caught the first starlight through six feet of sea.

And beyond it, past the town, past the shoals, at the very edge of seeing, a line of lights that shouldn't have been there. Steady white riding lights. Too many of them. Too evenly spaced.

Nell found them first. She always would remember that — that she found them first.

"Ma'am," she said, and her voice didn't shake at all. "Lights. Bearing oh-four-five. A lot of them."

Marguerite raised the glasses. A long stillness. When she lowered them, her face was gray in the starlight.

"That's not a patrol," she said. "That's a fleet at anchor." She turned toward the companionway, already moving. "Get Odile. Get everyone. Wake the dead if you have to."

Behind them, the fog bank dissolved into ordinary night. Ahead, the navy waited between the *Fairweather* and the truth, and the tide was turning whether they were ready or not.
