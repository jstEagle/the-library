## 8. The Drowned Town

The fog let them go grudgingly, the way a hand lets go of a wrist.

For six hours they'd sat in that gray blindness with sails down and engine idling low, listening to the navy tender's screws churn somewhere to the east—close enough that Nell had counted the beats of it against her palm flat on the hull. Then the sound had faded, and the fog had thinned from wool to gauze to nothing, and there was the coast again: black rock, white surf, and the long skeletal arm of an old loading pier reaching out into the water like something begging to be pulled ashore.

Behind the pier, half-drowned in its own harbor, lay the town of Sallow's Reach.

Marguerite stood at the bow rail and looked at it and felt the compass shift in her pocket as if it too were looking.

"Is that on any chart?" Odile asked beside her. She'd been uncharacteristically quiet since the fog—since Nell, sixteen years old and shaking so hard her teeth chattered, had talked them through the hide with a clarity none of the rest of them could manage. Odile kept glancing at the girl now, Marguerite noticed. Recalculating something.

"It's on mine," Ilya said from the helm. "Barely. A footnote. *Harbor silted, landing impracticable.*"

"Impracticable," Tam repeated, coming up from below with grease to his elbows. "That's one word for it."

The town had not drowned all at once. That was the thing about Sallow's Reach—you could see the stages of it written in the rooftops. The upper streets still stood dry on the shoulder of the hill, shuttered and salt-bleached but lived-in; smoke rose from two or three chimneys in thin, defiant threads. The middle streets stood knee-deep at high tide, doors opening onto water, staircases descending into green murk. And the lowest streets—the old waterfront, the fish market, the customs house—were simply gone beneath the surface, visible only as shadows and pale suggestions, a second town lying under the first like a body under a sheet.

"The Gideon's home port," Marguerite said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"You knew?" Odile turned to look at her properly.

"I knew the name. My father—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "He used to say he'd retire here. Buy one of the dry houses on the hill and grow things that didn't need him to go anywhere." Her hand found the compass through the fabric of her coat. "I thought the town was gone. Evacuated after the sinking."

"Evacuated," Ilya said, "is what the navy's report says."

"And yet." Tam gestured at the chimneys.

They took the boat in slow, past the pier's rotten pilings, past a church tower rising straight out of the harbor with its bell still hanging in it, green with weed, silent for twenty years. Children watched them from a rooftop—actual children, three of them, perched on the ridge tiles like cormorants—and did not wave. One of them blew a whistle, three short notes, and by the time they'd found a mooring among the drowned doorframes of the middle streets, people were already gathered on the raised walkway above the waterline, watching them come in.

Not hostile, exactly. Not welcoming either. The way you watch weather.

* * *

Her name was Maren Kestrel, though no one told them that at first. She was simply the one who came forward when the others hung back—an old woman in a man's oilskin coat, small and upright as a mast, with white hair cut short and hands that had clearly spent seventy years doing work that didn't care how old she was.

"You're not navy," she said. It wasn't a question.

"We're salvage," Marguerite said. "Independent."

"Mm." The woman's eyes went over each of them in turn—Ilya's size, Tam's tools, Nell trying very hard to stand like someone who hadn't been terrified six hours ago, Odile's city coat and city boots. They lingered last on Marguerite herself, on her face, and something moved behind them that Marguerite couldn't read. "Salvage of what?"

"Wrecks."

"Which wrecks?"

Marguerite held her gaze. "You know which."

A murmur went along the walkway behind the old woman—not anger, Marguerite realized. Fear. Twenty years, and these people still lowered their voices around the name of a ship that had sunk off their own coast.

"We need shelter for one tide," Marguerite said. "Water, mostly. We'll pay in trade—brass, canvas, tools. We'll be gone before dark tomorrow."

The old woman considered this. Then she said, "You'll bring your boat up to the dry street. Not here. The harbor's watched." And she turned and walked inland without checking whether they followed, which somehow made following feel less like a choice than a tide.

* * *

The house she gave them had been a chandlery once; you could still read the ghost of the sign on its lintel. It smelled of lamp oil and dried kelp and, underneath everything, the sea—the whole town smelled of the sea, Marguerite thought, because the sea had never finished taking it. Through the back window you could see the flooded street below, and once, passing, the pale shape of a doorway underwater, its paint still legible: *HALLORAN & SONS, ROPES AND CANVAS.*

Tam and Ilya went back to secure the boat. Nell got sent to help carry water up from the hill cisterns, and went gladly—Marguerite suspected the girl needed to be useful the way a fever needs to break. Which left Marguerite and Odile sitting across a scarred table from Maren Kestrel, drinking tea brewed from something that grew on the sea wall, while the old woman studied them both like a chart she was deciding whether to trust.

"Twenty years," Odile said, into the silence. "The navy reported this coast evacuated. Resettlement complete."

"The navy reports a great many things." Maren set down her cup. "When the Gideon went down, they came within the week. Officers. They told us the sinking was a boiler failure, that the loss of life was regrettable, that the area would be closed to fishing for safety reasons. They offered compensation to families who signed papers saying they accepted the finding." Her voice was level, unhurried, worn smooth like a stone. "Half the town signed. Half the town had already lost sons to the press gangs and knew what navy paper was worth. We stayed. You can't evacuate people who refuse to be evacuated, not really. Not without making it obvious why."

"Why would they bother?" Odile asked. "One wreck. One town."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. "You're the one with the permit," she said. "Word travels ahead of boats, even in fog. Vasseur. Concession holder." Something in her tone made Odile sit straighter. "You're diving for the strongroom."

"I'm employed to recover certain materials from—"

"Yes," Maren said. "Materials." She rose, joints cracking like rigging in a blow, and went to a cabinet in the corner. When she turned back, she was holding something wrapped in oilcloth, and her hands had changed—they'd gone careful, the way hands go around a wound.

"Before you answer any more of her questions," she said to Marguerite, "show me what's in your pocket. The thing you've been touching since you walked in, like a rosary. Show me."

Marguerite's mouth went dry. She hadn't known she'd been touching it. She drew out the compass—brass case gone soft-edged with handling, glass clouded, needle swinging in its own private weather—and laid it on the table between them.

Maren Kestrel looked at it and made a sound Marguerite had never heard a grown person make. Small. Broken at one edge. Grief, Marguerite thought, that has been waiting twenty years for somewhere to put itself down.

"That's Elias Yarrow's compass," the old woman said.

The room tilted slightly. Marguerite gripped the table's edge.

"He carried it on every run. He'd have it out on the bridge even when the ship had proper instruments, turning it over and over in his fingers. Superstition, the other officers called it." She touched the case with one finger, light as communion. "His wife had it engraved on the inside. Did you never look?"

"I—" Marguerite's voice failed. She'd had the compass since she was eleven years old. She'd pried at the case backing once, as a child, given up. She took the compass back with hands that did not feel like hers, worked the thumbnail into the seam the way she suddenly remembered her father showing her, years ago, laughing—*you have to want it open, little bird, it won't open for the idle*—

The backing came away.

Inside, scratched into the brass in a fine, sure hand:

*For M. — who will find her way. — E.Y.*

And beneath it, smaller, cramped, added later, in a hurry, the letters deeper where the tool had pressed hard:

*If it points wrong, follow it anyway.*

Marguerite stared at it until the letters blurred. Around her the room went on—Odile saying something, Maren answering—but it reached her as if through hull plating, muffled, distant. Her father's handwriting. Her father's actual hand, pressed into metal twenty years ago aboard a ship that the navy said had died of a boiler failure, writing instructions like a man who'd known he might not be there to give them in person.

"He knew," she said finally. Her voice came out scraped. "He knew something was going to happen."

Maren sat back down. For a while she just looked at her, and Marguerite understood that the old woman was seeing someone else—seeing the girl Marguerite had been, maybe, or seeing Elias himself in the set of her jaw.

"There are pages," Maren said quietly. "Logbook pages. He gave them to my son the night before the Gideon sailed on her last run. Told him to keep them out of the ship, whatever happened. My son drowned with the ship anyway—he was gunner's crew that season—but he'd left the pages home with me." She folded her hands. "I've kept them twenty years waiting for someone they belonged to. I'd begun to think no one was coming."

Odile had gone very still. Marguerite felt the stillness like a change in wind—her rival's mind running fast behind a smooth face, recalculating everything, and Marguerite found she didn't care. Let her calculate. Some things weren't cargo.

"Bring them," Marguerite said.

* * *

The pages were four, written in pencil, water-stained at the edges but legible, and Maren laid them out on the table with the tenderness of a surgeon. Marguerite read the first lines and the floor of the world shifted again.

It was her father's log, all right—the same hand, the same shorthand abbreviations he'd taught her without ever explaining why. But it wasn't navigation. It was testimony.

*Third watch. Strongroom inventory conducted under Adm. Corvane's direct order, contrary to articles. Cargo manifests do not match hold contents. I have counted twice.*

*I am told the "mutineers" are being held below. I have seen no mutiny. I have seen men ask questions.*

Marguerite read it twice, three times, the words refusing to arrange themselves into anything survivable. Mutiny. The word every record used, the word that had hung over her childhood like smoke—the reason the pension was denied, the reason the family name went sour in certain rooms, the reason her mother had stopped speaking of him entirely. Mutiny, and here was her father's pencil saying *I have seen no mutiny* in letters deep enough to score the wood beneath.

"They called it a mutiny," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "They told the families their husbands and fathers were traitors. They denied the pensions because—" Because traitors' families get nothing. Because shame is cheaper than truth. Because a massacre dressed as a mutiny costs the admiralty nothing but ink.

"Now you understand why we stayed," Maren said. "Why we keep our voices low. Admiral Corvane is not a man who tolerates loose pages, Miss Yarrow. Or loose towns."

Outside, the light was going copper with evening. Somewhere below the window, in the drowned street, water lapped gently at a doorframe that had been waiting twenty years for someone to come home. Marguerite became aware that Odile was watching her—not with triumph, not with pity, but with something that looked almost like fear, and that frightened Marguerite more than anything else in the room.

Because Odile knew who her patron was. Odile had always known. And if the strongroom held what these four pages said it held—

"I need the rest of tonight," Marguerite said, folding the pages along their old creases with hands that shook only a little. "And then we're going to talk about what your employer thinks is in that strongroom. All of it, Odile. Before another tide turns."

Odile met her eyes. For once, she didn't smile.

Above the drowned town, the first star came out over the silent bell tower, and in Marguerite's pocket, the compass swung—away from north, away from the sea lanes, pointing steady and true along the Gideon's last patrol route, like a finger raised in answer to a question twenty years old.
