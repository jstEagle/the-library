## 9. Her Father's Log

The old woman's name was Maren Sol, and she poured tea like she was arming a weapon.

She set three cups on the driftwood table—chipped enamel, mismatched, one with a hairline crack running through it like a river on a map—and filled each without asking whether anyone wanted any. In the drowned town, Marguerite was learning, you didn't ask. You offered, and you watched what people did with the offer.

The house leaned. Everything in Saltmere leaned—the whole village had been half-swallowed when the Gideon went down and the seawall failed with it, and the survivors had simply stayed, building upward as the water crept through their streets. The ground floor of Maren's house was flooded to the windowsills. She'd nailed planks across the gap where the front door used to be and entered her own home by ladder, like everyone else in Saltmere, like it was nothing.

"You'll want to dry off before I show you anything," Maren said. "Paper and saltwater are enemies."

Marguerite sat wrapped in a blanket that smelled of peat smoke and tar, her hands around the cracked cup, and tried not to stare at the woman across from her. Grey braid coiled at her neck. Hands like knotted rope. Eyes the color of shallow water over sand—pale, clear, and impossible to see the bottom of.

"You knew my father," Marguerite said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I knew Elias Yarrow for eleven years." Maren drank her tea. "I knew him better in the last six days than most men manage in a lifetime. Now." She set down the cup. "You said the compass points wrong."

"It doesn't point wrong," Marguerite said. "It points somewhere else. Along the Gideon's old patrol line. I've checked it against charts until my eyes bled. It's not deviation. It's not damage. It's—" She stopped herself before she said *it's deliberate*, because saying it out loud made it real, and real things had weight, and she wasn't sure she could carry more weight tonight.

Maren nodded slowly, as if Marguerite had confirmed something she'd suspected for twenty years.

"Then it's time." She rose, joints cracking like rigging in a blow, and climbed the ladder to the dry loft above.

* * *

The loft was small and warm and crammed with a life: dried herbs hanging from beams, a loom with a half-finished weave, photographs in tin frames gone soft-edged with damp. And a sea chest, iron-banded, its lock long since broken and replaced with a leather strap.

Maren knelt before it the way other people kneel at altars.

"Elias kept this aboard the Gideon," she said. "His logbook. Captains keep logs, but your father kept *this*—a second one, private, written small so it would fit in his coat pocket. He brought pages ashore whenever we put in here. He was careful. He thought he was being careful." Her mouth twisted. "Careful men were the first ones the navy noticed."

"Where did you get these?" Marguerite asked, though her heart already knew, and was bracing itself.

"The last time he came through Saltmere, he left them with me. Forty-one pages. He said—" Maren's voice caught, just once, a hitch like a rope snagging. "He said if he didn't come back for them within the year, I should give them to whoever came asking with his compass. And that if nobody ever did, I should burn them, because some truths are only worth the risk if someone's alive to carry them."

The fire popped in the little stove. Outside, the tide moved through the drowned street below the window, a slow dark breathing.

"He never came back," Marguerite said.

"He never came back." Maren lifted the strap. "Twenty years I've kept these. Twenty years I've waited for the compass to walk through my door. And here it is, in the pocket of a girl who has his chin and his stubbornness and, if I'm any judge, his talent for finding trouble." She drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and held it out across the space between them. "Take them."

Marguerite didn't move. Her hands stayed wrapped around the cooling cup.

"I've spent twenty years thinking he abandoned us," she said. "Or died stupid. Or ran. There were rumors. There were always rumors—that he turned coat, that he was selling navy charts, that he scuttled with the ship rather than face a court-martial. My mother believed none of them and it killed her anyway, believing cost too much. And you're telling me he planned this. He *planned*—"

"He planned for the truth to survive him," Maren said gently. "There's no greater act of faith a man can perform. Read."

* * *

The oilcloth fell open in Marguerite's lap and the smell rose up out of it—ink and salt and age, and underneath, faintly, tobacco, her father's pipe, and for one vertiginous moment she was eight years old again sitting on his knee while he read her weather signs off the horizon.

The handwriting was small and slanted, cramped to fit the pocket-sized pages. Dates in the margins. Sketches—her father had drawn the way he talked, quick and precise, all angles and intent. A plan of a corridor. A door. A lock mechanism rendered in cross-section with annotations in his tight engineer's hand.

Marguerite read. The loft, the fire, Maren watching—all of it receded, and there was only the voice on the page, her father's voice, speaking across twenty years of silence.

*Third day running the eastern shelf. Crew grumbling about the route. We are not surveying fish, whatever the fleet orders say.*

*Spoke to Voss today about the sealed compartment aft of the magazine. He laughed at me. Then he stopped laughing. "You don't want to know what's behind that door, Yarrow." I have known Tammas Voss fifteen years. He has never once told me not to know something.*

*Counted the crates going into the strongroom during resupply. Manifest says chart corrections. Chart corrections do not weigh four hundred pounds apiece or arrive under armed escort at midnight. I copied the escort roster. Names I will not write here. Some of those names outrank this ship.*

*I have begun keeping this book because I no longer trust the official one. If you are reading this, I am dead or worse, and the official record is a lie. Start with the date of the eleventh. Whatever they tell you happened on the eleventh, invert it.*

Marguerite's breath had gone shallow. She turned pages with fingers that wanted to shake and refused to.

*The eleventh.* She knew the eleventh. Every salvage diver on the coast knew the eleventh—the night the Gideon went down, officially lost to a mine strike, all hands mourned in a single paragraph in the shipping gazette. The night her father vanished into the water along with two hundred and thirty souls.

The next pages were dated the ninth and tenth. Then the eleventh itself.

*They have armed the strongroom watch with our own sidearms. Voss came to me at midnight, grey as paper. He told me what is in the crates. I made him say it twice. Gold is the least of it. There are papers. Orders. Signed orders, in the admiral's own hand, for things that were done to towns on this coast—towns like this one—and the gold is payment that was never meant to be seen by auditors. They mean to sink the evidence with the ship. That is what tomorrow is. Tomorrow is not an accident. Tomorrow is a filing system.*

Marguerite looked up. The fire had burned low. Maren sat perfectly still, watching her, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the page hadn't already said.

*Voss and I and six others mean to move the papers out before the charges go. Not the gold—let the admiralty drown with their gold. The papers. If the papers surface, the eleventh becomes what it actually was, and the men who ordered it answer for it. If we fail—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. The next page was different. Faster, the letters jagged, the ink blotted where a wet or shaking hand had pressed too hard.

*It is happening early. They have moved the timetable. Voss is dead—I saw it, I saw them do it, and they saw me see it. I have the papers in the strongroom antechamber, sealed in a tool chest behind the third bulkhead plate. Compass bearing 214 true from the main hatch, forty feet, port side. The compass will find it. I have keyed the compass. God forgive me, I have keyed the compass, and if my girl ever holds it, may it lead her better than it led me.*

*If anyone finds this: it was not a mine. It was not a mutiny. There was no mutiny. Write it down. Make them write it down.*

The last line was almost illegible, scratched deep:

*Tell Marguerite the debt was never hers.*

Marguerite closed the logbook pages and could not breathe.

* * *

The loft was very quiet. Somewhere below, the tide knocked softly against the drowned doorframe, patient as a creditor.

"The mutiny," Marguerite said finally, and her voice came out sanded flat. "The official record says there was a mutiny. That loyal officers put it down and the ship was lost in the fighting. That's the story. That's the story my mother was handed in a sealed envelope by a lieutenant who wouldn't meet her eyes."

"That's the story," Maren agreed. "And the men who survived—there were eleven who made it to shore in the ship's boat, and every one of them swore to the mutiny story at the inquiry, and every one of them lived comfortably afterward, and every one of them died quiet deaths in warm beds, and not one of them ever slept well, if the ones who came through Saltmere for a drink were any measure. One of them—wept into his beer every anniversary until the year he didn't come anymore. Drowned himself off the point, they said. Accidental, they said." She snorted. "This coast says a great many things *they said*."

"And the admiral." Marguerite's hands had closed around the oilcloth bundle hard enough to whiten her knuckles. "The admiral whose name is in these pages. Is he still—"

"Admirals retire," Maren said. "Admirals get statues. Admirals have sons, and nephews, and protégés, and the protégés end up running salvage fleets." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "You told me you have six tides before the navy's own salvage ships reach the Gideon. Ask yourself what the navy wants with a wreck it forbade anyone to name for twenty years. Ask yourself why now. Ask yourself what's still down there worth blowing a hole in the seabed for."

Marguerite stood up. She hadn't decided to stand; her body simply did it, the way a spring releases.

"They're going to destroy it," she said. "The papers. Whatever's left. They're going to close the strongroom forever and call it structural hazard mitigation, and my father stays a mutineer, and Voss stays a traitor, and two hundred and thirty people stay a footnote, and the admiral stays a statue." She was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the loft too small for it and not caring. "Twenty years. Twenty years my mother wrote petitions that came back unopened. Twenty years I dove that wreck for brass fittings and scrap copper while my father's actual words sat forty feet from the main hatch at a bearing of two-one-four, and I *held the key*—"

"Stop." Maren's voice cracked across the room like a halyard in a gust. Marguerite stopped. "Sit down, girl, and listen, because fury is fuel but it burns fast and hot and it will cook you before it cooks anyone else. Your father knew that. It's why he wrote instead of shouted. It's why he hid the papers instead of parading them. He wasn't a man of fury. He was a man of *purpose*, and the difference between the two is the difference between a flash fire and a forge."

Marguerite sat. Slowly. Her jaw ached from clenching.

"What do I do?"

Maren took the logbook pages from her lap, squared them with the tenderness of long practice, and rewrapped the oilcloth—but she left the top fold open, the first page visible, her father's handwriting facing up.

"You do what he did. You think like a salvager." She tapped the page. "Evidence is cargo. Cargo needs a route, a vessel, and a buyer. Your route is the Gideon. Your vessel—" she looked pointedly toward the harbor, toward the boat where Odile Vasseur and five crewmates waited, "—is whatever you can trust. And your buyer is everyone. You don't sell the truth to one person, girl. You make it worthless to hide by making it priceless to everyone."

"I don't even know if the papers survived twenty years underwater."

"Tool chest. Sealed. Behind a bulkhead plate." Maren's pale eyes held hers. "Your father built things to last. He built *you* to last. Go down and look. But hear me on one thing first."

Marguerite waited.

"Odile Vasseur didn't find you by accident, and she didn't pick this moment by chance. Someone told her where to point that concession permit, and someone wants that strongroom opened *for them*. So when you dive—and you will dive, I can see it in your face, you were always going to dive—you dive knowing that the person swimming beside you may be there to take, not to help. Trust carefully. Verify everything. And keep the compass on your body, not in your kit." She pressed the bundle back into Marguerite's hands and folded Marguerite's fingers over it. "Your father trusted the wrong people on the eleventh. Don't you dare do the same."

* * *

Marguerite walked back down to the harbor alone, taking the plank-walks over the flooded streets while the last light drained out of the sky. The bundle rode inside her jacket, against her ribs, over her heartbeat. It weighed almost nothing. Forty-one pages of paper. It weighed more than the boat.

At the dock, the *Kestrel's* lantern was lit and she could hear the crew's voices—Tam arguing numbers, Nell laughing at something, Odile's low measured tone threading through it all like a current under calm water. Home sounds. Almost-home sounds.

Her father's last line kept surfacing in her mind, over and over, like something buoyant refusing to stay sunk.

*Tell Marguerite the debt was never hers.*

All her life she'd carried it—the wreck, the shame, the name nobody would say, the creditors, the grinding salvage work on the very ship that ate him—as if grief were a loan she'd cosigned at birth. And he'd spent his final hour, drowning, hunted, writing in a shaking hand, trying to reach across twenty years and tear up the note.

She stopped at the head of the gangway and looked east, where the sea went black beyond the point, toward the shoal where the Gideon lay. Two-one-four true. Forty feet off the main hatch. Third bulkhead plate, port side.

Fury was there—white-hot, singing in her blood, wanting to burn everything the admiral had built. But underneath it, steadier, something else was setting its keel: purpose. A heading. A bearing she could hold.

She stepped aboard.

Tam looked up from his ledger, and whatever he saw in her face made him close the book slowly. "Marguerite? What happened up there?"

She put her hand flat against her jacket, over the pages, over her father's voice.

"Change of plans," she said. "We're not diving for the strongroom's gold." She looked past Tam, deliberately, to where Odile Vasseur stood at the rail, watching her with those careful merchant's eyes. "We're diving for what's behind the third bulkhead plate. And Odile—I want to know, right now, exactly who sent you."

Odile's hand stilled on the rail. And in the lantern light, Marguerite saw it—the flicker, the half-second hesitation of a woman deciding which lie to spend.

Behind her, unheard by either of them, Tam's intercepted wireless set crackled softly in the deckhouse, a coded message ticking in from a shore station, addressed to a patron whose name would change everything.
