## 10. Odile's Patron

Tam found the message the way he found most things: by being awake when everyone else had given up on the day.

It was past two in the morning and the *Kestrel* rocked at anchor off Sable Point, riding a swell that made the lamp swing and throw shadows like something pacing. Below decks, the boat smelled of bilgewater, tar, and Odile Vasseur's expensive perfume, which had gotten into everything since she came aboard — into the cushions, into the galley, into Tam's own bunk somehow, though he'd never once let her near it. He'd taken to sleeping in the engine room. It was warmer there, and it didn't lie to him.

The radio did.

He wasn't even listening for anything in particular. That was the thing people got wrong about him, that he was listening for something specific. You didn't find the needle by looking for the needle. You listened to the whole haystack. So he sat with his headset on and his tea gone cold and let the night bands wash over him — weather bulletins from three stations that disagreed with each other, a fishing co-op arguing about quotas, some preacher on a shortwave frequency selling salvation at four dollars a minute — and then, tucked between a navigation warning and dead air, a burst of code.

Not navy traffic. Navy traffic had a rhythm, crisp as drill. This was different: five groups, repeated twice, then silence. The kind of transmission you sent when you wanted one receiver only, and you wanted that receiver to know exactly how much trouble she was in if anyone else heard it.

Tam wrote it down without thinking about why. His hands were ahead of his brain, the way they always were. By the time his brain caught up, he'd already run the first group through the old merchant cipher his father had taught him, the one salvage crews used because the navy used it too and never imagined poor men could read.

The first group decoded clean. The second didn't. The third was garbage. The fourth—

The fourth was a name.

Not a name, exactly. A callsign. *KESTREL-6*, which meant nothing, and then beneath it, in the fifth group, half-garbled but unmistakable once you saw the shape of it: *VASSER CONFIRM DELIVERY WINDOW TIDE SIX NO EXTENSION.*

Vasser. Vasseur.

Tam took the headset off very slowly, the way you'd take your hand off a snake you'd just realized was venomous.

* * *

He didn't sleep after that. He worked.

By lamplight, in the engine room where nobody would think to look for him, he rebuilt the transmission piece by piece. The second group wasn't cipher at all — it was a routing header, stripped of its origin station but not its origin *format*. And the format told him things. The compression scheme, the padding, the little bureaucratic fingerprints of how an organization organized itself. Tam had spent six years working harbor comms for a naval yard before he'd gotten drunk one night and told a lieutenant commander exactly what he thought of him, and he knew those fingerprints the way you know handwriting.

This was fleet-level traffic. Not coastal patrol. Not harbor command. Flag rank.

He sat back and let that settle, and it settled badly, like ballast loaded wrong.

Odile Vasseur had a patron. Fine. Everyone with money had a patron; Marguerite had creditors, Ilya had ghosts, Nell had nobody, which was its own kind of patron. But Odile's patron ran fleet-grade encryption and set delivery windows measured in tides, and the Gideon — the wreck nobody named, the job they'd all sworn off, the sealed strongroom six fathoms down that they were now racing a navy salvage fleet to reach —

The strongroom somebody at flag rank wanted opened *fast* and *quiet*.

Tam thought about the admiral whose name came up whenever the Gideon came up, always sideways, always in the passive voice. *The order was given. The decision was made. Questions were raised and answered.* Vice Admiral Corvane. The man who'd commanded the flotilla the night the Gideon went down. The man whose report — the one copy that ever surfaced — described a mutiny, a scuttling charge, heroes and traitors, case closed, file sealed.

Marguerite's father had been aboard. Marguerite's father had not come home. Marguerite carried that like a stone sewn into her coat, and Tam had watched her carry it for eleven years, and he had never once said what he actually believed, which was that reports written by admirals about their own disasters were worth exactly the paper and the ink.

Now here was a coded message, fleet-format, telling Odile Vasseur that tide six was a hard deadline.

And out there, somewhere past the horizon, a navy salvage fleet was steaming toward the same wreck on a schedule that ended at — Tam checked his tide tables, because he was Tam and he checked everything — tide six.

Coincidence was a word for people who hadn't done the arithmetic.

* * *

Morning came up gray and mean through the hatch. Tam climbed out of the engine room with the decoded fragments folded into oilskin paper inside his shirt, against his chest, like contraband. Which is what it was. He felt every stair.

On deck, the crew was doing morning. Nell was coiling line with the exaggerated care of someone who'd been yelled at about it recently. Ilya stood at the rail drinking coffee and watching the fog burn off the water like it owed him money. Odile was asleep below — or pretending to be — and Marguerite was at the chart table with the compass open in front of her, staring at it the way she'd been staring at it for two days, ever since she'd figured out it pointed along the Gideon's old patrol route instead of north.

"Tam." She didn't look up. "You've got a face."

"I've always got a face."

"You've got a *face*. What is it?"

He glanced at Nell. At Ilya. Ilya set down his coffee, which was Ilya's way of saying *whatever this is, I'm already in it.*

"Chart room," Tam said. "Now. And wake Vasseur."

Marguerite looked up at that. "Why?"

"Because either she needs to hear this," Tam said, "or we need to watch her face while she doesn't. Either way, I want her in the room."

* * *

She came in wrapped in a borrowed blanket, hair loose, looking less like a rival salvage queen and more like what she probably was underneath: tired. She clocked the room immediately — Marguerite behind the chart table, arms crossed; Ilya by the door, which he'd closed; Nell perched on the locker, wide-eyed, because Marguerite had decided Nell stayed, and Tam agreed, because Nell deserved to know what she was sailing toward.

"Someone died?" Odile asked lightly.

"Someone transmitted." Tam laid the oilskin packet on the chart table and unfolded it. "Two-forty this morning, burst transmission, five groups, repeated twice. Fleet-grade format. I broke what breaks." He turned the paper so they could all read it. "*Vasser confirm delivery window, tide six, no extension.*"

Silence. The kind with weight in it.

"That's my name," Odile said.

"It is."

"And you intercepted my mail."

"I intercepted traffic on an open band addressed to no registered station. If it's your mail, you're receiving coded fleet transmissions on a salvage boat, which raises questions of its own." Tam kept his voice flat. Flatness was a tool. He'd learned it in the engine room, where panic and steam pressure obeyed the same rule: bleed it slow or it blows. "So let's raise them. Who's Vasser talking to?"

Odile's face did something complicated and fast — Tam watched it the way he'd promised himself he would — surprise first, genuine, he thought, then calculation, then a flicker of something under both that looked a lot like fear before she shut the lid on it.

"My broker," she said.

"Your broker uses fleet encryption."

"My broker has *connections*—"

"Your broker," Tam said, "sets deadlines that match the navy salvage fleet's arrival window to the hour. Tide six. No extension. I pulled the fleet's published schedule last night from the harbor board — it's public, they don't hide it, why would they — and their survey window closes at—" he tapped the tide table pinned above the chart rack— "tide six. Your patron isn't racing the navy, Vasseur. Your patron *is* the navy."

Nell made a small sound. Nobody looked at her. Everybody understood her.

"That's a leap," Odile said.

"It's a walk. Watch." Tam pulled the second sheet, the one he'd worked until dawn. "The transmission format matches fleet flag traffic. Not coastal, not customs — flag. There are maybe nine officers in this district who originate traffic in this format, and eight of them are administratively incapable of caring about a drowned frigate. The ninth—" he let it hang— "is Vice Admiral Corvane. Whose name, in case anyone's forgotten, is on the only report ever filed about the Gideon's sinking. Mutiny. Scuttle charge. Case sealed." He looked around the little room, at all of them. "The man who buried the ship wants the strongroom opened before his own salvage fleet can seal it properly. And he's using us to do it."

* * *

The argument lasted an hour, and Tam mostly let other people have it, because he'd already had his in the dark with the radio.

Ilya wanted to put Odile off at Sable Point in the dinghy and be done with her. "We swore," he kept saying, and each time he said it his voice dropped lower, until the last time it was barely air. "We swore off that wreck. We buried Pieter with that oath. Now we find out we've been sailing her errands since the day she stepped aboard."

"We haven't opened anything yet," Marguerite said.

"We've *sailed*."

Marguerite turned to Odile. Tam watched her do it — watched her hold the question in her mouth a moment before asking it, the way she did, like words cost money. "Is it true?"

Odile met her eyes. Whatever was behind hers, she kept it behind them. "The deadline's real. The rest is his arithmetic."

"Then answer the arithmetic."

Odile was quiet long enough that the hull creaked twice. When she spoke, her voice had lost its polish. "I have a backer. I've never met him. Letters, payments, instructions through intermediaries. He holds my concession permit — the real one, the one that keeps the navy from seizing my boat and my crew. He offered me the Gideon contract eight months ago. Delivery of strongroom contents by tide six, payment on completion, and I'll be honest with you, Marguerite, the number would have bought us both harbors." Her jaw tightened. "I didn't ask who he was. That was stupid. Maybe worse than stupid. But I needed this job, and you needed this job, and neither of us was going to get it by asking polite questions."

"So you knew the deadline matched the fleet," Tam said.

"I knew the deadline. I assumed my patron had sources inside the fleet." A beat. "I didn't assume he *was* the fleet."

"Assumed," Ilya said, from the door. It wasn't a question and it wasn't forgiveness.

"There's something else," Odile said, and now Tam heard it, the thing under the fear, the thing she'd been carrying since Sable Point. "If I miss the window — if the contents aren't delivered — the concession dies, and everything it protects dies with it. My boat. My crew. My—" she stopped herself, recalibrated, and Tam marked the stop, filed it. "There are consequences. For people who aren't on this boat."

"Your brother," Nell said quietly.

Everyone turned. Nell shrank half an inch, then held. "You talk in your sleep. On the crossing. You said his name."

For the first time since she'd come aboard, Odile Vasseur looked young. "Yes," she said. "My brother."

* * *

They voted with the hatches dogged and the engine idling, because Ilya insisted any vote about the Gideon happen with the sound of the sea in it, and nobody argued with Ilya about ritual anymore.

The options were simple, which didn't make them easy. Turn back: lose the fee, keep the oath, hand the strongroom to Corvane's fleet untouched. Confront Odile fully, cut her out, sail on alone: possible, except none of them knew the approach channels she'd memorized, and the fleet was closing by the hour. Or the third thing, the thing Tam had been building toward since two-forty in the morning, the thing that tasted like cold metal and clarity:

Use her.

"She's a channel," Tam said, when it was his turn. "Corvane thinks she's his tool. Fine. Tools transmit. Every instruction she gets, every deadline, every change of plan — I want it logged, timed, and decoded. She sails with us, she reports to us. Not because we trust her. Because right now she's the only wire we have into a vice admiral's head, and I intend to read his mail until the day we scuttle his secrets or he sinks us."

"And her brother?" Marguerite asked.

Tam looked at Odile. Odile looked back, chin up, hands flat on the table, a woman daring the room to make her beg.

"Her brother's leverage Corvane has," Tam said slowly. "Which means her brother is also a reason she can't be bought back off our side cheaply. I hate that math. I do it anyway." He spread his hands. "We don't trust her. We *verify* her. Everything."

The vote went three to one, Ilya against, Nell abstaining because Marguerite ruled she was too new to carry it. Ilya took the loss the way he took everything, silently, and went up on deck, and a while later Tam heard him checking the lifelines one by one, which was Ilya's way of saying he'd stay, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it.

* * *

Afterward, in the chart room, Marguerite stood over the compass with its brass case open, the needle swinging its wrong, patient arc along the dead patrol route. Tam watched her from the doorway.

"Your father's ship," he said. "Corvane's cover-up. Same wreck, same night. That's not coincidence either, is it. That's why the compass came to you."

"I don't know what it is yet." She closed the case. Her thumb lingered on the hinge. "But I know someone who's been lying to us by omission for three weeks, and I know her patron writes orders in an admiral's hand. Sooner or later, Tam, those two facts are going to meet."

"They met this morning."

"No." Marguerite shook her head, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before — not grief, not anger, something harder and more patient than either. Something that had waited eleven years and could wait a little longer. "This morning we learned who's pulling her strings. Tonight I ask her what she knows about the mutiny. And if she lies to me—" the compass clicked shut— "then we'll finally know exactly what she's worth."

Above them, footsteps crossed the deck. Odile's, light and even, walking to the rail to take the evening watch — right on schedule, punctual as a transmission.

Tam made a note of the time.
