## 11. Confrontation in the Hold

Odile heard the hatch close behind her and knew, before she turned, that she had made a mistake in the timing of it.

She'd come down to the hold to check the dive gear—legitimate work, necessary work, the kind of errand that looked like nothing—and she'd let herself believe the soft creak of the deck above was just Yarrow's boat settling into the swell. But the Gull was a small ship, and small ships have no privacy, and Marguerite Yarrow had been watching her for two days with the patience of a woman who used to lose to her and had decided never to lose again.

The lantern swung on its hook. Crates of brass and rope. The spare air bottles racked along the starboard side, their gauges catching light. And Marguerite standing at the foot of the ladder with a folded piece of paper in her hand, holding it the way you hold something you don't want to touch.

"Tam intercepted a message," Marguerite said. "Two nights ago. I've been waiting for you to make your move first."

"Have you."

"You didn't." Marguerite came down the last two rungs and stood on the deck plates, boots planted wide, the way sailors stand when they expect the floor to argue with them. "So here we are."

Odile kept her voice level. She had spent eleven years learning to keep her voice level in rooms where men decided things about her life, and this was not going to be the room where the skill failed her. "If you've read my correspondence—"

"I read four words before Tam stopped transcribing. He said he couldn't stomach any more of it." Marguerite held out the paper. "Admiral Corvane. Your patron. The man who signed the order that sank the Gideon's story."

The paper hung in the space between them. Odile did not take it.

She had rehearsed this. Not this exact moment—she wasn't a fool, she'd known exposure was possible since the fog bank, since Nell's white face at the rail—but some version of it. The denial. The counterattack. *You're reading my private mail, Marguerite, that's piracy even by your standards.* She had a whole architecture of deflection built and ready, load-bearing walls and all.

What she said instead was: "How much did he tell you?"

Marguerite blinked. Whatever she'd braced for, it wasn't that.

"Nothing," Odile said. "Of course. You stopped at four words." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too thin, a wire snapping somewhere inside the sound. "Four words is enough, isn't it. His name and mine."

"Odile." Marguerite's voice changed—not softer, exactly. More careful. Like someone walking toward a thing that might go off. "Tell me it isn't what it looks like."

"And what does it look like?"

"It looks like you sold us."

*There it is.* Odile turned away, busied her hands with the nearest crate, checking lashings that did not need checking. The hold smelled of bilgewater and lamp oil and the particular salt-rot of old brass, and beneath all of it, faintly, the Gideon itself—the smell had gotten into everything since they'd started diving her, into the rope, into Odile's hair, into the seams of her dreams.

"I need you to say it isn't true," Marguerite said behind her. "Because if it is true, then every hour we've sailed toward that wreck, we've been sailing your employer's errand. And I need to know whether I'm standing in a hold with a partner or a knife."

Odile's hands went still on the lashing.

Eleven years ago, in the survey school at Port Meridian, she had beaten Marguerite Yarrow on every practical exercise and lost to her on every single one that mattered—the ones judged by instinct, by nerve, by whatever nameless quality the instructors called *seamanship* and meant *the willingness to be wrong at speed.* She had hated her for it with a clean, clarifying hatred, the kind that organizes a young life. Then the school burned down in the winter of the great fire, and they'd gone separate ways down separate wrecks, and the hatred had aged into something more like respect wearing hatred's old coat.

And now here was Marguerite asking her the only question that mattered, and Odile discovered that the architecture of deflection was built on sand.

"My brother," she said.

The word came out before she could stop it. She stood there with her back turned, furious at herself, at the crack in her own voice, at the way three syllables could undo eleven years of craft.

"Your brother?" Marguerite said.

"Lucien. He's twenty-three. He crews coastal packets out of Sault Rive. Or he did." Odile finally turned around. There was no point in composure now; composure was a currency and she was bankrupt. "Eight months ago he was arrested at Sault Rive for smuggling. The charge was fabricated—I can prove it was fabricated, the manifest was altered, I've seen the original. But proving it requires a court, and courts require advocates, and advocates require—" She gestured vaguely upward, at the deck, at the sea, at the whole rotten apparatus above them. "He was sentenced to six years at the Corbeau Island penal station. Do you know what Corbeau Island is?"

Marguerite's jaw tightened. Everyone in the trade knew what Corbeau Island was. It was where the navy sent sailors it wanted to forget, a limestone quarry with a tide gate and no records.

"He lasted four months before the fevers took half his weight off him. And then a man came to see me. Not Corvane himself—a solicitor, gray suit, gray voice, the kind of man who reads other people's sentences aloud. He sat across from me in a café in Sault Rive and told me that Lucien's sentence could be commuted. Time served. Release within the month. All of it contingent on one small professional engagement." She smiled, and felt her mouth do it like a stranger's mouth. "A concession permit had become available for the Gideon. A salvage operation requiring an experienced diver of good reputation. Someone who knew the wreck's history. Someone who knew *you*, Marguerite, because the permit alone wouldn't get your crew aboard—you'd have refused anyone else, and they needed your crew, because your crew knows the Gideon's approaches better than the navy's own charts do."

"And the delivery?"

"The strongroom contents. Sealed, unopened, delivered to a designated tender at the rendezvous point on the sixth tide. I was told it was archival material. Records." Her laugh again, that broken-wire sound. "I asked him what kind of records require a salvage crew and a concession permit and a rival salvager's boat, and he looked at me with such mild, patient pity, and he said: *Madame Vasseur, the admiral admires your discretion.*"

The lantern swung. Somewhere above them, Nell's footsteps crossed the deck, quick and light, and faded aft.

"So yes," Odile said. "It looks like I sold you. I signed on to deliver the Gideon's heart to the man who buried it, and I brought you along as the key to the lock, and I told myself the same lie every night for eight months: that it was records, that it was paperwork, that no one would be hurt, that Lucien comes home and everyone lives." She spread her hands. "Then Tam pulled that message apart, and I read the rest of it myself this morning while you were all sleeping, and now I know there is no version of the sixth tide where everyone lives. Because it isn't records. Is it."

"No," Marguerite said quietly. "It isn't."

"What is it?"

Marguerite hesitated. Odile watched her weigh it—the old reflex, the hoarding reflex, the salvager's instinct to keep the best find below decks until the price was right—and then watched her set it down.

"Pages from my father's logbook. The real account of the mutiny. What happened in the strongroom corridor the night the Gideon's officers died." Marguerite's voice was flat and very controlled, which Odile recognized as the sound of a woman holding fury the way you hold a live charge. "Corvane didn't put down a mutiny. He ordered the killing of men who were about to report him. My father wrote it down. The navy has spent twenty years making sure nobody read it. And in nine days they intend to blow the strongroom closed with the evidence still inside."

The hold seemed to tilt. Odile gripped the edge of the crate.

"They're going to destroy it," she said. "With us possibly still attached."

"With us definitely still attached, if we're convenient. Read the message again. *Delivery confirmed or operation concludes.* That's not a payment clause, Odile. That's a disposal clause. We were never the crew of this job. We were the labor, and the labor gets written off."

Odile sat down. There was no dignity in it—she simply found that her legs had resigned without consulting her—and lowered herself onto an upturned bucket beside the air bottles. Twenty years in the trade and she had never once been the mark. She had been fast, she had been careful, she had been the woman other salvagers warned their apprentices about. And a gray man in a café had played her like a cheap concertina, because she had one weakness and he had found it in under a minute.

"He showed me a photograph," she said. "Of Lucien. In the quarry yard. Taken through a telephoto lens, so he looked very small. The solicitor said the admiral reviewed his file personally each quarter. Each *quarter*. As if my brother were a bond coming due." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I told myself I was saving him. I told myself a strongroom full of twenty-year-old papers was worth less than a living man, and maybe that's even true, Marguerite, maybe I'd sign the same devil's contract again tomorrow, but I didn't have the right to sign it for your crew too. For Nell. She's nineteen. I watched her thread the fog bank with her hands shaking and her voice steady and I thought, *what have I done, what have I brought this child into.*"

Silence. Long enough that Odile lowered her hands.

Marguerite had not moved. She stood with the paper still in her fist, crushed now, and studied Odile with an expression Odile couldn't immediately name—until she could. It was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic. Weighing what a person had done against why, against what they did next, against the unforgivable mathematics of the sea where you saved who you could save and mourned the rest.

"Ilya will want you off the boat," Marguerite said at last. "Possibly off the boat at speed, via the shortest route, which is over the rail."

"I know."

"Tam will want to rig the radio to send your patron a message composed entirely of words Ilya taught him."

"I know."

"But you told me anyway. When you could have lied. When lying was still on the table and you're better at it than I am." Marguerite crouched down to her level, forearms on her knees, close enough that Odile could see the salt-crack in her lips and the exhaustion underneath the anger. "So here's what happens now. You don't get forgiveness. Forgiveness is for after. What you get is a chance, and the chance works like this: your patron expects delivery on the sixth tide. Fine. We are going to give him a delivery. We are going to reach that strongroom, we are going to take every page of my father's logbook and every scrap of proof in that room, and we are going to decide—with *my* crew, by *our* vote—what gets delivered and to whom. And you are going to help us, not because you owe me, though you do, but because the fastest way to free your brother is to put Admiral Corvane in a position where he needs friends. Men like that only bargain when they're bleeding."

Odile looked at her for a long moment.

"You hate me," she said. "You've hated me since Port Meridian."

"Oh, enormously." Marguerite stood, and offered her hand anyway. "Hate's cheap. Lucien's life isn't. Come on. We tell the others together, and you get to watch Ilya's face, and then we plan."

Odile took the hand. It was rough and warm and it pulled her up harder than necessary, which she understood to be a form of honesty between them, and perhaps the first honest thing either of them had ever given the other.

They were halfway up the ladder when the bell began to ring.

Not the ship's bell—Tam's signal bell, three fast strikes, the pattern that meant *sail sighted.* Odile froze on the rungs. Through the open hatch, gray morning light fell across the deck, and she could hear Nell's voice, high and cracking:

"Sail! Two points off the bow—navy hull, steam wake—it's flying the fleet pennant!"

Marguerite met her eyes through the hatch opening, and Odile saw the arithmetic finish behind them.

The fleet wasn't due for two tides.

Corvane had moved up his schedule—which meant he already knew something had gone wrong.

* * *

Above, the deck erupted into motion. Ilya bellowing for canvas, Tam at the rail with his glass, Nell hauling lines with the desperate competence of someone who had learned fear early and made it useful. Odile climbed out into the wind and took the wheel when Marguerite pointed her to it, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing left to say that the helm didn't say better.

But as the Gull came hard about and the navy's smoke thickened on the horizon, Odile allowed herself one thought, cold and clear as ballast water:

Lucien, hold on. I'm coming to bleed your jailer dry.
