## 12. Third Tide, Rising Storm

Ilya had threaded worse water than this. He kept telling himself that as the *Kestrel's Lark* bucked through the building swell, her seams groaning like an old man climbing stairs. Worse water, worse storms, worse crews. He'd come up in the fishing fleets off the Skerries, where the water was cold enough to kill a man in four minutes and the skippers navigated by memory and spite. This was nothing. This was fine.

The barometer had been falling since noon.

He'd said so at the midday meal, quiet, the way he said everything. Tam had looked up from the engine manifest with the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. Marguerite had just nodded and gone back to charting their position against the tide tables, because there was no version of this voyage where they could afford to stop. Two tides lost already — one to the fog bank, one to the tender scare — and Odile's permit clock didn't care about weather. Six tides to reach the strongroom before the navy fleet arrived, and they'd burned through half of them hiding from the very people who were supposed to be keeping the sea safe.

So when the sky went the color of wet slate around the third bell of the afternoon watch, Ilya did what he always did. He climbed to the wheel, took it from Nell without a word, and started reading the water.

You could read a storm coming long before you saw it, if you'd spent enough years looking. Not in the clouds — clouds lied, or at least exaggerated. You read it in the swell. A storm two hundred miles out sent its messengers ahead of it: long, low, evenly spaced swells that rolled under your keel like something enormous turning over in its sleep. The fishermen called them the forerunners. Old Ilya's father had called them God's knuckles, rapping on the hull to ask if anyone aboard was fool enough to be out here.

They were fool enough. All of them.

"Swells are coming out of the southwest," Ilya said to Marguerite, who stood at the rail with her arms crossed against the wind. "Long period. Even spacing. That's not local weather. That's a system, and it's big."

"How long?"

"Four hours. Maybe less if it's moving fast."

Marguerite looked at the horizon, then at the chart spread on the deck box beside her, then at him. He watched her do the same arithmetic Tam had done — distance to the reef passage, speed against the storm, tides remaining — and he watched her arrive at the answer she didn't want.

"There's a passage," she said. "Through the Teeth."

"There is." Ilya had run it twice in his life, both times in daylight, both times in calm, both times with a pilot who knew it better than he did. The Teeth were a scatter of drowned granite shoals off the old coast road, remnants of some headland the sea had eaten centuries ago. Between two of the largest lay a gap maybe forty meters wide, with water over it that went from eight fathoms to two and a half without apology. In calm weather it was a shortcut that saved half a day. In weather like this, it was a throat, and the sea was deciding whether to swallow.

"Odile will say we should push through open water," Marguerite said. "Ride it out running."

"Odile can say what she likes." Ilya adjusted his grip on the wheel. The wood was worn smooth where his hands had polished it over eleven years. "In open water, we take green seas over the bow until the boat breaks or we do. In the passage, we take one bad hour and then we're through, into the lee behind the Teeth, anchored and waiting out the worst of it. We lose less time sheltering than we would losing the boat."

"And if you misjudge it?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to a skipper about seamanship got people drowned. "Then we're wrecked on granite in a rising gale, and none of this matters anymore."

Marguerite held his eyes for a long moment. She had her father's way of looking at you when she was weighing something — not suspicious, exactly. Measuring. Then she nodded once and turned aft. "All hands. We're making for the Teeth."

* * *

The argument happened below, which was where arguments belonged, though sound carried on a small boat the way smoke carries in a closed room.

Ilya stayed at the wheel and let it happen. He heard Odile's voice first — smooth, reasonable, pitched to carry conviction rather than volume. She was good at that. She'd been good at it twenty years ago too, back when she and Marguerite were two hungry divers splitting wrecks off the same coast and hating each other politely. *We lose more time anchoring than we lose pushing through,* she was saying now. *My patron's schedule doesn't bend. Neither does the navy's.*

Then Tam's voice, flat and dry as driftwood: "Your patron's schedule also assumes his contractor arrives alive. Boats don't sail on schedules. They sail on buoyancy, and we're about to have significantly less of it."

Nell said something Ilya couldn't catch — young voice, quick, probably defending whatever Odile had snapped back. The girl had taken to Odile with the uncritical enthusiasm of the young for anyone who seemed glamorous, and Ilya had been watching it with the mild dread of a man watching a child pet a strange dog.

Then Marguerite, cutting through all of it the way she did — not loudly, but with a finality that closed doors. "We go through the Teeth. Ilya has the con. Anyone who disagrees can take it up with the storm."

Silence below. Then footsteps on the companionway ladder, and Odile emerged into the cockpit rain-hood, salt spray already stippling the glass. She looked at Ilya the way she looked at everything — like she was pricing it.

"You've done this passage before," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Twice. With a pilot. In fair conditions."

"And today?"

"Today I do it alone, in the worst weather I've seen in six years, because the alternative is worse." He met her eyes. "You want to know if I'm certain. I'm not. Nobody is certain in weather like this. What I am is competent, and the boat is sound, and those are the only certainties the sea sells."

Something flickered across Odile's face — not respect exactly, but the recognition of it, the way a card player marks another player's tell. "My brother used to say something like that," she said quietly. "Before." Then she went forward to the bow, to stand anchor watch on a boat that wasn't anchoring yet, and Ilya wondered what the *before* had cost her, and filed the wondering away. There was no time for it now.

The first real gust hit at the fourth bell.

* * *

By the time the Teeth rose out of the murk — black fangs against a bruised horizon, spray tearing off their flanks in banners — the wind had built to a full gale and the sea had organized itself into something with intent. Ilya stood at the wheel with his whole body, boots braced, forearms burning, reading the water ahead the way other men read print.

Tam crouched at the engine controls beside him, one hand on the throttle, eyes on Ilya's face. They'd done this dance before, years of it, and they'd developed a language that didn't need words. Ilya's chin dipped: more power. His palm pressed flat and downward: ease off. His shoulders dropped a fraction: hold what you have. Tam translated each signal into fuel and revolutions, and between them they made the boat do things the builder had never intended.

Marguerite worked the chart on the deck box, weighted with a brass fitting salvaged from the Gideon itself, calling depths and bearings against the rain hammering sideways. Nell was on the bow with the lead line, sounding every few seconds, her voice cracking across the cockpit with numbers that mattered more than scripture: *by the mark five! by the deep four!* And Odile — Odile had wedged herself at the starboard rail with a boathook, ready to fend off death if it came close enough, watching Ilya with an expression he slowly recognized as trust being manufactured in real time, under pressure, whether she wanted it or not.

"Passage bears north-northeast," Marguerite shouted over the wind. "Between the two big rocks. Half a mile."

"I see it." He did. The gap showed as a slightly lighter chaos in the general chaos, white water streaming through it like a river deciding to become the sea. That was the thing about passages through reefs: the danger wasn't the rocks themselves. You could see rocks. The danger was the current — the tide funneling through the gap, meeting the storm swell head-on, stacking the water into standing waves that broke where no wave should break. Run the gap wrong and the sea itself would pick the boat up and introduce her to the granite.

"Tide's still ebbing," Tam said, reading his tide table by lightning-flash. "Against us through the gap. Current'll be running three knots, maybe four with this wind behind the swell."

"So we punch through against four knots of current in a gale," Odile said, "in a boat older than everyone aboard."

"Yes," Ilya said.

Nobody argued. That was the moment he understood something had shifted on this boat — had been shifting since the fog bank, since the tender, since Odile had stood her own watch without complaint and Nell had stopped treating her like a stranger. Trust wasn't a decision any of them had made. It was accumulating, tide by tide, the way silt accumulates in a harbor, until one day there's ground under you and you hadn't noticed it forming.

"Half a mile," Marguerite said again. "Lead line ready?"

"Ready!" Nell's voice had steadied. Whatever fear was in her, she'd packed it down somewhere useful.

Ilya lined up the gap. He picked his transits the way his father had taught him — the eastern fang of the outer Teeth notched against the western one, a line drawn through chaos to the one safe thread. Then he set the boat at it, at the exact angle the current demanded, crabbing sideways across the water while pointing straight at the gap, and gave Tam the signal for full power.

The *Lark* surged.

* * *

What followed took nine minutes. Ilya knew because he counted, the way he counted everything in bad water, seconds ticking in the back of his skull like a second heart.

The first hundred meters were the approach — green water over the foredeck, Nell clinging to the jackstay with one arm and swinging the lead with the other, screaming soundings through the roar. *By the mark four! By the deep four and a half!* The boat climbed a swell the size of a chapel and hung at the top, propeller singing in air, and Ilya felt the whole world tilt and waited for gravity to remember them.

It did. The hull slammed down and held together, and Tam fed the engine more, and they drove on.

Then the gap itself.

The current took them the moment they entered — a hand on the stern, shoving them sideways toward the northern fang where the water broke white over black teeth. Ilya felt it in the wheel first, a deadness, then a grab, the rudder biting water that wanted to go elsewhere. He corrected. Overcorrected. Felt the boat slew. And in that half-second of slewing, the standing wave rose off the southern shoal — a pyramid of gray-green water that hadn't existed a heartbeat before and would not exist a heartbeat after, there and gone, the sea clearing its throat.

"Left!" he roared, and Tam shoved the throttle to its stop, and Marguerite was suddenly beside him with her hand flat on the wheel beside his — not taking it, never taking it, just adding her weight to his, her strength to his, as if the two of them together could out-argue the ocean.

They nearly could. The *Lark* climbed the standing wave at an angle no designer ever drew, corkscrewed at the top with her port rail buried, and came down the far side into the channel proper with a crash that Ilya felt in his teeth and his hips and whatever part of a man holds his fear.

For three seconds nobody breathed.

Then Nell's voice, high and ragged and triumphant: "*By the mark five!* Deep water! We're through the narrows!"

The current eased. The breaking water fell astern. Ahead, the lee of the Teeth opened up — a broad pool of comparatively flat water, gray and rain-pocked but sane, ringed by black rock that stood between them and the gale like a fortress wall.

Ilya became aware that his hands had to be pried off the wheel. Marguerite's hand was still on it beside his. He looked at her; she looked at him; neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the last nine minutes hadn't already said better.

Tam killed the engine to an idle and put his forehead briefly against the bulkhead, a man setting down a weight. Odile sat down on the cockpit grating entirely uninvited and entirely without ceremony, and laughed — one short, cracked bark of a laugh, the laugh of someone whose body had been braced for death and had just been told to stand down. Nell coiled the lead line with shaking hands, then looked up and caught Ilya watching her, and grinned at him with all the fear draining out of her face at once, replaced by something brighter.

"That was—" she started.

"Don't," Ilya said. "Don't name it yet. Name it tonight, over hot food. Right now, drop the hook before the lee changes its mind."

* * *

They anchored in four fathoms, snug in the Teeth's shadow, and the storm arrived overhead an hour later and spent its fury on the rocks instead of on them. Through the night the wind screamed over the fortress wall and the boat swung gently at her cable, and the crew ate Tam's terrible stew and named the thing they'd done, as instructed.

Nell told it biggest, naturally — the wave as tall as a church, the wheel fighting like a live thing. Tam corrected the details with dry precision until the story and the truth converged somewhere entertaining. Odile listened more than she talked, and once, when Nell described how Marguerite had put her hand on the wheel beside Ilya's, Odile glanced at Marguerite with an expression Ilya couldn't read and didn't try to.

Ilya sat near the stove and said little, as usual. But when Nell finally asked him — directly, the way she asked everything — *how did you know the angle, how did you know the wave would be there*, he thought about it seriously before answering, because she deserved a serious answer.

"I didn't know," he said. "I read, and I guessed, and I trusted the boat and the people. That's all seamanship is. Reading, guessing, trusting."

"And if the guess is wrong?"

"Then you find out what you're made of." He shrugged. "Today we found out. It was enough."

Later, near midnight, Marguerite came and sat beside him while the others slept. She had the compass out, turning it over in her fingers, its needle swinging in that wrong, patient way it had been swinging for days — not north, not anywhere a needle ought to point.

"It did it again during the passage," she said. "Spun like a thing possessed while we were in the narrows. Then settled the moment we cleared the Teeth." She looked up at him. "Pointing northeast, Ilya. Straight along the Gideon's old patrol route. Like it knows where we're going."

Ilya studied the small brass instrument, older than both of them, that had belonged to a man the sea had kept. He was not a man who believed in much beyond weather, water, and work. But he'd seen the needle settle, and he'd seen the direction it chose.

"Then maybe," he said slowly, "it's not leading us into a trap. Maybe it's leading us home."

Outside, the storm began to ease, and far to the east — though none of them could know it yet — the first lights of the navy salvage fleet were coming down the coast, ahead of schedule, hunting the same water.
