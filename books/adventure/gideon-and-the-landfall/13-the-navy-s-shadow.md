## 13. The Navy's Shadow

Nell had never seen so many ships in her life.

They came over the horizon at first light of the fourth tide, a gray line stitched across the gray water—six hulls, maybe seven, with the low heavy silhouettes of salvage cranes riding their decks like gallows. Smoke stood up from their funnels in straight columns because there was no wind to speak of, and that was the worst part. No wind meant no chop, no chop meant no noise to hide behind, and the *Kestrel* sat on a glass-flat sea like a fly on a windowpane.

"Down," Ilya said, though he was already hauling the sail down himself, his big hands moving faster than Nell could follow. "Tam, kill the stove. Marguerite, get below."

"I'm not going below."

"You're going below or you're going overboard, and one of those keeps you dry." He didn't look at her when he said it. Nobody looked at anybody when they said things like that anymore; it was just how the crew talked now, four tides into a job that kept trying to kill them.

Nell went below, but she went below angry, and she took the spyglass with her, which nobody had told her not to do.

Through the little stern port she watched the fleet come on. It moved the way weather moves—unhurried, total, indifferent to anything small enough to be crushed. The lead ship flew no colors she recognized, which frightened her more than colors would have. Navy ships flew colors. Ships that didn't were ships doing something they'd deny later.

"They're not heading for the Gideon," Tam said beside her, and Nell jumped; she hadn't heard him come down. He crouched at the port with a chart across his knees and a pencil behind his ear. "Look at their bearing. They're standing off to the northeast. That's the deep-water anchorage past the shoals."

"So they're waiting?"

"Salvage fleets don't wait. Salvage fleets work." Tam chewed the pencil end for a moment. "Unless they're waiting for something else to finish first."

Above them, boots crossed the deck. Then Marguerite's voice, pitched low through the deck planks: "Ilya. Get us into their wake shadow. I want to know what they're waiting for."

* * *

The wake shadow turned out to mean trailing the fleet at the ragged edge of visibility, close enough to count funnels, far enough to be a smudge of nothing on the water. It was Nell who spotted the tender.

She'd been given the bow watch—her first real watch, not just mopping and coil-scrubbing—and she took it seriously in a way that made her chest hurt. She stood at the stem with the wind finally risen to a whisper, scanning the water ahead of the fleet rather than behind, because Ilya had told her once: *everyone watches where they've been. Watch where they're going.* And there, threading out from between two islands that weren't on Tam's chart, came a small fast boat running dark, no lights, cutting a white seam straight toward the fleet's lead ship.

"Tender," she called, and her voice cracked and she hated it, but she called again anyway. "Tender, bearing green, coming out of the islands!"

Ilya was beside her before she finished. He raised his own glass, held it steady for a long ten-count, and then did something Nell had never seen him do.

He swore.

"What? What is it?"

"That's a dispatch boat. Fleet pattern, but stripped for speed." He lowered the glass slowly. "It's carrying orders, girl. Fresh ones. And it came from the islands, which means somebody ashore is talking to that fleet, which means—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"Which means what?"

"Which means Odile's six tides might already be five." He turned and bellowed down the hatch: "Marguerite! Get up here! Now!"

* * *

They argued about it for an hour while the fleet steamed on, oblivious or indifferent, and Nell learned more about adults in that hour than she had in all her years ashore.

Odile wanted to run flat out for the Gideon and dive immediately, storm or no storm, tide or no tide. "We still have time if we stop wasting it," she said, arms folded at the rail, and her voice had that polished calm that Nell privately thought of as her armor-voice.

"With what air?" Tam shot back. "We lost a full tide in the fog bank. Our compressor runs hot after three fills. You want to send them down on thin tanks?"

"I want to send them down on *any* tanks, because in two days that strongroom is going to be a hole full of dead men's secrets and we'll have nothing!"

"And how do you know that's the timeline?" Marguerite asked quietly. She'd been silent through most of it, sitting on the rail with the compass in her open palm, watching the needle hold its wrong-and-steady bearing toward the wreck. "How do you know exactly when the permits expire, Odile? Down to the tide?"

The silence that followed had weight. Nell felt it press on her ears like diving too deep.

"My patron's terms are precise," Odile said at last.

"Your patron knows things your patron shouldn't know," Marguerite said. "And now there's a dispatch boat running orders from shore, and a fleet that isn't salvaging, it's *waiting*, and I want to know what it's waiting for before I put divers in the water." She closed her hand around the compass and stood. "So we're going to find out. We're going to get close enough tonight to hear what that fleet is saying to itself."

"You're mad," Odile said.

"I'm thorough," Marguerite said. "There's a difference, and it's why I'm still alive."

* * *

They went in after dark, engine at bare steerage way, running without lights through water that glowed faintly green where the keel stirred it. Nell had never been afraid of the dark on land. On the sea it was different—the dark here had layers, and things lived in all of them, and somewhere under the boat right now was the Gideon, and under the Gideon was whatever her father had died next to.

She was at the bow again. Ilya had put her there without being asked, which she understood, finally, was trust expressed as assignment.

The fleet lay anchored in a loose crescent, deck lights burning, crane booms silhouetted against the stars. As the *Kestrel* slid closer, sounds carried across the flat water: the clang of machinery, shouted orders, the rhythmic thud of something being lowered and raised, lowered and raised.

"They're laying cable," Tam whispered from the cockpit, ear pressed to a battered hydrophone he'd rigged from a copper kettle and sheer stubbornness. "Heavy cable. And—" His face changed. "Charges. They're lowering charge cases down the cable. I can hear the winch pattern. That's not salvage rigging. That's demolition prep."

Nobody spoke. The word *demolition* seemed to hang over the boat like fog.

"They're going to blow it," Nell said. Her voice came out very small. "The strongroom. They're going to blow the strongroom."

"Not yet," Marguerite said. But her voice wasn't steady either.

Then, closer—a voice, actual human words, drifting from the lead ship where two officers stood by the rail not thirty yards off the *Kestrel*'s beam. The night was that still. Nell froze at the bow, one hand on the forestay, and listened.

"—admiral wants it done at permit's end regardless. If the concession crew hasn't surfaced anything by then, seal it anyway. Nothing goes back into circulation. He was very clear." A pause, the clink of a cup. "Nothing *stays* in circulation either. Any records recovered are to be logged as sealed materiel and destroyed with the site."

"You mean the gold."

"I mean everything. Gold, papers, bodies if any are left. The Gideon stops existing at the end of this week. That's the order."

Nell's hands had gone numb on the stay. She didn't remember gripping it that hard.

Beside her, so close she could feel the warmth of him, Ilya had gone absolutely rigid. And behind them both, from the shadow of the mast where she'd insisted on standing the watch too, came the sound of Odile Vasseur breathing—fast and shallow, the armor-voice gone, just a woman breathing in the dark.

"Permit's end," Marguerite whispered. "He's not salvaging it. He's burying it. He's burying *them*."

* * *

They pulled away slow, slow, oars shipped, letting the tide carry them out of earshot before the engine even coughed. In the cabin the lamp burned low and everyone crowded around it, faces yellow and strange, and nobody sat down.

"The mutiny," Tam said. "The logbook pages from the drowned town. The admiral ordered the cover-up—if the strongroom holds evidence, real evidence, then blowing it isn't salvage policy. It's—"

"It's murder with extra steps," Ilya said flatly. "Second time around."

Nell looked at Odile. Everyone did.

Odile stood with her back against the bulkhead, and for once there was nothing polished about her at all. "My patron pays on delivery of the strongroom contents," she said. "That's what I was told. That's what I agreed to." A breath. "I was not told the delivery was a funeral."

"Would it have mattered?" Marguerite asked. Not cruelly. Just asking.

Odile met her eyes, and Nell watched her try to summon the old answer and fail. "I don't know," she said. "That's the honest answer. I don't know, and I hate that I don't know, and I hate more that you're all looking at me like I'm the reason my own brother's life is tied to a massacre."

"Then untie it," Marguerite said. She set the compass on the table between them, needle swinging, settling, pointing its true-wrong point toward the Gideon. "One tide left, maybe less, before that fleet seals the door forever. So here's what happens. We dive tomorrow at slack water. We go for the records—not the gold, the *records*. My father's logs, the muster books, whatever the strongroom holds that proves what happened aboard that ship. And then we decide together what gets carried up and what stays down."

"And my patron?" Odile asked quietly.

"Your patron," Marguerite said, "is about to learn what it costs to make an enemy of people who have nothing left to lose."

Nell realized her heart was hammering, and that she wasn't afraid—or she was, but it was the good kind, the kind that made her stand straighter. She raised her hand before she could think better of it.

"I'll dive," she said.

Every head turned. Ilya opened his mouth and Marguerite put a hand on his arm.

"You've got forty feet of bottom time and a borrowed mask," Tam said gently.

"I've got better eyes than anyone on this boat in the dark, and you know it." Nell's voice only shook a little. "You taught me the lines. All of you. Every day since I came aboard, you taught me, and I never knew why until now." She looked around the lamplit circle of faces—her strange, stubborn, broke, magnificent family. "It's not treasure anymore. It's the truth, and it's drowning, and somebody has to carry it up."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Ilya sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to the inevitable, and rummaged in the gear locker, and tossed her a dive knife that must have weighed half as much as she did.

"Belt sheath," he said gruffly. "Cut yourself free of anything that grabs you. And Nell—" his voice roughened, just slightly, "—you come back up. That's the job. Everything else is details."

Outside, the fleet's lights burned on the dark water like a city built to keep a secret, and one tide away, the Gideon waited with her dead and her truth, and above her the charges hung on their cables like seeds of forgetting.

Tomorrow, Nell thought, we take them back.
