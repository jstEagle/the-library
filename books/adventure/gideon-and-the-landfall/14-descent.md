## 14. Descent

The tide turned at four bells, and with it went the last of our time.

I stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Luck* and watched the Gideon's masts break the surface below us—two black fingers reaching up from forty fathoms of green water, like a drowned man pointing at the sky and asking why. Six tides we'd been given. We'd spent five of them dodging tenders, threading reefs, losing hours to fog and fear and one near-mutiny in the galley that I still hadn't fully forgiven anyone for.

One tide left. And the navy's salvage fleet was already a smudge of smoke on the southern horizon, close enough now that Tam could count the ships by their funnel smoke: seven. Seven ships coming to seal my father's grave with high explosive and official silence.

"You're certain about this," Odile said behind me. Not a question, really. She'd learned on this voyage that I didn't answer questions dressed as doubts.

"No," I said. "But certainty's for people who can afford it."

She came to stand beside me. In her diving suit, half-dressed, she looked smaller than she ever did on deck—Odile Vasseur, who'd once outbid me on three wrecks in a single season, who'd smiled at me across auction halls like a knife across a throat. Now her hands were shaking as she checked her air valves, and she didn't bother hiding it, which was somehow worse.

"Forty fathoms," she said. "Flooded corridors. No tender line, no backup bell, no navy divers pulling us out if we foul. Just you, me, and a compass that points at ghosts."

"And each other."

She looked at me sideways. "That's new."

"It is." I picked up the compass from the chart table where it had lain all night, needle steady, patient, pointing not north but down and east—down toward the Gideon's heart. My father's compass. The only thing he'd left me besides questions. "It's been waiting twenty years to be right about something. Least we can do is let it finish."

* * *

We went over the side at slack water, when the current through the wreck would be kindest.

The cold hit first—it always does, a fist closing around your chest even through canvas and grease—and then the light changed, the world above going silver and wavering, Nell's face at the rail growing small and strange. She'd wanted to come. I'd told her no, and she'd argued, and I'd put my hand on the back of her neck the way my father used to do to me and said, *Someone has to be up here pulling the line, and I trust exactly one person to do it.*

She'd gone red and stopped arguing. Ilya squeezed her shoulder once, hard, which from Ilya was a speech.

Then the water took us, and there was only the descent.

Forty fathoms is nothing and everything. Your ears scream and then surrender. Your lamp beam shrinks to a spear of yellow in water the color of old bottle glass. And the Gideon rises up to meet you the way a mountain rises for a climber—not gradually, but all at once, a wall of black iron weed-hung and barnacled, so vast that your mind refuses it and your body keeps swimming anyway.

Her name was still legible on the stern, gold leaf gone brown, letters furred with coral. *GIDEON.* The ship nobody names. I put my gloved hand flat against the hull and felt—nothing, of course, just cold iron—but I did it anyway, because some part of me had been doing it since I was nine years old, pressing my palm against things my father had touched, trying to feel him through the metal.

Odile tapped my arm. Pointed forward. *Move.*

Right. Sentiment was ballast, and ballast gets cut.

* * *

We entered through the gun-deck breach on the port side—the same breach Tam's charts showed, the same one the survivors' stories described, torn open the night the Gideon went down. Inside, the water was darker. Our lamps carved tunnels through it and the tunnels closed behind us like water closing over a stone.

Claustrophobia is a strange thing underwater. On land it's walls. Down here it's the opposite—it's the knowledge that the walls are everywhere, above and below and behind, and that every one of them is holding back enough ocean to crush you flat. My breath rasped loud in my own helmet. Bubbles climbed away from my exhaust valve in silver strings, and I watched them go with something like envy.

The compass sat in a leather pouch on my chest, and every few strokes I opened it and checked.

It didn't waver. That was the uncanny thing. A compass underwater should be useless, spinning, sulking. Mine pointed steady as a sermon—forward and down, along the corridor, past the collapsed mess hall where a table still stood set with plates furred white as frost, past an officer's cabin where a boot hung suspended in the doorway like the foot of a man mid-stride, frozen twenty years mid-step.

My father had walked these corridors. Had maybe run them. The mutiny, the massacre, whatever word you used for what the admiral had ordered—my father had been here for all of it, and then the sea had closed its hand, and everyone ashore had agreed to call it weather.

Odile swam beside me, economical, controlled, her lamp sweeping methodically left-right-left the way a salvage diver sweeps, cataloguing exits, counting bulkheads. Twice she caught my eye through her faceplate and held up fingers: *air*. Once she held up a fist and we both froze while a shoal of glassfish poured through a hatchway like spilled mercury, thousands of them, indifferent, glittering, gone.

At the third bulkhead the compass swung.

Not wildly—it swung the way a dog swings toward home. Ninety degrees, sure and smooth, pointing us through a low maintenance crawl-space that wasn't on any chart I'd ever seen. I stared at it, then at Odile. She shrugged inside her suit, a whole conversation in one shoulder: *your ghost, your call.*

I went first.

The crawl-space was tight enough that my tanks scraped overhead, tight enough that for ten full seconds I couldn't turn my head, couldn't see anything but the compass needle glowing faintly ahead of me and my own hands pulling me forward along rust-slick steel. This was the moment I'd feared since we planned it—the moment where the wreck decides whether to keep you. Somewhere behind me Odile's breathing echoed off the walls, doubled, tripled, until it sounded like a crowd following us into the dark.

Then the space opened, and I understood.

We'd come out into a section of the ship that shouldn't exist—a compartment between decks, unmarked, dry-planned, built into the original frames. And bolted to the bulkhead, fresh as the day it was screwed home, was a brass plate:

**STRONGROOM — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY — BY ORDER OF**

The rest was corroded away. But I knew whose order. We all did, now.

The compass needle lay flat against the glass, quivering, pointing directly at the plate. At the door beneath it. At my father's last workplace, his last secret, his last message to a daughter who'd been nine years old and waiting on a dock.

"He worked here," I said into my transmitter, and heard my own voice crackle and distort. "He stood right here."

Odile's lamp found mine. Through two layers of glass and green water, her eyes were steady. "Then let's read what he left."

* * *

The strongroom door was a vault door, proper naval work, six inches of steel with a combination lock the size of a dinner plate. It should have stopped us cold. It would have stopped any salvage crew in the islands.

But my father's logbook pages—the ones the old woman in the drowned town had pressed into my hands with both of hers, saying *he'd have wanted these to matter*—had described the mechanism. Officers' combination, day-code, and beneath it a mechanical override installed without authorization, because my father had been the kind of engineer who believed locks were arguments, and every argument deserved a counterargument.

His override was a hidden spindle behind a decorative vent plate. Twenty years underwater, and the spindle still turned when I braced and hauled, shrieking bubbles, my arms burning, Odile's hands suddenly alongside mine adding her strength to the pull.

The wheel spun. The bolts drew back one by one, deep metallic knocks that I felt in my teeth more than heard.

And the door swung inward on hinges that someone—my father, it must have been my father—had greased within the last year. Greased recently. Greased by a man who'd known he might never come back, and who'd made sure that whoever followed him could get in.

Inside, the strongroom was dry.

Not flooded—sealed, airtight, preserved in dead black air behind six inches of steel, and our lamps swept across it and I forgot how to breathe. Gold bars, yes, ranks of them, dull and stupid in the lamplight. Pay chests. A strongbox stamped with the Admiralty crest.

And on a desk against the far wall, squared neatly, weighted down with a brass rule: papers. Dozens of pages. Orders. Signals. A ledger in a careful engineer's hand. And on top of the stack, a sealed envelope with writing across it in ink that hadn't faded, because no water had ever touched it:

**For whoever comes asking. M.Y., if it's you—I'm sorry I missed your birthday again. Look under the gold. — Da**

I don't remember crossing the room. I remember the envelope in my gloves, too big, too fragile, my hands shaking so badly Odile had to help me hold it. I remember her voice in my earpiece, roughened: "Marguerite. Air check. Then read."

I checked my gauge. Enough. Enough if we were quick, enough if we didn't linger, enough if the sea outside stayed calm and the fleet on the horizon stayed slow.

I broke the seal.

* * *

What was written there, I will carry until I die, and some of it belongs in other chapters than this one. But the shape of it was this: my father had found the orders. Had copied them, page by page, in his neat engineer's hand. Had watched the admiral's marines do what the official record called *restoring discipline*, and had understood that the truth would drown with the ship unless someone carried it out—or sealed it somewhere no one would think to look, and trusted the sea to keep it better than the Admiralty kept its promises.

The last line of his letter said: *If they've sent you, little bird, then they're afraid. Good. Be more frightening.*

Above us, faint through forty fathoms and six inches of steel, came a sound I felt before I understood it. A long, rolling concussion, distant and enormous, like the sea clearing its throat.

Odile's head snapped up. Her hand closed on my arm, grip hard enough to hurt through the suit.

Another concussion. Closer.

Depth charges. Or survey charges. Or the fleet's divers laying their demolition pattern early, ahead of schedule, because admirals are men who move timetables when timetables become inconvenient.

The lamp beam trembled—not my hand; the water itself, shivering with pressure waves. Silt began to sift down through the corridor behind us like brown snow.

"We have minutes," Odile said. "Maybe less. Take the papers. Leave the gold."

I looked at the gold—my father's ransom, my crew's freedom, every debt I'd ever owed stacked in dull yellow rows—and then at the papers in my arms, and the choice wasn't a choice at all. It had never been a choice. That was the trick of it. That was the lesson, arriving twenty years late.

"The logbooks first," I said. "Then the letter goes in my chest pocket, against my skin. And Odile—" the deck shuddered under us, a long groan running through the Gideon's bones, "—we need a way out that isn't the way we came in."

She was already moving, lamp sweeping the strongroom's aft bulkhead, and her voice came back to me clipped and fast and, for the first time since Port Corvane, absolutely honest:

"Then pray my father taught me to read hull drawings as well as yours did. Because there's a coal chute on this deck, and it opens to open water—and it's our only door."

The third charge detonated. The lights of our lamps swung wild, and the Gideon began, very softly, to sing.
