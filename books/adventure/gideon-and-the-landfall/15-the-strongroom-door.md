## 15. The Strongroom Door

The compass needle held steady for the first time in Odile's life, and that was the most frightening thing she had ever seen.

It hung off Marguerite's wrist like a small captive bird, pointing not north but down and to port, along the axis of the Gideon's keel, toward the stern. Toward the strongroom. Odile swam beside her through the flooded corridor of the orlop deck, their lamp beams cutting cones of murk that filled with silt every time a fin kicked too hard. The water was cold enough to ache behind the ears. Somewhere above them, one tide's worth of air sat in tanks on the boat, and somewhere beyond the horizon, the navy was coming.

Marguerite stopped at a junction where three passages met, all of them choked with collapsed planking and the pale blooms of something growing on the wood—fungus, maybe, or coral, or just the sea eating. She raised the compass, checked it, pointed left.

Odile nodded. She didn't trust her voice inside the helmet. Not because of the breathing apparatus—Tam's rigs were crude but sound—but because everything she wanted to say right now was either a lie or a confession, and neither belonged in a wreck.

* * *

She had rehearsed this dive for six years without knowing it.

In her head it had always gone differently. In her head, she came down alone, or with hired hands who asked nothing, and she found the strongroom before anyone else knew it existed, and she took what her patron wanted and sold the rest quietly, and Vasseur & Vasseur rose from the mud of the harbor trade into something respectable. In her head there was no Marguerite Yarrow swimming at her shoulder with her father's compass, no brother in a navy cell whose release depended on her silence, no admiral's cipher burning a hole in the false bottom of her chart case.

In her head, the prize was gold.

The corridor narrowed. They went through sideways, lamps turned low, and Odile felt the Gideon close around her like a hand. This was the part divers died in—the part where the ship stopped being a structure and became a throat. A rusted pipe crossed the passage at shoulder height; Marguerite ducked under it and Odile followed, and her tank scraped the pipe with a sound like a struck bell that traveled through the water and into her teeth.

Below them, forty fathoms down the outer hull, the sea pressed in with the patience of something that had already won.

They passed the galley. Odile recognized it from the plans Tam had reconstructed—a scuttlebutt stand still bolted to the deck, a great dented kettle lying on its side like a dead animal. Men had eaten here. Men had slept two decks below and argued about pay and written letters home, and then one night the admiral's marines had come down those ladders, and—

No. She didn't know that yet. That was the thing about horror: you only ever recognized it afterward.

* * *

The compass pulled them past the wardroom, past a door hanging by one hinge, past a heap of something Odile's lamp touched and her mind refused for three full seconds to name. Then the passage ended at a wall.

Not a bulkhead. A wall. Iron plate, bolted, painted once in some naval gray that twenty years of brine had stripped to bare metal and bloom. In its center, a door. Round, submarine-thick, with a wheel handle crusted white. Above it, stenciled letters that the sea had half-erased:

STRONGROOM — AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY — BY ORDER OF —

The rest was gone. Odile ran her glove over the missing words and felt the ghost of the stencil in the corrosion, and thought: *by order of whom? By order of the man who sent us?*

Marguerite hovered before the wheel. Even through the helmet Odile could see her face change—not fear exactly, not greed, something older than both. A daughter arriving at a door her father had described in a logbook written twenty years ago, knowing he might never leave the room on the other side of it.

Marguerite looked back at her. Held up one finger. Then pointed at the wheel and made the motion of turning.

Odile checked her gauge. Fifty minutes of air. Enough. She nodded.

* * *

The wheel did not want to turn. Of course it didn't; nothing in the Gideon wanted anything anymore except to be left alone. Marguerite set both hands on it and hauled, boots braced against the frame, and the wheel gave with a shriek of breaking corrosion that Odile felt in her chest more than heard. Then again. Then a third quarter-turn, and something deep in the mechanism clunked, and the door swung inward an inch, and the water between them changed.

Odile noticed it before she understood it. The particulate in their lamplight stopped drifting. It hung. Twenty years of sealed water, undisturbed, sterile as a held breath—and now they were letting it mix with the corridor's rot, and the boundary line between the two waters shimmered like heat haze.

Marguerite put her shoulder to the door and pushed.

It opened onto darkness that their lamps could not finish. Odile went in second, because pride demanded Marguerite go first and terror agreed with pride for once.

* * *

The strongroom was not full of gold.

That was the first thing. Odile had built six years of ambition on the assumption that the Gideon's strongroom was full of gold—pay chests, prize bullion, the navy's quiet war-fund, whatever story her patron had whispered through intermediaries. And there *was* metal in here. Racks of it along the port side, coin chests stacked three high, their lids burst by corrosion so that silver spilled out in frozen avalanches, black with sulfide, beautiful and worthless-looking as slag.

But the starboard wall was paper.

Shelves. Dozens of them, sealed behind glass panes someone had installed with care, and behind the glass: ledgers. Logbooks. Bundles of correspondence tied with tarred cord. Signal flimsies in oilskin sleeves. An entire wall of the ship's memory, filed and preserved, in a room the official record said contained only specie.

Marguerite went to the shelves the way drowning people go to light.

Odile watched her pull the first ledger out through a broken pane—carefully, so carefully, gloved fingers handling the wet leather like a relic—and open it on a chest lid, and shine her lamp down onto the page. The ink had held. Navy ink held better than almost anything else aboard.

And Odile saw Marguerite's shoulders stop moving.

"Yarrow." Her own voice sounded enormous inside the helmet. "Talk to me."

Marguerite turned the ledger toward her. Pointed at a line of entries. Her hand was shaking, which Odile had never seen, not in the harbor, not in the storm, not when Ilya threaded the reef with the fleet's searchlights sweeping the water behind them.

Odile read.

* * *

It was a watch log. Ordinary format, ordinary hand—until the date. Three days before the sinking. The entries were routine: wind, coal, stations. Then, dated the evening of the twelfth:

*2240 — Marines embarked from launch, Lt. Harrow commanding. Ordered aft. No entry in ship's log.*

*2315 — Firing heard, orlop and lower decks. Duration approx. 6 min.*

*2320 — Ordered to secure strongroom. Cdr. Voss present. Cdr. Voss states mutiny suppressed. States casualties to be recorded as drowned in subsequent loss.*

*2327 — Cdr. Voss orders all witnesses' journals surrendered. This journal concealed.*

There was more. Page after page. A muster list with names struck through in a different ink—names Odile didn't know, thirty-one of them, each with a single word after it in the same later hand: *shot*, *shot*, *shot*, *drowned, bound*. A copy of a signal, sent the morning after the sinking, from the flagship: *MUTINY QUASHED AT COST OF SHIP. COMMEND HARROW. NO SURVIVORS TO BE LANDED.* And beneath it, in the same clerk's careful script, a note that made Odile's blood go colder than the water had managed: *Adm. Renard directs: no inquiry. Families notified: lost at sea.*

Renard. Admiral Edouard Renard. The name on the cipher traffic Tam had cracked. The patron. The man who owned her brother's cell and her concession permit and, if she was honest, the last six years of her life.

He hadn't sent them for gold.

He'd sent them because the Gideon was coming up—because the salvage fleet would breach the hull in weeks regardless—and the one room in the ship that could hang him had to be emptied first, quietly, by deniable hands. Hands that could then be lost. *No survivors to be landed.* He had written that order once already. Writing it twice would cost him nothing.

* * *

"Odile." Marguerite's voice, flat and strange. "Look at the last shelf."

The lowest shelf, behind the least-damaged pane, held no ledgers. It held letters. Personal ones, bundled by string, and on the top bundle, in handwriting Odile had seen reproduced a hundred times in Marguerite's cabin—in the margins of charts, on the back of IOUs, in the letter the woman read over and over on bad nights—was a name.

*Elias Yarrow.*

Marguerite's gloves were too thick for the task and she did it anyway, working the bundle free through the broken pane with a patience that frightened Odile more than panic would have. There were perhaps twenty pages. Water-stained, ink feathered but legible. Elias Yarrow had been the Gideon's purser's assistant. He had kept his own journal, hidden the way the watch officer had hidden his, and he had known about the strongroom because it was his job to know, and in his final days aboard he had written—

Odile read over Marguerite's shoulder, and the awe she had felt walking into the room curdled as she read, souring into something with weight and taste, something like swallowing seawater.

*I have counted the chests. Silver, mostly, and papers I am not cleared to read but have read. If the admiralty truly believes this was a mutiny, why does the strongroom hold the muster of the dead? Why does it hold Harrow's own report, unsent, describing orders given before any mutiny began?*

*I think we were sunk on purpose. I think the men who fired on the orlop deck were told we were the mutiny.*

*If anyone finds this: my daughter is Marguerite. She is nine. Tell her I was not a traitor. Tell her the compass was never wrong—it only points where I told it to point, and I told it to point at the truth.*

Marguerite made a sound. Odile would hear it for the rest of her life—a small sound, muffled by the helmet, the sound of a woman who had spent twenty years building herself around a wound and had just been handed the knife that made it.

Then Marguerite straightened, and wiped her face inside the helmet with a rough motion, and began, methodically, terribly calmly, to load the ledgers into the dive bag.

"We're taking all of it," she said. "Every page."

"The chests—" Odile started, and heard her own voice do something ugly, because the chests were the job, the chests were her brother's ransom, the chests were the lie she had told herself for six years.

"Forget the chests." Marguerite looked up, and through the lamp glare Odile saw that there was nothing left in her face of the weary salvager who bargained over brass. What was looking at her was something harder and much more dangerous. "Your patron sent us to empty this room so he could burn it. So did mine, in his way." She tapped the compass on her wrist. Its needle lay steady against the glass, pointing at the ledgers, at the letters, at the truth it had been pointing at all along. "We're going to be the reason he fails."

* * *

Odile's hands moved, helping, while the rest of her stood very still in the flooded dark and did arithmetic.

If they surfaced with the papers, Renard would know within a day. He would know she had seen them. Her brother would die in his cell—or worse, be transferred somewhere with no name, and Odile would spend the rest of her life learning what that meant. If they surfaced without the papers, if she betrayed these people in the water itself, she bought Luc another year and lost whatever it was she had been pretending not to feel since the harbor—the thing that happened in her chest when Nell laughed, when Ilya trusted her at the helm, when Marguerite Yarrow looked at her like she was worth more than her contract.

Thirty-eight minutes of air. The fleet was a day out, maybe less; Tam's intercepts said early. Charges at permit's end, Nell had reported from the shadowing run—they meant to blow the hull closed forever, burying this room and everything in it under a deliberate avalanche.

The prize was poison. It had been poison from the beginning, and she had drunk it gladly because thirst is its own kind of blindness.

"Yarrow," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "When we surface, there's something I have to tell you. About who sent me. All of it."

Marguerite paused, a ledger halfway into the bag, and looked at her for a long moment through two inches of glass and twenty years of rivalry.

"I know," Marguerite said. "I've known since the reef. We'll deal with it up top—together, if you're offering that."

Above them, faint through forty feet of water and the old ship's bones, came a sound Odile had hoped not to hear for another tide: engines. Many engines. Running heavy.

The fleet wasn't coming tomorrow.

The fleet was here.
