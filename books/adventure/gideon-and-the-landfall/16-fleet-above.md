## 16. Fleet Above

The first thing I feel is the thud. Not a sound—sound doesn't travel the way you'd think down here, it comes through your teeth, your ribs, the water itself pressing on your chest like a hand. A thud, then a long rolling groan through the Gideon's bones, and the silt around us lights up gold in the beam of Odile's lamp.

She's ten feet ahead of me in the gun-deck corridor, her fins kicking up a slow storm. She turns. Even through the mask I can read her face: *that wasn't us.*

We're forty feet inside a drowned ship with a compass in my pocket that points at my father's secrets, and something just hit the hull.

* * *

It had been a good dive. That's the thing I'll remember, if I remember anything—the way it had almost been easy. We'd come down the anchor line at slack water, one tide left on Odile's permit, and the Gideon had opened for us like a house with the door left unlatched. The compass in its oilskin wrap hadn't swung wrong once. It had pulled us through the orlop deck, past the collapsed galley where crabs the size of my palm clattered off the bulkheads, along a passageway my father's log described in his tight, careful hand: *third hatch aft of the magazine, the one the carpenter swears was never on the plans.*

We'd found it. A hatch with no hinges on any drawing, a seam in the plating packed with tar and sheet lead, and a lock that my father—quiet, methodical, a man who kept his own books—had left instructions for in the margin of a supply ledger. *The key is the key is the key,* he'd written, which meant nothing until Odile, crouched in the silt with her lamp between her teeth, said: *It's a word puzzle. He was a quartermaster. What word did he use for everything?*

*Ledger,* I'd said. *He called the ship's soul a ledger.*

The lock had opened on the second try.

And then the thud.

* * *

The second thud is closer. The corridor shivers and a rain of rust flakes comes down through our lamplight, orange as sparks. Odile grabs my arm and points up—not at the hatch behind us, but at the ceiling, at a seam where two plates meet, and through that seam, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, a light.

Not our light. Not the gray-green wash of daylight through forty feet of water.

A hard white light. A diver's lamp.

*They're here,* I think, and the thought arrives with a strange, floating calm, the calm I used to feel as a girl when the storm shutters went up and there was nothing left to do but sit with the lamp and listen. *The fleet's here. A tide early. They're here and they're diving and they're putting charges on the hull.*

Odile's hand closes on my wrist and squeezes twice. Navy signal, I realize. *Attention. Danger.* She taps her own gauge, then points up, then makes the flat hand—the cut signal, *surface now*—and I shake my head.

Because here is the triage, and I have perhaps thirty seconds to make it, and I make it the way my father would have, with the ledger open in my mind.

Behind us: the strongroom hatch, unlocked for the first time in nineteen years. Inside it, if his log told the truth—and the drowned town's old woman had sworn it did—his final messages, the muster rolls, the pages that would show the *mutiny* was no mutiny at all but a massacre with the paperwork reversed. Evidence enough to hang an admiral's name in the light where everyone could see it.

Above us: navy divers with demolition charges, and a hull that is about to become a very small, very wet room.

Between: two women with maybe twenty minutes of air each and one route out.

Odile grabs me again, harder, and this time I grab her back and point at the hatch. Her eyes go wide above the mask. She shakes her head—*no, we surface, we come back*—and I point at the white light bleeding through the ceiling seam, and then, slowly, I make the shape with my hands: a box, a charge, a bloom of fingers opening like fire.

She understands. I watch it land on her, the way it landed on me: *there is no coming back.* The navy isn't here to salvage. They're here to seal. To bury the Gideon's heart under rubble and call it a hazard to navigation. Whatever's in that room, in twenty minutes it becomes unreachable for a hundred years.

Her brother is collateral for this job. I know that now. And still she looks at the hatch, and then at me, and then she does the bravest, most infuriating thing I have ever seen a person do.

She turns and swims for the strongroom.

* * *

The room is small. That's my first thought when I come through the hatch behind her, dragging myself over the coaming into airless dark. Small, dry-sealed, and full.

My father kept a quartermaster's soul to the end. The strongroom isn't a pirate's cave of coin—it's an office. A writing desk, bolted to the deck, its surface furred with a fine gray silt that has crept in through the seams over nineteen years. A cabinet. A chest that I can tell by its shape holds coin, because the navy always pays its massacres in something. And on the desk, weighted down with a brass bar to keep it from drifting in the floods that must have come and gone, a bundle of oilcloth-wrapped pages, tied with tarred twine, and on top of the bundle, in handwriting I have known since before I could read it, my own name.

*For Marguerite Yarrow, if she ever comes.*

The thuds are coming faster now. Through the hull I can feel them arranging themselves, methodical, a quartermaster's rhythm—charges set at intervals, a pattern, a plan. Somewhere above us a lieutenant with a clipboard is ticking boxes.

Odile is already at the chest, prying at the hasp with her knife. I grab the bundle. The oilcloth is stiff as shell but intact; the tarred twine holds; my father's knots were good knots. I stuff it inside my suit, against my chest, where it sits like a second heart.

Then I look at the coin chest, and I think about Tam's repair bill, and Ilya's bad knee, and Nell's boots with the split soles, and I hate the arithmetic that comes next.

Because there isn't time. There is maybe enough time to do one thing in this room and get out, and the one thing I have already done. The coin would feed us for a year. The coin is a year of Nell eating breakfast and Tam sleeping nights. The coin is everything we came for, everything Odile's patron promised, everything the whole rotten economy of wrecks and permits and debts says a person should want.

The ceiling groans. Silt sifts down through the lamplight like gray snow.

Odile has stopped prying at the chest. She's looking at me, and she's seen the same arithmetic, because she was raised in the same arithmetic, and what she does is close the chest. Pushes the hasp down. Pats the lid twice, like you'd pat a coffin.

"Leave it," she says, and her voice comes through the water in my ears as a muffled, warped thing, and it is the clearest sentence I have ever heard. "Leave it. It's bait. It's always been bait. He knew that too—look."

She's pointing at the underside of the lid, where a plate of brass is screwed flat, and in the lamplight I can read the engraving, small and even, a quartermaster's hand to the last:

*Take the words. The gold is the navy's. Let them keep what they paid.*

I laugh. It comes out as a burst of bubbles and I don't care. Nineteen years dead and the man is still doing my bookkeeping.

* * *

We're at the hatch when the first charge goes.

Not the big one—the *small* one, a shaping charge on the far side of the ship, and the whole hull rings like a struck bell and the water in the corridor slams us against the frame. My ears go to white fire. Odile's lamp swings wild, and in its light I see the corridor behind us fill—not with water yet, but with movement, the ship flexing, plates shifting, and somewhere below us something structural lets go with a sound I feel in my spine.

The exit route is the anchor line, forward and up. The charges are being laid aft to forward, which means the next ones will be between us and the line.

Odile grabs my shoulder and points: forward, through the orlop deck, the way we came. And then her lamp catches something else—a hull breach, aft, a torn seam in the plating near the magazine, old damage from the sinking, a wound the ship never healed. It's a way out. It's also, I can see even from here, a way out that passes directly under whatever the divers are doing right now.

She looks at me. I look at the anchor line, forward, where the white lights are multiplying—two divers now, maybe three, and one of them is swimming toward the orlop hatch with something rectangular and heavy in his arms.

The third charge goes off. Closer. The corridor behind us collapses in a slow, majestic avalanche of rust and silt, and the strongroom—my father's office, his coin, his brass plate telling the navy to keep its wages—is gone under it, sealed by the same hands that wanted it sealed, and I feel grief and relief arrive together like twins.

Forward is closing. Aft is open. Aft is suicide.

Odile takes my hand. Through the glove, her grip is iron, and she points aft, at the breach, and then at my chest, at the bundle, and her meaning is so plain it needs no signal code: *You carry the words. I'll carry you if I have to.*

* * *

The breach is barely wide enough. We go through one at a time, me first because I'm smaller and because Odile shoved me, and the plating around the torn edge is sharp as a saw and I feel it open a line along my forearm and I don't even look, there's no time to look, the water around me is blooming red and I'm pulling myself through the ship's wound into open sea.

Open sea, and above me, forty feet up, the keels of the salvage fleet sit on the surface like a ceiling. I can see the hulls from below, dark shapes against the bright water, and the prop-wash of a tender churning down toward us like a slow white tornado. A diver's lamp sweeps the water twenty yards to my left. A charge drifts down on its line, sinking with terrible patience, a seed being planted.

Odile comes through the breach behind me, bleeding from the scalp where the plating caught her, and she points up and away, out from under the fleet's shadow, toward the reef shelf where the *Kestrel*—Ilya's hands, Nell's eyes, Tam's engine that barely runs—waits at the edge of the exclusion line.

We kick. My arm is on fire. The bundle against my chest is hard and real and heavier than any treasure I've ever lifted, and I kick for it, for him, for the man who sat in a steel room on a dying ship and wrapped his truth in oilcloth and tarred twine and addressed it to a girl who would spend nineteen years diving for brass to pay a debt he never owed anyone.

Behind us, the Gideon begins to die properly. I feel it through the water more than hear it—the big charge, the one meant to bring the strongroom's section down—and the pressure wave shoves us forward like a hand between the shoulder blades, and when I turn, the wreck's silhouette is sagging, folding, exhaling a vast cloud of silt and rust and nineteen years of kept secrets.

The white lights above us multiply. A boat engine starts directly overhead, close, too close.

And then, cutting down through the churned water from the surface, from the fleet, a line drops past us with a weighted end—a diver's descent line, and on it, coming down fast, a lamp, and under the lamp a face in a navy hardhat that turns and sees us.

Sees us, and stops.

Odile grabs my arm. My lungs are closing. My arm is painting the water red. And the navy diver, twenty feet away, hangs on his line and looks at us—two salvage divers, bleeding, fleeing, carrying something wrapped in oilcloth out of a ship the navy is busy burying—and slowly, deliberately, turns his lamp away.

Turns his lamp away, and points, with one gauntleted hand, toward the reef. Toward the *Kestrel*. Toward escape.

Then the fourth charge goes off, and the sea turns white, and I stop being able to see anything at all.
