## 17. What We Carry Up

The white takes everything.

For a moment I am not a body at all—no arm, no lungs, no name—just pressure and noise, a thing shaken loose from the world like scale off a fish. Then the water clears in ragged sheets and I am still here, which surprises me more than it should, and Marguerite is still beside me, which surprises me more than that.

She has hold of my harness strap. Her lamp is dead; mine is cracked but burning, throwing a wobbling cone of light through silt the color of old milk. Somewhere behind us the Gideon is groaning—a long, structural sound, the sound of a ship deciding what shape she wants to be now that men have started arguing with her in explosives.

I tap my gauge. Forty minutes of air. Maybe thirty-five if I keep panicking, and I intend to keep panicking.

Marguerite taps hers back: fifty-one. Of course. Even underwater she wins.

* * *

We had been in the strongroom for eleven minutes when the charges started. Eleven minutes to read a dead man's handwriting by lamplight while the ship around us ticked like something waking up. I have done salvage work for nine years and I have never once been inside a room that mattered before, and I want to say that now, later, on the surface, in whatever language we end up speaking to each other after this: it was not gold that made my hands shake down there.

It was the logbooks. Dozens of them, stacked in oilcloth against the far wall, wrapped with the care of a man who knew someone would come looking eventually. Captain Yarrow had written his account of the mutiny in a hand so small and so steady it looked typeset—the massacre laid out date by date, order by order, signed orders from Admiral Corvane himself, the ones the navy spent nine years and four drowned witnesses burying. And beneath the logs, in crates stenciled with naval marks: coin. Enough coin to buy a fleet. Enough coin to buy silence forever, which was, I understood even then, precisely what it was for.

Marguerite stood between the crates and the books for maybe four seconds. It felt like an hour. The ship was ticking. My brother's face was somewhere behind my eyes, the way it always is now, and I thought: take the gold, you fool, take the gold and he goes free and we both walk away rich—

She took the books.

And then she looked at me, and I took the books too, because there was no version of this where I let her carry them alone. Not anymore. Whatever I was when I came out here with a permit and a patron and a lie, I am not that person anymore, and the strange thing—the thing I will spend years not saying out loud—is that I don't know exactly when I stopped being her. Somewhere between the fog bank and the drowned town. Somewhere over Nell's laugh or Tam's coffee or the moment Ilya threaded the reef without swearing once, which for Ilya is a religious experience.

The gold stayed in its crates. We swam out through the flooded corridor with our arms full of paper, and I have never in my life made a poorer financial decision, and I have never once regretted it.

* * *

Now: the breach.

It was Tam who found it, hours ago, from the plans Marguerite's father drew—a coal scuttle hatch blown outward when the Gideon went down, aft of the engine room, on the side facing open water. Our way out. The way in was the way the divers came, down the mooring line, and the way in belongs to the navy now. Their tender is anchored four hundred yards north. Their divers are planting charges along the hull like men tucking in a bed they intend to burn.

Marguerite pulls me down the corridor with my bad arm cradled against my chest. The blood from my shoulder has stopped streaming—it clots strangely fast in cold water, a fact I learned today and did not want to learn—and every kick sends a dull bell tolling through the meat of me. I count strokes instead of pain. One. Two. Three. The corridor narrows. The Gideon groans again, closer now, and a rain of rust flakes comes down through my lamplight like red snow.

Then the light ahead changes. Not lamplight—daylight, gray-green, filtered through forty feet of sea. The breach. A ragged oval of open water framed in torn plating, and beyond it the reef slope falling away into blue, and beyond that—

Beyond that, the surface, where our boat waits, where Tam and Nell and Ilya are holding position under a navy tender's guns because two fools went down into a grave and came out carrying its testimony.

Marguerite goes through first. I follow, and the torn plating catches my wounded arm, and I make a sound into my regulator that no one will ever hear, and I do not stop. That is the whole of courage, I think, as the sea opens around me. Not stopping. Anyone can be brave in the four seconds before a choice. Bravery is the swimming after.

* * *

We come up the reef wall hand over hand, following the line Ilya rigged at slack tide, and the blue above us deepens toward silver, and then we break the surface and the world is wind and gray sky and the *Kestrel*, eighty yards off, riding low and patient as a dog that waited all day by a door.

Marguerite surfaces first and drags the oilcloth bundle up onto her chest, holding it above the chop like a midwife lifting a newborn. I come up beside her and immediately begin to cough up half the Serevan Strait.

"Odile." Her voice is wrecked, salt-raw. "Your arm."

"Later."

"Now."

"It's attached. Look at the water."

I look at the water. So does she. And we both see what I already felt in my ribs: smoke, thin and black, standing off the northern horizon where the salvage fleet lies at anchor. And closer—much closer—a launch, navy-gray, cutting across our patch of sea at speed, its wake a white stitch closing on us.

"They're early," Marguerite says. "Tam said end of tide. They're a full tide early."

"Of course they are." I spit seawater. "Corvane doesn't wait for permits. Permits are for people who need permission."

The launch is half a mile out. The *Kestrel* is eighty yards. Simple arithmetic, except nothing about today has been simple, and my arm has stopped being an arm and become a single bright note of pain, and Marguerite Yarrow—who I have hated, professionally and sincerely, for six years—puts her shoulder under my good one and starts swimming, hauling me through the chop with the logbooks strapped across her back, and does not say one word about it, because that is how she loves things: silently, stubbornly, with her shoulder.

* * *

Nell reaches down over the gunwale for us before Ilya has even killed the engine, and the look on her face when she sees the blood in the water around me—I watch her decide, in real time, to be the kind of person who doesn't scream. She's fifteen. She decides anyway. She gets a grip on my harness and hauls, and Tam takes my other side, and between the three of them I come over the rail like a landed fish, and the deck of the *Kestrel* has never felt like such an unreasonable luxury.

"Books?" Tam says. Just that. One word, eyes on the bundle.

"Books," Marguerite says.

"The gold?"

"The gold is staying with the ship."

Tam looks at her for a long moment. Then he nods, once, the way he nods at a weld that holds, and turns to the wheelhouse, and I understand that I have just watched a crew ratify a decision without a vote. Some crews argue. This one simply agrees, and the agreement sounds like an engine starting.

Ilya is already turning us toward the reef passage. He glances at my arm, at the blood slicking the deck, and says nothing, which from Ilya is a eulogy, a blessing, and a battle plan all at once. Nell wraps my shoulder with a pressure bandage from the med kit with hands that only shake a little, and I tell her she's doing it wrong, and she tells me I'm welcome, and I decide on the spot that if I ever have a crew of my own—and God help me, I think I want one now, which is the most expensive thing this entire expedition has cost me—I want a Nell.

Marguerite stands at the stern rail with the oilcloth bundle in her arms, watching the Gideon's masts lean away behind us, and I come to stand beside her because my legs have decided standing is easier than anything else.

"He wrote it all down," she says quietly. "Every order. Every name. He knew they'd kill him for it, and he wrote it anyway, and sealed it, and trusted the sea to keep it until—" She stops. Swallows. "Until someone came."

"Someone came," I say.

"You came," she says, and turns, and looks at me with those gray eyes that have stared me down across three auction houses and one very memorable fistfight in Port Callas harbor, and what I see in them now is not gratitude, exactly. Gratitude is a debt, and debts are the currency I have been trading in my whole life—my brother's freedom, my patron's leash, my own name signed away in rooms I wasn't fit to be in. What I see in her eyes is something worse and better than gratitude.

It's the absence of debt. She doesn't owe me. I don't owe her. We are, for the first time since we were nineteen years old and competing for the same wreck, simply two women in the same boat, and the boat is called *Kestrel*, and it is sailing directly away from everything either of us was promised.

"Vasseur," she says.

"Yarrow."

"When we get clear of this—" She stops again. The launch is visible now to the naked eye, close enough to see the gun mount on its bow, close enough to see a figure at the rail raising a signal lamp. "When we get clear. I'm going to buy you a drink so expensive it should be illegal."

"I'm going to let you," I say. "And then I'm going to tell you about the time you lost the *Meridian* contract, and I'm going to be insufferable about it."

"You've been insufferable about it for six years."

"And I'm going to be insufferable about it forever. That's what winning means."

She laughs. It's a short, cracked, salt-burned laugh, and it is the first time in nine years of rivalry that I have heard Marguerite Yarrow laugh at me rather than in spite of me, and I would trade every coin in that strongroom—which is to say, I already have—to hear it again.

* * *

The signal lamp flashes twice. Three times. Twice.

Tam, at the wheelhouse glass with a battered telescope, reads it aloud in a voice gone flat and careful. "That's naval code. 'Heave to and prepare to be boarded.'" A pause. "Second message. 'Noncompliance will be met with force.'"

"How much force?" Nell asks, very young, very steady.

Tam lowers the telescope. Ahead of us the reef passage opens in the rocks like a wound, narrow and white-toothed, the same throat Ilya threaded in the storm with the whole crew praying in three languages. Behind us the launch closes at speed. Beyond the launch, on the horizon, the smoke of the fleet thickens—and through Tam's glass, he says, they can see the fleet itself getting underway. All of it. Coming about.

And then Tam says the thing that turns my blood to seawater:

"There's a wireless set on the tender. Big one. They're not signaling the fleet, Odile." He looks at me, and his face is kind, which is how I know it's bad. "They're signaling ashore. They know whose boat this is. They know who your patron is. Which means Corvane knows his books just left the room."

Marguerite tightens the straps on the oilcloth bundle. Ilya puts the *Kestrel*'s bow into the reef passage. And the first shell, when it comes, lands close enough to throw spray across the deck like a handful of thrown knives.
