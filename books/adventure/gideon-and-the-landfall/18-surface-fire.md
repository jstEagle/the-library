## 18. Surface Fire

Tam heard the first shell before he saw the flash.

It came as a sound like the sky tearing a seam — a flat crack that arrived ahead of its own echo, and then the water off their port bow stood up in a white column and fell back down again, and the *Kestrel's Wake* shuddered as if she'd been slapped.

"They're not warning shots," Ilya said from the wheel. His voice was very calm, the way it always went calm when everything else went loud. "Warning shots go wide. That one wanted us."

Tam was already moving. Not toward the rail, not toward cover — toward the deck locker where he'd spent three nights wiring together the thing nobody else on the crew had believed would ever matter: the longwave transmitter he'd rebuilt out of salvage brass, a jury-rigged amplifier, and the coil of copper wire Nell had stripped for him by lamplight while the rest of them slept. It had cost him two fuses and most of his patience. It had never been tested at range.

"Marguerite!" he shouted up through the companionway. "I need power. Everything you can give me."

He heard her boots on the ladder before he heard her answer. She came up into the wheelhouse with salt spray in her hair and her father's logbook pages wrapped in oilcloth under one arm, and she looked at him the way she looked at a hull breach — fast, total, no wasted feeling.

"You're going to broadcast."

"I'm going to broadcast." He was already stripping the insulation back with his teeth. "The admiral wants this quiet. Quiet is the one thing I can take away from him."

Another shell. Closer. The concussion came through the deck plates and up through Tam's knees, and somewhere aft he heard glass go — one of the lantern ports, maybe, or the last of the good crockery Odile had brought aboard like a peace offering. The fleet was still hull-down behind them, smoke stacked against the morning gray, but the lead tender had cleared the headland and its guns were finding their range with the patience of something that had done this many times to many smaller boats.

"How long?" Marguerite asked.

"To send what matters? Ten minutes if the set holds. Fifteen if it doesn't." He jammed a connection home with his thumb. "But here's the trick of it, Captain. I don't need ten minutes of clear air. I need thirty seconds, repeated until somebody ashore writes it down. Open channels. All bands. Plain speech, no code, coordinates and names and dates. Any harbor operator, any ship's wireless man, any bored clerk with a receiver — they all hear the same words at the same time, and after that the navy isn't shooting at a salvage boat anymore. They're shooting at a story."

Marguerite stared at him for exactly one second. Then she turned and bellowed down the hatch: "Nell! Engine room! Give Tam everything she's got and don't let her stall!"

* * *

The engine room of the *Kestrel's Wake* was Tam's second home and first heartbreak, a space barely tall enough to stand in, hot as a foundry, smelling of diesel and rust and the particular burnt sweetness of a boat running past her limits. Nell was already there, wedged against the throttle housing, hands black to the elbow, feeding fuel line like she was hand-feeding something wild.

"She's overheating," Nell shouted over the roar. "Third time this month. If we push her—"

"We're pushing her." Tam dropped to his knees beside the generator tap and wrenched it open. Power surged through the line and his transmitter hummed up from a murmur to a whine. "She'll hate us for an hour and forgive us by winter. That's more than the admiral will do."

He keyed the microphone. His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself, distantly, the way you notice weather through a window — that after years of lying to creditors and customs men and his own reflection about how seaworthy this boat was, his hands had finally learned the difference between fear and focus, and had picked a side.

"This is the salvage vessel *Kestrel's Wake*, calling all stations, all bands," he said. "I am transmitting in plain speech because what follows is not secret. It is suppressed. Listen."

And he read.

He'd copied the pages fair the night before, by lamp, while the others argued about reef passages and charge timers — Marguerite's father's own hand, transcribed in Tam's careful engineer's block letters so that no wireless operator anywhere could blame bad penmanship for what they heard. The dates of the patrol rotation. The order signed by Rear Admiral Corvane authorizing the boarding action on the night the Gideon burned. The tally of souls aboard versus the tally the navy reported lost. The word *mutiny*, struck through in the old man's ink, and beside it, in the same tired hand: *There was no mutiny. There were forty-one men who saw what the cargo was and asked questions, and a captain who answered honestly, and an admiral who could not permit either.*

Outside, the world tried to kill them. Inside the engine room, Tam read the dead man's words into the open air, page after page, his voice level, and Nell watched him with her mouth slightly open, grease-streaked and seventeen and understanding, perhaps for the first time, what the machinery of a boat could actually be for.

"The fleet is firing on us now," Tam said, when the pages ran out and he started over from the top. "They are firing to stop this transmission. Ask yourselves why."

* * *

The third shell hit close enough to throw spray across the deckhouse windows, and above him Tam heard Ilya put the wheel hard over and felt the boat heel into a turn that made the generator scream.

He climbed the ladder two rungs at a time and came up into chaos rendered in gray and white — rain starting, smoke astern, and ahead of them the reef line rising out of the sea like the spine of something drowned, white water combing over it in long ragged rows. The same passage Ilya had threaded two days ago in a storm, except now there was no storm to hide in and a gunboat's wake cutting a straight white line toward them from open water.

"Second tender is flanking north!" Nell shouted from below, her voice coming up the voice-pipe. "They're trying to get ahead of us!"

"Then we won't be where they're going," Ilya said, and did not slow down.

Tam braced himself in the wheelhouse doorway. "Ilya. I need you to keep us alive for twenty minutes. Not clever. Alive."

"I have been keeping this boat alive for eleven years," Ilya said. "Twenty minutes is nothing." And then, quieter, almost to himself, in the accent he'd never quite shed: "The reef knows me now. We have an arrangement."

Marguerite was at the rail with the oilcloth bundle strapped tight across her chest, and Odile beside her, both of them watching the guns. Odile had gone very pale but very still, the way she went still before a dive, and when she spoke her voice carried the strange bright clarity of someone who has already decided how she dies if it comes to that.

"They'll stop shooting once the transmission is confirmed ashore," she said. "Corvane is ruthless, not stupid. Shelling a civilian boat is a scandal. Shelling a civilian boat that's already told the whole coast what you did — that's a confession with artillery."

"Then every minute I'm on the air is a minute he has to choose between killing us and letting us talk," Tam said. "I intend to make him choose wrong either way."

Marguerite looked at him. Something passed across her face that he'd rarely seen there — not gratitude exactly; she wasn't built for gratitude, it caught in her like a fishbone. Respect, maybe. Or recognition. The look of a captain realizing her engineer had quietly become the sharpest weapon on the boat.

"Keep reading," she said.

* * *

He kept reading.

He ran the transmission from the wheelhouse now, antenna lead strung through the broken lantern port, microphone in one fist, the other hand gripping the frame as Ilya took them at the reef passage at a speed Tam considered frankly insulting. The channel was narrow — thirty meters of brown-green water between coral walls that could open the *Wake*'s belly like a letter — and the tide was against them, and the gunboat astern had found a firing solution that didn't care about any of that.

A shell burst on the reef itself, fifty meters off the starboard bow. Coral geysered up black and white. Fragments ticked against the hull like hail.

"He's ranging on us through the smoke," Odile said, flat. "He'll walk them in."

"Then we won't be in a straight line." Ilya's hands moved on the wheel without hurry, small corrections, a language only he and the boat spoke. "Tam. How much longer?"

"As long as you give me."

"Then I'll give you everything."

What followed, Tam would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember a fire. The gunboat's third salvo falling short and throwing a wall of spray across the foredeck. Nell's voice through the pipe, cracking but holding: *"She's redlining, Tam, she can't—"*, and his answer, shouted down the tube: *"She can, tell her I said so."* The moment the passage narrowed and Ilya killed the engine to half and let the tide carry them sideways through a gap Tam swore was narrower than the boat, close enough that he could see the coral under the surface, purple and patient, waiting. The fourth salvo sailing clean over them because the gunner couldn't believe anyone would take that gap and hadn't corrected for it.

And underneath all of it, his own voice, going out into the wet gray air on every band that would carry it:

*"—forty-one men. The navy reported nine. Count the difference. Ask the drowned town of Sallowmere why their fathers never came home. This is the salvage vessel Kestrel's Wake, and we are still transmitting—"*

* * *

The gunboat broke off at the reef line.

Tam watched it happen — the white wake curving away, the guns falling silent, the hull settling back into the haze with the grudging slowness of something that has been given an order it hates. Somewhere on that bridge, an officer had just received the same message every harbor operator from Port Callow to the capital was receiving, and had done the arithmetic Odile had predicted: every shell fired now was a signature on a confession.

"They're standing off," Nell reported, breathless, hauling herself up onto the deck. "Standing off and — Tam, they're signaling. Flags. Ilya, what's—"

"Recall," Ilya said. He hadn't relaxed his grip on the wheel. "Or regroup. With that man, they are usually the same thing."

They came out the far side of the passage into open water, gray and empty and, for the moment, unshot-at. The engine settled into a grumble. Rain silvered down. In the wheelhouse, the four of them stood in a loose ring around the transmitter, which clicked and hummed and cycled through the pages again, faithful as a heartbeat.

Odile laughed. It was a short, disbelieving sound, wrung out of her. "You just made an admiral of the fleet retreat from a salvage boat with a homemade radio."

"I made him retreat from witnesses," Tam said. "The radio was just how I introduced us."

Marguerite put her hand flat on the transmitter casing, the way she'd touch a hull to feel for strain. "How far does it reach?"

"Clear channel, this weather?" Tam shrugged. "Port Callow for certain. The capital relay, probably. Every coastal station in between. By tonight, half the fishing fleet will know those numbers better than their own prayers. You can't unhear a thing like that, Captain. It doesn't work like gold. It works like dye in water."

Nell was staring at him with an expression Tam recognized from mirrors, years ago — the look of someone recalibrating what they thought they were. "You said you were just an engineer."

"I am just an engineer." He checked a fuse, because his hands wanted occupation. "Engineers build things that do what they're told. Today I told it to tell the truth. Same job, really."

* * *

They ran east for an hour before the reply came.

It came not as shells but as a signal lamp, blinking from the haze astern where the fleet had reformed — a long, formal sequence that Ilya read aloud without expression, character by character, because of course Ilya read signal flags and lamp code too, because Ilya was Ilya.

"*To the master of the vessel transmitting:*" Ilya translated. "*Cease broadcast. Stand down. Terms will be extended. Admiral Corvane requests parley.*"

The wheelhouse went quiet except for the transmitter, still patiently reciting the dead.

"Requests," Odile said.

"Parley," said Nell.

Tam looked at Marguerite. She was looking at the signal lamp's fading glow, and her face had gone the way it went in the strongroom, in the hold, at every moment when the past reached up and took her by the throat — grief and fury fused into something with edges. Her father's pages rose and fell against her chest with her breathing.

"He can't shoot us anymore," she said slowly. "So now he'll buy us. That's the whole of his vocabulary, in the end. Guns and gold." She turned. "Tam. Kill the transmitter."

Tam's hand stopped over the switch. "Captain—"

"Not forever." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "Just long enough that he learns silence has a price too. Long enough that when we answer him, he understands we heard every word he sent and we are not afraid. Then we decide whether we parley." A breath. "And whatever he offers — and he'll offer everything, Tam, he offered my father everything once, I've read it in his own hand — we walk in knowing that the truth is already out of our hands and into the world's. Dye in the water. It doesn't come back."

Tam held her gaze for a moment. Then he reached down and cut the power, and the transmitter died mid-sentence, and the silence it left was enormous.

"He's hailing again," Ilya said.

"Let him hail," Marguerite said, and turned the wheel herself, and the *Kestrel's Wake* came about toward the smoke on the horizon.
