## 19. The Admiral's Bargain

They came for us at slack water, when the tide forgot which way it was going and the sea lay flat as poured pewter.

I heard the launch before I saw it—the deep, unhurried thrum of a navy engine that had never once needed to hurry in its life. Tam was below decks with the transmitter, still cycling the logbook pages out on every open channel he could find, and Ilya stood at the wheel with his hands loose and ready, and Nell was up on the foredeck with the signal glass pressed to her eye.

"Single launch," she called back. "White ensign. No escort." A pause. "There's an old man standing at the rail."

Not a man, as it turned out. An admiral.

Vice-Admiral Corvane Halloway came aboard our salt-crusted, shell-pocked salvage boat like a man visiting a property he already owned. He was sixty if he was a day, silver at the temples, uniform pressed to a knife-edge that the sea spray didn't dare wrinkle. Two marines waited in the launch behind him, and behind them, far off to the east, the salvage fleet sat at anchor over the Gideon like carrion birds waiting their turn.

He looked around our deck with polite distaste—at the patched planking, the coiled line, the brass fittings we'd pulled from drowned men—and then his eyes found me and stayed there.

"Marguerite Yarrow," he said. "You have your father's face."

The words landed like a hook set deep. I hadn't seen my father since I was nine years old. I had spent twenty years building a life in the shape of his absence, and here was a stranger wearing gold braid telling me I wore his ghost on my skin.

"You have his signature," I said. "On the order that sank him."

Something flickered behind the admiral's eyes—annoyance, maybe, or respect. He gestured toward the stern, where we kept a bench beneath the awning, and when I didn't move he simply walked there himself and sat, unhurried, as though we were old friends meeting at a regatta.

"I'll stand," I said.

"As you like." He folded his hands. "Your engineer is very resourceful. Do you know how many receivers picked up your broadcast? I do. Four hundred and eleven within the first hour. Coastal stations, merchant vessels, two foreign news cutters sitting just outside territorial water, waiting for exactly this kind of scandal to fall into their laps." He said it without heat, the way a man reports the weather. "You understand my position."

"I understand you're cornered."

"I am *inconvenienced*," he corrected mildly. "Cornered implies I've run out of options. I haven't. But the options available to me now are all expensive, and some of them are ugly, and I have reached an age where I prefer elegance." He studied me. "So let me offer you something elegant instead."

Behind me, I heard the hatch open. Tam came up with the transmitter's key still clipped to his belt, and Odile followed, and Ilya left the wheel long enough to plant himself at the foot of the stairs with his arms crossed. Nell stayed on the foredeck, glass raised, watching the marines in the launch. My whole strange family arranging itself around me without a word spoken. The admiral noticed. He was the kind of man who noticed everything; it was how he'd survived forty years of covering things up.

"Go on," I said.

"First, the facts, so we bargain honestly." He counted them off on gloved fingers. "One: you are holding documents of unknown provenance, recovered from a naval wreck under a concession permit you obtained through misrepresentation. Two: you have broadcast classified material on open channels, which is a hanging offense in wartime and would be a very long prison sentence in this uneasy peace. Three: your boat has fired upon no one, but she *has* been fired upon by units under my command, which I deeply regret and which will never appear in any report I sign." He smiled thinly. "And four: you are, at this moment, sitting inside my gun range with a hold full of evidence and nowhere to run. Is that a fair summary?"

"It's your summary."

"It's the only summary that matters." He leaned forward slightly. "Now. Here is what I offer."

* * *

He offered it the way a jeweler lays out stones—one at a time, letting each catch the light before moving to the next.

"A full pardon," he said. "For you, for Vasseur, for every soul aboard. Whatever the courts decide about the broadcast—and they will decide quickly, because I will make sure they decide quickly—you walk free. Your concession permit becomes permanent. The Gideon is yours, legally, in perpetuity. Every fitting, every ingot, every scrap of salvage above the strongroom. That's a fortune, Miss Yarrow. You know better than anyone what that hull is worth. You could pay off every creditor in this harbor twice over and buy a new ship with what's left."

Tam made a small sound behind me—not quite a word. I didn't turn around.

"Second." The admiral's voice softened by a degree, and that softness was the most dangerous thing on the deck. "Your father's record. Cleared. Restored. Full honors, back pay to his date of loss, a memorial stone in the naval yard with his name carved in granite and his rank restored above it. His logbook entries entered into the official history. He goes down in the books as the officer who tried to stop a mutiny, not the mutineer who died in one." He paused. "I understand that matters to you. It should. He was a good man. That's rather the problem, from my perspective."

"And third?" I said, because there was always a third.

"Third." He reached into his coat and produced a slim envelope, sealed with wax, and set it on the bench beside him. "A letter of credit. Fifty thousand crowns, drawn on a bank that doesn't ask questions. Enough that neither you nor your crew ever has to dive another rusted hull or flinch at a patrol boat again. Enough that the next generation of Yarrows grows up hearing stories about the sea instead of debts to it."

The envelope sat there. Ordinary paper. The weight of it seemed to bend the light around it.

"In exchange for?"

"The documents. All of them. The pages your engineer is broadcasting—he stops, now, and hands me the master copies. The originals your divers brought up. Any duplicates. And your silence, going forward, witnessed by whatever legal instrument my advocates draft tonight." He spread his hands. "In return, everyone lives well. Everyone lives *free*. Your father's name comes home clean. And the navy closes an ugly chapter that happened before you were old enough to remember it."

The wind had died completely. The sea held its breath. Somewhere below decks, I could hear the transmitter's clock ticking through its cycle, getting ready to send the next batch of pages out into the world whether anyone bargained or not.

"And the truth?" I asked.

He looked at me with something almost like pity. "The truth, Miss Yarrow, is that thirty-one men died aboard the Gideon because my orders were ambiguous and my captain chose to interpret them in the worst possible way, and by the time I understood what had happened, burying it was the only thing that kept a fleet and a coastline from tearing itself apart. I have carried that for twenty-two years. I will carry it to my grave. What I will not do is hand a mob of journalists and politicians a story they'll use to gut the service that holds this coast together, over an event older than half the people who'd read about it." He stood, slowly, joints cracking. "That is not corruption. That is triage. You of all people should understand triage—you were doing it in a flooded corridor twelve hours ago, choosing what to carry and what to leave behind."

"That's the difference," I said quietly. "We left the gold. We took the truth."

"Yes," he said. "Sentimental. And expensive. Which brings us to the alternative, which I'd hoped to spare you." He nodded eastward, toward the fleet. "If you refuse, I take this boat, I take those documents, and I take all of you into custody. Some of you will be charged. Vasseur certainly—her record makes her convenient. Your engineer, probably. The girl—" his eyes flicked to Nell on the foredeck, and I felt something go cold and hard in my chest, "—the girl I can likely lose in the paperwork. Children drift through the system. But you, Miss Yarrow. You broadcast it. You're the name on it. Twenty years, minimum, and your father's name stays exactly where I buried it: in a file marked *mutiny*, in an archive nobody opens."

He let it sit. He was good at letting things sit. Forty years of practice.

"So," he said. "Pardon, fortune, your father's honor—or prison, ruin, and the lie preserved forever. Take your time. Though not much of it. My fleet weighs anchor in two hours regardless of your answer, and I would genuinely prefer we part as friends."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like your father's face, restored. It arrives speaking in the voice of every debt collector you've ever paid, every creditor you've hidden from, every night you've lain awake doing arithmetic on the cost of a new pump seal. It arrives offering not what you want but what you *need*, itemized, reasonable, wrapped in a pardon.

Fifty thousand crowns. I thought of Tam's hands, scarred and clever, always fixing what we couldn't afford to replace. Of Ilya, who hadn't slept a full night since the diver died on the Gideon seven years ago, and who might finally sleep if the wreck belonged to us legally and the ghosts could be given wages. Of Nell, who'd come aboard with nothing but a borrowed coat and who deserved, at minimum, a coat of her own. Of Odile's brother, locked in some navy cell as collateral—I glanced at Odile, and saw her watching the admiral with an expression I couldn't read, and remembered that a pardon for her meant a pardon that might reach him too.

My father's name in granite. His rank restored. *Officer who tried to stop a mutiny.* I would be able to visit a stone with his true self carved into it. I could put flowers there. I could tell the harbor, tell the world: *that was my father, and he was good.*

All of it. Everything I had wanted since I was nine years old and the navy boat came to our door without him.

For silence.

I walked to the rail and looked east. The salvage fleet sat fat and patient on the horizon, cranes folded like sleeping insects. Beneath them, down in the dark, the Gideon's strongroom gaped where we'd breached it, empty now of everything except the gold we'd chosen to leave and the charges the navy's divers had already planted against her ribs. They were going to blow her closed forever. Bury the room, bury the massacre, bury the last of my father's handwriting under a hundred tons of collapsed coral and steel.

And I thought about what he'd written in those final pages, in a hand gone shaky from cold and fear, by lamplight in a flooding ship while his own admiral's orders strangled the truth in its crib:

*If anyone reads this, don't trade it. Names are cheap. Say it out loud.*

"Dad," I murmured, to the flat gray water, "you couldn't have made it easy, could you."

The admiral misread my silence as negotiation beginning. I felt him shift behind me, felt the deck creak under his polished boots as he rose. "Miss Yarrow. Marguerite. May I call you Marguerite? I knew your mother, briefly, at a fleet review in—" 

"Don't," I said.

I turned around. The envelope still sat on the bench, fat with other people's money. I picked it up. The admiral's face did something complicated—hope and calculation braided together—and I held the envelope out to him, and watched the hope win, and then I tore it in half.

The sound it made was very small for how loud it felt.

"Fifty thousand crowns," I said, "doesn't buy back twenty-two years of calling my father a mutineer. A pardon doesn't un-drown thirty-one men. And a memorial stone—" my voice cracked, and I let it, and I watched him hear it, "—a memorial stone with a lie underneath it is just a rock. You can keep your rock."

"Marguerite—"

"No. You had your offer. Here's mine." I dropped the torn halves at his feet. "You recall your fleet. You disarm the charges on the Gideon. You release Odile's brother and every diver and sailor you've been holding as leverage. And then you sit in a public inquiry, under oath, and you say what you just said to me—that your orders were ambiguous, that your captain interpreted them in the worst possible way, that thirty-one men died and you buried it. You get to choose the words. You don't get to choose whether they're spoken."

For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the transmitter clock below decks, counting down to the next broadcast.

"Do you imagine," the admiral said softly, "that you have any leverage at all? Look around you. One rotten hull. Six souls. My fleet could end you between one wave and the next, and by nightfall every copy of those documents would be ash, and in a year nobody would even remember there was a question to ask."

"That's where you're wrong," said Tam, from the companionway, holding up the transmitter key with a grin that had no joy in it anywhere. "See, sir, the thing about radio is—it's already out. Four hundred and eleven receivers, you said. Make it five hundred by now. You can burn our copies. You can't burn theirs."

"And," Nell added from the bow, lowering the signal glass with a seventeen-year-old's terrible precision, "there's a cutter flying press colors anchored off the shoal light. Been there since dawn. I signaled them an hour ago. They're coming closer."

The admiral looked at her. Then at the torn envelope at his feet. Then at me—and for just a moment, behind the braid and the certainty, I saw the other thing he'd carried for twenty-two years, the thing under the triage and the arithmetic. Not guilt exactly. Fear. The fear of a man who has built his whole life on a buried room and hears, faintly, digging.

"You're making a mistake," he said.

"Probably," I agreed. "But it's ours to make."

He straightened his coat. He stepped back onto his launch without another word, and the marines pulled away across the pewter-flat water, and the engine note faded eastward toward the fleet.

Nobody on our deck moved for a full minute. Then Ilya exhaled through his nose—the closest he came to laughter—and said, "Two hours until they weigh anchor. Someone should probably decide what we do with them."

I looked at the horizon, where the cranes were beginning, very slowly, to unfold.

"We're not running," I said. "And we're not hiding. Get me the fleet's frequency."
