## 21. Scuttling the Prize

Ilya had spent thirty years on the water and had learned one thing early: the sea keeps what it takes, and it does not care what you think about it. Men raged against that. Men bargained. Men like the admiral in his white cutter sent divers and charges and fleets to argue with the sea, as if the sea were a debtor who could be leaned on.

The sea was not a debtor. The sea was a creditor who never called in the debt all at once, only in installments, and Ilya had paid his share the day he pulled Petros's body off the Gideon's rail with his own hands and felt the cold of the deep water come up the line and into his chest, where some of it had stayed ever since.

So when Marguerite stood on the deck of the *Fairweather* with the admiral's own detonator case open at her feet and said, "We sink it," Ilya did not argue. He had known, in the way he knew weather coming off a flat horizon, that it would end here.

* * *

The negotiation had ended an hour after dawn, and it had ended badly for the admiral, which was why Ilya kept one eye on the fleet's smoke to the north even as Tam rigged the charges.

The broadcast had done its work. Tam had stripped the logbook pages into plain code and pushed them out on every open channel, over and over, for six hours, in a voice that never wavered even when the shells started falling. By midnight the pages were in a dozen newsrooms, in the hands of the drowned town's survivors, on the lips of every harbor rat from here to the capital. The admiral could not silence it. Silence had been his whole currency, and Tam had flooded the market.

So he had come out to the *Fairweather* under a white flag, in a launch too clean for these waters, and he had offered Marguerite Yarrow everything.

Ilya had stood at the rail during the parley, arms crossed, watching. He'd heard the terms: full pardon, the salvage concession signed permanent, her father's service record restored and gilded, a sum of money that would have bought the harbor itself. All of it in exchange for the original logbook pages, the negatives, and a promise to say the Gideon had been lost to weather and nothing more.

Marguerite had listened all the way through. That was the thing about her that people who didn't know her got wrong — they thought her stubbornness was quick, hot, a refusal spat out. But she listened first, always. She let the man finish his beautiful offer. Then she'd said, quietly, "My father's name isn't yours to sell back to me," and turned away, and that was the end of it.

The admiral's face hadn't changed. That was the frightening part. Men like him didn't rage either. They recalculated.

Now the recalculations were done, and the fleet was moving, and the *Fairweather* was holding station over the Gideon's grave with the navy's own demolition charges lashed to her rails.

* * *

"They'll try to board before we can drop them," Odile said. She was at the chart table, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with what Ilya recognized as a length of signal cord she'd cut off a navy buoy herself. She'd stopped pretending, somewhere in the last two days, that she was anything other than one of them. Her brother's name was on the first page of her notebook, underlined twice, and she'd shown it to Marguerite without being asked. That act had settled something on this boat that no vote could have.

"Then we drop them before they board," Tam said. He had the detonator's wiring spread out on the deck like a surgeon's instruments, and his hands were steady, which Ilya was grateful for, because his own were not, quite.

"It's not that simple." Tam looked up. "The admiral's divers set those charges for a collapse-inward. Sealed tomb. That's what he wanted — close it forever, no one gets in. But we don't want a sealed tomb."

"We want a grave," Ilya said.

Everyone looked at him. He didn't talk much, and he knew it, and he'd made his peace with it, but some things you had to say out loud or they rotted in you.

"We don't want it hidden where a patient man can dig it out in twenty years," he said. "We want it *gone*. Collapsed and then collapsed again. Break the hull open, let the current through. The channel under her stern runs four knots on the flood. Give the sea the strongroom and let the sea do the rest. Gold doesn't survive that. Paper doesn't. Nothing a man could carry out in his hands."

Silence on the deck. Nell was coiling line and had stopped, the coil hanging loose in her hands.

"You want to blow the charges and then breach the hull," Tam said slowly, working it. "Two detonations. The first brings the strongroom down. The second opens her to the current."

"The second charge has to go off after we're clear of the reef," Ilya said. "And the first has to go off while their divers are still down there, or the admiral sends them in after we've gone and they cut the second charge loose."

Marguerite had come to stand beside him at the rail. She was looking down at the water — that particular green-black where the Gideon lay, forty fathoms down, the wreck nobody names.

"Her father's down there," Nell said softly. It wasn't a protest. It was just the truth, said the way the young say true things, without armor.

"Yes," Marguerite said. "And he's been down there twenty years, and he's not coming up, and he wouldn't want to. He'd want the truth up. The truth's already up. It's in a hundred hands by now." She looked at Ilya. "He'd want the rest of it drowned. He wrote that in the log. *Some things should stay under.* I thought he meant the gold. I think he meant the whole ship."

Ilya nodded once. It was all the speech he had.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the fleet worked too, and the two works were racing.

The navy's approach was not subtle. They had stopped pretending to be patient the moment Marguerite refused the bargain. Tenders spread out in a line north of the reef, and Ilya could read their intentions in their spacing — they meant to close the reef passages by afternoon and pen the *Fairweather* against the shoal. After that, boarding parties, arrests, and the admiral's version of events would become the only version with anyone alive left to tell it.

Except it was already too late for that, and everyone on both sides knew it, and that was the strangest part. The shells they might still fire. The arrests they might still make. But the story had slipped its mooring in the night, and no fleet in the world could drag it back.

"They're not coming at us full speed," Tam observed, squinting through the glass. "They're coming at us *carefully*. He doesn't want this in front of witnesses either. He wants us gone quiet, not gone loud."

"Then we'll be loud," Odile said, and for the first time in two days she smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and Ilya found he liked her better for it.

The plan, such as it was, had three parts.

First: Tam would rewire the navy's own charges — the divers had planted six of them along the strongroom corridor before the parley, and the detonator case the admiral's signalman had "lost" during the confusion of the broadcast was now in Tam's hands. The first three charges would fire together, collapsing the strongroom inward, burying the vault.

Second: the *Fairweather* would run the southern passage — Ilya's passage, the one he'd threaded twice already under shellfire, the one the navy charts marked as impassable because the navy had never asked anyone who actually lived here.

Third: at the mouth of the passage, in forty fathoms over open sand, the remaining three charges, dropped free on a timed fuse, would fire against the Gideon's stern hull — not to sink her further, but to *open* her, to let the channel current scour through the wreck like water through a broken jar. Whatever the first blast buried, the sea would unbury and scatter and take.

No tomb. A grave, and then not even that. Just sea.

* * *

Ilya took the wheel at midday, when the tide began to make.

He always took the wheel for the passages. It wasn't pride, exactly. It was that the reef was the one place where his silences were useful — where a man needed to stop talking and start listening with his hands. The wheel spoke to him in pressures and vibrations. The water ahead spoke in color. Green meant sand, brown meant rock near the surface, and the terrible blue-black seams meant deep water running fast between walls that would open a hull like a tin.

"Charges set," Tam called from below. "Timers armed. First firing on your mark, Ilya. Second is on the clock — eleven minutes after the first, no way to stop it once it starts."

"Then we don't stop it," Ilya said.

Marguerite stood at his shoulder. Odile was forward with Nell, working the fenders and the lines, and the two of them moved together now without speaking, which was its own kind of settlement. Bosun's business, Ilya thought. You could tell a crew by how they handled rope. A crew that trusted each other didn't watch each other's hands.

The fleet was four miles off and closing. A gun at the lead tender's bow swung toward them, deliberate, a threat made visible.

"They'll fire," Nell said.

"Probably," Ilya said.

"Does that ever stop being terrifying?"

He considered the question honestly, the way he considered most things. "No," he said. "You just get better at being terrified and steering at the same time."

Nell laughed — actually laughed, a short bright bark of a sound — and went back to her lines, and Ilya thought: *that one will be fine.* Whatever came of all this, that one would carry it well.

* * *

The first shell fell short, a white plume off the port bow, and the second walked closer, and Ilya put the *Fairweather* at the mouth of the passage with the sound of the third shell still cracking the air behind them.

"Mark," he said.

Tam fired the first three charges.

Even at forty fathoms, even through a hull of water, they felt it. It came up through the keel like a fist knocking from underneath — a deep, rolling concussion, and then a second, and a third, close enough together to be one long brutal syllable. Far astern, over the Gideon's grave, the sea swelled and bulged and then burst white, a geyser of spray and black water standing for a moment against the sky like a tree, and then falling.

The sound of it reached them a beat later, a flat boom rolling across the water, and then the sea closed over the place where the strongroom had been, and it was done. Twenty years of gold, of sealed orders, of the admiral's buried evidence, pressed into mud and rubble in the space of a breath.

Ilya had expected to feel triumphant. What he felt instead was lighter and sadder at once, the way he'd felt at his mother's funeral — grief and relief in the same hand, indistinguishable.

"Strongroom's down," Tam reported, voice rough. "I've got secondary collapse on the readout. She's folding in on herself."

"Good," Marguerite said. Then, quieter, so only Ilya heard: "Goodbye, Papa."

He didn't look at her. Some griefs you honored by not looking.

The fleet's guns kept firing, but the shells fell wider now — the tenders were turning, Ilya realized, watching their smoke bend, because their own charts told them the *Fairweather* had gone into water they considered suicide, and no commander wanted to explain to the admiralty why he'd followed.

"Fourth shell hit the reef," Nell called. "They're shooting at the rocks now. They're giving up on us."

"They're not giving up," Odile said. "They're choosing what to lose. There's a difference." But she said it without bitterness, almost with respect, the way you'd speak of an opponent who'd finally made a sensible move.

* * *

The passage took everything Ilya had, and he gave it gladly, because giving it meant not thinking.

The reef walls rose on either side, close enough to read the weed on the rocks. The current grabbed the *Fairweather*'s stern and tried to swing her broadside, and Ilya fought her nose-first through the seam, hands moving on the wheel in small corrections, the whole boat trembling like a living thing. Marguerite called the depths. Nell called the walls. Tam called the clock — nine minutes, eight — and Odile stood at the bow pointing out the dark seams before Ilya saw them, and somewhere in the middle of it Ilya understood that this was the thing he'd been keeping all these years without knowing its name.

Not the Gideon. Not the debt. Not even Petros's memory, though that lived in him still and always would.

This. The wheel in his hands and the voices behind him and the water trying to kill them all and every one of them doing their one job without being told twice.

"Seven minutes," Tam called.

"Then we're going to make it," Marguerite said, "or we're going to be very close."

They came out of the seam into the open channel with the sun full on the water, and Ilya put the wheel over and ran for the deep water over the Gideon's stern, and at four minutes on the clock he rang for full stop and they drifted, all of them at the rails, watching the flat green sea below.

* * *

The second firing came at three minutes, and it was different from the first.

Not a fist. A door opening.

The sea lifted in a long slow swell, not violent, almost gentle, and a ring of white foam spread out from a point below them and widened, and the water over the wreck changed color — went from green to a churning brown-gold as the current found the new breach and began, at four knots, to pour through the Gideon's broken stern like water through a broken jar.

"Scouring's started," Tam said softly. "In a month there'll be nothing in that hull but sand. In a year, not even the hull."

Nobody spoke for a while. The foam ring widened. The fleet hung back at the edge of the reef, smoke standing straight in the still air, doing nothing, because there was nothing left to do. The gold was gone. The evidence was gone. The admiral's secret was a hole in the seabed that the tide was already sweeping clean.

"It's right," Nell said at last, uncertain, asking more than saying.

"It's right," Marguerite said. "Some things should stay drowned. He knew that before any of us did."

Ilya looked down at the water and let himself do something he hadn't let himself do in twenty years: he said goodbye to the Gideon. Not to the wreck — to what she'd stood for, the debt and the dread and the name nobody spoke. Petros was not down there. Petros was in the drowned town, in the old woman's stories, in the logbook pages now in a thousand hands. Petros had gotten out after all. They all had.

The sea kept what it took. But it gave things back too, if you waited long enough, and if you had people willing to wait with you.

"Come about," Marguerite said. "Take us home, Ilya."

He put the wheel over, and the *Fairweather* came around slow and easy, her bow swinging toward the open water and the long light of late afternoon, and behind them the foam ring over the Gideon widened and softened and began, already, to fade.

* * *

They were an hour out of the reef when Nell, at the masthead, called down the thing that would shape everything after.

"Sail! Two sails — no, three. Small craft, working boats, from the north." A pause, and then her voice changed, went strange and bright. "They're flying signal flags. Tam — it's your broadcast code. They're saying—" she squinted, translating letter by letter, and then she laughed, a sound Ilya had never heard from her at sea, pure and unguarded. "They're saying *We heard you. We're coming to stand with you.*"

Ilya looked at Marguerite. Marguerite looked at the horizon, where the little fleet of the drowned town's survivors came on under plain sails, and something in her face let go — the last knot in the last line, slipping free.

"Well then," she said. "Signal them back."

"What do I say?" Nell asked.

Marguerite thought about it. Ilya watched her decide, watched the answer arrive in her the way the tide arrives — not all at once, but all the way through.

"Tell them," she said, "*the debt is paid. Come aboard.*"
