## 22. Tide of Our Own Making

The inquiry hall in Port Meridian smelled of beeswax and old paper, and Marguerite Yarrow sat through the third day of it with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral that wasn't hers.

They had given her a chair too tall for her, a table too wide, and a clerk who read questions off a list as though the answers had been pre-written and she was merely failing to match them. *Did you board the wreck designated Vessel 44-C? Did you remove materials? Were you aware your concession was irregular?*

"Vessel 44-C," she said, for the fourth time. "You mean the *Gideon*."

The clerk's pen stopped. Somewhere behind the raised bench, one of the three commissioners shifted his weight, and the leather creaked like rigging in a blow.

"The vessel's name is not a matter of record," the middle commissioner said.

"No," Marguerite agreed pleasantly. "That was rather the point of everything, wasn't it."

Tam, seated beside her with a satchel of printed pages and the particular stillness he wore when he was calculating odds, made a small sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Nell, on her other side, had gone very straight, the way she did when she thought trouble was coming and wanted to meet it standing.

Three weeks since the reef. Three weeks since Ilya threaded them out from under shellfire with the fleet's guns walking the water behind them like a man following footprints. Three weeks since Tam's broadcast went out on every open channel from the Gullet to the Sounding Sea—her father's logbook pages, transcribed in his own careful hand, describing a strongroom full not of pay-chests but of orders, correspondence, the admiral's own signature on directives that turned a mutiny into a massacre and a massacre into an accident. Three weeks since the crew stood together on a listing deck and refused the admiral's bargain with the whole horizon listening.

The inquiry had begun eleven days ago. It would run for months, everyone said. The commissioners were careful men; the journalists in the gallery were not; and between those two forces, the truth was being dragged into daylight the way you raise a drowned hull—slowly, painfully, and with everyone pretending they'd always intended it to happen.

"Captain Yarrow." The leftmost commissioner, a woman with spectacles and a face like a closed ledger, leaned forward. "I'll ask you directly. The charges against you and your crew are piracy, theft of naval property, and obstruction. The evidence you've produced suggests these charges may be... reconsidered. But I want to understand something no one else has explained to my satisfaction." She removed her spectacles. "You found gold down there. A great deal of it. You could have taken it. Instead you collapsed the strongroom with the navy's own demolition charges and came up with paper. Why?"

Marguerite had expected the question. She'd rehearsed answers on the walk over—something about legality, about leverage, about the value of evidence over bullion. All true. None of it the truth.

She looked at her hands. There was still a faint line across her palm where the compass's brass casing had pressed during the last dive, when the water above them had been shaking with detonations and Odile had shoved the logbooks into the oilskin bag while Marguerite held the light steady on her father's final message, inked in the dark of a sealed room by a dying man: *If anyone finds this, we were loyal. Remember that we were loyal.*

"Some things should stay drowned," she said. "Gold keeps. Truth doesn't—not unless someone carries it up. We only had hands enough for one."

The commissioner studied her for a long moment. Then she put her spectacles back on and said, "Let the record show the witness answered the question," which Marguerite suspected was as close to approval as that room ever got.

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold. The harbor lay flat and bright, masts thick as reeds, and somewhere among them—third berth from the customs house, where the paint was drying in long green stripes—the *Kestrel* waited.

Their boat. Repaired. Not patched, not jury-rigged, not held together with prayer and tar the way she'd been for six years, but properly rebuilt: new planking below the waterline, a fresh keel shoe, Tam's engine refitted with parts that hadn't been scavenged from three different wrecks. Paid for, officially, by "restitution for services rendered to the public interest"—a phrase the admiralty's lawyers had fought over for nine days before surrendering to it, because the alternative was explaining in open court why a salvage crew had needed restitution at all.

Nell touched Marguerite's elbow. "Odile's boat just cleared the point."

Marguerite turned. Far out past the breakwater, a white ketch with a crimson stripe was standing in toward the harbor under full sail, handling crisp and lovely even at this distance, because of course Odile Vasseur could not arrive anywhere looking less than immaculate.

"She telegraphed this morning," Nell said. "She has him with her."

*

They met at the customs steps, because Odile never docked anywhere so ordinary as a working berth if ceremony was available.

Marguerite watched the ketch come alongside and saw him before she saw his sister: a young man at the rail, lean and dark-haired, gripping it with both hands as though the solid world were something he'd stopped trusting. He was thinner than the photograph Odile kept hidden in her chart drawer—heavier than the ghost of him Odile carried in her voice whenever she spoke of home—and when the lines went ashore he didn't move until Odile climbed down to the steps herself and reached up and took his hand.

"Lucien," she said, quietly, in the voice she used for exactly nothing else. "Feet on stone. Go on."

He came down the steps like a man learning to walk again, and when his boots hit the granite he stood there swaying slightly, blinking at the ordinary noise of the harbor—gulls, cart wheels, a fishwife arguing about prices—as though it were a language he'd forgotten.

Odile turned to Marguerite. Her face was composed, her coat immaculate, but her eyes were wet and furious about it, and she didn't bother hiding either.

"The exchange happened at sea, as agreed. The admiral's people brought him out on a tender two days after the inquiry opened. They were very punctual." Her mouth twisted. "Punctuality is the last virtue of a collapsing institution."

"How is he?"

"He doesn't sleep well. He flinches at uniformed men. He asked me, twice, whether it was real." Odile looked at her brother, who had discovered Nell offering him a heel of bread and was regarding it with the caution of a man offered a live grenade. "It will be years before he's what he was. But he is free, and I am—" She stopped. Started again, and Marguerite understood what it cost her. "I am done owing anyone anything. Do you know how long it's been since I could say that?"

"I'm starting to find out," Marguerite said.

They looked at each other across twenty years of rivalry—two women who had spent their careers taking wrecks out from under each other's noses, who had lied and outbid and once, memorably, sunk each other's dinghies in a dispute over a bronze cannon—and Marguerite found that the old ledger had simply closed. No balance outstanding. No debt either direction. Just the strange lightness of a rival who had become, somewhere in a flooded corridor with the air running out, something closer to family.

"Dinner," Odile said briskly, before either of them could say anything unforgivably sincere. "All of you. My treat, and I've already reserved the good table at the Lantern, so don't embarrass me by wearing salt-crusted things."

"We're salvage divers," Marguerite said. "Everything we own is salt-crusted."

"Then wear your best salt."

Lucien, emboldened by the bread, said his first words ashore: "Is it true you sank her dinghy?"

"It was a war," Marguerite said gravely. "There were casualties."

His laugh came out rusty, half-broken, real. Beside her, she felt Odile go still with something that was almost peace.

*

The *Kestrel* sailed on the morning tide, four days later, and Marguerite stood at the wheel with the compass open on the binnacle in front of her and watched the needle do the most ordinary thing in the world.

It pointed north.

Not along the *Gideon*'s old patrol route. Not toward the drowned frigate or the sealed room or the coordinates of her father's last hours. North—honest, dull, reliable north, the needle settling with a small sigh against its mark like a dog lying down. Whatever current had run through the thing—whatever her father had done to it, or whatever it had become in the years it spent pointing at the truth instead of the pole—it had finished its work. Tam had opened the casing twice trying to find the mechanism and found nothing but good brass and older brass and, wedged beneath the card, a sliver of paper gone soft as cloth, with a single word in her father's hand: *enough.*

She'd cried over that, privately, at the rail, where only Ilya saw, and Ilya had said nothing, which was his way of saying everything.

Now the harbor fell away astern. The town shrank to a scatter of roofs, then a smudge, then a memory, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the long swell with her new planking tight and silent, no groans, no weeping seams, just the clean song of a sound hull doing what it was built for.

The crew assembled on deck as the coast opened up. It happened without anyone calling it—Nell up the shrouds before she was asked, Ilya at the sheets with his big hands gentle as a nurse's, Tam emerging from the engine space wiping grease onto a rag that had started life white sometime last year. Odile had declined to join them—"I have a brother to teach how to be a person again, and a reputation to rebuild, and you lot are terrible influences"—but she'd stood on the customs steps as they cast off and raised two fingers in her old salute, the one that meant *until next wreck*, and Lucien beside her had copied it clumsily, and Marguerite had laughed until her ribs hurt.

"So," Tam said, spreading a chart on the deckhouse roof and weighting the corners with winch handles. "Where are we actually going? Because I've been reading the papers, and there's a merchantman on the Corvane shoals—went down in '09, nobody's touched her, insurance dispute finally settled. And there's talk of a convoy loss off the Skerries that the navy hasn't gotten around to yet."

"There's always talk," Ilya rumbled.

"There's also," Nell said, dropping down from the shrouds and landing light as a gull, "the reward. For the testimony. The commission mentioned compensation for 'material assistance to the inquiry.'"

"They'll pay it late and grudgingly," Marguerite said.

"Then we take jobs in the meantime like honest people," Tam said, "and when it comes, we drink most of it and refit the rest."

"Honest people," Ilya repeated, in the tone of a man tasting an unfamiliar dish.

"We don't have to be *good* at it," Marguerite said. "Just adjacent."

They laughed—all of them, together, the sound going out over the water—and Marguerite felt it settle into the boat's timbers the way ballast settles, low and sure. Six years she had steered this crew by the weight of a debt: the money owed, the name owed, the father owed, the wreck owed. She had believed, without ever saying it aloud, that the debt was the keel—that without it they'd all drift apart, that obligation was the only thing holding a handful of broke, stubborn, damaged people into something resembling a family.

She knew better now. Keels weren't made of debt. They were made of choice, laid plank by plank, renewed every morning someone decided to stay aboard.

"Corvane shoals," she said, and put the wheel over, and the *Kestrel* came round sweet and willing onto the new heading.

*

They made the shoal grounds on the third day, running easy before a friendly wind, and anchored at dusk in the lee of a green island with sheep on it and nothing else. Tam cooked. Nell told a story about the fog bank that grew more heroic with each telling—Ilya now featured as a figure of myth, arms like capstans, threading reefs blind—and Ilya listened with his eyes closed and his face carefully neutral, which meant he loved it.

Afterward, Marguerite walked to the bow alone, as she did most nights now, and stood with the compass in her hand.

She didn't need to check it anymore. She checked it anyway, the way you visit a grave—not for information but for company. The needle swung, settled, pointed north. Behind her the crew's voices rose and tangled and softened; ahead the dark water ran out to the edge of the charts, where new wrecks waited with their pockets full of other people's secrets, and some of those secrets, she supposed, would need carrying up someday, by somebody, and she no longer feared being that somebody.

Her father had signed his last message *loyal*. For years she'd thought loyalty meant the navy, the flag, the service he'd died inside. Now she knew it meant the men beside him in the dark, and the woman who would come looking decades later, and the crew who chose the paper over the gold.

"Enough," she said aloud, to the compass, to the water, to him.

The needle trembled once—wind, probably, or imagination—and held true.

Ahead, far off on the black rim of the world, a light blinked twice: a lighthouse, or a ship, or the first star of the evening making landfall. Nell called from the cockpit that the tide was turning fair, and Marguerite Yarrow turned the boat to meet it, sailing out on a tide of their own making, into whatever rose next.
