## 1. The Lowest Tide in Forty Years

The tide went out so far that morning that it took the village's understanding of itself with it.

August Joubert noticed it first as a wrongness in the light. She was under the hull of Marta Ellery's fishing skiff, flat on her back on two sawhorses, scraping barnacles with a chisel she'd bought secondhand and sharpened herself, when the shadow under the boat changed quality. Not darker—*emptier*. She slid out, blinking salt from her lashes, and looked at the harbor.

Greyharbor's harbor was a generous word for a mudflat with ambitions. At high tide it held perhaps forty boats at their moorings, bobbing like patient dogs; at low tide it became a brown plain cut with channels, smelling of weed and old rope and the particular sourness of things the sea had decided to give back. August had lived beside it three years now, long enough to know its moods the way you know a landlord's moods—with vigilance and low expectations.

But this was different. The water had withdrawn past the channel markers, past the kelp line, past the black-stained rocks where the gulls held their parliament. It had gone out and out and kept going, until the horizon of mud stretched nearly to the headland, and the boats sat heeled over on their bellies like sleeping cattle, and people were coming out of their houses in their aprons and boots just to stand and stare.

"Forty years," said Marta Ellery, who had appeared at August's elbow with the silent suddenness of a woman who owned a skiff and therefore felt entitled to commentary. "My grandmother talked about a tide like this. Forty years ago, near enough. The Lowest Tide, they called it."

"You'd have been a child," August said.

"I wasn't born." Marta squinted at the exposed seabed with the expression of a woman appraising a rival's garden. "Look at all that. Somebody's lost anchor chain. Somebody's lost *dignity*, by the look of the Pellwicks' mooring."

August looked. And beneath the ordinary wreckage—the chains, the crab pots, the ancient and indignant starfish—she saw something else.

At the base of the headland, where the cliffs met the sea in a tumble of dark rock, there was an opening. A cave mouth, tall as a church door, black and dry, breathing out cold air. She had walked that stretch of shore a hundred times at low tide and never seen it, because it had never been there to see. The sea had kept it the way a miser keeps coins.

"What's that?" she asked.

Marta followed her gaze, and August watched the woman's face do something complicated. "That," Marta said, "is nothing we talk about."

"Everything here is nothing you talk about. That's practically the village motto."

"It's the Devil's Ledger." Marta said it the way you'd name a disease. "Old story. My gran said when the Lowest Tide came, her father and some other men went out to it with lanterns. Came back before noon, wouldn't say what they'd seen, and one of them never went to sea again. Took up turnip farming. *Turnips*, August. On that soil." She shook her head at the magnitude of the sacrifice. "They say the Ledger keeps accounts. What you owe. What you've done. The devil writes it all down in there."

"And your gran's father saw his account?"

"He saw something. Turnips after." Marta clapped her hands once, briskly, the way villagers did when a conversation approached feeling. "Well. I'll leave you to your barnacles."

She left. August stayed where she was, chisel in hand, and looked at the cave for a long time.

Here was the thing about August Joubert, and the thing she had spent three years teaching herself not to be: she was curious the way other people were hungry or afraid—constantly, bodily, without any respect for what was good for her. Curiosity had made her a mountain guide at nineteen. Curiosity had taken her up routes that sensible climbers named after the people who died on them. And curiosity, though she tried very hard not to draw the line in ink, had played some part—along with bad weather, worse luck, and a man whose name she did not let herself think whole—in the disaster that had ended her first life and begun this one, in which she mended boats in a village that called her *the quiet one* and meant it as praise.

The quiet one. That was the role. You kept your head down, you fixed what was broken, you paid Corvin the harbormaster his interest every Friday with both hands visible, and you never, ever gave anyone a reason to look at you twice. Three years of practice. She was good at it now. She could feel the instinct sitting in her chest like a well-trained dog, telling her: *stay under the hull, finish the barnacles, let the devil keep his own ledger.*

The dog also told her, in a smaller voice, that the tide would turn in four hours, maybe five, and the sea would take the cave back for another forty years.

August looked at the skiff. She looked at the cave. She thought about Marta's great-grandfather and his turnips.

"Well," she said aloud, to nobody, because being the quiet one didn't mean talking to yourself was off-limits—it just meant nobody listened. "Accounts want settling."

She put away the chisel. She got her boots, her coat, her storm-lantern from under the workbench, and a coil of good line off the wall, because a person could stop being a guide but a person did not walk toward holes in the world without rope. Old habits were the only inheritance she had left, and she intended to spend them.

* * *

The walk out across the seabed was like walking on the moon if the moon smelled worse.

The mud sucked at her boots in long, reluctant kisses. Channels of trapped seawater lay steaming faintly in the pale morning sun, and everything moved—the worms, the shrimp, the little darting fish stranded in pools, furious at the universe. Gulls stalked the flats like creditors at a funeral, picking over the sea's abandoned furniture. Behind her, half of Greyharbor had turned out to do the same, wandering the exposed bottom in clumps, exclaiming over anchors, arguing about salvage rights with a volume that carried beautifully across the flat.

Nobody, she noted, was walking toward the headland.

The crowd thinned as she went. By the time she reached the boulder field below the cliff, she was alone except for the gulls, who regarded her with the frank appraisal of creatures who knew exactly how the food chain worked and were merely waiting for confirmation.

The cave mouth was bigger up close. Tall as a church door, she'd said, and that had been modest; it was tall as a church, or near enough, a black arch in the cliff's dark stone, its rim furred with dead barnacles marking how high the sea usually reached—and how far above her head that was. Dry cobbles sloped down into the interior. Cold air flowed out of it, steady as breath, carrying a smell she couldn't place at first: stone, yes, and salt, but under it something older. Paper, almost. Wax. The smell of a room that had been closed a very long time.

She stood at the threshold and lit the lantern and had the argument with herself properly this time, out loud, because the gulls weren't going to gossip.

"Forty years," she told herself. "It'll be mud and a dead seal. It's always mud and a dead seal."

The well-trained dog in her chest whined.

"But," she added, reasonably, "I've already walked all the way out here, and my knees will be furious if it's for nothing."

She went in.

* * *

The floor rose gently, dry cobbles giving way to smoother stone, and the daylight fell away behind her until the lantern ruled alone. The cave ran deeper than it had any right to. Sea caves, in her limited experience—which was to say, one memorable afternoon in her twenties involving a seal, a swell, and language she still apologized for—went in thirty feet and ended in a wet throat. This one went in a hundred and then a hundred more, and the walls changed as she walked: rough wave-cut stone becoming something dressed, somehow, worked, the surfaces too flat, too deliberate. Her fingers found lines cut into the rock. Straight lines. Regular.

Not a sea cave, then. A sea cave that had eaten something older.

Her heart was doing a thing she refused to name. She made herself slow down, breathe, check her line, do the small professional rituals that kept the curious animal in her chest on its leash. Guide's discipline. Look with your eyes, not your hopes. Hopes got people killed on mountains; presumably they got people killed in caves too, though possibly by different mechanisms.

The passage opened.

The lantern light reached out and found a chamber like the inside of a bell—a round vault of stone, high overhead, dry as a sermon. And along the walls, set into niches cut with that same deliberate regularity, were boxes.

Dozens of them. Slate boxes, lead-sealed, stacked with the tidy patience of someone who had expected to come back and hadn't. Some had fallen and split, spilling their contents across the floor: rolls of oilcloth, bundles tied with tarred string, and—her breath caught despite herself—paper. Actual paper, dry paper, in a hole in the world that the sea visited twice a day and had somehow never touched. There was wax on the seals. There were stamps on the wax.

She knelt by the nearest spill without deciding to. Her hands knew how to be careful with old things; her father had taught her that too, unwrapping survey maps on kitchen tables, saying *dry hands, girl, the mountain keeps better records than we do.* The oilcloth crackled. Inside was a ledger—small, bound in grey leather gone stiff as bark—and she opened it with her thumbs the way you open anything you already know is going to change your day.

Columns. Figures. Names. Dates going back further than Greyharbor's church records, further than anything, written in a copperplate hand that never wavered, never hurried, the handwriting of an institution rather than a person. Tolls. Levies. Payments. And against certain entries, initials she didn't recognize, and against others—

Against one entry, dated sixty-odd years back, a single annotation in a different hand, sharper, angrier, pressed hard enough to tear: *This is not consent. This is arithmetic done at gunpoint.*

August sat back on her heels in the cold dark, holding a dead man's anger, and felt the well-trained dog in her chest lie down flat and go very still.

Because she knew that hand.

Not the name attached to it—she'd need more than initials—but the hand itself, the slant of it, the way the letters leaned forward like walkers into wind. She had grown up watching that hand fill survey notebooks at a kitchen table while a kettle sang. She had inherited her own handwriting from it, God help her, every letter leaning into the weather.

The lantern flame bent in the cave's slow breathing. Somewhere behind her, far off, the gulls were shouting about the turning tide.

"Father," she said quietly, to the dark, to the ledgers, to whatever account was being kept down here. "What did you find?"

There was no answer, naturally. But deeper in the chamber, past the fallen boxes, the light caught something on a stone shelf at the far wall—something that wasn't slate or lead or paper. Something brass, small and round, sitting in its niche as if placed there that morning, waiting with a patience that made her skin prickle.

August got up. The tide, she knew, was already thinking about coming home.

She walked toward the shelf anyway.
