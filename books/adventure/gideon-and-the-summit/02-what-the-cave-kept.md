## 2. What the Cave Kept

The tide kept going out.

August had lived in Greyharbor long enough to know the sea the way you know a bad-tempered dog—respectfully, from a distance, never turning your back on it. She'd seen spring tides strip the mudflats bare. She'd seen storms shove the water into the streets like an unwelcome guest. But she had never seen the harbor simply *leave*, the way it did that morning, sliding back past the channel markers, past the kelp beds, past everything with a name, until the boats sat heeled over in the mud like sleeping cattle and the whole village came out to stare at the ocean floor as if it were a scandal.

"Forty years," old Maren said beside her on the seawall, sucking her teeth. "My mother talked about a tide like this once. Said the sea went out so far you could walk to the Devil's Ledger."

"The what?"

Maren pointed with her pipe stem at a black seam in the headland, half a mile out and normally swallowed whole—a low arch of rock that even now was only half-revealed, dripping, glistening like something newly born. "Cave in the cliff. Fishermen used to say the Company kept its books down there. Its *sins*, if you liked." She knocked ash against the stone. "Nobody's been in since my grandmother's day. Tide comes back mean, they said. Takes what it finds."

August looked at the cave for a long moment, then made herself look away, which was the correct thing to do. The correct thing—the thing she had taught herself over six years of being nobody—was to go back to the boatyard, sand the hull of Corvin's skiff, keep her head down, and let the sea take its secrets home again by afternoon.

She lasted until noon.

* * *

The walk out across the seabed was like crossing the floor of someone else's house. Anemones hung open and stranded, their colors shocking in the flat light. A wrecked dinghy lay on its side, ribs green with age. Starfish the size of dinner plates clung to stones that hadn't felt air in living memory, and everywhere there was the smell—iodine and rot and cold salt—the smell of a world caught mid-sentence.

The cave mouth was taller than it looked from the wall, maybe eight feet, its lip furred with barnacles that crunched under her boots. Inside, the light failed fast. August lit her storm lantern and stood a while just listening: the drip of water finding its way down through the headland, the far-off slap of the retreating sea, her own breath.

"Right," she told herself quietly. "In, look, out. Before the water remembers us."

The passage narrowed, then opened into a chamber where the lantern light simply stopped making sense.

Shelves. Actual shelves, cut into the living rock, and on them—rows upon rows of lead-sealed cylinders, slate tablets stacked like loaves, oilcloth bundles gone stiff as bark. And against the far wall, a strongbox of black iron, its lock rusted open, its lid thrown back as though whoever came last had left in a hurry or never meant to come back at all.

She should have touched nothing. That was rule one of any mountain, any ruin, any place older than your understanding of it: look first, touch second, and only then decide whether you're a guest or a thief.

But her eye had already found the box's contents, and one item among them had stopped her heart mid-beat.

A compass. Brass, palm-sized, its case worn smooth by years of a thumb rubbing the same spot. She knew that wear pattern. She had watched that exact thumb—that broad, scarred, rope-callused thumb—do exactly that, ten thousand times, on the deck of a survey boat, on a ridge above the treeline, on the table while he read aloud after supper because reading silently seemed to him a waste of good words.

She picked it up with hands that were not entirely steady.

Engraved on the reverse, small and deep enough to survive decades of salt: **E.J.** — Elias Joubert. Her father's initials. Her father's compass, which had gone into the Vell gorge with him eleven years ago and never come out, according to every report, every inquiry, every sympathetic shake of the head she'd received before the sympathy ran out and the blacklisting began.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, because some part of her needed to hear how thin it sounded. "No, that's not possible."

But brass doesn't lie about its engraving, and grief doesn't care about possible.

She sat down on the cold stone with the compass in her lap and let the shaking have her for a minute. Six years she had built her life on the shape of that loss: father dead in the gorge, daughter disgraced, name struck from the guides' rolls for a collapse on the Whitefall route that everyone agreed had been her error, her arrogance, her fault. She had carried it the way you carry a stone in your boot—painfully, but familiarly, and always with the strange comfort that at least the weight was *hers*. Earned. Deserved. Knowable.

And now here was his compass, dry and waiting in a cave below the tide line, in a place connected to nothing except the Meridian Company's buried ledgers.

Her father had not died in the gorge. Her father had been *here*.

* * *

It took her an hour to open the oilcloth bundles properly, working the stiff folds loose with her knife tip, and what was inside them rearranged the world.

Survey records. Not copies—originals, in the spidery hand of Company clerks, stamped with the Meridian seal: a mountain peak inside a ring of chain. Water rights for the upper Vell. Timber claims written in language so clean it took her a moment to understand what was being bought and sold: entire valleys, entire watersheds, purchased from families who could not possibly have understood the documents, signed with marks instead of names. Toll schedules for bridges the Company itself had built across public rivers. A page headed *Disposition of Objections* that listed, in neat columns, the names of men who had protested—and beside each name, a date, and beside each date, a single word. *Settled.* Every one of them settled within a season.

She thought of the quarry families upcoast. Of Tam Ashby's people, though she didn't know his name yet—she only knew the shape of the story, the way everyone in Greyharbor knew it: quarry closed, family scattered, another valley gone quiet.

Then she found the map.

It was drawn on linen-backed paper, large as a tablecloth, and it was beautiful in the way that only honest work is beautiful. She recognized the hand immediately—the sure contour lines, the little annotations in the margins (*scree unstable after rain; do not trust the cairns*), the habit of drawing the river as a living thing rather than a blue squiggle. She had learned to draw maps from this hand. She had inherited this hand.

It was a survey of the river Vell, from its mouth at Greyharbor to—

To a place past the last marked point. Past the gorge country, past the high camps, past everything the Company's own charts admitted existed. The river narrowed on the paper to a thread, and the thread ended at a symbol her father had drawn with unusual care: a circle, and inside the circle, the Meridian seal—the peak in the chain.

He had marked the source of the Vell with the Company's own sign. Not discovered it. *Claimed* it—or recorded someone else's claim. In the margin, in pencil, faint almost beyond reading, he had written a single line:

*They sealed the vault themselves. Let them live with what's inside.*

August knelt there in the dripping dark with her father's handwriting two inches from her face and felt the ground shift under six years of settled grief.

*Let them live with what's inside.*

What was inside? What could a man bury at the top of a river, behind a Company seal, that he'd want them to *live with*?

She unrolled more of the ledger pages with hands that had stopped shaking and started moving with purpose, the old expedition reflex waking up in her blood like a lamp turned on. Dates. Accounts. Payments. And then, near the bottom of the strongbox, wrapped separately in waxed cloth as if someone had wanted it findable, a slim volume bound in grey leather with the Meridian stamp tooled blind on the cover.

A private ledger. Not accounts of money—accounts of *people*. She read three entries and had to put it down and press her palms against the cold stone until the nausea passed.

*Objection raised re: toll at Marrowfen bridge. Settled.*
*Objection raised re: water diversion, upper Vell. Settled.*
*Objection raised re: Founding Accord terms. Settled.*

And beneath the last entry, in different ink, added later, a note in a clerk's careful hand: *Original Accord documents deposited at source vault per instruction. Access restricted to Executive level. Surveyor E.J. dismissed.*

*Dismissed.* Not lost in the gorge. Dismissed—from a survey he'd been running at the source of the Vell, the same season the reports said he drowned there.

August sat back on her heels. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above her, muffled through forty feet of rock, she could hear the village going about its astonished day, and it sounded very far away, like news from another country.

Here was what assembled itself in her mind, piece by piece, refusing to be unassembled:

Her father had surveyed the source. He had found something there—something the Company considered worth sealing in a vault at the top of the world. He had been dismissed for objecting to it. And then he had drowned, conveniently, in the same gorge, the same season, leaving a daughter who would spend the next decade believing the river took him.

And when that daughter grew up to be the finest guide on the Vell—when she started taking clients up routes the Company wanted quiet, asking questions about tolls and water rights with the same stubbornness her father had shown—her name had been struck from the rolls for a route failure that, now that she made herself look at it honestly, had never quite fit. The anchors had been sound. The weather window had been sound. The report that condemned her had been written by a man she'd never met, a Company assessor named Halloway, whose signature she remembered because it was so strangely elegant for such an ugly document.

She had spent six years carrying a weight that might not have been hers at all.

The anger came slowly, which surprised her. She'd expected fury—had perhaps been saving some up for years without knowing it—but what arrived first was colder and worse: suspicion. The precise, mountaineer's suspicion of a slope that looks fine and isn't. If they had done this to her father, and then to her—if the whole architecture of her disgrace had been drafted in some Company office the way these tolls had been drafted—then what else in her life was load-bearing lies? What else had she been carefully not climbing because someone else had posted the warning sign?

"Hello?" called a voice from the cave mouth, thin with distance. "Anyone down there? Tide's thinking about coming home!"

Maren's grandson, probably, sent to fetch the fools. August moved quickly then, wrapping the ledger and the map and the compass in her oilskin coat, bundling them against her chest. She hesitated over the rest—the shelves, the strongbox, the whole drowned archive of it—and decided, with a pragmatism she wasn't proud of, that it would keep. The sea had kept it forty years. It would keep it another day.

At the cave mouth she paused and looked back once. The lantern light reached just far enough to show the first shelf, the nearest row of sealed cylinders, patient as eggs in a rookery.

"I'll come back for you," she said, and immediately felt foolish, and immediately stopped feeling foolish, because she meant it.

* * *

The walk back across the exposed seabed was faster and less reverent. The water was already turning in the deep channel, a slow brown rotation like something rolling over in its sleep, and by the time she gained the shingle below the seawall the first fingers of the tide were licking at the wrecked dinghy she'd crossed an hour before.

Maren was still on the wall, waiting, which August suspected was not accidental.

"Well?" the old woman said.

"Books," August said. "Old ones. Company ones."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes—something old and careful. "And you brought them up here, did you, out in the open, in front of the whole village?"

August adjusted the bundle under her coat. "No."

"Good girl." Maren leaned close, pipe smoke and salt. "There were men on the morning boat. Grey coats, soft boots, city voices. Asking after the cave-treasure, offering coin for anything folks found on the flats. Corvin's telling them the tide gave up nothing but weed." She straightened, looking out at the returning sea. "Company men don't come to Greyharbor for weed, August Joubert. Whatever you've got under that coat, it's worth more than you think, and worth less than they think, and both those facts are going to cause trouble."

August's arms tightened around the bundle—around her father's compass, around the map with its sealed source, around the grey ledger full of settled objections. Trouble. Yes. But underneath the trouble, humming like a taut rope in wind, was something she hadn't felt in six years: a direction. Upstream. To the source. To whatever her father had wanted them to live with.

She just had to survive the month first. Corvin's debt came due in four weeks, and now there were grey coats sniffing the tideline, and she was standing on a vanishing beach holding the most dangerous papers on the coast.

The first wave slid up the shingle toward her boots, cool and insistent, like the sea tapping its foot.

"Come along," Maren said. "You can tell me the rest somewhere with a door."

August followed her up off the beach, the bundle pressed to her chest, the compass riding against her heart like a second pulse, pointing—not north, she was almost sure of it already—not north at all.
