## 3. A Debt with Teeth

The tide came back, as tides do, and with it the ordinary miseries of Greyharbor: fish guts on the cobbles, gulls arguing over nothing, and Corvin the harbormaster standing at the mouth of August's boathouse with the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech and refuses to be cheated of it.

"August Joubert," he said, "you owe me forty crowns."

"Thirty-eight," August said, without looking up from the hull she was caulking. "You forgave two when I fixed your skiff's sternpost, and I have witnesses. Well. A witness. The cat."

"The cat doesn't count."

"The cat saw everything. The cat is very reliable."

Corvin was a broad man built like a bollard, with a beard he trimmed to look respectable and hands that had never once been respectable in their lives. He had lent her the money in the winter when her boat-mending trade had slowed to nothing, and he had done it, she suspected, out of a kindness he would deny under oath. Which made the current performance all the more theatrical.

"Forty crowns," he repeated, "within the month. Or I take the boathouse, the tools, and that sad little skiff you sleep in when you think nobody knows you sleep in it."

"Everyone knows I sleep in it, Corvin. I snore loud enough to wake the herring."

"A month," he said, and jabbed a finger at her, and then, because he was Corvin and not truly a cruel man, he softened by perhaps a degree. "I don't want your boathouse, August. I want to be paid. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'd feel bad about it."

"How generous of you to warn me in advance."

He left the way he'd come, boots ringing on the slipway, and August sat back on her heels and looked at the caulked seam in front of her and did not see it at all.

* * *

The trouble with a secret is that it gets heavy. August had spent two days telling herself the cave was a curiosity—a dead man's papers, a drowned strongbox, nothing that concerned a woman whose entire strategy for survival consisted of being forgettable. She had put the ledgers in a tarred canvas bag and buried them under the floorboards beneath her cot, under three loose boards, under a false bottom she'd built the first week she arrived in Greyharbor, back when she still believed hiding things was a skill and not a symptom.

But she kept taking them out.

At night, with a candle stub burning low, she read. The ledgers were the Meridian Company's—she was sure of it now, though the name appeared nowhere, because men like that never write their own names where a rope might rot and drop the evidence into the sea. Columns of figures. Payments marked only by initials. And recurring, page after page, a notation in a careful hand: *Vell survey — ongoing*, with sums attached that would have kept Greyharbor fed for a decade.

And the compass. She took it out now, turning it in her fingers. Brass, old, dented at the crown. Engraved on the reverse, in a script she knew better than her own heartbeat: *A.J.* Her father's initials. Her father's compass, which had gone into the mountains with him nineteen years ago and never come out, until it came out of the sea.

Compasses don't survive avalanches. They don't survive rivers. They don't survive nineteen years and then wash up in a cave forty miles from where he died, unless somebody carried them there, deliberately, and left them where the tide would one day give them back.

She didn't like the conclusions that thought led to. She poured herself two fingers of the terrible plum spirit she kept for emergencies and drank one finger of it, which by her accounting left her one finger into an emergency.

The map she unrolled last, always last, because it was the part that made her chest go tight. The river Vell, drawn in a surveyor's hand—her father's hand, she was almost certain, the same confident loops on the *V*—traced from Greyharbor's estuary up through the gorges, past Marrowfen, past the old ford, up into country where the ink simply stopped and a seal was stamped instead. The Meridian Company's seal: the mountain with the crown above it, the crown with the mountain beneath it, drawn so the two were indistinguishable.

At the seal, one word: *Source*.

Nobody went to the source of the Vell. That wasn't law, exactly. It was something older than law and enforced more reliably. The Company owned everything above the snowline—owned the passes, the timber, the water rights, the very weather, if the tolls on rain-shelters were anything to go by—and the source of the Vell was the crown jewel of all that ownership. The river fed every valley from the highlands to the sea, and whoever held its source held a thousand throats, gently, without ever squeezing.

August rolled the map up, put it back under the floor, and lay staring at the underside of the cot above her.

*A month,* Corvin had said.

Forty crowns. She did the arithmetic she'd been doing for two days, the same arithmetic, arriving at the same sum: she could mend boats until her hands fell off and not raise forty crowns in a month. She could sell the map—no. She could not sell the map, because selling the map meant showing the map, and showing the map meant explaining the map, and explaining the map meant the whole rotten story came out, and then she wasn't forgettable anymore, she was a headline.

She was still doing the arithmetic when she fell asleep, and in the morning there were strangers in the village.

* * *

She saw them first from the slipway, and her hands went still on the rope she was splicing, because her body had learned long ago what her mind took longer to admit: grey coats, good boots, the particular way certain men stand as though the ground has been leased to them.

Two of them. Company grey, cut clean, no salt stains, which meant they'd come by the coach road and not the coast. They were talking to Corvin at the harbor office, and Corvin—Corvin, who had argued with tax assessors, with naval officers, with a whale once, briefly—was nodding along like a metronome.

August kept her head down and her hands moving and listened with the whole of her skin, the way you learn to when being noticed has ever once cost you something.

They were asking about the tide. That much she caught in fragments on the wind: *lowest in forty years, the old man says. Devil's Ledger, the locals call it. Anything come up? Anything washed out?*

Corvin said something she couldn't hear. One of the grey men laughed—a sound like a drawer closing—and then they were walking the slipway, unhurried, looking at boats, looking at people, looking at August for exactly as long as it takes to decide a person is furniture.

"Morning," said the taller one, pleasantly. He had a face like a well-kept ledger: everything in its column, nothing out of place. "Fine weather for work."

"It's Greyharbor," August said. "We get this weather eleven months of the year. The twelfth month we complain about it."

The shorter one smiled. He had small teeth and a habit of looking at her hands rather than her face, and she understood at once that he was the dangerous one, because the tall one was the talk and this one was the arithmetic. "You're the boat-mender," he said. "Joubert, isn't it? Corvin mentioned."

"Corvin mentions everyone. It's how he fills the day."

"Mm." Small-teeth crouched, examined the seam she was caulking with the air of a man appraising a horse's teeth. "Good work. Careful work. You were a climber once, I hear. Upcountry."

"Everyone was something once."

"True." He stood, and dusted his knees, though there was no dust. "We're looking for relics. Old Company things. The tide's been generous, they say. If anything... surfaced... we pay fair. Better than fair. And we're discreet." His eyes did a slow circuit of her boathouse, her cot-skiff, the floorboards she was standing on. "Discretion's worth a lot to the right people."

"I mend boats," August said. "If a boat surfaces, I'll send word."

The tall one laughed his drawer-closing laugh. They moved on, unhurried, and August watched them go and noted that they walked the length of the village, spoke to the innkeeper, spoke to the priest, spoke to three children who would repeat everything they were asked for the price of a coin.

They were not leaving until they found what they were looking for. And they were looking for exactly what she had under her floorboards.

* * *

She gave them until evening. She mended a net for Widow Marsh and undercharged her, because if this was to be her last ordinary day in Greyharbor she wanted it to contain at least one good act. She ate bread and herring on the slipway and watched the gulls and thought about her father, who had taught her that the mountain gives you exactly one honest conversation per day, and the rest is negotiation.

Then, at dusk, she went to the harbor office.

Corvin was alone, doing accounts by lamplight, and he looked up with the wariness of a man who has been nodding along to grey coats all day and would like it noted that he didn't enjoy it.

"Before you say anything," August said, "I know they were here."

"I didn't tell them anything."

"You told them everything. You just did it with your face."

Corvin set down his pen. For a moment the bluster went out of him and what was underneath was tired and old and, she thought, a little frightened. "They asked about you, August. By name. Not *the boat-mender*. *Joubert*." He rubbed his eyes. "I've been harbormaster twenty-six years. I know what Company grey means in a town this size. It means someone upstairs has developed an interest, and interests like that don't blow over. They land."

"What did you tell them?"

"That you're the best mender on this coast and a month behind on your debt." He almost smiled. "Which is true, and which makes you sound boring, and boring is the best armor there is."

It was, she had to admit, well done. She sat down across from him, and the lamplight did its honest work between them, and after a while she said, "If I paid you the forty crowns tomorrow. All of it. Would you ask where it came from?"

Corvin looked at her for a long time. "No," he said finally. "But I'd worry. And I'd want you to know I'd worry, because I'm an honest man in all the ways that don't cost anything." He leaned forward. "Whatever you're sitting on—and don't tell me, I don't want it in my head—those men will find it. They always do. The tide showed that cave to the whole village, August. Half of Greyharbor's been poking around in it since. You think you're the only one who found something?"

The floor of August's world tilted a degree. "Who else has been down?"

"The Marsh boy found a spoon. Old Tam's lot found a boot. And the innkeeper swears there's a strongbox wedged in the deep crack nobody can reach without proper rope." He shrugged. "So far it's spoons and boots. But those grey men aren't here for spoons."

No, August thought. They're here for a map. And a compass with initials on it. And they'll tear the village apart, board by board, and when they find nothing they'll start on the people, and the people will point at the woman with the climber's hands and the upcountry past, because people always do, and they won't even mean to be cruel about it.

She stood. "Corvin. The debt. Give me your month. I'll have your forty crowns and I'll have them honestly, and you'll never have to feel bad about anything."

"Where in the name of the salt sea are you going to get forty crowns in a month?"

August looked at the window, where the last light was going off the water, off toward the hills beyond the estuary, where the river Vell began its long climb into country she had sworn never to look at again.

"Upstream," she said.

* * *

She packed that night, and packing, she discovered, is a confession. Everything you set out on the cot is a sentence about who you intend to be. Rope: I intend to climb. The ice axe her father's friend had left with her years ago, wrapped in oilcloth: I intend to go up, not away. Dried fish, hard bread, the plum spirit's remaining finger, poured into a flask: I intend for this to be difficult.

The ledgers, the map, the compass. She hesitated over the compass a long time, holding it, feeling its small stubborn weight. It didn't point north. She'd noticed it the first night and told herself it was broken, the way you tell yourself a scar doesn't ache. It swung and settled and swung again, restless, as if it were looking for something the way she was.

*Vell survey — ongoing.*

"Ongoing," she said aloud to the empty boathouse, and it came out sounding like a decision.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been circling for three days, the sum beneath the sum: the map was worth more than forty crowns. It was worth more than her boathouse, more than her name, more, she suspected with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, than her life. The Company had sent men in grey to a fishing village at the edge of nowhere within days of the tide turning. That meant they'd known the cave existed. That meant they'd been waiting for it to open, the way you wait by a grave. And it meant that whatever was at the source of the Vell—whatever her father had surveyed, whatever the ledgers paid out for year after year—mattered enough that they would cross the country to keep it buried.

And if it mattered that much to them, then it was the only currency in the world that could buy back a blacklisted name. Or burn down everything they'd built on the buying and selling of names. Possibly both. Possibly the same thing, viewed from different altitudes.

She was not a brave woman. She wanted that on the record, at least in her own head. She was a woman who had learned to be small and had gotten reasonably good at it, and every instinct she owned was screaming at her to put the map back, take the mending work in Marsh Hollow for the season, pay Corvin in installments, and grow old being forgettable.

But her father's compass was pointing upstream. And the grey coats were already in the village, and a month was a luxury the tide had stopped offering.

She buried the ledgers deeper—no. She almost buried the ledgers deeper, and then stood with the floorboard in her hand and understood that hiding them was just leaving them for the grey coats to find while she was gone. They came with her. All of it came with her. If the Company wanted its secrets back, it could climb for them.

She blew out the candle, shouldered her pack, and let herself out into the dark, where the tide was coming in over the Devil's Ledger, closing the cave's mouth for another forty years.

The village slept. The gulls slept. Somewhere out on the coach road, she'd have wagered, the grey coats slept too, in clean beds, with their ledgers of a face and their small teeth, and in the morning they would start asking better questions.

August walked the estuary road alone in the blue dark, and the compass swung in her pocket, restless, certain, pointing the way she was already going.

She was half a mile out of Greyharbor, at the bend where the road left the sea, when she heard footsteps behind her. Light ones. Quick ones. The footsteps of someone who had been following her since the slipway and had finally run out of patience.

She turned, hand on her knife, and a small shape detached itself from a drystone wall and stood there in the starlight, arms crossed, chin up, wearing a stonemason's apron far too big for him and an expression she would come to know very well indeed.

"You're going upstream," the boy said. "I'm coming with you. And before you say no—I know what you buried under your floorboards."
