## 4. The Apprentice Who Wouldn't Stay

August had stolen things before. She wasn't proud of it, but she'd learned early that pride was a luxury item, like fresh fruit in Greyharbor or a reputation you could still look at without flinching. What she had *not* done before was steal from a stonemason's yard while the stonemason's apprentice sat ten feet away on an upturned bucket, eating an apple and watching her do it.

She froze halfway through coiling the climbing rope off its peg. The rope was good stuff — waxed hemp, Company-made, which she'd normally refuse to touch on principle but would absolutely touch tonight because principle didn't hold a knot at altitude. Fifty feet of it. Enough to get two people down a cliff face, if it came to that.

The boy chewed his apple. He was small for fourteen, maybe, all elbows and jaw, with stone dust worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like he'd been carved himself and never quite finished. His eyes were the problem. They weren't the eyes of someone about to shout for the watch. They were the eyes of someone doing arithmetic.

"Evening," August said, in her best I-am-perfectly-ordinary voice.

"Evening," said the boy. "That's forty feet of rope."

"Is it."

"Forty-two, if you count the frayed bit at the end, which you'd want to cut off anyway." He took another bite of apple. "Master Hollis counts every coil twice before bed. He'll notice by morning."

August set the rope down slowly, the way you might set down a sleeping animal. "Then I'll be gone by morning."

"Where to?"

"That's a strange question to ask a stranger robbing your master's yard."

"It's a strange yard to rob," the boy agreed. "Everyone knows the only thing worth taking from a mason's is rope and chisels, and nobody takes rope unless they're going somewhere high." He pointed the apple core at her, judicially. "And nobody goes somewhere high out of Greyharbor unless they're running from something or toward something, and you don't look like you're running. You look like you're counting."

August had been called many things in her life. *Counting* was new, and uncomfortably accurate. She was counting: one month until Corvin's debt came due, three days since the men in grey had started asking questions at the Salt Loom, one map hidden under the loose plank beneath her cot, pointing upstream to a place marked with a seal she'd hoped never to see again.

"Goodnight," she said, and picked the rope back up.

"Take me with you."

"No."

"I haven't told you what I know yet."

"You haven't told me your name yet, either, and I'm already tired of you."

"Tam," he said, as though this settled everything. "Tam Ferrow. My father owned the quarry at Bracken Fell, up past the second bend of the Vell. Owned it until the Meridian Company decided they wanted the fell for a survey road and razed the works. Didn't buy it. Didn't lease it. Just came one morning with writs nobody could read and burned the sheds, and my da stood in front of the kiln and wouldn't move, and after that there wasn't a da anymore, just a name on a compensation ledger that paid out eleven crowns." He swallowed. "I was nine. Master Hollis took me in because my mother had died the winter before and because he's soft, though he'd deny it under oath."

August stood very still. The harbor breathed behind them — water slapping pilings, the creak of the drying racks, somewhere a gull complaining about the state of the world.

"Sad story," she said finally. "Doesn't make you less of a liability."

"Liability," Tam repeated, tasting the word. "That's a guide word. My da used words like that when the Company assessors came. Big flat words to make small people feel like weather." He tossed the apple core into the scrap bin with a neat flick. "You're a guide."

"I mend boats."

"You mend boats the way I sweep floors. Badly, and while looking at the horizon." He hopped off the bucket. Up close he was even thinner than he'd looked, wrists like knotted cord, but he stood square, planted, the way masons stand — like the ground owed him rent. "Here's what I know. Three days ago, men in grey coats came off the packet boat and started asking at the taverns about treasure from the Devil's Ledger cave. Yesterday they asked at the chandlery whether anyone had bought maps or river charts. This morning they asked Master Hollis if any of his apprentices had seen strangers buying climbing gear." He paused, savoring it. "You bought pitch and a whetstone Tuesday. From me. I remember everyone who buys anything, it's how Master Hollis keeps the books straight, and I keep the books straight because I'm the only one who can read the numbers."

The tide of it washed over August cold. They were already asking. Not searching — *asking*, politely, door to door, which was worse, because politeness meant they had time, and time meant they thought she was already caught.

"What do you want?" she said.

"To come."

"No. What do you want *not* to tell the grey coats that a thin boy saw a woman stealing rope?"

Tam considered this with the gravity of a man negotiating a bridge toll. "Same thing," he said. "To come. But now you owe me, which is different, because owing means you have to think about me, and thinking about me means eventually you'll realize I'm useful."

"I'm realizing no such thing."

"I can read. Can you read a survey map? Properly? With contour lines and datum marks?" He must have seen something flicker across her face, because he nodded once, satisfied, like a judge closing a case. "My da taught me. Said a mason who can't read the land is just breaking rocks. I've been reading the Vell since I was six. I know every ford between here and Marrowfen, and which ones the Company taxes and which ones they pretend not to tax and then tax anyway."

"Go home, Tam."

"I live above the workshop with four other apprentices and a cat that hates me. That's not home, that's storage." For the first time, something moved behind the arithmetic in his face — something younger and rawer, quickly shoved back down. "They burned my da's quarry. You want to go upriver where they buried whatever it is they buried. We're both walking the same direction. Only difference is you've got rope and I've got nothing, and you think that makes us different kinds of people." He shrugged. "It doesn't. It just makes you better equipped."

* * *

Tam walked home along the quay with the rope hidden under his coat, which had been the whole point of the evening's negotiation, though neither of them had said so out loud. That was the part he liked best about the deal — the part nobody said. She hadn't admitted she was going anywhere. He hadn't admitted he'd sell her out otherwise. But the rope was under his coat, and tomorrow at first light he'd bring it to the old salt shed past the shipwrights' yard, and if she wasn't there, well. Then he'd have a rope and a decision.

He'd been planning to follow her anyway, if he was honest, which he tried to be at least once a day because his da had said liars' hands shook on the chisel line. Ever since the grey coats started asking questions, Tam had been doing sums at night on his slate instead of sleeping. The sums went like this: the Company burned Bracken Fell. The Company was asking about the cave. Therefore the cave held something the Company feared, and things the Company feared were worth more than eleven crowns, which was the official price of his entire childhood.

He climbed the outside stair to the loft, stepping over the third stair that groaned like a dying man, and lay down in his narrow bunk between Pip, who snored like a bellows, and Osric, who slept with his mouth open and his fists closed around nothing.

The cat — a gray brute named Duchess who hated everyone equally, which Tam privately respected — landed on his chest and stared at him.

"I'm going upriver," Tam whispered.

Duchess blinked slowly.

"There's a woman. She mends boats but she's really a mountain guide. Her hands are wrong for boat-mending, too clean on the ropes, too quick on knots. And she looks at the Vell the way Da used to look at the fell — like it owed her an answer."

Duchess yawned, displaying an impressive array of knives.

"I know," Tam said. "Probably she's trouble. Probably she'll leave me at the first ford. But Da also said the mountain doesn't care whose son you are, it only cares if you pay attention. And I pay attention." He scratched the cat under the chin, an armistice older than either of them. "Eleven crowns, Duchess. Eleven. For a whole quarry and a whole da. Somebody upriver knows why the number was so small. I'm going to go ask them."

Duchess purred, which Tam chose to interpret as blessing rather than digestion.

* * *

The salt shed smelled of brine and old tar, and August was there before dawn, because she'd realized around midnight that she'd been outmaneuvered by a fourteen-year-old and needed to reassert some dignity before sunrise. She'd brought the rest of her gear: the ice axe wrapped in sacking, the brass compass, the map. She'd spent half the night with the map spread on her floor, tracing the Vell's blue thread upstream through gorge country, past Marrowfen, past the falls, into the white blankness at the top where the ink stopped and the seal began.

Her father's compass sat heavy in her palm. *A.J.* — Aldous Joubert — engraved in a hand she knew better than her own. It had been in the cave, in a dry box with the ledgers, and she still couldn't make the arithmetic work. Her father had been dead eleven years, lost on a Company contract above Whitefall, and his compass had been sitting in a sea cave full of Meridian records like a signature on a confession. Someone had put it there. Someone who knew him. Someone who wanted it found — or wanted *her* to find it, which was a different thing entirely and much more frightening.

The shed door scraped. Tam slipped in, hair plastered flat, coat bulging with more than rope. He upended it onto a flour barrel: the rope, a canvas satchel, a paper of dried fish, a flint, a coil of waxed twine, and — August stared — a small cold chisel, wrapped in oilcloth with the tenderness usually reserved for infants.

"You robbed your own master," she said.

"I borrowed. There's a difference and I'll be writing him an apology when I'm rich." He began repacking the satchel with brisk competence, heavy things low, light things high. "Also I left money in the till. Mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"He shorted my wages last quarter. We're square within a margin of error." He looked up, and in the gray light through the slats his face was suddenly very young and very stubborn at once, which August suspected was simply what his face was. "You could still say no."

"I could."

"But you won't, because you need someone who can read the river, and because if you leave me here I'll follow anyway, and you know I will, because you watched me sit on that bucket for twenty minutes without moving and you know what kind of patience that takes." He shouldered the satchel. It nearly toppled him; he corrected with a practiced stagger and pretended it hadn't happened. "So say yes properly, so it counts."

August looked at him for a long moment. She thought about her father teaching her knots on a kitchen table, her small fingers fumbling, his voice saying *again, and slower this time, the mountain doesn't hurry*. She thought about eleven crowns. She thought about the grey coats working their way door to door through Greyharbor like a slow tide, polite and patient, and about how a boy who kept books straight was exactly the kind of person they'd believe.

"One rule," she said. "When I say drop, you drop. When I say run, you run. When I say climb, you climb until your fingers bleed and then you climb more. No arguments, no clever remarks, no reading the map behind my back."

"Two rules back," Tam said instantly. "You teach me the high-country craft. Real rope work, real reading of ice and stone. If we're walking the same road, I come out of it knowing more than I went in with."

"That's not a rule, that's tuition."

"It's a rule," he said, "because rules are things you can't break, and you can't break a promise to teach someone their own mountains back."

The shed was quiet except for the harbor breathing outside. August held out her hand, and he took it, and his grip was stone-dust and certainty, far stronger than it had any right to be.

"Yes properly," she said. "Now listen. First lesson. We leave by the ebb tide, we walk the north bank until the old ferry stakes, and we do not — under any circumstances — let anyone see that rope. If the grey coats find us before the ford, you're my nephew and we're traveling to your aunt's funeral in Marrowfen, and you will look appropriately miserable."

"I can do that," Tam said solemnly. "I've had practice."

And then, because he was fourteen and couldn't help it: "What's actually at the source of the Vell?"

August picked up her pack and pushed open the shed door onto the pale beginning of the day, the river glinting far off like a drawn blade.

"Proof," she said. "Of something they killed people to bury."

Tam followed her out, and if his step quickened at that, she pretended not to notice — just as, three days later at the old ferry crossing, she would pretend not to notice the four men in grey coats waiting at the ford, rifles slung easy, watching the north bank path with the patience of people being paid by the hour.
