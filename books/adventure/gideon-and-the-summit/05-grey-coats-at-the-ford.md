## 5. Grey Coats at the Ford

The Vell ran shallow and quick-tempered at the ford, the way rivers do when they've spent a hundred miles being polite and have finally decided to stop. August crouched at its edge and studied the crossing the way she used to study faces in a card game—looking for the lie underneath.

"Looks easy," Tam said.

"That's what it wants you to think."

"It's a river."

"Rivers are the best liars I know." She picked up a stone and lobbed it into the middle of the ford. It vanished with a *plunk* far deeper than the water's surface suggested. "See that? The gravel bar shifts every spring. There's a channel cut through the middle of this crossing that'll take a grown man up to his chin and drag him half a mile downstream before he remembers how to be embarrassed about it."

Tam squinted at the water with new respect, then new suspicion, then—the expression she was learning to dread—the particular brightness of a boy who had just thought of something clever.

"So we go where you throw stones and it's shallow?"

"We go where I throw stones and it's *less deep*. There's a difference, and the difference is usually your dignity." She stood, knees cracking in a way she chose to interpret as weather rather than age. "We cross single file. You hold the rope. If the water takes you, let it spin you feet-first and don't fight it. Fighting is how people drown. Surrendering gracefully is how they live to complain about it."

"You're very cheerful for someone describing drowning."

"I'm from Greyharbor. Cheerful is a professional deformity."

They'd made good time since leaving the village two days past—better than August had expected, given that her expedition consisted of herself, a stolen coil of rope, a fourteen-year-old who ate like a dock crew, and a compass that hummed against her ribs like a guilty conscience. The map stayed rolled in oilcloth inside her shirt, next to her skin, where she could feel it with every breath. Some part of her had convinced itself that as long as she could feel the paper, no one could take it.

She should have known better. Things worth taking always get taken by people who know exactly what they're doing.

Tam went first, because August reasoned that if the river did eat him, she'd rather witness it than hear about it. He crossed hunched and furious, water shoving at his thighs, and came up the far bank soaked to the sternum and grinning like he'd personally defeated it.

"Ha! It tried nothing!"

"It was saving its effort," August said, and started across behind him.

She was mid-channel, the cold gripping her legs through her trousers, when she heard the horses.

Not horses, exactly. Hooves on stone, which is a different sound—harder, more deliberate, the sound of animals being told where to go rather than choosing. August froze in the current, water pulling at her, and looked upstream to the tree line where the trail bent around a granite shoulder.

Three riders. Grey coats, grey hats, grey expressions. Company men moved the way tax collectors move: unhurried, certain, already counting.

"*Get down*," August hissed, but Tam was already visible on the bank, standing in plain sight like an idiot, waving.

"No, it's fine—they might be surveyors!"

"They are not surveyors. Surveyors don't ride matched horses. Nobody rides matched horses except people whose boots cost more than our houses." She waded for the bank, no longer caring about the deep channel, caring only about the rope and the boy and the map against her skin. "Tam. Listen to me. Whatever happens, you don't know me. You're a stonemason's apprentice walking to Marrowfen for lime. You've never seen my face in your life."

"But I have—"

"*Never seen my face.*"

The riders reached the near bank and reined in. The one in front was broad through the shoulders with the kind of neat, expensive beard that suggested a man who owned combs he'd never admit to. He looked at Tam, then at August hauling herself dripping onto the stones, and smiled the way a ledger smiles.

"Afternoon," he said pleasantly. "You'd be the boat-mender."

August said nothing. Her heart was performing some kind of drum solo, but her face, thanks to years of practice, was a locked door.

"The harbor-master speaks well of you," the man continued. "Well—of your debts, anyway. Corvin's very thorough. We bought his paper last month, along with most of Greyharbor's. Funny thing, paper. It travels." He dismounted, boots landing without a splash, and strolled toward her with his hands loose at his sides. "Name's Brack. I'm looking for something that came out of the Devil's Ledger. A map, we're told. Old. Company property, technically—everything old is, eventually. Hand it over and you walk home free. Debts cleared. Maybe even a little gratitude, and gratitude from the Meridian Company spends better than coin."

"And if I say I don't have it?"

Brack sighed, genuinely regretful, the way a man sighs before closing a door on a cat. "Then we search you, and we take it anyway, and you spend the rest of your short acquaintance with us discovering how many interesting things can happen to a blacklisted woman that nobody will ever investigate. No offense meant. It's just arithmetic."

Behind him, the second rider swung down and began walking toward Tam, who had gone very still in the manner of small animals deciding whether to bolt.

And that was when the third voice arrived—from the wrong direction entirely, from downstream, low and dry as gravel:

"Gentlemen. You're standing on my river."

* * *

August knew the voice before she turned around. That was the terrible thing. Ten years, and her body still recognized it before her mind could object—a voice that had once talked her off a cornice in a whiteout by describing, in exhaustive detail, the stew waiting for them at base camp.

Ede Vasq came up the bank with a ranger's long rifle held easy across his chest and a retired man's belly straining his coat buttons, and he looked, August thought wildly, *older*, grey at the temples, lines carved deep around eyes that had once been the merriest thing on any mountain. He stopped ten paces from Brack and did not smile.

"Vasq," Brack said, and something flickered behind his pleasantness. Recognition, and not the comfortable kind. "I heard you'd retired."

"You heard right. This is me, retired." Ede's thumb shifted on the rifle stock, a small motion, conversational. "Retired men get particular about their rivers. Now, here's what's going to happen. You're going to mount up, ride back down the valley, and report that the ford was empty. And you're going to do it because there are three of you and one of me, and because you know exactly what I was, and because the math embarrasses you."

Brack's smile thinned. "One rifle, Vasq."

"One rifle and forty yards of open ground between us, and I've been shooting this river since before you could grow that beard. Also—" Ede tilted his head toward the trees downstream, where nothing whatsoever was hiding, "—my friends are getting tired of waiting."

It was a bluff so old it predated the Company. It worked anyway. Brack weighed it, glanced at August—at the map-shaped bulge she realized too late she was guarding with her whole arm—and made the calculation of a man paid to survive.

"This isn't finished," he said.

"It never is," Ede agreed. "That's what makes it a river."

They mounted and rode out at a trot that wanted very badly to be a gallop. August watched them until the bend took them, and then she turned around slowly, water still streaming off her hem, and looked at Ede Vasq for the first time in ten years.

He opened his mouth—to greet her, to explain, to do whatever men like him did—and she hit him.

Not hard. She was tired, wet, and honest enough to admit her heart wasn't in it. But her fist caught his jaw and his head rocked back and the rifle clattered on the stones and Tam made a sound like a kettle.

"Ten years," August said. "Ten years, and the first time I see you again, it's because I'm about to be robbed by men in matching coats."

"August—"

"You testified." The words came out of her like splinters coming out of a wound, slow and jagged. "I lost everything. My license, my name, my father's routes—I couldn't guide a *goat* up a *hill* after that hearing, and you sat there in your ranger's uniform and told the tribunal you'd watched me falsify a client's experience rating. You watched me do it. You *swore* you watched me do it."

"I know what I swore."

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and now her voice shook, which she hated. "Because I've had ten years to imagine every version of why. Money? Was it money? Or did you just decide I was guilty and wanted to be tidy about it?"

Ede picked up his rifle, slowly, keeping his hands where she could see them, and when he straightened his eyes were wet and furious at himself, which was somehow worse than any defense would have been.

"Coercion," he said. "There. It's a small word for such a big cowardice, but there it is. They had my sister's debts, August. All of them, every note she'd signed, called in on the same morning. The Company bought her paper the way they bought Corvin's—except nobody offered to clear mine. They offered to *keep* it. Keep it clean, keep her house, keep her children fed. All I had to do was stand up in a hearing room and say six sentences about you." His jaw worked. "Six sentences. I told myself they were almost true. You'd cut corners on that climb. Everyone knew it. I just—moved the corner-cutting from a place where it didn't matter to a place where it ruined you."

"Almost true." August laughed, and it came out ugly. "My father taught us both. He taught *you*. You carried his rope."

"I know whose rope I carried." He met her eyes and didn't flinch, and she hated him for that too, hated how steadily he took it. "I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you the shape of it so you know I'm not lying to you twice. Halloway wrote my testimony himself. I read it aloud. Then I resigned my commission, because I couldn't wear the uniform after, and I've spent ten years walking ridelines alone, telling myself penance was the same thing as payment." He gestured upstream, toward the high country, toward the source marked with the seal. "Then word comes down the valley that the Devil's Ledger opened, and a Joubert walked into it, and suddenly the Company's fixers are riding like the sky's falling. So I stopped walking away and started walking toward. That's all this is. Not redemption. Arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," August repeated flatly.

"That's what I said."

"She doesn't like arithmetic," Tam offered from the bank, wringing out his shirt. "She says it's what liars call math."

"Your apprentice is wise," Ede said gravely.

"He's not my apprentice. He's a hostage situation with opinions."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the ford, under a leaning granite slab that held the day's heat like a stove. August sat apart, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, while Ede built a fire with the competence of a man who had done nothing else for a decade, and Tam interrogated him about the mountains with the shamelessness of youth.

"Did you really talk her off a cornice in a whiteout?"

"Who told you that?"

"She mutters in her sleep. Mostly about ropes. Once about a man named Vasq and a lot of soup."

"There was a stew," Ede admitted. "Mutton. I described it for four hours. It may have been the finest piece of route-finding in highlands history and it has never once appeared in an official account."

August snorted despite herself, and hated herself for the snort, and kept sharpening.

Later, when Tam slept, Ede came and sat an arm's length away—not closer, she noticed; he'd measured that distance deliberately, the way you measure distance to a cliff edge—and stared into the fire.

"You should send me away," he said. "I mean it. I'm a liability. They know my face, they know my habits, and if they catch me with you, everything gets worse. And worse than that—" he turned to look at her, firelight catching the lines of his face, "—you can't trust me. Not fully. Not ever again, maybe. A person should climb with people they can trust."

"A person should," August agreed. "Now name me another option. Who else is going to walk into the high country with a blacklisted guide and a fugitive child? Sefa's twenty years gone. Half the rangers who remember my father won't spit on my name. And you—" she pointed the knife at him, not threateningly, more the way you'd point at weather, "—you know the upper Vell better than anyone alive. You surveyed it. Before."

"Before," he echoed, and something moved behind his eyes.

"What aren't you saying?"

Ede was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he'd used on the cornice, calm and level, the voice of a man reading terrain.

"When I surveyed the source country, there were things in the records that didn't add up. Elevations that had been corrected. Dates that had been moved. I flagged them, and my flags came back erased, and my supervisor told me the Founding Accord settled all questions about that ground and I'd do well to let it rest." He looked at her. "I let it rest, August. That was the second time I let it rest. There's a pattern, if you look."

August turned the knife over in her hands. Above them the stars came out hard and cold, the way they did over high country, and somewhere upstream the Vell talked to itself in the dark, running from the mountain toward the sea without knowing what it carried.

"If you come with us," she said at last, "you carry rope, you cook, and you tell me everything the moment you think of it. Every flag, every erased record, every memory that smells wrong. I am done being surprised by people I trusted."

"Fair terms."

"And if you betray me again, Ede Vasq, I won't hit you. I'll leave you somewhere educational."

A ghost of the old grin surfaced, battered but recognizable. "Somewhere educational."

"Highly."

He nodded, and lay back against his pack, and within minutes was breathing the even breaths of a man who had finally set down something heavy. August stayed awake longer, watching the fire, feeling the map against her ribs and the compass beside it, both humming their quiet tune.

*Two of us now,* she thought. *A disgraced guide, a traitor with a rifle, and a child who eats like a dock crew.*

It was, she had to admit, the largest expedition she'd commanded in ten years.

Above the tree line, faint as a scratch on glass, she thought she saw a light moving along the ridge—a lantern, or a signal mirror catching starlight, tracking the river's course the way they were. Watching. Waiting.

Following them up.
