## 6. Upstream Rules

Ede Vasq had spent thirty years learning the Vell, and in all that time it had never once forgiven him for anything. That was what he liked about it. A river kept honest accounts. You gave it respect or it took something from you—your footing, your gear, your pride—and it never pretended to be your friend afterward. He had known too many people who pretended.

He was thinking about this as he watched Tam try to cross a slick shelf of granite above a churning pool, arms windmilling like a scarecrow in a gale.

"Feet," Ede called. "Stop looking at the water. The water doesn't care about you. Look at your feet."

"I *am* looking at my feet!"

"You're looking at the water through your feet. There's a difference."

Tam made it across on the fourth attempt, red-faced and furious at the rock, which was in Ede's opinion the correct way to feel about granite. Behind them, August said nothing at all, which was its own kind of weather. She had been saying nothing at all for two days now, since the ford, since she'd stood over him with her hand still shaking around a climbing axe and asked him one question: *"Why are you here, Ede?"*

He'd given her an answer. It had been true as far as it went. But he could feel her testing it the way you test river ice—one careful step, then wait, then another.

*Good*, he thought. *Keep testing it.* He hadn't earned anything better.

* * *

The gorge narrowed by afternoon into a corridor of grey stone where the Vell ran fast and green and loud enough to swallow conversation. Ede had walked this stretch twice in his ranger years, both times on Company business, both times with his eyes fixed carefully on the trail ahead of him so he wouldn't have to look at what the Company was doing to the banks. Now he looked. The old survey stakes were still there, brass-capped, marching upriver every hundred paces like a row of teeth. Somebody had paid good money to measure every inch of this water, and nobody had paid anything at all to ask the villages downstream whether they minded.

"Ranger," Tam said—he'd taken up calling him that instead of Ede, which stung in a way the boy probably intended—"how do you know if a current's lying?"

"It never lies," Ede said. "It just doesn't tell you everything. See that smooth water past the boulder?" He pointed. "Looks restful. That's where the river drops four feet. Smooth water over a drop is called a tongue, and it'll hand you straight down into the hole below. Ugly water"—he nodded at a stretch of white chaos—"that's honest. All that foam means rocks near the surface. You can read it."

"So the scary-looking part is safe?"

"The scary-looking part is *knowable*. Different thing." He crouched and drew in the gravel with a stick: the boulder, the tongue, the hole, the eddy behind the rock where the current folded back on itself. "Rule one of moving water. If you fall in, don't fight toward the bank. Float on your back, feet downstream, and ferry yourself sideways into an eddy. The river will carry you somewhere calm if you let it aim you."

"That sounds made up."

"It sounds made up until you need it." Ede brushed the gravel drawing flat. "Everything true about rivers sounds made up until you need it."

August had stopped ten paces back, ostensibly checking her bootlace, actually listening. He knew her listening posture from twenty years ago—the particular stillness of her shoulders. Some things a body didn't forget, even when the mind had every reason to.

She finished with the lace, caught him watching, and gave him a look that said, plainly: *I see you being useful. I haven't decided what that means.*

Fair enough, Ede thought. Fair enough.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar beneath an overhang, out of the wind that funneled down the gorge after dark. Ede cooked—barley and dried fish and, when Tam wasn't looking, a handful of wild sorrel he'd gathered, because growing boys needed greens and would not eat them if told they were eating them.

"Teach me the knots again," Tam demanded, mouth full. "The ones from this morning. I've forgotten two."

"Forgetting is how you learn. Show me the ones you remember and we'll find the holes."

The boy remembered five of seven. His bowline was sloppy but his clove hitch was beautiful, tied with the quick economy of someone who'd spent years lashing scaffolding. Stonemason's hands, Ede thought. The Company had burned the quarry and scattered the trade, and here was the trade anyway, living in a fourteen-year-old's fingers like a coal under ash.

August sat apart, the cave map open on her knees even though there was no light left to read it by. She was memorizing it. Ede understood why. Paper could be taken; a map inside the skull stayed put unless somebody took the skull, and August Joubert had been making herself expensive to kill for a long time.

"Ede." Her voice came across the fire, level. "The ford. When you shot the rope out of the fixers' hands—you didn't hesitate. Where did a retired ranger learn to shoot like that?"

"Retired," he said, "is a generous word for what I am. Rangers keep their warrants. We just stop getting paid. And I taught the Company's own marksmen for six years. They were worse students than Tam."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

The fire popped. Tam had gone very still, the way boys did when they sensed an adult storm building and wanted no part of the lightning.

"You want to know if my remorse is real," August said. "So let's do it properly. Tell me about the testimony. Not the version you gave at the ford. The whole shape of it."

Ede put down his spoon. He had rehearsed this speech for eleven years, in ranger barracks and rented rooms and once, memorably, halfway up a cliff face with nothing else to occupy his hands. Every rehearsal had come out polished. Polished was useless. Polished was exactly what she'd smell as a lie.

So he said it unpolished.

"They came to me three days after the Kestrel Ridge collapse. Two men in grey and a magistrate's clerk who wouldn't meet my eye. They had my service record open on the table. They had my sister's address written underneath it." He kept his voice flat, recitation-flat, because if he let any feeling into it he'd have to stop. "My sister lived in Marrowfen with her two girls. Company town. Company housing. Company school. One word from the right man and her lease evaporates, and there's nowhere in four valleys a blacklisted woman's sister can rent. They didn't threaten me. Threats you can report, refuse, survive. They just showed me the paper and waited."

"And you testified I cut the anchor line."

"You testified—" Tam started, and August's hand moved slightly, and the boy subsided.

"I testified that you cut it," Ede said. "That I saw you do it. I described a knife I'd never seen, a moment I'd invented, and I did it in a clear voice because I'd been a witness in court before and I knew how witnesses sounded. Eleven years ago. Three climbers died on Kestrel Ridge because of a storm and a bad call that was mine, not yours—I was lead, I pushed the schedule—but the Company needed it to be you, because your father's name was already inconvenient and yours was attached to his. And I gave them what they needed." He looked into the fire. "I've tried to make the arithmetic work ever since. Two nieces safe against three strangers dead against one guide ruined. It doesn't work. It was never going to work. That's the thing about debts with interest like this—they compound faster than you can pay."

Silence, except the river.

"You could have told me," August said finally. "Eleven years, Ede. One letter."

"A letter would have gotten us both killed and changed nothing. Your name was already mud. Mine would have joined it, and your father's memory would have had two disgraced children instead of one." He shook his head. "I know how that sounds. Like I'm still doing arithmetic. Maybe I am. Maybe that's all I know how to do anymore. But I'll tell you what I told myself at the ford, when I saw those grey coats closing on you." He met her eyes across the fire. "I can't give you back eleven years. I can't give those three climbers back their lives. What I can do is walk upstream with you and stand between you and whatever the Company sends next, and teach this boy to read water while I'm at it. It's not payment. It's not even close to payment. But guilt is heavy, August, and I'd rather carry it uphill than sit under it."

Tam, softly: "That's the most words I've heard him say since I met him."

"Hush," said August, but something in her face had shifted—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but the look of a woman recalculating. She rolled up the map. "Sleep. We move at first light."

Later, when the boy snored and the stars wheeled over the gorge rim, Ede lay awake listening to the Vell and thought: *she didn't forgive me, but she didn't send me away either.* From her, he'd take it. From her, he'd take anything she chose to give.

* * *

Two days further up, the gorge opened into a drowned valley—a side channel where some old slide had dammed the river and made a brown lake among the pines, dead trees standing waist-deep in the floodwater like a congregation mid-prayer. And sticking out of the water on the far shore: tents. Or what was left of them.

"Company canvas," Ede said, before he'd consciously decided to say anything. "Survey pattern. Blue stripe on the fly."

"Recent?" August asked.

"Old camp. New visitors." He pointed with his chin. "See how the guy-lines are cut, not untied? Nobody packs out by cutting lines. Somebody went through that camp in a hurry, and somebody before them left in a bigger hurry."

They found a way around the shoreline and reached the camp by late afternoon. It had been a proper survey station once—four tents, a stone hearth, a drying rack for instruments, a cache platform built high on poles against animals. The flood had taken the lower half of everything. Mud lay over the site like a second blanket, and in the mud were boot prints. Dozens. Overlapping. Days old at most, the edges still sharp.

"Somebody searched this place," Tam said, crouching. "After the flood."

"After," Ede confirmed, reading the prints the way he'd once read sign for a living. "These went tent to tent, methodical. These—" he moved along the cache platform "—went straight for the strongbox. See the pry marks? Then straight out. Different men. The methodical ones were Company. The pry-mark ones wanted whatever the Company wanted, and got there first."

August had gone quiet in the way that Ede was learning meant danger rather than calm. She stood at the edge of the flooded surveyor's trench, staring down into brown water where the corner of something pale showed through the silt.

"There," she said.

It took them an hour to wade it out—a waterproof document case, Company-stamped, half-buried in the trench. Inside: rotted field notes, a rusted sextant, and a single sheet that the case's wax seal had somehow kept dry. August held it to the last light.

Ede watched her read it. Watched her face go through three expressions in the space of a breath—recognition, disbelief, and then something cold and bright that he'd only seen once before, on Kestrel Ridge, seconds before she did the bravest stupid thing he'd ever witnessed.

"What is it?" Tam asked.

"My father's handwriting," August said. "A requisition order. Dated six weeks before he died." She turned the page so the fading light caught it. "He ordered survey equipment for the source of the Vell. Officially, the Meridian Company has always claimed the source region was charted by accident, an afterthought." Her voice dropped. "This says otherwise. This says my father was sent there deliberately. And this stamp—" her finger touched a small inked mark in the corner, a mountain split by a line "—is the Founding Accord's seal. The same one from the cave ledger."

Ede felt the hair rise on his neck. "Then whoever searched this camp—"

"Knew he was here," August finished. "Knew what he left. And missed this." She folded the page into her coat, against her chest, with the map. "Which means they're not ahead of us. They're behind us, and they're thorough, and when they figure out what they missed—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Downstream, faint but unmistakable on the evening air, came a sound Ede had hoped never to hear again in his life: a whistle signal, three notes, repeated. Ranger code. *Contact made. Moving up.*

Company rangers, on the Vell, above the last marked point on any honest map.

"Tam," Ede said quietly, already reaching for his pack. "How are those knots coming along?"
