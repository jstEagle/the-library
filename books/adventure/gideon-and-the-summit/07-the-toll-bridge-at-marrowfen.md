## 7. The Toll Bridge at Marrowfen

The Vell widened as it came down out of the gorge country, spreading itself flat and brown and patient, as if it had all the time in the world to reach the sea and intended to take it. Marrowfen sat where the river bent around a shoulder of low hill, and from the ridge above it looked almost gentle—a scatter of slate roofs, a bridge with three arches, smoke rising straight up in the windless morning.

Almost gentle. Then August counted the flags.

Company grey flew from the tollhouse, from the granary, from the chapel—*the chapel*, she thought, that took a certain nerve—and from a tall new pole planted dead center in the market square, flying nothing yet, as if the Company were saving the space for something.

"They own the bridge," Ede said, crouched beside her in the gorse. "That's the whole town, really. The bridge and the road through it. Everything else just grew up around the toll."

"Then we pay the toll and walk through like anyone else."

"With your face?" Tam said. He was chewing on a stalk of something he'd pulled up and had no intention of eating. "No offense, but you've got a face people remember."

"People remember my *name*," August said. "The face is negotiable."

It was, at least, a plan of sorts, and plans of sorts were what they had. Ede produced three canvas satchels from his pack—surveyor's kit, genuine, left over from his ranger years, stamped with a department that had probably been dissolved and reabsorbed twice since. A theodolite in oiled cloth. Folding rules. A ledger of blank gridded paper. He'd carried the kit out of the service the way other men carried out grudges: quietly, and for years, without quite knowing why.

"Surveyors," he said. "Company surveyors, if anyone asks. There's a re-survey of the upper Vell going on—I heard it from a ferryman two days back. Nobody questions a survey crew. Nobody *wants* to question a survey crew. It's like being asked about weather."

"And me?" Tam said.

"You're the apprentice. You carry things and look bored."

"I can do that without practicing."

They went down through the gorse and the wet grass, and the town took them in the way towns do—without ceremony, without interest. Marrowfen on a market morning was onions and wet wool and a man arguing with a mule about the philosophy of bridges. August kept her hood up and her eyes down and bought nothing, and felt the particular loneliness of moving through a crowd you cannot join.

The bridge was worse than she'd expected. Not the structure—that was old and good, built by masons who'd known their stone, and Tam stopped dead in the middle of it to run his thumb along a joint and mutter, "*Look* at that, they didn't even need mortar, look at the—" before Ede hauled him along by the collar. It was the tollhouse. It was the man in the tollhouse, and the board beside him.

The board was a wanted notice. August had seen her own wanted notice before, in Greyharbor, years ago, when the blacklisting was fresh and the ink still had the smell of a decision being made. This was not that notice. This was new. The paper was bright, the printing crisp, and the bounty figure at the bottom had been struck through and reprinted higher—substantially higher, in a heavier font, the way you'd price a thing you'd decided was worth having.

*WANTED FOR THEFT, SEDITION, AND CONSPIRACY AGAINST THE LAWFUL INTERESTS OF THE MERIDIAN COMPANY. AUGUST JOUBERT. LAST SEEN HEADING UPRIVER. REWARD: 800 CROWNS.*

Eight hundred. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eight hundred crowns was a boat. Eight hundred crowns was a house with a roof that didn't sing in storms. Eight hundred crowns was Corvin's debt twice over and Greyharbor's whole winter besides. Somewhere in Marrowfen, probably within a hundred yards of her at this moment, there were a dozen people who'd look at that number and start doing the same sums.

"Keep walking," Ede murmured. "You're a surveyor. Surveyors look at *ground*, not at boards."

She kept walking. But something in her chest had gone cold and precise, the way it did before a bad pitch on bad rock. Eight hundred crowns meant the Company wasn't annoyed with her. It meant the Company was *invested*. It meant Halloway—or whoever sat behind Halloway's grey coat—had signed a paper authorizing that number, and men like that did not sign papers for a boat-mender who'd gone quietly mad in a sea cave.

They know about the cave, she thought. Or they know about the map. Either way, they know, and they're frightened, and frightened money prints big numbers.

The tollman took Ede's coin, glanced at the satchels, and waved them through with the magnificent indifference of a man paid by the crossing and not by the question. On the far side, Tam exhaled like a diver surfacing.

"Eight hundred," he said. "That's—"

"Don't," August said.

"I was going to say that's a lot of rope."

"I know what you were going to say. Don't."

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which August took to be either very old local humor or a warning. It stood back from the square with its sign hanging by one chain, and it was the only place in Marrowfen where three surveyors could sit in a corner with a pot of tea and a plate of bread and cheese and not be remarkable. Surveyors ate in inns. Surveyors complained about weather. It was practically regulation.

The innkeeper was a woman of about sixty, built like the keel of a boat, with forearms that suggested a life of hauling things that did not wish to be hauled. She took their order without writing it down, which August approved of, and looked at August's face for one second too long, which she did not.

Then she went back to the kitchen, and August let her hood down an inch, because the room was warm and her neck ached, and that was the mistake, or the honesty, or whatever it turned out to be.

The woman came back with the tea and set it down and said, quietly, without looking at her, "Your father took his tea the same way. Bitter enough to trot a horse across."

August's whole body went still.

The woman set down the bread. "Bren Joubert. Surveyor. Came through Marrowfen maybe fifteen years back, mapping the upper Vell for the Company before the Company decided it already knew everything worth knowing. He sat where your apprentice is sitting. Argued with me about the sign." She nodded at the Drowned Bell's chain. "Said a bell that's drowned can't warn anybody, and what kind of fool names a thing after its own failure."

"What did you tell him?" August managed.

"Told him the bell still rings underwater, if you're standing in the right place on the bridge at low water. Told him that's the point." The woman wiped the table with a cloth that had outlived several empires. "He liked that. Wrote it in his book. I saw him do it."

"You remember him," August said. It came out strange, half question, half accusation. "After all this time. With everything in the papers. With *my* face on the board in the square—"

The woman looked at her then, straight on, with the unhurried eyes of someone who had watched forty years of Marrowfen come and go and had stopped being impressed by any of it.

"Girl, I've fed Company men and crown men and smugglers and one bishop, and I'll tell you what I've learned. Papers tell you what a man's enemies paid to have said. Tea tells you what he actually drank." She refilled August's cup without being asked. "Your father overpaid. Every time. I'd tell him not to and he'd do it anyway, said the difference was for the sign-chain, which was rusty, which it was, because he's the only man in twenty years who *looked at it*."

She paused at the corner of the table.

"There's a checkpoint at the fen road, north end. New since spring. They're stopping everyone going upriver, asking questions about a woman with dark hair and a scar through one eyebrow." She looked pointedly at August's hood. "They're not very bright, the checkpoint men. But they're not alone anymore. There's a grey coat with them now. One of the quiet ones. Those are the ones who count coins in their heads."

"Thank you," August said. "I don't—"

"Save it." The woman was already turning. "You can thank me by not getting caught in my inn. I've a license to think about." She stopped, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He came back through once more, near the end. Looked tired. Looked like a man carrying something heavy up a hill he'd already climbed twice. I asked if he was all right and he said—" she frowned, retrieving it, "—he said, *'If I don't come back, my girl will. She's better on rock than I ever was.'*"

The kitchen door swung shut behind her.

August sat with her tea going cold and her hands not quite steady, and Tam and Ede had the good grace to study their bread as if it were a survey subject of unusual complexity.

* * *

They took a room at the back because it was that or the road, and the fen road after dark was checkpoint country. The room had two beds, a window over the woodpile, and a water stain on the ceiling shaped, Tam declared, exactly like the Company seal, which he spat at, and which Ede made him stop spitting at.

August sat by the window with the cave map spread on her knees in the last of the light, not reading it—she knew it by heart now—but holding it, the way you hold a hand. The river ran up the page in its careful old ink, and at the top, under the Meridian seal, the source waited.

"He knew," she said, to the room, to no one. "Fifteen years ago, sitting in that kitchen, arguing about a bell. He knew what he was carrying, and he knew they'd come for it, and he—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He told a stranger I'd come. He *planned* for me to come."

"Is that so bad?" Tam said. He was upside down on the bed, feet on the wall, the way fourteen-year-olds sit when no one is teaching them manners. "Sounds like he believed in you."

"It sounds like he handed me a debt," August said. "And I've had enough of debts."

"You could sell it," Ede said, from the other bed, where he lay with his boots off for the first time in days, staring at the ceiling stain. "Eight hundred crowns is what they think the map's worth. It's probably worth more. Walk into the tollhouse tomorrow, name a figure, retire to the coast."

"Ede."

"I'm saying it so we all know I said it. Somebody should. Tam's thinking it. You're thinking it. I'm just the one with the courage to be hated before supper."

"I'm not thinking it," Tam said, with the immediate loyalty of the young, and then, after a moment, "Well. I'm thinking about it the way you think about a cliff. You look. You don't go."

August almost smiled. "That's the healthiest thing anyone's said in this room."

She folded the map away and sat a while longer in the dark, listening to Marrowfen close up around them—shutters, dogs, the last cart over the bridge, the toll bell ringing its flat Company note. Somewhere north, a quiet man in grey was counting coins in his head. Somewhere above all of it, the source waited under its seal, holding her father's last heavy cargo.

The innkeeper had said the bell still rang underwater, if you stood in the right place at low water. August thought about the Devil's Ledger, and the tide going out, and everything the sea had uncovered that everyone had agreed to leave buried.

Low water comes for everything eventually, she thought. The only question is who's standing on the bridge when it does.

She was still thinking it when Tam, at the window, went rigid and said, in a whisper with no air in it:

"August. The grey coat from the checkpoint. He's crossing the square. And he's stopped under the notice board—and he's looking at our window."
