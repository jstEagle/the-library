## 8. Ledger of Small Crimes

The fire was Tam's job, and he took it seriously in the way he took everything seriously, which was to say he'd built it three times before it behaved.

The first attempt smoked like a wet chimney and made Ede cough theatrically from across the hollow. The second caught, flared, and died with a sound like a disappointed sigh. The third — dry birchbark, feathered sticks, the little pyramid Ede had shown him on the second day of walking — finally took hold and threw honest light against the overhang where they'd sheltered above a bend in the Vell.

"There," Ede said. "Now you're a fire-builder instead of a fire-arguer."

"I wasn't arguing."

"You were negotiating with it. Fire doesn't negotiate." Ede stretched his long legs toward the flames. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, retired-ranger soft, with grey coming into his beard and hands that looked like they'd been carved for holding tools and then used for holding nothing for too long. "It just agrees with whoever prepared properly."

Tam decided not to say that August had built her own fire without any negotiation whatsoever, because August was off at the edge of the hollow doing something private with the brass compass, turning it over and over, and Tam had learned in four days of traveling with her that there were rooms inside August Joubert you did not walk into uninvited.

So he sat by his good fire and unwrapped the oilcloth bundle from his pack, and that was how the evening became the strangest evening of his life, which was saying something, because four days ago he'd been chiseling a lintel in Greyharbor and now he was upstream of everywhere, wanted-adjacent, eating beans out of the same pot as a disgraced guide and a traitor ranger.

The ledger was bigger than he remembered from the cave. In the dark of the Devil's Ledger — which was what the villagers called the cave, and which Tam now understood was less a nickname than a warning — everything had been shapes. Shapes of books, shapes of boxes, shapes of paper gone the color of old teeth. Now, in firelight, with the water stain map spread flat on a rock and weighted at the corners with four stones he'd selected with great care, the thing had weight. Real weight. Not stone-weight. Paper could be heavier than stone if enough people had bled on it. He didn't know yet that this was true. He was about to find out.

"August," he called, before he could stop himself. "You should see this."

She came over slowly, the way she did everything, like a person who had learned that hurrying got you killed on scree. She crouched. Her eyes went to the cover first — stamped leather, cracked, but the seal still legible in the fire's light: a mountain split by a river, crowned with a star. The Meridian Company mark. Tam had seen it burned into toll gates and branded on crates his whole life, the way other children saw saints on church walls.

"Open it," she said.

"You open it."

"It's your find."

"It's *your* compass," Tam said, and immediately regretted it, because something crossed her face like weather. But she reached out anyway, and together — though he'd have denied it under torture — they opened the first page.

*Meridian Company. General Ledger, Volume the Ninth. Year of the Accord.*

The handwriting was beautiful. That was the first horror of it, though Tam didn't know it was a horror yet. Whoever had kept this book wrote a fine, patient, clerkly hand, every number dressed in its best clothes, columns ruled straight as fence posts. Entries about freight. Entries about wages — wages so low Tam read them twice, thinking he'd misread the column. Entries about *disbursements to valley factors*. Entries about timber, ore, wool, passage fees on the Vell itself, because the Company owned the river the way some men owned dogs, and charged it for running.

"Read it aloud," August said quietly. "Some things want air."

So Tam read. His voice cracked once on the first entry and then steadied, because he was a stonemason's apprentice and stonemasons' apprentices learn to keep their voice level while hammering, and this felt like hammering.

He read about the year of the Founding Accord. He read about the survey expedition sent upriver — nine names listed, each with a wage, each wage crossed out later in red ink and marked *forfeit*. He read about the source of the Vell, described in a surveyor's careful terms: *a glacial vault, stable, dry, suitable for secure storage.* Suitable for secure storage. As if the beating heart of all the water in the highlands were a warehouse.

Then he turned a page and stopped reading aloud, because the words went strange, and August leaned in, and even Ede — who had been whittling a stick with the serenity of a man who whittled to avoid talking — set down his knife.

*Disbursement. To Warden-Captain Halloway (the elder), for services rendered at the source settlement, per instruction of the Board. Forty men, provisioned. Itemized: powder, forty pounds. Ball, two hundred. Whisky, six casks.*

"Services rendered," Ede repeated. His voice had changed. It was flat, the way stone is flat right before it falls on you.

Tam kept reading, faster now, the firelight jumping. There was no word in the ledger for what happened at the source settlement. That was the second horror, and this one he understood immediately, because his master had taught him that the most important lines in any contract were the ones that weren't written. The ledger simply recorded that forty men had been provisioned, and then, eleven days later, it recorded a different kind of entry entirely:

*Disbursement. Burial details, sixty laborers, three days' hire.*

And below that, in the same beautiful hand:

*Disbursement. New survey required. Prior surveyor's records found unsound. Replacement engaged.*

"Unsound," August said. Just the one word. She said it the way you'd press a thumb into a bruise to see how deep it went.

Tam looked at her. Then he looked back at the page, and turned it, carefully, because the paper was so old it seemed embarrassed to be touched, and there — folded into the binding, thin as an onion skin — was a letter that had never been sent.

He knew it had never been sent because it was addressed to no one. It began *To the Board, and let this stand as my refusal,* and the handwriting was different from the clerk's — quicker, slanting, a hand that wrote the way water runs downhill, impatient with obstacles.

Tam knew that handwriting.

He'd seen it exactly once, scratched inside the lid of a tool chest that had come up for sale in Greyharbor after its owner drowned, and he'd copied it into his practice book because it was the finest hand he'd ever seen, and because the chest had belonged to a man named Gideon Joubert, and everyone in Greyharbor still lowered their voices slightly when they said that name, though nobody could quite say why anymore.

"That's him," Tam said. "That's your father's writing."

August didn't answer. She had gone very still, the particular stillness of a climber testing ice, and Tam understood that he was watching someone read their own father's last words by firelight in a gorge above a river the man had refused to lie about, and that no amount of mason's training would get his voice level for what came next, so he did the only decent thing, which was to read it for her, slowly, so she wouldn't have to ask.

*I have surveyed this valley honestly for eleven years. I will not certify records I know to be false. Whatever was done at the source settlement was done to living people — I counted them myself, two hundred and thirty-one souls, and I will not write them out of the mountain. If the Board requires a surveyor who can make the dead agree with the ledgers, they must find another man. I am returning my commission. My daughter will eat poorly this winter, but she will eat as the daughter of an honest man.*

There was more, but August took the page gently from Tam's fingers then, and read the rest herself, silently, her lips moving, and Tam looked away because some things are doors you don't watch people open.

Ede fed the fire. It was the most useful thing any of them could do, and he did it with a care that Tam recognized, suddenly, as guilt wearing work clothes.

* * *

They read until the moon crossed the overhang and the fire needed feeding again, and what they assembled, piece by piece, entry by entry, was this:

The Meridian Company had not found the highlands rich. The highlands had been rich forever — freeholds and quarry towns and shepherd villages, the whole spine of the range lived on and worked on by people who paid their taxes to nobody but winter. The Company had arrived with capital and contracts, and when contracts proved slower than powder, it had used powder. At the source of the Vell there had been a settlement of families who held their grazing rights by older law than any charter, and the Company had wanted the headwaters, and the Board had authorized "services rendered," and forty men had gone up with whisky and two hundred balls, and eleven days later sixty laborers had been hired for burial detail, and the Founding Accord — the sacred founding document, the one printed in schoolbooks with the mountain-and-star seal glowing at the top like a benediction — had been signed over the graves.

Two hundred and thirty-one souls. Tam kept coming back to the number. His father had taught him numbers the way other fathers taught prayers: a wall is only as true as its plumb line. Two hundred and thirty-one. He said it under his breath until it stopped sounding like a figure and started sounding like what it was, which was a village.

"They teach the Accord in school," he said. His voice came out wrong, too loud in the small hollow. "Master Pellum made us copy it out. *In concord with the mountain and all its waters.* We had to write it in our best hands or do it again."

"The best hands in the valley," Ede said grimly, "have been copying that document for sixty years."

"And the ledger says—" Tam flipped back, found the page, stabbed his finger at it. "It says right here. *Prior surveyor's records found unsound.* They didn't just kill them. They killed them and then corrected the paperwork."

"That's what a company is," August said.

Tam had never heard her say so many words at once. She was sitting with the refusal letter flattened on her knee, and the firelight did something complicated to her face, half bronze and half shadow, and she spoke quietly, almost conversationally, which was somehow worse than shouting.

"A company is a machine for making a crime look like weather. A flood happened. A hardship occurred. Regrettable losses were sustained. Nobody did anything. Things simply *were done*." She tapped the ledger. "Except somebody kept this book. Somebody ruled these columns straight and wrote in his best hand and filed it all away in a cave under the tide line, because even liars need somewhere to put the truth, or it rots the whole house."

"Your father," Tam said, and stopped, and started again. "Your father wouldn't sign it. And then they—"

"Blacklisted me?" August's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "No, lad. They blacklisted me twenty years later, for climbing routes I had every right to climb, on charges nobody would write down. I spent two decades thinking I'd failed some test I couldn't see." She looked at the ledger, at the red ink, at the crossed-out wages. "Turns out the test was written by the same office. You don't punish a man by ruining him. Ruin dies with him. You ruin his daughter. That teaches the next surveyor exactly what refusing costs."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the gorge the Vell ran loud and cold and constant, carrying snowmelt down past Marrowfen, past the fords, past Greyharbor, past every toll gate and branded crate, all of it rising from a vault where the Company had stored its sin like a man storing coins under a floorboard.

Tam discovered he was angry in a new way. He'd been angry before — when the quarry closed, when the Company men came with their papers and their polite voices and razed the kilns, when his mother coughed herself into the ground in a Company town breathing Company dust. But that anger had been the anger of a boy against weather. This was different. This was anger with a *paper trail*. This was anger you could hold.

"They buried it at the source," he said. "The original documents. The real Accord. That's why the map marks it. That's why the seal is on the map." He looked up at August. "They didn't hide the proof because it shames them. They hid it because it *owns* them. Every toll, every wage, every deed above the snowline — it all comes from that signature. If the real papers ever see daylight—"

"Then the mountain changes hands," Ede said.

All three of them were quiet. The fire settled. Tam thought about Master Pellum making the class copy the Accord in their best hands, and about his own practice book, and about the fact that he was fourteen years old and had just learned that the entire world above the treeline was built on a grave, and that the grave had a maintenance budget, itemized, in a beautiful hand.

"We should burn it," he said suddenly. "This ledger. Right now. So they can't—"

"No," August said, and it wasn't sharp, it was final, the way bedrock is final. "You don't burn the record of the dead, Tam. That's the whole trick they pulled — erasing people. We don't return the favor." She gathered the pages with both hands, careful as a mason setting a capstone. "We carry it up. All the way. And we put it where it belongs."

"Which is where?"

August looked up the gorge, into the dark where the river came from, and for a moment she looked so much like the handwriting in the letter — quick, slanting, impatient with obstacles — that Tam's throat hurt.

"At the source," she said. "Where the truth was buried, we'll dig it up. What happens after that—" she glanced at Ede, and something passed between them, old and damaged and unresolved, "—happens after."

* * *

Later, when Ede took first watch and August slept with the ledger bundled against her chest like a child, Tam lay awake looking at the stars through the gap in the overhang.

He thought about the compass. He hadn't told anyone yet, but he'd noticed it that afternoon, when August wasn't watching: the needle didn't settle north. It swung, hesitated, and pointed consistently upstream — up the Vell, toward the glacier, toward the vault, as if the old man had tuned it like an instrument before he died, so that even his tools would refuse to point anywhere but home.

His father's initials on the case. His father's handwriting in the binding. His father's route up the river. Tam was an apprentice; he knew an inheritance when he saw one, even when the inheritor didn't.

He rolled over. Across the coals, August slept with her jaw set even in sleep, and Tam thought: *they tried to erase her and they couldn't even erase her properly.* Two decades of blacklist, and here she lay, upstream of everywhere, carrying the whole crooked history of the mountains against her ribs like kindling.

Tomorrow they'd reach the drowned survey camp. Tomorrow the country would get steeper and the Company's reach would get longer. But tonight the fire was good, and the ledger was heavy, and Tam fell asleep angry, and it was the best anger he'd ever had, because for the first time in his life it had an address.

Upstream. Where the water came from. Where everything came from.
