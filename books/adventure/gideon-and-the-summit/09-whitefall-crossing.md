## 9. Whitefall Crossing

The storm came down the valley like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just wind and white.

August had been watching the sky all morning, the way her father taught her before she was old enough to hold an ice axe properly. *The mountain doesn't ambush you, Gussie,* he'd said, tightening her bootlaces with hands cracked from thirty years of rope work. *It posts its bills in advance. Most people just don't read them.* The bill today was written in lenticular clouds stacked over the eastern shoulder like plates waiting to be dropped, and August had been reading it since dawn.

"We camp," she said, when they stopped at midday to eat. "Here. Now."

Tam looked up from his hardtack with crumbs on his chin and the particular expression of a fourteen-year-old who has decided an adult is being dramatic. "We're not that far from the crossing."

"Which is exactly why we're camping." She nodded at the clouds. "That's a Whitefall building. By nightfall the whole gorge will be a wind tunnel with knives in it."

Ede said nothing, which from Ede was agreement. He was already unrolling the tent fly, moving with the economy of a man who had spent decades making himself small and useful in bad weather. But August caught him glancing at the sky too, and there was something in his face—not fear exactly. Recognition. Like a man reading a letter he'd hoped never to receive twice.

They pitched camp in the lee of a granite spine, and by three in the afternoon the storm arrived to prove her right, which gave her no pleasure whatsoever. It came as a wall of sound first, a freight-train roar up the gorge, and then the snow arrived sideways, and then the world simply ended at arm's length.

They spent the night taking turns holding the tent poles. Around midnight something above them—a cornice somewhere on the flanks of the ridge—let go with a boom like a cannon, and Tam's hand found August's sleeve in the dark and gripped it, and she let him, because there are moments when dignity is a luxury item.

"You've crossed Whitefall before," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"In summer," August said. "In summer it's a river you hop. In winter it's—" She considered how much truth a frightened boy needed. "It's a bridge. A bridge that argues back."

* * *

Morning came grey and grinding, the storm reduced to a sullen spitting wind, and they broke camp into a world remade. The gorge walls wore fresh shawls of powder. The air had that ringing, scrubbed quality that follows heavy snowfall at altitude, beautiful and treacherous in equal measure—the kind of day that kills people who mistake clarity for safety.

Whitefall itself appeared around a bend: a broad cascade frozen mid-plunge, a staircase of ice and packed snow dropping two hundred feet through a notch in the gorge wall. In summer it roared. Now it lay silent under a smooth white lid, arched over the hollow where the water still ran, and the arch was thin in places—you could see daylight through it like the membrane of an egg.

"That's not a bridge," Tam said flatly. "That's a rumor of a bridge."

"It's held for forty years," Ede said.

"So has my luck, and look how that's going."

August allowed herself half a smile despite everything. The boy's fear talked constantly; her father would have called it a good sign. *The quiet ones on the ice are the ones I worry about, Gussie. Fear that speaks gets managed. Fear that sulks takes people with it.*

She studied the crossing the way she'd once studied ledgers in another life—looking for the lie in the numbers. The upper third was thick, blue, trustworthy. The middle section sagged where meltwater had thinned it from beneath. And at the bottom, where the fall flattened out toward the pool, the snow lid spread wide over slower water—broad, inviting, and rotten to its bones, because slow water is patient water, and patience eats bridges from underneath.

"We go high and fast through the middle," she said. "Roped, twenty feet apart. Nobody stops. Nobody looks down. If it starts to go, you throw yourself flat and spread your weight, and you do not—I mean this, Tam, with my whole heart—you do not stand up to run."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would. Everyone does. That's why I'm saying it now, while you can still hear me over your own pulse."

Ede rigged the rope without being asked, his knots quick and old-fashioned, the kind her father used to tie. She watched his hands and hated how familiar they were. Twelve years ago those hands had belayed her up the north face of the Craghorn in a lightning storm, steady as bedrock, while she screamed at him to hurry. Four years ago, according to a transcript she'd read exactly once, those same hands had signed a statement that ended her career.

*Steady as bedrock,* she thought, clipping in. *And bedrock shifts.*

* * *

The middle of Whitefall was every bit as argumentative as promised.

They went in single file, Ede leading because he insisted and August allowing it because his weight was less than hers and if anyone was going to test the ice, better it break under the man who owed her. For fifty feet it went beautifully—boot-front kicks, two axes, the crisp rhythm of it, Tam behind her breathing in loud deliberate counts because she'd told him counting kept panic in a cage.

Then the bridge spoke.

It wasn't a crack so much as a sentence—a long, groaning syllable that traveled up through their boots, and August felt the entire arch flex beneath her like the flank of a living thing. Ahead, Ede froze. Behind her, she heard Tam's counting stop.

"Keep moving," she said quietly. "Slow is smooth. Smooth is—"

The ice under Tam opened like a book.

Later, she would piece it together in fragments: the whip-crack report of the fracture, the white lid tilting, Tam's axe skidding off glassy ice as the surface dropped away beneath him—and then the hole, black and roaring, and the boy sliding toward it feet-first with the terrible smoothness of a stone down a chute, and the rope between them snapping taut with a sound like a struck harp string.

He stopped with his boots over the void.

The rope had caught him across the chest, jammed against the lip of the broken lid, and he hung there half-in, half-out of the gap, arms windmilling, and below him the water made its hungry circular sound. His weight was dragging the fracture wider. She could see it propagating, hairline lines racing outward from the hole like frost climbing a window.

"TAM." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Flat. Spread yourself. NOW."

He threw himself forward, and it saved his life—his chest and hips came down onto solid ice, arms wide, only his legs still through the gap, boots kicking in open air above the current. But the motion drove the cracks further. A chunk the size of a door calved off beside him and vanished with barely a splash, swallowed whole.

And August's mind did something strange. It went quiet, and in the quiet her father's voice was very clear, the way it always was when it mattered most.

*When someone goes through the lid, Gussie, you don't go to them. You go past them. You anchor beyond the break, because the ice near the hole is already dead, and dead ice will take you both. Get above the fracture. Get your weight distributed. Then you bring them home along the strongest line.*

She was already moving. Not toward Tam—past him, up-slope, driving her axes in with short savage strokes until she reached the thick blue ice of the upper falls. There she kicked her heels in, sank to her knees, and drove both axe shafts deep, becoming her own deadman anchor. The rope ran from her harness, past Ede—who had done the same thing ten feet to her left, she realized, without a word passing between them, the old choreography rising out of twelve years of silence like water finding an old channel—and down to Tam.

"On three we haul," she said. "Short pulls. He walks his arms forward between them. Tam—when we pull, you kick your legs and claw. Don't try to stand. Come to us like a seal."

"A what?"

"Like a seal, Tam."

"I don't know what seals DO—"

"ONE."

They hauled. He clawed. The ice shrieked but held along the old strong line, and inch by inch, pull by pull, the boy came out of the mouth of Whitefall like something being born backwards—hips over the lip, chest, finally one boot swinging free, and then he was sprawled on the blue ice, gasping, white-faced, alive.

For a long moment none of them moved. The waterfall muttered under its lid. Somewhere above, the wind worried at the cornices.

Then Tam rolled onto his back and started laughing—a high, unhinged, entirely understandable laugh, the laugh of a boy who had just met the bottom of a waterfall and been sent home. "I'm wet," he announced. "Only my legs. How am I only wet in my LEGS—"

"Because you listened," August said, and found her own voice had gone rough. "You listened exactly right."

She got him moving immediately—wet legs in deep cold is a clock ticking, and she knew the arithmetic by heart. They crossed the remaining distance fast, the lower slopes of the fall mercifully solid, and within the hour had them in the shelter of an overhang with a fire going and Tam's boots steaming and the boy wrapped in every spare layer they owned, complaining with tremendous vigor about the itchiness of wool, which August encouraged, because complaining meant his blood was running warm again.

It was Ede who sat down heavily on a stone afterward and put his face in his hands.

* * *

"He was dead," Ede said, when Tam had finally gone to sleep inside the nest of blankets, exhausted past wakefulness. "Do you understand? If you hadn't—that anchor trick, going past instead of to—he'd be under Whitefall right now, and it would have been the second person I've fed to this river."

August fed the fire a stick and waited. She was learning that Ede Vasq, given silence, would eventually tell the truth. It was the one reliable thing about him.

"The testimony," he said. "At your hearing. You've read it. You know what I signed." He rubbed his jaw. "But you don't know the shape of it. So let me give you the shape, because you've earned the whole ugly thing, and because I'm tired of carrying it in pieces."

The fire popped. Downstream, Whitefall murmured to itself.

"Halloway didn't threaten me," Ede said. "That's what I need you to understand. I'd imagined threats—money, violence, something I could have resisted heroically in the retelling. Instead he sat me down in a room with tea and showed me a file." His voice went flat, reciting. "My sister's debts. My nephew's school fees. My pension, which was Company-administered, like everyone's. And then he showed me a second file—yours. Every route you'd ever guided, every client, every incident, including ones I'd forgotten better than you had. And he said, 'Mr. Vasq, you can confirm the surveyor's daughter falsified the Craghorn assessment, or you can explain to your family why the pension stopped.' No shouting. He poured the tea himself."

August stared into the fire. "There was nothing to confirm. I never falsified anything."

"I know that now. I knew it then, God help me—that's the worst of it. Halloway didn't even ask me to believe it. He asked me to say it. He said the difference wouldn't matter to anyone who counted." Ede's laugh was a dry, awful sound. "And he was right. One signature. Four words of confirmation. And the valleys closed to you like a fist."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because today I watched you do what your father taught you, on ice your father named, to save a boy neither of us owes anything to." He looked at her across the fire, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise. "Twelve years I told myself I'd been cornered. Today I watched you cornered—properly cornered, a boy dying on a rope—and you didn't sign anything. You didn't trade anyone. You just did the next right thing, and then the next, until the right thing was done." He shook his head slowly. "I could have done that. In that room, with the tea. I could have done what you did. I chose not to, and I've dressed it up as coercion for twelve years, and I'm done dressing it."

August sat with that. The fire settled. Above the overhang, the wind had begun to die, and through the gap she could see stars coming out over the gorge, hard and cold and indifferent, and somehow comforting for exactly that reason.

"Ede," she said at last. "Why did you really come find us at the ford?"

He was quiet a long time. "Because after they blacklisted you, I requested the file. Your file. I wanted to know how bad I'd made it." He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, soft with handling, and passed it across the fire. "This was in it. A note, unsigned, in the surveyor's hand. Your father's hand. It had been misfiled—or hidden—in the Craghorn assessment records for twelve years. Halloway's clerks missed it. I didn't."

She unfolded it. The paper was brittle, the handwriting slanted and sure, a surveyor's hand used to writing legibly in wind:

*A.J. — If you're reading this, they've come for the source. Trust the compass, not the map. The compass knows the way home. — G.*

August read it three times. The firelight trembled on the page. *G.* Her throat closed around the old trail-name she hadn't heard spoken aloud since she was nine years old.

"Gideon," she whispered.

"What?" Ede leaned forward.

"Nothing." She folded the note carefully, the way you fold something that has just become the most important object in the world, and tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, next to the brass compass. "Get some sleep, Ede. Tomorrow we climb past the map's edge."

Above them, far up the dark gorge, something glinted—brief, rhythmic, unmistakable even at distance. A signal mirror, flashing from a height no honest traveler should occupy.

Someone was following them up the Vell. And they were gaining.
