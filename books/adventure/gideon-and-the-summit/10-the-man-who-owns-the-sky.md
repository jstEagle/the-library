## 10. The Man Who Owns the Sky

They took her at dawn, and they took her politely.

That was the worst part, August thought later. Not the ropes—though the ropes were good ones, waxed cord, tied by someone who knew knots—and not the six grey-coated men who rose out of the scree like crows off a carcass, but the *politeness*. A voice from behind a boulder saying, "Miss Joubert. If you'd be so kind as to keep your hands where we can see them," in the tone of a man asking her to pass the salt.

She'd had maybe four hours of sleep on a ledge above the treeline, Tam curled against Ede's pack like a burr, the fire long dead. She woke to boots on stone and knew, before her eyes were fully open, that it was over. You didn't outrun six fresh men when you'd been eating half-rations for nine days.

"Tam," she said quietly. "Ede."

"I know," Ede said. His voice was flat as slate. "Don't run. Running's how they justify things."

"Hands," said the voice again, closer now, and then they came around the rock with their carbines slung low—not aimed, which was its own kind of message—and their faces arranged into expressions of professional regret.

It was the youngest one who searched her, and he was gentle about it, and somehow that made her angrier than violence would have. He took the map tube from inside her coat with both hands, reverent, like a priest handling relics. He took the brass compass too, turning it over once, frowning at the needle that swung lazily to point at nothing north.

"That doesn't work," August said.

"It works fine," the young man said. "Just not for us." And he wrapped it in cloth without another word, which told her more about how much the Company understood her father than anything else that morning.

* * *

They walked them down into a cirque August had never known existed, though she'd guided these ranges for eleven years. That was the thing about Meridian money: it could hide a whole camp in a fold of country and make the maps agree to lie about it. Tents in Company grey, a cookfire, mules, a wireless mast guyed against the wind. A table set up under an awning with a white cloth on it. A white cloth, at eleven thousand feet.

And at the table, drinking tea, sat Warden Halloway.

She'd seen him twice before in her life. Once at a distance, at her father's funeral, standing at the back in a black coat, hat in hand, wearing grief like a borrowed garment. Once across a hearing room, seven years ago, while three men read out the findings that ended her career: negligence, falsified route reports, the death of a client she still dreamed about. She hadn't spoken to him either time. He hadn't looked at her either time.

Now he looked at her directly, and his face did something complicated—something that might have been pleasure, or might have been relief, or might have simply been the satisfaction of a collector completing a set.

"Miss Joubert." He stood. He actually stood. "You'll forgive the theater of the ropes. My men are cautious men. I've asked them to cut you loose, if you'll give your word you won't do anything foolish."

"And if I don't give my word?"

"Then we'll all stand here awkwardly until you get cold, and you will get cold, because you're wet through and this is a glacier." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who had never once needed to buy a drink. "I'd rather skip to the tea."

August gave her word. It cost her nothing; she intended to break it before lunch. The young fixer—his name, she gathered, was Pell—cut the cords and rubbed feeling back into her wrists with a briskness that bordered on apology.

They sat her at the white-clothed table. They brought her tea, real tea, with milk, and bread that wasn't hardtack, and a bowl of stewed apples, and her body betrayed her utterly by responding to all of it with gratitude. Tam got a bowl too, and ate like a small wolf pretending not to be afraid. Ede refused to sit. He stood behind August's chair with his arms folded, and two grey coats stood near him with the particular patience of men who had been told *not yet*.

"You've come a long way," Halloway said. "Nine days upstream. The ford at Kessick—the Marrowfen tolls—you've left a trail a child could follow, which I confess surprised me. Your father taught you better tradecraft than that."

"My father taught me plenty," August said. "You'd know. You read his reports."

Something flickered across Halloway's face. "I did," he said. "Every one. He was the finest surveyor the Company ever employed. Do you know what I thought, the first time I met him? I thought, *there is a man who will die on this mountain, because he loves it more than he loves being sensible.*" He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I was nearly right. He died in a valley instead. But the principle held."

"He died because your Company sent him upriver to falsify a survey," August said, "and he wouldn't."

The camp went quiet around them. Even the mules seemed to stop chewing.

Halloway sighed, and it was a real sigh, tired at the bottom of it, and that frightened her more than the carbines had. She had prepared herself for a monster. Monsters were simple. This man sounded like a foreman explaining why the bridge couldn't be finished by winter.

"Yes," he said. "He wouldn't. And here we are, thirty years later, still paying for it." He laced his fingers together on the white cloth. "Let me tell you what I think you believe, Miss Joubert, and correct me where I'm wrong. You believe there are documents at the source of the Vell. The original Founding Accord. The orders given before it. Correspondence. Perhaps a ledger of payments. You believe these documents prove the Company massacred the valley settlements at High Vell to clear the way for its charter, and that my predecessors buried the proof in ice rather than let it see daylight. And you believe that if you bring those documents down, the truth will unmake us." He paused. "Have I missed anything?"

"The part where you ruined my name to make sure nobody ever went looking," August said. "Seven years ago. The hearing. The falsified findings. You wrote them yourself—I've had time to check the phrasing against Company correspondence. You have a tell, Warden. You love the word 'regrettably.'"

Pell, standing by the awning pole, made a very small sound that might have been a swallowed laugh.

Halloway didn't deny it. That was the second frightening thing. "Yes," he said. "That was mine. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. You were becoming your father, Miss Joubert. Asking questions on routes no one asked you to survey. Talking to old men in valley taverns. Taking clients into country the Company had closed. I closed you the way one closes a road before a flood—regrettably, and with paperwork." He spread his hands. "And now look. The flood has found another channel anyway. So perhaps we can stop pretending, you and I, and speak plainly about what happens next."

"Plainly," August said. "From the man who owns the sky."

"I own the passes," Halloway corrected mildly. "The sky owns itself. But yes. Everything above the snowline, everything below it that drinks from our rivers, every bridge, every toll, every rope sold in every chandlery from Greyharbor to the headwaters—that's mine, or mine to administer, which amounts to the same thing. You've spent nine days learning what that means. You've seen the camps we burned. You've seen the survey stations stripped. You know what my fixers do to people who carry Company paper they shouldn't." He leaned forward slightly. "So here is what I offer instead."

He nodded, and Pell brought a leather folder and laid it on the table between them, and opened it.

Inside was paper. Good paper, cream-colored, stamped with seals August recognized from a lifetime ago: the Guild of Mountain Guides, the Crown Survey Office, the Company itself.

"Full reinstatement," Halloway said. "Not quietly, not grudgingly—a public restoration. The findings of 7th Bellis, Year of the Long Frost, vacated in open session, with the Guild issuing a formal apology for the error. Your father's record restored alongside it. Every report he filed, re-entered into the official archive under his own name, with the refusal letter included—" August's breath caught, and Halloway saw it catch, and he was honest enough not to pretend he hadn't— "yes, that letter, the one everyone says doesn't exist, entered into the permanent record. Your father honored in the books of the institution that erased him. That's the first page."

He turned pages as he spoke, unhurried, a man showing goods.

"The second page is money. Enough to buy Greyharbor's harbor if you wanted it, though I suspect you'd want something smaller. A house. A boat. Debts settled—yes, including Corvin's, though I understand he's been patient with you, which speaks well of him. The third page is position. Senior guide, Company charter, first pick of routes, authority to reopen closed country. You'd have legal power to walk anywhere in these mountains, Miss Joubert. Anything the valleys can't give you, the law would."

He closed the folder and rested his hand flat upon it.

"In exchange, you surrender the map. You tell me where the compass points. And you walk away from the source, down the mountain, into the life that was stolen from you—and I give it back with interest."

* * *

The terrible thing—the thing August would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would confess to Sefa later with her face in her hands—was how long she let herself look at it.

Not the money. Money was paper; she'd slept under boats for seven years and hadn't drowned. Not even the reinstatement, though God, the reinstatement. To hear the word *negligence* unsaid. To walk into a Guild hall and have men rise. To sign a client's ledger with a name that meant competence instead of scandal.

It was her father's name. That was the hook, and Halloway knew it, and to his credit—or whatever you called the virtue of a man who was excellent at his work—he didn't disguise it. Her father had died a Company employee whose final report was marked *unsatisfactory*. Thirty years of climbers had learned the Vell headwaters from redacted charts. Children in Marrowfen grew up thinking the man had been a mediocrity or worse. And here was a folder that would fix all of it, signed, sealed, public, permanent.

All it cost was the truth.

"Say I take it," August said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Say I give you the map and the compass and I go home rich and restored. What happens to the documents?"

"They stay where they are," Halloway said. "Frozen. Guarded. Forever."

"What happened at High Vell stays unwritten."

"What happened at High Vell," Halloway said, "happened eighty years ago, to people who are dead, by men who are dead, for reasons that built every granary and schoolhouse and bridge from here to the sea. Yes. It stays unwritten." He said it without flinching, and she realized he had rehearsed this speech, possibly for decades, waiting for someone worth saying it to. "You want a villain, Miss Joubert. I've watched you since Greyharbor; you want a man in a black coat twirling his mustache, so that burning it all down feels clean. There isn't one. There's just the mountain, and the money the mountain makes, and ten thousand people downstream who eat because the money flows. Pull up the Accord and wave it in the square, and what changes? The Company dissolves. The charters void. The tolls burn. And then what? Who keeps the passes? Who feeds the towns? You'd trade functioning order for a truth, and the truth would comfort no one, because the dead stay dead either way."

"Is that what you believe?" August asked. "Or is that what you're paid to believe?"

For the first time, something moved behind Halloway's eyes that wasn't calculation. It might have been pain. It was gone quickly.

"I believed it once the easy way," he said. "Now I believe it the hard way. It's heavier, the second version. But it holds weight." He pushed the folder an inch toward her. "You don't have to answer now. Eat. Warm up. Think about your father's name sitting in a drawer for another eighty years, and ask yourself whether the dead care more about being avenged or being remembered rightly."

"He cared about being *accurate*," August said. "It's in the letter. You've read it. He wrote that a surveyor's only possession is the truth of the ground under his feet, and that a man who sells it has nothing left to stand on." She looked up. "You quoted him at the hearing, Warden. Did you know that? When they struck him from the rolls, the presiding officer used his own words against him. *A surveyor's only possession.* You've been living inside my father's sentences for thirty years. Doesn't it ever get crowded?"

Halloway looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, genuine, startled laugh, the first honest sound he'd made all morning.

"There it is," he said. "There's Gideon in you. I wondered." He stood, buttoning his coat. "Rest. We march at noon whether you decide or not. Pell will see to your people—they'll be treated well, I give you my word, whatever you choose." He paused at the edge of the awning, the wind pulling at his grey collar, and for a moment he looked less like a warden and more like a tired man on a cold mountain, which was, she supposed, what he was underneath everything.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I offered your father a version of this folder once. Different words. Same arithmetic." He didn't turn around. "He chose the ice. I've often wondered, in thirty years, whether he was right. I'd genuinely like to know, Miss Joubert, before I die, whether it was worth it—being right, and alone, and frozen."

He walked away toward the wireless mast, and the grey coats parted for him, and August sat with the folder in front of her and the steam rising off her tea and understood, with a clarity that felt like the moment a rope comes taut, exactly what power costs.

It cost this. It cost a man who owned the sky asking a disgraced boat-mender whether his life had meant anything, and meaning it. Power had bought him every pass and every toll and every map, and it had never bought him an answer, and he knew it, and he kept buying anyway, because stopping would mean admitting the purchase had failed.

Her father had answered him with silence and a sealed vault.

August looked at the folder. Then past it, to Tam, who was watching her over the rim of his bowl with the fierce, terrified hope of a boy whose whole family had already been erased once. Then to Ede, standing guard behind her chair out of a guilt he'd carry to his grave, whatever she decided. Then to the west, where the clouds had lifted off the high shoulder of the range, and somewhere beyond that shoulder, past the last line on any map, the river Vell began as ice that remembered everything.

"Noon," she said quietly.

"Ma'am?" said Pell.

"Nothing." She picked up her spoon and ate the stewed apples, because her father had taught her that a climber eats when food is offered, always, because strength is the one argument no one can confiscate. "Thank you for the apples."

* * *

At half past eleven, while the camp packed mules and Halloway dictated a wireless message in a low voice, Pell came to strike the tent where they'd been held. He was alone. He was young. He knelt to coil the tent lines, and as he passed August he said, very softly, without looking at her:

"The compass. When the master examined it—he turned it over twice. The second time, the needle moved. He didn't notice. I did." He tightened a knot that didn't need tightening. "It pointed at you, miss. Both times. Straight at you."

He stood, shouldered the tent canvas, and walked away whistling.

August sat very still in the cold, and felt the shape of the next hour assemble itself out of rope and weather and a boy's small treason, and began, silently, to count the guards.

Noon was coming. So was the source.
