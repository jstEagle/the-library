## 11. Escape from the High Camp

Tam had learned three things about the High Camp in his first day as a prisoner, and all three of them were useful.

One: the Company men were lazy about their stores. They stacked the rope and rations in a canvas lean-to at the edge of camp, because nobody stole supplies at eleven thousand feet — there was nowhere to run to. Two: the cook, a broad unhappy man called Dree who hummed while he worked and never once looked anyone in the eye, was not Company to the bone. He'd slipped Tam an extra heel of bread that morning without a word, and when Tam said thank you he'd flinched like he'd been struck.

Three: Ede Vasq could take a beating better than any man Tam had ever seen, and that was a thing you wished you didn't have to learn by watching.

Tam sat on a supply crate near the cook-fire, wrists loose in their bindings, doing what he did best: looking stupid. Fourteen years of being small and orphaned and underfoot had taught him that people talked over your head if they thought there was nothing inside it. The fixers talked plenty. About the woman in the tent. About the bounty. About how Warden Halloway wanted her alive and the map intact, and how the old ranger was worth nothing dead or alive but might still know where she'd hidden things.

They kept August in the command tent with a guard at the flap. They kept Ede tied to a sled frame out in the open, "for the cold to think him over," one of them said, and laughed at his own joke twice.

And they kept Tam loose-ish, because he was fourteen and filthy and had cried the first night, which had been an investment, really, when you thought about it.

The rope in the lean-to was Meridian issue — grey-braided, waxed, the best climbing line money could buy, which was funny, because it was going to be used to cut their own throats, supply-wise. Tam had watched the packers work. Every morning, two men hauled the food crates down to the cache ledge below camp and roped them for the mule train coming up from Marrowfen. Every evening, the mule train's driver drank himself stupid and slept in the second tent. And every night, one boy sat by a dying fire with hands that could dress stone, splice wire, and — as of roughly four days ago, when a girl with a compass had taught him knots on a riverbank — tie off a load so it would hold until exactly the moment you didn't want it to.

He'd need a knife. He'd need darkness. He'd need the guard at August's tent to look away for ninety seconds.

What he got, instead, was Dree.

The cook came and crouched by the fire across from him, stirring a pot that needed no stirring. Up close, Tam could see the man's hands — scarred across the knuckles, quarry-scarred, the kind of scars you got from years of splitting stone before the Company brought in machines and men like Tam's father lost everything but their debts.

"You're the quarry man's boy," Dree said quietly. "Fennick's quarry. I knew your da."

Tam's heart went strange in his chest. He kept his face blank and stupid. "My da's dead."

"I know." Dree stirred the pot. "I sent money home after. It never reached you, did it."

"No," Tam said. Because it hadn't. Because the Company held the quarry accounts, and the Company held the town, and somewhere between Dree's pocket and Tam's empty belly there had been a ledger entry and a signature and nothing else.

Dree looked at the fire for a long time. Then he set down the spoon, and while he leaned forward to do it, something small and cold dropped into the snow between Tam's boots: a knife, wrapped in oilcloth.

"Lean-to latch sticks," Dree said, standing. "Third plank from the left. Lift, don't pull." He raised his voice back to its normal flatness. "More wood, boy. Fire won't feed itself."

* * *

Night came down blue and bitter. The stars up here were close enough to bite.

Tam waited until the watch changed — the new sentry always spent his first twenty minutes stamping warmth into his feet, facing away from the lean-to — and then he went to work. Lift, don't pull. The third plank gave with a groan he felt in his teeth more than heard. Inside, the stores smelled of wax and paraffin and rope-oil, and Tam moved through them like he'd been born in the dark, which sometimes felt true.

He didn't cut the ropes holding the cache loads. That would be noticed by morning, blamed on weather maybe, but noticed. Instead he did worse. He untied each load's binding and retied it — same knots to the eye, different knots underneath — so that every wrap would slide free the moment a mule shifted weight or a handler tugged wrong. Three days of supplies, scattered down a mountainside. He spliced the lantern fuel line so it would weep instead of pour, slow enough that nobody would smell it till the tank was half-empty. He put a hairline crack in the signal mirror with a tap of a stone, careful, careful, the way his father had taught him to test slate.

It wasn't burning anything down. It was arithmetic. You couldn't chase anybody far on an empty stomach in the dark.

Then he went to the sled frame where Ede hung.

The old ranger's eyes opened before Tam touched him. They always did. "You're supposed to be crying by the fire," Ede whispered.

"Retired that act."

"Shame. It was convincing." His lips were split and one eye had swollen mostly shut, but his voice was steady as a plumb line. "Blade."

Tam sawed through the wrist bindings, then the ankles. Ede came off the frame like a man forty years younger and immediately ruined it by swaying; Tam caught his elbow and pretended not to, for both their dignities.

"Tent," Ede breathed. "Guard changes at the bell. Ninety seconds, like clockwork — Halloway runs this camp like a ferry schedule. We go on the bell."

"The bell hasn't rung yet."

"It will." Ede smiled with the unsplit half of his mouth. "I asked the cook to ring it early. Man owes me a debt older than you are."

As if summoned, the camp bell clanged — once, twice, wrong-footed, and the sentry at August's tent turned toward the sound, cursing, and for ninety seconds the whole world was looking the other way.

Tam slipped under the tent's back wall where he'd loosened the pegs at dusk. August was already awake — of course she was, she'd probably been awake since they'd tied her, sitting in the dark doing sums of her own — and she had her wrists freed before Tam finished crawling in, working the last of the cord off with a motion so practiced it made him feel better and worse at once.

"Tell me," she whispered.

"Cache loads will fail on the haul-out. Fuel's bleeding. Mirror's cracked. Mule driver's drunk."

August stared at him in the firelight leaking through the canvas, and something crossed her face that Tam decided, privately, to treasure forever: she looked proud and appalled in equal measure.

"Fourteen," she said.

"Fourteen and a half."

"Never tell me otherwise." She took the knife from him, weighed it, handed it back. "Ede?"

"Free. Waiting at the south rocks."

"Then we're robbing them on the way out." Her teeth showed white in the dark, and it wasn't quite a smile, it was something hungrier. "Rope, rations, and my compass off Halloway's desk. Come on."

* * *

They nearly made it clean.

Nearly. Rope and rations went into a salvaged pack. The compass came off the warden's desk inside a ledger folio, and August closed her hand around it with a sound that wasn't a word. They were at the south rocks, Ede limping beside them, the dark valley opening below like a held breath —

— and then a lantern swung up from the latrine trench, and a voice said, "Oi," and it was the young sentry, nineteen at most, rifle half-raised, confusion on his face faster than alarm.

"Stop there," he said, without conviction.

Ede stepped in front of them. Just stepped, unhurried, a big tired man putting himself between a boy and a gun barrel, and said, "Son, go back to your post."

"I have to report—"

"You'll report we went north." Ede's voice was gravel and patience. "Or you'll report whatever keeps you honest tonight. Either way, stand down."

The boy's rifle wavered. And then boots crunched behind them — Halloway's voice, smooth as banked coals: "Vasq. Still collecting strays, I see."

What happened next happened fast and ugly. A whistle blew. Lanterns bloomed. Ede turned to meet the first two men coming up the slope and did it deliberately, arms wide, buying seconds with his body the way other men bought them with coin. Tam saw the baton come down and heard the sound it made and would carry that sound longer than he wanted to. But August had his collar in her fist and the slope under their boots, and then they were running, down and down, into a darkness with no lanterns in it, while behind them the High Camp lit up like an angry hive and someone shouted about the cache loads scattering down the mountain like a broken beehive's wages.

Ede caught up at the tree line. Limping. Bleeding from the ear. Grinning.

"You're mad," August panted. "Both of you. That was — you should've gone when I said—"

"And miss the view?" Ede wheezed. "Boy, remind me to teach you nothing else. You're dangerous enough."

* * *

They walked most of the night, following a streambed Ede seemed to know the way a man knows his own kitchen — turning left where there was no path, ducking under a fallen spruce, always moving as though the mountain had told him a secret years ago and he'd only just gotten around to using it.

Dawn found them in a place that shouldn't exist.

The gorge narrowed, then opened — not gradually but all at once, like a door — into a hanging valley cupped between shoulders of rock, green to the treeline and terraced above it. Fields. Actual fields, small and stubborn, stitched into slopes too steep for any surveyor to bother claiming. Smoke rose from chimneys of dry-stacked stone. There was a mill wheel turning on the stream. There were goats. There was, unbelievably, washing on a line, snapping cheerfully in the wind like flags of some country with no name.

Tam stopped walking and stared.

"What is it?" he said.

"Home," said a voice above them, "if you're the right sort of person. Trouble, if you're not. Which are you?"

A woman stood on the terrace wall with a hoe over her shoulder, sixty-odd, built like the rock she stood on, watching them with eyes that counted everything: August's torn coat, Ede's bloodied ear, Tam's bound wrists, long since cut, and the Company rope on their packs.

"We're the right sort," August said carefully. "We're the blacklisted sort."

The woman's face did something complicated and settled on welcome. "Then you're the majority here, love. Come down before the porridge goes. Names later. Porridge first — that's the law."

And that was the other thing Tam couldn't stop staring at, all through the impossible morning that followed: the laws. There weren't any, not written ones, and there were all of them, unwritten, and everyone knew them. You carried water even if you hadn't drawn it. You taught what you knew to whoever wanted learning. You didn't ask a person's past unless they offered it, because everyone here had a past, and most of them had fled theirs.

There was a stonemason with a warrant from three duchies who spent the afternoon showing Tam how they'd keyed the mill foundation without mortar. There was a former toll clerk who'd burned her own ledgers and now taught letters to children on slates. There was a shepherd who had, allegedly, stolen an entire flock from a Company factor out of pure spite, and wore the fact like a medal. Nobody was from anywhere anymore. Everybody was from here.

"For the first time in my life," Tam said, around midday, mouth full of bread and honey, "I'm normal."

Ede laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway. "Enjoy it, lad. Being normal's underrated."

August stood a little apart, at the valley's edge, looking up — not at the terraces or the smoke, but higher, at the white shoulder of the peak beyond, where the river Vell was born behind walls of ice. Her hand was in her pocket, around the compass. She had the look of a woman reading a letter she already knew the ending of.

The woman with the hoe — whose name, it turned out, was Sefa, and who watched August the way you watch weather — came and stood beside Tam.

"She'll want to go up," Sefa said. Not a question.

"Is that bad?"

Sefa chewed on it. "Depends," she said, "on whether she goes alone."

That night, the valley held a council by firelight — not a court, nothing so grand, just everyone who cared, which was everyone — and the talk was all of the map in August's coat and the source of the Vell and what a person owed to the people who'd taken her in. Tam fell asleep against Ede's good shoulder listening to arguments that sounded, for once in his short loud life, like they might actually end somewhere.

He woke once, deep in the night, to see August standing at the fire's last coals, compass open in her palm, its needle swinging — not north, he noticed, not north at all — and her face lit orange and absolutely certain.

Above the valley, unseen, the mountain waited. So, Tam suspected, did whatever Sefa was going to charge for the road up.
