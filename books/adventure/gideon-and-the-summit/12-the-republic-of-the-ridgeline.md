## 12. The Republic of the Ridgeline

August had expected the hidden valley to be a hole in the world—a cave system, maybe, or a scoured basin where nothing grew and nobody wanted to look. What she got instead was terraced gardens climbing the walls like green staircases, smoke rising from a dozen chimneys, laundry strung between pines, and a boy chasing a goat across a rope bridge with the particular fury of someone who has chased that goat before.

"Close your mouth," Tam said, not unkindly. "You'll catch flies."

"I'm not—" She closed her mouth. "How many people live here?"

Ede, walking beside her with his ribs wrapped in strips of Tam's shirt and his face the color of old porridge, managed something like a shrug. "More than the Company thinks exist. Fewer than they fear." He paused at the head of the bridge, breathing carefully. "They call it the Republic of the Ridgeline. Nobody elected anybody. It just sort of happened."

The bridge swayed under them as they crossed. Below, a river ran clear over slate—the Vell's little sister, August guessed, fed by the same snows—and two women were pulling fish from it with their bare hands, laughing about something. On the far bank, an old man sat mending a net, and he looked up as they came off the planks, and his face did something complicated.

"You're Joubert's girl," he said.

August stopped. It had been years since anyone had said her father's name to her face without spitting around it. "I'm August."

"Aye, that too." He went back to his net. "Sefa's been expecting you since the granary fire started. She says fires are how your family sends messages."

* * *

They put Ede in the house of a midwife named Brannoc, who took one look at him and one look at August and said, "You let him take a beating for you," in a tone that made it unclear whether this was a question or a diagnosis. Then she shut the door on all further discussion.

Tam vanished immediately. August found him an hour later in the smithy, holding a horseshoe he had absolutely not been invited to hold, listening to a woman explain quenching temperatures with the rapt attention he usually reserved for food. When August caught his eye across the forge, he didn't look guilty. He looked—she searched for the word and was disturbed by how easily it arrived—*home*. Like a nail finding its board.

She left him to it. That, she was learning, was most of what loving a fourteen-year-old consisted of: leaving him to things, and being nearby when the things went wrong.

Sefa found her at dusk, sitting on the low wall above the terraces where the whole valley laid itself out below like a map someone had loved into existence.

"You have his way of sitting," Sefa said.

August turned. The woman was sixty-odd, built like the mountain had decided to try a human shape and kept some of the angles. Grey braid, hands like tree roots, eyes that did not blink so much as decide when to be open.

"You're Sefa."

"And you're the reason three Company fixers are currently explaining themselves to a very unsympathetic audience downriver." Sefa sat beside her on the wall, unhurried. "Your father used to sit exactly like that. Perched. Like he was ready to leave even when he'd only just arrived. I told him once he should learn to land. He said landing was for birds who trusted the branch."

August's throat did something she chose to ignore. "You knew him well."

"I carried rope for him for eleven years. He carried my pride for me the year my knee went and I wouldn't admit it." Sefa looked out at the valley. "So. You've come all this way following a dead man's compass. Ask me what you came to ask."

"The source of the Vell," August said. "What's buried there."

Sefa was quiet long enough that somewhere below, the goat finally won.

"The Founding Accord," she said at last. "The original. Not the pretty copy in the Company archives with the wax seals and the signatures of men who never saw snow. The real one. The orders Halloway's grandfather signed authorizing the clearing of the high valleys. The surveyor's reports that were falsified afterward—and the one report that wasn't." Her voice didn't change, but the air around it seemed to get colder. "Your father wrote that report, August. He refused to say the valleys were empty. He named every family the Company moved off that ground, and where they went, and what was done to the ones who wouldn't go. They buried the paper at the source because they couldn't bury him there—not without making him a martyr, and martyrs ask questions. So they buried his name instead. Made him a cautionary tale. A disgraced guide's line, passed down like a disease."

"They blacklisted me before I ever tied on a rope," August said quietly. "I thought it was me. Something I'd done."

"It was you. It was you being *his*." Sefa turned and looked at her directly, and August understood why men followed this woman up mountains. "Halloway doesn't punish crimes. He punishes bloodlines. A crime ends. A bloodline might remember."

* * *

The debate began after supper, in the longhouse, and August spent the first hour understanding why the valley had no elections.

There must have been forty people crammed between the beams—fishers, quarrymen, a woman who'd been a Company clerk until she'd read one ledger too closely, two brothers who'd walked out of a toll garrison with the toll box and their consciences intact. Tam sat cross-legged near the fire with his chin on his fists, absorbing arguments the way he absorbed everything. Ede lay propped on a bench near the door, bandaged, gray, but present, because Ede seemed constitutionally incapable of being absent from anything that might go badly.

The question was simple and enormous: the documents at the source. What should become of them?

"Burn them," said Brannoc, arms crossed. "Burn them and be done. As long as that paper exists, someone will fight to own it. The Company owns it now; tomorrow a crown could own it; next year some exile council decides it knows best. Paper is just power waiting for hands."

"And then?" This from the former clerk, a thin man named Osk whose voice had the flatness of someone who had done arithmetic for a living. "Then the Accord exists only in memory, and memory is a rumor with good posture. Halloway's grandchildren will write histories saying the valleys were always empty. We'll know better. Our grandchildren won't."

"Publish it, then," someone called. "Print it in every town on the Vell. Nail it to church doors."

"With what press?" Osk asked. "With what protection? The moment it surfaces, it becomes evidence in a trial that will be held in a courtroom the Company built, judged by laws the Company wrote. We hand them our truth and they'll spend thirty years litigating it into dust. Justice delayed past a generation is just archaeology."

"Then bargain," said a quarryman near the back, and the room went quiet in a different way. "We hold the one thing Halloway fears. We trade it. Recognition. Land titles. Amnesty for everyone in this valley. Legal existence. We could come down off this ridge as *citizens*, not fugitives."

"That's trading the dead," Brannoc said sharply.

"That's feeding the living," the quarryman shot back. "My daughter has a cough that gets worse every winter up here. There's medicine in the towns. There's schools. You want to talk about the dead? Talk to the children we're raising on a ridge because their parents' names are dirty."

The argument rolled on. August listened, and watched, and noticed something she couldn't have noticed a month ago: nobody looked at her to settle it. In Greyharbor, in the Company towns, everything bent toward whoever held the most weight. Here, the argument simply held itself, and she was welcome to carry a corner of it if she wished.

It was the strangest gift she'd ever been given.

"He's not wrong, though," Tam said suddenly, into a lull. Every head turned. Fourteen-year-olds in most rooms learned to shrink. Tam had apparently never been taught. "The quarryman. My family's quarry was razed when I was nine. I've been angry about it so long I forgot to be hungry. If there was a deal that got kids fed and schooled—" He faltered, then pushed through. "But you can't trade it either. Because the second it's worth something, it belongs to whoever pays, and then it's theirs, not ours. And then in fifty years somebody sells it back to the Company and we're here again." He looked around the room, defiant and earnest. "I don't know the answer. But I know it's not selling."

Silence held for a moment. Then Brannoc, gruffly: "The boy argues like his mother did."

"You knew my mother?" Tam's voice cracked straight down the middle.

"I stitched her arm once, after a rockfall. Now hush, you've said your piece, and it was a good piece." But she smiled at him, and Tam sat back down looking like a boy who had been handed a lantern in a dark house.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to embers and the longhouse had emptied to a handful of sleepers, Sefa found August outside again. Of course she did. August was beginning to suspect the woman navigated by grief the way other guides navigated by stars.

"You didn't speak in there," Sefa said.

"I didn't have anything worth adding."

"Liar. You had the only thing that matters—you're the one going up there." Sefa leaned against the wall beside her. "So I'll tell you what I told your father, the last time we stood under the source ice. He said, 'This proof should belong to everyone.' And I said, 'Everyone isn't a person, Gideon. Everyone won't climb up and get it. Someone has to carry it down, and whoever carries it down owns the choosing.' He laughed and said that was why he liked rope teams—one choice, many hands."

August looked at her. "You keep calling him Gideon."

"Because that was his name. His trail-name. The name the mountain gave him." Sefa's mouth twitched. "You didn't know."

"I didn't know anything," August said, and heard the crack in her own voice and hated it. "I knew a father who came home quiet and taught me knots and died owing money. I didn't know he refused the Company. I didn't know he had friends. I didn't know there was a whole—" she gestured at the valley, the terraces, the smoke, the impossible ordinary life of it, "—a whole country of people like me."

"There is," Sefa said. "And here's the price of admission, and I'll only say it once, because I'm old and repetition bores me." She turned to face August fully. "You can stay. You'll be fed, housed, argued with, and loved in the specific annoying way these people love—which is constantly, and without asking permission. But you stop walking alone. No more solo decisions. No more burning bridges in secret because you've decided you know best. Your father walked alone at the end and it cost him everything except the truth. You've got more than he had. Use it."

August stared out at the dark valley. Somewhere below, the goat bleated, victorious at last.

"I burned a bridge in secret?" she said. "Which one?"

"Sefa told me," Tam said from the doorway, where he had absolutely been eavesdropping, "that you're planning to burn the documents at the source. Everybody thinks so. It's written all over your face. It's been written all over your face since Marrowfen."

"Tam—"

"She's right," Sefa said. "Are you?"

August opened her mouth to say *yes, obviously, it's the only way no one can own it*—and found the sentence wouldn't come out clean. Because Osk was right too. Because the quarryman's daughter coughed all winter. Because Tam deserved to know his mother's name spoken in towns instead of whispered on ridges. Because every clean answer she'd ever had had turned out to be a cliff edge with good lighting.

"I don't know," she said, and it felt like the first honest thing she'd said in years.

Sefa nodded slowly, as if August had passed something. "Good. Doubt climbs better than certainty—it checks its footing." She pushed off the wall. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow I start teaching you the route your father cut, and the day after that, we go up. And August—" she paused at the door, firelight catching the grey of her braid, "—the day after that may need to move. Brannoc pulled a sliver of metal out of Ede's coat lining this evening. Company workmanship. A tracker."

The cold that went through August had nothing to do with the altitude.

"How long?"

"Since his capture, by the look of it. Which means Halloway hasn't found us yet—but he's looking. And a man like that, looking hard enough, finds everything eventually." Sefa's eyes were steady and grim. "Sleep fast, guide. The mountain's about to test whether this republic of yours can march."
