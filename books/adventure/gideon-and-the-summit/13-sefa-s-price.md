## 13. Sefa's Price

The free valley smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, faintly but unmistakably, of onions.

August stood at the edge of the terraced gardens with a tin cup of something hot in her hands—barley tea, Sefa had called it, though it tasted like it had been argued with—and watched the morning organize itself around her. A woman repaired a roof two houses down, singing badly. Children chased a goat that had clearly won this game many times before. An old man sat on a bench mending nets with the same patient rhythm August used on boat canvas, and when he caught her watching, he nodded as if they'd been introduced years ago.

Nobody had asked her name. That was the strangest part. In Greyharbor, her name had been a debt and a warning. Here it was just a word people hadn't gotten around to needing yet.

"You're up early," said Tam, appearing at her elbow with his hair sticking out in six directions and a heel of bread already half-eaten. "Or you didn't sleep. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're arguing with somebody who isn't there."

"She's winning, at least," August said.

Tam considered this with the gravity of a boy who had spent fourteen years learning which adults were lying to him. "Is it about yesterday? The council?"

"It's about today."

Because today was the day. Sefa had said *come to the longhouse after breakfast*, and Sefa did not say things like that unless she'd made a decision, and August had spent the night turning over every version of what that decision might be, and none of them let her sleep.

Ede was already at the longhouse when they arrived, sitting on the bench outside like a man waiting for a verdict he'd earned fair and square. His ribs were still strapped from Halloway's camp—he'd taken a beating covering their escape and wore it the way he wore everything, quietly, as if pain were weather—but he'd shaved, which meant he thought the meeting mattered.

"You look terrible," August told him.

"Thank you. I practiced."

Inside, the longhouse smelled of pine resin and old smoke. Maps hung from the rafters—not Company maps, with their tidy borders and their seals stamped over everything like wax over a wound, but hand-drawn ones, layered and corrected over decades, the ridgelines annotated in three different hands. Sefa stood beneath them, arms folded, silver-haired and built like the kind of tree that survives avalanches by refusing to have opinions about them.

She waited until Tam and Ede had settled. Then she looked at August, and August felt the look go all the way through her, the way her father's could, the way only certain people ever managed—the ones who had known you before you knew yourself.

"I'm going to take you to the source," Sefa said.

Relief hit August so hard she nearly sat down. Then Sefa raised one finger, and the relief curdled.

"On one condition."

*There it is.*

"When we find the Founding Accord—when we find your father's letters, the massacre orders, all of it—it comes back here. To this valley. Not to a magistrate in the capital. Not to a crown court. Not to whatever passes for justice downriver." Sefa's voice was level, unhurried, the voice of someone stating the price of flour. "It comes home first. The valley decides what happens to it."

August opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again, which was not like her, and she hated that it was happening at all.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully. "Sefa. Those documents are evidence. Real evidence. If they go to a court—if they're entered properly, witnessed, sealed—then no one can ever call them forged. No one can say the exiles made them up out of spite. The law protects them. That's what the law is *for*."

"And who does the law belong to?" Sefa asked.

"To everyone."

"Say that again slower," said Tam from the corner, "and think about the toll bridge at Marrowfen while you do."

August shot him a look that would have wilted any apprentice she'd ever trained. Tam, being Tam, absorbed it like a stone wall absorbs rain and went on chewing.

"He's not wrong," Ede said quietly.

"Ede."

"No, listen." He leaned forward, wincing a little at his ribs. "I was the law, August. Twenty-two years a ranger. I carried the writs, I read the statutes, I believed—I *believed*, right down to my boots—that if you did things properly, through channels, the channels would hold." He looked at his hands. "And then Meridian came to me with a choice between my pension and my testimony, and I learned that the channels are a pipe, and pipes have owners. Every court that could try the Company has a Company man in it. Every archive that could hold those papers has a Company key. You'd be handing the truth to the people who buried it and asking them nicely to dig it back up."

"They wouldn't bury it again," August said. "Not once it was public record. Not once—"

"Not once what?" Sefa asked, gently. "Once it passed through a clerk's hands? Once it sat in a locked room in the capital for the two years it takes a case like this to even be heard? August. I knew your father. He was the most careful man I ever walked with. Do you know why he refused to falsify the survey records? Not because he trusted the courts. Because he didn't trust *anyone* to hold the truth except the truth itself. He wanted it in the ground where no hand could reach it until hands existed that could be trusted."

"And these hands can?" August gestured at the valley around them—at the whole republic of the ridgeline, the blacklisted and the burned-out and the fled, wonderful and desperate and human. "Sefa, these are people with grievances. Real ones. Give them the massacre orders and half of them will want them published and half will want them burned and a third will want to march on Marrowfen by Friday. At least a court has *procedure*. Procedure is slow and it's ugly and it's owned by the wrong people, but it's the only thing standing between justice and a mob with good reasons."

The words came out harder than she'd meant them, and she saw them land—saw Tam flinch, saw Ede look away—and she realized, with a cold little turn in her stomach, what she'd just said. What she'd just admitted.

*A mob with good reasons.* She'd been one, once. Blacklisted, ruined, reason enough to burn anything.

Sefa studied her for a long moment. Then she pulled out a stool and sat down across from August, close, knee to knee almost, the way you sit with family when the conversation has stopped being a negotiation.

"Tell me what you're actually afraid of," Sefa said.

"That's not—"

"You hesitated. My condition is simple, and you hesitated. So tell me the true thing underneath the speech about procedure."

August looked down at her tea. It had gone cold. Everything in her wanted to keep talking about courts and clerks and chains of custody, because that argument was safe, it was made of stone walls, it kept the real thing at arm's length.

But Sefa had known her father. And there was a limit, apparently, to how long you could stand in front of a woman like that and lie with architecture.

"I want it to count," August said finally. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "I spent six years being erased. Six years of knowing exactly what happened to me and having no document, no witness, no court that would hear it. I know what it is to be right and powerless. So when we find those papers, I want—"

She stopped. Breathed.

"I want it done *properly*. I want it witnessed and recorded and undeniable, because I never got that, and I—" A short, unhappy laugh escaped her. "God help me, I want my name cleared by an institution. I want the thing that broke me to be the thing that fixes me. Officially. With a stamp."

The silence that followed was not unkind. Even Tam had stopped chewing.

"There it is," Sefa said softly. "You don't distrust the valley, August. You distrust anything that isn't the machine that hurt you. Because if the machine can't save you, then nothing could have, and those six years were just weather. Random. Unpaid-for." She reached out and put her hand over August's cold ones. "But child. Listen to me. The machine doesn't clear names. Machines process them. Your name gets cleared the way mine did—by people deciding, house by house, ford by ford, that they know you and they believe you and they'll stand beside you anyway. There's no stamp for that. There never was."

"And if the valley decides wrong?" August whispered. "If they burn the papers and the story dies with them?"

"Then the story dies free," Sefa said, "instead of living its whole life in a cage." She squeezed August's hands once and let go. "That's my price. The documents come here. The valley decides—together, in the open, everyone's voice counted, which is more than any court in the capital has offered anyone in forty years. Say yes or walk without me. I'll be sad about it either way, but I'll understand."

August looked at Ede. Ede looked at the floor, offering nothing, which was its own kind of respect: *this one's yours.*

She looked at Tam. Tam looked back at her with those too-old eyes and said, "They razed our quarry on a Tuesday. There were warrants. Proper ones. Signed." He shrugged. "Procedure came for us. It just came carrying torches."

*Can justice exist outside the law?* The question had been walking beside her since the cave, since the ledgers, since the compass that pointed not north but *home*. And here, in a smoky longhouse under maps drawn by outlaw hands, the answer assembled itself—not grandly, not with trumpets, but the way the tide goes out: gradually, and then all at once, undeniable.

Maybe the law wasn't the frame justice lived in. Maybe the law was just one village's attempt at it—one attempt among many, some corrupted, some honest, all of them human. And maybe the proof of justice wasn't the seal on the paper. Maybe it was whether the people in the room could look each other in the eye afterward.

She thought of the old man mending nets. Of the goat, victorious. Of Greyharbor turning out at the ford—no, that hadn't happened yet, but she found she could see it, the way you can sometimes see a thing that hasn't happened because it's already true somewhere upstream.

"Yes," August said.

Sefa didn't smile, exactly. Something in her face settled, like snow finding its angle of rest.

"All right then." She stood, brisk now, decision converted instantly into motion. "We leave tomorrow, before light. Stair of Names. Three days' climb if the weather holds, five if it sulks." She pointed at Tam. "You'll carry the spare rope and stop eating the expedition."

"I would never—"

"You ate the reserve cheese last night. Don't argue with a woman who counts."

Tam had the grace to look mildly ashamed, which for Tam was practically a confession under oath.

*

The rest of the day went to preparation, and preparation in the free valley turned out to be a communal event, the way everything here seemed to be. Someone appeared with dried fish. Someone else with a better tent than theirs, traded straight across for Ede's promise to teach net-mending come spring. A smith reforged August's ice axe head without being asked, humming, and refused payment with a wave that meant *you'll pay it forward or you won't, and either way the axe is sharp now.*

August found herself, absurdly, teaching two teenagers how to coil rope properly behind the granary, and enjoying it, and catching herself enjoying it, and deciding not to examine that too closely.

It was near dusk when Tam found her at the valley's edge, where the path climbed toward the hidden mouth of the Stair. He sat down beside her on the rock wall without asking, which was also becoming a habit.

"You changed your mind in there," he said. "About the courts."

"I did."

"Why?"

August considered giving him the polished answer. Then she looked at him—this skinny, stubborn kid who'd blackmailed his way onto a mountain because the lawful world had bulldozed his home with paperwork in order—and gave him the true one.

"Because I've been waiting six years for an institution to tell me I matter," she said. "And the whole time, the people who actually decided whether I mattered were a boat-mender who fixed my sail cheap, and a boy who wouldn't stay behind, and a liar who came back. Institutions write things down. People decide." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "You were the evidence, Tam. All along."

He went pink to the ears and examined the horizon very intently, and said, gruffly, "That's the nicest thing anybody's said to me and also you still owe me for the rope."

"Extortionist."

"Apprentice extortionist. Get it right."

They sat while the light went amber and then blue. Below them the valley lit its lamps one by one, windows warming against the dark, and August thought: *this is what the law was trying to be, before it got big enough to forget.*

She was still thinking it when Ede came up the path at a jog, which was wrong, because Ede did not jog, Ede had not jogged since the ribs, and the expression on his face emptied her thoughts entirely.

"August." He was breathing hard. "Scout came down from the north col. Riders on the far ridge. Grey coats." He swallowed. "A lot of them. And they're not searching—they're *waiting*. Like they know exactly where the door is."

August stood slowly, the cold coming up through her boots, and looked back down at the lamplit valley—at the granary, the longhouse, the children, the goat—and understood, with a certainty that arrived whole and terrible, that the Company had never needed to find the hidden valley at all.

They had simply followed the right man in.

Her eyes went to Ede. Ede's eyes were already on hers, and he had gone very pale, and neither of them said the word yet.

But it hung there between them, in the blue dusk, patient as winter:

*Tracker.*
