## 14. Halloway's Rope

Ede Vasq had spent thirty years learning to read weather, and the first thing he read that morning was wrong.

The valley woke soft. Mist off the melt-stream, woodsmoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys, somebody down by the terraces already arguing cheerfully about drainage ditches the way mountain people argue about everything—loudly, at length, and with no intention of changing their minds. It was the kind of morning that made a man believe he'd earned his rest.

Which was exactly why Ede didn't trust it.

He stood at the edge of Sefa's garden with his hands in his pockets and let himself feel it: a prickle along the back of the neck like a hair out of place. Not wind. Not birdsong gone quiet—though now that he listened, the birds were quieter than they should be on the east slope.

"Tam," he said, without turning around. The boy had been shadowing him since dawn, ostensibly to learn snare-setting, actually because Tam considered sleep a personal insult. "Go fetch August."

"I'm not done with the—"

"Now, lad. Walk, don't run. If you run, everyone watches you, and I want nobody watching anything."

Something in Ede's voice must have landed, because Tam went without another word, moving quick and casual through the bean rows like a boy who'd been told a secret worth keeping.

Ede turned slowly, scanning the eastern ridge where the Stair of Names disappeared into cloud. The tracker. He'd known about it for three days now—the hot ache under his collarbone where Halloway's men had pinned him during the capture, the wound that wouldn't scab properly, too clean at its edges. He'd shown it to Sefa's herb-woman, Maren, who had cut it open and drawn out a sliver of grey metal no bigger than a grass seed, and the two of them had agreed, quietly, not to tell anyone until they knew what it meant.

What it meant was this: either the Company thought the valley was empty and the tracker was dead weight, or the Company knew exactly where they all slept.

The mist on the eastern ridge shifted against the wind.

That was the thing. Mist doesn't move uphill into weather. Men do.

* * *

By the time August reached him, Ede had counted eleven shapes in the fog and didn't like the arithmetic. Eleven was a skirmish party. Twelve would have been an assault. Eleven meant Halloway wanted them frightened before he wanted them broken.

"You felt it too," she said. She'd come with her hair still unbraided from sleep and her father's compass in her hand, and she looked, Ede thought, more like her father every day—which was a cruel thing for his conscience, given how much of her father's ruin sat on Ede's own shoulders.

"Felt it," he confirmed. "How many fighters can the valley raise?"

"Forty, maybe, if you count the quarry folk and old Sefa with his knees." She said it flat, doing sums she hated. "They're farmers, Ede. Half of them came here to never hold a weapon again."

"And the other half?"

"The other half are very angry people who've been waiting years for an excuse." She glanced at him sideways. "You're going to suggest something stupid. I can see it forming. It looks like your face when you eat something you don't want to admit you hate."

"It's not stupid. It's arithmetic." He kept his voice low, steady, the voice he'd used for twenty years talking climbers off ledges. "Eleven men came up the Stair following a signal. The signal is me. They want one thing in this valley, and it isn't grain or goats. I walk out there, I give myself up, they turn around, and nobody burns anything."

August was quiet for a moment. Below them, the valley went on being innocent—a woman beating a rug, two children racing a dog along the irrigation channel, the smell of bread.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the whole—"

"I heard the whole thing the moment you opened your mouth. You think your life is the coin this valley spends. It isn't. And I'll tell you something else, Ede Vasq, since we're being honest at last: if you walk out there to buy forgiveness with your hide, you haven't understood a single thing about penance. Penance is staying. Penance is carrying what you did and building anyway. Dying is just the easy version."

"That's easy for you to say from behind forty farmers."

"It's easy for me to say because I've watched you carry it for six hundred miles and you've gotten good at it." Her jaw set. "You're not walking out there."

And then the granary caught fire.

It started small—a whump of orange light at the valley's north end, where the long timber barn held most of a winter's rye—and then the smoke went up black and greedy, and every innocent sound in the valley curdled at once. The rug-beating stopped. The dog stopped mid-chase. Somewhere a bell began ringing, fast and ragged, the alarm they'd drilled twice and hoped never to use.

"They split their force," Ede breathed. "Two came in from the north while we watched the east. That's why eleven—" He was already running, and August ran beside him, and whatever argument they'd been having was over, because fire doesn't wait for consensus.

* * *

The granary was lost inside ten minutes. That was the ugly truth of it; dry timber, dry grain, a fire set by professionals. What could be saved was saved by people—chains of exiles passing sacks and tools and one extremely indignant cat out the north door while the south end roared. Ede hauled timber off a collapsing wall until his palms smoked through his gloves, and Tam appeared at his elbow with wet sacking over his face, fourteen years old and furious in a way Ede recognized from mirrors.

"They fired it on purpose," Tam shouted over the crackle. "There wasn't even anyone in it! It's just—it's just to hurt us!"

"Yes," Ede said, because lying to the boy would have been worse than the fire.

By noon the flames were down to sullen smoke and the skirmish was down to its ledger: no dead, miraculously—two exiles burned, one fixer unconscious in Sefa's cellar with a shovel-shaped argument on his forehead—and a granary that had stood sixty years now a black ribcage against the sky. The whole valley stood in the ash-melt looking at it. Nobody spoke. Grief, Ede had learned, sounds like silence when a community does it right.

That was when the fixers' sergeant walked out onto the terrace wall with his hands empty and his voice pitched to carry.

"Vasq!" he called. "Warden Halloway extends terms. The man who carries the beacon walks down to us by sundown, and the Warden's writ ends at this ridgeline. Refuse, and we come back with thirty, then sixty, then as many as it takes. The Warden is patient. Granaries are flammable. Your call."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Ede looked at the burnt granary. He looked at the faces of the exiles—Sefa leaning on his stick, Maren with bloodied bandage-wraps on her hands, the quarry families, the blacklisted surveyors, all of them looking anywhere but at him, because they were too decent to look at him while hoping.

He looked at August, who was already shaking her head.

"Sergeant," Ede called back, stepping forward, and his heart was calm in a way that frightened him more than any fear could have. "Tell the Warden I accept. Sundown. Alone, unarmed, at the foot of the Stair."

"Ede—" August's voice cracked across the terrace like a whip.

"—and tell him," Ede went on, louder, "that if one more roof burns in this valley after I'm in his custody, the beacon stops mattering, because I'll be dead of natural causes and he'll have gained nothing but a corpse and a reputation. The Warden understands leverage. Make sure he understands this kind."

The sergeant studied him a long moment, then nodded once and dropped back below the wall.

* * *

They argued for four hours. Ede would remember that afterward with something close to fondness—the sheer quality of the arguing. Sefa invoked valley law, which apparently had seventeen provisions about hostages. Maren threatened to sedate him, which he believed. Tam tried bribery, offering to go instead, which broke Ede's heart so cleanly he had to leave the room.

But it was August who cornered him at dusk behind the granary ruins, and August who had stopped shouting and gone quiet, which was infinitely worse.

"You know what my father used to say about rope?" she asked.

Ede kept coiling the line in his hands. "I expect you're going to tell me."

"He said rope is honest. It only holds what it's tied to. Tie it to nothing, it holds nothing. Tie it to a falling man, it holds the fall." She stepped closer. "You're trying to tie yourself to our fall, Ede. But that's not how rope works. You go down there, and Halloway gets his prisoner, and then he gets his intelligence, and then he gets the map, and then he gets the source, and the only thing your sacrifice bought is the pleasure of watching us lose slower."

"And if I stay, he burns the valley house by house."

"Then we fight, and some of us die, and it costs what it costs—but it buys something. Your way buys nothing. It's not penance, it's escape. You'd finally get to stop feeling guilty, and you'd call it justice." Her voice gentled by a degree that hit harder than the shouting had. "I testified against you in my head for years, you know. After the blacklist. Blamed you for every door that closed. And then I learned what coercion does to testimony, and I learned what you've been doing since—every mile upstream, every debt paid forward—and I forgave you somewhere around Marrowfen without telling you, because you were insufferable enough already."

Ede's throat worked. The coil of rope slipped from his hands.

"So no," August said. "You're not walking down that hill. Not because your life is worth more than theirs—it isn't, none of ours are, that's rather the point of this valley—but because we don't pay debts to monsters, and we don't let our people volunteer for slaughter to soothe a conscience that's already been forgiven." She picked up the rope, pressed it back into his hands, and closed his fingers over it. "Tie it to something that holds."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Down in the valley, someone had begun singing—one of the quarry work-songs, low and unhurried, the kind sung to keep rhythm while rebuilding. Others joined. The sound rose off the burnt granary like heat.

"All right," Ede said finally, hoarse. "All right. Then we need a better plan than dying nobly, and I have exactly one idea, and you're going to hate it."

"Those are usually your best ones."

"The tracker's out of my shoulder, but Halloway doesn't know that. Which means the beacon still works—for whoever carries it." He met her eyes. "We don't send me down the hill. We send the signal. We make Halloway chase a lie all night through country we know and he doesn't, and while his thirty men are chasing a dead man's heartbeat up a goat track, this valley rebuilds its granary, moves its stores, and disappears."

August stared at him. Then, slowly, terribly, she smiled.

"That's a horrible plan," she said. "It uses you as bait, it risks everything, and it's built entirely on the enemy's arrogance."

"I told you you'd hate it."

"I love it." She was already turning toward the settlement, raising her voice. "Tam! Sefa! Get everyone who can walk—we're going to build a false Ede!"

From the shadows by the well, Tam's head popped up, eyes alight with the particular joy of a fourteen-year-old offered legitimate mischief. "A false what?"

"A decoy," Ede sighed. "God help us all."

* * *

They built it in an hour: Ede's coat, Ede's boots, a pack frame padded to the size of a broad-shouldered man, and at its center, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped where a heart would be, the tracker itself—Maren had boiled it clean, and it still pulsed its little grey signal faithfully, a dead man's heartbeat singing to the dark. Two of the quarry youths volunteered to carry it up the goat track eastward, the one that ended in scree and a hundred-foot drop, the one no sane pursuer would follow past midnight.

At full dark, the decoy went up the hill, and the beacon went with it, and somewhere below the ridgeline Ede imagined Halloway's fixers reading their instruments and grinning at each other: *the traitor's running.*

The valley, meanwhile, performed its own miracle. By lantern-light and moonlight, every sack of grain, every barrel, every seed store moved into the limestone caves above the terraces that Sefa's people had stocked against exactly this kind of winter. Children carried lamp-oil. Old men carried nails. The singing never stopped—work-song after work-song, verse after verse, loud enough to cover the creak of cartwheels, and Ede, hauling his third barrel up the cave path, found Tam beside him under a sack nearly his own size.

"You know what I figured out?" the boy panted.

"Enlighten me."

"Halloway thinks the granary burning broke us. You can hear it in how his man talked—like we'd fold." Tam grinned in the lantern-glow, soot-striped and fierce. "But he burned a building. We still got the grain, the valley, and each other. He spent fire and got ash. That's bad arithmetic. August says bad arithmetic loses wars."

"August," Ede said, shifting the barrel higher, "says a great deal, and the terrifying thing is she's usually right." He paused at the cave mouth and looked back down at the valley—smoke still rising, lanterns strung along the terraces like a constellation brought down to earth, the whole blacklisted world humming with stubborn life. Once, this view would have meant nothing to him but altitude. Now it caught in his chest like a hook.

Then the eastern scree lit up with torches—thirty of them, maybe more, swarming up the goat track after a coat stuffed with straw and a dead man's heartbeat—and a horn sounded from the ridge, and a second horn answered it from the far side of the valley, from the west, where there should have been no one at all.

Ede went cold. The decoy was east. The horns were west.

"They split again," he said. "Or—" He grabbed Tam's shoulder. "Get August. Get Sefa. Wake the caves. Whatever's coming, it isn't coming up the Stair—it's already inside the ridgeline."

Below them, faint on the night wind, came the sound of boots on stone, and a voice Ede knew from a high-altitude prison camp calling orders in a tone he had hoped never to hear again:

Halloway was already in the valley.
