## 15. The Stair of Names

Tam had decided, somewhere around the third day in the free valley, that Sefa was the oldest person alive.

Not old the way the harbormaster was old — bent and complaining and smelling of tar. Old the way stone was old. Sefa moved like something that had been standing a long time and intended to keep standing, and when she laughed, which she did more than Tam expected from a legend, it came out of her like weather off a ridge: sudden, loud, and over before you'd properly braced for it.

"You walk like your boots are arguing with you," she told him on the morning they left.

"They are," Tam said. "I'm winning."

"Good lad." She looked him up and down, then August, then Ede, whose ribs were still strapped from Halloway's camp and who had been pretending for two days that he wasn't in pain. "We climb today. Not the way the Company climbs — their routes go where the money is. We go where the dead went."

Nobody asked what she meant by that. Tam suspected nobody in the valley ever did. Questions here got answered eventually, but only after you'd earned the answer by carrying something heavy or standing watch in the cold, which Tam privately thought was a very fair system and one he intended to steal for any crew he ever ran.

They left before dawn, four of them plus two of Sefa's people — a quiet woman called Bren who carried half her weight in rope, and a boy of maybe sixteen named Osk who kept looking at Ede like Ede was a story he'd been told at bedtime and hadn't believed. The valley gave them provisions wrapped in waxed cloth, and an old woman pressed a charm of knotted wool into Tam's hand and said, "For the Stair," and would not explain further no matter how he wheedled.

The route out of the valley went up through a hanging wood, birch giving way to pine giving way to nothing but rock and lichen, and then the trees stopped altogether as if a line had been drawn and everyone had agreed to respect it. Beyond the treeline the world went grey and enormous, and Tam felt the familiar smallness come over him, the feeling he'd first had at his family's quarry — that he was a mite on the skin of something vast and patient.

Sefa stopped at the base of a wall that rose out of the scree like the side of a drowned ship.

"Here," she said. "Take off your gloves."

August frowned. "It's freezing."

"Take them off anyway. You'll want your fingers for this."

* * *

The wall was not a wall. That was the first thing Tam understood, once he got close enough to touch it.

Steps had been cut into the rock — narrow ones, shallow-treaded, the kind you'd only trust if you had no other choice — and every single step bore a name.

Not scratched. Carved. Cut deep and clean with a chisel hand, letter by letter, the way his father had cut inscriptions into lintels and grave markers. Tam knew good stone work the way other people knew faces, and this was *good*. This was work done slowly, by people who knew they might not live to finish, and who did it anyway.

He crouched over the first step and read it aloud without meaning to.

"Maren Ostrova."

"Read them all," Sefa said quietly. "That's what they're for. A name you don't say is a name the mountain keeps instead of you."

So they climbed the Stair saying names. It was slow going — each step required you to stop, find the letters under the frost, speak them — and Tam realized after twenty steps that this was deliberate too. You could not hurry up this route. You could not arrive at the top breathless and unthinking. The Stair made you carry the dead at exactly the pace the dead deserved.

"Milo Ferren. Ansa Ferren. Dov Ferren—" He stopped. "A whole family."

"The Founding Accord took whole families," Sefa said. Her voice didn't waver, but her hand, resting on the rock, had gone white-knuckled. "Ninety-one names on the lower steps. The ones who died at the source itself. Higher up, there are more — the ones who died after, resisting, or hiding people, or just being inconvenient. The Company calls all of it 'the settlement period.' We call it what it is."

Ede climbed behind Tam, and Tam heard him saying every name too, low and steady, like a man paying out rope one arm-length at a time. Once, on a step reading *Ilse Vann*, Ede's voice caught and he had to start again, and nobody pretended not to notice, and nobody said anything either.

Bren and Osk fell silent entirely except for the names. Even the wind seemed to lower itself.

And August — Tam kept glancing back at her because she was climbing strangely. Slowly even for the Stair. Her bare fingers traced the carved letters of each name before she spoke it, following the grooves the way you'd follow a scar, and her face had gone somewhere Tam couldn't read. Somewhere far above the weather.

* * *

Halfway up, the route traversed sideways across a face, and the steps became a ledge, and the ledge became something else: a gallery. The rock here was smoother, and the carvings weren't just names anymore. There were figures — climbers roped together, a woman holding a lantern, hands passing something along a chain of arms. And beneath the figures, longer inscriptions, worn nearly smooth by a century of wind.

Sefa let them rest there, backs to the rock, feet dangling over a drop that made Tam's stomach file a formal complaint.

"My grandmother's grandmother cut some of these," Sefa said. "There was a guild, before the Company. Free climbers. They ran the high routes for trade and rescue and because, if you asked them, the mountain belonged to whoever could walk it gently. When the Meridian Company came upriver with its ledgers and its guns, the guild refused to sign. So the Company solved them the way the Company solves everything." She tapped the rock beside her. "But the guild had apprentices everywhere, and the apprentices remembered, and they started cutting names. First the dead. Then the truth. Every generation added a little. That's why the Company has never found this route — it isn't on any map because it's still being written."

Osk spoke for the first time in hours. "My mother cut three names. She made me practice my letters on the practice boulder for a year before she'd let me touch the Stair."

"You'll add yours someday," Bren said.

"When?"

"When you've earned a name worth cutting," Sefa said, and grinned so suddenly that Osk went red and Tam laughed despite himself, and the laugh echoed off the face and felt, briefly, like something the mountain permitted.

They ate cold oatcakes and snow-melt. Ede dozed against the rock, finally surrendering to his ribs. And Tam found himself sitting next to August, who was staring at the gallery of figures — specifically at the woman with the lantern, cut larger than the others, standing at the head of the chain of hands.

"She's the first," Sefa said, settling down across from them. "The one who started the carving. Nobody living knows her real name anymore. We call her the Lamplighter."

Tam watched August's face do something complicated.

"Can I ask you something," Tam said, because he was fourteen and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving things alone, "about your father? You never talk about him except when you're talking about climbing."

August was quiet long enough that Tam thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "He taught me knots before I could write. He taught me that a rope is a promise you make to someone else's life. He taught me to read weather and stone and people, in that order." She turned her hand over, looking at the palm as if the calluses were text. "He never taught me anything about any of this." She nodded at the gallery. "I used to think that was because there wasn't anything to teach."

"And now?" Sefa asked.

"Now I think I wasn't listening."

* * *

The last third of the Stair was the hardest — steeper, colder, the steps smaller, the names higher up the wall where frost clung longest. Tam's fingers went numb twice and he had to warm them in his armpits, hating himself for it, because stopping felt like disrespecting the names. On a step reading *Petra Ilves*, he slipped on verglas and Ede's hand shot out and held his shoulder until he steadied, and neither of them mentioned it, and it became one of those things between them that didn't need mentioning anymore.

Near the top, the character of the carvings changed again. The names of the dead grew fewer. In their place appeared lists of another kind — shorter lines, deeper cuts, names followed by small marks like tally strokes.

"What are these?" Tam asked.

Sefa had been waiting for the question. He could tell by the way she stopped climbing and turned around on the step below him, her weathered face tilted up.

"Those are the carvers," she said. "Every name on the Stair was cut by someone, and the guild's rule was that the cutter signs their work. Not for glory. For accountability. If you carve a man's name into a mountain, you're swearing you verified it — that he lived, that he died, that he mattered. Your name stands surety for theirs."

She pointed with her chin. "Read the tallies. Each mark is a name that carver confirmed."

Tam read. *Halla Voss, seven marks. Jem Arden, twelve marks. Corin Gideon—*

He stopped.

The letters were different from the others. Not better — though they were better, actually, cleaner, a chisel-hand with real confidence — but *familiar*. Tam had spent six years cutting stone beside a master, and hands had signatures the way faces did. He knew this hand. He'd seen its descendants in August's careful rope coils, in the precise way she pitched camp, in everything she did that was measured twice before done once.

"Ambrose Gideon," he read aloud. "Fourteen marks."

Behind him, he heard August stop breathing.

She came up past him — she didn't use the steps, she climbed the rock directly, which Tam would have found alarming if he'd had attention to spare — and put her bare hand flat on the carving. The wind pulled at her hair. Below them the free valley lay in its cradle of ridges, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, and above them the ice fields went up forever, and August stood between them with her hand on her father's name and said nothing at all.

"He was guild," Sefa said softly. "We wondered. When you came down out of the pass with his compass, we wondered. Ambrose Gideon cut fourteen names on this Stair, and every one of them checks true — we've verified them across three generations. He was the last carver before the Company sealed the high country. Then he went downriver, married, raised a child, and never spoke of it, apparently." Her eyes glinted. "Until he taught that child to read stone without knowing he was doing it."

"My father was a surveyor," August said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like ground after frost.

"He was a surveyor the way a locked door is a piece of wood." Sefa climbed up beside her. "He surveyed for the guild. He mapped the routes the Company wanted erased. And when they ordered him to falsify the source records — well. You know what he did instead. There's a letter in the vault at the top of this route that says it better than I can. But the Stair says it too." She touched the tallies, one, two, three. "Fourteen people, Ambrose Gideon swore for. Fourteen names he stood surety on. That's not a surveyor. That's a keeper."

Tam watched August's shoulders. He'd spent enough months around her now to read her the way she read weather, and what he saw was a woman watching a door open onto a room she'd lived beside her whole life without knowing it existed. Everything she was — the double-checking, the rope-promise, the stubbornness about names, the way she'd refused to sell the map even when it might have bought back her reputation — none of it was new. It was inherited. It had just taken the mountain this long to show her the will.

"He never told me," she said finally. "All those years. He let me think he was just... a man who liked high places."

"Maybe he was waiting to see if you'd find it yourself," Tam said.

Both women looked at him. He shrugged, ears going hot. "That's what my master did. Never told me I was ready for my own chisel. Just waited for me to pick it up. Said if I needed telling, I wasn't ready, and if I picked it up without asking, I already knew." He nodded at the carving. "You picked it up."

August made a sound that was half laugh and half something else entirely, and pressed her forehead briefly against the cold stone, right beside her father's name, the way you'd press your forehead against a beloved's shoulder.

* * *

They reached the top of the Stair at late afternoon, where the carved route ended at a shelf of granite facing the ice fields — and beyond the ice fields, catching the low sun like a blade drawn halfway from its sheath, the summit massif that the maps called the Headwater Crown and the exiles called, simply, home's roof.

The final step was blank.

Unmarked granite, smooth and waiting, and Tam understood it the way he understood stone, instantly and completely. It was for the next name. It had always been for the next name. The Stair wasn't finished because the truth wasn't finished, because somewhere the Company was still making dead people and somebody, someday, would need to stand here with a chisel and swear for them.

Sefa unpacked a bundle and handed August something wrapped in oilcloth. Inside lay a chisel — old, well-kept, edge freshly dressed — and a mallet with a handle worn concave by generations of grips.

"The guild passes these at the top," Sefa said. "Whoever inherits the keeping takes the tools. We'd assumed it would be me, eventually. But the Stair has opinions." She nodded at the carving forty steps below. "Ambrose Gideon's line isn't finished. It's just been waiting."

August took the chisel the way Tam had seen her take a rope — testing its weight, checking its edge, measuring twice. Her hands weren't shaking. Tam thought they should be. His would have been.

"I don't know the rites," she said.

"There aren't any. There's just the rule: verify, carve, stand surety." Sefa smiled, and for once it wasn't weather off a ridge; it was something gentler. "You've been doing it your whole life, girl. Now you'll do it with better tools."

They made camp on the shelf, the last flat ground before the ice. Osk built the fire low and shielded. Bren set the watch. Ede lowered himself down with a grunt and said, "Tomorrow, then," in the voice of a man who fully intended to see tomorrow and had learned not to take it for granted.

Tam sat close to the fire, turning the knotted-wool charm the old woman had given him. He thought he understood it now. Knots for the names. A rope is a promise. A stair is a ledger. Everything on this mountain was the same sentence written in different materials, and he'd been learning to read it since he was eight years old at his father's quarry, and the Company had burned the quarry thinking fire erased things, and here he was, proof it didn't.

Across the fire, August sat apart, the compass open in her palm. Even in the dark Tam could see it wasn't pointing north. It was pointing up — up the ice, toward the Crown, toward whatever waited at the river's forbidden source. Toward the vault, and the Accord, and the letter his father had written that said it better than anyone could.

Then the needle shivered.

Tam saw it — a tiny stutter, a tremor in the brass card, like a pulse skipping. August saw it too. She went very still, the way she went still before bad weather, and raised her head slowly, and looked not at the compass but past it, out into the darkening ice fields where the light was failing.

Far below and eastward — impossibly far, a day's hard march at least — a pinpoint of light flickered against the glacier. Steady. Deliberate. Repeating.

Signal mirror. Someone down there was flashing a message up the mountain, and the compass in August's hand was trembling toward it like a dog scenting its master's killer.

"That's not the valley's relay," Sefa said quietly. "Wrong bearing."

August closed the compass. When she spoke, her voice was the flat, measured voice Tam had learned meant danger counted and options weighed.

"Halloway's found the route."

The fire popped. Above them the blank step waited for its name, and below them, coming up through the dark, the mountain's newest dead began their climb.
