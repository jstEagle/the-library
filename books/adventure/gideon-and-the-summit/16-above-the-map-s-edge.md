## 16. Above the Map's Edge

The map ended at a fold of paper no wider than August's thumb.

She had studied it by firelight a dozen times since the Stair of Names—Sefa's careful tracing of the Vell from Greyharbor to its glacial cradle, every bend annotated in her father's small, patient hand. And then, two-thirds of the way up the icefield, the annotations simply stopped. No contour lines. No route notes. Just blank vellum and, pressed into the corner as if in apology, a single word: *Gideon.*

"Your father drew maps for a living," Sefa had said that morning, rolling the chart with something like reverence. "He didn't stop drawing. He stopped *showing*."

Now August stood where the showing stopped, boots on blue ice that no survey had ever blessed, and understood what Sefa meant.

Above them the glacier rose in a wall of fractured seracs, pale green in the dawn light like sea glass the size of houses. The wind came off the summit ridge in long, even breaths, carrying the smell of nothing at all—that high-altitude emptiness that always made her feel the world had been scrubbed clean of witnesses.

"Well," said Tam, squinting up at it. "That looks climbable."

"That looks," said Ede, "like a graveyard with ambitions."

"You're both wrong," August said. "It looks like my father's handwriting." She tapped the ice with her axe. "See how the crevasse field runs diagonal, left to right? He always routed diagonals. Said straight lines were for men in a hurry to die."

Tam looked at her sideways. "You can read a mountain like a letter?"

"I can read *this* mountain like a letter. Because he taught me on mountains exactly this rude." She shouldered her pack before the feeling in her chest could become visible. "Rope up. We're past the map's edge now. Everything above this line is memory and guesswork."

"And the compass?" Ede asked quietly.

August took it out. The brass was warm from her pocket despite the cold—the old man's trick, she'd thought, some wax lining in the case—and the needle swung once, twice, and settled. Not north. It hadn't pointed north since they'd passed the last marked cairn yesterday. It pointed up and slightly east, into the serac wall, steady as a plumb line.

"It points," she said, "wherever it damn well pleases."

* * *

They climbed through the morning in the diagonal her father would have chosen, and it held. That was the thing she couldn't shake: it *held*. Every guess she made from his habits turned out to be his actual decision, thirty years old and still load-bearing. A snow ramp where he'd have trusted one. A rock band bypassed on the left because he hated chockstones. Twice she found drilled holes in the ice, ancient anchors rusted to red ghosts, and each time Tam whooped like they'd struck silver.

"He was *here*," the boy said, hanging off an anchor with the fearless stupidity of fourteen. "He actually stood here!"

"People stood here," August corrected. "Then they climbed on and didn't come back down this way, or we'd have their names."

"Maybe they did come back. Maybe they just didn't write it down." Tam patted the ice almost affectionately. "Not everything important gets written down. You told me that. About the ledger."

August didn't answer. The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, and she could feel it there the way you feel a bruise—constantly, and more when you pressed.

Ede climbed behind her all morning without complaint, which was itself remarkable; Ede complaining about altitude was as reliable as weather. Instead he watched her placements with an expression she was learning to translate. It wasn't doubt. It was a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the sum of.

At midday they stopped in the lee of a serac and ate cold oats and harder bread. Tam fell asleep between bites, sitting up, which made Ede laugh—a real laugh, rusty, surprised out of him.

"He sleeps like his quarry collapsed under him," Ede said. "Anywhere, any surface, gone."

"He sleeps like someone who spent years waiting for adults to stop arguing about whose fault he was," August said. "You sleep fast when nobody's watching out for you. You learn to bank it."

Ede was quiet a moment. Then: "I've been meaning to ask. The compass. When did you first notice?"

"Last night. It stopped agreeing with the stars."

"And you didn't say anything."

"I wanted to be sure I wasn't imagining it." She turned the instrument over in her gloved hands. The engraving caught the light—her father's initials, *A.J.*, and beneath them, so fine she'd needed Tam's young eyes to find it, a second line she'd never noticed in all her years of owning the thing:

*Tuned at the source.*

She showed Ede. He read it twice.

"Tuned," he repeated. "Like an instrument."

"Like an instrument," August agreed. Her voice came out strange. "It doesn't point north. It points *home*. To wherever he tuned it. Which means—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence made it true, and true things on uncharted ice deserved care.

"Which means your father walked to the source of the Vell," Ede finished for her, gently, "and came back down. And left you a needle that remembers the way."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of dogs.

* * *

They moved after that. Nobody had to say move; the sound did the saying. Halloway's party had made the Whitefall Crossing behind them faster than Sefa's estimates, which meant either better climbers than expected or fewer scruples about losses. With Halloway, August suspected, the two qualities were identical.

The afternoon was a blur of pitch and kick, the serac wall leaning over them like a wave deciding. Tam, awake now and scared in the way that made him talk, kept up a running commentary.

"Do you think the dogs mind the cold? Do dogs get frostbite? Ede, do dogs—"

"Eat," said Ede.

"—get frostbite on their *noses*, is my actual question—"

"Then yes," Ede said, "and eat."

August led. That was the arrangement now, unspoken since the ice bridge at Whitefall: when the ground got honest about wanting to kill them, she went first. Not because Ede couldn't—he'd been a ranger twenty years—but because something had shifted on the snow bridge, some weight transferred, and they'd all felt it settle. Trust, maybe. Or just the correct distribution of terror.

Near dusk the angle eased and they gained a shoulder of wind-packed snow below the final headwall, and there—impossibly, absurdly—they found a cairn.

Not one of the Company's survey cairns, square and stamped. This one was a heap of flat stones stacked with a mason's eye, and on the topmost stone, scratched deep enough to outlast the century: a name. *M. Joubert.* And beside it, smaller, in the same hand: *For whoever comes after. The route is good. The view is earned.*

Tam sat down hard in the snow.

"He knew," the boy whispered. "He knew people would come after. He built the road before anyone asked for it."

August knelt and put her bare hand, briefly, on the stone. It was bitter cold and she didn't care. Thirty years ago her father had stood on this exact shoulder, looked at this exact headwall, and thought not of himself but of some hypothetical stranger—some daughter, though he couldn't have known, though he could only have *hoped*—who would need a kind word at altitude.

"The mountain isn't testing us," she heard herself say. "I had it wrong. It's not a test. It's a correspondence. He wrote ahead. We're just... answering."

"Poetic," said Ede, scanning the slope below with his battered spyglass. "Also, we have company. Six figures, two dogs, moving well. They'll reach the cairn by midnight if they push."

"Then we don't sleep here."

"We can't climb the headwall in the dark, August. Even you."

"We can climb it in the dark *slowly*," she said, "which is how all the best climbing gets done anyway. Rope length by rope length. Tam belays. You anchor. I lead by feel and by needle." She held up the compass. Its glow—inexplicable, faintly green, another of her father's secrets—pointed unerringly into the black face above them. "He left me a lamp that points home. Be rude not to use it."

* * *

Night climbing is a conversation conducted entirely in verbs. *Tight. Slack. Up. Watch.* August had learned it young, on her father's rope, and her body remembered the grammar even when her nerves forgot the vocabulary. The headwall rose in three great pitches—ice, then mixed rock furred with rime, then ice again—and she led all of them by the green whisper of the needle and the memory of a man's habits.

Once, halfway up the second pitch, her axe struck hollow ice and the whole sheet shivered. Below her Tam's breath stopped audibly.

"August?"

"Still here."

"The ice sounded—"

"I know what it sounded like. Climb like it's asleep. Don't wake it."

They woke it anyway, near the top of the third pitch, because the mountain keeps its own schedule. A serac the size of a chapel calved somewhere off to their left—not on their line, but close enough that the report rolled across the icefield like thunder clearing its throat, and the whole face shed its loose skin in a long white sigh. August flattened herself against the slope and waited out the spindrift, eyes shut, listening to the mountain exhale.

When she opened them, the clouds had torn open too, and the sky above the summit ridge was full of stars, and the compass needle in her breast pocket was pulling against her ribs like a hooked fish.

"There," she breathed. Not to Tam, not to Ede. To the dark, to the man in the stones. *I see it.*

Because she did see it. Above the last pitch, where the map ran out and the stars began, the glacier gathered itself into a broad saddle—and in the saddle, catching starlight like a held breath, was a wall of ice that was not glacier-blue but a deeper, older color. Green-black. Glassy. Standing alone in the saddle like a door.

The needle pointed straight through it.

* * *

They topped out at what Tam solemnly declared was "either very late or very early," and collapsed into the lee of the saddle's rim. Below and behind them, torchlight crawled up the route they'd climbed—Halloway's party, six hours back, dogged as debt. They'd seen the calved serac's debris field. They were coming anyway.

"They'll reach the saddle by midmorning," Ede said. He was favoring his ribs where the fixers had worked him over at the high camp, and his voice had gravel in it. "We rest two hours, no more. Then whatever's behind that ice wall, we're through it before they arrive."

"Through it," August repeated. She stood at the edge of the saddle and looked at the green-black wall. Up close it resolved into something worse and better than ice: it was *clear*. Frozen meltwater, centuries thick, laid down in perfect sheets, and deep inside it—far down, at the limit of sight—shapes. Angular. Deliberate. Man-made.

A vault. Her father had frozen a door into the mountain, and the mountain had kept it shut, and the key was a brass compass tuned like a song.

"August." Tam had come to stand beside her, small and wiry and utterly unafraid in the way that had gotten him into her expedition in the first place. "What happens when we open it? What's actually in there?"

"The Founding Accord," she said. "The massacre orders. My father's refusal." She said it evenly, because Tam deserved evenness. "Enough truth to burn a company to the waterline."

"And Halloway wants it."

"Halloway wants to own it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

Tam nodded slowly, processing. Then, with the terrible practicality of the young: "And when he gets here—and he will—what do we do? Fight? Run? Bargain?"

August looked at the wall of ancient ice. At the shapes sleeping inside it. At the compass in her hand, pointing home to a home she'd never seen, built by a man who'd died believing she might follow.

"We decide," she said, "before he arrives. That's the only advantage left to us—we get to choose first." She turned to Ede, whose face in the starlight was old and tired and steady. "Two hours' rest. Then we cut our way into my father's vault, and whatever we find in there—"

The compass needle shivered. Once. Twice. And settled, quivering, not toward the wall anymore—

—but toward the torches climbing the glacier below.

August stared at it. Then at Ede. Then, slowly, understanding arrived like cold water: the needle didn't point to a place. It had never pointed to a place.

It pointed to the source. And the source, the true source of the Vell, the spring her father had tuned his compass upon—

Was not behind the ice wall at all.

It was coming up the mountain to meet them, wearing Company grey, and it had her father's other secret in its pocket.

"Change of plan," August said quietly. "Wake up, Tam. We need to talk about what Halloway's carrying."
