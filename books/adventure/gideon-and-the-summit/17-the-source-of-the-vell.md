## 17. The Source of the Vell

The compass stopped pretending to be a compass at dawn.

August had known it for days, of course—had felt the little brass needle straining against its pivot like a dog against a leash, dragging east-northeast when east-northeast had nothing to do with north at all. But watching it now, held flat on her palm above the ice, she felt the last of her doubt fall away the way a climber's last doubt falls away at the top of a pitch: not with triumph, but with a strange, hollow quiet. There was no more map after this. There was only what her father had tuned this instrument to find.

"It's pulling," Tam said. He crouched beside her, breath fogging, eyes bright with the particular fever of a boy who had not yet learned what mountains charge for curiosity. "It's been pulling all night. Look—it's trembling."

"Everything trembles at this altitude," Ede said from behind them. He said it mildly, but August heard the weight in it. Three days since the skirmish at the valley's mouth. Three days since the granary burned and Ede had stood in front of Halloway's fixers with his arms out and offered himself like a coin on a counter, and August had put a gun in the arithmetic and refused the trade. His ribs were strapped. He climbed anyway. He climbed the way penitents climb, which is to say without complaint and without mercy toward himself.

"It's trembling *toward* something," Tam insisted.

August closed her hand over the compass. "Then let's go meet it."

The source of the Vell was not what the maps said. The maps—the Company's maps, the crown's maps, the schoolroom maps that hung in every classroom in Greyharbor with the river drawn as a tidy blue thread running from a tidy white star—showed a spring, a seep, a modest beginning. What August found instead, after six hours of climbing a glacier that no map admitted existed, was a wall of blue ice perhaps two hundred feet high, curved like the inside of a cupped hand, and set into its face, half-buried in a century of snowfall, a door.

Not a natural door. A door with hinges. A door with a seal.

The Meridian Company's seal, pressed into a disc of lead the size of a dinner plate, the mountain-and-star crest worn smooth but still legible, still smug, after all these years.

"Your father's route came up the east rib," Ede said quietly. He had gone very still, the way he did when he was feeling something too large to move normally with. "Sefa's drawings showed it. I thought he was being fanciful. A vault. Who builds a vault into a glacier?"

"Someone who wanted the cold to keep his secrets," August said. "And wanted the mountain to decide who got to knock."

She found the route in an hour. Of course she did—that was the joke of it, the warm and terrible joke. Her father had cut holds into the ice wall with the same hand that had taught her to cut holds, the same economical strokes, the same little doubled notch he always left at the top of a sequence like a signature at the bottom of a letter. She climbed his route the way you'd read a letter from the dead, and every hold was a sentence, and by the time she swung onto the ledge before the door she was crying without any of the drama of crying, just water on her face in the wind, an inconvenience, a climate.

The lock was a puzzle of the old surveyor's kind: three brass wheels, each stamped with letters. She looked at them a long time.

Then she laughed, one short bark that startled the others on the rope below.

"What?" Tam called up.

"He spelled it," she said. "He *spelled* it." Her fingers moved. G-I-D-E. The lock opened like a held breath released.

* * *

The vault was small—smaller than her cottage in Greyharbor, smaller than Corvin's boatshed where she'd mended nets and nursed her grudge. But it had been built by people who understood what a room was *for*. Dry-stone walls behind the ice. Cedar shelving, still fragrant after forty years. And on those shelves, wrapped in waxed canvas and sealed with lead, the archive of the Meridian Company's founding.

August unwrapped the first bundle with hands that would not entirely steady.

The Founding Accord itself came first. She had imagined a document—she had spent the whole climb imagining it—and it was a document, but it was also a confession wearing a contract's clothes. She read the opening clauses aloud because Tam demanded it and because her voice needed something to do besides shake.

*"In consideration of certain lands, waters, and improvements, the parties herein agree that the valley of the Vell, from source to sea, is conveyed entire to the Meridian Company, its heirs and assigns forever—"*

"Forever," Tam repeated. He was fourteen and he said *forever* the way you'd spit.

"Keep reading," Ede said.

She did. And there it was, in the annexes, in the schedules, in the dry little appendix where the bodies were filed. The massacre orders. Signed by the Company's first directors. Dated eleven days before the Accord itself—because the land had to be emptied before it could be *conveyed*, and the herders and quarrymen and freeholders of the upper Vell had declined to be emptied. The orders were brief. Brevity, August thought, was the true signature of atrocity. No one had wasted words. *Clear the valley. Burn the winter stores. Let the river do the rest.* Forty-one names followed, in a clerk's neat hand, under the heading *Losses Sustained by Company Operations.*

"Losses," Tam said. His voice had gone flat and adult. "They wrote *losses*."

"Ledger men," August said. "They put people in the same column as mules."

And then, at the bottom of the second shelf, in a wrapping different from the others—finer, kept with a care that stood out like a lighthouse—she found the letter.

She knew the handwriting before she broke the seal. She had seen it on shopping lists and birthday notes and the margins of her childhood arithmetic exercises: *August, if you must write seven times seven, write it in the snow where I can check it.*

*To whoever finds this, and especially to my daughter, if it is my daughter:*

*I was the surveyor for the Meridian Company, and I am the reason the map of this river is a lie. They asked me to move the source. To place it where the Accord needed it to be, so that the water rights would read clean. I refused. They took my name and gave it to a man who would sign. Everything above the snowline that the Company claims, it claims on my signature, and I never signed it.*

*I have put the true records here, where the river begins, because a lie should be kept next to the thing it lies about. If you are reading this and you are angry—good. Anger is a load-bearing emotion. Build with it.*

*But hear me on one thing. The truth is not a treasure. It is a tool, and tools belong to the hands that use them. Do not let anyone—not the Company, not the crown, not a committee of the righteous—lock this room again. A truth with a lock on it is just a secret with better manners.*

*—Gideon Joubert, surveyor, liar by reputation, honest by stubbornness*

August sat down on the cold stone floor and held the letter and did not read it aloud, and Tam, to his eternal credit, did not ask her to.

* * *

They were still there when the light changed.

It was Ede who noticed—Ede, whose ranger's eye had never fully retired, who read a snowfield the way Corvin read tide tables. He touched August's shoulder and said only, "Company grey, coming up the glacier. Eleven of them. And they're not hurrying, which is worse than if they were."

There was no other way down. August had known that when she chose the vault; her father had chosen this room because it could be defended by one person with patience, and could be besieged by eleven people with nothing. The blue ice wall rose behind them, sheer and beautiful and useless as an escape. The glacier's tongue ran out below in a long white ramp, and up that ramp, small and orderly and patient as a funeral, came the grey coats.

And between them, walking where the walking was easiest, roped wrist to wrist like cargo: Sefa, and old Marta who kept the valley's bees, and a boy of maybe ten whose name August had learned three nights ago over burned bread—Pell, who had shown Tam how the exiles braided rope.

"No," Tam said. It came out of him like a reflex, like a cough. "No, no, no—"

"Steady," Ede said, and his voice was a hand on a shoulder, though he stood ten feet away.

They came up the ramp at the pace of men who had already won. Halloway led them himself, which August should have expected. He was not a man who delegated the moments that mattered to him, and this, she understood with a cold clarity, was the moment that had mattered to him for forty years. He stopped twenty feet from the vault mouth and looked at the door, at the broken seal, at the three of them standing in it like the world's smallest and most ragged garrison.

"Surveyor Joubert," he said. He used the title like a knife used politely, to cut bread at a table where everyone knows it has killed. "Or do you prefer *August*? I confess I've wondered, all these years, which name you grieved for."

"You have children down there roped to your men," August said. "Take them off the rope."

"When we're finished." He folded his hands. He was a compact man, neat as a ledger line, with the terrible unlined face of someone who had never once been surprised by a consequence. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and I want you to understand that I am making it in good faith, because I have admired you since you were a girl, and I say that without flattery."

"I can't wait."

"Your name," Halloway said. "Restored. Not just cleared—*restored*. Warrant of rehabilitation, signed by the Company and countersigned by the crown assizes. Your father's name restored with it, his survey re-entered into the record under his own hand, his honor made a matter of public document. A guide's license, first class, with the Company's patronage behind it—which is to say, clients for life. A house in Greyharbor. Corvin's debt, extinguished. And the boy apprenticed wherever he likes, with a stipend, because I am not a monster, whatever the valley's beekeeper has been telling you."

Tam made a sound beside her, small and furious.

"And in exchange," Halloway went on, as if the boy had not spoken, as if the hostages were furniture, "you give me the Accord, the orders, and the letter. All of it. And the compass, which I confess I have wanted back for a very long time." His eyes went to her pocket, to the small brass weight of it, and something moved in his face that was almost, almost human. "Your father was the best man I ever corrupted and failed to corrupt in the same year. I have missed having his things near me."

August looked at him for a long moment.

"You're offering me everything I've wanted since I was twenty-three years old," she said. "You know that. You've known it since you engineered my blacklisting. You took my name away so that you could be the one to give it back. That was the whole design, wasn't it? Not punishment. *Investment.* You were waiting for me to find this room, because you couldn't find it yourself, and you knew that if I found it, you could finally buy it—with the only currency I'd ever accept."

Halloway smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on boards of directors. "Then we understand each other."

"We understand each other perfectly." August turned and looked at the hostages. Sefa met her eyes and shook her head, slowly, once: *don't*. Marta was watching with the level calm of a woman who had outlived two governments. Little Pell had started, very quietly, to cry, and the man roped to him had put his grey-coated shoulder against the boy's shoulder, the smallest possible kindness, and Halloway had not noticed, because Halloway did not notice kindness; it was not in his column.

"Here is what I see," August said. She did not raise her voice. The glacier did the acoustics for her; the ice carried it the way a church carries a whisper. "I see a man offering me my whole life back. My name. My father's name. The work I loved. And all it costs is a piece of paper that says forty-one people were *losses*—a piece of paper that says the water belongs to him, that the sky above the snowline belongs to him, that every family on this river has been paying rent on their own history for forty years." She took a breath. "And I see a boy who lost his quarry, a beekeeper who lost her valley, a child who is crying because a grown man with a neat face roped him like a mule. And I find—" and here her voice did finally shake, and she let it, because her father had taught her that a guide who hides fear from her party gets everyone killed— "I find that the arithmetic is not difficult at all."

"You will die on this mountain," Halloway said, and the smile was gone, and what was underneath was smaller and older and uglier. "Your name dies with you. Nothing changes. The Company owns the river the day after your funeral, and no one will even know what you refused."

"Then I'll have died owing nothing," August said, "which is more than I can say for you."

She reached into her coat and took out the Founding Accord.

Halloway's men shifted. Somewhere below, a rifle's safety clicked, a small sound, enormous on the ice.

"August," Ede said softly, at her shoulder. Not *don't*. Just her name, the way you say a name when you are standing next to someone at the edge of something.

"I'm not giving it to him," she said. "And I'm not hiding it, either. Hiding it is how we got here. Hiding it is forty years of this." She looked at the hostages, at Sefa, at the boy. "Sefa. The signal mirrors on the east ridge. The relay your people built for the beacon."

Sefa's eyes went wide. Then she smiled—the first thing on this glacier all day that was not grey. "Every station between here and the valley," she said. "And the valley has eyes on every station."

"Read it to them," August said, and held out the Accord to her through the cold bright air, past the rifles, past the neat-faced man who had spent a lifetime buying things that were never for sale. "Read it where everyone can see. And then—"

She looked at the document in Sefa's hands. Forty-one names. One river. One terrible, patient lie, kept all these years next to the thing it lied about, waiting in the cold for someone stubborn enough to climb to it.

"Then we'll decide together what a truth is worth," August said, "when nobody gets to own it."

Halloway took one step forward onto the ice, and the glacier, which had been listening all along, chose that moment to speak.

The crack ran under their feet like a sentence being written.
