## 18. Burn It Where They Can See

The vault was colder than grief and twice as patient.

August stood before the sealed archive with the brass compass in her palm, its needle no longer trembling but fixed—pointing not north, not south, but straight at the seam of ice where her father had hidden the truth of the Meridian Company forty years ago. The needle had never pointed north. It had been pointing here all along, through every market stall and harbor fog and sleepless night in Greyharbor, waiting for her to catch up.

"Well," said Tam, breath steaming, "that's either very moving or very creepy."

"Both," said Ede. He was leaning against the ice wall, ribs strapped, one eye still swollen from Halloway's camp, and he grinned anyway. "Most of your father's work was both."

August didn't answer. She pressed her bare hand against the ice seam, and it gave—not all at once, but grudgingly, the way old doors do when they've been waiting a long time for someone specific to knock. A slab the size of a coffin lid swung inward on a channel her father must have cut and flooded and let freeze again, season after season, year after year, so that only patience could open it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed leather, lay the Founding Accord.

She knew it before she unwrapped it. She knew it by its weight—the weight of paper that had decided who lived. Beneath the Accord were the massacre orders, signed in a hand so neat it looked apologetic. And beneath those, folded separately, worn soft at the creases from being read too many times: her father's refusal letter.

*To the Board of the Meridian Company,* it began. *I will not falsify these records. You may find another surveyor. You may find many. But you will not find my name on this.*

His name wasn't on it. That was the whole point. And so they had taken his name everywhere else instead.

August read it three times. On the third pass, her eyes stopped working properly, and Tam—fourteen years old and tactless as an avalanche—put a hand on her shoulder and said nothing at all, which was the most grown-up thing anyone did that day.

* * *

They heard Halloway coming before they saw him. Of course they did; Halloway had never once in his life arrived anywhere quietly. Boots on glacier, the jingle of a man who liked his equipment to announce him, and voices—voices that didn't belong to fixers.

Voices that belonged to the valley.

"They've got Sefa," Ede said, going pale under his sunburn. "And Marta's boy. And—" he squinted down the icefall "—is that Corvin?"

"It is," August said, and felt something cold settle behind her sternum that had nothing to do with altitude. The harbormaster of Greyharbor, up here above the map's edge, in Company grey he'd clearly been given nowhere to refuse. "He came looking for me. Idiot."

"He came looking for you because half the river's already burning their toll bridges," Tam said. "Word travels faster than people think. Faster than we climbed."

Below them, spread across the glacier shelf like ink spilled on white cloth, Halloway's party arranged itself into a tableau. Warden Halloway stood front and center in a coat too clean for the altitude, flanked by six armed men—and held in front of them, roped wrist to wrist, stood Sefa, a boy of maybe eleven, and Corvin, whose expression suggested he was composing a strongly worded invoice even now.

Halloway raised a megaphone. Of course he had a megaphone. August would have bet the compass on it.

"AUGUST JOUBERT." His voice bounced off the ice walls and came back flattened and strange. "You have in your possession property of the Meridian Company. I am authorized to offer full reinstatement of your guide's license, restoration of your father's name to the Company rolls, a settlement of five thousand crowns, and safe passage for yourself and your companions. In exchange: the documents, unharmed."

He paused, savoring it.

"Or I begin with the boy."

The wind moved. Somewhere below, a raven complained about the drama.

Tam looked up at her. "Five thousand crowns," he said. "That's a lot of rope."

"It is a lot of rope," August agreed.

"You're not actually considering—"

"I'm considering how much I hate this man's megaphone."

But she was considering more than that, and she knew it, and she hated herself for knowing it. There it was, laid out on the ice like a market stall: everything she'd lost, priced and tagged. Her name. Her father's honor. Enough money to buy Greyharbor's harbor twice over. All she had to do was hand over the papers and let them disappear into some Company archive, where truth went to be embalmed.

Justice, in official hands. Filed. Stamped. Owned.

She looked at Sefa, standing straight-backed in the rope with frost in her beard, and Sefa caught her eye and shook her head—one small, fierce motion. *Don't you dare trade us for it.* And then, because Sefa had never met a moment she couldn't undercut: she mouthed, *Also your hair looks terrible.*

August laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

"No deal," she called down.

Halloway tilted his head, a man prepared for negotiation. "Name your price."

"You misunderstand. I'm not negotiating. I'm making an announcement." She turned to Tam. "Get the mirrors out."

* * *

They'd carried four signal mirrors up the Stair of Names without quite admitting why. Climbers' habit, Ede had called it. Old ranger superstition, Tam had called it. August had called it insurance, which was what she called everything she couldn't explain yet.

Now she explained it.

The route down from the source zigzagged across the icefall in long traverses, and along its length, at intervals her father had chosen decades ago—she found the little drilled sockets in the rock, exactly where the compass needle seemed to suggest them—there were stations. Her father hadn't just hidden the truth. He had built a road for it to travel on, in case anyone ever found it brave enough to carry.

Sefa's exiles had manned the lower stations since dawn. August had thought they were lookouts. They were audience.

She set the first mirror on its stone cairn, angled the flash down-valley, and watched it answer—a wink of light, then another, relayed station to station, all the way down into the green dark where the valley waited. Thousands of people below, she realized. The blacklisted, the burned-out, the quarry families and the drowned villages, everyone the Company had ever erased, all of them watching a chain of small suns climb the mountain.

"What are you doing?" Halloway demanded, megaphone lowering slightly, which for him constituted alarm.

"Reading," August said.

She unfolded the Founding Accord, and she began to read it aloud.

Her voice carried strangely on the glacier—too big, too flat—but the mirrors did the real work. At each station below, a reader waited with a copy of the text Sefa had transcribed weeks ago in the free valley, in three hands because one kept cramping. When August's voice failed against the wind, the mirrors flashed in sequence, and the readers below spoke the words onward, down and down, until the Accord reached the valley floor and, beyond it, the river, and beyond the river—who knew. Rivers are terrible at keeping secrets.

*"Be it accorded between the undersigned parties,"* August read, *"that the settlement of the Vell headwaters shall proceed notwithstanding local objection, and that objection shall be answered in such manner as to discourage its repetition—"*

She read the orders that followed. She read the numbers. She read the names—forty-one names, written in a clerk's tidy column, each one followed by the word *resolved*, as though they were entries in a stock book. Tam stood beside her holding the lantern, and when his voice broke on the eleventh name, August put her hand on his head and kept reading for both of them.

Down on the ice, Halloway's men shifted. One of them—August marked him, young, chapped lips, the look of a man who'd joined for wages and never been told what the wages bought—took his hat off.

"Stop her," Halloway said. Not shouting now. The megaphone hung forgotten at his side, and August understood suddenly that he had never imagined this, because men like Halloway don't imagine audiences they can't buy.

Nobody moved toward her. The ice was wide, and the truth was loud, and six men with rifles discovered simultaneously that shooting a woman reading names aloud makes a very short list of stories, and none of them end well for the shooter.

She finished the column of names. Then she unfolded her father's letter, and read that too—all of it, every stubborn line—and when she reached *you will not find my name on this*, her voice held, barely, and somewhere far below the mirrors flashed so fast and bright it looked like the mountain itself was applauding.

Then she took out the flint.

* * *

"Now hold on," said Corvin, from the ropes, with the tone of a man watching someone burn currency. "Joubert. That document is worth—"

"I know what it's worth."

"That's rather my point! Five thousand crowns, and you're using it as kindling—"

"Corvin." She looked at him across the ice, this hard, narrow man who had hounded her debt for two years and fed her father's memory an innkeeper's kindness without ever saying so. "If I sell it, it belongs to whoever buys it. If I hide it, it belongs to whoever finds it. If I burn it—" she struck the flint, and sparks jumped onto the oilcloth "—it belongs to everyone who just heard it."

The Accord caught slowly, the way old paper does, reluctant and then all at once. The massacre orders went up in a bloom of orange that reflected off the glacier until the whole world seemed briefly on fire. August fed her father's refusal letter to the flames last, and watched the words *I will not falsify these records* curl and blacken and rise, and thought: *You didn't. You never did. Look what it cost, and look what it built.*

"No one owns this now," she said, to Halloway, to the mountain, to the mirrors flashing the news down into the valley. "Not the Company. Not the crown. Not Sefa's council, though they'll be furious about it. Not me. Everyone heard it. Everyone keeps it. That's the only archive that can't be bought."

Halloway stared at the burning papers with an expression August would remember for the rest of her life: not rage, but accounting. A man watching his entire balance sheet go up in smoke and trying to calculate what remained.

What remained was his gun, and his pride, and neither had ever been known to reason well.

"You've destroyed evidence," he said quietly. "Of a historical matter. Do you understand what you've done? Without the originals, there's no case. No court. No restitution. Nothing but—"

"Nothing but everybody knowing," Tam said.

"Exactly," said August. "Isn't it wonderful?"

The last page fluttered up off the ice, burning like a small furious bird, and rose on the thermal, and all of them—fixers, hostages, orphans, wardens—watched it go until it was ash against the sky.

Sefa started laughing first. Deep, unrestrained, tears-in-the-frostbeard laughter, there in the ropes, and the boy beside her caught it, and even Corvin made a sound that in a better man might have been a chuckle.

Halloway turned to his men.

"Shoot her," he said.
