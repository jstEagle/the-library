## 19. Descent Under Judgment

Tam had learned three things about gunfire in his fourteen years, and all of them were wrong.

The first thing he'd learned was from the quarry boys, who said a rifle crack sounded like a whip. It didn't. It sounded like the sky tearing its shirt. The second thing he'd learned was from a drunk carter in Marrowfen, who said you heard the bullet before it came. You didn't. The bullet came first, and the sound arrived afterward like an apology from someone who'd already hit you. The third thing he'd learned — the one he'd believed deepest, down in his bones where belief lived — was that he would never be close enough to one to care.

The first shot spanged off the ice twenty feet to his left and threw up a fan of glacial flour that glittered like ground glass.

"Down!" August's voice, flat and hard as a piton.

Tam went down. His knees found ice through his trousers and he didn't care even a little bit. Above him, at the lip of the glacial vault, Warden Halloway stood with his grey coat snapping in the wind and his arm extended like a man pointing out scenery, except he was pointing a revolver, and behind him a dozen Company men were bringing their rifles up in a line as neat as fence posts.

They had come up out of the storm with hostages — Sefa's people, the exiles, marched ahead of them as shields — and Halloway had made his offer, and August had read the Founding Accord aloud into a signal mirror until the whole valley below could see the flashing of it, word by word, and then she had burned the originals in a brazier of her own hands' making while the wind took the ash sideways like a flock of startled birds.

And now this.

"Fire," Halloway said.

Nobody fired.

It was such a small thing, Tam thought later. Such an enormous, impossible small thing. A dozen men with rifles leveled at four people standing on open ice, and not one of them pulled a trigger. The wind gusted. Somewhere below, faint as a memory, the signal mirrors down-valley were still flashing — relaying, Tam realized, everything. Every word August had read. The mirrors hadn't stopped. The whole ridgeline was talking.

"You heard me." Halloway's voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, like river ice under a boot. "Fire."

A rifleman on the left end of the line — a big man with frost in his beard and the look of someone who'd been standing in the cold a very long time for wages that had stopped meaning anything — lowered his rifle six inches. Just six inches. Just enough.

"I signed nothing," the man said, "that says I shoot children."

"She burned Company property—"

"She burned *paper*," the man said. "I've burned paper. Everyone here has burned paper. We're not shooting anyone over paper, Warden."

Another man lowered his rifle. Then another. It went down the line like a wave going out, rifles tilting toward the sky one after another, and Tam watched Warden Halloway — the man who owned the sky, Ede had called him once, back when the name still frightened — stand alone in the middle of his own authority and discover it had been made of weather the entire time.

Halloway turned his revolver on the big frost-bearded man instead.

That was when Ede moved.

* * *

Later, Tam would try to remember it properly and fail, because the truth was it happened too fast and too slow at once, the way avalanches do. He remembered fragments. Ede stepping between the revolver and the rifleman, hands open, saying something — Tam never learned what, some old ranger phrase maybe, or maybe just Ede's name for himself, the one he'd been trying to earn back since the ford. He remembered Halloway's face going from fury to something colder and more terrible, which was decision. He remembered the shot.

He remembered Ede looking surprised.

Not hurt. Surprised. Like a man who'd been promised something wouldn't happen and had just watched it happen anyway. Ede looked down at his own chest with the mild interest of a man checking a coat pocket, and then his knees did what knees do, and he sat down on the ice of the source of the Vell, and he did not get up.

The world went strange after that. Tam knew, in the distant bookkeeping part of his mind, that August screamed — he'd never heard August scream before, and part of him filed it away forever, the way you file away the first time you see a mountain fall. He knew Sefa's people broke free in the confusion, that the frost-bearded man struck Halloway's arm aside, that more shots went wild into the white. But mostly what Tam remembered was himself, running, sliding on his knees across the ice to where Ede lay, and putting his hands where the blood was coming from, and finding there was so much of it, and it was so warm, warmer than anything on this whole frozen mountain, and thinking: *this is wrong, this is exactly backwards, blood should be cold up here, everything up here is cold.*

"Hey," Ede said. His voice was already smaller. "Hey, apprentice."

"Don't talk." Tam's hands were shaking. He pressed harder. "Don't — I'm stopping it, I'm stopping it, you just hold still—"

"It's fine." Ede smiled, and it was the worst thing Tam had ever seen, that smile, because it was gentle, and gentle things shouldn't be allowed in places like this. "I did the arithmetic, lad. Back at the ford. I owed you a life. This is just... settling accounts."

"You don't get to settle anything," Tam said, and heard his own voice break like a boy's, because that's what it was. "You don't get to — you taught me rivercraft. You taught me how to read water. You don't get to leave before I'm finished learning, that's not how apprenticeship works, there are *rules*—"

Ede laughed. It cost him; Tam saw it cost him; a thread of red appeared at the corner of his mouth and he laughed anyway. "There it is. There's the stonemason in you. Everything's got rules." His hand came up — heavy, slower than it should have been — and closed around Tam's wrist. Not squeezing. Just holding. "Listen. Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once and I've been practicing it the whole climb. What I did to your August — testifying, the trial, all of it — I can't undo. There's no payment. Understand? No payment exists. I spent my whole life thinking if I suffered enough it would balance out, and that's not how it works, and I'm sorry I made you watch me learn it this late."

"Ede—"

"But you," Ede said. "You I could teach. That counted. That counted for something real." His eyes went past Tam, up, and softened. "August."

Tam turned. August was kneeling on Ede's other side, and she had taken his hand, and her face was doing something Tam had no words for yet — grief and fury welded together into one expression, like a blade forged from two metals.

"You stubborn old mule," she said. "You absolute — you had *one job*, Ede Vasq, you had one job on this mountain, which was to stay alive long enough to be forgiven properly, in a warm room, with a drink, over a very long argument—"

"That does sound nice," Ede agreed.

"You don't get to skip it."

"I know." His thumb moved once against her knuckles. "Forgive me anyway. Not for me. For you. Carrying it's heavier than giving it away. I know about carrying."

August bent her head. Her shoulders shook once, twice, and stopped, because August Joubert did not shake for long; she folded it away somewhere, the way she folded everything, and when she lifted her face again it was calm in the way that quarries are calm, which is to say full of broken stone and ready for work.

"I forgive you," she said. "Now fight. Fight, you mule. Stay."

But Ede's eyes had gone past both of them, up the ice, to the pale ribbon of the glacier climbing toward the summit, and Tam watched him look at it the way Tam had once watched his father look at a half-finished archway — seeing the whole shape of it, the finished thing, the thing on the far side of the work.

"Gideon's route," Ede murmured. "Go finish it. Both of you. That's the payment. That's the only—" And then the sentence didn't end, it just stopped being said, the way a bell stops ringing, gradually and then all at once, and his hand went slack in Tam's grip, and the warmth in it began, horribly, to cool.

* * *

They buried what they could carry of him in snow, because the mountain was the only grave available and, Sefa said, gripping Tam's shoulder with a hand like a vise, it was also the right one. *Above the treeline,* she said. *Where the Stair begins. We'll bring him down proper when the passes clear.* Tam carved his name into a slab of blue ice with his chisel — the good chisel, his master's chisel, the one thing he'd carried out of the quarry — and the letters came out clean and deep, and he worked until his fingers bled because the letters had to be clean, they *had* to be, because Ede Vasq deserved better than shaky work, everyone deserved better than shaky work, and if Tam thought about that too long he would stop functioning, so he didn't think about it, he just cut: E-V-A-S-Q, and beneath it, because he was a stonemason's apprentice and proud of it and Ede had called him *lad* like it was a title:

*RANGER.*

Then the storm arrived for them properly, as if it had been waiting politely at the edge of things for the business to conclude, and Sefa put her mouth near Tam's ear and shouted over the wind: "Down, lad! Down the Stair! Storm's our cover — Halloway's got no authority left worth following and half his men know it, but the other half are still dangerous, and grief makes bad climbers. Move!"

So they moved.

* * *

Descent in a whiteout is a peculiar kind of blindness. You don't see where you're going; you feel it, through your boots, through the rope, through the small tugs and hesitations of the person ahead. Tam went third, behind Sefa, with August fourth, roped together in a chain that Tam understood now was not really about the ice at all. It was about not losing each other. It was about the fact that a rope is a promise you can feel.

The Stair of Names passed under his feet step by step, invisible in the blowing snow, but he knew it was there. He could feel the carved names through his boots, faint irregularities, a texture like reading with your soles. All those names. All those people the Company had erased from the valleys and written into the rock instead, because rock keeps what paper won't. And now Ede's name was up top in ice, temporary, melting come spring — and Tam decided, somewhere around the two-hundredth step, with his legs shaking and his eyes crusted with rime and his chest hollowed out like a bad quarry, that come spring he would recut it in granite. Proper granite. Deep enough to outlast the Company, deep enough to outlast the language. That was a thing he could do. That was a thing his hands knew how to do, even when the rest of him knew nothing at all.

Somewhere above and behind them, faint through the storm, a rifle cracked. Once. Twice. Then nothing — and Tam couldn't tell if it was pursuit or the remnants of Halloway's command arguing with itself, the lie of his authority cracking apart in the wind like sea ice. Sefa held up a fist. They stopped. They listened. The storm said nothing useful. They went on.

At the shelter of a serac, huddled in its lee while Sefa checked the route below, August spoke for the first time in an hour.

"Tam."

He looked at her. She was gray with cold and her lips were split from the wind and she looked, he thought, like every quarry worker he'd ever known at the end of a shift: used up and unbowed.

"When we're down," she said, "you'll want to be angry at me. Or at him. Or at the mountain, or the Company, or God if you keep one. You'll want someone to blame so the shape of it makes sense."

"Is that advice?" Tam said. His voice came out rougher than he meant. "Because I've had a lot of advice lately and most of it's been from people who then died or tried to arrest us."

Something moved at the corner of August's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one, waiting for better weather.

"No," she said. "It's a confession. I'm going to want that too. And I'm telling you now so we can want it together, because wanting it alone is how people end up walking into the white and not coming back." She looked down the mountain, into the murk. "Ede told me once that guilt is just love with nowhere to put itself. So we'll find somewhere to put it. Both of us. That's the work now. That's the route."

Tam thought about that. He thought about his chisel, and the name in the ice, and the granite waiting in his future. He thought about how, four weeks ago, he had been a boy stealing rope from a stranger, and now he was a boy descending a sacred stair inside a storm, having watched a man die for him, having helped burn the founding lie of the highlands, and the strangest part — the part he kept turning over like an odd-shaped stone — was that he wasn't lost. He'd expected to feel lost. Instead he felt *placed*. Like a stone set into an arch. Under load, yes. Bearing weight, absolutely. But placed.

"I'm not angry at you," he said finally. "I'm angry at everything else, but there's plenty of it to go around without rationing any for you."

This time it was a smile. Small, wind-burned, real.

"All right," August said. "Down, then. All the way down."

* * *

They came out of the cloud line near dusk, below the icefall, where the storm thinned to ordinary rain and the world reassembled itself in grays and greens. Below them, impossibly far, the valley of the Vell unrolled — and it was not quiet.

Tam stood on a ledge with the rain running off his chin and stared. Fires. Not one fire — dozens, strung along the riverbanks like beads on a wire. And lights moving. Lanterns crossing the toll bridge at Marrowfen in a crowd, a *crowd*, at night. Smoke rising from somewhere downstream that was too black and too much to be a chimney.

"What is that?" he asked.

Sefa had a battered spyglass up. She was silent a long moment. When she lowered it, she was wearing an expression Tam had never seen on her weathered face — something between disbelief and a laugh fighting to get born.

"That," she said, "is the ford at Greyharbor. And the bridge at Marrowfen. And, if I'm not mistaken, every landing between here and the sea." She handed the glass to August. "Your mirror-reading went further than the valley, girl. The dockworkers have it. The ferrymen have it. It's been traveling downriver faster than we've been traveling down-mountain."

August took the glass. She looked for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet enough that Tam had to lean in to catch it under the rain.

"They're burning the tolls," she said. "Look — that smoke, that's the Marrowfen tollhouse. That's Corvin's stretch of river." A pause. "Corvin's going to be insufferable."

"He's going to be *free*," Sefa said. "Insufferable and free. Worst combination there is."

Tam took his turn with the glass. Through the rain-blur he found the bridge, the crowd on it, tiny figures with lanterns, and though he couldn't hear them at this distance, couldn't possibly hear them, he found that he could imagine the sound perfectly — the sound of a lot of people who had read something true and decided, all at once, without asking anyone's permission, that the story belonged to them now.

His chest did something complicated. Grief and courage, side by side, sharing the same small space, the way he and August shared a rope.

"Come on, then," he said, and was surprised to hear it come out steady. "We shouldn't keep them waiting."

August shouldered her pack. Sefa spat for luck, an old ranger habit, and led them down onto the last slopes, toward the fires, toward the river, toward whatever a name was worth when ten thousand people had already decided the answer for themselves.

Behind them, high in the clearing cloud, the summit cairn caught the last light and held it, patient as a letter waiting to be opened.

Tam didn't see it. But the mountain did.
