## 20. The Trial Nobody Presided Over

The news traveled faster than they did.

August learned this on the second morning of the descent, when a charcoal-burner flagged them down at the edge of the treeline, breathless, his mule loaded with sacks that smelled of resin and rain. He looked at August the way people look at weather they've only heard about—a storm with a name now.

"You're her," he said. Not a question. "You're the one who read it out."

"I read something out," August said carefully. "On the mountain. I don't know what you heard."

"Everything." He said it with the particular satisfaction of a man delivering everything. "The ferryman at Whitefall heard it from a drover, and the drover had it from the signal relay—your mirror-flash, they say, word for word, spelled out in long and short. The mill folk copied it down. There's a woman at Marrowfen who reads, and she stood on the toll bridge and said it to four hundred people." He paused, savoring. "Then she said it again slower for the ones writing."

Tam made a sound beside her that was half laugh, half disbelief. "Word for word? August, you were shouting into a wind."

"Wind carries," the charcoal-burner said solemnly, as though this were scripture. "Downriver, anyway."

He pressed a heel of bread and a twist of salt pork on them before they could refuse, and would not take payment. "You paid," he said, when Tam tried. "Forty years of tolls. All of ours."

They walked on, and by midday they began to see what he meant.

The first toll station on the Vell was a stone arch August remembered from her father's time, when she'd been small enough to ride his pack and the gate-warden had given her a dried apple for luck. The arch still stood. The gate did not. It had been levered off its hinges and lay in the shallows like a felled tree, and someone had painted across the stone, in letters a boat could read from midstream: **THE RIVER WAS NEVER THEIRS.**

Below it, the water ran free. Boats passed through without stopping. One of them—the burnerman told them later, or maybe it was a fisherwoman at the next bend; by then the accounts had begun braiding together—had a fiddler aboard playing something fast and unrepentant.

"They'll rebuild them," Ede said quietly. He'd been walking stiff since the high camp, one arm bound against his chest, and he said little, but when he spoke it was with the flat care of a man measuring his own guilt daily. "Companies always do."

"Let them try," Tam said. "There's more of us than there are gates."

August didn't answer. She was watching a boy of perhaps ten wade out to the ruined gatehouse, pull a plank loose, and carry it home over his shoulder like firewood. That was the thing about it, she thought. No army had done this. No decree. Just people, one plank at a time, deciding that a thing was finished.

It should have felt like victory. Mostly it felt like standing in a house where all the furniture had been moved two inches left—everything familiar, nothing where she knew how to reach for it.

* * *

At Marrowfen, the town had stopped pretending.

The checkpoint at the bridge was gone—not abandoned but *dismantled*, the Company grey paint scraped off the barrier posts so thoroughly the wood beneath showed pale as bone. The innkeeper's wife, the same broad-armed woman who had fed them stew and warnings a lifetime ago, spotted August through the window before August had decided whether to come in at all.

She came out wiping her hands on her apron and looked at August for a long moment.

"You look terrible," she announced.

"I've been up a mountain."

"Mm." The woman considered this. "Well. You'll eat anyway." And that was all the ceremony there was, except that when August sat down, half the room turned to look, and an old man by the fire raised his cup to her, and nobody asked her a single question, which was somehow louder than questions.

It was here they got the shape of what had happened downstream.

Halloway's party had come through the ford below Marrowfen three days behind them—or rather, Halloway himself had come through, because his party had come apart on the mountain like wet rope. Some of his fixers had simply walked away into the valley and not come back. Two had gone downriver ahead of him, and by every account had gone *quietly*, heads down, men shedding a uniform the way a snake sheds skin. Halloway had arrived at the lower Vell expecting the machinery of the Company to catch him and carry him.

Instead he'd found dockworkers.

"It was the coal porters started it," the innkeeper's wife said, refilling bowls without being asked. "They'd had the words written out fair on a board—your words, love, from the reading—and they stood on the quay and made every barge master listen before he could tie off. Then the tally-clerks refused to sign his cargo manifests. You can't move freight without paper, and you can't make paper without clerks, and the clerks had all heard the story too." She shrugged, a great economical shrug that moved her whole shoulders. "He stood on the quay shouting about warrants and authority, and forty men just looked at him. Nobody touched him. That was the worst part, he said after. Nobody would even touch him."

"What happened?" Tam asked, leaning forward, spoon forgotten.

"The harbor watch came for him in the end. But here's the thing—" she lowered her voice, delighted by it, "—the watch is elected now. Since the tolls burned. They took him to the customs house and locked him in the strongroom, and there he sits, waiting for a trial that nobody's quite sure how to hold. Because—" she spread her hands, "—who presides? The magistrates are Company men. The courts are Company courts. Half the valley says hang him and the other half says no, no, we're not going to start *that* way, and meanwhile he eats his meals through a slot and everyone takes turns bringing them."

"Bringing him meals," Ede repeated.

"Well, we're not barbarians." She sniffed. "My sister does Tuesdays. She says he's very polite and it makes her furious."

August stared into her bowl. This was justice? A man in a strongroom, fed on schedule by the sister of a woman who hated him, awaiting a court that existed nowhere? It was absurd. It was shambolic. It was—

It was exactly what she'd burned the papers to make possible. Truth belonging to everyone meant everyone got a hand on the scales, and hands are clumsy, and the weighing takes forever, and somebody's sister has to bring the bread.

She found, to her surprise, that she wouldn't have traded it for the cleanest verdict in the world.

* * *

They heard Greyharbor before they saw it.

They'd come down along the Vell's last stretch, past the drowned survey camp and the ford where Ede had once driven off Company grey with a ranger's rifle and a ranger's fury, and at the bend above the tidal flats the sound reached them—voices, hundreds of them, carried upriver on the salt wind like the smell of a hearth.

August stopped walking.

"They know," she said. "Somehow they already know we're coming."

"Everyone knows everything now," Tam said. He'd grown on the journey—not taller, exactly, though probably that too, but *larger*, the way green wood swells. He'd stopped flinching at loud noises somewhere around the Stair of Names. "The ferryman told the eel-boats, the eel-boats told the fish-driers, and the fish-driers tell everybody everything, it's practically their trade."

"I could go around," August said. It came out smaller than she intended. "Through the marsh. Come in quiet. I spent six years getting good at coming in quiet."

Ede looked at her. His face was a ruin of bruises fading into yellow-green, and under it all he wore the expression of a man who had finally set down something heavy and wasn't sure yet what to do with his arms.

"August," he said. "They're not coming to judge you."

"How would you know?"

"Because nobody else came to judge you either. I've been watching for a week. They feed you. They tell you stories. They put your words on boards." He shook his head slowly. "I testified against you in a room with three lawyers and a seal, and it felt official, and it was worth nothing. Whatever this is—" he nodded toward the voices, "—it's the opposite of that. You don't get to sneak around the opposite of that. It'd be rude."

Tam snorted. August glared at both of them, which had no effect whatsoever, and walked on toward the sound.

* * *

The ford at Greyharbor was where the river gave up being a river and became the sea's business, wide and brown and patient. On the near bank, spread along the shingle and up the lane and hanging off the seawall like bright barnacles, was the village.

All of it. Every soul August had spent six years avoiding.

She recognized faces as she came down the shingle: the net-mender with the squint. Old Pella who sold bait and opinions in equal measure. Corvin the harbormaster, standing slightly apart in his good coat, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man attending a funeral he disapproved of but had helped arrange.

And at the front, holding a lantern even though it was full afternoon, was a girl of maybe eight whom August had once paid to stop following her along the seawall. The girl held the lantern up higher as August approached, as if presenting her.

"There she is," the girl announced, to the crowd, to the river, possibly to the nation.

August stopped an arm's length from the lot of them. Six years of practice at invisibility, and every trick of it failed her at once. She opened her mouth to say something measured, something about how they shouldn't have troubled themselves.

What came out instead was: "Who's watching the boats?"

Nobody laughed harder than Corvin. He pushed forward through the press, and for one horrible moment August thought he was going to embrace her, but he settled for gripping her shoulder hard enough to leave a mark, which from Corvin was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"Your debt," he said, gruffly, staring out at the river rather than at her. "The boatyard money. Consider it—" he waved his free hand vaguely at the horizon, "—lost in the general disorder. Terrible bookkeeping lately. Tolls burning, clerks striking. Who can keep track?"

"Corvin—"

"Don't thank me, it'll ruin my reputation." He finally looked at her, and behind the bluster his eyes were wet, and he let her see it for exactly one second before turning away to shout at someone about mooring lines that needed nothing.

The crowd didn't part so much as fold around them, drawing the three of them up the lane toward the tavern, and everyone wanted to shake Tam's hand because word had come downriver about the ice and the supply lines and a fourteen-year-old with a crowbar and no sense of self-preservation. Someone had already composed a song about Ede. It was inaccurate and unflattering and Ede stood through two verses of it with his ears burning scarlet, and August caught his eye across the room and mouthed *every word of this is your fault* and he smiled like a man whose debts were, at last, being paid in a currency he could bear.

Later, much later, when the tavern had thinned to diehards and the tide had come back in over the Devil's Ledger, swallowing the cave and its secrets for another forty years, August stepped out onto the seawall alone.

The village was dark behind her, warm-lit and loud. Downriver, somewhere past the last bend, a strongroom held a polite prisoner, and a valley argued about what to do with him, and no seal and no magistrate and no ledger anywhere had the final say. It was messy. It was slow. It belonged to everybody, which meant it belonged to no one, which meant—for the first time in the history of the Meridian Company—it couldn't be bought.

Her name wasn't clean. She understood now that it never would be, and that the wanting of it had been the smallest thing about her.

But tomorrow, she'd been told, Sefa was sending word about a route. An old one. Her father's route, up the north face, unfinished—the route he'd named for a trail-name carved in a summit cairn that August had never seen.

*Gideon.*

She turned the compass over in her pocket, feeling the worn initials under her thumb, and watched the river run silver to the sea, carrying everything downstream at last.
