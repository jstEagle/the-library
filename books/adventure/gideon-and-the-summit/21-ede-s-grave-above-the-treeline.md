## 21. Ede's Grave Above the Treeline

The Stair of Names began where the trees gave up—where the birches thinned to scrub and the scrub thinned to stone, and the mountain stopped pretending to be gentle. August had walked this stretch twice now: once fleeing upward with Halloway's rope around her future, and once coming down with a dead man carried on a litter of climbing slings and borrowed poles.

The second time was slower.

They buried Ede Vasq on the third morning after the storm, in a shelf of ground above the treeline that Sefa chose without hesitation, as if he'd been keeping it in reserve for forty years. Perhaps he had. Rangers knew their ground the way masons knew their stone, and Sefa had been both.

"He'd hate the fuss," Tam said. The boy's voice was rough from crying and from wind, and he kept his hands very busy tightening knots on the litter straps that didn't need tightening.

"He'd hate the fuss," August agreed, "and then he'd stand there checking everyone's bootlaces anyway, because if you're going to be at a funeral you might as well make sure nobody turns an ankle on the way down."

That got a wet, surprised laugh out of Tam, which had been the entire point. Grief was like a snow bridge, August had learned long ago at her father's knee: you crossed it lighter in company, one at a time, testing each step.

There were more of them than she'd expected. Word had come down the valley ahead of them, as word did now—it seemed to travel on its own legs since the burning—and exiles came up from the free settlements in ones and twos and small determined families. A woman who ran the rope-walk at the lower camp. Two brothers who fished the tarns. An old woman named Brigit who had once, it turned out, been arrested in the same year as Ede for the same crime of selling survey maps to people the Company hadn't cleared. She brought a flask of something amber and terrible and poured a measure onto the ground before drinking any herself.

"For the dead," she said. "He'll want his share."

Sefa led the digging himself, refusing help until Tam simply picked up a second spade and started, and Sefa looked at the boy—at the set of his shoulders, so much like a stonemason's son squaring to a block of granite—and let him.

August stood apart, the way she always had, and noticed that this time standing apart was a choice rather than a sentence. That was new. She was still learning what to do with it.

* * *

They carved his name into the rock above the grave, because that was what the Stair was for.

The first steps of the hidden route rose just beyond the burial shelf, each tread cut by pre-Company climbers and etched with the names of the massacred—the Founding Accord's dead, chiseled into living stone by hands that had refused to let the Company own even the memory of its victims. August's father had carved here too. She had found his name among the makers, not the mourners, and she still hadn't decided what to feel about that beyond the feeling itself, which was large and unnamed and sat in her chest like ballast.

Tam did the carving. Nobody asked him to; he simply took out his father's chisels—the good ones, wrapped in oilcloth, carried four hundred miles through gorge country and across a collapsing snow bridge—and knelt at the rock face beside Ede's grave.

"EDWARD VASQ," he said aloud, spelling it out. "Was that actually his name? He always went by Ede."

"It was Edward," Sefa said. "He hated it. Said it sounded like a man who filed reports on time." A pause. "Which, to be fair, he did. Just not for the people who wanted them."

Tam laughed again, that startled sound, and then bent to work. The chisel rang against the stone in the cold bright air—a clean, patient music, the oldest music there was. August watched the letters emerge stroke by stroke: E-D-E. He'd earned the short version. Let the mountain keep it.

Below ED-E, after some deliberation, Tam added three more words, small and deep: HE CAME BACK.

August read them and had to look away for a moment, up at the icefields, at nothing.

Because it was true, and it was the whole truth, and it was the truth Ede had spent his last years trying to carve into the world by harder means than a chisel. He had testified against her. He had let the Company bend him with threats against his family, and the testimony had blacklisted her, ended her guiding career, sent her to Greyharbor to mend boats and keep her head down for seven bitter years. And then he had come back. Up the river, into the gorges, across the ice, taking a beating in a high camp so two other people could run, planting himself between danger and an orphaned boy right up until the moment the mountain took the payment instead.

She had refused his sacrifice at gunpoint. The mountain had overruled her.

"I never said it," she told the grave quietly, when the others had drifted toward the flask and the fire. "While he was alive. That I understood why he did what he did. I understood it the whole time, and I made him carry it alone anyway, because being angry at him was easier than being angry at myself."

The wind moved through the scrub. Somewhere below, a ptarmigan complained about the company.

"So there," she finished. "You have it in writing. Carved by proxy. Don't say I never told you anything."

It wasn't a eulogy. It would have embarrassed him thoroughly, which meant it was probably exactly right.

* * *

Greyharbor met her at the ford.

Not all of it—but enough of it that August, coming down the river road with Tam and Sefa and a loose tail of exiles, stopped dead on the near bank and stared. Boats were drawn up along the shallows. Nets hung drying on improvised lines. And standing in the mud of the crossing with his ledger under his arm and his face arranged into an expression of elaborate indifference, was Corvin.

The harbormaster had aged, she thought, or perhaps she was only seeing him clearly for the first time. His grey whiskers were longer than she remembered. His coat had been patched at both elbows. He watched her approach with the wary stillness of a man who has rehearsed a speech and is now praying nobody makes him give it.

"Joubert," he said, when she was close enough. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other side of the mountain."

"I've heard about the other side of the mountain. Everyone's heard about the other side of the mountain. There are children in Marrowfen reciting your signal-mirror speech in the schoolyard, which I imagine is driving the Company's schoolmaster to drink." He opened the ledger with the crisp finality of a man executing a warrant. "Which brings us to business."

Her stomach dropped, old reflexes firing. Of course. The debt. Fourteen crowns and eleven shillings, accrued over years of boat-rent, bait, and the slow charity of a harbormaster who had never quite admitted he was being charitable. She'd climbed a mountain, burned an empire's founding documents, toppled a warden, and still the arithmetic followed her down like a faithful dog.

"Corvin—"

"Hold still." He licked his thumb, turned pages with theatrical care, found the entry, and then—slowly, deliberately, holding her eye the entire time—drew a single line through it. Then he initialed it. Then he blotted it, closed the book, and tucked it under his arm as though nothing whatsoever had occurred.

"That's not—" August's voice came out strange. "I can pay. I will pay. I'm going back to work the moment the boats need mending, which is immediately, knowing this harbor."

"The debt's forgiven."

"You can't just—"

"I can. It's my ledger. That's rather the point of a ledger; you get to decide what goes in it." He sniffed, gazing out at his boats with tremendous concentration. "Besides, the account was wrong. Has been wrong for years. I kept meaning to correct it and kept not correcting it, on the grounds that a Joubert with a debt was a Joubert who stayed in Greyharbor, and Greyharbor needed the company." He finally looked at her, and beneath the gruffness his eyes were wet. "Your father mended my father's boat once, when we couldn't pay. Never said a word about it. Found out years later from his logbook. So the account was wrong, Joubert. It was wrong the day I opened it. Consider it balanced."

August discovered that she could not speak. She managed a nod, which would have to do, and Corvin received it with a curt jerk of his chin, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was somehow larger than any ceremony she'd ever attended.

Then he ruined it beautifully, because he was Corvin.

"The boat-shed's a disaster, by the way. Some fool left tar pots open and a boy's been sleeping in the loft. You'll want to get back to it directly."

"The boy is standing right here," Tam said.

"Then he can start sweeping," said Corvin, and Tam grinned, and the ford was suddenly full of noise and greeting, and August stood in the middle of it with her debt struck through in ink and understood that she had come home to a place that had decided, without asking anyone's permission, to keep her.

* * *

Sefa stayed three days.

He slept in the boat-shed loft beside Tam—claimed it instantly, the way old soldiers claim bunks—and spent the days walking the harbor, examining hulls, interrogating fishermen about tides, and generally behaving like a man conducting a survey no Company had ever commissioned. On the second evening August found him on the sea wall, watching the light go copper on the water, and sat down beside him without asking, because that was how things were done between them now.

"He asked me," Sefa said, without preamble. "Before we came down. In the valley, while they were setting his arm. He asked me to take the boy."

August went very still.

"Not as a favor to the boy," Sefa continued. "As a penance for himself. He said—" and here the old guide's voice caught, just slightly, on the memory— "he said, 'I broke one climber's life already. Let me finish by building one.' As if Tam were a wall he could lay to square the account." Sefa shook his head. "Foolish man. Beautiful, foolish man. But I told him yes, and I don't break my word to foolish beautiful men, even the dead ones. Especially the dead ones."

"He'd want Tam to have a choice."

"Tam has a choice. I offered it this morning over breakfast. He threw a bread roll at me and said, and I quote, 'Obviously.'" Sefa smiled at the water. "So it's settled. Stone in the summer, rope-work in the winter. A mason who can climb and a climber who can build. There hasn't been one of those since—" he glanced sideways at her— "well. Since Gideon."

The name landed softly, but it landed. Her father's trail-name. The thing waiting for her at the top of the summit cairn, three weeks' hard travel away, that she had not yet gone to find. Sefa saw her expression and had the grace not to comment, which was its own kindness.

"There's room in the valley for you too," he said instead. "Whenever you want it. That offer doesn't expire, August. The valley doesn't issue debts."

"I know." She turned her father's compass over in her pocket—its needle no longer pointing north, pointing home, pointing wherever her father had tuned it to point. "But I think my ledger's here."

Sefa nodded slowly, as though she'd passed some test he hadn't known he was administering. Then he stood, groaned in the manner of men whose knees have opinions, and said, "Come inside. Your apprentice's apprentice is attempting fish stew, and someone ought to supervise before the boy inherits a burnt pot along with everything else."

* * *

On the morning Sefa left, taking Tam with him for the season's first term in the valley, August walked them as far as the ford.

Tam had grown, somehow, in the weeks since the mountain—or perhaps he'd simply stopped trying to disappear. He stood straighter. He wore Ede's old ranger knife at his belt, given formally, with Sefa as witness, in a little ceremony the boy pretended to find embarrassing and would clearly remember for the rest of his life. At the crossing he turned back and looked at August with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone deciding who they were going to be.

"You'll write?" he demanded.

"If the harbor has paper."

"And you'll come up at midsummer? For the reading? We're going to read the Accord every year, Sefa says. Out loud. On the Stair. All the names."

"All the names," August agreed. "I'll be there."

"You promise?"

"Tamsin." She used his full name deliberately, gravely, the way he'd once used hers. "I have recently learned that promises made in front of mountains are binding. Ask anyone."

He grinned, hugged her quickly and ferociously around the middle before she could escape it, and then splashed across the ford after Sefa, not looking back, because he was fourteen and immortal and the whole river valley belonged to him now. August watched until the two figures merged with the road and the road merged with the hills.

Then she turned back toward Greyharbor, and the boats, and the shed that smelled of tar and pine, and the life that had been handed back to her not clean but chosen.

There was a letter waiting on her workbench. No seal, no mark—just her name in careful block capitals, the handwriting unfamiliar, and beneath the name, in smaller letters, three words that stopped her breath:

*From the summit.*

She turned the envelope over twice. The harbor sounds went on around her—gulls, rigging, Corvin shouting at somebody about mooring lines—and the compass in her pocket seemed, absurdly, to grow heavier, its needle trembling toward the high white country where a cairn held her father's trail-name and whatever message he had left at the top of the world.

August reached for her coat.
