## 22. Gideon and the Summit

The name had been waiting for her the whole time.

Sefa told her on the last morning in the free valley, while they packed rope into canvas sacks that smelled of pine resin and old snow. August had asked him—finally, after weeks of not asking—why her father's letters home were signed *Gideon*, when his name was Aldous Joubert. She'd found three of them tucked inside Sefa's expedition trunk, brittle as moth wings, addressed to a younger Sefa at a basecamp long since swallowed by ice.

Sefa laughed, and it was a good laugh, the kind that came up from somewhere weathered. "Gideon wasn't a name, girl. It was a promise. Your father said every climber gets one word to carry them through the bad places. He chose Gideon because Gideon was the one who broke things down so people could see what they were made of." He tapped the compass in her pocket, gently, like greeting an old friend. "He carved it himself. Top of the cairn. You'll see."

"You've been up there?"

"Once. Forty years ago, with your father and six others. Three of us came down." His eyes went distant, then came back. "He said he'd finish the route someday with his daughter. Said you'd have hands for it." Sefa looked at her hands—scarred, salt-cracked from boat work, rope-burned from a winter she'd never forget—and nodded as if something had been confirmed. "He was right."

* * *

August left Greyharbor before dawn, alone, because some doors you have to walk through by yourself or they don't count.

Corvin met her at the harbor wall anyway, because Corvin had never once let her leave anywhere without commentary. He pressed a parcel into her arms: bread still warm, a jar of pickled herring, and—wrapped in oilcloth—a coil of new rope.

"That's not charity," he said gruffly, before she could say anything. "That's an investment. You're going to be guiding parties out of this village come spring, and I want first claim on outfitting you. Harbormaster's privilege."

"I haven't agreed to guide anyone."

"You will." He squinted at the eastern sky, where the high country was still a dark rumor above the treeline. "Half the village asked me already. The miller's boys want to see the source. Old Marta wants her husband's ashes taken up past Whitefall. And Tam—" Corvin's voice did something complicated around the boy's name, "—Tam says you promised him the summit route when he was sixteen. Says you swore it on a burnt ledger."

"I swore no such thing."

"He says you did. And between the two of you, I believe the fourteen-year-old." Corvin shrugged. "Go on, then. The tide's out and the mountain's in. Even trade."

August walked the shore road with the bread warm against her ribs, and behind her she heard the village waking—the slap of hulls, the rattle of nets, someone singing badly. A year ago she would have kept her head down and been gone before any of it noticed her. Now the sounds followed her like a dog that had decided she was its person.

She didn't look back. Looking back was for people who weren't coming home.

* * *

The lower approach was kind to her, almost apologetic. The Vell ran clear and quick beside the trail, and at the ford where Ede had once driven off Company fixers with nothing but a ranger's staff and a truly magnificent vocabulary, she stopped and ate half the bread.

"I'd have brought you," she told the water. "If you'd stayed upright for five more minutes, I'd have dragged you up there myself and made you complain the whole way."

The river said nothing, which was more courtesy than Ede had ever shown her. She laughed until her eyes stung, which surprised her, and then she shouldered her pack and climbed.

Above Marrowfen, the scars of the old world were fading faster than she'd expected. The toll bridge stood open, its gatehouse window dark, a hand-painted sign nailed over the Company crest: *PASS FREELY — THE VELL BELONGS TO THE VALLEYS.* Someone had tried to burn the crest off the stone and given up halfway, so the meridian line now ended in a scorch mark that looked, if you squinted, like a bird taking flight. August squinted at it for a long moment and decided that was about right.

In the inn where the innkeeper had once fed three fugitives and lied beautifully about it to a checkpoint patrol, she paid for a room with honest coin and slept eight hours without dreaming. In the morning the innkeeper refused payment for breakfast, saying her father had once carried the man's mother down off a mountain on his back, and that the debt had been accruing interest for fifty years.

"Everyone keeps telling me my father paid for things," August said.

"Well, somebody did," the innkeeper said, sliding the plate across. "Might as well be him."

* * *

The Stair of Names took two days.

She climbed it slowly, reading as she went. Every step bore a name cut deep enough to outlast the rock around it—thousands of them, the dead of the Founding Accord, carved by hands that had known they might be next. Near the top, where the steps narrowed and the wind got personal, she found the carvers' marks her father had left: small, precise chisel-work, a surveyor's signature in stone. *A. JOUBERT — FOR THE ONES WHO COULDN'T CLIMB.*

Below it, newer, rougher, cut with a borrowed knife by a boy with more grief than technique:

*EDE VASQ — HE CAME BACK.*

August sat on that step for an hour. The wind moved through the pass and made a sound almost like breathing, and she told Ede about the descent through the storm, about how Tam hadn't cried until they reached the valley, and then had cried exactly once, briefly, like a boy who had learned crying was a tool you used sparingly. She told him the granary had been rebuilt bigger. She told him Halloway was in a dockworkers' jail awaiting a trial that nobody expected to be fair, and that she felt no triumph about it, only a tired sort of settling, like silt finding the riverbed.

"You'd hate all this sentiment," she said. "Good. Sit with it."

Then she got up and climbed.

* * *

Above the map's edge, the mountain stopped being anyone's territory and became itself again.

There was no trail here. There was her father's route, reconstructed from a compass that didn't point north and a daughter who had spent thirty years learning to read ice without knowing why. The glacier rose in blue seracs like a city built by patience, and the compass needle in her palm swung steady—not toward magnetic north, but toward a notch in the ridgeline that no map had ever named.

Her father had tuned it. Of course he had. A man who refused to falsify a record would build truth into his instruments, hide his findings in brass and glass where no executive could edit them. *Follow me,* the needle said. *I checked the way.*

It took four days. There was a pitch below the summit ridge—a wall of rotten granite stitched with ice—that nearly beat her, and in the worst hour of it, hanging from two tools and a prayer with poor collateral, August understood why he'd needed a word like *Gideon*. Not for courage. For demolition. You had to break yourself down up here, strip away everything you were below the snowline—the disgraced guide, the debtor, the woman whose name had been worth less than paper—until what remained was just a creature doing the next move, and the next, and the next.

She topped out at sunset.

The cairn stood where Sefa said it would, a rough tower of stones older than the Company, older than the valleys' grief. And there, on the flat capstone, worn shallow by forty years of wind but unmistakable, a single word:

**GIDEON**

Beneath it, smaller, in the same sure hand:

*FOR AUGUST, WHEN SHE COMES.*

August sat down hard on the ice and put her face in her hands and let the mountain hold her while she wept—not the sharp grief of the cave, not the furious tears of the burning—but something slow and enormous, the sound of a door opening after years of bracing against it shut.

He had known. All those years ago, standing on this roof of the world, he had looked down the whole length of his life and seen a daughter he hadn't yet disappointed, a name he hadn't yet blackened, a route unfinished—and he had left the light on.

* * *

She stayed three days.

On the first, she finished his route properly, climbing the last unroped pitches to the true summit and building a small cairn of her own beside his, because traditions should grow or die. On the second, she read aloud from memory the parts of the Founding Accord she could not forget, giving the names on the Stair their altitude back, letting them stand at the top of the sky for a little while. On the third, she took out the compass.

It was harder than she'd expected. Her fingers didn't want to open.

"You'd tell me not to be sentimental," she said to her father, to the wind, to whoever was listening. "You'd say a tool belongs where it does the most work." But that wasn't quite it either, and she knew it, and after a while she stopped pretending. The truth was simpler: the compass had pointed her home—through caves and courts and storms and betrayals, all the way back to herself. It had done its work. Now it belonged to the mountain, and to whoever came next, lost and looking.

She set it on the capstone, needle swinging, and adjusted it until it pointed down-valley. Toward Greyharbor. Toward the ford, the inn, the rebuilt granary, the boy who was probably at this very moment driving Sefa mad with questions.

Toward home.

"There," she said. "Now it points the way for everybody."

* * *

The descent took six days, and she was not alone for five of them.

She met Tam at Whitefall Crossing—or rather, Tam met her, materializing out of a snowdrift with the smugness of a cat presenting a dead bird, Sefa's spare rope coiled over his shoulder and frost in his eyebrows.

"You're supposed to be in the valley."

"I was in the valley. Then I wasn't." He fell into step beside her as naturally as if they'd planned it, which they hadn't, which somehow made it better. "Sefa said you'd try to do the last stretch alone and he said I was to intercept you at the crossing because you're sentimental about finishing things by yourself and it's a stupid habit."

"It's not a stupid habit."

"It is. He says solitude's a tool too, and you leave yours out in the rain." Tam glanced sideways at her, suddenly careful. "Also Marta sent biscuits. For your father. We're leaving them at the cairn."

"We already passed the cairn."

"I know." He produced the biscuits from his coat, slightly crushed, entirely intact in spirit. "So we carry them down instead. Sefa says that's the older tradition. You take the mountain's gifts down; you don't leave yours up. Otherwise the summit just becomes another place people store things." He paused. "Like a Company vault."

August looked at this boy—this orphaned, blackmailing, infuriating, extraordinary boy who had talked his way onto her rope team with a stolen coil of rope and never once let go—and felt something in her chest rearrange itself permanently.

"Marta's right," she said. "You're going to be insufferable when you're grown."

"Sefa says I already am."

They walked down together through the falling snow, carrying biscuits.

* * *

Greyharbor saw her coming from a mile off, because Greyharbor had been watching the road for two days.

She crested the shore hill at low tide—the ordinary, dependable, twice-a-day tide, nothing historic about it—and the whole village was on the sand. Boats drawn up in a crescent. Nets strung with lanterns. Corvin front and center with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had organized a celebration and would deny it under oath. Marta. The miller's boys. The innkeeper from Marrowfen, who had apparently traveled three days for the occasion. Fishermen, netmenders, children, dogs. And at the front, holding a banner that read WELCOME HOME GUIDE in paint still wet enough to glisten, the apprentices of the newly founded Greyharbor School of Rivercraft and Mountaincraft, enrollment nine, head instructor conspicuously absent until this exact moment.

August stopped at the top of the sand.

A year ago she had come to this village to disappear. She had mended boats with her head down and paid Corvin late and learned the names of only as many neighbors as survival required. She had believed, with the certainty of a woman who had been burned, that belonging was a thing granted—by companies, by courts, by committees of strangers reviewing your record—and that hers had been revoked, fairly or not, forever.

But nobody down on that sand was granting her anything. They were just *there*. The way a harbor is there. The way a river is there. Not because you earned it. Because you came back, and they kept the light on.

"She burned the Accord!" someone shouted, and the cheer went up ragged and enormous, and Tam—whooped beside her like it was his own name being cheered, which in every way that mattered it was.

Corvin met her at the waterline. Up close, his eyes were suspiciously bright, though his voice remained at full gruffness.

"The school needs a head instructor," he said. "Pays badly. The students are terrible. One of them is that Tam boy, God help us all."

"What are the terms?"

"No terms. That's the point of a school." He stuck out a hand, then ruined the dignity of it by pulling her into a hug that smelled of tar and herring brine. "Welcome home, Joubert. Your name's not cleared, you know. There's no court left to clear it. Half the valleys think you're a saint and the other half think you're a menace, and both halves are probably right."

"Sounds about right," August said into his shoulder.

"Good. First lesson's tomorrow. Tide's at dawn." He released her, sniffed once, violently, and turned away to bellow at the apprentices about their banner-holding technique.

* * *

That night, the village feasted on the sand, and August sat among them with a bowl of fish stew in her hands and the fire painting everyone gold.

Tam fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, defending his position that the summit route should be renamed *the Joubert Line* over August's strenuous objections. Marta covered him with a blanket without breaking conversation. Sefa, who had arrived by the long road with two exiles and a crate of valley apples, sat beside August and watched the fire.

"Your father used to say a name isn't what's written about you," Sefa said. "It's what people call you when you're not in the room. Do you know what they call you in the valley?"

"What?"

"*The one who read it aloud.*" He smiled into his stew. "Not the one who burned it. Anyone can burn. Reading takes longer."

August looked out past the firelight, to where the sea breathed against the sand and the Devil's Ledger cave lay drowned and dark beneath the returning tide, keeping whatever secrets it had left. Somewhere above it all, past the treeline, past the Stair, past the edge of every map, a brass compass sat on a cairn pointing steadily down toward this beach, toward this fire, toward these people.

Not cleared. Not pardoned. Not restored.

Chosen. Which was better, and cost more, and could never be taken by any company, crown, or council, because it had never been theirs to give.

"Head Instructor Joubert," Tam mumbled from under his blanket, surfacing just long enough to test the words. Then he grinned, half-asleep. "First lesson's at dawn. Don't be late. I'm telling everyone you cry at summits."

"I do not cry at summits."

"Corvin says—"

"Corvin," August said, reaching for a biscuit, "says a great deal for a man who's never been above the treeline."

And the fire cracked, and the tide came in, and the story went on—which was, in the end, the whole inheritance: not a name on a cairn, not a compass pointing home, but a story that belonged to everyone who carried it, and a village full of people learning, one lesson at a time, to climb.
