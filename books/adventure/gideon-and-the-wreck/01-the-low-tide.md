## 1. The Low Tide

The ferry to Skarnvik cost Gideon Blackwood the last of her cash and most of her dignity.

She stood at the rail for the whole crossing with her pack between her boots — forty kilos of rope, rack, and regret — watching the fjord walls slide past like the pages of a book someone else was reading too fast. The water was the color of gunmetal and old bruises. Gulls hung in the wind off the glacier without moving, as if pinned there. Somewhere behind those walls, she knew, were peaks she would have once sold her soul to climb. Now she just wanted a bed, a meal, and a contract that paid before it killed her.

The blacklist had followed her for eleven months. Not officially — there was no document, no letterhead, nothing she could hold up in front of a hiring committee and argue with. It lived instead in the pauses on the phone lines, in the way emails went unanswered after a first polite reply, in the word *concerns* that kept surfacing like a body that wouldn't stay down. *We've heard things. Concerns about judgment. Concerns about the Halvard incident.* As if Halvard had been an incident, a fender-bender, a spilled drink. As if Erik Halvard hadn't been a warm man with a laugh like a rockslide who had gone into white water on her rope and come out of it not breathing.

She had been cleared by two inquiries. It didn't matter. The mountain world ran on rumor the way rivers ran on meltwater, and hers had frozen solid around one fact: *Gideon Blackwood lost a client.* The truth — that the serac had failed, that the route had been sound, that she'd held him on the line for forty minutes with her hands freezing to the steel while he stopped answering her voice — was irrelevant. A guide's job, the whisperers said, was to bring people home. She hadn't. Case closed.

So: Skarnvik. Population, according to the ferry manifest, four hundred and something. Industry: fishing, a little shipping, and whatever Aldous Vey's freight office did with the deepwater berth at the harbor's mouth. She'd found the salvage posting on a board in a hostel in Tromsø three weeks ago, typed on paper yellowed at the edges, and she'd been telling herself ever since that it wasn't desperation. *Salvage diver or climber wanted. Wreck of the* Meridian *at Skarnvik headland. Fair pay, food included. Inquire at Oyelaran Salvage, harbor road.*

Fair pay. Food included. That was the whole of what her life had contracted to, and she knew it, and she took the ferry anyway.

* * *

Skarnvik announced itself the way small towns do when they have no interest in impressing you: a smear of red and gray roofs at the base of a green-black slope, a church spire, a breakwater arm reaching into the fjord like a man testing bathwater. The mountains rose behind it in stacked ranks, going blue with distance, and above them all crouched the glacier — the Viddjebreen, the ferryman called it when Gideon asked, pronouncing it carefully, the way you pronounce the name of something that has killed your neighbors.

"You'll be wanting work," the ferryman said. He was sixty-odd, built like a bollard, with a beard the color of dirty snow. "Salvage, is it? For Torvald Oyelaran?"

"That's the posting."

He grunted. It was not an approving grunt. "Torvald pays fair," he allowed, after a mile of silence. "Better than some." Another mile. "You're not from here."

"No."

"Good season for it." He nodded toward the headland, where the land shouldered out into the fjord and the water broke white against black rock. "Lowest tide in ten years, they're saying. Moon and storm and all that. Whole seabed's showing its bones." He glanced at her sidelong. "Cave's open too."

"What cave?"

His hands tightened on the wheel, just slightly. She saw it because she'd spent fifteen years reading micro-expressions on clients deciding whether to tell their guide they were scared. "No cave," he said.

"There's a cave."

"There's water," he said, "and rocks, and fish. That's all Skarnvik has ever needed from the sea." And he shut his mouth like a trapdoor until they docked.

Gideon filed it away. She was good at filing things away; it was most of what she did now. Fear in a client's eyes, a cornice that looked wrong, a partner who'd started drinking at altitude — you noted it, you weighed it, you decided whether it changed your plan. The ferryman's fear changed nothing about her plan, which was to eat, sleep, and get paid. But she wrote it down anyway, in the ledger behind her eyes where she kept everything she couldn't afford to feel yet.

* * *

Oyelaran Salvage occupied a corrugated shed at the end of the harbor road, smelling of diesel, tar, and old rope. The workshop inside was a museum of half-finished things: a boat hull mid-repair, winches under blankets, a wall of tools hung with the loving precision of a man who believed order was a form of prayer. A girl of maybe twenty-five was up a ladder rehanging a chain hoist, dark hair tied back, sleeves rolled over forearms scarred with the small white history of metalwork.

"Closed," she said, without looking down.

"The posting said inquire."

That got her attention. She came down the ladder slowly, wiping her hands on a rag, and looked Gideon over with the frank inventory of someone who hired bodies for dangerous work: boots, worn but sound. Hands, scarred and steady. Eyes — she lingered there a moment longer than was polite, and Gideon let her, because everyone always lingered there eventually, looking for whatever it was they'd heard about.

"Gideon Blackwood." The girl said the name flatly, testing it. So the blacklist had reached even here. Of course it had. "The Blackwood?"

"I climb. I dive. I do contract work." She set her pack down against the doorframe so it wouldn't look like she was ready to leave, though she was. "Your posting asked for a climber or a diver."

"It did." The girl crossed her arms. "I'm Mara. Mara Oyelaran. My father runs this shop." A pause with weight in it. "He's not here right now."

"When will he be?"

"Depends how long you stand there making me decide things that are his to decide." She chewed the inside of her cheek, and Gideon watched her do arithmetic — wages owed against work available against whatever private pressure lived behind her eyes. Gideon knew that arithmetic intimately. She'd been running it for eleven months. "The wreck's the easy part," Mara said finally. "*Meridian*, went down in '09, sits in twelve meters off the headland, mostly stripped already. What we need is the cave."

"There is a cave, then."

Mara's jaw tightened. "There's a cave the tide's opened for the first time in ten years, and my father signed a contract to clear it, and then—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Gideon recognized the maneuver; she'd performed it often enough lately, the swift closing of accounts you didn't want strangers reading. "And then he hurt his back, and I can't free-dive a silted sea cave alone, and the tide window closes in nine days. That's the job. Twelve meters down at high water, dry at the bottom of this spring tide for maybe three hours twice a day. You go in, you bring out anything worth money, you don't stay past the turn. Fair pay, food, a bunk in the loft."

"And the catch."

"The catch," Mara said, "is that nobody in this town will say the name of that cave out loud, including me, and if you ask them why they'll suddenly remember errands." She held out a hand, calloused, unapologetic. "My father can explain the rest tonight. Do you want the work or not?"

Outside, the light was going long and coppery across the water, and somewhere beyond the headland the sea was drawing itself back off stone it hadn't touched in a decade. Gideon thought about her bank balance, which was a joke; about the hostel beds she could no longer afford; about momentum, which was the only thing still carrying her, the way a current carries a swimmer who has stopped believing in the far shore.

"I want the work," she said.

* * *

Torvald Oyelaran turned out to be a big man gone soft at the edges, moving through his own workshop like a ship dragging an anchor — his back, clearly, was worse than his daughter had let on. He shook Gideon's hand with both of his, studied her face for a long moment with pale, intelligent eyes, and said only: "You'll do. Mara says you'll do, and Mara's eye is better than mine ever was."

They fed her cod and potatoes and hard bread at a table scrubbed white with use, and Torvald talked with his mouth full about the *Meridian*, about winch loads and silt behavior and the spring tide tables pinned above the stove, and it was almost possible to pretend this was an ordinary contract in an ordinary town. Almost. Twice, Gideon caught the old man glancing toward the window, toward the darkening shoulder of the headland, with an expression she'd last seen on a client's face at a high camp the night before a summit push — dread wearing the mask of anticipation.

"Tell me about the cave," she said, the second time.

The knife in Torvald's hand paused over his plate. Mara, clearing dishes at the counter, went very still.

"Cave's a hole in the rock," Torvald said. "Tide goes out, you go in, you bring back what the sea left. That's all anyone needs to know."

"With respect, that's what the ferryman said. Nearly word for word. Either you rehearsed it or this town's got one story and nobody tells it."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

Torvald set down his knife. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he knows is thin. "There are stories. There are always stories in a place like this — the sea takes people, so the sea gets blamed. Nineteen years back an expedition went up onto the Viddjebreen and never came down. Six men. One of them was my wife's father." His hand flattened on the table, steadying himself or the table, hard to say. "The town decided the mountain ate them, and the mountain's neighbor, the sea, got the blame too, and the cave at the headland got folded into it because caves always do. That's all. Superstition. Bad weather and bad luck and grief looking for a place to put itself."

"But?"

"But nothing." He pushed back his chair, wincing, and the movement closed the conversation like a door. "Low tide's at six-forty tomorrow morning. You dive at slack water, you're out before the turn, and you take Mara with you on the surface line. Anything valuable comes back here. Anything that looks like—" He stopped. Started again. "Anything that isn't obviously salvage, you bring back too, and we decide together what it is. Agreed?"

It was a strange instruction. Gideon noted it, weighed it, filed it. "Agreed," she said.

* * *

She woke at five to a sky the color of pewter and a wind that smelled of ice, and walked out along the harbor road while the town slept. The headland rose ahead of her, black rock laced with old tide lines, and the sea was doing something she'd read about in the contract's fine print but hadn't truly imagined: leaving. It drew back and back, uncovering kelp forests lying flat as drowned hair, sandbars gleaming, boulders furred with barnacle, the whole seabed exhaling. And there, at the headland's root, where the cliff met the retreating water, was a gap.

Not a gap. An entrance. Four meters wide, three high, going back into the rock beyond the reach of the gray dawn light, its floor wet-shining and strewn with the sea's leavings — driftwood, tangle, a boot sole, stones rounded like knuckles. Ten years of tide had kept this door, and now the moon and the storm and whatever else the ferryman had meant had taken the lock off it for a few hours a day, for a few days only.

Gideon stood at the edge of it with her hands in her pockets and her breath smoking, and every instinct she owned — the ones that had kept her alive through fifteen years of mountains and the eleven months since, which had required more skill — told her the same thing at the same volume: *this is not your kind of trouble.* Caves the locals won't name. Contracts signed by a man who flinches at windows. A town with one story and a vow of silence. She had exactly enough money to leave, if she left today.

But the tide was going out, and the window was nine days, and she had given her word to a man with a ruined back and a daughter doing sums behind her eyes, and — this was the truth she circled without landing on — she had nowhere else to go that wasn't just another version of here. Momentum. It carried you whether you believed in the far shore or not.

She checked her watch. Five hours to slack water. Behind her, footsteps on the gravel road — Mara, in rubber boots and a canvas jacket, carrying coiled line over one shoulder and two sets of fins over the other, her face set against the wind.

"You couldn't sleep either," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"I don't sleep much." Gideon looked back at the entrance, at the darkness inside it where the water lay flat and waiting, and felt the old pull she'd sworn off — the pull of the unnamed thing, the closed door, the route marked *do not attempt*. "Your father said anything that isn't obviously salvage comes back to him."

"He did."

"He didn't say why."

Mara followed her gaze to the cave, and for a moment something moved behind her face — quick, guarded, gone. "No," she said. "He didn't."

The tide slid further out, and the cave breathed, and somewhere inland, high above the town, the glacier held whatever it held.
