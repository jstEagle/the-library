## 2. What the Sea Kept

The tide gave her four hours. Gideon had counted them twice against the almanac in the harbormaster's office, and the old man behind the counter had watched her do it with the flat patience of someone who'd already decided she was going to die and only wanted to know what day to mention it to his wife.

"Four hours," he'd said, when she finally asked him directly. "Maybe three and a half if the swell's up from the southwest. And the cave doesn't like visitors."

"The cave," Gideon said, "doesn't like anything. It's a hole in rock."

He hadn't smiled. Neither did anyone in Skarnvik, now that she thought about it — a town built of gray timber and gray stone at the throat of a fjord so deep the water looked like poured iron. They were polite the way people are polite at a funeral. She'd been paid half her fee up front by Mara Vinter, the salvager's daughter, in coins counted out on a workbench still smelling of tar and diesel, and the girl had looked at her with an expression Gideon couldn't quite read — something between apology and warning.

*You're the first one who's said yes in eleven years,* Mara had told her.

Gideon should have asked why it had been eleven years. Instead she'd pocketed the coins, because she was down to her last forty kroner and a reputation that opened exactly no doors anywhere north of Bergen, and because momentum was the only thing keeping her upright these days. You didn't stop to ask questions when stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the accident, and the accident meant —

She shut that door the way she always did, firmly, and turned her face into the wind coming off the water.

* * *

The cave mouth sat at the base of the northern headland, revealed only when the tide dropped below the two-meter mark — a black seam in the cliff face, maybe three meters wide, narrowing toward the back like the pupil of an eye. The locals called it nothing at all. That was the thing she'd noticed in her two days in Skarnvik: not that they avoided speaking its name, but that they behaved as though it had never been given one. On the nautical charts it was marked as a mere indentation in the coastline, *skjærhulen*, skerry hollow, the kind of label cartographers give features they've never bothered to look at. The fishermen gave it a wider berth than they gave the reef at the harbor mouth, and the reef had sunk two boats in living memory.

Gideon stood on the wet shelf of rock below it and let the wind push at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades.

Her gear was modest: headlamp, spare lamp, a coil of static rope she'd hocked and re-bought twice in the last year, a knife, chalk bag, the waterproof shell that had once been expedition-grade and was now held together at the left shoulder by duct tape gone gray. A guide's kit stripped down to a scavenger's kit. Somewhere south of here there was a family who still sent her a card every December, unsigned, and she burned them unread, and she kept sending them money through a lawyer who never asked questions.

*Three and a half hours.* The tide was already sighing out of the cave mouth, draining back in long, reluctant pulses, as if the sea were breathing around whatever lay inside.

She went in at the low-water slack, when the surge eased to a shallow rill running over rounded cobbles, and the ceiling came down low enough that she had to bend nearly double for the first ten meters. Cold air moved past her, out of the dark, steady as a draft from a cellar door. Caves breathed. Every caver knew that. But this breath smelled wrong — not of salt and rot and seabird guano, but of something older and drier underneath, mineral and cold, like the air off a glacier's tongue.

Like the air, she thought, of the high country. Two hundred kilometers inland. A thousand meters up.

That was impossible, and she noted it, and kept walking, because curiosity had always been the flaw in her armor. Instinct said turn around. Curiosity said: *the air has to come from somewhere.*

* * *

Past the entrance squeeze the passage opened, and her headlamp beam lost itself in volume rather than distance. She was standing in a chamber the size of a church nave, walls of banded gneiss slicked black with moisture, the floor a jumble of storm-tossed debris — driftwood bleached white as bone, tangles of kelp drying into brittle ropes, fishing floats, half a rowboat's hull driven in by some winter gale decades ago and left where the sea had thrown it. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the regularity of a clock.

And everywhere, the marks of human hands.

Not recent ones. Old ones, weathered soft: soot smears on the wall where lamps had leaned, a rusted iron ring bolted into the rock at chest height, grooves worn into a ledge as if generations of palms had gripped it. She ran her fingers along the ring. It turned freely, oiled by use long ago or by someone more recently. There were other things too, once she started looking — a wooden peg hammered into a crack, the kind used for stretching nets; a shard of green glass; and, scratched into the wall beside the peg, a set of tally marks, dozens of them, grouped in fives, the hand that cut them growing shakier toward the end.

Someone had lived here. Or waited here. For a long time, or many times.

Gideon stood very still in the dripping dark and felt the hair rise on her forearms beneath her shell jacket. This was not a cave the sea had merely opened. This was a cave people had *used*, and used deliberately, and the town above had agreed, all together, without ever saying a word to each other, to forget it.

*Curiosity,* she reminded herself, *is how clients die.* But her boots were already moving deeper.

* * *

The passage narrowed again beyond the chamber, climbing gently over a sill of smooth rock scoured by some ancient flow, and here the character of the cave changed. The salt smell faded. The walls dried. And the drip of the seawater was joined by another sound, fainter, from ahead — a rushing whisper, continuous, like wind through a keyhole.

A river. Underground. Her pulse picked up with an eagerness she distrusted.

The passage bent left, then opened into a second chamber, smaller, drier, and her lamp caught the far wall and stopped her cold.

There was writing on it. Painted, not carved — a stencil of white marine paint, the kind used on hulls and crates, applied by someone working fast with a cut template. The letters had run slightly, but they were legible:

**N.A.E. — STATION 3 — DO NOT DESCEND WITHOUT LINE**

Below it, smaller, in different handwriting, in what looked like grease pencil refreshed over the years until the original strokes were buried under layers of renewal:

*Vandret ved lavvann. Ikke alene.* — *Passable at low water. Not alone.*

Beneath that, a date, renewed in the same way: **1911**.

Gideon's breath fogged in the cold air. 1911. Sixty years ago. Someone had been maintaining this message within the last decade — the grease pencil was bright, barely smudged. Which meant someone in Skarnvik, recently, had come down here at low tide with a pencil stub and a steady hand, and touched up a warning left by men sixty years dead.

*Nordlands Arktiske Ekspedisjon.* She knew the name the way any mountain person knew it — the way you know the name of a ship that went down with all hands. The NAE had gone onto the Svartisen icecap in the spring of '11, nine men and a fortune in backing, hunting the source of the underground river that local legend said drained the glacier's heart. Seven had come back. Two hadn't. And the seven who returned had scattered to the ends of the country and never, in fifty years of newspapermen offering them money, said one word about what happened up there.

The legend said the mountain took them. Legends usually mean *someone made sure we couldn't talk.*

She heard the river clearly now, somewhere below and ahead, a deep continuous roar muffled by stone. Station 3. Which meant stations 1 and 2 existed, further in, further up. An expedition doesn't number its camps unless it intends to come back.

Gideon clicked her spare lamp on, checked her watch — ninety minutes since slack, the tide already turning somewhere behind her in the dark — and told herself she was just going to look at the next chamber. Just the next one. Then out, before the water came home.

* * *

Station 2 was a ledge camp above a side channel where the underground river showed itself for the first time: a flood of meltwater pouring through a slot in the rock, white and furious even now, in the dry weeks of late summer. In spring, she realized, looking at the scour-marks two meters above the current waterline, this whole lower cave would be full. The NAE had timed their pushes for the autumn low. Everything about the place spoke of method, patience, competence — and of men who knew they were doing something their backers would deny knowing about.

It was in Station 2, wedged into a crack behind a flat stone that had been pried loose and replaced — the fresh scrape-marks on the surrounding grit told her that much before she'd even lifted it — that she found the satchel.

Rubberized canvas, brass buckles gone green, the flap stamped with the same stencil: **N.A.E.** It was heavy. It was dry inside. Whatever sealant they'd used in 1911, it had done its job, because when she worked the buckles open with numb fingers, the oilcloth wrapping within crackled like new.

Inside: a brass compass, glass intact, needle swinging lazy and true. A tin of waxed matches, half spent. A folded chart, linen-backed, hand-annotated — and she spread it carefully on her knee and her breath stopped.

It was the glacier. The whole massif, drawn from survey data she recognized as decades ahead of anything official, and running beneath it, in confident ink, a line: the river's course, traced from the sea cave through the mountain's roots to a point high on the icecap marked with a small, careful circle and a single word in pencil, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Kilden.* The source.

And last, wrapped separately in its own oilcloth sleeve, a journal. Bound leather, swollen with damp decades but sound, the spine cracked white. The name on the flyleaf was written in a copperplate hand:

**Eirik Vinter. Expedition diarist.**

Vinter.

Gideon sat down on the cold rock without deciding to. The name was common enough on this coast — half the graveyards between here and Trondheim were full of Vinters. But the salvage contract in her pocket had been signed *M. Vinter*, and the girl who'd signed it had watched Gideon take the job with that look between apology and warning, and had said, *you're the first one who's said yes in eleven years*, and Gideon had been too broke and too tired to ask the obvious question.

Eleven years. What had happened eleven years ago?

She opened the journal.

* * *

Eirik Vinter wrote the way surveyors draw — precise, economical, every word load-bearing. She read by lamplight with the tide roaring faintly closer behind her and the river roaring ahead, and the entries pulled her forward page by page the way the current pulled at the slot in the rock.

May 1911: the approach up the fjord. June: Station 1 established, the sea cave mapped. July: the push inland along the gorge, following the river where it vanished under the glacier's snout. August: *we have entered the mountain. The tunnel runs true. Halvard says the ice is singing.*

Then September, and the handwriting changed. Not worse — tighter. Frightened people often write more carefully, as if neatness could hold the world together.

*We have found it. I will not write here what we have found. The patron's instructions were explicit on this point and I understand them now, God forgive me, I understand them completely. What is in the mountain is worth more than the mountain. Worth more than the coast. There are men in Kristiania who would burn this valley to the waterline to own it, and one of those men is our patron, and he has known what we would find since before we ever shipped north.*

*I have hidden the duplicate record where only my family would think to look. If we do not return, let them know: the danger was never the ice.*

The entries after that grew short. Rations short. Weather turning. And then the last entry, dated October 9th, 1911, written in a hand gone ragged at the edges:

*Seven of us will walk out tomorrow. We have agreed on the story. The mountain took the others. May the mountain forgive us for what we let be done in its name.*

Two men had stayed on the ice. Or been made to stay. And Eirik Vinter had walked down out of the mountains carrying a secret worth a nation's ransom, hidden a duplicate of the record in the cave his family had guarded for generations — *only my family would think to look* — and then gone home to Skarnvik and kept silent for the rest of his life.

Gideon closed the journal and sat in the dark with her heart going hard and slow, the way it did before a bad pitch.

The patron. The journal didn't name him. Of course it didn't — Vinter had been careful even in hiding. But a shipping magnate in a fjord town, rich enough in 1911 to bankroll an arctic expedition, whose family still owned half the waterfront a lifetime later — she'd seen the name painted on three warehouses and a chapel roof since she'd arrived. Vey. Aldous Vey, who had bought her dinner the night before with courtesy fine enough to slice, and asked, smiling, whether she'd found anything interesting in the cave, and accepted her *nothing yet* with a nod that had somehow felt like a hand checking her pockets.

The tide. She checked her watch and the arithmetic hit her like cold water: two hours ten minutes since slack. The entrance passage would be flooding now, the low squeeze underwater, the current in the slot channel rising. She had perhaps forty minutes to get out, and the exit was a hundred meters of flooded crawl behind her.

She got the journal, the chart, and the compass into her own drybag, cinched it down, and turned for the entrance — and her lamp swept the wall of Station 2, and stopped.

Boot prints. In the fine glacial silt along the ledge, partially smeared by old seepage but crisp-edged, unweathered: days old at most. Two sets, large, hobnailed or lug-soled, coming in and going out. And beside them, in the dust of the supply crate the NAE had left — a crate now empty, its contents long since carried away — the clean rectangle where something the size of a strongbox had sat until very recently.

Someone had found this station before her. Had searched it, taken what they wanted, replaced the stones, touched up the grease-pencil warnings to keep the curious away — and missed the satchel behind the flat rock, or missed it until now, because the scrape-marks around that stone were fresher than everything else. Fresher than the boot prints.

Hours old. Maybe less.

Gideon killed her lamp and stood in absolute darkness, listening. The river roared. The tide breathed in the entrance passage behind her, longer and harder now, the sea coming home. And somewhere — above, in the entrance chamber, or out on the rocks where the wind covered everything — she could not tell whether she heard footsteps, or only wanted to.

The trap wasn't sprung ahead of her. It had been sprung behind her, quietly, while she read a dead man's handwriting. Whoever had searched this cave knew someone else was coming. Had perhaps *made sure* someone else was coming — a broke, blacklisted outsider with no ties and no protection, hired by a Vinter, pointed at the one place in Skarnvik nobody would follow her into.

*Four hours,* the harbormaster had said, watching her with flat patience. As if he already knew how the arithmetic would fall.

Gideon switched her lamp back on, put the drybag strap across her chest, and started for the entrance at a pace just short of a run, because the tide was rising and the water in the low squeeze was already knee-deep and pulling, and because whatever was waiting for her outside the cave, she liked her odds better in daylight, on open rock, with the journal against her ribs and the whole weight of a sixty-year lie finally, finally in her hands.

Behind her, deep in the mountain, the river sang on toward its forbidden source — and ahead, at the mouth of the cave, silhouetted against the graying evening light, someone was standing on the rocks, waiting for her to come out.
