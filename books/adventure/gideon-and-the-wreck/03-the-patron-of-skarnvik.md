## 3. The Patron of Skarnvik

The invitation came the way weather comes to a fjord — without warning and with an air of inevitability.

Gideon was in the workshop's back room, cataloguing the salvage by lamplight, when Mara appeared in the doorway with a folded card on a silver tray, as if the tray itself were part of the message. *Aldous Vey requests the pleasure of your company at Veysholm, this evening, seven o'clock.* The card stock was heavy enough to stop a bullet. Someone had written her name at the bottom in ink that caught the light like oil on water.

"He doesn't invite people," Mara said. Her arms were crossed; she leaned against the doorframe as though she might need it to hold herself upright. "He summons them."

"Then I'm summoned."

"You could decline."

"I've declined better men than him." Gideon set down the inventory ledger. "Worse ones too. It never seems to matter." She looked at the card again. *Veysholm.* She'd seen the house from the water that first morning — a long grey pile of granite and glass perched on the headland above the harbor, all vertical lines and cold light, like something the mountain had grown rather than something anyone had built. From the deck of the ferry it had looked less like a home than a watchtower.

Mara hadn't moved. "You should know what you're walking into."

"I know what I'm walking into. A rich man wants something I found in his fjord." She stood, rolled her shoulders until her spine cracked, and felt the exhaustion shift like ballast. Three days of bad sleep and saltwater dreams. "The only question is what he thinks it's worth, and whether his number and mine are going to be neighbors."

"They won't be," Mara said quietly. And then, when Gideon looked at her: "Nothing in Skarnvik is ever worth what it costs."

* * *

Veysholm at dusk was lit from within like a lantern, every window burning amber against the blue hour, and the effect was so deliberate that Gideon understood it immediately as architecture of a different kind than stone. This was a man who had decided how he wished to be seen and had built the seeing.

A housekeeper let her in without a word and led her through rooms that smelled of beeswax and old money and, beneath both, faintly, of diesel — the smell of the fleet, the smell of the actual business, no matter how much polish sat on top of it. The walls held paintings of ships. Not the pretty kind. Working hulls, ice-scarred, riding heavy, painted by someone who had clearly been paid to make them look noble anyway. In the last frame before the study, a younger Aldous Vey stood on a quay in a good coat, one hand resting on a bollard as if the harbor belonged to him.

It did, of course. That was the joke the painting was making.

He rose from behind a desk of dark timber when she entered — a tall man gone slightly stooped, silver-haired, with a face like a cliff face: handsome from a distance, all fracture up close. His handshake was dry and precisely calibrated, firm enough to signal respect, brief enough to signal that respect was a currency he spent carefully.

"Gideon Blackwood." He said her name the way a man tests a rope — taking its weight, checking for fraying. "The guide. Or the former guide. Forgive me — I understand the distinction matters to you."

"It does."

"Then let us not pretend otherwise." He gestured her into a chair across the desk from him and sat back down himself, folding his hands. On the desk between them lay nothing. No papers, no photographs, no leverage displayed for effect. Just clean wood and a single lamp turned low. He had learned, somewhere in forty years of buying towns, that an empty table made people fill it themselves.

"You'll take tea," he said. It wasn't a question. Tea arrived — he must have signaled the housekeeper with a look Gideon missed — and he waited until she'd taken the cup before he spoke again, so that her first act in his study would be acceptance of his hospitality. She noticed it. She drank anyway. Refusing would have been its own kind of tell.

"I'll be direct," Vey said, "because I'm told you're a woman who respects directness, and because courtesy wasted on the wrong audience is a weakness." A thin smile. "You recovered items from the cave at Gråtide. The salvage manifest lists a satchel, waterproofed, military pattern. The manifest lists a journal inside it. I would like to see the journal."

"The manifest is accurate."

"And the journal?"

"Is mine. Salvage rights are written into my contract. Your harbormaster signed it."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Interest. The interest of a man who has played this game ten thousand times and is pleased, briefly, to find an opponent who knows the rules. "Contracts," he said, "are how the sea talks to lawyers. Out here we have older arrangements." He leaned forward, and the lamp threw his shadow up the wall behind him, huge and slightly wrong, like a second, larger Vey standing at his shoulder. "I'll pay you three times the salvage fee. Tonight. Cash, or transfer, whichever you prefer. The journal, and anything else you found in that cave — charts, instruments, personal effects. All of it comes to me, and the matter ends there."

Three times the fee. It was a fortune dressed as a rounding error, and they both knew it, and that was the point. The number wasn't an offer. It was a measurement — of the journal's value, of his urgency, of exactly how much trouble the thing in her pack was worth to the most powerful man on this coast.

Gideon set down her cup. She made the small motion last as long as she decently could.

"What's in it?" she asked.

"Pardon?"

"The journal. What's in it that's worth three times a salvage contract to a shipping magnate who owns everything else in this town already? I've read enough of it to know it's not cargo manifests. It's a dead man's handwriting, Mr. Vey. Dead men write about two things — where they were going, and who sent them." She met his eyes. "Which one are you buying?"

For a moment the room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the harbor breathed — halyards ticking against masts, the long slow slap of water on pilings. Vey's face did not change, which was itself a change; a man that controlled had to spend effort not reacting, and she watched him spend it.

"You've read it," he said.

"Some."

"Then you know it's the property of grieving families. Men who died with their names attached to a failure. My failure, in the town's telling — I financed the expedition, openly, and I have carried the blame for twenty years with whatever grace I could manufacture." He spread his hands, and the gesture was beautiful, practiced, hollow. "I would like to give those families their dead back with some dignity intact. Diaries full of despair and recrimination serve no one. There are children in this town who grew up believing their fathers drowned doing something brave. I'd prefer they keep that."

"That's almost moving," Gideon said. "Say it again slower and I might cry."

The temperature of the room dropped by a degree she couldn't measure but could feel, the way you feel a glacier before you see one. Vey studied her for a long moment, and she had the distinct sensation of being appraised not as an opponent but as an object — tonnage, draft, seaworthiness. Whether she could be bought, and if not bought, then sunk.

"You're not what I expected," he said finally. "The reports described a woman running from something. Exhausted. Careless with her own safety. People like that usually want the money more than they want the principle."

"People like that usually haven't been offered money by someone who needs them to take it."

"Need." He tasted the word and didn't like it. "I don't need anything from you, Ms. Blackwood. I am offering you a kindness. You're a stranger in a town that doesn't love strangers, holding an object that certain people died near. Skarnvik remembers the cave. Skarnvik remembers the expedition. And Skarnvik, despite appearances, takes its instructions from me — which means that tonight, while you sit in my warm study drinking my tea, you are the safest you will be in this fjord." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was worse. "I'd think about what happens after you leave."

"Is that a threat?"

"Heavens, no. It's geography." He stood, and she stood with him, and the handshake he offered this time lasted half a beat longer than the first. "Keep the journal tonight. Sleep on my offer. Triple the fee, and my protection besides — which out here is worth triple the cash. You'll find me reasonable. Everyone finds me reasonable, right up until they don't."

At the door he stopped her with one sentence, delivered gently, almost kindly, to the back of her head:

"Do give my regards to Mara Halvorsen. Her father owes me a great deal. I do hope she understands what a fragile thing a debt can be."

* * *

She walked back down through the town with the offer sitting in her chest like swallowed lead.

Triple the fee. She turned the number over and over on the walk, testing it against the arithmetic of her life. Triple the fee cleared the debt on her gear. It bought passage south, a season of breathing room, maybe — maybe — the start of buying back a name. Every practical instinct she owned screamed *take it*. Take the money, hand over the dead man's book, let the sea keep its secrets the way it always had. She owed the dead nothing. She barely owed the living anything. That had been the whole point of coming to a place where nobody knew her face.

But she kept hearing the way he'd said *need*. Not needing it. Needing her not to have it. Those were different hungers, and only one of them got men killed.

And she kept seeing the journal's last legible line, the one she'd read by lamplight three nights ago and told no one about, the one that had been sitting in her like a fishhook ever since: *If we do not return, ask Vey why the ship left early.*

She climbed the stairs to the loft above the workshop with the harbor lights swimming below her, and she told herself she'd decide in the morning, the way people tell themselves things at night.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and hard, and with it the discovery that she had already been robbed.

Not robbed — searched. Nothing was missing. That was the obscenity of it. Her pack sat where she'd left it, but the buckles were rethreaded wrong, the straps tucked under in a way she never tucked them, and the false seam in the roll of her sleeping mat — where she kept the spare compass and the folded emergency chart — had been opened and restitched by someone competent, someone quick, someone who had done this to other people's belongings enough times to have a technique.

She went through everything with the methodical fury of a woman counting fingers after a handshake. Journal: still there, still wrapped in oilcloth, still hidden inside the hollowed heel of a spare boot. Charts: untouched. The little tin of letters she carried and never read: undisturbed, or disturbed so well she couldn't tell. They had taken nothing because they had found nothing, and they wanted her to know it. That was the message. Not *we can reach your things.* *We already did.*

Downstairs, the workshop smelled of coffee and sawdust, and Mara was at the bench repairing a winch drum, her hands black with grease, and when she looked up at Gideon's face she knew — Gideon watched the knowledge arrive in her, watched her go still.

"They were here," Mara said. "Last night. After you left for Veysholm. Two men I didn't recognize, in a boat I didn't recognize. My father told me to stay in my room." Her jaw tightened around the words. "He said it the way you say things when you're ashamed of them."

"They took nothing."

"No. They wouldn't. Taking something proves theft. Looking at everything proves ownership." Mara wiped her hands slowly on a rag, and her eyes came up, and there was something raw underneath the anger — fear, maybe, or its close cousin, shame. "That's how he works. Nobody in this town has ever been able to prove a single thing about Aldous Vey. Not the boats he's lost, not the men he's ruined, not the prices he sets. He doesn't break laws. He owns the space where laws would be."

Gideon set her pack on the bench between them, deliberately, and unlaced the boot, and drew out the oilcloth bundle, and laid the journal on the scarred wood in the morning light.

"Then it's time somebody put something in that space," she said.

Mara looked at the book — at the salt-stained cover, the swollen pages, the ghost of a dead man's hand pressing through from twenty years ago — and then at Gideon, and whatever she saw in Gideon's face made her set down the rag.

"There's something you should know about that journal," Mara said quietly. "About who wrote it. Before you decide what it's worth."

Outside, the tide was turning, and far up the fjord, so faint it might have been thunder, the glacier spoke.
