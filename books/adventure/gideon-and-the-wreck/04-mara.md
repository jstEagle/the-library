## 4. Mara

Mara had watched the stranger from the moment the ferry bumped against the pilings, and she had learned three things in three days.

First: Gideon Blackwood ate like someone who expected the meal to be taken away. Fast, efficient, no pleasure in it — bread torn, fish stripped to bone, coffee drunk scalding because waiting was a luxury she'd forgotten how to afford.

Second: she checked the door of every room she entered. Not obviously. A flick of the eyes, half a second, cataloguing exits the way other people checked the weather.

Third: she was good. Better than good. Mara's father had hired her for the cave salvage because she was cheap and desperate and came with her own gear, and by the second morning she had organized their winch lines and air tanks and tide tables into a system so clean it looked like it had always existed, like the workshop had simply been waiting for her hands.

Mara knew what that kind of competence cost. Her grandfather had been that competent. It had gotten him exactly nowhere except dead, somewhere out under the glacier, in a place the town agreed not to think about.

So on the third night, after her father had gone up to bed and the workshop lamps were turned down to embers, Mara climbed the stairs to the loft where Blackwood slept and did not knock before she opened the door.

The woman was already awake. Of course she was. She sat on the edge of the cot with her boots on, knife loose in her hand, and when she saw who it was she didn't put the knife away — just set it on the blanket between them like a fact being stated.

"You didn't knock," Gideon said.

"You'd have heard me either way." Mara leaned against the doorframe. "I want to talk about the cave."

"Your father's contract covers the salvage."

"My father's contract doesn't cover what you found."

Something moved behind Blackwood's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. But Mara had grown up reading faces across a salvage table, watching buyers decide how much to lie about the value of what they wanted. She knew the look of a person recalculating.

"I don't know what you mean," Gideon said.

"The satchel. Waterproof, brass-buckled, military pattern. You brought it up the winch line wrapped in your jacket like it was a wounded bird." Mara stepped into the room and let the door swing shut behind her. "My father taught me every trick of this trade, Ms. Blackwood, and hiding the best find from the client is the oldest one there is. I'm not angry about it. I'm asking what was in it."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the eaves and, far below, the sea slapping the pilings in its patient way.

"A journal," Gideon said finally. "Waterlogged but legible. An expedition log."

"Which expedition?"

"You know which one."

And there it was. Mara felt it land in her chest like a dropped anchor — not surprise, because she had known, had known since she saw the brass buckle, but confirmation is its own kind of wound. She sat down on the crate opposite the cot because her legs suggested sitting might be wise.

"The Vey Expedition," she said. "That's what my grandfather called it, before he stopped calling it anything. Before he rowed up the fjord with five other men and never came back."

"He was on it?"

"He financed his own berth. Sold our boat — the *Kestrel*, the first boat my family ever owned — to buy his place among them. My grandmother never forgave him. My father grew up on stories about it. The glacier eats what it takes, he used to say. That was the whole legend. The ice took six men and gave back nothing, and the town decided the lesson was don't go looking." She looked up. "You've read it. You know something we don't."

Gideon's jaw worked. Mara watched her weigh it — watched the arithmetic run behind those grey eyes, the same arithmetic Mara had seen her do over the tide tables, risk against return, exposure against silence.

"There's a river," Gideon said at last. "Under the glacier. The journal describes it. It runs through the ice to a source — something they went looking for and apparently got close enough to find that somebody didn't want them coming home."

"Somebody."

"The handwriting changes halfway through. The last entries are different ink, different hand. Someone else finished the log." Gideon paused. "Or started finishing it."

Mara turned that over. Outside, a dog barked twice down by the cannery and quit, as if corrected.

"And you sat on this," she said. "Three days. You've been diving my father's contract and sitting on my grandfather's grave."

"Yes."

"No apology?"

"Would you believe one?"

It was such an honest answer that it almost made Mara laugh. Almost. "No."

"Then I won't waste it." Gideon set the knife fully aside, deliberate, showing her empty hands. "Here's what I'll give you instead. I came to Skarnvik because it was far from anywhere I have a reputation, and the work was ugly and nobody would ask questions. I wasn't looking for your grandfather or anyone's ghost. But I've read that journal cover to cover now, twice, and I can tell you that whoever wrote those last pages was afraid. Not of the ice. Of people."

"People," Mara repeated. "Which people? There were six men and a glacier."

"There were also money and maps. Expeditions like that don't outfit themselves." Gideon stood, restless, and moved to the small window that looked out over the black water of the harbor. From here you could see the Vey Shipping offices, still lit, one window burning yellow on the top floor at an hour when honest businesses slept. "Who bankrolled it? Six men, a chartered vessel, cold-weather kit that would have cost more than this whole workshop. Someone paid."

Mara felt the answer arrive before she allowed it into her mouth. It arrived the way bad news always did in Skarnvik — quietly, from the direction of the big house on the hill.

"Nobody ever said," she said slowly. "The inquiry was closed fast. Vey Shipping handled the families' compensation. Generously, everyone said. My father got a settlement that kept us fed for two years after Grandfather died." She heard her own voice go flat. "Aldous Vey paid for my childhood."

Gideon turned from the window. For a moment neither of them spoke, and in that silence Mara understood that something had passed between them — not trust, nothing so clean as trust, but recognition. The look of two women who had both done sums they didn't like about the people who held their leashes.

"You're bound to him," Gideon said. It wasn't a question.

"We're all bound to him. Half this town owes Vey Shipping money, and the other half works for it. My father's debt bought the winch, the compressor, the dive gear you've been using all week." Mara spread her hands. "You want to know why I confronted you tonight instead of going to him? Because if I go to him, I do it knowing what I am. And if I don't, I do it knowing what I am too. There isn't a version of this where I'm free."

Gideon looked at her for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had lost its careful flatness.

"I know the shape of that cage," she said. "Mine just has better views."

"What did you do?"

"That's not mine to tell tonight."

"Whose, then?"

"The mountain's." Something crossed her face then — brief and terrible, a door opening onto a lit room and shutting again. "Ask me again when we're somewhere high and cold. I tell it better where there's weather."

Mara studied her. The famous Gideon Blackwood — she'd placed the name on the second day, once she bothered to ask the ferry clerk for the passenger manifest out of professional habit. *Blackwood.* The guide from the Kebnekaise accident, the one the papers had chewed for a season: the client who died on the rope, the inquiry, the license revoked, the name dragged through every climbing forum from Tromsø to Vancouver. Mara had read the articles at two in the morning and then sat in the dark workshop thinking about what it must be to be so good at the thing that defines you, and to have the thing turn on you in front of witnesses.

"You could have lied to me," Mara said. "About the journal. About any of it. Why didn't you?"

"Because you knocked without knocking." The ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile touched Gideon's mouth. "Because you came up here alone, at night, with no leverage except the truth, and bet it all on me being worth talking to. I've met a lot of people who hedge. You didn't hedge. People who don't hedge tend to be worth telling things to."

"It wasn't brave. If you'd been Vey's creature, I'd be in the harbor right now."

"If I'd been Vey's creature, I'd have taken his money on day one and been gone." Gideon crouched, bringing herself level, forearms on knees — a guide's posture, Mara realized, the posture of someone talking a nervous client off a bad ledge. "Listen to me. That journal names names. I haven't finished the last page — the water got to it — but I will, tomorrow, with alcohol and patience. And when I do, one of two things happens. Either it says what I think it says, and Aldous Vey sent six men into that glacier and closed the door behind them. Or it doesn't, and I sell the thing back to you and your father for the salvage fee and catch the next ferry south."

"And if it does say it?"

Gideon held her gaze. "Then you have a decision to make. And I'd rather you made it knowing than not."

Mara sat with that. Below them, the workshop breathed — the tick of cooling metal, the sea against the hull of the *Kestrel II*, the boat her father had built with Vey's loan money and named, in some defiance nobody said aloud, after the one Vey's world had taken.

There was a version of tonight where she walked downstairs and told her father everything, and her father did what he always did: calculated, endured, survived. He was good at surviving. He'd been doing it for thirty years inside a town that belonged to another man's money.

There was a version where she went to Vey himself, up in the lit window, and traded the journal for her father's debt, and bought her family out of the cage with her grandfather's bones. Clean transaction. Everyone in Skarnvik would understand it. Everyone in Skarnvik would do it.

She thought about her grandfather's face from the one photograph that survived — squinting into sun on the deck of a charter boat, rope coiled on his shoulder, grinning like a man going toward something instead of away from it. Six men. The ice took them and gave back nothing. Except now the ice had given something back, at the bottom of a forbidden cave, at the lowest tide in ten years, into the hands of a disgraced stranger who owed this town nothing.

Maybe that was how the truth traveled. Not through loyal people. Through people with nothing left to lose.

"Tomorrow," Mara said, standing. "You finish the last page. And you do it here, in the workshop, where I'm standing next to you. Not alone in this loft with the door shut."

"Fair."

"And Blackwood — if it names Vey, you come to me before you go to anyone else. Before you sell it, before you run, before you get clever. Me first. Swear it."

Gideon rose too, and for a moment the lamplight caught the old scar along her jaw that Mara had noticed the first day and pretended not to. "Me first," she echoed. "Sworn."

Mara nodded once and reached for the door. Her hand was on the latch when Gideon spoke again, quiet, almost to herself.

"For what it's worth — whoever wrote those last entries loved your grandfather. You can tell. People write differently about the dead they respected."

Mara stood very still in the doorway, looking out at the harbor, at the single yellow window burning on the hill above the town like an eye that never closed.

"Then he'd better hope," she said, "that whoever killed him has forgotten how to count tides."

She went down the stairs without looking back, and behind her she heard the loft go silent — the particular silence of a woman lying awake, listening, doing sums of her own.

* * *

Her father was still up.

She found him in the workshop, not in bed, sitting at the long table with a cup of tea gone cold beside him and the salvage ledger open under the lamp. He didn't look surprised to see her. He rarely did; twenty-six years of raising her had worn the surprise out of him.

"Long conversation," he said. "Upstairs."

"You were listening."

"I was minding my business, which happened to be directly beneath yours." He turned a page without reading it. "Well? Did you get it out of her?"

"Most of it."

"And?"

Mara pulled out the stool across from him and sat, elbows on the scarred wood, the way she had since she was nine years old and learning knots. "It's the expedition, Da. Grandfather's expedition. There's a journal."

Her father's hands went still on the ledger. Not dramatically — just still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Vey will want that," he said.

"I know."

"He offered her triple already. She told me today, laughing about it, like it was a joke." He rubbed his thumb along the table's edge, back and forth, a habit older than Mara. "Triple the fee. For paper."

"She refused."

"I know she refused. I also know Aldous Vey." He looked up at her then, and in the lamplight Mara could see the whole map of him — the young man who'd lost his father and his boat in the same winter, the survivor, the debtor, the man who had spent his life paying installments on his own grief. "When Vey wants a thing, Mara, he doesn't send men to take it. He sends obligations. He calls in a favor, or he offers to forgive one, and you find yourself handing it over and thanking him for the privilege. That's how he's run this town for forty years. Nobody's ever robbed Skarnvik. Skarnvik sold itself, piece by piece, politely."

"So what do we do?"

Her father was quiet for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, the tide was turning — she could feel it in the pilings, the faint change in the water's voice against the hull, the sea beginning its slow withdrawal from the cave it had opened.

"We do what we've always done," he said at last. "We survive. Whatever she found, whatever it names — it's not ours. Our contract is salvage, and salvage is done Friday, and Friday we get paid, and Monday the ferry takes her south and this town goes back to sleeping soundly. That's the play, Mara. It's the only play we can afford."

"And if the journal says Vey murdered Grandfather?"

Her father closed the ledger, gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

"Then it says it," he said. "And we survive anyway."

He kissed the top of her head, the way he had when she was small, and carried his cold tea up to bed, and Mara sat alone in the workshop with the lamp and the ledger and the sound of the turning tide, and understood that her father had just told her, in the only language he had left, that he was afraid.

Not of the ice. Of people.

Just like the journal said.

Outside, the sea kept pulling back from the land, baring more of the cave's black mouth, and above the town the yellow window on the hill burned on — awake, she was suddenly certain, awake and watching, the way it had burned every night since a stranger stepped off the ferry with a salvager's contract and a dead man's secrets in her kit.

Friday, her father said. Two days.

Mara looked at the clock, and at the stairs leading up to the loft where Gideon Blackwood lay awake over a journal that named names, and began, quietly, to plan how she was going to be standing next to that woman at dawn — whether she swore it or not.
