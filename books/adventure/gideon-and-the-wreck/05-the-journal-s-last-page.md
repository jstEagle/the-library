## 5. The Journal's Last Page

Gideon didn't sleep. She told herself it was the wind off the fjord, the unfamiliar creak of the salvager's boathouse where Mara had stowed her for the night, the salt-stiff blankets that smelled of tar and old rope. But that was a lie she let herself keep until midnight, and after midnight there was no point pretending.

The satchel sat on the workbench under a hanging lamp, dripping its last meltwater into a tin pan. She'd dried the journal page by page over the stove, careful as a surgeon, and now it lay open in front of her like something dissected — forty-odd leaves of waterproofed paper, the ink feathered where the sea had gotten in but stubbornly legible, written in a hand that started out confident and ended up cramped and slanting, as if the writer had been losing light.

She'd read expedition journals before. Dozens of them. They all had the same architecture: optimism at the front, inventory in the middle, and either triumph or silence at the end. This one had a third thing she'd never seen, and it was threaded through every page like wire through meat.

Fear of their own patron.

* * *

She went back to the beginning to be sure.

The first entry was dated eleven years ago, early spring. *Departed Skarnvik under charter of A.V., terms confidential per contract.* No name. Just initials, and a payment schedule written in a code so simple Gideon cracked it over two cups of bad coffee — a substitution cipher keyed to the first line of some hymn or folk song the writer assumed everyone knew. The amounts were staggering. Enough to outfit three expeditions. Enough to buy a small shipping company, if a man were inclined to expand.

*A.V. insists on discretion,* the writer noted. *Says the source must be secured before any survey is registered. Says men who talk about what they find below the ice don't get to come back and talk about it.*

Gideon had read that line four times, telling herself it was expedition bravado, the kind of joke tired men made around stoves. But the next entry wasn't joking.

*A.V.'s man Torvaldsen joined us at the last supply cache. Says he's along as "logistics." He counts everything we carry. He counts us too. I've seen him doing it.*

Torvaldsen. The name snagged somewhere in her memory — one of Vey's dock supervisors, still on his payroll as far as she knew, a broad silent man who watched the harbor from the warehouse balcony with binoculars he never seemed to need. She'd seen him twice since arriving in Skarnvik. Both times, he'd been watching her.

She turned pages. The expedition had gone up the river gorge in June, hauling a collapsible boat in sections, following the water into the glacier's throat. The handwriting stayed steady through the technical passages — soundings, ice conditions, rope anchors — and Gideon found herself reading those with professional hunger despite everything. The route they'd described was real. It was *good*. A subglacial river running open beneath the tongue for nearly nine kilometers, roofed by blue ice thick enough to walk on in places, thin enough to hear the water talking everywhere else. In her years of guiding she'd heard rumors of such tunnels, dismissed them the way you dismiss all rumors in mountain towns: as stories towns tell to keep outsiders out, or keep themselves in.

This wasn't a story. This was a survey. Soundings in fathoms, air-gap measurements, notes on which sections calved seasonally and which held year-round. Someone had done this work carefully, competently, at enormous expense — and then someone had decided none of it would ever see daylight.

* * *

The middle of the journal was where the confidence died.

*Day 19. Found the seam.* That was all the entry said about what they'd found, and Gideon understood why only later, when she understood what the writer had already understood: that the journal itself might be read by the wrong eyes. Everything after that day was written in the cipher, even the mundane entries, even the weather. A man doesn't start encrypting his weather reports unless he believes his own camp has ears.

She worked through the cipher entries slowly, pencil scratching, the stove ticking down to coals. Day 20: *The seam runs the length of the lower gallery. E. says he's never seen concentrations like it outside a refinery. Says it isn't a vein, it's a deposit — the whole wall of it, exposed where the river cut through. Worth more than the town. Worth more than the coast.* Day 21: *A.V.'s man sent a signal flare east at dusk. We are nine days from anywhere. There is nothing east but the icefield. I asked him who he signaled. He smiled and said, "The people who paid for this."*

Day 22 was the last cipher entry, and it was short.

*Boat gone this morning. Radio gone. Torvaldsen and two others departed up-glacier before dawn with most of the food. A.V.'s instructions, they say. We are to wait at the tunnel mouth for "resupply." I have counted the food. I have counted the days. I am writing this in case the sea finds it instead of God.*

Below that, back in plain ink, in a hand gone loose and shaking:

*If anyone reads this: Aldous Vey of Skarnvik financed this expedition in secret. When we found the deposit he cut us loose to die above the ice so no claim would ever carry our names. Seven of us. Six now. Halvard fell yesterday — or stepped. In this cold it is hard to tell the difference and I am ashamed that I thought it.*

*The river will take this out when the tunnel floods in spring. The sea keeps what the mountain discards. Let the sea testify.*

Gideon set the pencil down.

The lamp hissed. Outside, the tide was turning — she could feel it in the boathouse pilings, a long slow shudder through the wood, the fjord exhaling and drawing breath again. Somewhere down the shore a dog started barking and stopped.

Seven men. Six. Then the entries stopped entirely, and the last leaf was blank except for a single line pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath:

*Tell my daughter I did not turn back.*

Mara's grandfather. The man the town had turned into a cautionary tale — *old Eirik Solheim, greedy fool, led seven men into the ice and none came home* — had been murdered by inches on a glacier, abandoned with a dead radio and a countdown of rations, and the town had spent eleven years using his corpse as a fence to keep people away from the very thing that killed him.

And the man who'd done it owned half the shoreline Gideon could see through the frosted window. Owned the boats in the harbor. Owned, if the debt Mara kept not mentioning was what Gideon suspected, the boathouse she was sitting in.

* * *

She got up and paced. It was an old habit from base camps, from storm days, from the long white hours when the mind starts eating itself — move your body so it doesn't vote with your fear. Four steps to the door, four steps back. The floorboards knew her weight by the third pass.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out cold.

Option one: sell the journal. Not to Vey — she'd refused him once and meant it — but to a newspaper, a survey office, anyone south with money and no ties to this coast. Safe-ish. Profitable. And worth exactly nothing, because a journal without corroboration was a story, and stories about rich men killing poor ones were the oldest cargo on any coast. Vey would have it discredited inside a month. He'd had eleven years to build the legend; he'd built it well. Old Eirik the fool. The forbidden cave. The tide that takes the curious.

Option two: give it to the rangers. Honest, lawful, and slow as glacial drift. The regional office was three hundred kilometers south. By the time a survey team reached Skarnvik, the journal would be ash, the cave would be underwater, and Gideon Blackwood — disgraced, blacklisted, conveniently foreign to this fjord — would be a missing person whose disappearance nobody investigated, because everybody knew what happened to fools who entered the cave.

Option three.

She stopped pacing and looked at the journal again, at the route map sketched across the final pages in that dying hand. Nine kilometers of subglacial river. Air gaps marked. Anchors marked. The seasonal flood window marked — *tunnel runs clear roughly six weeks after spring thaw, closes with the first heavy calving* — and spring thaw had come early this year. Everyone in Skarnvik said so. The low tide that had opened the cave was part of it; the whole coast was running weeks ahead of itself.

Six weeks, minus whatever had already burned since thaw. Maybe four. Maybe less.

Go up the river. Reach the source. See the seam with her own eyes, photograph it, bring back proof that no one could bury — because you can discredit a journal, but you cannot discredit a wall of ore standing in a cathedral of ice with a living witness in front of it.

It was a suicidal plan. She catalogued the ways with a guide's efficiency: the tunnel could flood without warning; the ice could close behind her; Vey's men had a nine-day head start on knowing the terrain and infinite resources besides; and she'd be doing it with borrowed gear and a salvager's daughter whose loyalty was mortgaged to the very man she'd be moving against.

Then she thought about the client she hadn't saved.

That was the thing she couldn't outrun, the thing that had chased her from range to range until she'd run out of ranges and washed up here. Two years ago, on the Kesselhorn traverse, the rope had failed — not her knot, not her anchor, the rope, a manufacturing flaw, the inquiry had cleared her, the *inquiry had cleared her*, and it hadn't mattered, because the family didn't want a verdict, they wanted someone to have held on harder, and so did she. She'd been a guide because guiding was the one craft where competence wasn't abstract. You tied the knots. You read the snow. You got people home. And when the system failed anyway, the world needed a name to blame, and hers was the one printed on the permit.

Since then she'd taken jobs that used none of what she was. Ferrying tourists. Inventory work. Salvage. Momentum without direction, because direction hurt.

But this — this was a client who'd left her instructions. *Let the sea testify. Tell my daughter I did not turn back.* Seven men abandoned above the ice by a man who signed the checks and never once got his hands cold. And a route, drawn by a dying surveyor, that ran straight through the kind of country Gideon had been born for and exiled from.

Nobody was going to carry this testimony out. The sea had kept its end. The rest was on whoever read the page.

She looked at her hands. Steady. That surprised her — she'd expected them to shake, expected the old fear, the Kesselhorn ghost whispering *you get people killed*. Instead there was just the flat clean calm she remembered from good decisions, the kind that arrive already made.

"I'm not selling you," she told the journal quietly. "And he's not getting you back."

* * *

She was rewrapping the satchel in oilcloth when she heard the footstep on the dock outside — one plank, deliberately weighted, the way you step when you want to be heard coming. Her hand went to the knife at her belt and stayed there.

The door opened on Mara, backlit by the gray pre-dawn, still in her salvage slickers, hair wet with fog. She looked at the journal on the bench, at the cipher worksheet covered in Gideon's pencil, at Gideon's face — and Gideon watched her do the math, watch her understand exactly how much had been read and what it meant, and watch her decide, visibly, painfully, whether to say it.

"You're leaving," Mara said. Not a question. "Up the river."

"The tunnel closes in four weeks. Maybe three." Gideon slung the satchel over her shoulder. "Your grandfather drew the map. I'm going to follow it."

Mara stood in the doorway with the fog moving behind her, and for a long moment neither of them breathed. Then, from up the hill, toward the Vey warehouse, a light came on — and another, and another, three windows waking in a row, like eyes opening.

"They know," Mara said softly. "About the journal. My father just got the summons."
