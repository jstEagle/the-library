## 6. Debt of Blood

Mara knew something was wrong before she reached the workshop, because the gulls were wrong.

They always mobbed the drying racks at this hour, screaming over the fish heads her father's crew tossed out with the morning's offal. Today the racks stood empty of birds. The whole harbor side of Skarnvik held a silence like held breath, and the tide was coming in over the flats in long gray sheets, erasing everything the low water had shown.

She had spent the night at Gideon Blackwood's rented room — not by design, exactly. She'd gone to confront the woman about the cave, about the journal, about what her grandfather had died looking for, and instead they had talked until the lamp burned dry and neither of them had said the thing that mattered most: *I am afraid.* Mara had walked home through the blue hour before dawn with salt wind in her teeth, rehearsing arguments for her father, and had arrived to find the workshop doors standing open to the weather.

Her father never left them open. Twenty-two years hauling salvage off the fjord bottom, and Halvard Voss still closed his doors against the damp like a man closing a wound.

"Mama?" she called, though her mother had been eleven years dead and the word came out anyway, the way old words do when the body is frightened ahead of the mind.

No answer. The forge was cold. That was the second wrong thing — her father banked the coals every evening so the morning would find them ready, an inheritance from his own father, a small stubborn faith in continuity. Cold ash sat in the firebox like a held breath released.

The workshop smelled of oil and hemp and the particular iron tang of old chain. Everything else looked ordinary. The salvage ledgers lay squared on their shelf. The diving helmets hung on their pegs, brass faces turned to the wall the way her father insisted, "so they don't watch you." The long table by the window held the day's intended work laid out in careful order: a bronze fitting to be descaled, new gaskets cut and waiting.

Ordinary. Except for the drawer.

The third drawer down on the tool chest stood open two inches. Her father's drawers did not stand open. He had a place for every file and punch and drift, and he closed each drawer with his palm flat, a little push, the way he ended sentences.

Mara crossed the floor and pulled it wide.

Inside, the tools were all there. But beneath them, where her father kept the papers that mattered — the boat's papers, the debt papers — the space was empty except for one object lying centered on the bare wood like a thing placed rather than left.

A white stone, sea-smoothed, the size of a gull's egg.

She knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a crowd. Every child in Skarnvik knew it. When Aldous Vey wanted a debt remembered, he didn't send letters. He sent a boy with a white stone, and the stone went on your mantel or your workbench where you saw it every day, and everyone who visited saw it too, and no one ever asked about it aloud because asking was itself a kind of default. Her father had carried one for six years after the *Kestrel* went down — his boat, his loan, Vey's money — until the winter he'd finally paid enough to send it back. Six years of that smooth white weight sitting in the lamplight, watching him eat dinner.

There hadn't been a stone on this bench since she was fourteen.

Mara stood very still. Outside, the tide crept up the slipway, filling the tracks of a cart — heavy-tired, recently gone, the ruts sharp-edged, not yet softened by rain. Someone had come in the night, taken her father somewhere, and left a stone in his drawer to tell her why.

* * *

She found Torv first, because Torv was the only one of Vey's men who ever met her eyes.

He was on the quay by the *Sea-Wife*, Vey's black-hulled freighter, checking lashings on a cargo that didn't need checking. Up close he had the flat calm of a man who has decided how things will go and is waiting for the world to agree. But when he saw her face, something moved behind the calm — a flicker, quickly drowned.

"Morning, Miss Voss."

"Where is my father?"

"Fishing, I'd expect."

"The *Kestrel* is at her mooring. His gear is in the rack." She kept her voice level the way her father had taught her to keep a knife — blade down, edge away from your own hand. "His forge is cold. There's a cart track going up toward the Vey house. And there's a white stone in his drawer."

Torv looked past her at the water for a moment. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into something almost gentle, which from him was more frightening than a threat. "You should talk to Mr. Vey."

"I'm talking to you."

"And I'm telling you what will help you." He turned back to the lashings. "Talk to Mr. Vey. Take the stone back to him yourself, if you've got the nerve. Men have bought back worse than debts that way."

"With what? What does he want?"

Torv's hands stilled on the rope. For one heartbeat he looked at her — really looked, the way a man looks at a door he knows he shouldn't open — and then the flatness came down again like a hatch. "Ask him," he said. "Not me."

* * *

The Vey house sat above the town on its shelf of granite, all dark timber and tall windows, built by a man who wanted to look down on the harbor without ever smelling it. Mara had been inside once, as a child, when her mother was alive — some charity function, wool blankets for widows, Vey's broad smiling face above it all. She remembered the smell of beeswax and the way her mother's hand had tightened on hers in the hallway, not from awe. From warning.

Now she walked up the drive in her work clothes with salt drying white on her sleeves, and let the housekeeper take her name, and waited in a hall full of ship models under glass like specimens in a museum — which, she thought, was exactly what they were.

Vey received her in a study lined with charts. Not harbor charts. Deep-water charts, fjord charts, the whole coast of the ice, annotated in a small precise hand. He rose from behind a desk of black walnut and spread his hands as if she were a gift the morning had brought him.

"Mara Voss. You've grown into your mother's bones, God rest her. Sit, sit. Can I offer you anything? Coffee? Something stronger? It's early, but grief keeps its own hours."

The word landed like a hook set behind the eye. *Grief.* She didn't sit.

"Where is my father?"

"Gone fishing, I understood." Vey smiled. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-bearded, with the kind of face built for portraits — but the eyes were small and patient, a fisherman's eyes, reading water. "Though between us, I think we both know better. Sit down, child. This will go easier sitting."

She remained standing. He shrugged, unbothered, and settled back himself, lacing his fingers over his stomach.

"Your father owes me a considerable sum. More, I regret to say, than the *Kestrel*'s insurance ever covered, and the business has been... thin, these last years. I've carried it. Carried it gladly — Halvard Voss is the best salvage man on this coast, and loyalty should count for something." He sighed, a performance of sorrow so well-made it might have been real even to him. "But lately he's fallen in with poor company. A stranger. A woman with a ruined name, digging in a cave the whole town knows better than to name. And it seems she's found something in there. Papers. A journal."

"You want the journal."

"I want what's mine." The smile stayed; the warmth left it entirely, the way sun leaves water when a cloud crosses. "That expedition was financed with my capital. Whatever it recorded belongs to me by every law of property and decency. Your father agreed last night to recover it. He's a reasonable man. He understands obligations — he's lived under one for twenty years." Vey tilted his head. "So you understand my confusion this morning, when he changed his mind. Quietly. Completely. Said he'd sooner see the paper burn. Do you know what got into him?"

Mara thought of her grandfather — Eirik Voss, lost on the ice thirty years ago, mourned by a town that told a story about storms because the truth was too expensive. She thought of her father's hands, which could feel a flaw in bronze through a glove, and what those hands must have done last night when Vey showed him whatever it was that made him refuse. She thought of the cold forge, the open drawer, the white stone.

"No," she said. "But I know my father. If he refused you, he had a reason that would hold underwater."

"A reason." Vey nodded slowly, as if tasting the word. "Everyone has reasons. I deal in results." He leaned forward, and for a moment the portrait-face fell away and she saw the machinery underneath, cold and oiled and very old. "Here is what happens now, girl. The debt comes due at month's end. All of it. The boat, the workshop, the house your father was born in — sold at auction to cover it, and I'll be the only bidder, as usual. Unless the journal appears on my desk within the week. Then we'll discuss terms. Generous ones. I'm told the Blackwood woman trusts you. Use that."

"And if I don't?"

Vey spread his hands again, that same benediction, that same trap. "Then Skarnvik will go on being Skarnvik. Tides come, tides go. Boats are lost. Old men take risks on the water they shouldn't." He smiled at whatever crossed her face. "The sea is dangerous, Mara. Everyone here knows that. It's why they believe me when I say it."

* * *

She walked down the hill with the sky pressing close and the harbor glittering below like broken glass, and she made herself think, because thinking was the only weapon her father had ever given her that couldn't be taken away.

Vey wouldn't kill Halvard Voss outright. Not yet. Dead men don't retrieve journals, and dead men's daughters stop negotiating. Her father was leverage — kept breathing, kept somewhere, squeezed gently until the journal appeared. That was the arithmetic. She clung to it like a handhold on loose rock.

But then she reached the workshop and saw the door standing open again — wider than she'd left it — and the arithmetic broke.

Someone had been through the place while she was on the hill. Drawers pulled and dumped, the tool chest gutted, mattresses slashed in the loft, the ledgers thrown across the floor with pages torn out. They hadn't found what they wanted, because what they wanted wasn't there. But they had found her father's strongbox under the floorboards by the forge — pried open, empty — and beside it, dropped in the rush, a coil of tarred twine of the kind used for lashing cargo on the *Sea-Wife*.

And pinned to the wall above the workbench, driven through the wood with a marlinspike, was a page torn from one of her father's own ledgers. On it, in a hand she didn't know — small, hard, unhurried:

*The debt stands. Bring the journal or the account closes.*

Below it, pressed into the wood grain where the spike had punched through, a single line of older ink had bled up from the board itself — her father's writing, from some ledger entry years dead, visible only because the spike had split the plank along it:

*Eirik Voss — final payment received in full.*

Mara stared at it until the words stopped being words. Her grandfather's account. Closed. In full. Paid by whom, and with what currency, thirty years before the storm story was ever told?

The workshop swam. She put her hand flat on the cold forge, the way her father did, grounding herself in iron.

Then she heard the gulls return — a slow wheeling cry over the harbor, settling back onto the racks, resuming the ordinary world — and underneath their noise, from the direction of the water, a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: the creak of the *Kestrel*'s mooring line, and footsteps on her father's dock.

She was moving before she decided to move, out the door and down the slipway, and there at the end of the pier stood a figure in oilskins with his back to her, and for one whole second her heart leapt so hard it hurt —

— and then the figure turned, and it was Torv, holding her father's boathook, and behind him the *Kestrel* rode empty at her lines, and Torv's face, for the first time since she'd known it, had nothing flat about it at all.

"He wouldn't come quietly," Torv said. "I want you to know that. Whatever they tell you — he wouldn't come quietly, and he didn't give them anything." He held out his other hand, and in it lay a folded scrap of oilcloth, tied with tarred twine. "He made me swear I'd get this to you if it went bad. I'm a month late and ashamed of it. Read it, and then decide fast, girl — because Vey's men are already asking after the Blackwood woman, and they're not asking politely anymore."

Mara took the oilcloth with hands that had gone strangely steady, the way they did at the worst moments, her body stepping outside her grief to do what needed doing. Inside, in her father's blunt carpenter's hand, half a page:

*Mara — if you're reading this, Vey has collected me, and I won't insult you by pretending I'll talk my way out. Listen. The journal isn't just a logbook. Eirik's expedition found something at the source worth killing for, and Vey paid for it all — I've known since the night he showed me the original charter with Eirik's signature forged beside his own. I refused him because your grandfather's name is the only thing our family owns outright, and I won't sell it twice. Don't trade the journal for me. Trade it for the truth. Take it to the rangers at the head of the fjord, or better, take it up the river itself and see what he buried. And find the Blackwood woman — she's the only one in this town stubborn enough to finish what Eirik started. Go upstream, Mara. Before the calving seals it all for another year.*

*Your father, who was proud of you every day and said it too seldom.*

Mara read it twice. The second time, her tears fell on the ink and she watched them bead on the waterproof oilcloth without staining it, and something in her chest that had been breaking all morning finished breaking and began, quietly, to harden into something with an edge.

Grief was a current. Her father had taught her that much about water — you don't fight it, you don't drown in it, you *use* it. You let it carry you where fighting never could.

She folded the letter into her jacket, over her heart, and looked up at Torv, who was watching her with the expression of a man measuring a swell he'd decided to sail into anyway.

"They'll be watching the road out," he said. "And the harbor. But nobody watches the gorge at night — it's suicide in daylight and invisible in the dark, which makes it the one route in Skarnvik nobody guards." He hesitated, jaw working. "I've done things for Vey I'll answer for someday. Let me square a little of it. There's a skiff cached below the old salmon ladder. Take the Blackwood woman and go tonight."

"And you?"

"I'll be where I always am." His mouth twisted. "Close enough to be blamed, far enough to be useful. Now go, before I come to my senses."

Mara ran. Through the lanes, past the racks, up the hill to the rented room where a disgraced guide from the south sat sharpening a knife by the window with the patience of someone who had already guessed the morning would come to this.

Mara hit the doorway hard enough to make the frame jump.

"They took my father," she said. "And I know how we get to the river."
