## 7. Upstream

We left by the tide's clock, not the town's.

Mara had explained it once, in the flat voice she used for facts she didn't want to make important: Skarnvik slept in two shifts, the fishing families with the dark and the harbor crews with the tide, and between them there was a dead hour when no one walked the seawall but gulls and drunks. That was our hour. The moon had gone down behind the western ridge an hour before, and the fjord lay black as poured tar, breathing against the pilings.

I came down the back stairs of the salvage shed with my pack already buckled and stopped at the bottom because Mara was standing in the doorway of her father's workshop, just standing there, one hand flat against the doorframe like she was holding the building up.

"You don't have to come," I said. It was the third time I'd said it. I said it again anyway because it was true and because saying it let me pretend I'd given her a real choice.

"I know." She stepped out and pulled the door shut behind her. She didn't lock it. There was nothing left inside worth locking — we both knew that too. "Don't say it again."

I didn't. We went along the seawall in that dead-hour silence, boots soft on wet planking, past the fish racks hung with split cod that turned on their lines like hanged men in the wind off the water. Vey's warehouse rose ahead of us, three stories of black timber with his name painted across the gable in letters you could read from a mile out to sea, and I felt it watching us the way I imagined a man feels a rifle scope — not seeing it, just knowing where it pointed.

The gorge trail started behind the smokehouse, where the river left the mountains and fell into the fjord in a series of drops the locals called the Stair. Nobody used the trail above the second drop; there was no reason to. The salmon couldn't climb it, the timber crews had stripped the lower valley forty years ago, and the upper gorge belonged to the river and whatever the river hadn't bothered to take.

Which meant nobody would think to look for us there for exactly as long as it took Vey to stop thinking like a shipping man and start thinking like a hunter.

"Day," Mara said. Maybe an hour into the climb, maybe two. She said it without turning around, her voice pitched under the sound of the water. "Maybe less. Torv drinks at the Anchor until midnight, then he reports. That gives them a start at dawn."

"A day is enough if the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold."

"No," I agreed. "But it'll hold longer than they'll hold their nerve on scree in the dark."

She glanced back at me then, a pale shape in the dark with her hair tied back and her father's old climbing jacket swallowing her shoulders, and I caught something in her face that might have been the beginning of a smile or the end of one. Hard to tell, in the dark. Hard to tell with Mara most days.

* * *

We climbed through the night because stopping meant cold and cold meant slow, and slow was the only thing that could actually kill us before Vey's men did. The trail was barely a trail — a goat path stitched to the gorge wall, sometimes widening to a shelf wide enough for two, sometimes narrowing to ledges where the river thundered six feet below and spray iced the rock in sheets. I went first. Not because I was better — though I was, at this, and pretending otherwise would have gotten us both killed — but because going first meant I found the bad ground before it found her, and calling back warnings kept me honest about how tired I was.

"Loose flake, left side."

"Got it."

"Rope length here. Hand line's still good — someone drove pins, years ago."

"Whose pins?"

"Doesn't matter. They're holding."

They were holding. Old iron pins furred with rust, driven into the limestone by hands long gone, and I tested each one with my full weight before I trusted it, and each one held, and somewhere around the fourth pitch I realized I had stopped thinking about the journal, about Vey, about the blacklist and the inquiry and the name that followed me through every port in the north like a debt collector. I was thinking about iron and ice and the angle of a ledge, and the simplicity of it was so clean it felt almost like grief.

That was the thing nobody told you about running. You didn't run from your life. You ran toward the part of it that still worked.

Above the fifth drop the gorge opened and the river spread out over a gravel fan, braided and shallow, loud in a different way — not the roar of falling water but the endless mutter of moving water over stone. We stopped there because we had to. My legs were shaking in a way I recognized as the body cashing checks the mind had written, and Mara sat down on a boulder without being asked and started working a heat tab into her stove with fingers too numb to argue with.

"We make the tree line by midmorning," I said. "Camp in the forest, sleep through the light hours. They won't move at night either."

"They will if Vey pays them enough."

"Vey pays men to be careful. Careful men camp."

She looked up at me across the little blue flame. "You know a lot about how he thinks."

"I know a lot about how money thinks. It's the same everywhere." I crouched opposite her and fed sticks to the flame, small ones, keeping it low. "Money buys patience right up until it buys speed. He's spent a week being patient. Now he's spent. From here on every day costs him more than the last, and men who feel themselves getting expensive start making mistakes."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"What do mistakes cost you?" She said it evenly, not an accusation, just a question, the way she asked everything — like she was testing the load-bearing walls of whatever I told her.

I watched the fire instead of her. "More than I can afford," I said. "So I try not to make any."

"That wasn't what I asked."

"I know."

She let it go. That was another thing about Mara — she knew which doors to leave closed. We ate hardtack and smoked fish from her father's stores, and drank snowmelt, and when the sky in the east went from black to the color of a bruise healing, we climbed another hour to the forest edge and made camp under a spruce thick enough to hide our little fire's glow, and slept in shifts, and I dreamed about water, which was either a mercy or a warning.

* * *

The second day the rain came, as promised, and it changed everything and nothing. It hid our tracks within minutes of making them. It also turned the gorge trail into a waterfall's gutter, filled every handhold with running water, and dropped the temperature until the wet rock grew a skin of verglas that rang faintly under our boots like thin china. By afternoon I was leading on rope, placing protection into cracks that spat water, and Mara — who had grown up on this coast and learned knots before letters, by her own account — handled the belays with a competence that made me revise, quietly and permanently, everything I'd assumed about salvagers' daughters.

"Where did you learn to belay?" I called down, hanging on a traverse with the river chewing at my heels.

"My father ran salvage on wrecks below the high-tide line. Somebody has to hold the rope." A pause, the rope sliding taut through her brake hand. "He always wanted it to be family. Said hired hands let go when it gets bad."

"Do they?"

"Hired hands are just people, Gideon. People let go when it gets bad. Family sometimes does too." Another pause. "But they come back for the rope."

I thought about that for the rest of the traverse, which took twenty minutes and cost me skin on both palms. When I reached the stance and brought her up, she arrived soaked and gray-lipped and utterly steady, and I understood that she had been telling me something in the only language she trusted — not comfort, not confession, just specification. This is what I am. This is what I do. Test it or don't.

I tested it. All that day and into the next, in the grammar of the mountain: I gave her the sharp end on easy pitches and watched her read the rock; I called holds wrong on purpose, twice, and she caught both; I let her navigate a fog-bound bench section by the sound of the river alone, because she knew this water, knew its moods the way I knew a rope's. And she tested me back. She asked about the accident on the third evening, sitting across a fire in a hollow beneath an overhang while the rain hammered the world outside, and she asked it plainly, no cushioning: "What happened? The real version. Not the inquiry's."

The real version. I had given the real version twice in my life — once to the inquiry board, who wrote it down and used it against me, and once to no one, in the dark, over and over, the way you press a bruise to check if it still hurts.

"His name was Anders Vik," I said. "Twenty-six. Pharmacist from Bergen, first big mountain, paid for a guided ascent of the Sørtind couloir. Weather window closed early. I called the turn at the col — we should have gone down the east flank, long way, safe way. He wanted the summit. His brother had stood on it. So I —" I turned my hands over, looked at them. Rain drummed the overhang. "I let him talk me into a direct descent. Shorter. Faster. I told myself I could manage the risk. I'd managed worse. That's the whole disease of it, Mara. Every guide who ever killed a client told themselves the same sentence."

"And then?"

"And then the slope avalanched, and he went into a crevasse field on a rope I'd set, and the anchor held, and I got to the lip, and I could see him. Forty feet down. Alive. Talking to me." My voice did something strange and far away. "And my second rope was stashed half a kilometer back because I'd cut weight for the summit push. Because I was managing risk. I climbed down to him on a single line and I got my hands on him and the line wouldn't take both of us, and I had to choose, and I chose, and I've been choosing it every day since."

The fire cracked. Outside, the river said what rivers always say.

"He let go," Mara said quietly. "Didn't he. So you'd live."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to add. She nodded slowly, the way people nod at a grave, and she didn't offer absolution, and I loved her a little for that — for understanding that what I needed wasn't forgiveness but witness. Someone who knew the whole weight and didn't try to shift it.

"That's why the blacklist doesn't scare you," she said after a while. "You already sentenced yourself. Their verdict is just paperwork."

"Something like that."

"And Vey offering to buy you — buy the journal, buy your silence — that's not really about the journal."

"No."

"It's about finding a woman who knows what guilt costs and assuming everything has a price."

I looked at her across the fire. Rain, rock, river, and this sharp-eyed woman wrapped in her dead father's jacket, reading me like a route card. "When did you work that out?"

"The cave," she said. "First day. You looked at that satchel like it was a coffin. Nobody looks at evidence like that unless they've been evidence."

* * *

On the fourth morning the gorge narrowed for the last time and spat us out into the high valley, and we saw what we were climbing toward.

The glacier filled the head of the valley like something poured and frozen mid-pour — ten miles of it visible from where we stood, blue-white in the broken sunlight, descending from the shoulder of the massif in a frozen cataract. The river ran out from under its snout, gray with rock flour, cold enough to hurt, and above it the ice curved away into country no map in Skarnvik had ever bothered to draw correctly.

Mara stood beside me and didn't speak for a long time. When she did, her voice had gone strange.

"My grandfather's last letter said the source sang," she said. "My grandmother kept it. One page. He wrote that the river didn't start at the ice — it started inside it, and if you listened at the right place you could hear it coming up from underneath, singing under the glacier like a wire under tension." She shook her head. "I always thought it was the cold talking. Men alone up there, months in the white. But standing here—"

She stopped. And because the wind had dropped and the valley had gone still in that particular way high valleys go still, I heard it.

Faint. Below hearing, almost — less a sound than a vibration that arrived through the soles of my boots and settled somewhere behind the sternum. The river's voice, doubled, trebled, coming not just from the snout ahead but from everywhere at once, from under our feet, from the whole buried plumbing of the ice. Water moving through the glacier's dark interior in channels no one had ever mapped, all of it converging somewhere upstream, somewhere inside, at a place the drowned expedition had named in a journal I carried against my spine like a second vertebra.

"Singing," I said.

"Singing," Mara agreed.

We stood there a moment longer, two fugitives with wet boots and four days of hardtack between us, listening to a mountain keep a secret. Then I turned and looked back down the valley — the way we'd come, the gorge a dark seam in the rock, and beyond it, invisible now but perfectly legible to my imagination, the smoke of Skarnvik and the black gable with VEY painted across it.

There were figures on the gravel flats below the gorge mouth. Three of them, maybe four, tiny at this distance, moving with the unhurried purpose of men being paid by the day. Through the binoculars I confirmed it: Torv's broad shoulders, two others I didn't know, ropes and rifles. A day behind, as promised. No more.

"They're not hurrying," Mara said.

"They don't need to hurry yet. They know where we're going. There's only one way up." I slung the glasses and looked at the glacier again, at its white miles and its hidden singing heart, and felt the trap of it close gently around us even as the awe of it pulled us forward. One route in. One route out. And somewhere ahead, a man rich enough to charter helicopters waiting at the end of it.

"Then we stop being predictable," I said, and pointed to where the valley wall met the ice — a lateral moraine, a rubble highway running straight up the glacier's flank, ugly, exposed, and fast. "We take the high road. We beat them to the snout, and we're inside the ice before they can see us go."

Mara looked at the moraine, then at me, and adjusted her pack straps without another word.

Behind us, far below, the river carried on singing — and under its song, if you knew how to listen, something else was starting to carry up the valley on the wind. Faint. Rhythmic. Like rotors.
