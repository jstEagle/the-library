## 8. The First Rapid

The gorge told us what was coming before we saw it.

I'd been reading water since I was fourteen years old — before the certifications, before the clients, before the name on my license became a thing people said in the tone you'd use for a spoiled batch of milk. You learn to listen with your whole body. The sound changes first: the river's murmur sharpens into something with edges. Then the walls of the gorge begin to lean in, the rock going from gray to black where spray has painted it for ten thousand years. The air itself gets colder and tastes of pulverized stone.

By midmorning of our second day upstream, the Kaldvík River had stopped being a river and started being an argument.

"Is that—" Mara stood at the bow of our raft, one hand knotted in the cargo netting, her braid whipping in the wind that funneled down the gorge like breath through a flute. "That's not on any map."

"No," I said. "It wouldn't be."

The river dropped away ahead of us through a slot no wider than a road, and the water in it wasn't flowing so much as falling sideways — a chaos of white stacked on white, churning between walls close enough to touch from either gunwale if we'd been fool enough to stretch out our arms. The roar came up through the raft's timbers and into my boots and settled somewhere behind my sternum, familiar as a heartbeat.

Mara looked at me. She'd been looking at me differently since we'd slipped out of Skarnvik two nights ago — not with suspicion exactly, but with the particular attention of someone re-reading a document they'd skimmed the first time. "You've run things like this before."

It wasn't a question, but it deserved more than a nod, and she deserved more than the version of me I'd been selling since the fjord. I crouched at the stern and checked the lashings on our gear — the journal wrapped in oilcloth and double-bagged, the rope coil, the pitiful remains of our food, the patch kit I'd already used twice.

"I ran rivers for eleven years," I said. "Commercial trips first, then private expeditions. Class IV, class V. The Karakoram headwaters. The Stikine in flood." I kept my eyes on the knots. "There's a reason I know how to do this."

"There's a reason you don't anymore."

The wind took my answer before I found one, which was probably just as well. I tested the oar locks instead, working grease into the bearings with my thumb. The raft was a salvage-yard animal — four inner tubes and fuel drums lashed under a platform of split planking, patched in three places with Mara's father's rubber cement and a strip of conveyor belt. It had carried us forty kilometers of flatwater without complaint. What it was about to carry us through would be a different negotiation entirely.

"We portage around?" Mara asked.

"Can't. Look at the banks." She looked. The walls went straight down to the waterline on both sides, sheer granite furred with ice, no shelf, no trail, nothing a goat could hold. "We run it or we climb out and lose three days on the rim, and Vey's men are only one day behind us. Maybe less."

"And if we run it wrong?"

I met her eyes then. Gray-green, steady, her father's stubborn jaw set against the cold. She was twenty-four years old and she had hired a disgraced stranger off a dock because he was the only salvage diver desperate enough to take the contract, and now here she was, orphaned by circumstance, standing on a garbage raft above a rapid that had never been named because no one had ever survived to name it.

"If we run it wrong," I said, "the river will decide what happens next. Not us."

* * *

Here is what I told almost no one, and what the river made me tell Mara:

Three years ago, in the spring, I guided a party of six down the upper Søråi. Private contract, well-paying, the kind of trip that keeps a guide solvent through the lean seasons. Five experienced paddlers and one man named Elias Brandt, who was not experienced but whose brother-in-law owned the outfitter and whose money cleared anyway. Forty-eight years old. A laugh too big for the raft. He called everyone *champ*.

On the second day we came to a rapid the locals called the Maw — a class V with a recirculating hole at the bottom that had eaten better boats than ours. I scouted it from the bank, as I always did, and I read it clean: enter left of the tongue, ferry hard across the seam, thread the gap between the two rocks, punch through below the hole. I'd run it eleven times. I briefed them on the bank for twenty minutes. Elias Brandt nodded along, grinning, calling me *champ*, and when we pushed off I watched him take his line and it was perfect — textbook, beautiful, the best run of his life right up until the eddy fence at the bottom caught his bow and spun him sideways into the hole.

I have replayed the next ninety seconds so many times that they've worn smooth, like a stone handled for years. I was off the water, on the rock, rope coiled. I threw. My first throw missed short — I'll say that plainly, I'll say it forever, my first throw missed short by two meters, and by the time I'd hauled in and thrown again he had gone under the boil and come up once, face bloodied, mouth open on a word I have chosen to believe was not my name, and gone under again. The hole held him. That's what those holes do; they're washing machines made of the whole river's weight. We got him out eleven minutes later with hooks and a human chain and every trick I knew, and he was dead, and the coroner wrote *drowning, accidental*, and the outfitter wrote *guide error*, and the industry wrote *blacklisted*, all three documents saying the same true thing in different fonts: Gideon Blackwood was the person on the water when someone died, and the person on the water owns the water.

What I learned afterward — what I have never been able to decide whether it makes it better or worse — is that my second throw, the one that reached him, had landed across his chest. He'd had the rope in his hand. There were rope burns on his palm. He had held on, and the tension had torn him out of my grip when the hole rolled him, because I was braced wrong, because I was fifty kilos of grief anchored to wet rock instead of a hundred and eighty pounds of anchor buried behind a boulder the way they teach you, the way I *had* been taught, the way I taught other people.

You can survive failing. What I couldn't survive was knowing the failure had a technique to it, a fixable mechanical cause, a thing I could point to in the sand and say *there, that's where I lost him*. It meant the universe hadn't taken him. I had.

So I stopped guiding. And momentum being the only honest thing left in my inventory, I kept moving — salvage work, dock work, dive contracts in cold water where nobody asked about your history because your hands were too busy to talk. Running from a thing looks a lot like running toward other things, if you keep your pace up.

* * *

"We scout from the rim," I said. "There's a ledge on the left, sixty meters up. We read the whole drop, mark our line, and then we commit. No halfway."

"You can see the line from up there?"

"You can see everything from up there. That's the problem." I shouldered the coil. "Come on."

The scramble up the left wall took forty minutes and cost skin I'd miss later. The ledge was a seam of harder rock jutting out over the gorge, and from it the rapid unrolled beneath us like something anatomical — throat, gullet, teeth. I lay on my stomach at the edge with the wind trying to peel me off the mountain, and I did what I used to do, what my body remembered doing even while the rest of me had spent three years pretending otherwise: I read the water.

The entry was guarded by a fanged rock on the right that would wrap a boat like wire. Past it, the main tongue poured down a smooth green ramp into a standing wave train — three waves, big but friendly, the kind you punch through with speed. Then the gorge dog-legged left around a buttress, and there — there it was, the reason this drop killed people. The current slammed the outside wall and rebounded into a lateral wave that fed directly into a recirculating hole backed up against the lower buttress. A keeper. From up here I could see the foam pile turning over and over on itself, patient as a spider.

But past it, on the far side of the seam, there was a lane. Narrow — a raft-width and a half — of broken, ugly, survivable water threading between the hole and the wall. It required entering high, carrying speed through the wave train, and then making one ferocious ferry stroke at the exact moment the gorge turned, driving left across the rebound current before it could shove you into the keeper. One stroke, timed to the river's own rhythm. Early and you'd wash out wide into the wall. Late—

Late, you'd find out what Elias Brandt found out.

"There," I said, and put my finger on it, and made Mara look until she saw it too. Her breath was coming fast and white. She was scared. Good. Fear is just respect with its coat off.

"That lane closes in high water," she said. "If the level comes up—"

"It won't come up today. Sky's been clear for two days, no melt surge." I sat back on my heels. "We run it once. We run it right. And Mara — whatever happens in the bottom half, you stay low, you hold the netting, and you do not stand up. People die in rapids because their legs turn into anchors. Promise me."

She promised. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised me, and then didn't. This was a woman who had grown up hauling drowned things out of a fjord, who had found her father's workshop ransacked and her father gone and had packed a bag and walked into the night with a stranger rather than wait for Vey's men to come finish the arithmetic. Fear lived in her house. She knew its habits.

We climbed down. We lashed everything that could move. I wrapped the journal in its oilcloth one more time and strapped it inside my jacket, against my ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat. Then I took the oars, and Mara took the bow line, and the gorge roared at us like a door opening onto fire.

"Whenever you're ready, champ," I muttered to myself, and pushed off.

* * *

The entry came up faster than it looked from the ledge. Everything does. That's the first lesson of whitewater and the last one people learn: the river is always faster than your eyes.

"Fang on the right — hard left!" I dug the oars and the raft swung, the fanged rock sliding past close enough that I heard the water hiss against its flank, and then we were on the tongue and the green ramp took us and the world became noise and spray and the planks flexing under my boots like a living back. The first standing wave hit us square and lifted the bow skyward — Mara's weight shifting perfectly low and forward, exactly as promised — and we punched through in an explosion of white that soaked us both to the marrow.

Second wave. I angled to keep us square. Third wave — bigger, its crest feathering in the wind — and I felt the raft stall for half a heartbeat on its face, that terrible weightless pause, and then we burst through the back side and the gorge turned left and the river tried to kill us.

The rebound hit the starboard tube like a fist. The raft slewed. For one full second I was looking straight down the throat of the keeper — the foam pile turning over and over, deep green underneath, the color of places that keep what they catch — and Elias Brandt's face surfaced in my memory with the clarity of a photograph, mouth open on that word, and my hands moved.

Not my mind. My hands. Eleven years of hands. I buried the left blade, leaned my whole body into the stroke the way you lean into a door that's closing, and the raft came around — slow, agonizing, the current peeling us toward the hole — and then the lane opened ahead of us, that narrow ugly thread of survival, and we shot into it sideways, scraping the buttress with a shriek of drum-on-granite, bouncing once, twice, and through.

Below the drop the gorge widened and the river gathered itself back into something like calm, and I realized I was screaming. I hadn't known I was screaming. I cut it off, breathing hard, arms shaking, and looked at Mara.

She was drenched, wide-eyed, both fists still locked in the netting, and she was looking at me the way you look at a landscape after lightning shows you its real shape.

"You've done that before," she said again, but it meant something else now. "That last stroke. You didn't think about it. You just — knew."

"Yeah," I said. "I knew."

"And the man you couldn't save." She said it quietly. "In the story you started telling on the ledge. There was a man."

The river carried us. I shipped the oars and let it, for a while, because steering suddenly seemed like more than my arms could negotiate. "Elias Brandt," I said. "He liked to call people champ. I threw him a rope and I braced wrong and the river took him out of my hand." I watched the water slide past, brown-green and indifferent. "They took my certification. They were right to. That's the part nobody wants to hear — the part I couldn't say in Skarnvik, because you'd have hired somebody else. I earned the blacklist, Mara. Not the crime they said. But the failure. That was mine."

Mara was quiet for a long time. The gorge walls slid by, streaked with old ice, catching the noon light.

"My grandfather's journal says the expedition leader froze," she said finally. "When the weather turned. Froze — couldn't make a decision, wouldn't send anyone back down, just sat in the tent while his people died one at a time. My grandmother hated him her whole life." She turned to look at me, spray still beaded in her lashes. "But you're not him. I just watched you make a hundred decisions in ninety seconds and every one of them was right. So maybe the difference isn't whether you fail. Maybe it's whether you freeze after."

I didn't have an answer for that. The river didn't need one; it took us around the next bend at its own pace, and I picked up the oars because the current asked, and because my hands wanted the work.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar beneath a wall of columnar basalt, and Mara built a fire small enough to hide and cooked the last of the dried fish, and neither of us talked much, and the silence was the good kind — the kind you build things in.

It was nearly dark when I heard it. Not from downstream, where Vey's men would come from, but upstream — faint, rhythmic, carried on the evening air like a pulse.

An engine. Small. High-pitched. A boat motor, working its way upriver against the current, from the direction of the glacier.

Mara heard it too. She looked at me across the fire, and I watched the color leave her face.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "Nothing comes from upstream. Nothing's ever come from upstream."

The sound grew louder. Around the bend above us, where the last light still touched the water, something was coming down the river toward Skarnvik — toward us — and it was moving fast.
