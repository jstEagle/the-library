## 9. The Checkpoint

The river told us before the map did.

For two days it had been narrowing, gathering itself, running faster and shallower over stone the color of old iron, and now it bent north into a gorge so tight the walls leaned together overhead like conspirators. And there, wedged where the gorge pinched to a throat no wider than a cart track, stood the ranger station.

Mara saw it first from the ridge above the water — a squat timber building with a sod roof going green at the edges, a mast with a signal wire strung down to it, and a fence of peeled poles that meant nothing to anything except the idea of a boundary. Smoke rose thin and straight from its chimney. Someone was home.

She crouched in the heather and felt her stomach do something complicated.

"Rangers," she said, though Gideon was already beside her, already looking, already wearing that particular stillness that wasn't calm at all but a kind of held breath.

"One," Gideon said. "Maybe two. One chimney."

"That's not the problem."

"No," Gideon agreed. "The problem is what they're stationed here for." She said it lightly, the way she said most true things, as if weight were a courtesy she could decline. But Mara had learned to hear the floor under her voice. The source was forbidden. Not discouraged, not closed for season — *forbidden*, by order of the Crown Survey and the Glacier Commission both, which between them had never agreed on anything except this. The river under the ice ran to a place no permit named, because no permit existed. The drowned expedition hadn't died breaking a rule. They'd died proving one necessary.

And here was Gideon Blackwood, blacklisted guide, fugitive from a town's good opinion, standing in the heather with a dead man's journal sewn into her coat lining, calculating angles like a woman planning a polite visit.

"We can go around," Mara said. "Over the shoulder of the ridge. It adds two days—"

"It adds two days we don't have, and it puts us on open scree where anyone with a glass can pick us off." Gideon's eyes never left the station. "Vey's men are behind us. The glacier is ahead of us, and it doesn't care about our schedule. We go through."

"Through means lying."

Gideon turned then, and looked at her, and something in the look made Mara wish she'd kept quiet. Not anger. Worse — patience. "You hired me to get you up a river," Gideon said. "This is what getting up rivers looks like. Sometimes it's rope work. Sometimes it's weather. Sometimes it's a man at a desk who wants a story more than he wants the truth."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then I'll tell him a better one."

* * *

They came down to the station in late afternoon light, when the shadows were long enough to be honest about the day being nearly spent. Mara carried the raft frame on her shoulder, broken down, the way a salvager's daughter would — honestly, visibly, nothing hidden. That had been Gideon's instruction: *be exactly what you are, everywhere except the part that matters.* It should have sounded cynical. What frightened Mara, walking down through the dwarf birch with her father's tools clinking against her back, was how much it sounded like sense.

The ranger came out when their boots hit gravel. He was older than she expected — sixty, maybe, with a white beard trimmed short and hands folded around a tin mug, and he looked at them the way men look at weather they've already decided to endure.

"River's closed above the weir," he said. No greeting. He'd seen them coming; the wire mast would have given him an hour.

"We're not going above," Gideon said, and produced the paper.

Mara watched the ranger take it. Watched his thumb move across the seal — a survey stamp, blue wax gone brittle, the kind that hadn't been issued since before the war reorganized the commissions. She knew, because she'd helped make it. Three nights ago, in the dark of a bothy, by candlelight, while Gideon talked quietly about waxes and presses and the fact that every district office in the fjords still used the same die because replacing them cost money nobody wanted to spend. She'd held the candle. She'd watched the forgery take shape under Gideon's careful hands, and she had told herself it was a copy of a real class of document, a ghost of a permission that might have existed, and she had almost believed that mattered.

The ranger read. His eyebrows did not move.

"Salvage survey," he said. "Lower gorge section only."

"Section four through seven," Gideon said. "Weir to the ice margin. We're contracted out of Skarnvik. There's expedition material in the gorge from eleven years back — frames, line, a stove, possibly a body or what's left of one. The family wants it recovered before calving season takes the rest." She let that sit. "You'll know the story. Everyone knows the story."

Something moved behind the ranger's eyes. Eleven years ago was his whole career ago; it was the reason stations like this existed at all. "I know the story," he said carefully.

"Then you know why the family waited." Gideon's voice dropped half a tone, into sympathy, into the register of someone sharing grief rather than asking favors. "They weren't ready. Now they're running out of ready."

Mara's father had been dead nine days, and she stood there listening to a stranger use the drowned like currency, and her hands wanted to close into fists and her face wanted to crack open, and she did neither, because Gideon had taught her without teaching her that the trick of lying well is having somewhere real to put your pain while you do it.

The ranger looked at the paper again. Looked at Mara. "Your partner's quiet."

"She's the one doing the hauling," Gideon said. "Talkers don't carry raft frames."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him. He handed the paper back. "Ice margin's as far as you go. If your survey touches the tunnel mouth, that's a different conversation, and it isn't one I'm authorized to have. You log in, you log out, and if you see anything up there worth seeing—" he paused, choosing the words like footing on wet rock, "—you report it. That's the job. Both our jobs."

"Understood," Gideon said, and signed the ledger he slid across the sill, and her signature was steady and legible and belonged to a woman named Halvard who had, apparently, worked salvage out of Skarnvik for six years without once existing.

They were through the fence before the smoke from the chimney changed direction.

* * *

They camped that night above the weir, on a shelf of gravel the river had abandoned centuries ago, and Mara sat with her boots off and her feet in the meltwater until they burned cold, because burning cold was better than the other thing she was feeling.

Gideon built the fire small and shielded, the way she built everything — economical, invisible from any angle that mattered. She was checking the raft lashings when Mara finally spoke.

"Halvard," Mara said. "Where did you get a name like that?"

"Borrowed it. There's a Halvard who runs chandlery in Havnvik. Dead man's name, near enough — he lost his license years ago, nobody checks references on ghosts." Gideon didn't look up from the lashing. Her fingers moved through the work with the same fluency they'd moved through the lie, and that was the thing, that was the whole thing, and Mara couldn't stop watching it. "It'll hold."

"I'm not talking about the raft."

Gideon's hands went still. For a moment the only sound was the river below, running fast and shallow over its iron stones, saying the same thing over and over the way water does.

"You want me to apologize," Gideon said.

"I want to understand how it was so easy."

Now Gideon looked up. Firelight moved on her face and made it hard to read, which Mara suspected was the point of firelight for people like her. "Easy," Gideon repeated. "You think that was easy."

"I think you forged a government seal in three hours. I think you lied to a man whose job is the truth, and you used my grandfather's grave to do it, and your hand never shook. I stood right there, Gideon. I watched. It was like watching someone speak a language I have to study."

"Yes," Gideon said. "That's what ten years of guiding does. You learn that the mountain doesn't care about your honesty, the client doesn't either, and the difference between alive and dead is very often whether you can say the wrong thing convincingly at the right time." She set down the lashing cord. "You want the ugly version? The version where I'm a bad person? Fine. Here it is. I've been lying since I was nineteen years old. To clients about conditions. To myself about risk. To a coroner, once, about what happened on the Vantaa descent, because the truth would have ended me and the lie only ended my career." Her voice stayed level. That was the worst part. It stayed level the way deep water stays level. "Lying isn't a decision I make anymore, Mara. It's just the ground I walk on. I forgot it was even there until you started looking at me like that."

Mara pulled her feet from the water. The cold ached up her ankles like a warning. "And the journal? When it comes to that? Will the ground be there too?"

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means Vey offered you money for it and you refused. It means the rangers asked you to report what you find and you said *understood* in a voice I believed, and I've known you nine days and I believed you, and I don't know anymore whether that's because you're honest with me or because I'm easy to lie to." She heard her own voice rise and hated it, and kept going anyway, because some things once started will not be stopped. "My father died refusing to bend for Aldous Vey. Quietly. Stupidly, maybe. He could have handed over the journal and lived, and everyone in Skarnvik would have called him sensible, and he wouldn't do it. And tonight I watched you talk your way past the law in ninety seconds flat, and I thought — *this is the person I chose.* This is who I'm following up a glacier. A woman for whom the truth is a tool with a handle worn smooth from use."

The fire cracked. Somewhere across the gorge a rockfall went off like distant artillery, the glacier shedding, always shedding, counting down in a language of rubble.

Gideon sat back on her heels. When she spoke again, the levelness was gone, and what was underneath was worse — tiredness, real tiredness, the kind that has nothing to do with sleep.

"You're right," she said.

Mara had prepared for defense. She had no plan for surrender.

"The seal was easy because I've done worse and gotten away with it, and every time I got away with it, it got easier, and I told myself competence and conscience were different ledgers." Gideon fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "But listen to me, because this part is true and I need you to have it. The journal goes to the rangers, or the commission, or the newspapers, whichever survives long enough to read it. Not because I'm good. Because Vey killed eleven people to bury it, and if I trade it — for money, for my name, for anything — then I'm just a better-dressed version of the men chasing us. My father used to say a liar's worst enemy isn't getting caught. It's forgetting which stories were hers." She looked up, and the firelight was just firelight now, and her eyes were just tired. "I haven't forgotten yet. Check me. Whenever you're afraid I have — check me. That's yours to do. I'm asking you to do it."

Mara sat with that. The river ran. The ache in her feet throbbed in time with it.

"My grandfather is up there," she said finally. Quieter now. "In the ice, or under it, or wherever the expedition stopped. I've known it since I was small enough to believe legends were kindnesses. And I keep thinking — when we find him, I want it to mean something clean. And nothing about how we're getting there is clean."

"No," Gideon said. "Nothing ever is. That's not a flaw in the route. That's the route."

* * *

They took the water at first light, running the weir chute with the raft riding high and light, and the gorge swallowed them in shadow and spray. By midmorning the walls fell back and the valley opened onto the glacier tongue — a river of dirty white descending from the clouds, cracked and groaning, vast past sense — and Mara understood suddenly how small the word *source* had been in her mouth all these years.

That was when she heard the whistle.

One note, high, from the ridge behind them. Then another, answering, from ahead — from the direction of the ice.

Gideon's head came up like a bird dog's. "Off the water," she said, low and fast. "Now."

Mara drove the raft for the shingle bank, heart slamming, and as they grounded it she looked back downstream and saw them: three figures on the trail they'd walked yesterday, moving with the unhurried economy of men who knew exactly where the river went and were content to let it deliver them.

And ahead, at the glacier's edge, half-hidden in the blue shadow of the ice, something glinted that had no business being there.

A cairn. Fresh-built. Marked with a strip of red cloth.

Someone had reached the tunnel mouth before them — and left a sign for whoever came next.
