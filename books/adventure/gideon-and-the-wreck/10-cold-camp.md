## 10. Cold Camp

The storm found them an hour past the ranger station, and by the time the light went it had erased the world.

Gideon had seen weather like this before — on the Karakoram, in Patagonia, once on a ridge in the Wrangells where she'd spent two days buried and come out thinking she'd died and this was what came after. It wasn't wind so much as a solid thing, a white animal with weight and intent, and it wanted them off the glacier tongue the way a dog wants a bone out of your hand.

"We can't go on," she shouted, and Mara didn't argue. That was one of the things Gideon was learning about her: she argued when arguing mattered and saved her breath when it didn't. Most people couldn't tell the difference. Mara could.

They'd been climbing since dawn along the glacier's eastern margin, where the old moraine gave purchase and the crevasse field lay to their left like a mouth full of broken teeth. The plan had been to reach the lateral moraine's high shoulder before nightfall and camp in the open, tents pitched behind a wind wall of cut snow blocks. The storm had other ideas. It came down off the icecap sideways, freighted with spindrift that stung like flung gravel, and visibility collapsed from a hundred meters to ten to nothing at all.

Gideon navigated by compass and by feel, counting paces, reading the slope through her boots. Twice she stopped dead with a raised fist because the ground under her had gone wrong — too hollow, too quiet — and twice they detoured around blue shadows in the white that meant air where there should have been ice.

Then, mercifully, the mountain offered them a door.

It was a meltwater cave in the glacier's flank, carved by some summer torrent years ago and abandoned when the ice shifted, its entrance half-choked with drifted snow. Gideon found it because the wind changed note — a lower, hollower sound — and she'd learned long ago to trust her ears over her eyes in weather like this. She dug for twenty minutes with the shovel while Mara held their rope taut behind her, and then the drift gave way and there it was: a throat of blue ice going back into darkness, dry-floored, maybe four meters deep.

"Home," Gideon said.

* * *

Camp in a snow cave was a discipline, and Gideon performed it like liturgy because ritual kept the cold out better than speed did. They marked the entrance with a wand so they could find it blind. They leveled the floor and laid their foam pads and the folded sailcloth from the raft. They got the stove going low in the entrance's lee, the flame a small orange secret behind a windscreen of snow blocks, and melted snow in shifts because you could not — never, ever — melt snow directly against your skin or your lips would crack and split and refuse to heal at altitude.

Mara watched all of it with that flat, cataloguing attention of hers. She was competent — more than competent; she'd grown up on water and salvage and cold — but glacier craft was a different grammar, and Gideon could see her learning it, filing it away.

"You've done this a lot," Mara said. Not a question.

"Enough."

"Enough to be good at it."

Gideon fed another armful of snow into the pot. "Enough to know the mountain doesn't care how good you are. It only cares whether you're careful today."

"And were you careful? The day it went wrong?"

The question landed without warning, the way Mara's questions always did — no run-up, no softening. Gideon kept her eyes on the stove. Outside, the storm pressed against the entrance like something listening.

"Eat," she said. "We'll need the calories."

Mara didn't push. She handed over the bag of trail food and ate her own share in silence, and Gideon understood that the silence was not a retreat. It was patience. Mara Voss — no, Mara Salvesen, though everyone in Skarnvik called her by her father's trade — was a salvager's daughter, and salvagers knew that some things stayed down until they were ready to come up, and that hauling on them early broke them.

They rigged the stove line, stowed everything inside the vestibule of sailcloth, and crawled into their bags back to back, which was warmth and also distance, both necessary. The storm howled its one long note against the ice. Gideon lay awake listening to it and thinking about a river under a glacier and a man named Aldous Vey who had sent five people up that river and then closed his hand.

She must have slept eventually, because the dream came.

* * *

It was always the same river. The Talung, high water, the crossing at Drukpa Gorge where the porters had warned them and the client had laughed. Daniel Reyes. Thirty-four years old, a tech money man who'd bought himself an A-list expedition the way he bought everything, who wore his helmet crooked because he thought it looked casual in photographs. He'd insisted on the raft instead of the portage. He'd insisted, and Gideon had been young enough and hungry enough for the fee and the reputation to let herself be persuaded, and she had put him in the boat, and at the top of the gorge the river had taken him out of it in less time than it takes to say his name.

In the dream she was in the eddy again, running the bank, seeing the green flash of his jacket downstream. In the dream she always caught him. Her hand closed on the strap of his PFD and she hauled and he came up gasping and alive, and for one whole second — the length of a heartbeat, the length of a lie — she felt the relief like sunlight.

Then the dream did what it always did. His weight came into her arms and it was wrong, too heavy, too loose, and his face when it turned toward hers was the face from the recovery three days later, gray and swollen and peaceful in the way that nothing living is peaceful, and his mouth moved and said, in Daniel Reyes' easy laughing voice: *You let go.*

"I didn't," she said aloud, in the dark of the ice cave, and woke with her heart slamming.

The storm still roared. Beside her, Mara's breathing had changed — awake, had been awake, maybe for a while.

"You talk in your sleep," Mara said quietly.

"What did I say?"

"'I didn't.'"

Gideon stared at the ceiling of the cave, where the stove light made faint amber ghosts in the blue ice. Ten thousand kilometers and six years from the Talung, and the river had followed her here anyway. Rivers did that. They got into you and stayed.

"There was a client," she said. Because it was night, and because the storm had sealed them in like a tomb, and because Mara had earned it — had bled beside her on the moraine, had held the rope without being asked, had walked away from Skarnvik and her father's grave-in-waiting on nothing but Gideon's word. "On a river called the Talung. Six years ago. I was leading a commercial expedition. One of the clients insisted on running a gorge we should have portaged, and I let him talk me into it. The river took him out of the boat at the top of the drop. We recovered his body three days later, forty kilometers down."

Mara said nothing. In the dark, her silence had a different quality now — not waiting, but receiving.

"The inquiry cleared me," Gideon went on. The words came out flat, rehearsed by years of saying them to lawyers and boards and her own reflection. "Properly cleared. The decision to run it was his, documented, witnessed. But his family hired people to make noise, and the industry doesn't need a reason to bury a guide, it just needs a smell. I lost my certification. My bookings. My — " she almost said *my name*, and heard how it would sound next to what Vey had done to her, and stopped. "After that, nobody hires Gideon Blackwood for anything that matters. So I do salvage contracts. Wrecks. Dead men's boats."

"You could have told me in Skarnvik."

"You'd have looked at me differently."

"I'm looking at you now," Mara said.

Gideon turned her head. In the dim red glow of the dying stove coals reflected through the ice, Mara's face was close, watchful, unarmored in a way Gideon hadn't seen before. Not soft — Mara was not soft, might never be soft — but open. A door propped with a stone.

"Differently how?" Gideon asked.

"I don't know yet." A pause. "But not worse."

Something in Gideon's chest, a thing clenched for six years, loosened by a single degree. It was such a small motion. It nearly undid her.

* * *

Morning was a rumor. The storm had not spent itself; if anything it had settled in for the duration, and the wind at the cave mouth sang its one note like a monk at prayer. They were storm-bound, and both of them knew what that meant: a day burned off the calendar, and the calving season coming on, and Vey's men somewhere behind them in the white, suffering the same arithmetic.

They ran the camp well anyway. Melted water in quantity. Checked feet, checked each other for frostnip — cheeks, fingertips, the places cold steals quietly. Ate hot oatmeal with butter and sugar, the luxury meal, the morale meal. Gideon re-taped Mara's blistered heel and Mara repacked Gideon's abraded knuckles from the dig-out, their hands working with a familiarity that neither of them remarked on, because remarking on it would have made it a thing, and things had to be survived too.

Midday, the storm dropped its voice for twenty minutes — the false lull that kills more mountaineers than the peak of the blow — and Gideon crawled to the entrance and dug a viewing slot and read the sky. Cloud deck low and layered, pressure falling, the wind veering northwest. This was not a blow. This was a system.

"A day," she reported, backing inside. "Maybe two."

Mara sat cross-legged on the pads, mending a torn strap with dental floss and a sailmaker's needle, her hands steady, her eyes distant. Gideon had learned to read those distances. There was the working distance, when she was planning. There was the wary distance, when she was watching Gideon. And there was this one — far away, fixed on nothing — which Gideon had only ever seen pointed north, back down the valley, toward Skarnvik.

"Tell me about him," Gideon said. "Your father."

The needle paused.

"You don't have to," Gideon added.

"I know." Mara bit the floss, tied it off. For a while the only sound was the storm and the tick of cooling metal from the stove. Then, without looking up: "He was the one who taught me to read water. Before anyone. I was six, standing on the dock at Skarnvik, and he put my hand flat on the surface and said, *feel how it pulls? The sea is always telling you where it's going. Most people don't listen.*" Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I thought every father taught their daughters that. Took me years to learn most fathers teach fear instead."

"He sounds like a good man."

"He was a careful man." She corrected herself gently, the way she corrected Gideon's knots. "Careful is better than good. Good is what people call you when they want something." She wound the floss. "He took Vey's contract because of me. My mother's treatment, when she was sick. The debt was fifteen years old and Vey never once mentioned it, never once leaned, until the journal surfaced. And then he came to the workshop and stood in our doorway like he owned the air in it, and my father — " Mara's jaw worked. "My father, who never raised his voice in his life, told Aldous Vey no. To his face. Quietly. And two days later the workshop was wrecked and my father was gone, and everyone in town says drowned fisherman, terrible accident, happens every winter."

"Do you believe that?"

"No." Flat. Final. "And I believe it the way you believe your client's death wasn't your fault. With the part of me that knows, and against the part of me that wishes."

Gideon felt that sentence go through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. *With the part of me that knows, and against the part of me that wishes.* Yes. That was the exact architecture of it. Six years of knowing and wishing, load-bearing walls of a life.

"Mara." She waited until Mara looked at her. "What happened to your father is Vey's doing. What happened on the Talung was a river's doing and a man's own choice. Those aren't the same weight. Don't carry mine as yours."

"Then don't carry his either." Mara's voice cracked, just barely, on *his*, and she looked down fast, and Gideon saw her press the heel of her hand hard against her eye, once, briefly, the way people do when they have decided crying is a luxury the schedule doesn't allow. "God. Listen to us. Two women in a hole in a glacier, trading our worst nights like rations."

"It's what nights are for," Gideon said. "Out here. You burn them or you spend them."

Mara laughed — a short, wet, real laugh — and it was the first time Gideon had heard her laugh since Skarnvik, and it did something to the temperature of the cave that the stove never could.

* * *

The second night the storm reached its full voice, and the glacier groaned around them like a ship in heavy seas — deep internal reports, the ice settling and shifting, and once a crack so sharp and close that both of them came halfway out of their bags with knives in hand before common sense caught up.

"Settling," Gideon said. "The storm loads the surface, the ice answers. It's talking to itself, not to us."

"You're sure?"

"No. But it helps to say it like I am."

Mara snorted and lay back down. And then, after a while, in the dark: "The checkpoint. The permit you showed the rangers."

Gideon's stomach dipped. "What about it."

"It was forged. Beautifully. You lied to those rangers like you were reading scripture, and I stood there believing every word, and afterward I couldn't decide if I was impressed or frightened." A pause. "How easily you bend, Gideon. Toward survival. I keep wondering what else you'd bend toward."

The question hung in the cold. Gideon considered lying — it would have been easy, it was always easy, that was exactly the problem — and then spent the truth instead, carefully, like the last of the fuel.

"I'd bend toward keeping you alive," she said. "That's the honest answer, and I don't love it. I've spent six years paying for one flexible decision. So I watch myself now. Every bend costs me something, and I make it anyway, and I count the cost after." She turned onto her side, facing Mara's shape in the dark. "At the source, if it comes to choosing between the truth and getting out — I don't know what I'll pick. I'd like to tell you I'll pick right. I won't promise it."

Mara was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone rough at the edges.

"My grandfather went up that river for the same reason we are. Because the truth was worth more than the safe road home." A breath. "If you bend, bend toward that. That's all I ask. That's all he'd ask."

"I can aim for that," Gideon said. "Aiming I can do."

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, in the blue dark of the ice, something passed between them that had no name either of them would have used — not yet, maybe not ever — but it had weight, and warmth, and it settled over them like the sailcloth over their gear, thin and insufficient and somehow enough.

Gideon slept without dreaming of rivers.

* * *

She woke to silence.

Not the silence of sleep — the silence of absence. The storm's great single note was gone, and in its place was the enormous ringing quiet that follows a gale on the ice, and a quality to the light at the cave mouth that meant clear sky.

Gideon was moving before her mind finished waking. She crawled to the entrance, swept the drift aside, and looked out.

The world had been remade overnight. The glacier stretched away under a hard cobalt sky, scoured clean, glittering, featureless except where the wind had carved sastrugi into frozen waves. And to the northeast — she raised the binoculars, braced her elbows on the drift — perhaps eight kilometers out, at the edge of resolution, a line of figures moved across the white. Four of them. Roped. Moving fast and light, on skis, on a bearing that needed no interpretation.

They were no longer behind. They were abreast, cutting the corner, racing them for the glacier's headwall and the tunnel mouth beyond.

Behind her, Mara stirred. "Storm's done?"

"Done." Gideon lowered the binoculars, and her voice came out level and cold as the morning. "Pack everything. Eat while you walk. They've found our line — and they're a day ahead of where I thought they'd be."
