## 11. Hunted on the Ice

The glacier tongue announced itself a full day before we reached it — a white shoulder of old light rising out of the valley's throat, cracked and groaning like something asleep that dreamed badly. By the time we stood at its toe, where the river emerged from under the ice in a brawling grey torrent, my hands had memorized the fear before my head caught up.

"Crevasse fields," I said, mostly to myself.

Mara looked up from retying her bootlace. "Is that bad?"

"It's honest." I shaded my eyes against the glare bouncing off the lower icefall. "A glacier tells you exactly what it is. Every crack in it is a question. You just have to answer them correctly, every single time, for as long as you're standing on it."

"And if you answer wrong?"

"You get to stop answering questions forever."

She took that the way she'd learned to take most of what I said about mountains — filing it away without flinching, deciding later whether it was wisdom or theater. Three days running the gorge together had taught me she did that. She'd also taught me things: how to read her silences, which were never empty; how her father's absence moved through her like weather, front after front, none of them breaking. We didn't talk about him much. We didn't need to. Grief shared in silence weighs less than grief explained.

We roped up at the glacier's edge. Fifty meters of line between us, knots checked twice, because the journal's last pages had described this exact stretch of ice and the men who'd died learning it, and I had no intention of joining their company.

The plan was simple, which is another way of saying it was fragile. Cross the tongue above the icefall, cut east along the medial moraine where the footing was rock instead of rotten ice, and gain the high shelf below the seracs before nightfall. From there, two days to the tunnel mouth. Torv's warning sat in my chest like swallowed stone: *Vey has a helicopter.* A helicopter meant we couldn't outrun him. It meant the only thing left was to be somewhere he couldn't land, couldn't follow, couldn't buy.

The first hour was almost kind. The ice was wind-scoured and firm, our crampons biting cleanly, the sun flat and silver behind high cloud so there were no shadows to lie to us. Mara moved well — better than I'd expected from a woman who'd spent her life on water, not ice. She had a salvager's balance, that instinct for surfaces that shift under you. Twice I watched her feel out a hollow bridge of snow with her pole before trusting it, patient as a heron.

Then the wind came down off the ice cap, and with it came the sound.

Not the glacier. The glacier spoke constantly — settling cracks, deep hydraulic groans, the muffled percussion of meltwater working its way through blue darkness. This was different. This was a whistle. Thin, human, carried on the wind from the moraine behind us.

I turned. Mara turned a half-second later, reading my body before the sound resolved.

Three figures on the lateral moraine, small with distance but unmistakable in their intent — moving fast, roped together, one of them pointing. The sun chose that moment to tear through the cloud layer, and light flashed off something in the lead man's hand. Glass. Binoculars. Or a scope.

"Torv said four," Mara said. Her voice was very level. "Where's the fourth?"

"Flanking." The word left my mouth cold. "They'll have sent someone wide to cut the moraine route. That's why they're not hurrying."

We weren't being chased. We were being herded.

I looked east, toward the high shelf, and saw it — or rather saw the shape of the trap. The moraine narrowed ahead into a defile between the ice wall and a rib of exposed bedrock. Perfect ground for two men with rifles and patience. Vey hadn't gotten rich by chasing people. He got rich by making sure people ran where he wanted them.

"We can't take the moraine," Mara said.

"No."

"So we go over the ice."

I looked at the crevasse field spread across the tongue's midsection like the shattered shell of something enormous. Ridges of fractured ice, snow bridges sagging between them, blue slots going down into dark that had no bottom anyone had ever measured. In good conditions, with time, with a day to probe and flag a route, it was serious mountaineering. In these conditions, pursued, with the afternoon already leaning west—

It was the only direction they wouldn't want us to go. Hunters want their quarry on predictable ground. Nobody chooses a crevasse field.

"Mara." I made myself say it plainly. "If we go in there, everything depends on the rope. On me reading the ice. If I'm wrong once—"

"You won't be." She said it fast, without ceremony, the way you hand someone a tool when their hands are shaking. "You led us through the gorge. You read water like my father reads salvage charts. Ice is just slow water."

*Ice is just slow water.* I would think about that sentence for a long time afterward. It wasn't true. But it was the kind of untrue that keeps you alive.

We turned off the firm ice and into the broken ground, and the world changed key.

* * *

Inside the field, the scale inverted. From the moraine it had looked like rubble. Up close, each fracture was architecture — walls of ice ten meters tall, leaning, translucent, lit from within by that deep glacial blue that seems to generate its own weather. Snow bridges arched over black slots wide enough to swallow a truck. Meltwater clicked and trickled somewhere beneath us, patient as a clock.

I went first. That was non-negotiable, and Mara didn't argue, which told me she understood the arithmetic better than she let on. I probed every step with the pole, punched test holes through suspect bridges, chose lines the way you choose words in a language you half-remember — by feel, by rhythm, by the memory of a hundred crossings in a life I'd tried to bury.

And here is the thing I have never admitted to anyone except the inside of my own skull: I was *good*. The old skill didn't surface like a memory. It surfaced like an organ resuming function. My feet knew things my mind had forgotten knowing. Where the snow sounded drum-tight I crossed without slowing; where it gave that soft dead thud I detoured without conscious decision. Somewhere behind my eyes a client named Elias kept dying, the way he always did, in the green water below the third pitch — but my body had made its peace with that ghost years ago, and it worked anyway. Maybe that's the definition of craft. Working anyway.

Behind me, Mara followed my steps exactly, planting her feet in my prints, keeping the rope in that taut-but-not-strained tension that means trust given mechanically, without debate. Twice I heard her breath catch as a bridge flexed under her. Twice she crossed anyway.

The hunters reached the field's edge twenty minutes after we did. I heard them before I saw them — voices, sharp and frustrated, carrying strangely across the ice. Then a rifle shot, flat and cracking, and the bullet went somewhere above and behind us with a sound like tearing cloth.

"They're shooting blind," Mara panted. "They can't see us."

"They don't need to see us." I pulled her down behind an ice ridge as a second shot spanged off rock somewhere to our right. "They need us to move fast. Fast people don't probe. Fast people pick the wrong bridge." I wiped sweat that froze instantly at my hairline. "Every shot is a suggestion."

So we moved slow. Slower than instinct screamed. Bullets came at intervals — deliberate, unhurried, herding shots — and we treated them like bad weather, hunching through them, refusing the gift of panic. Once a round struck the ice wall beside my head close enough to spit chips against my cheek, and I felt the animal part of me lunge for speed, and I held it back with both hands, and we crossed the next three bridges at a walk.

It was working. That was the terrible thing. We were beating them at their own game, trading time for safety, letting them waste ammunition on ice. The far edge of the field rose ahead — clean firn slope, then the shelf, then cover. Forty minutes, maybe less.

That's when the bridge broke.

Not under me. Under Mara.

She'd done everything right. I'd tested the crossing myself, felt the snow hold my weight with margin to spare. But snow bridges don't fail by weight alone; they fail by *fatigue*, by the accumulated insult of a dozen crossings, by sun weakening the arch from within while it looks identical from above. My print had been the straw. Hers was the weight that mattered.

The rope snapped taut and she dropped, and the world contracted to physics.

I threw myself flat, boots dug in, arms wrapped around the rope, and the arrest hit me like a train — all her weight plus gravity plus the friction of the lip burning through the line. I slid. Ten centimeters. Twenty. The edge of the hole bit across my forearms where I'd hooked them over the rope, and I felt skin open, felt blood freeze slick on the cordage, and stopped caring about any of it because the only fact in the universe was that the rope held and she was hanging in the blue dark and I was not moving another millimeter.

"Don't move!" My voice came out shredded. "Mara — don't move. Don't look down. Look at me."

Below me, thirty feet down the slot, her face tilted up. White with cold and terror, but her eyes were hers. "Gideon—"

"I've got you. Listen to my voice. I'm going to anchor, then I'm coming down the rope. Count your breaths. Four in, four out. Can you do that?"

A pause. Then, steady as a metronome: "Four in. Four out."

Good. God, she was good.

I got a picket driven behind a ridge of solid ice, backed it with the coil of rope, and rigged the anchor with hands that wouldn't stop bleeding and wouldn't stop working either. Then I descended into the crevasse.

Inside, the light was submarine. Blue fell upward around us. The walls were striated like tree rings, decades of winters stacked in glass, and Mara hung among them turning slowly at the end of the rope, and I thought, absurdly, *this is what the drowned expedition saw last*, and put the thought away like a knife back in its drawer.

I reached her. Checked her harness, her circulation, the rope over the lip — found the spot where it had sawed into the ice edge and padded it with a sling. Her ankle was wrenched where she'd slammed the wall on the fall; she could stand on it, barely, and lied about how much it hurt, and I let the lie stand because there was nothing to be done about it up here.

"Hauling you out will take both of us and I'm the only one up top," I said. "So we do it the hard way. Prusik loops. You climb the rope. I'll belay from above. Your ankle takes what it has to take."

"Gideon." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Just — say something true first."

I looked at her hanging in the blue. "You are not dying in a hole today. That's true. Everything else is negotiable."

Something moved through her face — not quite a smile, too fierce for that. She clipped in the loops and climbed.

It took forty minutes. It took everything either of us had. When she came over the lip we lay side by side on the firn, gasping, and the rope between us was scored pink with my blood, and neither of us said anything for a long time because nothing needed saying. Trust isn't built in motion, whatever I'd believed in the gorge. It's built in holes. In the moment someone hangs at the end of your line and finds out what you're made of.

But survival writes its invoice in the same hour it pays you.

* * *

The cost came due at dusk, when we took inventory in the lee of a serac.

The fall had done more than bruise Mara. Somewhere in the chaos — the arrest, the haul, the frantic minutes at the lip — her pack had swung against the crevasse wall and torn, and what it lost, the blue dark had kept. We laid out what remained on a groundsheet and stared at it the way you stare at a wound.

One stove. Half a fuel canister. Two sleeping bags, one soaked through where the rope drag had dragged it through slush. Six days of food, and we needed four, if nothing else went wrong — and something had already gone wrong twice today. The rope itself was damaged, core-shot in two places from the lip and the rock. I coiled it and marked the bad sections with tape, and we both understood without saying it that our lifeline now had a lifespan.

"The medical kit's gone," Mara said quietly. "All of it."

I looked at my forearms, raw and crusted, and at her ankle, swelling inside her boot like something breathing. "Then we don't get injured again."

"That's the plan?"

"It's the only plan I've got."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it and real life in it somehow, the laugh of a person who has been at the end of a rope in a blue hole and come back up. "My father used to say salvage planning is just deciding which losses you can afford before the sea decides for you."

"He sounds like he'd have liked glaciers."

"He'd have hated them. He hated anything that took things slowly." She was quiet a moment. "He'd have hated Aldous Vey most of all, then. Slowest thief there is."

We ate cold rations because the stove fuel was now counted in hours, not days, and every gram of heat might be needed later for something worse than comfort. Above us the seracs creaked in the falling temperature, contracting, complaining. Below, far behind, I swept the field one last time with the binoculars.

The hunters were still at the crevasse field's edge. Camped. Waiting.

They hadn't followed us in. Of course they hadn't — they'd seen the arrest, or deduced it, and they knew what I now knew: that field had nearly killed us, and it would certainly kill anyone who entered it at night without a guide. So they'd do what Vey's people always did. They'd wait for morning. They'd find the trail where we exited. And they'd push through the broken ground in daylight, taking the time we no longer had, closing the gap by degrees, patient as the ice itself.

Unless the helicopter beat them to it.

I passed the glasses to Mara and watched her find the camp, watch her jaw set. "How far to the tunnel mouth?" she asked.

"A day and a half. Less if we push. More—" I glanced at her ankle, at my arms, at the shrunken pile of supplies. "More if the mountain votes otherwise."

"Then we push."

"We push," I agreed. "And tomorrow, when they come through the field, we need to be somewhere with a door."

Mara lowered the glasses. In the last violet light her face was drawn and frost-nipped and absolutely certain. "What kind of door?"

I looked north, up-glacier, to where the tongue narrowed toward the black seam in the icefall that the journal had marked with a dead man's careful hand. A seam in the ice. A mouth.

"The kind," I said, "that the drowned expedition went into and didn't come out of."
