## 12. The Village Under the Ice

We smelled it before we saw it.

That was the thing none of the legends mentioned — that a dead camp has a smell. Kerosene gone waxy and old, canvas rotting at the seams, and underneath it all something sweeter and worse that I didn't have a name for yet but my body did, because my stomach clenched and my throat closed before my mind caught up.

"Wind's from the north," Gideon said behind me. She'd stopped walking. I heard her stop — the crunch of her boots going quiet on the glacier's crust, the creak of her pack frame settling as she shifted its weight to her shoulders. "Mara."

"I know." The wind came down off the icefall in long slow breaths, and each one carried that smell to us like a hand offering something we couldn't refuse. "It's here, isn't it. The last camp."

"It's here."

The fog had lifted an hour after dawn, and what it left behind was a world of white so clean it hurt to look at — the glacier tongue stretching south toward Skarnvik, the blue walls of the seracs standing east of us like the pillars of some ruined church, and ahead, tucked into a shallow valley the ice had carved for itself, a scatter of dark shapes that might have been rocks.

They weren't rocks.

I'd grown up on my father's stories of the *Valkyrie* expedition — thirty-one years ago now, the summer my mother was carrying me. Six men and my grandfather Halvard, who was not a guide but a surveyor, a man who drew maps of places no one had asked him to map because he believed every river deserved to be known. They went up the glacier to find where the under-ice river began. They never came back. The town said the mountain took them, the way the mountain takes everyone eventually, and my grandmother put a stone in the churchyard with his name on it and nothing underneath, and she never once in forty years let anyone say his name aloud in her kitchen.

I was walking toward his grave, and it wasn't in the churchyard at all.

"Mara." Gideon's hand on my shoulder. Her glove was stiff with frost and it still felt warm through my coat, or maybe that was just the way she touched people — deliberate, like everything else she did, like she'd decided to do it. "We go in together. We look at what there is to look at. And then we take what proves it and we leave. We don't stay longer than we have to."

"You think I'm going to fall apart."

"I think you're about to meet your grandfather," she said quietly, "and I don't know any other way to do that than badly."

* * *

The camp lay in the hollow like a village abandoned mid-sentence.

There were three tents, two of them still half-standing, their poles bowed under decades of snow-load, the fabric frozen into shapes like gestures — a hand raised, a shoulder turned. A cook stove sat on a sled runner with a kettle still on the plate, fused to it by rust. Ropes were coiled against a supply crate. A pair of boots stood upright outside the nearest tent, empty, patient, waiting for feet that had been somewhere else for thirty-one years.

And everywhere, small things. A tin cup on its side. A pencil, snapped. A page of a logbook pressed flat under a stone, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. A mitten. A compass with its glass starred white, pointing faithfully at nothing.

"They left fast," Gideon said. She moved through the camp the way she moved through rapids — reading it, letting her eyes do the work her hands wouldn't risk. "Look. The tents are struck from the inside, mostly. They packed in a hurry but they packed. That's not panic. That's a decision made at night, quickly, by people who expected to be moving again by morning." She crouched by the stove. "Kettle's still here. You don't leave your kettle if you're planning a retreat. You leave it if someone told you to drop everything and run."

"Run from what?"

Gideon didn't answer right away. She was looking at the far side of the hollow, where the ice wall rose in a clean blue face — and where, cut into that face, dark against the blue, was a line of shapes I hadn't wanted to see from the ridge and couldn't pretend not to see now.

Six of them. No — seven. Frozen into the ice like flies in amber, some upright, some slumped, cloth and leather and bone held in the glacier's cold grip the way the sea holds things it means to keep.

My legs took me there without asking me. That's the truth of grief nobody tells you: it doesn't wait for permission. It moves your body for you while the rest of you is still standing in the camp saying *run from what.*

The first body was a young man, barely older than I am now, curled on his side with his arms over his head. Not sheltering. Protecting. The second was face-down, and I made myself not turn away, because turning away felt like leaving them twice. The third —

The third wore a surveyor's harness. Old style, leather and brass, the kind they don't make anymore, the kind I'd seen in a photograph on my grandmother's shelf my whole childhood: a lean man squinting into a theodolite, one hand resting on a boy's shoulder. Me. The boy in the photograph was my father. The hand on his shoulder belonged to Halvard Oyelaran, and the harness he wore in the picture was this harness, cracked now and furred with frost, the brass buckles green with age, and inside it was my grandfather.

He was sitting up. That was the worst of it, or the mercy of it, I couldn't decide. He was sitting with his back against the ice, his hands in his lap, and someone had folded them there — someone had taken the time, in that killing cold, to fold a dead man's hands. His face was turned slightly toward the camp, toward the tents, as if he'd been watching the others when the end came. There was frost in his beard. His eyes were gone but the set of his brow was still there, still readable, thirty-one years later, and what it read was not fear.

It was anger. Cold, settled, permanent anger. The anger of a man who knew exactly what was happening to him and exactly whose fault it was, and had run out of time to tell anyone.

"Halvard," I said. My voice came out strange, small, like it belonged to someone standing very far away. "Grandfather. I'm — "

I didn't know what I was. Sorry. Late. Here. None of the words fit. I put my bare hand — I don't remember taking the glove off — against the ice beside his shoulder, and the cold went up my arm like a current, and I stayed there, and Gideon stayed three steps behind me and let me, and did not say one word, which is how I learned that she understood silence better than anyone I'd ever met.

* * *

It was Gideon who found the proof, because grief had taken my eyes and hers still worked.

She found it in the fourth body, the one lying apart from the others, closer to the camp — a man frozen mid-crawl, reaching back toward the ice wall, toward the others, as if he'd been trying to get to them when he fell. In his outstretched fist, preserved by the same cold that had killed him, was a notebook. Not the expedition journal — Gideon had that, waterproofed, recovered from the cave, the journal that had started all of this. This was smaller. A pocket book. A man's private record.

We built a windbreak from a tent fly and worked by the grey light with our backs to the wind, and Gideon turned the brittle pages with a care that made my chest ache, because I knew what that care cost her. Every page she turned was a page closer to confirming what she'd already guessed, and she turned them anyway.

"The dates match," she said finally. Her voice was flat in the way I'd learned meant she was holding something down with both hands. "This is Eirik Lund. He was the expedition's second — the journal mentions him. Last entry is dated the ninth of October." She looked up at me across the page. "Mara. Listen to this."

She read, and the wind filled the pauses:

*"Vey's men came at dusk. Four of them, armed, wearing no colors. Torvald recognized one — the big one, the one Vey keeps at the shipping office. They said the contract was void. They said the source was to be sealed and we were to come down the mountain and speak of it to no one. Halvard refused. He said a map belongs to everyone. They shot Torvald first. Then they told the rest of us to run — up-valley, into the ice, away from the route home. They are not driving us off the mountain. They are herding us onto it. E. Lund, final entry."*

The wind came down the glacier and made the tent fly snap like a flag, and I sat with my grandfather twenty meters away, folded-handed and furious in the ice, and I understood at last what the legend was.

Not a warning. Not a curse. A cover-up. Thirty-one years of *the mountain takes them*, thirty-one years of my grandmother's silent kitchen, thirty-one years of a town looking at Aldous Vey's ships and mills and generous winter charities and seeing a patron, because the alternative was seeing what was frozen into the glacier above their heads.

"He didn't die of the cold," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "He was murdered. They all were. And Vey — " My hands were shaking. I watched them shake as if they belonged to a stranger. "Vey paid for the expedition. The journal proved that. And when my grandfather found the source, Vey sent men to erase them, and then he spent thirty-one years being mourned with. Being *sympathized* with. He came to my grandmother's house. He sent flowers every year on the anniversary. He — "

I stopped, because if I kept going I was going to be sick, and I needed what was in my stomach.

Gideon reached across and closed the notebook gently and put her hand flat on top of it, on top of mine. "Mara."

"He killed my family," I said. "And he's been eating dinner with what was left of it my whole life."

"Yes." She didn't soften it. That was another thing I'd learned about her — she thought kindness was telling the truth at the right volume. "And now we have the journal naming him as financier, and we have Lund's account of the killings, written by a dying man in his own hand, and we have the bodies themselves, which the rangers can exhume and the courts can examine. Bullets stay in bones, Mara. Ice keeps them. This is no longer a story. It's evidence."

"And he knows we're coming."

"He's known since Skarnvik." She looked past me, up-glacier, toward the high white silence where the source waited — where whatever my grandfather had found, whatever was worth six lives and thirty-one years of lies, waited under the blue. "Torv's party will have found our cold camp by now. We have maybe half a day. Less, if they're good, and the big one is good."

"Then we take Lund's book and we go."

"We take Lund's book," Gideon agreed. But she didn't move. She was looking at my grandfather, at the folded hands, at the anger set into the frost-rimed brow, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into something I hadn't heard from her before — not the guide, not the fugitive. Something rawer. "Mara. Before we go. Is there anything you want to do? Anything you need? Because there won't be a second time. When the calving comes, this whole hollow goes, and everything in it."

I looked at my grandfather for a long moment. At the surveyor's harness. At the hands someone had folded with such terrible tenderness in the last hour of the world.

"He wanted the river mapped," I said. "Every river deserves to be known. That's what he used to say — my father says it, says it was his father's creed, the only thing that survived him." I wiped my face with the back of my glove and found the words coming steadier than I had any right to expect. "So we finish it. We go to the source, and we document it — photographs, samples, coordinates, everything — and we bring it down the mountain and we give it to everyone. To the rangers, the papers, the whole goddamn world. That's what we do for him. Not a grave. A map."

For the first time since I'd known her, Gideon Blackwood smiled like it cost her nothing.

"That," she said, "is the best plan I've heard all month."

We broke camp in eleven minutes. I took Lund's notebook inside my coat, against my chest, where it rode like a coal. And as we climbed out of the hollow, heading up-glacier into the narrowing blue throat of the ice, I looked back once at the little frozen village, at the seven shapes the mountain had kept safe all these years for exactly this purpose.

Behind us, far to the south, faint as a heartbeat, I heard it: the crack and boom of the glacier beginning to calve — days early.

The mountain was closing its hand. We had to move.
