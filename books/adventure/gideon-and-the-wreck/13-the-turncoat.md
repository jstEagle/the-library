## 13. The Turncoat

The third night on the ice, the wind died.

That was what woke me — not a sound but its absence. For two days the storm had screamed across the glacier tongue like something with teeth, and we'd learned to sleep inside that scream the way sailors learn to sleep inside a hull: not comfortably, but completely. Then it stopped, all at once, and the silence was so total I could hear my own blood moving in my ears.

I lay in the lee of a serac with my eyes open and listened to the glacier settle. Ice groans when it's cold and honest. It creaks, ticks, sighs through its fractures like an old house at night. But there was another sound underneath, faint and rhythmic, that didn't belong to the glacier at all.

Footsteps. One set. Unhurried.

I rolled Mara awake with a hand over her mouth before she could gasp, and by the time her eyes went wide and furious above my fingers, I'd already pressed the axe into them. *Quiet. Behind the ice. Now.*

She went. She was learning fast — faster than I liked, because everything she was learning out here she was learning for the worst reasons.

The footsteps came up the wind-scoured slope toward our camp, and they came alone, and they came without any attempt at stealth, which frightened me more than stealth would have. Vey's men moved like wolves when they hunted in a pack. A man who walked openly across open ground at night was either stupid or finished being afraid.

I waited behind the serac with my knife low and my weight on my back foot, and I watched him come into the thin blue light of the stars.

He was big. Not heavy — big the way load-bearing timber is big, built to carry things. He wore the same grey expedition shell as the men who'd chased us out of Skarnvik, but he'd shed most of his gear somewhere behind him, and he carried his rifle slung across his back instead of in his hands. He stopped ten paces from our dead fire, looked at the ash, and then said, quietly, to the dark:

"I know you're watching me, Blackwood."

My name in his mouth. Not *the guide*. Not *the woman*. My name.

"Come out where I can see you," he said, "or don't. But I'm going to put the rifle down either way, and after that I'd rather talk than freeze."

I didn't move. Neither did he. The glacier ticked beneath us, patient as a clock.

Then he shrugged the rifle off his shoulder, bent, laid it flat on the ice, and stepped back from it with his hands loose at his sides. He turned slowly, a full rotation, showing me the empty slope behind him, and when he faced us again he pulled off his balaclava and let the cold hit his face like a man who wanted to feel something.

"Torv," he said. "Eirik Torv. You'll have heard the name or you won't. I was one of the four Vey sent up the gorge after you." He paused. "I'm not anymore."

I came out from behind the serac with the knife visible and the axe in my other hand, because I am many things now but I have never been a fool. Mara emerged a moment later, lower down, flanking him without being asked. Good instincts. Terrible education.

"Talk," I said.

* * *

We didn't trust him near the gear. We made him sit against the serac's windward face while we rebuilt the fire between us — small, screened, the kind of flame you can kill with one boot — and I kept the rifle where I could see it and he kept his hands where I could see those. Mara watched him the way you watch a dog that's bitten someone: not with hate, exactly. With arithmetic.

"Four of us came up the gorge," Torv said. "Halvard, Brenner, the twins' cousin Oskar, and me. Vey's orders were simple enough that Halvard read them aloud twice, like scripture. Find the women. Recover the journal. Leave no witnesses." He said it flatly, the way you'd recite a shipping manifest. "I've done dirty work for that man eleven years. Escorting cargoes he didn't want inspected. Persuading fishermen who complained about his quotas. I told myself it was all the same trade — muscle for hire, and everyone knows what muscle is for."

"And?" I said.

"And then Oskar asked him, at the last camp below the icefall, what *leave no witnesses* meant when one of the witnesses was nineteen years old." Torv looked at the fire. His face was a slab of weathered granite, but something moved behind it, deep down, like a fish under ice. "And Halvard laughed and said the girl was salvage too."

Mara made a sound. Small. I put my hand on her arm without looking away from him.

"Oskar went back down this morning," Torv went on. "Said he'd tell Vey he lost us in the storm. Maybe he will. Maybe Halvard already radioed ahead about him too. It doesn't matter to me anymore which way that falls." He raised his eyes to mine, and they were pale, almost colourless, the eyes of a man who has spent his life squinting into weather. "I'm telling you this because I want you to understand I'm not here to trade. I'm not here to bargain. I've got nothing you want and I know it. I'm here because I did the arithmetic myself, three nights ago, lying in the snow listening to Halvard plan it, and the sum came out wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong in the way that doesn't wash out." He rubbed his jaw. "You spend eleven years telling yourself stories about the work, and then one night you hear a man laugh about a girl, and every story you ever told yourself comes apart at once. Like pulling one thread on a net." He shook his head slowly. "I helped build that net. I just never looked at what it caught."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off under the ice, the glacier boomed — a deep, tectonic note, like a struck bell, and I felt it come up through my boots and into my teeth. Torv glanced toward the sound and his mouth tightened.

"That's the second one tonight," he said. "You've noticed them getting closer together?"

"I've noticed."

"Then you know what I'm about to say before I say it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time he spoke quickly, urgently, a man unloading cargo before the deck goes under. "Vey chartered a helicopter out of Bodø. It's already south. He's coming himself — I heard him on the radio two nights ago, before the storm cut us off. He wasn't shouting. That's the thing I keep turning over. Eleven years, and I've only heard Aldous Vey raise his voice maybe four times, and this wasn't one of them. He was calm. He gave the pilot coordinates that aren't on any map I've ever seen, and he told him to bring cargo straps."

"Cargo straps," Mara repeated. Her voice was very even. "For what?"

"For whatever's at the source." Torv looked at her, and then at me, and his voice dropped. "I didn't know what was at the source until yesterday. None of us did. We were told it was about the journal — paper, leverage, a drowned man's handwriting. But Halvard got new orders relayed through the ranger station at Kvannhol before the storm closed the frequency. I stood close enough to hear the operator read them out. And the orders weren't about a journal anymore."

The wind had started again, faintly, a whisper of it moving along the ice cap, and it carried his words to me with terrible clarity.

"The source isn't a lake," Torv said. "It's a seam. A mineral seam running through the blue ice like a vein through marble — rare earths, the operator said. Neodymium, dysprosium, terbium. Names I had to write down phonetically because I'd never heard half of them. Enough of it, the operator said, to supply a fifth of the world's demand for twenty years." He paused. "Enough to be worth more than Skarnvik has earned since the town was founded. More than the fjord. More, maybe, than a nation's ransom. And Vey has known for thirty years."

The fire burned between us. Mara had gone very still beside me, the stillness of a person holding something fragile and deciding whether to set it down or throw it.

"He financed the expedition," I said slowly, working it forward the way you work a rope through a jammed pulley. "Thirty years ago. Your employer sent nine people up that river."

"Eight," Torv said. "Nine counting the surveyor who turned back at the gorge. Eight went under the glacier. My grandfather—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "Mara's grandfather went under the glacier. And the story the town has always been told — the legend, the warning, the *sea cave that mustn't be entered* — that story exists because Vey needed the route forgotten. Not feared. Forgotten. Fear fades. Maps get redrawn. But if a place becomes taboo — if children are raised knowing their grandfathers died there and God punished curiosity — nobody goes looking. Nobody surveys. Nobody files a claim." He spread his hands. "The legend is the lock. He built it himself. I've heard him do it — I've sat in the Seamen's Hall and listened to him tell the story of the doomed expedition, voice full of sorrow, buying drinks for the widows' families, and the whole time he knew exactly where they died and exactly why and exactly what they'd found before they died."

"How do you know that last part?" I asked. "That they found it?"

"Because of the orders." Torv's voice hardened. "The orders from Kvannhol said the seam's location was confirmed by samples recovered from the cave in Skarnvik. From your salvage, Blackwood. From the satchel." He nodded at my pack, sitting against the serac with the journal inside it, wrapped in oilcloth. "There were core samples in that bag. Vey's had them assayed within a day of you surfacing. That's why he offered triple the fee. That's why he searched your gear. That's why four armed men came up the gorge after two women and a dead man's notebook." He smiled, and it was the ugliest smile I've ever seen, because there was no humour anywhere in it. "It was never about the journal. The journal is just the confession. The mountain is the fortune."

* * *

Mara stood up. She walked ten paces away, to the edge of the firelight, and stood with her back to us looking north along the glacier's spine, where the ice rose in slow blue waves toward the col and, beyond it, the hidden throat of the source. I let her go. Some things a person has to measure standing up.

Torv watched her too. "Her grandfather," he said quietly. "Rurik. He was the expedition's boatman. I grew up on stories about him — every kid in Skarnvik grows up on stories about Rurik Ovesen, the man who rowed into the ice and never came out. They named a buoy after him." He shook his head. "Half the town thinks he was reckless. The other half thinks he was cursed. Nobody thinks he was murdered, because nobody's allowed to think it."

"And now they will be," I said.

"If anyone's left to tell them." He turned back to me, and the urgency came up in him again, banked but bright. "Listen to me. The helicopter changes everything. Vey isn't sending men up the river behind us anymore — why would he, when he can land on the ice above the source? He's going to fly over the calving fields, put down on the plateau, take what he wants, and seal the route behind him with the spring melt. If he gets there first, there's no evidence left to find. No camp. No bodies. No seam — because he'll have stripped it or buried it or bought the survey rights through three shell companies before the rangers finish their tea. Everything we've carried, everything we've bled for, becomes a story again. Another legend. Another lock."

"So we get there first."

"We get there first," he agreed. "But there's a problem, and you already know what it is, because you've been counting the booms same as me."

I had. All evening the glacier had been talking to itself — those deep bell-notes, closer together each time, the ice under tension the way a rope is under tension before it parts. Calving season came late in a normal year, weeks off still, the guides' almanacs all agreed. But nothing about this year was normal. The storm had loaded the icefields with snow; the meltwater was running early under the tongue; and the whole lower glacier was shifting on its foundations like a sleeper turning over.

"The tunnel closes early," I said. "Days, not weeks."

"Maybe less." Torv reached into his jacket — slowly, showing me his hand the whole way — and drew out a folded sheet of paper, soft with handling. "Which is why I brought you this."

I took it. A photocopy, grainy, of a survey chart — old, pre-digital, the linework drawn by hand. The glacier tongue, the gorge, the river's course marked in dashed ink disappearing *under* the ice and re-emerging beyond the col. And in the margin, in faded pencil, a notation I recognized from the journal's final pages: depth soundings, air-pocket intervals, a route. Rurik Ovesen's hand. The boatman's map.

"Where did you get this?"

"Halvard's kit. Vey gave him a copy years ago — insurance, in case the helicopter option ever opened up." Torv's mouth twisted. "Man keeps maps of his own crimes in his foreman's pack. That's the kind of careful he is." He nodded at the paper. "The pencil marks are the drowned expedition's. The soundings show air pockets in the tunnel — cathedral-sized ones, places where the river drops and the roof lifts. That's how they got through, and it's how you'll get through, if you go fast and the ice holds."

"And if the ice doesn't hold?"

"Then the mountain keeps its secret," Torv said simply, "and everyone in it."

Mara came back into the firelight. She'd heard all of it — I could see it in her face, in the red around her eyes and the set of her jaw, grief and fury fused into something harder than either alone. She looked at Torv for a long moment.

"You carried a rifle up this glacier to hunt us," she said.

"Yes."

"And you put it down."

"Yes."

"Why should I believe you won't pick it up again?"

Torv held her gaze without flinching. "Because I've spent eleven years picking it up," he said, "and I finally looked at what was on the other end of it. You can believe me or shoot me. Those are the options I brought. I'd prefer the first, but I'll take the second if it makes your arithmetic come out right."

Mara looked at me. And I looked at the fire, and thought about trust — about how you never really decide to give it, the way you never really decide to fall asleep. You just stop fighting it, one exhausted inch at a time, and hope the ground holds.

"Can you navigate the calving fields?" I asked him.

"Better than anyone alive. I've run cargo across that tongue for nine seasons."

"Then you walk in front," I said, "and you stay where I can see you, and the first hour you give me a reason to doubt you, the mountain gets your rifle and your coat and we get a head start. Understood?"

"Understood." Something loosened in his shoulders — not relief exactly. More like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Mara crouched and fed the fire the last of our dry wood, and the flames jumped, throwing our three shadows long across the blue ice. Far to the north, the glacier boomed again — deeper this time, longer, a note that went on and on until it stopped being sound and became a feeling in the chest, and the three of us sat and listened to it fade.

"Dawn in four hours," I said. "We move at first light. The tunnel, straight through, before the roof comes down or the helicopter lands." I looked at the boatman's map in my hands, at the dashed line running into the heart of the ice, and felt the old pull in my blood — the guide's pull, the current's pull, the thing that had gotten a client killed and might, if there was any justice left in the world, get the truth out alive. "One window. One chance. And if Vey's aircraft beats us to the plateau—"

"It won't," Mara said.

I looked at her. In the firelight her face was all edges and certainty, and for a moment she looked so much like the grandfather she'd never met — the man in the journal's pages, rowing into the dark with his lamp lit — that my breath caught.

"You don't know that," I said.

"No," she agreed. "But he doesn't know we're coming. And he doesn't know the ice is waking up." She nodded north, toward the booming dark. "Let's see whose luck runs out first."

Above us the stars wheeled, indifferent. Below us the glacier counted down.
