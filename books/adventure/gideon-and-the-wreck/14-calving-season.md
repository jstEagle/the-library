## 14. Calving Season

The mountain spoke first, before any of us saw it move.

We were three days out from the drowned camp, camped on a shelf of moraine above the glacier's tongue, when the sound came up through the ground and into our bones — a deep, grinding groan, like a door the size of a cathedral being forced open. Torv was on his feet before it finished, head cocked, listening the way other men listened for footsteps behind them.

"That's not wind," he said. "That's the ice letting go."

I had heard calving before, from a distance, the way you hear thunder from a neighboring valley — a crack, a boom, a white plume rising off the glacier face where it met the river. It was supposed to be weeks away yet. The old-timers in Skarnvik set their calendars by it. The calving season was the mountain's own law: the route upriver closed when the ice began to shed, and it stayed closed until the melt drew back in spring. Everyone knew that. Everyone planned around it.

But the glacier wasn't reading anyone's calendar.

The second crack came louder, closer, a sound like the sky tearing along a seam. Gideon scrambled up beside me on the ridge of rock, and we watched, in the grey half-light of the early morning, as a slab the size of a church sheared off the glacier's snout and fell into the river. The splash reached us a full ten seconds later — a white wall of spray that rose, hung, and collapsed back into water that had gone from green to churning brown in a breath.

The river surged. I watched it happen — watched the water climb the banks, swallow a shelf of gravel we'd crossed only the evening before, and keep climbing. Then it fell again, all at once, as if the mountain had inhaled and thought better of it.

"Days," Torv said quietly. "Not weeks. I was wrong. I told you we had a week, maybe more." He didn't look at either of us. He was still watching the glacier face, where new fractures were spreading like frost on glass. "I was wrong."

Gideon said nothing. She had that look she got — the one I'd learned to read on the river and in the ice tunnel and in every bad hour since Skarnvik — where her whole face went still and her eyes went to work. Measuring. Counting. Turning the world into a problem with a solution, because the alternative was to stand there and feel it.

"The tunnel," she said at last. "How long do we have before the entrance floods?"

Torv shook his head slowly. "You're not hearing me. It's not the tunnel. The tunnel will hold — the tunnel's been there ten thousand years. It's the river." He pointed down at the brown water, already running higher than it had at midnight. "Every piece of ice that falls raises the level. Every surge backs water up into the lower galleries. By tomorrow night the first chamber will be under a meter of water. By the day after, the current through the narrows will be something you can't swim, let alone raft."

"Then we go through tomorrow," Gideon said.

"Through the narrows," Torv said, "in the dark, with the ice groaning overhead, on a river that's rising by the hour. And if we're slow — if anything goes wrong down there — there's no coming back the way we went in. The water won't let us."

"Then we don't go wrong," Gideon said.

I almost laughed. That was her, all over. The whole mountain coming apart at the seams and she talked like she could argue with it. But I'd stopped mistaking that for arrogance somewhere back in the gorge, the night she'd held the raft line with her bare hands in freezing spray and never once raised her voice. It wasn't arrogance. It was the only way she knew how to stand up in the world — by refusing, out loud, to be small in front of it.

"And if we don't go through at all?" I asked.

Torv and Gideon both looked at me.

"If we turn back," I said. "If we take what we have — the journal, the photographs, the camp — and we go down to Skarnvik and give it to the rangers and let them come back next season with a proper expedition and proper equipment and none of us dead on the ice. Isn't that the sane thing?"

It wasn't that I wanted to turn back. It was that I wanted someone to say out loud why we couldn't — because I could feel the pull of it, the way you feel the pull of warm bedding on a cold morning. My father was dead. The journal was safe in its oilcloth. We had enough. Wasn't that enough?

Gideon looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide not to soften it.

"Because Vey has a helicopter," she said. "And because next season, the rangers come back, and Vey's lawyers come with them, and the survey permits come with the lawyers, and by the time anyone drills a core sample, the seam will belong to a company with a post office box and no name. Because the drowned camp melts out a little more every summer, and in ten years there'll be nothing left to find. Because we're the only ones who know where it is, and the only ones who know what was done to get it, and if we turn back now we were never more than two women running away with a dead man's book." She paused. "And because your father died for this, and I won't hand it to Vey by being careful."

There it was. She always found the way to say the thing that took the ground out from under your feet.

"And you?" I said to Torv. "You were one of his men a week ago. You want to tell me you're doing this for my father too?"

Torv took the question without flinching. He'd been doing that since the night he'd put down his rifle and told us what Vey had ordered him to do. "I'm doing it because I helped bury it once," he said. "And because when this is done, someone has to stand at the source and mark it, and swear to it, and I'm the only one of the three of us who can read ice well enough to survive up here alone." He looked back at the glacier. "But mostly I'm doing it because the ice is calving early, and I think I know why."

Gideon's head came around. "Why?"

"Vey's survey team," Torv said. "Two years ago. They ran seismic lines all over this tongue. Charges in the ice, dozens of them, mapping what's underneath." He nodded at the calving face. "You don't set that much explosive in a glacier and walk away clean. You weaken it. You crack the bones. I've been watching the fractures since the camp, and they're not natural patterns. He's been breaking this glacier's back for two years, and now it's coming apart under all of us." He spat, deliberately, down the slope. "He didn't just kill them. He's killing the mountain that hides them."

* * *

We spent the rest of that day doing nothing but preparing, and it was the longest day of my life.

Torv went over the route with us twice, drawing in the snow with a ski pole: the tunnel entrance, a black slot in the ice wall two kilometers upriver, hidden behind a serac the size of a house; the first gallery, wide and high, where the drowned expedition had cached their boats; then the narrows, a kilometer of river compressed between walls of ice so close you could touch both at once; then the long climb, where the river dropped away beneath the glacier and the tunnel rose toward the source in a series of icefalls that the old expedition had roped.

"The narrows are the kill zone," Torv said, tapping the pole in the snow. "Rising water, no eddies, no places to hold. If the river's running at more than knee-height in there, we don't go. We wait, and we probably die waiting. If it's below knee-height at first light, we move, and we don't stop for anything. Not for dropped gear. Not for each other." He said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you'd read a weather report. "I've seen what a surge does in a confined tunnel. It doesn't drown you. It compresses you. You'll hear it coming before you feel it — a sound like a freight train in a pipe. When you hear it, you get high, and you hold, and you pray the gallery roof is solid where you're standing."

"And if it isn't?" I asked.

"Then the mountain decides," Torv said. "It's been deciding for ten thousand years. We're just asking it a question."

Gideon spent the afternoon at the raft. We'd salvaged it — patched, re-lashed twice, more cork than boat by now — from the drowned expedition's cache, and she went over every seam with a candle, hunting pinholes, re-caulking with pine pitch she'd been carrying since Skarnvik like a miser's coin. I watched her work and thought about how strange it was that I knew her hands better than her face. In the dark of the ice tunnel I'd followed those hands by touch and rope and sound. I knew the burn scar on the left thumb, the split in the right palm that wouldn't heal in the cold. I knew that when she was afraid, her hands got slower and more precise, as if fear were something she could stitch shut.

"You should sleep," she said, without looking up.

"So should you."

"After you."

I lay down in the shelter of the rocks and watched the light go out of the sky, and I listened to the glacier. All night it talked. Not the deep slow groans of a living mountain, but sharper sounds — cracks, pops, the whistle of settling fractures — a language I didn't know and didn't want to learn. Once, near midnight, the whole slope shuddered, and somewhere upvalley a serac the size of a ship came down with a roar that went on and on, and the echo came back off the far wall like the mountain answering itself.

I thought about my father's hands. He'd been a salvager his whole life, a man who made his living off what the water gave back, and he'd died because he finally refused to give something back. I thought about the last thing he said to me — *mind the tide, Mara, the tide always tells the truth* — and how he'd been wrong about that, in the end, because the only tide that mattered was the one Aldous Vey had been running under Skarnvik for thirty years.

And I thought about Gideon, twenty feet away, awake, watching the ice, and how I'd come to measure the safety of any hour by whether she was in it.

* * *

First light came up grey and windless, which Torv said was the worst kind of light — warm enough to start the melt, still enough to let the ice hear its own cracks.

We reached the tunnel entrance by mid-morning. The black slot in the ice wall was exactly where he'd said, and it was already weeping — a sheet of meltwater spilling down the ice below it, feeding the river, which had climbed another hand's width overnight and was running the color of wet cement.

Torv stood at the entrance and listened. That was his ritual, and I'd stopped thinking it strange: a man who'd spent years hunting people for Aldous Vey, standing in the mouth of a glacier with his eyes shut, listening to the mountain like a priest taking confession.

"Water's at the mark," he said finally. "Knee-height, maybe a little under. We go now, or we don't go."

We went.

The first gallery was as he'd promised — vast, vaulted, blue-dark, the river running through it in a broad slow flood that echoed like a drum. The drowned expedition's cache was gone, taken or crushed, but their soot marks were still on the walls, handprints and torch-smears, and I kept my eyes off them because looking at them too long was like looking at my grandfather's body in the frozen camp: it made the dead too present, too close, too much like people who had stood exactly where I was standing and believed, for a while, that they'd get out.

The narrows began an hour in, and I understood then why Torv had called it the kill zone.

The walls closed to the width of a coffin. The river, compressed, went from a flood to a torrent — white water, loud as the world ending, running fast and deep between walls that dripped and groaned. We ran the raft through it in relays, Gideon on the bow line, Torv on the stern, me on the middle rope, and the craft went through the worst of it like a leaf in a spout — slammed against one wall, spun, slammed against the other, while the spray came off the water so hard it was more like drowning than rafting. I remember Gideon's voice through it, not loud, never loud, counting the rhythm of the rapids the way she counted everything: *two — brace — three — pull —* and my hands doing what her voice told them because my own judgment had drowned somewhere back in the first chute.

We made the far side of the narrows by early afternoon, and I have never in my life been so glad to stand on bad ice.

That was when the mountain asked its question back.

The sound came up the tunnel from behind us — from the direction of the entrance, from the river's mouth — and it was exactly what Torv had said it would be: a freight train in a pipe. A surge. The glacier had calved again, upvalley, and the river had swallowed the fall, and the water was coming down the tunnel like a fist through a sleeve.

"High ground!" Torv roared, and there was no high ground, there was only a shelf of ice two meters above the waterline, and we went for it — all three of us, gear and raft and all, hauling the boat up by main force while the sound grew and grew, and then the water came.

It came like the hand of God. The river rose two meters in under a minute — I watched the mark on the wall where the water had been at knee-height disappear, watched the flood climb past my boots, past my knees, past my waist before it slowed, and the whole tunnel filled with a roar that made speech impossible, made thought impossible, made everything impossible except holding on to the ice shelf and to each other and to the raft, which tried twice to tear free and was held only because Gideon had lashed it to a spur of ice with a knot I would later learn had a name and a history and had saved better expeditions than ours.

The surge lasted four minutes. It felt like four years.

When the water fell back, it fell fast, hissing and sucking, and the tunnel went quiet in that ringing, ear-damaged way, and the three of us stood on the shelf in the blue dark, soaked to the skin, alive, and nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Torv started to laugh. It was a terrible sound, a man laughing at his own funeral, and it went on until Gideon put her hand on his arm and he stopped.

"The entrance is gone," he said, when he could speak. "You understand that. That surge was the entrance closing. There's no going back the way we came. It's the source or nothing now."

I looked back down the tunnel, into the dark where the water had been, and I felt — not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. Relief, maybe. The strange, shameful relief of a door closing behind you, of the choice finally being taken out of your hands.

Gideon was already re-rigging the raft. Her hands were shaking — I saw it, the fine tremor she'd never let me name — but her voice was level as a spirit line.

"One more icefall," she said. "Then the source. Keep moving. We're not done, and neither is Vey."

And far above us, muffled by a hundred meters of ice, the mountain groaned again — long, deep, and getting ready.
