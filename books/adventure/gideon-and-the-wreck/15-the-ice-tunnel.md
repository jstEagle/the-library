## 15. The Ice Tunnel

The river went into the glacier at a place the old maps called the Maw, and the journal called it nothing at all — just a smudged ink line where the cartographer's hand had shaken.

Gideon stood at its edge and listened.

That was the first lesson of moving water under stone: you didn't look, you listened. Sight was a liar in the dark. Sound was honest. The river spoke in dialects — the flat hiss of shallow sheet flow, the hollow boom of water dropping into a pothole, the thin whistle of air being dragged through a gap too small for anything but breath. She had learned them twenty years ago on rivers in three countries, and she had unlearned them for one bad season and one dead client, and now, standing at the mouth of a tunnel bored through four hundred meters of living ice, she found they came back to her like a language spoken in childhood.

"It's breathing," Mara said beside her. She had her head tilted, her braid stiff with frost, her father's canvas satchel strapped across her chest like armor. "You can hear it."

"Glaciers breathe," Gideon said. "Pressure changes with the temperature. Air moves through the tunnels at night when the surface freezes hard." She checked her watch against the grey light leaking over the icefall behind them. "Which means we go now, while it's exhaling. At dawn the whole system inhales, and if we're still inside when it does—"

"Don't finish that."

Gideon almost smiled. "There's a chamber about two kilometers in. The journal calls it the Vestry. High ground, dry floor, big enough for three. If the tunnel's shape hasn't changed since 1911."

"And if it has?"

"Then we find out what changed and adapt." She said it lightly, and knew Mara heard the lie underneath. Glaciers did not keep faith with old maps. A tunnel that had held a roof for a century could close like a fist in a single warm week, and this had been the warmest calving season anyone in Skarnvik could remember. Torv's parting words were still lodged in her chest like a splinter: *Three days, maybe four, before the meltwater backs up and the low route drowns. After that, nobody goes in or out until next summer.*

They had spent one of those days already, waiting out the storm in the snow cave, trading their worst nights like currency. They had spent most of another descending the icefall with Torv's rope and Gideon's knots, Mara learning to trust the ice the way you learn to trust a stranger who hasn't hurt you yet. That left two. Maybe less.

She looked at the Maw.

It was not an arch so much as a wound — a ragged oval where the subglacial river surfaced briefly from whatever dark plumbing carried it, foamed white and furious for thirty meters, then dove under the ice again as though ashamed of the light. The walls around the entrance were scalloped and blue, carved smooth by ten thousand summers of water finding its level. Old iron rings were set into the rock above the entrance, rusted to lace. The drowned expedition had anchored there. Somebody had gone down those rings with a lantern and never come back up them.

Mara touched one of the rings. Her glove came away orange with rust. "My grandfather's hands touched this," she said quietly. "Maybe."

"Maybe." Gideon wouldn't give her false comfort; Mara had made her promise that in the snow cave, in the dark, with the storm screaming outside — *don't soften it, I've had enough softening* — but she put her hand over Mara's anyway, brief and cold. "We'll know more at the other end."

"The other end being the source."

"The other end being daylight." She clipped into the anchor line, tested it with her full weight, then twice her weight, bouncing on her heels until the old iron groaned but held. "Rope protocol. Thirty meters between us, no closer — if the ceiling comes down I want one of us to survive to cut the other loose. You call every hundred meters, loud, even if it's just my name. If I stop answering, you retreat to the last solid anchor and you do *not* come back for me. Swear it."

"I don't swear things I intend to break."

"Mara."

"Gideon." Mara's eyes were steady in a way that frightened her more than the tunnel did. "I watched my father get taken because he finally refused to do the safe thing. I'm not doing the safe thing again. Lead."

So Gideon led.

* * *

The first kilometer was a cathedral and she hated it.

That was the thing nobody told you about ice tunnels: they were beautiful the way avalanches were beautiful, beauty deployed as a distraction from the killing mechanics underneath. The walls rose in fluted columns, backlit by nothing, glowing faintly with their own compressed blue — ice so old and so dense it had squeezed every air bubble out of itself and turned into something closer to glass than frozen water. Overhead, the ceiling arched forty meters up in places, ribbed like the vaulting of some drowned church, and meltwater ran down the columns in threads that caught their headlamps and scattered them into galaxies.

Gideon gave it exactly none of her attention.

She gave it all to her ears. Left boot, right boot, listening between steps. The river ran along the right-hand wall here, deep and quiet, a black ribbon sliding over stones it had been polishing since before Skarnvik existed. Its voice was low and even. Good. An even river meant an even gradient, which meant the tunnel floor wasn't about to drop out from under them into a shaft. Where the water got louder without getting faster, there was a drop. Where it got quieter, there was a siphon — a section where the river pressed against the ceiling and sealed the passage, and any caver who entered a siphon became a statistic.

"Hundred," Mara called behind her, right on schedule.

Gideon raised a fist, stopped, listened. Ahead, the tunnel bent left around a buttress of ice, and past the bend the river's voice changed — dropped half a tone, gained a rattle. Stones moving. Water picking up speed.

She edged around the bend with her lamp shielded low and found the first problem.

The floor dropped. Not catastrophically — six meters, maybe eight — but the drop was polished vertical ice, and at its base the river spread wide and shallow over a gravel bar before narrowing again into what sounded like a run of small rapids. Someone had been here before them. Two pitons, modern, gleaming, were hammered into the wall above the drop with a faded sling threaded through them. Vey's survey team, probably. Or the helicopter crew scouting the route before Vey decided to come personally.

Or — her stomach tightened — the drowned expedition. No. The pitons were steel, not iron. Recent. Within the year.

"Drop ahead," she called back. "Eight meters. There's gear in place but I don't trust it. We're setting our own."

She built the anchor herself, because this was the part of the work that lived in her hands independent of fear or memory: two ice screws driven at opposing angles into blue ice, load-tested, backed up with a loop of cord around a rock horn protruding through the tunnel wall where the glacier had scraped bedrock bare. She tied a butterfly knot at the eight-meter mark for Mara to clip, double-checked the friction hitch on her own descender, and went over the edge into the dark.

The rope hummed. The ice streamed past her face, cold enough to burn exposed skin. At the bottom she landed on the gravel bar, boots crunching, and swept her lamp across the rapids downstream — and froze.

Bones.

Not human. A ptarmigan, maybe, or an Arctic fox, bleached white and picked clean, wedged among the stones. It had come in with a flood pulse and starved in the dark. She looked away from it deliberately, professionally, the way you looked away from a body on a highway — and then made herself look back, because looking away twice would be worse later.

"Coming down," Mara called, and descended in a controlled slide, boots against the wall, rope singing through her descender. She landed light. She'd gotten good at this in nine days, faster than any client Gideon had ever taught, and Gideon understood why: Mara learned survival skills the way drowning people learned to swim.

"Bones," Mara said, seeing them. Her jaw tightened. "Animal?"

"Animal."

"Okay." She exhaled. "Okay."

They moved.

* * *

The trouble started at the fifteen-hundred-meter mark, and it started with silence.

Gideon stopped so abruptly that Mara nearly walked into her back. "Quiet," she breathed, and killed her headlamp.

Darkness total. The kind of dark that had weight, that pressed on the eyeballs. In it, the river's voice filled the world — and Gideon listened to it the way a doctor listens to a heart, hunting the arrhythmia.

There. Underneath the water-sound: a ticking. Faint, irregular, somewhere above and ahead. Like knuckles cracking. Like a house settling.

Ice under stress.

She turned her lamp back on, hooded it with her palm, and played the beam upward along the tunnel wall — and found the crack. It ran diagonally across the ceiling for twenty meters, a black seam finer than a hair, invisible unless the light grazed it at a shallow angle. Fresh. The edges were clean, not weathered. And below it, a wedge of the ceiling had already begun to sag, a lens-shaped slab the size of a truck hood, hanging by cohesion alone.

"How long has it been like that?" Mara whispered.

"Since about five minutes ago." Gideon kept her voice flat and low. Sound traveled strangely in tunnels; panic traveled fastest of all. "The storm warmed the surface. The heat's migrating down. This section is adjusting." She pointed to the far side of the passage, where the wall met the floor in a shadowed angle beneath an overhang. "We cross under it fast, single file, no talking, no stopping. If it lets go while we're under it, dive for the wall and cover your neck."

"And if it lets go while we're *not* under it?"

"Then we count ourselves lucky and take a different route home." She clipped her lamp to her chest strap, freeing both hands, and moved.

Ten steps. Fifteen. The sagging slab hung overhead like a held breath. Gideon counted her footfalls the way she'd once counted strokes for clients on Class V water — rhythm as prayer, rhythm as control. Twenty. Twenty-five. The ticking overhead grew louder, faster, ice fibers failing one by one somewhere in the dark above the slab—

Thirty. Clear.

She turned, waved Mara through, and watched her come — small and quick and terrified and absolutely steady, eyes up, feet placing themselves with a precision she hadn't had a week ago. Mara passed under the slab, reached the clear wall, and leaned against it, shaking, saying nothing, which was its own kind of eloquence.

Then the glacier exhaled.

It came as a sound first — a vast, subterranean groan rolling through the ice from somewhere miles upstream, the pitch of it falling, falling, like a ship's hull twisting — and then the air moved. A wind rose out of the depths of the tunnel, warm and wet and smelling of stone and ancient trapped air, blowing *against* their direction of travel, hard enough to stagger. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Somewhere behind them, deep in the dark they'd crossed, came a crack like a rifle shot, then a heavy muffled boom, then the particular rushing silence of several tons of ice arriving at rest.

Neither of them moved for a full minute.

"That was the inhale starting," Gideon said finally. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, scraped thin. "Early. The storm accelerated everything."

"Then the slab—"

"Is someone else's problem now." But she marked it on the map in her head, the way she marked everything: *sagging slab, fifteen hundred meters, do not route return traffic under it.* Because there would be a return. She was done planning trips with no way back. That arrogance had cost a client her life, and Gideon Blackwood did not make the same mistake twice; she made new ones, carefully rationed.

They pushed on. The Vestry, according to the journal, lay two kilometers in. Behind schedule, with the glacier's clock running fast.

* * *

They found the drowned expedition's marks at the entrance to the Vestry, and Gideon had to hold Mara upright for a moment.

Carved into the ice wall beside the chamber mouth, protected by a century of sublimation-free cold, were initials. Dozens of them. *E.H. 1911. R.M. 1911.* And lower, smaller, in a hand that shook: *T.O. — forgive me.*

"Tomas Oyelaran," Mara said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "That's him. That's my grandfather's handwriting." She touched the letters with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "He was scared. He was scared and he wrote it anyway."

"He was honest," Gideon said. "That's rarer."

The Vestry itself was everything the journal promised — a high dry chamber off the main tunnel, floored with gravel, big enough for three and their ghosts. The ghosts were literal. Against the far wall lay the expedition's leavings: a rusted Primus stove, a coil of hemp rope gone to brown powder, a tin plate, a leather boot with the laces still tied. And stacked neatly, weighted under a stone, a bundle of oilcloth that Gideon unwrapped with hands that wanted to shake and didn't.

Inside: survey notes. Mineral assays. A brass theodolite lens cap. And a letter, sealed with wax, addressed in careful script: *To whoever finds this — we were lied to.*

Mara read the first line aloud and couldn't continue. Gideon read the rest silently, by lamplight, while the glacier ticked and settled around them, and what she read confirmed everything the journal had accused Vey of, in the dead man's own hand, with dates and figures and the names of the men who'd fired the shots.

"We have it," Mara whispered. "This is it. This is everything."

"This is half of it." Gideon folded the letter into the waterproof satchel, beside the journal, beside the assay samples Torv had described. "The other half is the source itself. Photographs. Coordinates. Proof no lawyer can bury." She looked toward the chamber's far side, where the tunnel narrowed and the river's voice changed again — deeper now, older, funneling down toward the heart of the ice. Toward the glowing seam the journal described. Toward the place Vey's helicopter would be landing within the day.

She checked her watch. Checked it again, because the first number seemed impossible.

Dawn was coming. The glacier would inhale. And somewhere above them, rotors were already turning.

"Rest's over," Gideon said, standing, shouldering the satchel, feeling — for the first time since Skarnvik, since the scandal, since the river that had taken a client and given her back a ruin — exactly like what she was. A guide. On route. With people to bring through alive.

"Two hours to the source," she said. "Stay behind me. Listen for everything."

Ahead, the tunnel bent into darkness, and the river sang.
