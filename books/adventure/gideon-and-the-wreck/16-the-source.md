## 16. The Source

The tunnel changed its voice in the last hour.

Gideon had learned to read it the way a sailor reads weather — the tick of settling ice, the long groan of the glacier flexing against its own weight, the thin whistle of air moving through cracks too narrow to see. For three days inside the mountain of ice, that voice had been their only companion, and she had learned every word of it. Now it went quiet. Not silent — never silent — but hushed, the way a cathedral goes quiet when you step through the door.

She stopped and held up her fist. Behind her, Mara halted without a sound. Three days had taught her that too.

Ahead, the tunnel opened like a throat.

The headlamp beams reached into it and came back changed — not the flat gray return of ice but something deeper, bluer, light that seemed to have traveled farther to get home. Gideon killed her lamp and stood in the dark, listening. Water moved somewhere below them, patient as a pulse. The cold was different here. Older.

"Come on," she said, and her voice came back to her from all directions at once.

They walked out of the tunnel and into the source.

* * *

Gideon had stood on summits. She had hung from ropes over crevasses deep enough to swallow a church, had watched auroras pour across the sky like spilled light, had once seen a calving face drop a hundred thousand tons of ice into the sea with a sound like God clearing his throat. She had thought she knew what awe was. She had thought it was a big thing, a loud thing.

This was neither.

The chamber was vast and utterly still. The ice overhead arched away into darkness, thick enough to hold centuries, and it was blue — not the white-blue of surface ice but a blue with weight to it, the blue of deep water, of twilight an hour after sunset, of the space between stars. Their lamps barely mattered. The ice itself gave off light, or seemed to, some faint luminescence trapped in it like breath held for ten thousand years.

And beneath their feet, running through the floor of the chamber like veins through marble, the seam.

It glowed. Not brightly — that was the terrible beauty of it. A soft mineral phosphorescence, green-white and silver, branching and rebranching through the ice in patterns that looked deliberate, looked written, a script no one alive could read. Where the meltwater had cut channels through it, the glow pooled and brightened, so that the whole floor of the cathedral seemed lit from below by drowned lanterns.

Mara made a small sound. Not a word. Gideon looked over and saw her standing with her bare hand pressed flat against an ice column, her head tipped back, tears freezing on her lashes, and she wasn't wiping them away because she'd forgotten they were there.

"My grandfather walked here," Mara said. "He actually stood here."

"He did." Gideon's voice came out rougher than she intended. "And then he walked out to tell people about it."

The words landed between them and stayed. They both knew what had happened to the ones who walked out.

Gideon forced herself to do the work. That was the discipline that had kept her alive for twenty years in the mountains: wonder was a luxury you paid for afterward, and the bill always came due. She circled the chamber's edge, checking the ice, reading the architecture. The dome was sound — old ice, dense, compressed until it forgot how to be snow. But along the eastern wall, where the seam ran brightest, the meltwater had been busy. Channels, fresh-cut, still weeping. And above them, hairline fractures spreading like frost ferns.

The glacier was dying faster than it should. Calving season had come early, and this whole cathedral sat on the edge of the tongue.

"Torv's window," she said quietly. "We're inside it. Barely."

"Then let's find it," Mara said. "Before the window closes."

They found it forty minutes later, and Gideon would remember for the rest of her life the exact moment she stopped being able to pretend it was anything other than what it was.

* * *

It was at the far end of the chamber, where the seam plunged down into a crevasse-like cleft and the glow rose out of it strong enough to cast shadows. Someone had rigged ropes there once — decades ago, the fibers fossilized into the ice, but the anchors were cut clean, deliberately, from above. The drowned expedition had come down here. And someone had made sure they couldn't leave the same way.

Mara went first, down the cleft on the modern rope they'd brought, and Gideon followed, and at the bottom the seam opened into a pocket the size of a chapel, and the ore was naked in the walls.

Not a vein. A body of it. Crystalline structures the size of a man's fist, clustered like grapes, glowing with that same soft green-silver light, so that the little chamber needed no lamp at all. Gideon had seen photographs, once, in a geologist's tent years ago — rare-earth minerals under UV, the strange gardens they grew. This was that, except nothing had planted it. The mountain had. Alone, in the dark, over ages.

"There must be tons of it," Mara whispered. Her breath fogged the glow. "Gideon. There must be *tons*."

"I know what it is." Gideon turned slowly, taking it in, doing the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Rare earths. The metals that lived inside every phone, every battery, every motor in the modern world — metals that nations went to war over, that governments hoarded, that men like Aldous Vey built empires on. A deposit like this, exposed by a retreating glacier, on land everyone had agreed for a century meant nothing—

No. Not agreed. *Told.* Told it meant nothing. Told the cave was cursed, the river forbidden, the source a legend. A town raised on a story designed to keep them from ever looking upriver.

"It was never superstition," Gideon said. "The legend. It was a fence. Vey's grandfather — or whoever started it — they built Skarnvik a wall of ghost stories and put the treasure behind it. And when Halvard Vey's expedition found the door—"

"They cut them loose." Mara's voice had gone flat and hard. "My grandfather found this, and Aldous Vey had him murdered for it, and then he told my town my own family died of greed and bad luck."

The glow lit her face from below, and Gideon saw something there she recognized from mirrors: grief that had finished becoming anger, which is the most dangerous substance a person can carry into a place like this.

"We take samples," Gideon said. "Photographs. Coordinates. The journal pages, the execution camp evidence, this. Enough to bury him in court, not enough to make us rich enough to be worth killing twice. Then we go."

"You think he'll let us go."

"I think we're going to find out."

And that was when they heard the helicopter.

* * *

It came as a vibration first, felt through the ice before heard — a tremor in the soles of their boots, wrong, mechanical, a heartbeat that didn't belong. Then the sound arrived, muffled and monstrous, echoing down through however many meters of glacier lay above them, and Gideon's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

There was no landing zone up here. No shelf, no plateau. Which meant—

"The sinkhole," Mara breathed. "The collapse basin, two kilometers back. Torv said it was wide enough."

Vey hadn't sent men. Vey had chartered a machine that could cross the icefield in an hour what had taken them nine days, and he had come himself, because a man like Aldous Vey did not delegate the burial of things this valuable. He had waited out the storm season, tracked them through Torv's defection, and flown straight up the river route while they crawled through it on foot.

Or worse — he'd known the route all along. It had been in the journal. He'd financed the first expedition; he'd have memorized every page.

Gideon killed their lamps. In the dark, lit only by the seam's glow, she grabbed Mara's arm. "Options."

"Hide and hope he leaves?"

"He won't leave. He'll seal it. Explosives, a 'natural' calving event, whatever story plays best in the papers. We're standing in the grave he dug for six men fifty years ago, and he thinks it holds two more."

"So we run for the tunnel—"

"And meet his men coming down it. Or stay and be found." Gideon closed her eyes for one second, two, and did what she had trained herself to do her whole life: she stopped thinking like a fugitive and started thinking like a guide. Terrain. Light. Time. "How many can a helicopter carry? Pilot, plus six, maybe seven. He'll keep two topside with the machine. Four come down. Maybe five."

The sound above them swelled, circled, began to die — touching down.

"Gideon." Mara's hand found hers in the dark. Her grip was ice-cold and steady. "Whatever happens — I need you to know I don't regret one step of this."

"Don't say goodbye."

"I'm not saying goodbye. I'm saying it was worth it."

Above them, boots on ice. Voices, faint, distorted by the dome — and one voice cutting through the others, smooth and unhurried, a voice that had never once needed to raise itself to be obeyed.

Aldous Vey had come down into his cathedral.

Gideon drew Mara back into the shadow of the cleft, behind a buttress of ancient ice, and watched the lights appear at the chamber's far end — four beams swinging, and among them, moving slowly, almost reverently, the silhouette of a broad-shouldered man who stopped at the center of the glowing floor and looked up at the blue vault overhead as if he were inspecting property.

Which, Gideon understood with a clarity that felt like falling, was exactly what he was doing.

He had owned this secret for fifty years. He had paid for it in blood and kept the receipts. And now, with the glacier calving early and the evidence walking around loose inside his vault, he had come to close the account personally.

Vey said something to his men — too low to catch — and one of them laughed, and the sound echoed through the cathedral of ice like a crack through glass.

Gideon pressed back into the dark, Mara's hand locked in hers, and counted the lights.
