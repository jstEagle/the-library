## 17. Bargain at the Source

I heard the helicopter before I saw it — a sound like the mountain tearing its own throat, rolling down the ice cathedral and coming back changed.

We had maybe two minutes of warning. Gideon used them well. She killed our headlamp, pulled me behind a fin of blue ice that rose from the tunnel floor like the rib of something enormous, and pressed her mouth to my ear.

"Stay low. Stay quiet. If it goes wrong, you run back down the tunnel and don't stop for anything. Not for me."

"Not for you," I repeated, because arguing would have cost us both.

The seam of ore ran beneath our feet like a vein of buried lightning — a ribbon of pale fire frozen in the glacier's heart, throwing light upward through forty feet of ancient ice so that the whole chamber glowed from below, as if we stood inside a lantern. The drowned expedition's journal had called it *the lamp under the world*. My grandfather had died within sight of it, or close enough that his bones might have heard this same sound: rotors, engines, men arriving where no men should be.

The helicopter set down on the shelf of bare ice at the chamber's eastern edge — Vey must have known about the shelf, must have studied the same surveys he'd tried to buy out of existence — and the rotor wash came through the cavern in a white scream, blasting powder snow into spirals that caught the underlight and burned like sparks.

Three men climbed out. Then a fourth, smaller in the frame but unmistakable even at distance, even through snow-haze: Aldous Vey, in a parka too clean for the mountains, walking across my town's stolen ground like a man crossing his own parlor.

Gideon's hand found mine in the dark. Her fingers were cold as the ice around us, but her grip was steady.

"Torv said he'd come himself if the deposit proved real," she breathed. "He came."

"He always comes when there's something to own."

* * *

They didn't search for us. That was the first strange thing. The three hired men fanned out along the chamber's edge with survey instruments — tripods, laser levels, a core drill already being unpacked from a hard case — while Vey stood at the center of the glowing floor with his hands clasped behind his back, looking up at the vaulted ice the way other men look up in churches.

Then he spoke, without turning, in a voice pitched to carry.

"Miss Blackwood. Miss Oyelaran. You may as well come out. The acoustics in here are remarkable — I could hear your breathing from the landing shelf."

Gideon went very still beside me. I felt her weigh it — the tunnel at her back, the crevasse fields beyond, all the ways a desperate woman might still run — and then I felt her choose otherwise. She stood, and pulled me up with her, and walked out of the shadow of the ice fin with her chin level and her hands loose at her sides, the way she'd walked into Skarnvik three weeks ago: broke, blacklisted, and refusing to look it.

"Mr. Vey." Her voice echoed off the dome. "You're a long way from your office."

"I go where my investments are." He turned at last. Up close, in the underlight, his face looked older than it did in Skarnvik — the sea-light of the fjord flattered him, and this buried sun did not. His eyes found the satchel at Gideon's hip and stayed there a half-second too long before returning to her face. "You have something of mine."

"The journal isn't yours." I hadn't meant to speak. Gideon's hand brushed my arm — not a warning, exactly. A steadying. "It belonged to the men you sent to die."

Vey regarded me with something almost like patience. "Mara Oyelaran. Your father was a good man. Stubborn, but good. I'm sorry for your loss." He said it the way he'd say *sorry for the weather*. "Though I'd caution you against repeating accusations you can't support. In a court of law, grief is not evidence."

"In a court of law, neither is a helicopter full of survey equipment on forbidden land."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Calculation. He filed me away and turned back to Gideon, and everything in his manner changed. Warmed. Softened. I watched him do it, watched the blade slide back into its velvet, and I understood suddenly why half of Skarnvik loved him and the other half simply obeyed.

"Miss Blackwood. Gideon, if I may." He took a step closer, boots crunching on ice that had never been walked on by honest feet. "Do you know what I see when I look at you? I see a woman who was the best guide on the coast before she was thirty. I've read the old reports — the Kessel traverse, the winter ascent of the Horn, clients who wrote letters saying you'd pulled them out of situations no one else could have survived. And I know what happened after. I know what they took from you." He paused, letting the word *took* do its work. "And I know — because I make it my business to know things — that the accident wasn't yours. The inquiry was sloppy. The family wanted someone to blame, and the association wanted the insurance settled, and you were convenient. You were ruined by other people's cowardice."

Beside him, one of the hired men shifted his weight, and I saw the rifle slung across his back and understood how thin the velvet really was.

"So here is what I'm prepared to do." Vey reached inside his coat and produced a slim leather folder, holding it out flat on his palm like a card player showing his hand. "Full exoneration. Not a pardon — an exoneration. The inquiry reopened, the record corrected, the blacklist struck. Your certification restored. Letters of reference from men whose names open doors from here to Chamonix. Compensation for six lost years, at whatever figure your lawyer sets." He let the folder sit there in the cold light. "Your name, Miss Blackwood. Clean. Whole. Everything they stole from you, returned. All you have to do is give me the journal, walk away, and forget what you've seen here. Forget this room. Forget the river. Forget Skarnvik ever hired you."

* * *

I want to say I knew instantly what she'd do. I want to say I read her face and saw the answer written there, plain as the ore-vein under our feet.

But the truth is worse, and truer: I watched her hesitate.

Not long. Maybe four seconds. Maybe five. But I had spent enough nights beside her — in the gorge, in the snow cave, in the ice tunnel with her voice counting rope-lengths in the dark — to know every weather of her face, and what crossed it in those five seconds was not calculation. It was hunger. It was a woman looking at the one thing she had told me she'd stopped believing in, held out on an open palm, close enough to take.

Her name clean. Six years given back. The client she couldn't save — the inquiry that had gutted her — undone, corrected, forgiven. I thought of what she'd told me in the snow cave, her voice low against the storm: *I didn't kill him, Mara. But I couldn't prove it, and after a while I stopped trying, and after a while after that I stopped believing I deserved to try.* And here was proof, packaged and offered, redemption gift-wrapped by the very machine that had buried my grandfather under forty feet of ice.

She looked at the folder. She looked at Vey. And for five seconds, she was somewhere I couldn't follow.

Then her eyes came to me.

That's what saved her, or damned her, depending on which of us you ask — though I've only ever been able to ask myself. Her eyes came to me, standing in the glow of the thing my grandfather died reaching for, and whatever she saw in my face made the hunger gutter and die.

"No," Gideon said.

Vey didn't move. "Take the night to consider—"

"There's nothing to consider." She unslung the satchel from her hip — and for one lurching moment I thought she was handing it over — but she only held it up between them, turning it slowly, so the waterproof canvas caught the underlight. "You financed Halvard Oyelaran's expedition. When they found this—" she nodded at the burning seam beneath us, "—you cut their supply line and left them to the ice, because dead men don't file claims. Eleven people, Mr. Vey. One of them was her grandfather." She tipped her chin toward me without taking her eyes off him. "The journal names you. The camp upstream has your supply crates, marked with your shipping code. Torv will testify — he's already marked the site for the rangers. Killing us doesn't help you anymore. The truth got out of this mountain days ago."

Something in Vey's composure finally cracked — not much, just a hairline, just enough to show the pressure underneath. "Torv," he said softly. "I wondered which way he'd break."

"You should wonder about yourself," I said. "You're standing on the evidence."

For a moment nobody moved. The drill crew had gone still among their instruments; the armed man's hand had drifted toward his sling; the whole chamber seemed to hold its breath along with us, the light pulsing faintly in the ice as if the glacier itself were listening.

And then Aldous Vey smiled, and folded the leather folder closed, and put it back inside his coat with the unhurried care of a man who has never once needed to hurry.

"Well," he said. "I did try courtesy."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I remember it in pieces.

The armed man swinging the rifle off his shoulder. Gideon shoving me hard toward the ice fin, her body already moving between me and the muzzle. Vey's voice, sharp and cold: *"Not here — the survey first, we need the cores intact—"* — and God help me, even now, even with rifles drawn over my grandfather's grave, the thing that stays with me is that: *the survey first.* Men were about to die and his concern was the integrity of his samples.

We made the fin. Bullets cracked into the ice above us, shedding shards that glittered as they fell, catching the underlight like the snow-spirals from the rotor wash — beautiful, in the way terrible things are beautiful when you're too fast-moving to be afraid yet. Gideon dragged me deeper into the maze of blue fins, away from the landing shelf, toward the far side of the chamber where the tunnel mouth opened onto the route down.

"We can't outrun a helicopter," I gasped.

"Don't need to outrun it." She was scanning the ceiling, the walls, reading the ice the way she read everything — water, rock, weather — for the flaw, the seam, the weakness. "Need to outrun *them*. On foot, in this maze, in the dark. They'll have to shut down to follow." Her eyes came back to mine, and there was something in them I hadn't seen since the snow cave — not fear, not resolve, but the raw aftermath of the choice she'd just made, still bleeding. "Mara. What I almost—"

"I know."

"It was five seconds. I need you to know it was five seconds and it felt like—"

"*I know.*" I caught her face in both mittens, ice-crusted and freezing, and held it. "You gave it back. That's the whole story. You gave it back."

Behind us, across the chamber, Vey's voice rose over the rotor spooling up again — shouting orders, coordinates, and one phrase that dropped through me like cold water:

*"Seal the tunnel. Nothing leaves this mountain with my name on it."*

Gideon's grip tightened on my arm. Above us, deep in the glacier's throat, something answered — a groan, vast and slow, the sound of ten thousand tons of ice remembering that it moves.

The calving had started early. And Aldous Vey had just decided to help it along.

"Run," Gideon said.
