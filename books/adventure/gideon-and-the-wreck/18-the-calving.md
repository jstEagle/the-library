## 18. The Calving

The source was a cathedral, and we had walked into it like thieves into a church.

Blue ice arched a hundred feet overhead, thick as cathedral glass and lit from below by the mineral seam — a vein of something that glowed a faint, wrong green through the ice floor, as if the glacier had swallowed a piece of the aurora and couldn't digest it. The light came up through our boots. It came up through Mara's face when she turned to look at me, and for one moment neither of us said anything, because there was nothing in either of our pockets equal to it.

Then the helicopter came.

We heard it first as a tremor in the ice under our feet — a shudder too low for the ear, felt in the teeth, in the bones of the ankle. Torv had stayed behind at the tunnel mouth with the flare gun and a promise: *I'll mark the route. I'll hold them as long as I can.* He'd bought us what he could. It hadn't been enough.

The sound arrived next, bouncing down the tunnel we'd spent two days crawling through, amplified by every curved wall of ice until it seemed to come from everywhere at once, from inside my own skull. Mara grabbed my arm.

"They can't land here," she said. "There's no—"

The rotor wash hit us before she finished, a fist of displaced air that flattened the snow drifts along the chamber's edge and sent a spray of ice crystals scything past our faces. The helicopter swung into the cavern mouth sideways, huge and black against the blue, its skids nearly kissing the ice arches, and set down on the flat shelf of glacier maybe forty yards from where we stood. The downdraft screamed off the walls. Somewhere above us, deep in the glacier's spine, something groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, like a ship taking water.

"Get behind the seam ridge," I said. "Now."

We ran for the outcrop of dirty ice where the glowing vein surfaced, crouched into its shadow. My hands were shaking, and not from cold. Everything we'd carried — the journal, waterproofed and wrapped against my chest, the photographs from the drowned camp, the ledger page with Vey's own signature authorizing an expedition that officially never existed — all of it was about to matter or be destroyed, and I didn't get to choose which.

Aldous Vey stepped out of the helicopter before the rotors finished turning.

He was smaller than his reputation. That was the first thing. In Skarnvik he was spoken of the way weather is spoken of — vast, inevitable, beyond argument — but the man who crossed the ice toward us wore a city parka over a city sweater and moved like someone who had never once had to think about where his next footfall would land. Two of his men flanked him. I recognized neither, which meant Torv and the ones who'd hunted us on the tongue were either left behind or no longer on the payroll. Behind them, a third man held a camera, filming everything, documenting the claim.

Vey stopped ten feet from our outcrop and looked around the chamber — at the blue vault of it, at the green light seeping up through the floor — and I watched something move across his face that frightened me more than any gun. Not greed exactly. Recognition. The look of a man finding the thing he'd been promised existed, the thing he'd paid for in other people's blood twenty years ago, still waiting for him, patient as a debt.

"There it is," he said softly. To himself. Then, louder, to me: "Miss Blackwood. You've made this very difficult."

"You made it difficult," I said. "Twenty years ago. When you cut loose eight people in a hole in the ice because they found your mine."

"My *deposit*." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had opened doors for him his whole life. "And they weren't cut loose. They were released from their contract. There's a difference. Legally, there's a considerable difference."

"The journal says otherwise."

"The journal," Vey said, "is a dead man's spite, written by men who were dying and wanted company. No court will read it as anything but that." He took another step forward. His men fanned wide, cutting the angle toward the tunnel mouth. "But you're not going to court, Miss Blackwood. You're going to be reasonable. Because you want something from the world, and I'm the only man alive who can give it to you."

Mara's hand found mine in the shadow of the ridge. Her fingers were freezing and steady.

"I know what you want," Vey went on, and now his voice dropped into the register of a man who has closed a thousand deals and never lost one. "Gideon Blackwood. The guide who walked her client off a mountain. I've read the file. I've read it closely. And I'm offering you a clean page. Full exoneration. The inquiry reopened, the findings reversed, your certification restored, your name cleared in every registry from here to Oslo. One condition." He extended his open hand, palm up, patient. "The journal. The photographs. Everything you pulled from that camp. And your silence, going forward, which I suspect will be the easier half of the bargain for you."

The green light came up between us. His face floated above it, half-lit, half-rotted with shadow.

And God help me, I hesitated.

Not long. A breath, maybe two. But I felt it happen — felt the whole shape of my life tilt toward that open hand. A name without a stain on it. Walking back into Skarnvik as something other than the woman parents warned their children about. Sleeping a whole night without hearing Erik Lund's last shout come off the ice. It rose in me like floodwater, warm and filthy, and for those two breaths I would have traded anything in the world for it.

Mara's grip tightened. She didn't say a word. She just looked at me, and in her face was her grandfather frozen mid-flight in the camp under the ice, and her father's empty workshop, and every person Vey had ever priced and sold, and I understood that whatever I chose in the next three seconds, I would have to live inside the choosing for the rest of my life.

"No," I said.

Vey's hand stayed open a moment longer. Then it closed, slowly, the way a trap closes.

"I was told you'd say that," he said. "Torv said you were stubborn. Torv also said—" and he sighed, genuinely regretful, a man inconvenienced by the weather, "—that you'd need to be handled. I dislike handling women. It's inelegant. But I've come a very long way."

His men started forward.

That was when the glacier answered.

* * *

It began as a sound I knew from my old life — the crack of stressed ice releasing, sharp as a rifle shot — except it came from everywhere, from the ceiling itself, and it did not stop after one report but rolled on and on, gathering echoes, multiplying. The green light beneath us flickered. Dust sifted down through the rotor wash like snow falling upward.

"Get back to the aircraft," Vey snapped, and for the first time his voice cracked its polish.

Nobody got anywhere. The calving, when it came, came all at once.

The far wall of the chamber — the great blue face where the glacier met the meltwater channel — let go. Not a chunk. A country. I heard it rather than saw it first, a roar like the sky tearing, and then the whole wall was moving, tons upon tons of ancient ice shearing free and plunging into the channel below the shelf, and the shockwave came through the floor and threw us all down. The helicopter's tail swung. Its main rotor clipped the ice shelf's edge with a shriek of metal that I will hear for the rest of my life, and the aircraft slewed, tipped, and went over onto its side in a bloom of fuel and shattered plexiglass.

The wave came next. Meltwater displaced by the fall surged across the shelf in a brown-white wall, waist-high and faster than running, and I grabbed Mara and hauled her up the seam ridge as it hit, the cold punching the air out of my lungs, debris hammering past us — ice blocks the size of tables, a rotor blade spinning end over end like a scythe thrown by a furious god. One of Vey's men vanished under it. I heard him go — one short cry, cut off — and then the water was past, draining away through cracks in the shelf, and the chamber rang with aftershocks, secondary calvings, the glacier shedding weight in great groaning slabs.

"Vey!" I shouted into the din.

I found him thirty feet away, pinned. Not crushed — pinned. A slab of calved ice, blue and house-sized, had come to rest against the shelf's edge, and his leg was trapped beneath its lip in the flooded hollow where he'd fallen. The camera man was gone — swept into the channel, or fled, I never learned which. The second man lay ten meters off, motionless, his head at an angle heads don't make.

Mara reached my shoulder. "Gideon. We have to go. The whole shelf is going."

She was right. I could feel it under my knees — the deep, irregular shudder of a structure losing its argument with gravity. Cracks were propagating outward from the impact zone in slow, visible lines, ticking like a clock.

Vey looked up at me. Water to his chest, one leg pinned under a hundred tons of his own inheritance, and his face was still composed. That was the terrible thing. Even now, even drowning in the ruin of twenty years of murder and money, the composure held — and underneath it, visible only because everything else had been stripped away, was the plain arithmetic of a man who had never once believed the world could collect from him.

"Blackwood," he said. "Help me."

Every reason to walk away stood around me in the form of ice. His men had hunted us across a glacier. His money had put bullets near Mara, had put her father in debt and then in the ground. Eight people had died in this ice because of his signature, and he had come here to buy my silence with the one thing I wanted most, knowing exactly what it was worth to me, the way you know what a hook is worth to a fish.

Mara's hand was on my sleeve. "Gideon."

I went down the ridge and into the floodwater.

The cold took my breath again. I got my shoulder under the slab's edge where it bridged his leg, found what purchase the ice offered, and lifted. It gave — an inch, two — grinding, shrieking, settling. "Pull," I gasped. "Pull *now*."

Vey dragged himself backward through the water, screaming — actually screaming, the composure gone at last, ripped away like a bandage — and his leg came free, boot and all, and we fell apart into the shallows as the slab settled into the space where he'd been trapped and kept going, the whole mass sliding, the shelf beneath it finally surrendering. The crack line I'd been watching reached us. The ground tilted.

We ran. All three of us, the hunter and the hunted and the daughter of the man in between, scrambling up the seam ridge as the shelf behind us broke apart and avalanched into the channel in a rising column of spray. The wrecked helicopter went with it, sliding, rolling, swallowed. The green light under the ice pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat, and dimmed under the fresh rubble.

We gained the high ground at the chamber's edge, gasping. Vey collapsed on his side, coughing meltwater, his ruined leg bent wrong below the knee. And I knelt beside him, shaking so hard I could barely keep my hands from the ice, feeling the absurd, furious tenderness that comes after you've saved someone — the body doesn't know better, the body just knows it carried a weight and set it down.

"You're alive," I said. "Do you understand what that cost? Do you understand what I—"

He moved fast for a crippled man. Faster than I'd credited. His hand came out of the waterlogged pocket of his parka with a pistol in it — compact, black, dry because he'd kept it bagged the way he kept everything, protected, insured — and the muzzle came up toward my face from a distance of four feet.

"*You should have taken the deal,*" Aldous Vey said.

There was no regret in it. That was what I remember most, afterward. Not rage, not desperation. Just a businessman revising a forecast. I had become an expense, and he intended to write me off.

Mara hit him.

She came off the ridge low and hard, a salvager's tackle, the kind you use on things trying to sink, and drove her shoulder into his gun arm. The shot went off — a flat crack that slapped around the chamber and knocked a whistling echo off the ceiling — and the round went wide, close enough that I felt its heat pass my ear. The three of us went down together in the shallow water. The pistol spun free, skittering across the ice.

Vey fought like a man who had never been told no. Elbow, heel, teeth — he got a hand into my collar and slammed the back of my skull against the ice, and stars burst across my vision, and through them I heard Mara shouting my name, and I understood with perfect clarity that this man would kill both of us with his bare hands if the glacier gave him five minutes to do it.

The glacier did not give him five minutes.

The shelf we were fighting on had been dying since the calving, and it chose that moment to finish. The ice beneath Vey — the section he'd been dragged clear of, the section riddled with the old crevasse his own surveyors had mapped and flagged twenty years ago, the crevasse his expedition had been ordered across because it saved time — dropped out from under him with a sound like a door closing.

He didn't even scream. There was time for him to look at me — one full second, hanging, arms spread, the green light coming up around him out of the dark he was falling into — and what was in his face wasn't fear. It was outrage. Pure, aristocratic outrage, the expression of a man discovering at the last possible instant that the invoice had been in his name all along.

Then the dark took him, and the ice closed over the place where he'd been, and the sound of his fall went down and down and did not come back.

* * *

Silence, after. The particular silence of a place that has just eaten something large.

Mara and I lay in the shallows ten feet from the new crevasse edge, tangled together, breathing. My skull throbbed where he'd hammered it. Her lip was split. Neither of us moved for a long time.

"He tried to shoot you," she said finally. Her voice sounded scraped. "After you pulled him out."

"Yes."

"And then he just—" She gestured at the crevasse, at the smooth blue lips of it, already refreezing at the edges. "Just like that. Like the ice had been waiting for him."

"Maybe it had," I said. "Eight people waited in it long enough."

We got up. We had to; the chamber was still shedding, smaller pieces now, the tantrum fading into sobs. The wreck site steamed where fuel slicked the floodwater. Somewhere under the new rubble lay the helicopter, the radio, the camera, two of Vey's crew, and every quick way home. What remained of Vey's expedition to the source was the three of us — and one of us was a leg and a lie down in the dark.

Mara found the pistol at the shelf's edge and picked it up with two fingers, like something dead. She looked at it a long moment, then at me.

"We can't carry him out," she said. "Not through the tunnel. Not with that leg. You know that."

I knew it. I looked at the crevasse, and I thought about ropes, and anchors, and the arithmetic of a two-woman team hauling a hundred and ninety pounds of unconscious man up a vertical ice wall while the glacier came down around our ears, and I thought about Torv alone at the tunnel mouth, and the route sealing behind us hour by hour, and I hated every number in the equation.

"He's alive down there," Mara said quietly. "Probably. Shelf collapse like that — there'd be ledges. Chambers. People survive falls like that."

"People," I said. "He isn't people. He's what's left when you take everything else away."

"Gideon." She caught my arm. "If we leave him, we're the ones who decide who the ice keeps. That's his job. It's always been his job. Don't do it for him."

The green light pulsed under the ice between us, slow and patient, lighting her face from below.

Far off, back toward the tunnel mouth, a flare went up — red, stuttering, Torv's signal, the one we'd agreed meant *route's open, come now, hurry*. And beneath it, fainter, almost lost under the glacier's groaning, another sound. Fainter still, but unmistakable.

A voice. Calling up out of the crevasse. Aldous Vey, alive in the dark, shouting for help — and, God help me, shouting my name.
