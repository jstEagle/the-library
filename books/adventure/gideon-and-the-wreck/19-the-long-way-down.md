## 19. The Long Way Down

The source closed behind us like a mouth.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like the whole mountain clearing its throat, a deep grinding groan that came up through the ice and into my boots and my teeth and the marrow of everything I was. Gideon had my arm and we were running, actually running, on a leg I was no longer sure belonged to me, and behind us the tunnel we had climbed out of was folding in on itself with a patience that felt almost deliberate.

"Torv," I said. Or tried to. The name came out as a breath.

"He's marking it," Gideon said. She didn't slow. Her voice was flat in the way it went flat when she was spending everything she had on the next step and had nothing left over for feeling. "He has the flares. He has the radio. He knows what he's doing."

He knows what he's doing. I thought about that the way you think about anything when your body has been reduced to a list of failures: he knows what he's doing, and what he was doing was standing alone in a cathedral of blue ice with a signal marker and a promise, waiting for men in government jackets who would not arrive for days, while the glacier came down around him like a house deciding to become a grave.

I didn't look back. Looking back was a luxury. I had learned that in the last hour, in the worst hour, when Vey's helicopter had come apart against the ice wall and the calving had swallowed the sound of it whole, and Gideon had stood at the lip of a crevasse with Vey's hand in hers, and he had looked up at her — I saw it, I was ten meters away, I will see it until I die — and he had smiled, and reached for the knife at his belt, and she had let go.

She had let go. And he had fallen into the crevasse he had ordered cut, the one that had nearly taken us on the ice, and the glacier had closed its ledger on Aldous Vey the way it closes everything: without comment, without cruelty, without caring that the man it took had spent twenty years thinking himself the author of this country.

Now the glacier was finishing its sentence, and we were inside the punctuation.

* * *

The river tunnel was dying around us.

That was the thing no one tells you about ice — that it is not a place, it is an event. It is always becoming or always unbecoming, and we had entered it during the becoming, when the meltwater had carved its blue cathedral through the glacier's heart, and now we were leaving during the unbecoming, when the same water that made the road was taking the road apart behind us, section by section, like a bridge crew working in reverse.

Gideon led. Of course Gideon led. She had a rope around her waist and my rope tied to hers and she moved through the failing tunnel with her head tilted, listening, the way she had listened for three days now, reading the ice by sound the way other people read a page. When the tunnel groaned she stopped. When it sang — that high thin note, water under pressure finding a new path — she changed direction without explaining, and I followed, because explaining was a luxury and trust was not.

"How's the leg," she said. Not a question. A check, the way a pilot checks an engine.

"It's attached."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

She glanced back at me then, and in the blue light of the tunnel her face looked carved out of something older than a person, and for a moment I thought she was going to say something — about the leg, about Torv, about the satchel strapped to my chest with its journal and its photographs and its proof that my grandfather had been murdered a hundred meters from the truth he'd given his life to reach. She didn't say it. She turned back to the ice and put her hand flat against the wall, feeling for the vibration, and said, "Fifty meters to the outflow. Then it's the river. Keep your weight on the rope, not the ice."

The ice chose that moment to demonstrate its opinion of instructions.

It came from above — a crack like a rifle, then a section of the ceiling the size of a door letting go with a sound I felt in my lungs. Gideon shoved me forward and down and the world went white and loud and then very, very quiet, the way it goes quiet after an avalanche, that held-breath quiet where you take inventory of your body by asking it questions and waiting to see which parts answer.

I answered. All of me, more or less. My left leg answered late and rudely. Gideon was on her feet already, hauling me up by the rope, and where the ceiling section had landed there was now a wall of shattered ice between us and the tunnel behind us, and ahead of us the passage narrowed to a slot of blue light that meant the outflow, that meant the river, that meant sky.

"Walk," Gideon said.

I walked.

* * *

The river took us the way a hand takes a coin it intends to keep.

We had no raft. The raft had died on the glacier with the rest of our margin, and what we had instead was a length of rope, two packs that weighed almost nothing because almost nothing was what remained in them, and the satchel. The satchel I carried against my chest like a second heartbeat, dry inside its oilskin, holding everything: the journal with its final page in my grandfather's hand, the photographs of the executed camp, the survey plates that showed the seam of rare-earth ore glowing under the ice like a vein of green fire, the manifest with Vey's signature financing all of it. A nation's ransom in paper, and it weighed less than my boots.

We went down the outflow gorge in the water itself, wading when we could, swimming the short brutal channels when we couldn't, the river doing the work our legs refused. The gorge walls rose on either side, black rock streaked with snowmelt, and above them the glacier filled the sky like a wall being built by God in a hurry. Every few minutes the mountain spoke — that groan, that grind — and each time it spoke, the sound came from lower down the valley than the time before. The calving was moving toward the river. The route was sealing. Behind us, section by section, the year was closing its door.

Gideon counted the sounds. I watched her count them, watched her lips move, and somewhere in the third hour of the descent I realized she had stopped talking altogether except for single words: *Rock. Rope. Breathe. Now.* Survival stripped down to its grammar. There was a strange mercy in it. There was no room in that language for grief, and grief was the thing I was carrying that the satchel couldn't hold.

My father was in that language nowhere, and everywhere.

* * *

We made the lower gorge as the light went from white to pewter, and there — wedged in a bend where the river slowed and spread — was the raft.

Not our raft. The drowned expedition's raft.

It had been there for years, folded into the rocks like something the river had given up trying to digest, its frame of lashed birch gone gray, its skin of sealed canvas cracked and bleached. My grandfather's expedition had come down this river. Some of them had come down this river alive, I thought, and then corrected myself, because the photographs had told me how that ended — but the raft had come down, and the river had kept it, the way the river keeps everything, and now it was going to carry his granddaughter out on the same water that had carried his killers in.

Gideon turned it over and ran her hands along the frame the way a doctor examines a patient, and I watched her face do the arithmetic. "Frame's sound," she said. "Skin's the problem." She pulled the canvas taut and put her thumb against the worst crack and looked at it for a long time. "We patch it. We run it slow. We stay in the eddies and we walk anything I don't like the look of." She looked up at me. "And we run it tonight, or we don't run it at all. When that tongue lets go up there, this gorge becomes a wall of water with a river's name."

So we patched it. With what we had, which was the tarp from the ruined tent in the frozen camp, cut into strips, and the pine pitch Torv had taught us to tap from the stunted shore pines on the way up, and Gideon's hands, which were steady, which were always steady, which I had watched thread a rope through collapsing ice without a tremor and which trembled now, only slightly, only when she thought I wasn't looking, and I understood that her hands were not shaking from the cold.

"Torv," I said, into the work. Just the name. I had been holding it for six hours and it needed to come out or it was going to start breaking things.

"He'll make it," Gideon said.

"You don't know that."

"No," she said. She worked the pitch into a seam with her thumbnail, precise, thorough. "I don't know that."

"He stayed so we could run."

"He stayed because someone has to be standing there when the authorities come, or the source is a story again. He knows that. He chose it." She tied off the patch and sat back on her heels, and for a moment she let her face be tired, all the way tired, in a way I had never seen it, not in the storm cave, not on the ice. "That's the thing about this whole country," she said quietly. "It's always asking someone to stay behind so the rest can carry the proof out. My whole career was that question, asked over and over. Who stays. Who carries." She looked at the river. "I used to think the guide's job was to make sure the answer was nobody. I don't think that anymore. I think the job is to make sure the staying means something."

I put my hand over hers on the raft's frame. She didn't pull away. That was all we said about it, because the light was going and the mountain was counting down, and some things you say with your hands or you never say at all.

* * *

We ran the lower gorge in the dark.

I will not pretend I remember it clearly. Memory under that kind of fear comes back in pieces, in flashes, the way lightning shows you a landscape: Gideon's silhouette at the steering paddle, her body reading the water ahead of the water; the white seams of rapids appearing out of the dark like something exhaling; my own hands on the forward line, doing what she called, in that clipped survival grammar, when she called it. The patched raft flexing and holding. Flexing and holding. Once — I will keep this flash — the raft struck something and the whole world tilted and the satchel swung out over the water on its strap and I caught it with both hands and held it against me and felt my grandfather's journal through the oilskin, hard as a bone, and I said, out loud, into the spray, to no one, to him: *I've got it. I've got it. I'm bringing it home.*

We came through. That is the whole of the story of the night: we came through, into the widening valley where the river slowed and spread and went silver under the first gray of dawn, and the gorge behind us was silent in a way that was worse than noise, and when I looked back — finally, finally allowed to look back — the glacier tongue was down. The route was closed. A new wall of ice stood where the outflow had been, blue and clean and final, and no one would pass that way for a year.

Whatever was happening at the source, at the mouth of that tunnel, was happening without us now. Torv. The flares. The waiting. I turned my face forward and did not turn it back again, because there is a kind of looking back that is just a way of falling, and I had done all the falling I intended to do on this river.

* * *

We walked the raft through the shallows in the gray morning, because neither of us trusted the water and neither of us would say so, and somewhere in that long wading silence Gideon began to laugh.

It was a small sound, cracked and disbelieving, and it frightened me until I turned and saw her face and understood it wasn't madness. It was the other thing. It was what comes out of a person when the weight lifts before the ground does.

"What," I said.

"Three weeks ago," she said, "I took a salvage contract because I was broke and blacklisted and I wanted a warm bed and a fee I didn't have to lie for." She shook her head. The morning light caught her and she looked, for the first time since I'd known her, like someone who was not running. "And now I've got a murdered man's journal, a dead shipping magnate, a sealed glacier, a defected hunter holding a mountain for the authorities, and a raft held together with pine pitch and spite."

"Sounds like a contract fulfillment issue."

"It sounds," she said, "like the most honest work I've ever done."

We came around the last bend as the sun cleared the ridge, and there it was: Skarnvik, small and red-roofed on its shelf above the fjord, smoke standing straight up from the chimneys, the harbor water flat as hammered tin. Ordinary. Impossible. A town that had spent a hundred years telling itself a story about a forbidden cave and a cursed expedition, and did not yet know that its story had just come walking down the river with proof in an oilskin satchel.

I stopped. Gideon stopped with me.

The satchel was against my chest. The journal was in the satchel. My grandfather's handwriting was in the journal, and the last page named Aldous Vey, and Aldous Vey was under the ice, and every house in front of us was a house he had built or bought or broken.

"They'll want to know who to blame," Gideon said quietly. She was looking at the town the way she had once looked at the first rapid — reading it, weighing it, deciding what it could kill. "It's how towns work. Something breaks, and the truth is expensive, and blame is cheap. Some of them will decide this was our doing. That we brought the mountain down on them." She glanced at me. "You can still turn back. Take the raft down the fjord, take the satchel to the rangers at the coast station, be a stranger when it all comes apart."

"And leave you to walk in there alone?"

"That was the original plan," she said. "Back when I had one."

I looked at the town. I looked at my father's workshop, invisible from here but exactly where I had left it, ransacked, empty, the debt of blood Vey had called in and my father had refused to pay. I looked at the harbor where his boat was tied, if it was still tied, if anyone had untied it, if the town that watched everything had watched that too and said nothing, the way Skarnvik had always said nothing, the way the silence itself was the thing Vey had really purchased with all that money, all those years.

"No," I said. "We go in together. We go in loud."

Gideon looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I had never seen her do. She smiled — not the flat survival curve, not the courtesy blade, but an actual smile, tired and crooked and real, gone almost before it arrived.

"Loud," she agreed. "I'm told I'm out of practice."

We took up the towline, and the river — the same river that had carried my grandfather's killers, that had kept his raft, that ran from a forbidden source under a dying glacier all the way down to the gray water of the fjord — carried us the last stretch toward the town, and the first people were already coming down to the shore to see what the mountain had sent them.
