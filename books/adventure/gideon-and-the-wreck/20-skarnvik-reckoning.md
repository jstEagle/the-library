## 20. Skarnvik Reckoning

The river gave us back to the sea on the ninth day.

I knew we were close before I saw the town — the water widened and slowed, went from white noise to a brown-green murmur, and the smell changed from stone and ice to salt and fish oil and woodsmoke. Skarnvik's smell. I'd hated it when I arrived. Now it hit me like a hand on the shoulder, and I had to stop walking for a moment because my legs were shaking and I didn't want Mara to see.

She saw anyway. She always did.

"Sit down," she said, and there was no argument in her voice, which meant she was past arguing with me, which meant I'd been refusing to rest for too long. My left arm was strapped to my chest where the ice had taken its toll on the glacier tongue — not broken, the ranger medic at the source station had thought, but torn in a way that made every step forward a negotiation. The wound along my ribs had been cleaned and sewn with fishing line by Torv before he stayed behind, his hands steady, his eyes already somewhere else, up at the source he would hold alone until the authorities came.

"Ten minutes," I said.

"Fifteen." She dropped the pack against a boulder and sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. Below us, through a break in the pines, the fjord lay flat as hammered tin, and Skarnvik sat on its shore like something that had grown there rather than been built — crooked rooftops, drying racks, the masts of Vey's ships standing in the harbor like a row of needles waiting to be threaded.

We had come down out of the mountains carrying everything: the journal, waterproofed twice over; the photographs Mara had taken of the frozen camp; the ledger pages from Vey's own strongbox that Torv had copied before he defected; my testimony, spoken into the ranger's recorder at the station while my voice cracked like river ice. And we carried the dead. Not their bodies — those belonged to the mountain now, all of them, the drowned expedition and Aldous Vey both — but their names, their dates, the whole architecture of what had been done to them and why.

"You can still turn around," Mara said. It was the third time she'd said it. "Walk north. There are towns up the coast that have never heard of you or him."

"And leave you holding this alone?"

"I'm not asking you to leave me holding it. I'm asking if *you* want to be here when they decide what to do with it."

I looked at the town. At the harbor. At the big house on the headland with its white trim, where lights were burning even though it was midday.

"No," I said. "I spent two years running from a crowd that got one part of the story wrong. I'm not going to hand them another one and walk away before they read it."

Mara didn't answer. She just took my good hand, squeezed once, and stood up. We walked down together.

* * *

The rangers came first.

We didn't go to them, exactly — we went to the harbor master's office, because Mara said her father's old friend Runa held the keys and the radio and the only safe in town bigger than Vey's, and because Mara had learned somewhere in the last three weeks that the way to move through Skarnvik was sideways, through the people who actually kept it running. Runa met us at the door with a shotgun across her forearm and her face gone the color of cod.

"Mara." Her eyes went over us both — the bandages, the frost-scars, the way I leaned on the doorframe. "They said you were dead. Both of you. Vey's men put it about that you'd gone into the gorge."

"We went into the gorge," Mara said. "That part's true."

Runa looked at me then, longer. Whatever she'd heard about Gideon Blackwood — and by then, in that town, she'd heard plenty — she seemed to be weighing it against the woman standing in front of her, and finding the accounts didn't quite balance either direction.

"The radio," I said. "Call the district ranger station at Hovden. Tell them Torv Lindqvist is holding position at the source coordinates in the journal, and tell them to bring enough people that Vey's remaining men don't get any ideas. Then lock this door."

It took four hours. In those four hours, word spread through Skarnvik the way dye spreads through water — first a thread, then a cloud, then everywhere. People began to gather outside the harbor office. First a handful of fishermen who'd known Mara's father, then dockworkers, then women from the cannery still in their aprons, then men I recognized from the tavern the night I'd first asked about the cave, faces that had turned away from me then and were turning toward me now with an expression I couldn't read and didn't trust.

By the time the rangers' boat came around the headland — two boats, then a third, orange hulls and hard eyes — there must have been two hundred people packed along the quay.

The senior ranger was a broad, gray-haired woman named Halvardsson who shook my left hand carefully and listened to me give the whole account a second time without interrupting once. When I finished, she looked at the evidence laid out on Runa's table — the journal open to the last page, the photographs fanned like a losing hand of cards, Torv's copies of the ledgers showing payments routed through three shell companies to outfit an expedition Vey had officially never heard of, and the final entry, dated eleven years ago, naming the man who had ordered the survivors abandoned at the source and paid to make sure no one ever went looking.

"This will take months," Halvardsson said. "There will be hearings. There will be lawyers — his lawyers, and they're good. And there will be people in this town who call you a liar to your face."

"I know," I said. "I've met them before. They look like everyone else."

She almost smiled. Then she straightened, squared the papers, and said, "Then let's go give them something to argue about."

* * *

I want to say the town rose up as one. That's the version that will end up in the newspapers, probably, because newspapers need clean lines the way rivers need gradient. But the truth was messier, and I was standing in the middle of it.

Halvardsson read the core of the evidence aloud from the fish-market steps, her voice carrying off the harbor walls, and the crowd did not gasp or roar. It went quiet — that particular northern quiet, dense as fog — and in the quiet I could hear individual sounds: a woman saying *no, no* under her breath, over and over, like a prayer being unsaid. A man laughing once, horribly, and stopping. Someone vomiting against a piling. Old Sivertsen, who'd told me the legend of the cave my first night in town, pushing through to the front, staring at the photograph of the frozen camp, at the figure Mara had marked with a red circle — her grandfather, kneeling, hands raised, shot from behind — and making a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

And then the crowd broke, not toward Vey's house but apart, into knots, into arguments, into families turning inward. Because that's what truth does in a small place. It doesn't unite. It sorts.

"That's not proof." The voice came from my left, from a knot of men near the chandlery, and I knew it — Brandt, one of Vey's foremen, a man whose boats I'd watched load my first week. "Papers can be forged. Photographs can be staged. This outsider comes down off the mountain with a story that ruins the man who employs half this town—"

"Her grandfather is in that photograph," someone shouted back. "Mara Oyelaran's grandfather. You think she staged that?"

"I think grief makes people believe anything!"

"And money makes people deny it!" That was Runa, from the steps, and the crowd made a sound at that — not agreement exactly, but the sound of a thing shifting, a keel coming around.

Brandt looked for support and found less than he expected. He found some, though. That was the part I kept noticing, all through that long afternoon. For every face that turned toward me with dawning horror, there was another that hardened — men who owed Vey money, women whose sons crewed his ships, people who had built their lives inside the structure he'd built and could not yet imagine the structure falling. One of them spat near my boots. Another shouldered past me hard enough to stagger my bad arm, and Mara caught my elbow before I went down, and her grip said *not now, not here,* and I let it go, because she was right, because I'd learned something in the ice about which fights choose you and which ones you just walk into.

"He'll have lawyers," a woman said, not unkindly, to no one in particular. "He'll be dead and his lawyers will still be winning."

"Aldous Vey is dead," Halvardsson said flatly. "His helicopter went into the ice with six of his men. What's left of his company answers to the courts now. So does this town." She folded the papers. "Hearings begin Monday. Anyone with knowledge of the expedition, the financing, or the events at the source will be called to testify."

Her eyes found mine when she said it. So did two hundred others'.

Monday. Three days. I stood on the fish-market steps with the whole town looking at me — the disgraced guide, the outsider, the woman who'd come back from the forbidden place carrying the bodies of their dead — and I understood that whatever happened next, I would never again be invisible here. I had spent two years learning how to pass through crowds without touching them. That skill was gone. I'd spent it, along with everything else, somewhere above the calving line.

* * *

That night, Mara's father's workshop.

She hadn't been able to go in since we'd found it ransacked, since the night Vey's men had come for the journal and found a stubborn old salvager instead. Now she unlocked the door with the key she'd worn on a cord around her neck through glaciers and rapids, and stood in the doorway breathing, and I waited, because some doors you have to walk through yourself.

Inside, it was worse than I remembered and better too. The ransacking had scattered everything — tools on the floor, drawers pulled out, shavings everywhere — but the shape of the room was still his. The bench where he'd taught Mara to splice line. The wall of photographs: boats launched, storms survived, a girl of maybe eight grinning on a raft with a paddle too big for her.

Mara righted things in silence for an hour. I helped where I could, one-armed, mostly staying out of the way. Near the end she stopped at the workbench and picked up a half-finished repair — a lantern frame, brass, delicate — and held it a long time.

"He refused them," she said. "You know that. Torv told us. Vey's men offered him double the debt forgiven, and he told them the journal wasn't his to sell." Her voice didn't break. It had gone somewhere past breaking, into a flat clear register like deep water. "Everyone in this town thinks my father died because he was weak. Because he owed Vey and couldn't pay. He died because he was the strongest man in Skarnvik and nobody, including me, ever bothered to notice."

"They'll notice now," I said. "Monday. Tell them that."

She set the lantern down carefully, aligned it with the bench edge, and nodded once. Then she turned and looked at me with an expression I was starting to know better than my own reflection — the one that meant she was about to say something I wouldn't like and was going to say it anyway.

"And you? What are you going to tell them Monday?"

"The truth."

"All of it? Including the checkpoint?" She held my eyes. "Including the forged permit, Gideon. Halvardsson will ask how we passed a legal barrier into restricted terrain, and you're going to stand in front of this town and lie fluently, or you're going to confess, and either one costs you. I've watched you bend for three weeks. I love—" she stopped, recalibrated, started again, "—I *know* what you are. They don't. They'll want you to be clean. And you're not clean. None of us is. That's the whole point of what we carried down the mountain."

I looked at the lantern. At the photographs on the wall. At the girl with the too-big paddle.

"I'll tell them all of it," I said finally. "The permit. The lies at the station. All of it. If this town is going to convict a man for buying the truth, they can watch me pay retail for mine."

Something moved through Mara's face — not relief exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone watching another person choose the harder door and knowing they'd have chosen it too.

* * *

Monday came gray and windless, the fjord like a held breath.

The hearing was held in the church hall because nothing else in town held three hundred people. I testified for six hours. I told them about the low tide and the cave and the satchel; about Vey's offer, triple the salvage fee, delivered in a voice like warm oil; about the search of my gear; about the journal's last page and the names of the dead. I told them about the checkpoint and the forged permit, and watched the room absorb that — some of them nodding, *practical, necessary*, others writing it down like a debt. I told them about the frozen camp, and Mara's grandfather kneeling with his hands up, and I watched old Sivertsen close his eyes. I told them about the rare-earth seam glowing blue under the ice, worth more than every ship in this harbor multiplied by every year this town has existed, and about a man who looked at that and saw not wonder but ownership.

Vey's lawyers were there, three of them, gray suits and gray voices, and they did exactly what Halvardsson had promised. They attacked the chain of custody. They suggested the journal was a forgery planted by a disgraced guide with a grudge. They brought up my name — *Gideon Blackwood, formerly of the Alpine Guides Registry, dismissed following the Kessler incident* — and read the old findings aloud in a courtroom voice, and for a moment I was back in that other hearing room two years ago, the one where I'd sat silent while men who'd never roped up in their lives explained to me what had happened on a mountain I'd descended alone.

But this time I wasn't silent, and I wasn't alone.

When the lead lawyer finished with my record and sat back, satisfied, expecting the room to see a liar, Mara stood up from the gallery without being called and said, "Ask her what happened on the Kessler route. Ask her what she *did*. I've read the transcript — my father kept a copy, he followed her case, he said the registry buried the wrong person." And then she sat down, and the room turned, and Halvardsson, after a long moment, said, "The witness may answer."

So I answered. I told them about the client I couldn't save — all of it, the decisions, the weather window, the rope, the ten seconds where any choice killed someone and I chose and I've lived with it every day since. I told them the registry needed a name to hang it on and mine was convenient. I watched the town weigh it, and I understood, sitting there, that some of them would never believe me, and that this was fine, that this was simply the price of standing in front of people instead of slipping past them, and that I had somehow, without noticing, stopped needing them to believe me. Needing the truth to be *said* was different. Cheaper. Lighter.

The verdicts didn't come that day, or that week. But the empire cracked while we talked. By Thursday, Vey's harbormaster had resigned. By Friday, two ship captains withdrew their contracts. The bank froze the company's accounts pending investigation, and the big house on the headland went dark at night for the first time in thirty years. Some people in Skarnvik thanked me. Some crossed the street. A rock came through the harbor office window one night with a note tied to it — *outsider poison* — and Runa swept up the glass and said, "That's how it goes. Truth's a flood. It takes out the good bridges along with the dam."

Mara's father got a funeral the town will talk about for fifty years. Half of Skarnvik walked up to the cemetery in the rain, and the pastor — who had taken Vey's donations for decades and knew it, and stood there anyway with his jaw set like a man doing penance in public — spoke about a man who kept his debts and paid his debts and knew the difference between the two. Mara stood at the graveside with her chin up and her hand in mine, and when they lowered the box she said, quietly, so only I could hear:

"He'd have liked you. He'd have hated everything you cost me, and he'd have liked you anyway."

"That's the best review I've had in years," I said, and she laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one — and the rain came down on all of us equally, the guilty and the grieving and the merely present, which is the only kind of justice weather knows how to do.

* * *

On the tenth morning after the hearing, a letter came to the harbor office addressed to me in a clerk's careful hand. The Alpine Guides Registry. An expedited review. New evidence, corroborated witness statements, a recommendation — *recommendation* — that the Kessler finding be vacated and my certification restored in full, with backdated clearance and a formal apology under consideration.

Restored. Cleared. My name, laundered and pressed and handed back to me on official letterhead.

I read it twice, standing at Runa's counter while she pretended to inventory rope. Down the quay, Mara was re-rigging her father's salvage boat, working the splices he'd taught her, and she glanced up at me through the window with a question in her face she was too polite to ask out loud yet.

I folded the letter along its crease, and then again, smaller, and put it in my pocket, and went out to help her rig the boat — because there was a season opening on the northern rivers, and a wreck reported up the coast that nobody local wanted to touch, and for the first time in two years, no one was chasing me anywhere.

But the letter rode in my pocket like a stone, and I hadn't decided yet what it was worth, or whether it was worth anything at all — and Mara, watching me across the deck with her father's rope running smooth through her hands, was waiting to find out.
