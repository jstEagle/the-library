## 22. Moving Water

The morning they left Skarnvik, the tide came in gentle as an apology.

Gideon stood at the harbor wall with her pack at her feet and watched the water climb back over the rocks that had spent a decade bared to the air — the same rocks that had given up the cave, the satchel, the journal, the dead. The sea was taking them all back now, folding its gray-green sleeve over the evidence, over the legend, over the place where her old life had finally finished drowning. It looked like forgiveness. She knew better than to trust that. Water didn't forgive; it just kept moving.

"You're doing the thing," Mara said beside her.

"What thing?"

"The standing-and-staring-at-the-water thing. You've done it every morning for two weeks. Torv says it's a guide habit. He says you're reading the river even when there isn't one."

"Torv talks too much for a man who's supposed to be lying low."

"Torv is in a ranger station forty miles from here being debriefed by people with clipboards. He can talk as much as he wants." Mara bumped her shoulder against Gideon's arm. "Besides, he earned it."

He had. They'd all heard the testimony read aloud in the town hall — Torv's flat, unflinching account of what Vey's men had done on the ice, what Vey himself had ordered, what the source was worth and how far he'd been willing to go to own it alone. Gideon had sat in the third row and listened to her own name come out of strangers' mouths: *the guide who found it, the outsider who brought it back.* Not *the guide who lost a client.* Not *the woman who got people killed.* She still hadn't decided how she felt about that. Some mornings it felt like a coat that fit. Most mornings it felt like a coat she hadn't agreed to wear.

Below them, the harbor was waking. Fishing boats coughed diesel and swung out past the breakwater. On the quay, men were loading crates marked with the rangers' seal — evidence going south, to courts Gideon would never see. Vey's ships sat idle at their moorings, impounded, their paint already dulling. His house on the headland stood empty behind its iron fence, windows blank as drowned eyes. There was talk of auctioning it. There was talk of a lot of things. Skarnvik had discovered that grief and greed made the same noise when a town talked, and it couldn't stop talking.

"They voted yesterday," Mara said. "You know that."

"I heard."

"The memorial. For the expedition. All of them — Grandfather, the others, Papa." Her voice did the small hitch it still did around her father's name, then steadied. "They want your name on the plaque too. And mine."

Gideon turned from the water. Mara was watching her with that look she'd learned on the river — patient, level, waiting out the rapid instead of fighting it.

"It's not our names they should carve," Gideon said. "It's theirs. We just carried the paper."

"We carried each other, too. That counts for something where I'm from."

"Where are you from, exactly? Because I've been trying to figure out if I'm allowed to say you're from Skarnvik or if that's a fight."

Mara smiled — slow, crooked, the smile that had gotten Gideon through the worst night of the ice tunnel, when the roof had groaned like a living thing and the only thing holding either of them together was the sound of the other one breathing. "I'm from wherever my boat is," she said. "Same as you."

* * *

They left without ceremony, because ceremonies were for towns and they were done with towns for a while.

Mara's father's salvage boat — hers now, though she still caught herself calling it his — sat low in the water with gear lashed under tarps: ropes, pitons, the patched raft rolled tight, two months of food, a stove that lit on the third try if you asked it nicely. Gideon had sold almost nothing to fund it. That was the strange part, the part she kept turning over like a stone in her pocket: for the first time since the accident, since the blacklist, since the long gray years of taking contracts she hated in places she couldn't name, she wasn't running from anything. And she wasn't running toward anything either. There was no contract. No client. No deadline except the seasons, no reputation to protect except the one she could live inside.

Just a river and a choice. She'd said that to Mara three nights ago, sitting on the workshop steps while the town argued itself hoarse about Vey's empire, and Mara had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Then let's pick one before we talk ourselves out of it."

So: north and east, up the coast to the mouth of the Kestrel, the river that fed the fjord country nobody mapped properly. The glacier would be calving all summer — dangerous, loud, alive — but the high passes would be open by late July, and there were tributaries on the far side of the ice that no salvage contract had ever mentioned because no one had ever needed anything from them. Water that ran for its own reasons. Gideon wanted to see it. Not to guide anyone through it. Not to prove anything. Just to see it.

"You have the journal copy?" Mara asked, throwing off the bow line.

"In the dry bag. Originals went to the magistrates. This one goes—" Gideon paused. "Actually, I don't know where this one goes. Maybe nowhere. Maybe it's just ours now."

"Ours." Mara tried the word out, weighing it. "Huh."

"Too much?"

"No." She stepped aboard, sure-footed on the wet deck in a way that still made something loosen in Gideon's chest. "Not enough, maybe. But we've got a whole river to work on it."

* * *

They cleared the breakwater at slack tide and turned north with the flood.

For the first hour neither of them spoke much. That was the thing about the weeks they'd spent together in the ice — they'd burned through the need to fill silence. Gideon steered; Mara worked the chart and the tide tables, her father's habits riding her hands even as she corrected his figures with her own. The coastline unspooled: black basalt, green meadows stitched with streams, snow still lying in the high couloirs like linen left out to bleach. Somewhere behind them Skarnvik shrank into its fjord and became, gradually, a story they had both been in.

Around noon the wind came up fair and Mara killed the engine and they sailed, the boat leaning easy, spray coming off the bow in cold bright sheets. Gideon found herself laughing at nothing — at the gull pacing them, at the way Mara's hat kept trying to escape, at the sheer animal fact of moving fast through open water with nowhere to be.

"What?" Mara called over the wind.

"Nothing. I just realized I haven't been scared in about six hours. Might be a record."

"Don't jinx it. We haven't seen the rapids yet."

"There aren't any rapids. We're in a boat. In the sea."

"There are always rapids. You taught me that. Sometimes they're just shaped like weather, or people, or your own stupid decisions." Mara grinned. "You also taught me that the trick isn't avoiding them. It's reading them early enough to pick your line."

Gideon looked at her — salt in her dark hair, sunburn starting across her nose, her hands easy on the sheet — and felt the full weight of what the river had given them settle somewhere below her ribs. On the ice, in the tunnel, in the water, they had become the kind of team that didn't need words. That was rare. Gideon had guided for fifteen years and could count on one hand the partners she'd trusted with a rope. Two of them were dead. One had testified against her. And then there was Mara, who had started as a debt-bound girl hiring a disgraced stranger for a job neither of them understood, and who had ended as—

As whatever this was. Gideon still didn't have a word for it. She suspected the word existed somewhere upstream, and that they'd find it or they wouldn't, and that either way the finding was the point.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a cove on the mainland shore, a pocket beach below a waterfall that fell two hundred feet off a hanging valley and hit the tidewater in a roar of white noise. Gideon built a fire out of driftwood while Mara rigged the tarp, and they ate smoked fish and hard bread and drank tea that tasted faintly of the tin, and none of it — none of it — cost anything.

That was the part Gideon kept circling back to, lying awake later under the tarp with the waterfall thundering and Mara's shoulder warm against hers. Everything in her old life had been transactional. Contracts, fees, deposits, liability waivers. Every kindness priced, every rescue billed, every failure invoiced. Even her disgrace had come with paperwork. She had spent years believing that was just what the world was — a market with weather in it — and then a girl with her father's debts had handed her a rope in a gorge and asked for nothing except the truth, and the whole architecture had quietly collapsed.

Stopping, for once, cost nothing. That was the revelation. She could sit by a fire with a woman she loved and a cup of bad tea and owe no one, answer to no one, and the world would keep turning. The mountains didn't require her to be useful. The river didn't require her to be redeemed. Those had been other people's ledgers — Vey's, the guild's, the clients', her own — and she had finally, at thirty-eight years old, closed the book on them.

"Mara," she said into the dark. "You awake?"

A pause. "Define awake."

"Fair." Gideon shifted, looking up through the gap in the tarp at stars crowding the sky above the waterfall's mist. "When we get up the Kestrel. Past the glacier. When there's nothing left to run and nothing left to find. What then? What do you want?"

She felt Mara turn toward her in the dark. "Nobody's ever asked me that. My father asked what I'd do with the business. Vey asked what I'd do for the debt. You're the first person who asked what I *want*."

"So?"

Another pause, longer. The waterfall filled it.

"I want to learn the rivers up there," Mara said finally. "All of them. I want to put them on maps — real ones, honest ones, the kind the rangers use. I want a boat big enough for winter and small enough to portage. I want—" She stopped, and Gideon heard her take a breath, the way she did before rapids. "I want to stop bracing. Every day since Papa died, some part of me has been waiting for the next blow. Up there, I think it might finally stop. That's what I want. To stop bracing."

"Yeah," Gideon said quietly. "That."

"And you?" Mara's hand found hers under the wool blanket. "What does Gideon Blackwood want, now that nobody's paying her?"

Gideon considered the question honestly, the way she'd consider a route — testing holds, checking for hollow rock. What did she want?

"To be good at something again," she said. "Not famous for it. Not paid for it. Just good. I want to read water and be right. I want to lead through the tunnel and have everyone come out the other side. I want—" She laughed softly at herself. "I want to stand at the source of something and have it not mean money or murder. Just mean *beginning*."

"That's not a plan. That's a prayer."

"Up there, those might be the same thing."

Mara squeezed her hand. "Then we'll pray with good rope," she said, and rolled closer, and the waterfall roared them both to sleep.

* * *

They reached the mouth of the Kestrel on the fourth day.

It came out of the mountains in a braided silver fan, cold and violent and utterly indifferent to them, carrying rock flour down from the ice in curtains of pale green. Gideon stood in the bow as they crossed the seam where sea met river — the line visible in the water, a rippling border between two worlds — and felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked so long she'd stopped noticing it.

There was no sign here. No ranger station, no checkpoint, no permit required. No legend forbidding entry, because no one had bothered to make one. The drowned expedition had never come this way; Vey had never bought this ground; no one had died here and no one had lied about it. The river simply ran, from the ice to the sea, doing the oldest work in the world.

"Line looks clean," Mara said at her shoulder, reading the current the way Gideon had taught her — the V's pointing downstream, the eddies tucked behind the boulders, the tongue of smooth water threading the middle like a path someone had swept. "We could push up on the flood tide, make the first gorge by dark."

"We could." Gideon didn't move. "Or we could anchor in the cove tonight and run it fresh tomorrow. Nobody's chasing us. Nothing's closing."

Mara looked at her, and then at the river, and Gideon watched her do the arithmetic she herself had done a hundred times on a hundred desperate routes — and then watch her set it down. Let it go. Stand in the bow of an idle boat with no clock anywhere in the world.

"Fresh tomorrow," Mara agreed. "God. Listen to us. Being *sensible*."

"Don't tell Torv. He'll think we're dying."

They anchored in the cove beneath the river's fan, and ate, and watched the light go gold and then rose and then blue-black over the icefields inland, where the source lay sealed under a summer of calving, where Torv was marking the route for the survey teams, where everything had happened that needed to happen. Tomorrow they would start upriver. It would be hard — rivers like this were always hard — and they would be good at it, and that would be enough.

Gideon took out the journal copy one last time and held it over the stove. The waterproof pages were warped, stained, the ink of the dead men ghosted with damp. She thought about keeping it. She thought about burying it at the source, returning it to the ice. Then she fed it page by page into the fire and watched the drowned expedition finally finish dying — gently, in warmth, witnessed — until only ash remained, and she stirred that into the coals and let the tide take the rest.

"What was that for?" Mara asked softly.

"So it belongs to us now," Gideon said. "Not the story. Just the river."

Outside, the Kestrel ran on in the dark, making its own map, needing no one. Gideon stood at the rail a long time, listening to it — the sound she'd followed her whole life, the sound that had nearly destroyed her and had, against all odds, put her back together — and for once she wasn't reading it for danger, or profit, or a way through.

She was just listening. And the water moved, and moved, and asked nothing, and gave everything, and she stood there in the moving dark beside the woman she loved, at the beginning of a river with no name on any contract, and found — quietly, completely, for the first time in her life — that stopping cost her nothing at all.

Tomorrow they'd go up.
