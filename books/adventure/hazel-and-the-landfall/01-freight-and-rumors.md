## 1. Freight and Rumors

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Sebastian Yarrow used every minute of the grace period, loading crates of penicillin and rehydration salts into the Cessna's belly while the airfield still slept. His hands knew the work without him — the way they always did — sliding each crate into the tie-down netting, testing the weight distribution by feel alone, the way a man tests a rope bridge with one foot before trusting it with all his weight.

Kestrel Field didn't deserve the name. It was a scraped rectangle of coral gravel at the edge of a mangrove delta, one hangar whose roof sheeting had been half carried off in last year's cyclone, a fuel bowser that leaked more than it dispensed, and a windsock faded to the color of old bone. The strip belonged to him now, or he to it — three years since old Manu Kestrel had died owing the bank more than the bank wanted to admit, and Sebastian had signed for the debt because the alternative was leaving, and leaving had never once worked out for him.

He cinched the last strap and stood in the gray light, breathing the smell of wet gravel and avgas and rotting vegetation, and felt the familiar restlessness settle over him like a second skin. Somewhere out past the tree line the river was running high with the season's first real rains, brown and muscular, carrying the interior down toward the sea. Everything here moved. Nothing stayed.

"Yarrow." The voice came from the direction of the fuel shed, where Toma Lefanu sat on an upturned drum as he sat on it most mornings, drinking tea from a tin mug and watching the world fail to improve. He was seventy if he was a day, built like driftwood, and he was the closest thing Kestrel Field had to a population. "You're flying early."

"Medicine run. Sera Station, upriver." Sebastian wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth. "Fever outbreak. They need the antibiotics before noon or not at all."

Toma grunted, which from Toma was a paragraph. Then, after a silence long enough for a heron to cross the strip: "You hear about the Vane money?"

Sebastian did not sigh, though it was a near thing. "I heard about it Tuesday. And Friday. And the week before that."

"The man who bought the mine claim came through on the coastal steamer." Toma took his time with the tea, savoring whatever he was about to say the way other men savored tobacco. "Crane, his name is. Aldous Crane. Wearing white shoes, if you can believe it, in this country. Asking questions about the old Vane operation like he'd never heard a word of its history, which nobody believes for a second."

"There's no history worth hearing," Sebastian said. "That's the whole point of the story. If there were ever any money, it went into the ground with the mine sixty years ago."

"That's what people say." Toma set the mug down with the care of a man laying a card face-up. "What people say is that Elias Vane found copper in those cliffs north of the drowned coast, enough to buy the whole territory twice over, and that his partner bled it dry behind his back and let the sea have the workings when the shafts flooded. What people say is that the proof of it — the ledgers, the letters, whatever a man keeps to prove he was robbed — went into a sea cave below the cliffs, sealed up with the tide, waiting." He smiled with maybe four teeth. "People say a lot of things."

"People say the river fish walk on land during the dry season, too."

"They do say that." Toma nodded seriously. "And yet you never see them try it, do you? Because the ones who'd know are dead, and the ones who are alive only know what they've been told." He picked the mug back up. "This Crane fellow chartered nothing, booked nothing, asked for no plane. But he wrote down the name of every pilot on this coast, Yarrow. Including yours. I watched him do it."

Sebastian looked at the Cessna, at the medicine crates, at the ordinary, honest freight of a morning that had been simple twenty minutes ago. "Maybe he's shopping for charters."

"Maybe." Toma drank. "Or maybe a man doesn't write down names unless he's planning to need them."

* * *

Sera Station was a mission outpost on a bluff above the river, a cluster of tin-roofed buildings that appeared out of the haze like something remembered rather than seen. Sister Agneta met him on the strip with two orderlies, her habit gray with dust, her hands already reaching for the crates before the propeller had fully stopped.

"You flew fast," she said.

"I flew early. Weather's building."

She glanced west, where the sky over the interior had gone the color of a bruise coming up, towering cumulus stacked in ranks like a fleet at anchor. The monsoon wasn't due for another ten days by the almanac, but almanacs were written by optimists, and the air had that heavy, expectant weight it got before the season turned for good. "You'll make it back before it breaks?"

"I'll make it back before lunch."

She signed his manifest, counted the crates twice, and then paused, pen hovering. "One more thing, Mr. Yarrow. A passenger came through yesterday asking after transport down to the coast. A gentleman. He offered to pay triple your rate."

"Triple." Sebastian kept his voice level. "For a medicine run?"

"Not a medicine run. Down to the drowned coast. He said he was surveying mining claims." She handed back the manifest, and her eyes, mild as milk, held his a moment longer than courtesy required. "He gave a name. Crane. I told him our aircraft arrangements were made through Kestrel Field. I hope that was right."

"It was right," Sebastian said, and heard how flat his own voice came out.

He took off into thickening air, climbing through air that shrugged against the wings like a living thing, and turned south along the river toward home. Below him the country unrolled — green going silver where the flooded flats caught the light, the river braiding and rebraiding itself, patient as arithmetic. This was the part of flying he had never learned to be tired of: the moment the wheels left and the world became geometry, and everything that weighed on a man on the ground — the debt, the leaking bowser, the hangar roof, the fact that he ate dinner alone at the field most nights because there was no one to eat it with — all of it shrank to scale, manageable, a map's problem rather than a life's.

Loneliness was easier to carry at altitude. That was the truth of it, the one he didn't say even to himself on the bad days. Up here he wasn't a debtor or an orphan of other people's families; he was competence itself, a man alone in a machine doing exactly what it was built to do, and the country below needed him in a way that required nothing of him but skill. Skill he had. It was the rest that had never quite come together.

He was forty minutes out from Kestrel Field when the radio crackled.

"Kestrel One, Kestrel One, this is Crane." The voice was smooth as varnish, pitched to carry confidence, the accent of someone educated at a distance from weather. "I'm told you're the reliable man on this coast. I need a charter tomorrow, coastal run, my own survey work. Name your figure."

Sebastian keyed the mic and watched the storm line crawling in off the western hills. "Weather's turning. Window's closing."

"The window closes the day after tomorrow, then. I'm flexible." A pause, silk over wire. "I understand you fly the drowned coast. I'm told the tides there have been behaving strangely this season. Unusually low. Exposing things." Another pause, shorter, and when the voice came again it had dropped its polish by a degree, which was somehow worse. "There's a cave system in the cliffs north of the old Vane landing. I want a look at it from the air. That's all. An hour of your time, Mr. Yarrow, at triple rate."

Every instinct Sebastian owned — the ones that had kept him alive through twelve years of marginal strips and monsoon margins — said *no*. Triple rate meant desperation dressed as generosity. A stranger who collected pilots' names meant purpose. And *exposing things*, said in that voice, meant the man already knew exactly what he was looking for.

But the bowser needed rebuilding. The hangar roof needed replacing before the monsoon peeled the rest of it off. The bank's letter sat in the drawer under the radio, third notice, polite the way collection letters learn to be polite. And the truth underneath all of it, the one he kept in the same drawer: a full week of Crane's money would clear the year's worst of it, and a man who owed nothing could finally choose where to fly instead of being chosen for.

"One condition," Sebastian said. "We fly at first light, ahead of the weather, and we're back on the ground by nine. If the ceiling drops, we turn around, no argument, fee stands."

"Done," said Crane, so quickly it sounded rehearsed. "First light. There's a landing at the old Vane wharf — you know it?"

"I know it."

"Wonderful." The varnish came back, warm as lamplight. "Until tomorrow, Mr. Yarrow. Sleep well."

The radio clicked off, and Sebastian flew the last miles home in silence, the storm line growing behind him like a wall being built by patient hands. At Kestrel Field, Toma was waiting where Toma was always waiting, and read the answer off Sebastian's face before his wheels had stopped rolling.

"You took it," Toma said.

"I took it."

Toma looked at the western sky for a long time, at the light going strange and yellow-green above the mangroves, the color the country turned when it was about to take something back. "My grandmother used to say the drowned coast keeps its accounts," he said. "Everything the sea takes, it writes down. And someday it sends a bill." He stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and shuffled toward the fuel shed. "Triple rate, was it?"

"Triple rate."

"Then charge him for the fuel too," Toma said. "Men like that always find a reason not to pay."

Sebastian tied down the Cessna against the coming wind, checked the knots twice, and stood a while in the rising breeze, listening to the mangroves hiss. Tomorrow, first light, he would fly a stranger along a coast he'd crossed a hundred times, to look at a cave everyone had a story about and no one had ever seen.

Above the tree line, the first thunder of the season rolled inland, long and low, like something enormous clearing its throat.
