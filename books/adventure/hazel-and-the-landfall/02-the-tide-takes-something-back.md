## 2. The Tide Takes Something Back

The coast came up out of the grey like the spine of something drowned.

Sebastian Yarrow had flown this stretch a hundred times in better weather — the long scalloped reach between Port Meridian and the river mouths, cliffs the color of wet iron, forest pressing down to the very lip of the rock as if the land itself were leaning out to look at the sea. In fair light it was beautiful. Today it was not trying to be. The monsoon was still two days south by every forecast he'd read, but its outriders were already here: ragged cloud dragging its belly along the headlands, rain that came in slantwise sheets and then stopped altogether, as if the sky couldn't make up its mind.

He flew low because the charter paid by the mile and the fuel gauge paid him back in worry. The Cessna 180 — *Wren*, painted on her tail in letters some previous owner had never bothered to renew — bucked and wallowed through the gusts with the patience of an old dog being dragged past a butcher's shop. She was thirty-one years old and had opinions about turbulence, all of which she shared freely through the airframe.

"Easy," he told her, the way he talked to her when there was no one else to talk to. "Easy, girl."

Behind him, lashed into the cargo bay under netting: two crates of antibiotics, a box of veterinary supplies for the Kessler station upriver, mail, a carburetor somebody had ordered six weeks ago and would complain about receiving late. And one item that didn't fit any manifest category — a sealed wooden case, stenciled with a name that had been needling at him since Port Meridian.

**A. CRANE. MERIDIAN MINING & LANDFALL ESTATES.**

Aldous Crane had come to the airfield himself, which was unusual. Men like Crane sent drivers. This one had arrived in a white Land Cruiser that cost more than Sebastian's entire operation, stepped out in a linen shirt that had never seen labor, and looked around the strip — the sagging hangar, the fuel drums rusting on their pallets, the windsock faded to the pink of an old scar — with the expression of a man pricing up a demolition.

"Yarrow." He'd said it like he'd rehearsed it. "I'm told you'll fly anything anywhere. Is that true?"

"I fly things I can land," Sebastian had said. "That's most places."

Crane smiled as if Sebastian had passed a test he didn't know he was taking. The crate went into the Wren's belly before Sebastian could ask what was in it. He asked anyway. Crane had looked out at the water and said, lightly, "Family papers. Nothing you'd want to read." Then, turning back, that smile again: "You know the Vane country? North coast?"

"Nobody lives there."

"Not anymore." Something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "Fly wide of the cliffs if the sea's dropped strange. Old ground there. Unstable."

And now here Sebastian was, flying exactly along those cliffs, because the direct route saved eleven minutes and eleven minutes mattered when your engine was older than your career. He thought about Crane's warning the way you think about a splinter — small, irritating, impossible to leave alone.

*Old ground. Unstable.*

The rain quit. For perhaps ninety seconds the whole coast stood revealed in flat, sourceless light, and that was when Sebastian saw it.

At first he took it for a shadow. A black seam in the cliff face where the rock fell away — but shadows don't have depth, don't have *interior*, don't catch the light on wet stone forty feet back inside themselves. He banked without deciding to, the Wren tilting her wing toward the sea, and looked down at a thing that should not have existed.

An opening. Enormous. A cave mouth yawning in the base of the cliffs, tall enough to fly a plane through standing on its wingtip, and dry — dry above a sea that should have been filling it twice a day for ten thousand years. The tide below was wrong too. He knew this coast's tides the way he knew his own pulse, and this was no tide he knew. The water lay far out from the rocks, exposing shelves and boulders he'd never seen, gleaming brown and green like the bones of the world left out after a flood. An anomalous low. The kind of thing that happened once in a generation, maybe once in a lifetime, and only ever in stories fishermen told each other over cheap rum.

The cave breathed. That was the word his mind offered and refused to take back. The dark inside it seemed to move, slow and tidal, as though the cliff were inhaling.

Sebastian felt something lift in his chest that he hadn't felt in years — not since he was nineteen and first solo, that pure animal astonishment at being alive in the air. *Awe*, the word was. He'd forgotten the word existed. There was a place down there that the sea had kept secret, and the sea had just, for one hour of one afternoon, shown it to him alone.

Then the engine coughed.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the horror of it — how quiet it was. A hesitation, a stumble in the rhythm, like a heartbeat skipping. The propeller's drone faltered, caught, faltered again. Sebastian's hands were already moving before his fear caught up: mixture rich, carb heat, fuel selector, boost pump. Twenty-two years of habit running the checklist while the part of him that was still a person watched the oil pressure needle slide left like a man walking off a pier.

"No," he said. "No, no — come on, girl, not here—"

The Wren answered with a bang and a shudder, and then a thin gray ribbon of smoke unwound from the cowl and tore away behind them. The smell came into the cockpit a second later: hot metal and scorched oil, the smell every pilot knows and prays never to know from inside.

He was at four hundred feet, half a mile off a coast made entirely of vertical rock, carrying a stranger's sealed crate and a cargo of other people's medicine, and his engine was dying.

The training took over and he let it. Best place to land: nowhere good. Second best: the flattest thing available. The anomalous tide had laid bare a shelf of sand and shingle below the cliffs — narrow, littered with exposed reef, but flat, and flat was the only currency that mattered now. He turned downwind, then base, then final, flying the dead-stick approach with the smoothness that twenty-two years buys, throttle useless at idle, trimming her nose-down, watching the shelf grow in the windshield with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own sentence.

Wind fifteen knots, quartering. Swell breaking white on the outer reef. The shelf ran maybe three hundred yards between black walls of stone.

"You can do this," he said aloud, to the plane or himself or both. "Come on. Come on."

Two hundred feet. The stall horn sang its thin note. One hundred. The cliffs reared on either side like the walls of a throat. Fifty — and the wind shifted, shoved the left wing down, and Sebastian kicked the rudder and caught it, barely, the Wren slewing sideways over the sand—

The main gear hit hard. Too hard. A crack ran up through the airframe like a rifle shot, the right gear folding, and then the world became noise and violence and the specific, unforgettable chaos of a crash: sand hammering the windshield, the propeller chewing itself into shrapnel somewhere ahead, the fuselage slewing, the reef rushing up—

He threw his arms across his face.

The impact, when it came, was almost gentle compared to what he'd braced for. The Wren's nose dug into soft shingle above the waterline and the tail swung and settled, and then there was silence except for the sea, and the tick of cooling metal, and Sebastian Yarrow breathing like a man hauled out of deep water.

He sat there for a full minute, hands still crossed over his face, taking inventory. Fingers. Toes. Ribs — bruised, probably cracked, one of them, on the left, where the harness had done its job. Blood on his knuckles. Nothing bent that wouldn't heal straight.

Then he climbed out, because the fuel line might be intact and intact lines burn.

The Wren lay on her belly like a shot bird, right gear folded under her, propeller reduced to a single bent blade, cowling peeled back to show an engine compartment full of blackened ruin. Whatever had failed had failed completely and finally. Sebastian walked around her twice, touching her here and there — the warm aluminum, the torn fabric of the tail — the way you'd touch a hurt animal, apologizing.

"There she is," said a voice in his head that sounded like his old instructor, dead nine years now. "There's your fortune, Yarrow. One airplane, total loss. Insurance lapsed three weeks ago."

He laughed, which surprised him. It came out wrong, half sob, and he sat down on the shingle next to his dying plane and let the laughter run itself out against the sound of the surf.

* * *

The light was going. That was the fact he kept circling and refusing to land on. The anomalous tide was already turning — he could see it, the water crawling back up the exposed shelves with a patience that felt deliberate, reclaiming inch by inch everything it had lent the world. By morning the cave mouth would be drowned again. By morning, if anyone ever asked, there would be nothing to show for what he'd seen.

He got the survival kit out of the wreck: flares, a tarp, a first-aid tin, a machete, two liters of water, a signal mirror, a waterproof bag with a radio that had been unreliable since the Carter administration. He got the crates out too — the medicine, the mail, the carburetor. The vet supplies. He stood looking at Crane's wooden case for a long moment, water lapping closer to its stenciled name.

*Family papers. Nothing you'd want to read.*

He should open it. Every instinct he owned said a man who crashes with a sealed crate has the right — maybe the duty — to know what he nearly died carrying. But the tide was coming and his ribs were singing and the cliffs were going dark, and so he dragged the case above the wrack line instead, wedged it under an overhang, and told himself he'd decide tomorrow.

Tomorrow. He almost laughed again. Tomorrow he was marooned on a stretch of coast that didn't appear on his charts as anything but a hazard symbol, with a broken radio, a broken body, and a broken airplane, waiting out the leading edge of a monsoon.

He built what shelter he could — tarp strung between two fallen rocks, kit piled inside, the Wren's emergency blanket wrapped around his shoulders. He ate half a ration bar and drank a careful mouthful of water. The rain started again around full dark, soft at first, then with intent.

And that was when he saw the light.

Not lightning. Not a flare. A steady yellow point, far down the coast to the north, bobbing slightly — moving along the base of the cliffs where no road ran and no house stood, moving the way a lantern moves in a hand. It vanished behind a headland. Reappeared. Closer.

Someone was out there. On foot, or by boat, in this weather, on this drowned shore, heading toward the wreck of his plane the way water finds a hole.

Sebastian killed his own small light and sat in the dark with his back against the warm dead metal of the Wren, listening to the sea climb the shingle, and watched the little yellow flame come on through the rain — and understood, with a certainty that needed no evidence, that whatever was happening on this coast had begun long before his engine failed, and that he had not crashed into a story.

He had been written into one.
