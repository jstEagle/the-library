## 3. The Woman Who Pulled Him Out

The sea gave him back in pieces.

Hazel saw the first piece from the boathouse window: a dark shape tumbling in the surf where no shape should be, rolling with the rhythm of something that had stopped fighting. She'd been watching the water all morning anyway — watching it the way you watch a wound, to see if it's still bleeding — because the tide was wrong again. Third day running. The charts on her grandfather's wall said one thing; the ocean outside her door said another, and it kept saying it, pulling back farther than it had any right to go, baring ribs of black rock that hadn't seen light since before she was born.

She was down the shingle path before she'd decided to move, boots skidding on wet stone, salt wind flattening her hair against her face. The shape came in on a wave and she went in after it, up to her thighs, the cold punching the breath out of her, and caught a fistful of oilskin jacket as the sea tried to take its prize back out.

He was heavy the way drowned men are heavy, all his weight surrendered at once, and she hauled him through the shallows by main strength, cursing, heels digging trenches in the sand. Up past the wrack line, onto the stones. He coughed seawater into the shingle and groaned, which she took as a good sign. Dead men don't complain.

"Come on," she told him, dragging him higher. "Come on, then. You're not dying on my beach. I haven't got the paperwork for it."

* * *

The boathouse had been her grandfather's pride once, or so the photographs claimed — a long low building of silvered timber with doors wide enough to swallow a launch whole. Now it swallowed only rust and rot and Hazel herself, three years alone on the point since the last of the family lawyers stopped writing. She got the pilot onto the old workbench, stripped of its tools, and cut away his soaked flight jacket with a filleting knife.

He was maybe thirty-five, lean in the way of men who work with their bodies and eat when they remember to, a fresh gash over one eyebrow, hands rope-burned and knuckles split. A pilot's wings stitched inside his collar — cheap thread, real wings. She worked while he shivered, getting the wet off him, wrapping him in the wool blanket that still smelled faintly of engine grease because everything in this building did.

He surfaced toward dusk, eyes finding hers with the sudden sharp focus of a man taking inventory.

"Where—" His voice came out ruined. He coughed, tried again. "Where am I?"

"Vane Point. My point." She held a tin cup to his lips. Rainwater, flat and metallic. "Drink. Slowly."

He drank. Studied her over the rim of the cup with eyes the color of shallow water over sand — a pilot's habit, she thought, reading the ground before committing to it. "Your plane," he said suddenly, and winced. "My plane. The *Kestrel*. Is she—"

"I found no plane. I found you." She set the cup down. "What's your name?"

"Sebastian Yarrow." He said it like a man checking whether the name still fit. "Bush charter, out of Corrigan Field. I was flying a client down the coast and—" He stopped. She watched him reach for the memory and flinch from it. "There was an opening in the cliffs. North of here. A cave. The tide was out impossibly far and there it was, just — open. Like a mouth."

Hazel went very still.

Outside, the wind worried at the loose flashing along the roofline, a sound like someone patiently picking a lock. She made herself move, made herself busy at the little stove, feeding it driftwood, and kept her voice level when she answered.

"Caves open and close along this coast all the time."

"Not like this one." He'd pushed himself up on his elbows, watching her too closely now. "It wasn't a sea arch. It went *in*, deep, with timber framing visible near the entrance — old timber, rotted. And there were rails. Mine rails, running down into the water."

The stove popped. Somewhere under the floorboards the tide knocked against the pilings, patient as a debt collector.

"My grandfather told stories about rails," Hazel said.

Yarrow didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice had changed — quieter, careful, the way men speak when they realize they've wandered onto someone else's land without knowing it. "He was a miner?"

"He was a thief," Hazel said. "Or he was robbed. Depends which version of the story you get, and everyone who could tell me true is dead." She turned to face him fully. "This coast used to belong to the Vane Consolidated Mining Company. Copper, mostly, in the cliffs north of here. My grandfather Elias Vane built half of it and my grandmother's family owned the other half on paper, and between them they took out a fortune that vanished the year the mine flooded. Officially, the sea took it. Unofficially—" She shrugged. "Unofficially, everyone on this coast has spent sixty years repeating a story they don't believe, about ledgers and a confession sealed in a chamber behind the workings, proof of who stole what from whom. My grandfather forbade me to go near those cliffs when I was a girl. Forbade me like it was scripture. You don't forbid a child a place unless the place matters."

"And you think that cave is it."

"I think the sea has been keeping it," Hazel said, "and today it forgot to."

* * *

She hid him an hour after full dark, when the running lights of a boat appeared in the channel.

She heard the engine first — the lazy, throttled-back burble of a vessel moving slow and looking at things — and had Yarrow on his feet and behind the stacked lobster traps in the loft before he'd finished protesting. Through the gap in the boards she watched the boat come on: a hard-chined runabout, new paint, running dark except for a single shielded lamp sweeping the shoreline. It lingered opposite the boathouse. It lingered a long time.

Nobody ran a pleasure boat slow past Vane Point in weather like this. Nobody looked at her shore with a lamp unless they were looking for something. Or someone.

The boat moved on at last, north, toward the cliffs, and Hazel let out a breath she'd been holding since the surf.

"You can come down," she said.

Yarrow descended the ladder stiffly, favoring ribs she suspected were cracked. He didn't ask why she'd hidden him. He was smarter than he looked, or the sea had knocked the questions out of him and left only the answers.

"That boat will be back," she said instead. "Boats like that are never lost. They're sent." She poured coffee from the dented pot — real coffee, hoarded, the last luxury of a woman who'd stopped needing most things. "So we're going to have this conversation properly, Mr. Yarrow, because I think you've been lying to me by omission, and I've been doing the same, and neither of us can afford it anymore."

He wrapped his burned hands around the cup. "Fair enough."

"Who chartered your flight?"

"Aldous Crane." He said the name flatly, but she saw it land somewhere in him, some knowledge he'd been carrying since the crash finally finding its weight. "Representative of the Meridian Recovery Group. They hold the mineral claims on this stretch of coast now — bought up everything after the mine failed. Crane hired me to fly him down the coastline for a survey. Then he asked me to fly a second leg alone, a package delivery to a cove south of your point. Weather turned. Engine quit. I put her down in the shallows and the sea did the rest."

"And Crane?"

"Wasn't on the second leg." Yarrow looked into his coffee. "Which I've been telling myself means nothing. But I've been flying freight and rumors out of Corrigan Field for six years, Ms. Vane, and I know what it feels like when a man pays you to be somewhere you weren't meant to survive being."

The fire cracked. Down beneath the floorboards, the tide had begun its long withdrawal again, sucking at the pilings, going out farther than the charts allowed, going out toward the cliffs and the mouth it had opened and might close again at any hour.

Hazel sat down across from him. Three years of solitude sat down with her, and her grandfather's forbidden stories, and every night she'd spent telling herself the inheritance didn't matter because there was nothing left to inherit but ruin.

Here was what she knew: a stranger had fallen out of the sky carrying the name Aldous Crane, and washed ashore within sight of the one place on earth that could prove what her family had been — thieves or victims, guilty or robbed — and the sea had opened a door it had kept shut for sixty years, and someone in a fast boat was already looking for him.

Here was what she decided: she was done waiting for permission from dead men.

"The cave entrance won't stay open," she said. "Tides like this come maybe twice in a generation, and the monsoon's three days out at most. After that, that door seals until the next anomaly, and I'll be dead before it opens again." She leaned forward. "You need a way off this coast, Mr. Yarrow, and I need to get into that rock. You're the first person in three years to wash up on my shore, and I intend to make use of you."

Something moved behind his tired eyes — wariness, calculation, and underneath both, unmistakably, relief. The look of a man offered a rope in deep water.

"What exactly," Sebastian Yarrow said slowly, "are you proposing?"

Hazel smiled, and was surprised at how easily it came.

"A partnership," she said. "You help me find what my grandfather buried. I'll help you find out why Aldous Crane wanted you drowned."
