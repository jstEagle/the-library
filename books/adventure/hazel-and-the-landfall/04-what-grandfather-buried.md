## 4. What Grandfather Buried

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rain, and Hazel had learned, over the years, to read its moods the way other people read weather. Tonight it was sulking — the tin roof ticking as the first outriders of the monsoon tested themselves against it, the tide breathing in and out beneath the floorboards like something asleep and dreaming badly.

She set a mug of coffee in front of Sebastian Yarrow without asking whether he wanted it. He'd earned the coffee. He'd also earned the questions, but she wasn't ready to spend those yet.

He sat wrapped in her father's old canvas jacket, hands around the mug, watching her with the particular patience of a man who had spent his life waiting on weather. His plane was somewhere out there under the grey water, folded into the shallows like a paper bird someone had stepped on. She'd seen him look toward the window four times since sundown, each time with the same expression: not grief exactly, but the hollow arithmetic of a man totaling what he'd lost and what he still owed.

"You keep looking at the water," she said.

"Old habit." He turned the mug a quarter-turn. "You count what you've got before the storm takes inventory for you."

"And what have you got?"

"One charter contract. A debt at Meridian Field that grows interest while I sit here drinking your coffee. And—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to say it, "—a memory I can't make sense of."

"The cave."

"The cave." He said it carefully, the way a man handles a bone he suspects might be human. "I've flown this coast eleven years, Ms. Vane. Eleven years, and I know every notch in these cliffs the way you know the lines of your own hand. That opening wasn't there yesterday. It isn't *supposed* to be there. The charts show solid rock from Point Serrate down to the drowned quarry — forty meters of limestone that hasn't opened its mouth since—"

"Since 1949," Hazel said.

Sebastian went very still. It was a small thing, the way he stopped — no flinch, no dramatics — just a sudden total attention, like an engine finding its idle. "You know the date."

"I know a lot of dates." She pulled the second chair out from under the workbench, the one with the wobbly leg her grandfather had shimmed with a folded chart of the very coastline they were sitting above, and sat down across from him. "But if we're going to do this, pilot, we're going to do it properly. My name is Hazel Vane. Not Ms. Vane. And what I'm about to tell you, I've told exactly two people in my life, both of them dead, one of them by choice."

Outside, the wind found its voice — a low moan through the gap where the boathouse met the cliff, the sound her grandmother used to say was the mine sighing in its sleep. Hazel had grown up believing that was nonsense. She was no longer sure.

"My grandfather was Elias Vane," she began. "You may not know the name. Most people don't anymore. But sixty years ago, Vane & Crane Consolidated Tin owned half this coast — the dredges, the smelter at Port Lachlan, the company town, the rail spur, all of it. Two men built it. Elias Vane, who held the leases and knew the ground like a surgeon knows anatomy. And his partner, Marcus Crane, who handled the money."

Something moved behind Sebastian's eyes. It was brief — a flicker, quickly banked — but Hazel had spent years learning to read men who were deciding how much truth they could afford, and she caught it.

"Crane," he said.

"Marcus Crane's grandson is Aldous Crane," she said. "Who currently holds the consolidated mineral claims to everything my family lost. Who lives in Singapore most of the year and comes back once annually to inspect what he owns and pay what taxes he must. You may have heard of him."

"I haven't just heard of him." Sebastian set the mug down. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice and listening for the crack. "Aldous Crane chartered my flight. Yesterday morning. At Meridian Field. Cash in advance, which should have been my first warning, because nobody pays cash in advance for a medicine run into the interior unless they want the flight unrecorded."

Hazel felt the floorboards shift under her — or perhaps only her understanding of them. "What did he ask you to carry?"

"Medical supplies, supposedly. Sealed crates, manifest unsigned. And a letter of instruction telling me to fly the coastal route instead of the inland pass — 'for sightseeing,' the note said. To take photographs of the cliffs between Point Serrate and the quarry." Sebastian laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "I thought he was a rich eccentric. Surveying land he already owned. I flew the route twice because he paid me to fly it twice."

"And did you see anything?"

"I saw nothing the first time. The second time—" He stopped. Ran a hand through hair still stiff with salt. "The second time the light was wrong. Low sun, long shadows. And there was a seam in the cliff face that shouldn't have been visible, a darkness below the high-tide line that the charts say doesn't exist. I logged it as an anomaly. I was going to report it to the coastal authority when the weather cleared." He looked up at her. "I never connected it to anything. Until now."

*Neither of us found this place by accident.* The thought arrived in Hazel fully formed, cold and complete, like a fish brought up from deep water. She made herself say it aloud, because saying things aloud was how she kept herself honest.

"Sebastian. Think about what that means. Aldous Crane paid you to photograph a stretch of coast he has owned on paper for thirty years. Why would a man need photographs of his own property?"

"Because the property changed," Sebastian said slowly. "Because something appeared that wasn't on his surveys. Because—" and here his voice dropped, almost to himself, "—because the sea gave something back, and he needed to know what it was before anyone else saw it."

"He knows what's inside," Hazel said. "Or he fears it. Those are the same thing, in our family's story."

* * *

The storm arrived properly around midnight, and Hazel told the rest of it by lamplight while the rain came down in ropes and the boathouse groaned around them like a ship at anchor.

"Elias and Marcus Crane were partners for nineteen years," she said. "My grandfather found the lode — the great strike at Serrate Deep, the one that made the town, that employed three hundred men, that put the name Vane on the smelter and the schoolhouse and the church. But it was Marcus who kept the books. And in 1947, my grandmother Ruth began to notice things. Shipments leaving Port Lachlan heavier than the manifests claimed. Dividends that never arrived. Elias trusted Marcus absolutely — called him brother, though they weren't kin. When Ruth showed Elias her figures, he refused to believe her. Called it jealousy. Called it a wife's arithmetic."

Sebastian listened without interrupting. He had the gift of it, she was realizing — the rare patience of a man whose profession punished impatience with death.

"So Ruth kept her own books," Hazel continued. "Quietly. For two years. And in the spring of 1949, she finished them. Every diverted shipment, every falsified assay, every payment routed through a shell company in Hong Kong — all of it documented, cross-referenced, witnessed. Enough to take the whole enterprise away from Marcus Crane and hand it to the courts. Enough to ruin him."

"But she didn't use it."

"She meant to. They both did. Elias finally believed her — too late, some said, when the shame of having doubted his own wife had curdled into something he couldn't look at directly. They planned to present the evidence to the mining tribunal in June of 1949. And then—" Hazel gestured vaguely upward, toward the cliffs, toward the sea beyond them. "Then came the slide. The Serrate Deep workings flooded. Nine men died. The tribunal inquiry blamed unstable ground, bad timbering, the usual. The mine closed. The company dissolved. And every scrap of paper that could have proven anything — Ruth's ledgers, the correspondence, the confession Marcus Crane signed when Elias confronted him the week before the flood — vanished."

"Vanished," Sebastian repeated. "Or was hidden."

"Hid." The word tasted like iron in her mouth. "That was Ruth's doing. She didn't trust tribunals, and she didn't trust Marcus Crane's reach — he had judges in his pocket, magistrates, port officials. So she took everything and sealed it where no court could subpoena it and no thief could steal it. In the cave. The sea cave below Serrate Cliff, the one the old miners called the Tollhouse, because the tide collected its fee from anyone who entered without knowing the water. She had it blasted shut behind her — or so the story goes — and she carried the knowledge of it to her grave, except for what she told my grandfather, and what he told my mother, and what my mother told me in the kitchen of a house we lost anyway, because the story was all that was left to inherit."

The lamp guttered. Hazel steadied the wick and found Sebastian watching her with an expression she couldn't entirely parse — sympathy tangled with something harder, something like recalculation.

"Your grandmother sealed her own evidence in a tomb," he said. "And named it after a toll collector."

"She was a practical woman. She figured the sea would hold it safer than any lawyer. And she was right for sixty years." Hazel leaned forward. "Until last week, when the tide went out further than it has gone in living memory, and the Tollhouse opened its door."

* * *

They sat in silence after that, the good kind and the bad kind alternating like the pulse of the surf. Sebastian got up once to check the window, and Hazel watched him study the black sheet of rain as though he could read the water's intentions in it.

"It doesn't add up," he said finally, coming back to his chair. "Crane hires me — a bush pilot with debts, no connections, no reason to be loyal to anyone — to fly a photographic survey of exactly the stretch of coast where his grandfather's crimes are buried. Then the cave opens. Then I crash practically on your doorstep, the one person on this coast who knows what the cave means." He shook his head slowly. "Coincidence is a load-bearing wall in my business, Ms.— Hazel. I've flown on worse odds than coincidence. But this is too much weight on one beam."

"You think he chose you."

"I think rich men don't hire strangers. They hire *disposable* men." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "A pilot who crashes surveying his own coastline is a tragedy. An insurance claim. Nobody asks what he saw, because dead men's photographs develop poorly. And if the crash happened close enough to the cave—" He stopped.

"Close enough that you might find it first," Hazel finished. "Or die trying. Either way, useful."

The rain hammered the roof. Somewhere out in the channel, a boat engine coughed and died, coughed again, and faded — too far off to identify, too near to ignore. Hazel marked it and said nothing yet.

"There's one more thing you should know," she said. "Before you decide what kind of trouble you're in. The legend says the cave holds more than ledgers. Ruth's papers prove the theft. But my grandfather's last letter to my mother — the one thing we salvaged — says Marcus Crane confessed in writing. Signed it. Dated it. Admitted everything, including the shell companies and the bribes. That document wouldn't just ruin the Crane name. Under the terms of the original partnership deed, which my family still holds a copy of, proof of fraud voids every transfer made under it. Every claim. Sixty years of Crane ownership, unwound."

Sebastian was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful evenness of a man reading instruments in turbulence.

"Aldous Crane didn't charter me to photograph his land," he said. "He chartered me to find out whether the past had surfaced. And now he knows I survived the crash. And he knows whose doorstep I washed up on."

"He'll come here," Hazel said. "Not with lawyers. Not yet. With charm and an offer, the way Cranes have always done business. And we have maybe two days before the monsoon clears enough for boats to move."

Sebastian looked at her across the lamplight — this stranger he'd known for one day, this woman with her drowned inheritance and her steady, unreadable eyes — and Hazel watched him arrive at the same crossroads she had been standing at for years.

"So we go in first," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"At low tide. Tomorrow night. Before the storm peaks." She stood, and began pulling oilskins and rope from the lockers her grandfather had stocked against a world that never came. "Because whatever else is true, pilot, one thing is certain."

"What's that?"

"Aldous Crane paid for your eyes. And tomorrow night, we're going to show him exactly what they saw."

Out in the channel, the engine coughed again — closer now — and died.
