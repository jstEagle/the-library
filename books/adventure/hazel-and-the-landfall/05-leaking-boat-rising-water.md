## 5. Leaking Boat, Rising Water

The boat was called *Kestrel*, which Sebastian thought was a lie the moment he saw her — nothing about her suggested a bird of prey, or even flight. She was twenty-two feet of weathered cedar and patched fiberglass, her paint gone chalky, her name stenciled over with something older that Hazel had never bothered to sand away. She sat on the shingle below the boathouse like an old dog pretending not to be arthritic.

"She'll hold," Hazel said, though she said it the way people say things they need to be true.

Sebastian ran his thumb along the transom, feeling for soft wood, and found none. That surprised him. Everything else on this coast was rot dressed up as structure. "When did you last take her out?"

"Eight months." Hazel hauled the fuel cans down the stones without breaking stride. "Before the winter she took water in the stern. I fixed it."

"You fixed it."

"I fixed it."

He let it go. It wasn't his boat, and by tomorrow night it wouldn't matter whose it was, because the monsoon was coming down off the headlands like something with intent, and the window to cross the channel to the mainland was closing with every hour. The barometer at the boathouse had been falling all morning, steady as a held breath.

They loaded in silence that had stopped being uncomfortable somewhere around dawn. That was its own kind of strange. Two days ago he'd been surf, coughing salt, half convinced he was dead; now he was handing this woman fuel cans across a gunwale like they'd been doing it for years. She moved like someone who'd grown up alone on a coast that didn't forgive mistakes — economical, watchful, never once turning her back fully on the water.

The cave was four miles north along the cliffs. The tide tables pinned inside the boathouse door — her grandfather's handwriting still on them, faded brown ink — showed a low tide at 3:40 in the morning, two days out. After that, the spring tides turned and the monsoon would own the coast for a week. Which meant they had one shot, and it meant crossing open water ahead of the storm to reach the beach below the cliff path, because Hazel refused to approach the cave mouth by sea.

"My grandfather forbade it," she'd said last night, over tinned fish and a lamp that smoked. "Said the rock remembers who comes by water."

Sebastian didn't believe rocks remembered anything. But he believed in reading weather, and the weather was telling him the same story from three directions: go now, or don't go at all.

* * *

They cleared the point at half past noon under a sky the color of wet slate. The channel between the island and the mainland shoulder was three miles of gray-green water already roughening, whitecaps starting early, the wind out of the southeast carrying the smell of rain that hadn't arrived yet but was thinking hard about it.

Hazel took the tiller. She handled the *Kestrel* the way she did everything — without wasted motion, correcting before the waves finished forming, reading the water's mood a beat ahead of it. Sebastian sat amidships with the duffel between his knees: rope, two lamps, the crowbar, dry clothes wrapped in oilcloth, and the tide tables copied out in his own hand because he trusted no paper he hadn't written himself.

Halfway across, he noticed the sound.

It was small. A tick in the engine note, there and gone, like a word almost heard through a wall. He told himself it was the chop. Then it came again, longer, and beneath it — fainter, wronger — a second sound, a liquid slap that had no business being inside a hull.

"Hazel."

She looked back. He pointed down. "Bilge. Listen."

She cut the throttle and the engine noise dropped away, and there it was, unmistakable now: water moving where water shouldn't be. Sebastian yanked up the floorboard aft and the bilge looked back at him, six inches deep and rising, black-green and glittering.

"Plug," he said. "Stern drain. It's gone."

"That's impossible. I checked—"

"I believe you." He was already pulling the floorboards wider, running his hand along the stringers, and what he found made his stomach drop lower than the water level. The drain fitting hadn't worked loose. The plug had been pulled clean and set aside — and the bung that should have seated in it had been wedged into the limber holes forward, jammed so the automatic flow toward the drain couldn't work right even if the plug were back in. Someone had done this deliberately, and done it knowing boats. "This isn't neglect. This is engineering."

Hazel had gone very still, both hands locked on the gunwale. "Crane."

"Or Crane's man. He was on the dock yesterday asking after you — I saw him talking to old Tam." Sebastian grabbed the hand pump and started working it, fast, mechanical, letting his body do the arithmetic while his mind did the rest. "Think about it. If he wanted us dead, there are easier ways. He could have scuttled her at the mooring. We'd have drowned in the boathouse slip and nobody would ever have known we left."

"Then why—"

"Because a drowned pilot is an accident, but a marooned pair is leverage." The pump chattered, spitting water over the side in silver arcs. "He wants us stranded somewhere specific, somewhere he can arrive with blankets and a rescue offer and a contract. Somewhere we can't say no."

The radio confirmed it before he'd finished speaking. He'd tried it out of habit ten minutes earlier and gotten only the dry hiss of dead air; now he popped the cover and looked inside, and there it was — the antenna lead disconnected, coiled neatly, tucked behind the set like a courtesy. Not smashed. Disconnected. Whoever did it wanted them silent, not unable to fix it if they got lucky.

"They'll be watching the channel," Hazel said quietly. "Waiting for us to turn back or sink."

"Then we don't do either."

* * *

The mainland shoulder rose out of the murk ahead, black rock under streaming cloud, and beyond it the long shingle beach that curved toward the cliffs — the beach below the cliff path, the one place on this stretch of coast with any shelter from a southeast blow. Sebastian had been aiming for it since the plug came out, recalculating constantly: distance, freeboard, pump rate, the storm's arrival time. The numbers kept getting worse.

The *Kestrel* was taking water faster than the pump could argue with. His arms burned. Hazel bailed with a bucket when the pump clogged, working beside him without complaint, spray and rain beginning to come together until he couldn't tell which was which.

"We're not making the far anchorage," he shouted over the wind.

"I know."

"The beach, then. Bow-on, ride the surge up the stones, get her high enough that she doesn't beat herself apart."

"On a falling tide with a monsoon behind us." Her jaw was set, but her eyes were bright, alive, the look of a woman who had spent her whole life waiting for a reason to be afraid and finally had a worthy one. "You've done this before."

"Once. In daylight. In a boat that wasn't leaking."

"Then it'll be novel."

He laughed — actually laughed, there in the rising water with the sky coming apart above them — and steered for the beach.

The last hundred yards were chaos. The swell had built while they fought the leak, long gray rollers marching in from the open sea, and the *Kestrel* surfed the third one with her stern awash, Sebastian holding her straight with the rudder and his whole body, Hazel forward with the anchor ready. The shingle came up fast — not sand, stones, acres of rounded gray stone that would grind a hull to splinters given half a chance — and then the wave lifted them and threw them at the land and it was all collision and cold water and Hazel's hands fisted in his jacket hauling him over the side into waist-deep surf.

They got the anchor out and the bow line run up the beach and tied to a driftwood stump older than either of them. By the time the *Kestrel* was secured, sitting on the stones with the tide dropping away beneath her, the rain had arrived in earnest — not rain anymore but the monsoon's advance guard, horizontal, warm, relentless, hammering the shingle flat.

They dragged the duffel above the tideline and found what passed for shelter: an overhang at the base of the cliff where the rock curled out like a shelf, dry-ish, wind-shadowed. Hazel got a tarp strung while Sebastian wrung out his shirt and tried to think.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake and made himself name the feeling, because naming fear was how you took its weight off. It wasn't the crash — crashes were quick, a bright violence and then whatever came after. This was slower. This was knowing that somewhere behind the rain, a man named Aldous Crane was watching this exact beach through rain-blurred glass, timing his entrance, rehearsing his generosity.

"He'll come tonight or tomorrow," Sebastian said. "Under a flag. Food, dry clothes, a plane ride out. All he asks is a signature."

Hazel crouched beside him, watching the surf tear itself white against the stones. "And if we sign?"

"Then your grandfather stays a thief in the family story, and Crane keeps everything, and we spend the rest of our lives knowing we were bought cheap."

"And if we don't?"

Sebastian looked north along the beach, to where the cliffs began their climb, black and streaming, hiding the cave mouth that the falling tide would expose in less than thirty hours. The storm would be at its worst then. The path would be suicide. The galleries would be flooding.

Every reason to wait. Every reason to let Crane come, negotiate, survive.

But the man had pulled a plug and cut a radio lead, and that told Sebastian something more useful than any chart: Crane needed them alive, needed them willing, needed them *unfound*. Which meant whatever was in that cave, Crane hadn't seen it yet — and couldn't afford anyone else seeing it first.

"He wants us marooned," Sebastian said slowly, "not dead. Yet. That means we're worth more to him breathing and desperate than buried. And it means—" he turned to her, rain running off his chin, "—it means he doesn't know what's in that cave. Not really. If he did, he wouldn't need us at all."

Hazel was quiet a long moment. Lightning walked the horizon out at sea, silent, counting seconds.

"So we don't wait for his rescue," she said.

"No. Tonight, while his men are watching the channel for a boat that isn't coming — we go up the cliff path in the worst weather this coast has seen in ten years, and we're inside the rock before the tide turns." He grimaced. "I want it on record that this is a terrible plan."

"Noted." She almost smiled. "My grandfather used to say the mountain doesn't care about your plans. Only your footing."

"Then let's hope your footing's better than mine."

Far up the beach, through the sheets of rain, something glinted — brief, deliberate, gone. Like glass catching light. Like binoculars.

Sebastian went very still.

They were already being watched.
