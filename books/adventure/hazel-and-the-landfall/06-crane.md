## 6. Crane

Hazel saw the boat before she heard it — a white sliver threading the gray water between the headlands, running lights burning against a sky that had no business being that dark at midday. She stood in the mouth of the sea cave's shadow with her arms wrapped around herself and watched it come, and she thought: *of course*. Of course he would come. Men like Aldous Crane did not send storms; they arrived after them, wearing dry coats, carrying umbrellas they offered to share.

"Sebastian." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "We have company."

He came up from the shingle where he'd been trying to coax a spark of life out of the drowned radio, his hair plastered to his skull, salt crusted white along his jaw. He looked at the boat, then at her, and something passed over his face that she was learning to read — the particular grimness of a pilot recognizing an aircraft he'd flown before.

"That's his tender," Sebastian said. "Off the *Meridian Star*. I'd know her lines anywhere. I've hauled freight off that deck."

"Then we know who's coming to rescue us."

"Do we?" Sebastian wiped his face with the back of his hand. "Because I keep asking myself what a man like Crane is doing within two hundred miles of this coast in monsoon season, and every answer I come up with has your grandfather's mine at the bottom of it."

The boat came on, unhurried, absurdly confident in seas that would have swallowed anything less seaworthy. Hazel made herself stand still and let it come. There was no point running; there was nowhere to run. The island behind them was a green knuckle of rock and dead mining works, the mainland a rumor beyond the storm wall, and the tide was already beginning its slow, patient argument with the beach.

* * *

Aldous Crane stepped ashore like a man arriving at a garden party that had been slightly delayed by weather. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, wearing a yellow foul-weather jacket so new it still held its creases, and he smiled at Hazel with the warmth of a man greeting a niece he'd never quite gotten around to meeting.

"Hazel Vane," he said, extending a hand. "You have your grandmother's chin. I hope you'll forgive the familiarity — I feel as though I've known you since you were in swaddling clothes. Which, I suppose, in a manner of speaking, I have."

She took the hand. It was dry, warm, and perfectly calibrated in pressure. "Mr. Crane."

"Aldous, please. We're family, after all. Distant, complicated, litigious family — but family." His eyes went past her to Sebastian, and the smile adjusted itself by a few degrees, cooling into something more appraising. "And you must be the pilot. Yarrow, isn't it? You came highly recommended. That's rather the problem, as it turns out."

"You chartered me," Sebastian said.

"I inquired about chartering you. There's a difference, and I suspect you're sharp enough to know it." Crane sighed, and for one unguarded moment he looked tired — genuinely tired, a man carrying something heavy up a long stair. "Look at the state of you both. Come aboard. Hot food, dry clothes, a radio that actually transmits. My captain can have us in Port Calloway by tomorrow evening if the sea gives us a window."

"And in exchange?" Hazel asked.

Crane turned back to her, and the charm reassembled itself, seamless as water finding its level. "In exchange, nothing sinister. A signature. A formality, truly." From inside his jacket he produced a document case, oxblood leather, immaculate despite the spray. "Your grandfather's claim lapsed in 1974. Legally — and I want to stress how very legally — everything under that headland belongs to Meridian Consolidated. Which is me. However." He opened the case and held out a single sheet toward her. "I'm prepared to execute a deed of gift. A settlement, if you prefer. Enough to buy a life anywhere you like, twice over. All it requires is your signature renouncing any interest in the Vane estate, the mine workings, and — this is the important part, Hazel — the coastal parcels. Including the caves."

"The caves," Hazel repeated.

"The caves," Crane agreed pleasantly. "Which are, I'm told, full of interesting rocks and absolutely nothing else. The surveyors found nothing. Decades of surveys. Nothing at all."

* * *

They sat in the tender's cabin while the rain came down like nails on the canopy, and Crane talked. He talked about her grandfather — Elias Vane, whom Hazel remembered only as a smell of pipe smoke and a voice reading shipping manifests aloud on Sunday evenings — and he talked about her grandmother, Maren, whom he spoke of with a strange, careful tenderness that set Hazel's teeth on edge. He talked about the mine, the good years, the bad years, the year the ledgers burned. He was, Hazel admitted privately, magnificent at it. Every detail rang true. Every pause landed exactly where a pause should land.

And all of it was performance. She knew that the way she knew weather coming — not through evidence but through the animal certainty of someone who had grown up around men who lied for a living. Her grandfather had been one. Perhaps her grandmother too. Perhaps, said a small cold voice, the whole family line ran thick with them, and she was only the last of the breed, sitting here drinking Crane's terrible coffee and smiling at his lies.

"You must understand my position," Crane was saying. "Elias was my partner once. Closer than a brother. When he died, I inherited his debts along with his grudges, and I have spent thirty years paying both down. I don't want a war with you, Hazel. I want to close the account. Let the dead rest, let the living live. Isn't that worth more than — what? A story? A legend about papers in a hole in the rock?"

"Is that what it is?" Hazel asked. "Just a story?"

Something flickered behind Crane's eyes — quick, involuntary, gone. "What else would it be?"

"I suppose we'll find out."

The silence lasted three seconds. Then Crane laughed, warm and rueful, and refilled her cup, and Hazel understood that she had just told him everything he needed to know: that she believed the papers existed, and that she intended to look for them. She cursed herself even as she smiled back. Playing along required letting him believe she might sign. Instead she'd shown him her hand like an amateur.

She drank the coffee anyway. It was hot, and she was so cold.

* * *

Sebastian was waiting for her on the beach when she came back down, standing at the tideline with his hands jammed in his pockets, watching the tender ride at anchor. Rain streamed off his chin. He didn't ask what Crane had offered; he'd heard enough through the cabin door before they'd asked him to wait outside.

"Well?" he said.

"He wants me to sign away the coast. In exchange for enough money to disappear forever." She hugged herself against the wind. "And you?"

"I went back to the boat."

Hazel turned. The skiff lay where the storm had thrown it, above the high-water mark, half-buried in shingle. "It's holed. We knew that."

"It's holed in three places." Sebastian's voice was low and very steady, the way a man speaks when he is holding something down. "One's from the reef — I put us on that reef myself, I'll own it. But the other two are clean. Bored clean, from the inside, with something like an auger. And there's this." He held out his palm. In it lay a short brass fitting, green with salt, threaded at one end. "A drain plug. Off our own boat. Found wedged in the bilge stringer, which is a strange place for a drain plug to be, unless somebody pulled it, stowed it, and meant to come back for it later."

Hazel looked at the little brass thing lying innocently in his hand and felt the beach tilt beneath her.

"When?" she managed.

"Not when we were flying. Before. Before I ever took that charter." He closed his fist. "Somebody pulled the plug on my boat at the airfield dock, then handed me a charter that would put me on this exact stretch of coast right before the monsoon. They wanted us stranded here, Hazel. Not dead — dead pilots get investigated, and dead heiresses get probate lawyers sniffing around. Stranded. Stuck on this island until the season turns or until somebody comes to collect us." He nodded toward the tender. "Until he comes."

"But why strand us? If he wanted the cave sealed, why not just—"

"Buy us first. And if we wouldn't sell—" Sebastian shrugged. "Leave us to winter here. People change their minds about signing when they're hungry enough. Or they die of something natural, and the story dies with them."

The rain hammered down. Out in the anchorage, the tender's riding light swung gently, patient as a lure.

"He gave us coffee," Hazel said, and heard how foolish it sounded, and didn't care. "He talked about my grandmother like he loved her."

"Maybe he did." Sebastian pocketed the plug. "Men like that love plenty of things. It doesn't slow them down."

* * *

That night, in the boathouse loft where they'd dragged the salvaged tarpaulins and the last of the tinned food, Hazel lay awake listening to the storm work the coastline like a jaw. Beside her, Sebastian slept in snatches, exhausted, one arm thrown over his eyes.

She thought about the document in Crane's oxblood case. She thought about the number he'd named — a number that could have bought back the airfield ten times over, paid off every debt in Sebastian's ledger, bought her a house on high ground far from any sea. Safety, signed and notarized. All she had to do was agree that some things were better left rotting in the dark.

And she thought about her grandmother, Maren, whose chin she supposedly had. Maren, who had burned the ledgers — or so the family always said. Maren, whose name Crane spoke like a man handling something fragile. If the confession in the cave was real, it might name Maren as a thief alongside Elias's partner. It might name worse. Hazel had spent her whole life believing her family had been robbed; she had never once let herself ask whether they might have been robbers too.

Signing would end the question forever. Signing would make the lie official, comfortable, hers.

She turned her head in the dark. "Sebastian."

He surfaced instantly, the way pilots do. "Mm."

"The tide tables. The ones you worked out on the beach. When's the next low?"

He was quiet a moment, and she could feel him waking fully, understanding. "Two nights from now. Near midnight. It won't be as low as the one that exposed the cave mouth — the storm surge will fight it. But there's a gap in the reef shelf on the north side, a blowhole passage. I saw it when we came in. If the charts are honest, it runs up into the higher galleries."

"If they're not?"

"Then we drown in the dark with the truth." He said it without drama, simply, the way he'd say *the fuel gauge reads empty*. "Crane sails on the morning tide day after next. He told his captain so. After that, we're alone until the season breaks."

"Then we go tomorrow night," Hazel said. "While he's still anchored here, watching the front door. While he thinks he's already won."

In the darkness she felt him turn toward her, and though she couldn't see his face she could hear the ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "You know he'll figure it out eventually. Men like him always do."

"Then we'd best be fast," Hazel said, and outside, the monsoon gathered itself and threw the whole weight of the sea against the island, as if it had been listening.
