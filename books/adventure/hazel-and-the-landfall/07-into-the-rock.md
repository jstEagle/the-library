## 7. Into the Rock

The storm's worst hour came at two in the morning, and Sebastian Yarrow had learned to read storms the way other men read faces. This one had a lie in it. The wind would hold its breath around three, the barometer would lift just enough, and for forty minutes — maybe fifty — the rain would fall straight down instead of sideways. Crane's men, huddled under tarps at the headland with their generator and their thermoses of bad coffee, would believe the worst had passed.

It hadn't. It was only reloading.

"You're sure about this window," Hazel said. She stood at the mouth of the sea cave's approach, a shingle spit below the bluff where the water had withdrawn like something ashamed of itself. She wore her grandfather's oilskin coat, too big for her, the collar turned up, and she held a storm lantern that threw her shadow long and trembling across the wet stones.

"I'm sure about nothing," Sebastian said. "That's the whole point of weather. But I've flown into worse lies than this one."

She almost smiled. He'd been counting those almosts all week, cataloguing them the way he'd once catalogued engine sounds — the healthy hum versus the rattle that meant metal was thinking about failure. Hazel Vane was mostly rattle. Mostly warning. But underneath it there was a hum he trusted more than he wanted to.

They went down the spit single file, bent double against the wind, the lantern hooded to a coin of light between them. The tide had pulled back farther than any tide had a right to go — the same anomalous ebb that had shown him the cave from the air, that impossible black mouth in the cliff face. Now, standing before it, Sebastian understood why the old charts marked nothing here. The entrance wasn't hidden so much as *withheld*, a secret the coast kept on behalf of whoever had paid it best.

"Grandfather called it the Counting House," Hazel said quietly. "I thought it was a story to keep me off the rocks."

"What was it really?"

"A bank," she said. "A very private one."

* * *

Inside, the wind died as if a door had closed on it, and the sound changed — became water instead of air, the deep percussion of surf somewhere behind them, filtered through rock. Sebastian went first with the lantern, testing each footing. The floor was slick clay over stone, grooved with old cart tracks worn into the living rock by wheels that had stopped turning sixty years ago.

The galleries opened like the ribs of some enormous animal. Their beams crossed overhead — pit props, black with age but still true, still carrying weight — and along the walls ran iron rings, rusted to lace, where crates had once been slung above the flood line. Someone had moved a great deal of something through here, carefully, repeatedly, in the dark.

"Ledgers," Hazel murmured, running her hand along a ring. "He told my mother stories about ledgers. We all thought it was senility. A man who'd been a bookkeeper talking about books even after the mine closed."

"And your grandmother?"

Hazel didn't answer. Her hand came off the ring slowly, the way you take your hand off a door you've decided not to open yet.

The first gallery flooded at mid-shin. The water was cold enough to make his teeth ache, and beneath its surface the cart tracks continued, guiding their feet when the lantern couldn't reach the bottom. Sebastian kept count of the turns — left, left, right, the map he'd drawn in his head from Hazel's recited fragments of her grandfather's story. *Past the weeping wall. Where the roof comes down to meet the water, duck and breathe shallow.* The roof did come down; they waded bent nearly double, the lantern light flattened against black water, and for ten steps Sebastian breathed the way he breathed in a cockpit losing altitude — small, rationed, deliberate.

Then the passage opened again, climbed, and the water fell away to their ankles, and there it was: a wall.

Not natural stone. Dressed blocks, fitted without mortar, sealed with what looked like pitch gone glassy with age. And set into the center, a door — iron-strapped timber, absurdly domestic, like the back entrance to a cellar in a house that had never existed.

Hazel stood before it for a long moment. The lantern shook in her grip, and Sebastian saw that she was crying without any of the noise or collapse of crying, just water running down her face into the collar of the borrowed coat.

"He used to say," she managed, "that the truth was heavy, so he put it somewhere strong."

Sebastian took out the pry bar they'd brought wrapped in sacking. "Then let's not insult him by being gentle."

* * *

The door gave with a crack that echoed down every gallery behind them, and both of them froze, listening, half-expecting the sea itself to object. Nothing came but the drip of water and the far-off boom of surf. The smell that rolled out was paper and pitch and time — dry, preserved, a room that had been holding its breath since before either of them was born.

Inside, shelves lined three walls. On them sat oilcloth-wrapped bundles, stacked with a bookkeeper's precision, labeled in faded ink: dates, initials, sums. Hazel cut the twine on the first bundle with a pocketknife and lifted out a ledger bound in green cloth, and when she opened it, the lantern light found columns of figures so neat they might have been printed.

"It's all here," she whispered. "Every shipment. Every payment. The mine never failed — it was *drained*. Look — these disbursements, month after month, to an account in—" She stopped. Her finger stayed on the page.

"Hazel?"

"There's a name next to the countersignatures." She turned the ledger toward him. "V.M.V. My grandmother. Vivian Marchmont Vane." Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening to it. "These are her initials. All of them. Every withdrawal my grandfather recorded, she co-signed."

They worked through the bundles in silence after that, and the silence got heavier with each one. Letters between her grandfather and his partner — the partner whose name had become a dynasty, whose heir now sat at the headland drinking bad coffee with hired men. The partner's own hand, admitting in a letter dated three years before the mine's official collapse: *Your wife understands the arrangement better than you do, old friend. She knows which side of the ledger survival is written on.*

And then the last document, a confession in her grandfather's stiff, upright script, witnessed and dated, naming names, tracing the money through shell companies and a shipping line and finally into the foundations of the fortune Aldous Crane now defended with lawyers and, apparently, saboteurs.

At the bottom of the final page, a postscript in different ink, added later, the letters shakier:

*I do not write this to be forgiven. I write it because Vivian asked me to keep it, and I could never refuse her anything, which is how this all began. If you are reading this, you are family, and I am sorry. Family was the lock. I mistook it for the treasure.*

Hazel set the confession down on the shelf with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might detonate. Then she laughed — one short, ugly note that bounced off the stone.

"She helped him," Hazel said. "My grandmother. The woman who told me bedtime stories about our honor, about how the Cranes robbed us blind. She *co-signed*. She was in it. Maybe she started it." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I built my whole life on being robbed, Sebastian. It's a very comfortable thing, being robbed. You get to be innocent forever."

Sebastian said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a cruelty. He knew about comfortable wounds; his whole airfield was one, a debt he serviced the way other men serviced faith.

"The truth's uglier than the legend," he said finally. "It usually is. But it's yours now. Both halves of it."

Hazel looked at the ledgers, at the confession, at sixty years of a dead man's careful penmanship preserving a poison in a dry room under the sea. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, with something new kindled behind it.

"They'll want this back," she said. "Crane will burn the coast down before he lets this leave the rock. Which means we have maybe an hour, maybe less, before—"

The sound reached them then, faint but unmistakable, carried down the flooded galleries on the acoustics of the stone: voices. More than one. And the knock of boots on rock, coming from the direction of the entrance they'd left open behind them.

Sebastian killed the lantern. In the sudden dark, Hazel's hand found his arm and gripped it, and above them, through thirty feet of limestone, the storm went on hammering the coast like something demanding to be let in.
