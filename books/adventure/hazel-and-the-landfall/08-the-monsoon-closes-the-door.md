## 8. The Monsoon Closes the Door

*Sebastian*

The water told them first.

They were deep in the sealed chamber, Hazel's lantern held over the ledgers spread across a shelf of stone that had waited eighty years for human eyes, when Sebastian heard it — not the storm overhead, which had become a kind of weather they'd stopped hearing, but something underneath it. A low, patient hiss, like the sea drawing breath through its teeth.

"The tide," he said. "It's turning."

Hazel didn't look up. Her finger traced a column of figures in her grandfather's hand — the same careful, schoolmaster's script Sebastian had seen on the charter manifest Crane had signed. "It can't be. We have two hours. The almanac—"

"The almanac doesn't know about a monsoon surge." He was already gathering the documents, sliding them into the oilskin wallet Hazel had brought for exactly this purpose. The confession lay on top: three pages in a stranger's hand, naming Elias Vane's partner — Crane's grandfather — as the thief of the mine, and beneath that, in a paragraph that had made Hazel go very still when she read it, the name *Margaret Vane*, her grandmother, listed among those who had helped bury the truth. "We come back. We know where it is now."

"There is no coming back." She said it flatly, without drama, the way people say things they've known for a long time and only just admitted aloud. "Once this chamber floods, the silt shifts. The passage we came through will be choked by morning. You said so yourself."

He had said so himself. He'd stood in that gallery an hour ago with water lapping at his boots and made a pilot's assessment: the cave was dying around them even as they looted it, the monsoon pressing the tide higher and earlier than any chart predicted. He'd said it and then he'd kept reading, because the ledgers were right there, because the truth was finally in his hands after days of drowning and sabotage and cold, and because some part of him had believed — stupidly, humanly — that the rock would wait for them.

The rock did not wait.

By the time they reached the first gallery, the water was at their knees and rising in visible pulses, each wave of it pushing further up the sloped floor than the last. The blowhole passage — their escape route, the crack in the ceiling Hazel had spotted on the way in, wide enough for a person if not for dignity — was still above the flood line. Barely. Sebastian measured it against the wall, marked the waterline with a slash of his knife, and did not like the arithmetic.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "Maybe less."

"Then we climb now."

They climbed. The chimney of rock was slick with spray, and Hazel went first because she was lighter and because she'd grown up on these cliffs and knew how stone lied to you about its grip. Sebastian followed with the oilskin wallet strapped tight across his chest, feeling like a man carrying a bomb. Below them the gallery filled with a sound like slow applause — the sea, settling into the rooms it had built.

Then the light changed.

Not from above. From behind. From the entrance passage they'd used at low tide, the one that should have been under four feet of black water by now. A beam swept across the flooded gallery, caught the churning surface, and held.

Voices. Two of them, maybe three, distorted by echo into something guttural and wrong.

"They found another way in," Hazel breathed from above him. "There's a fissure on the north face — my grandfather mentioned it once. I thought it was a story."

"Crane's full of stories that turn out to be true." Sebastian killed his own light and pressed himself into the rock. "How much do you want to bet one of your allies drew him a map?"

He felt her go rigid above him. He hadn't meant to say it like that — an accusation dressed as a joke — but the dark had a way of stripping the varnish off things, and what was underneath was ugly. Because there *was* a traitor. Someone had told Crane about the cave, about the timing, about the blowhole. And there were only four people alive who knew all three: Sebastian, Hazel, old Tomas who kept her boat, and Miriam, the postmistress who had passed messages between them since the crash.

"Hazel," he said quietly. "Who knew?"

Above him, silence. Then, very small: "Miriam knew about the fissure. I asked her to check it last week. I thought—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "I thought she was checking it for *us*."

* * *

*Hazel*

The betrayal sat in her chest like swallowed seawater.

She'd known Miriam since she was seven years old, since the woman had pulled her out of a tide pool with a laugh and a licorice stick and never once, in twenty years, asked for anything. That was the thing about loyalty in a place like this coast — it wasn't proven by grand gestures. It was proven by decades of small ones. And it could still rot from the inside while the outside looked whole, the way her grandmother's reputation had rotted, the way the family legend had rotted, everything on this coast apparently built over hollow chambers that filled with water eventually.

Below her, torchlight bloomed across the flooded gallery, and Aldous Crane stepped into it.

He looked absurdly composed for a man standing waist-deep in a drowning cave in a monsoon — dry-suit zipped to the throat, headlamp blazing, that same reasonable smile he'd worn on the beach when he'd offered to fly them out if only Hazel would sign away her claim. Two men flanked him, wading, rifles slung high across their backs to keep them clear of the water.

"Hazel!" His voice carried beautifully off the stone; he'd have made a fine preacher. "I know you're up there. The fissure opens onto the same gallery — Miriam was very helpful once she understood the situation. Everyone understands the situation eventually."

Hazel's hands shook on the rock. Not from fear. From the specific, nauseating heat of having been *right* about the world and hating it.

"You gave her money," she called down.

"I gave her *options*. There's a difference." Crane waded forward, unhurried, his men sweeping their lights along the walls, along the waterline, upward toward the shadows where Sebastian and Hazel clung. "You're holding eighty years of paper up there, Miss Vane. Ledgers. Letters. A confession, if my information is correct. Here's what I know about paper: it burns, it sinks, and it means nothing unless someone's willing to act on it. So let's act. Give me the wallet, and I'll leave you the fissure. Refuse, and my associates will simply wait below this chimney until the water does the negotiating for me. The tide's already ahead of schedule. Did you notice? The monsoon does wonderful things to the tables."

Sebastian's hand found her ankle in the dark — a squeeze, steady, deliberate. *Don't answer yet.* She realized he was counting. She could feel him counting, the way he counted everything: fuel, distance, seconds, odds.

"And if I give you the papers?" she said, buying him time. "You burn them and I'm just the granddaughter of a thief's accomplice with no proof and no fortune."

"You were always going to be that," Crane said gently. "The fortune's been mine since before you were born. This was never about money, Hazel. It's about the story. About which version of my grandfather walks out of this cave. Yours or mine."

And there it was — the true shape of him, visible at last through all the charm. He didn't want the ledgers because they threatened his inheritance. He wanted them because they threatened his *inheritance story*, the clean myth he'd been curating his whole life, and a man defending a myth will stand in rising water forever.

Which meant he would not leave. Which meant the water would not care.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The water reached the base of the chimney while Crane was still talking, and Sebastian stopped listening.

He'd spent the climb mapping the fissure in his head the way he mapped every approach — wind, gradient, load, margin. The crack ran eight feet up to a squeeze, then opened into the blowhole shaft, then daylight. Climbable wet. Climbable in the dark. Not climbable carrying a fight.

But Crane wasn't climbing. Crane was waiting, because waiting had always worked for him, because patience was what wealth bought. And that was the flaw in it, Sebastian thought, watching the torchlight wobble as the water lifted the man's footing — patience only works when the ground holds still.

"He's going to drown down there," Hazel whispered. "With us pinned above him. Sebastian—"

"I know."

"If we go up, we leave him the papers' location. If we stay—"

"We don't stay." He unstrapped the wallet. "But he doesn't get all of it either."

He worked fast, by touch: the confession pages out, folded flat against his chest beneath his shirt, waterproofed in a condom wrapper from the emergency kit because pilots are sentimental about nothing except contingencies. The ledgers stayed in the wallet. Then he leaned out over the gallery and let the wallet drop.

It hit the water with a slap and began to drift, ink-black pages ghosting pale beneath the surface, turning slowly like something dead.

"There!" One of Crane's men lunged for it. Crane shouted — *careful, the ink, lift it flat* — and for ten full seconds both lights, both rifles, both men's attention went to the floating paper.

Sebastian climbed.

He went up the chimney the way he'd gone up a hundred ice-scarred rock faces in the interior, fast and ugly, hands burning, Hazel hauling from above with a strength that surprised him even now. Behind them the gallery erupted — shouting, splashing, a rifle cracking once against stone in a bright concussion that spat rock fragments past his ear. The water took the sound and mangled it.

At the squeeze, Hazel went through first, and then it was Sebastian's turn, shoulders wedged in wet stone, the whole mountain pressing down, and behind him — below him — he heard Crane stop shouting orders and start shouting his name.

"Yarrow! *Yarrow!* The water — the exit's flooding — you know it's flooding — take me up with you!"

Sebastian hung in the squeeze, half in, half out, and looked down.

The gallery was a churn of white now. Crane's men had scrambled for the fissure behind them; whether they'd make it was their problem and their gamble. But Crane stood alone in the torchlight, which was failing, which was guttering in the water, and the water was at his chest and rising faster than anything had a right to rise, and his face — stripped of charm, stripped of story, stripped of everything but arithmetic — was doing the same calculations Sebastian had done an hour ago and arriving at the same terrible sum.

Their eyes met across the dark.

Every secret between them surfaced with the water in that moment — Crane's sabotage, the beach, the pulled plug, the bought postmistress, the whole architecture of a life built on a stolen mine — and beneath all of it, plain as bone, the simple fact that Sebastian Yarrow was the only ladder left in the room.

He put out his hand.

"Take it. Now. *Now.*"

Crane looked at the hand. Looked at the water. And Sebastian saw it happen — saw the man glance, one last time, toward the drifting wallet, toward the eighty years of paper dissolving in the dark, and choose. Choose the story over the hand. Choose the myth over the breath in his lungs.

"No," Crane said. "It's *mine*—"

The surge came early, the way everything on this coast came early, and took the rest.

* * *

*Hazel*

She pulled Sebastian through the squeeze by the collar of his shirt, both of them screaming wordlessly into the roar, and then they were in the blowhole shaft with the sea detonating below them like artillery, climbing toward a coin of grey light that shrank and shrank and did not disappear.

They came out onto the cliff top in the teeth of the monsoon, flattened by wind, soaked past meaning, alive. Hazel lay on the turf with the rain hammering her back and laughed — or sobbed, there was no difference left — until Sebastian rolled over beside her and pressed the folded confession pages into her hand.

"You dropped the ledgers," she managed.

"I dropped the *duplicates* of nothing. The confession's the only document that matters, and it never touched the water." He was breathing hard, staring up into the rain. "Crane?"

She looked back at the blowhole. Grey water fountained from it, rhythmic, enormous, indifferent. Nothing else came out.

"Gone," she said. "The sea keeps what it's owed."

Below them, faint through the storm, the cliff face shuddered. Somewhere inside the rock, a gallery roof let go, and the cave that had guarded her family's shame for eighty years began, at last, to close its door.

Hazel closed her fist around the confession and got to her feet.

"We have to get off this headland before the surge cuts it off," she shouted over the wind. "And Sebastian — whoever told Miriam to sell us out is still out there, and they'll be looking for what we're carrying."

He nodded once, and together they turned into the storm.
