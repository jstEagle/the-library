## 9. What the Sea Keeps

The water came for them the way debt comes for a man — slowly, then all at once.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it, a cold kiss against his boot in the dark of the outer gallery, and his stomach dropped through the floor of him. The tide had turned. Not at dawn, not on any schedule printed in an almanac, but now, early, greedy, the monsoon shoving the whole weight of the sea back into the cave's throat like a creditor who'd stopped waiting politely.

"Hazel." His voice cracked against the rock. "It's turning."

Ahead of him her headlamp swung, catching the glint of black water already climbing the walls of the passage they'd come through. The way in was gone. He'd known it might be — he'd told himself he'd accepted it — but knowing was a ledger entry and this was the bill arriving. The gallery that had been ankle-deep shingle an hour ago was now a churning knee-deep channel, and the sound had changed too: not the gentle suck and sigh of tidal breathing but a low, continuous roar, the cave swallowing seawater by the ton.

They had the ledgers. Two oilskin-wrapped bundles lashed to Hazel's chest with rope she'd cut from the boat, and the confession folded flat against Sebastian's ribs inside his jacket, paper soft as cloth from eighty years in the dark. Her grandfather's handwriting. The theft laid out line by line — and her grandmother's name beside it, complicit, signature steady. Hazel hadn't spoken since they'd read it. She didn't speak now. She just turned toward him, lamp beam trembling, and he saw in her face what he felt in his own chest: the truth had cost more than the lie ever would have, and there was no version of this where they put it back.

"Blowhole," he said. "There has to be one. Caves like this always have one — the old miners wouldn't have sealed a chamber without venting it or they'd have suffocated on their own bad air."

"You're guessing."

"I'm a pilot. Guessing is most of the job." He grabbed her wrist. "The rest is committing before you're sure."

* * *

Crane found them at the mouth of the inner gallery.

Sebastian heard him first — boots in water, a voice pitched to carry over the roar, that broadcast warmth Aldous Crane wore like a flight jacket two sizes too big. "Hazel! Thank God. I've been half out of my mind — the boy told me you'd come in here, poor lad was beside himself—" Tam, then. Tam had sold them. Sebastian filed the name away with all the other weights he carried; grief could wait its turn behind survival.

Crane came around the rock fin with a lamp in one hand and something long and dark in the other — a boat hook, or a gaff, held casual, which meant it wasn't. Water swirled around his thighs. Behind him the outer gallery was a sea of moving shadows, white lines of foam crawling up walls that had been dry stone when they'd entered.

"Give me the ledgers," Crane said, and dropped the charm like a coat he no longer needed. "The papers, Hazel. Then we walk out together, all three of us, and by morning none of this ever happened. You'll be rich. Your family's name restored, clean, whole—"

"My grandmother signed those pages," Hazel said. Her voice was strange — level, almost gentle. "You want me to hand you the proof so you can burn it and call restoration what it really is. Erasure."

"I want us both to survive."

"No," Hazel said. "You want to survive *as*. There's a difference, and the water out there knows it."

Crane moved. It happened fast and stupidly, the way these things do — he lunged for the bundle at her chest, the gaff swinging wide, and Hazel twisted away and went down hard into the flood, and Sebastian did the only arithmetic left to him: he hit Crane across the arms with everything he had, a pilot's tackle, graceless and total, and the two of them went into the black water together.

The surge took the decision out of everyone's hands.

It came through the outer gallery like a train — Sebastian heard it, a deepening of the roar, a pressure change in his ears — and then it arrived, waist-high, freighted with sand and broken shell, and it picked the three of them up like driftwood. He lost his lamp. He lost his bearings. He lost Crane's collar and regained it, and for one lucid, terrible second the two men were face to face in the churn, close enough that Sebastian could see the naked animal astonishment in the other man's eyes — the look of a man discovering that the world had never actually been for sale.

Sebastian got an arm around a rock rib jutting from the wall and held on, and his free hand shot out and closed on Crane's forearm, and he hauled, screaming over the water: "*Take it! Take my hand!*"

And here was the thing Sebastian would carry out of this cave and to the end of his days: Crane looked at the hand. He looked at it the way a man looks at a door he's spent his life refusing to walk through. And then his eyes went past Sebastian's shoulder, to where Hazel clung to the rocks with the oilskin bundles still strapped to her body, and Sebastian watched the arithmetic happen — the ledgers, the fortune, the inheritance, all of it weighed against a stranger's grip in rising water — and he watched Crane choose.

He wrenched toward the bundles instead.

The next surge gave no warning. It came green-black and total, ripped Crane off the ledge, and took him out through the gallery mouth into the roaring dark beyond, and the last Sebastian saw of Aldous Crane was a lamp beam cartwheeling through foam, once, twice, and then nothing but the sea keeping what it kept.

* * *

Afterward there was no time to feel anything, which was perhaps a mercy.

"The blowhole," Hazel gasped, hauling herself against him, water streaming off her, the bundles somehow still lashed to her chest. "Tell me you weren't guessing."

"I'm never sure," Sebastian said. "Come on."

They climbed. That was all it was in the end — climbing, hand over hand, up a chimney of rock in the northeast corner of the gallery where the air moved instead of stagnated, where spray hung suspended and the wind screamed down through a fissure overhead. The water chased them. He could hear it below, filling the gallery they'd fought in, erasing the scene of it, and he climbed faster than he'd climbed anything in his life, Hazel's boot finding his shoulder, his hand closing on hers, both of them hauling each other up through a crack barely wider than their chests until suddenly there was sky — actual sky, gray and violent and raining sideways — and they spilled out onto the cliff top like things born.

The storm took them the moment they cleared the rock. Wind flattened them to the turf; rain hit like thrown gravel. Below and behind them, the cave mouth was a boiling white eye in the cliff face, and even from here, even shouting distance above it, Sebastian could hear the change in its voice. The tide was going high. By nightfall the entrance would be under fathoms of water, and the galleries, the flooded passages, the sealed chamber with its empty shelves — all of it would be shut again, sealed as thoroughly as her grandfather had sealed it, and this time there would be no anomalous tide, no impossible opening, no way back for anyone.

"It's gone," Hazel shouted, staring down at the white water. "All of it. The mine, the records room, everything else down there—"

"We got what matters." He touched his ribs, where the confession rode flat and safe against his skin. "We got enough."

She turned to him then, rain hammering both of them, and her face was doing several things at once — grieving and furious and lighter than he'd ever seen it, as if the truth had been a stone she'd carried so long she'd forgotten its weight until it was gone. "Tam sold us," she said. "He told Crane where we went."

"I know."

"He was my father's friend. He taught me to gut fish when I was six."

Sebastian said nothing for a moment. The wind screamed. Somewhere below, the sea finished its work without ceremony.

"Grief without regret," he said finally. "That's what my mother used to call it. When you lose a thing and you'd still make the same choice. You can hold both, Hazel. You don't have to pick."

She looked at him a long time, water running off her chin, and whatever passed between them passed without words, because some cargo doesn't need manifesting.

Then she nodded once, toward the mainland, toward the gray smear of coast where the storm was already fraying at its eastern edge, and shouldered the ledgers like a pack.

"Boat's on the shingle," she said. "If it's still there."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we swim," Hazel Vane said, "and we publish anyway."

They started down the cliff path together, two soaked and battered survivors carrying eighty years of stolen truth out of the rock, while behind them the cave closed its mouth on everything else — the fortune, the rot, the man who'd wanted it too much — and kept it, the way the sea keeps all things, forever.
