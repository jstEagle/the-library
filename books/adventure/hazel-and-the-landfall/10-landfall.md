## 10. Landfall

The storm broke at dawn the way a fever breaks — all at once, leaving everything washed and trembling and new.

Hazel stood in the mouth of the blowhole passage with salt crusted white on her sleeves and the ledgers wrapped in oilskin against her chest, and watched the sun come up over water that had tried to kill them. The sea lay flat as hammered tin. Somewhere beneath it, behind the curtain of tide that had already risen to seal the entrance, was the cave, and Aldous Crane, or what remained of what he'd wanted. She had stopped trying to make herself feel one particular thing about that. Grief, she was learning, did not always come with a name attached. Sometimes it just sat in the chest like ballast, keeping you steady.

Sebastian was already down at the shingle, ankle-deep in the retreating wash, hauling their ruined boat above the tideline. He moved carefully — his ribs were cracked, though he'd only admitted it when he could no longer hide the way he breathed — but he moved, which two days ago she would not have bet on. The boat looked like something a dog had been chewing: holed hull, snapped thwart, the outboard fouled with kelp. It should not have floated again. But Sebastian Yarrow had spent three days of the storm's worst hours inside its hull with a hammer, a sheet of salvaged zinc, and a vocabulary that would have embarrassed a dockworker, and when the water finally calmed, the boat floated the way stubborn things float — badly, and anyway.

"We'll make the mainland by midafternoon," he said, not looking up. "If we don't sink."

"If we don't sink," Hazel agreed.

He glanced back at her then, and there was something in his face she'd been watching for since the cave — not pity, exactly, and not awe either. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who had seen what she'd seen down there in the dark and understood that neither of them came out of it the person who went in.

"You're sure about this?" he asked. Not about the boat. About the bundle against her chest.

She was sure. She had been sure since the moment they'd spread the ledgers open by flashlight in the flooded gallery, while the water climbed their shins and Crane's voice echoed off the rock behind them, promising, threatening, bargaining. Her grandfather's handwriting. Her grandmother's initials in the margins, neat as prayer. Sixty years of theft set down in ink so confident it hadn't even bothered to disguise itself. The Vane fortune had never been stolen from her family. It had been built by them — and hidden from everyone the theft had crushed.

She could have claimed it. That was the thing nobody would ever quite believe afterward: the claim was real. Blood and paper both said the money was hers, if she wanted to spend the rest of her life in courtrooms proving it, fighting Crane's estate and every cousin who'd smell profit. She could have been rich. She could have been *Vane* again, the name restored, the boathouse rebuilt, the whole drowned dynasty dredged back up and polished.

Instead she was going to give it away.

Not all of it. Not to charity, not in some grand gesture — she wasn't that clean, and she didn't pretend to be. She was going to publish it. Every page, scanned and sworn and sent to the coastal papers, the mining archives, the descendants of the families whose wages her grandparents had pocketed. Let the truth be worth what truth was actually worth, which was nothing anyone could bank, and everything no one could take back.

"I'm sure," she said.

* * *

The crossing took six hours and cost Hazel most of her remaining composure. The patched hull seeped in a slow, insulting trickle that Sebastian bailed with a bucket while steering with his knees, and twice the swell found the seam of the zinc patch and made them both very quiet. But the mainland came up green and ordinary out of the haze — the harbor town, the breakwater, the rusted crane arm over the fuel dock — and when the keel ground into the sand of the public slip, Hazel stepped out onto solid earth and felt her knees nearly go.

People came. Of course people came; a wrecked plane and a missing charter pilot and the daughter of the Vanes walking out of the monsoon with an oilskin bundle — the town had been starving for news for a week. Hazel let them look. She let the harbormaster take her statement, let the constable read the first pages of the confession with his face going gray, let old Mrs. Okonkwo from the chandlery grip her hand and say, "Your grandmother, child? *Eleanor?*" with a grief so old it had fossilized into something almost like relief.

By evening the story was loose, and by the end of the week it was everywhere. The papers ran the ledger photographs across their front pages under headlines Hazel refused to read more than once. Lawyers materialized. So did cousins, distant and otherwise, all of them suddenly warm, all of them suddenly certain the fortune ought to be *managed*, protected, kept in the family — meaning theirs. Hazel signed nothing, claimed nothing, and answered every inquiry with the same sentence, delivered flat as a tide table:

"The documents are public. Do with them what your conscience allows."

Some of them hated her for it. She'd expected that. What she hadn't expected was the letters — dozens of them, then hundreds, from the grandchildren and great-grandchildren of miners whose names appeared in the wage columns, written in careful hands, saying *thank you*, saying *we knew*, saying *our mother told us and no one believed her*. Hazel read them at the boathouse window with the rain gone and the light coming in clean, and each one lifted a little more of the ballast out of her chest.

The fortune itself dissolved the way fortunes do when exposed to air — into lawyers' fees and frozen accounts and claims and counterclaims, none of it hers, all of it poisoned past any honest use. She watched it go without regret. That was the inheritance she chose instead: the truth, ungelded, belonging to everyone it had ever belonged to.

* * *

In spring, she flew north to see the airfield.

It wasn't much to look at yet — a scraped runway through the scrub, one hangar with a new roof, a windsock standing crooked against a sky full of weather coming and going. But Sebastian met her on the apron with grease to his elbows and a grin he didn't try to hide, and beside the hangar, gleaming secondhand and slightly dented, sat a plane.

"Partners draw salaries," he said, before she'd even got her bag off her shoulder. "Modest ones. And partners get a say in the paint color, and I've already decided against orange, so don't ask."

Hazel looked at him. Three months ago he'd been a stranger drowning in her surf, a debt-ridden pilot who took charters he shouldn't because the airfield was dying and pride is expensive. Now the airfield had a name stenciled on the hangar door in fresh white letters: **YARROW & VANE — CHARTER AND SUPPLY**. He'd done it without asking. Or rather, he'd done it the way he did everything — by simply assuming she'd come, and being right.

"The medicine runs start next month," he said, walking her toward the hangar, talking fast the way he did when something mattered. "Interior clinics, mail, whatever pays. And I want to run the coast route regular — there are villages out there that see a plane twice a year if they're lucky." He stopped, and rubbed the back of his neck, and for a moment the competent mask slipped and underneath was just a tired, hopeful man. "I keep thinking about the cave. How long it stayed shut. Everything in this country stays buried until somebody goes looking, and mostly nobody goes looking."

"Somebody went looking," Hazel said.

"Yeah." He smiled. "Took a plane crash to manage it."

They stood together in the hangar doorway while the evening came down gold over the scrub, and Hazel thought about tides — how they take and how they give back, how the sea keeps what it keeps and returns the rest changed. She had lost a family legend and gained a family truth. She had walked out of a cave with empty hands and somehow owned more than she ever had.

"So," Sebastian said. "First run's north, day after tomorrow. Weather looks fair." He glanced sideways at her, half challenge, half invitation. "Want to co-pilot?"

Hazel laughed — really laughed, the first time since the water — and looked up at a sky wide enough to hold anything.

"Get me a checklist," she said. "I intend to be insufferable about procedure."

And somewhere far south, beneath a fathom of indifferent green water, the cave held its silence — door closed, secret spent, keeping nothing now but the sound of the sea, which had never needed anyone's permission to tell the truth.
