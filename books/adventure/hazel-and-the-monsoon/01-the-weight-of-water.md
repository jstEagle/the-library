## 1. The Weight of Water

The temple had been drowning for two hundred years, and it was in no hurry to finish.

Oscar Nakamura hung forty feet down in the green half-light, her breath rasping through the regulator in slow, deliberate pulls, and let the water hold her. Below her the flooded sanctuary spread out like a drowned cathedral—columns furred with silt, a broken naga balustrade reaching up from the mud with its stone jaws open as if still singing. The monsoon had come early this year and stayed late, and the river had swallowed the old sanctuary whole, which was why the salvage company was paying her, and why she was here at all. Work like this came once a season if you were lucky. She'd been lucky twice now, which by her own arithmetic meant she was overdue for bad luck.

She checked her gauge. Forty minutes. The foreman, a squat man named Deng with a voice like gravel in a tin cup, wanted the sanctuary floor cleared by week's end, and Oscar had learned long ago that foremen were weather—you didn't argue with them, you just read them and adjusted your sails.

She kicked downward.

The silt on the sanctuary floor was soft as flour and twice as treacherous. One careless fin-stroke and the whole chamber would cloud into milk, blind her for twenty minutes, maybe cost her a column or a lintel. So she moved the way her mother had taught her to move, thirty years ago, in another country, in another life: flat and slow, hands leading, weight distributed, *reading* the bottom before touching it. Her mother used to say the mud remembered everything that ever fell into it, and it resented being disturbed. Oscar had thought that was poetry when she was nine. Now she knew it was procedure.

Her lamp swept across the floor. Broken pots. A bronze bell crusted green. The socket where a statue had been pried out sometime in the last century by someone with less patience than she had—the gouge marks were ugly, greedy, human. She catalogued each find mentally, marking positions against the columns the way the survey team wanted, and worked her way toward the inner sanctum, the deepest room, the one the sonar had flagged as anomalous.

The doorway was narrow. She turned sideways to pass through, and the water changed.

It was a small thing, the kind of thing you'd miss if you hadn't spent your life underwater. The temperature dropped. The current died. The sound of her own breathing seemed to fold inward, as though the room beyond the threshold were listening. Old divers called rooms like this *quiet water*, and every one of them had a story about quiet water, and none of those stories ended well.

Oscar went through anyway.

Her lamp found the far wall first—a frieze, buried to its waist in silt, carved with figures in procession. Not the local style. The faces were wrong for it, the headdresses wrong, the script running beneath the figures in tight vertical columns that she didn't recognize and couldn't read. She'd dived sites from the Mekong's mouth to the Irrawaddy delta, and she knew the region's visual grammar the way she knew her own hands. This wasn't it. This was something older, or something else, or both.

*Log it and move on,* said the sensible part of her, the part that kept her alive. *It's not yours. Nothing is yours. That's how you've survived.*

She logged it and moved on.

The chest sat in the corner behind the altar block, tucked into the angle of the walls like something deliberately hidden rather than accidentally lost. Lead-lined. Wooden staves gone black with age but still sound under her gloves—waterlogged hardwood, dense as iron. It was perhaps the length of her forearm, and it was stamped on the lid with a seal: a circle, and inside the circle a design she could not parse at first—a river? a root system? a tree with too many branches?—and around the rim, characters in that same unknown script.

She knelt in the silt beside it and did not touch it for a full minute.

Because there was a feeling she got, sometimes, down deep, that she had never learned to name. Her mother would have named it. Her mother had names for everything—had spent her life naming things no one else believed existed. But her mother was twelve years dead, and Oscar had spent those twelve years making sure she never needed anyone's vocabulary but her own. So she just knelt there in the cold and the green dark, breathing, while the feeling rose in her chest like water rising in a sealed chamber, and she waited to see whether it was dread or recognition.

It was both.

The lock was small, bronze, set into a hasp that had corroded only at the surface. And hanging from the hasp on a rotted cord was a key.

A key. On a two-hundred-year-old chest, at the bottom of a flooded temple, in a country that had never had a name for the nation whose seal was pressed into its lid—and the key was just *there*, as if the last person to lock it had meant for it to be found, or had run out of time, or had died before they could take it with them.

Oscar looked at the key for a long moment. Then she looked back toward the doorway, toward the distant flicker of Deng's crew working the upper chambers, toward the world above the water where everything had a price and a paperwork trail.

She took the key off the cord and put it in the zip pocket of her wetsuit, against her sternum, where she kept the few things that mattered.

Then she lifted the chest.

It came up heavier than lead alone explained. She got it into the lift bag, clipped the line, sent it rising, and followed it up through the green silence, past the naga with its open mouth, past the light line where the river went from night to noon, and broke the surface into a morning so bright and loud it felt like surfacing into a different century altogether.

* * *

Deng met her at the barge rail with his clipboard already open.

"Anything?" he asked, which was foreman for *what did you find that I can sell*.

"Pottery," Oscar said. "A bell. A looted socket. The frieze in the inner sanctum is unusual—different tradition, maybe pre-dating the site. You'll want an archaeologist before anyone touches it."

"We have an archaeologist." He said it the way other men said *we have a lawyer*. "The anomaly?"

"Empty room," Oscar said. "Silt and old air."

His eyes narrowed. Deng was not a stupid man; stupid men didn't last as foremen on salvage crews, because stupid men trusted their divers. He looked at her the way he looked at sonar returns that didn't match the charts—with suspicion, and then, because suspicion cost money, with decision. "Survey says the mass reading was concentrated."

"Mass readings don't account for waterlogged timber," Oscar said. "Or for how much mud a room collects in two hundred years. Ask your own people."

He wrote something on his clipboard. Whether it was about the temple or about her, she couldn't tell, and she'd learned not to care. She signed the dive log—*empty chamber, debris field consistent with collapse*—and walked aft to the gear bench, where the chest lay hidden inside her collapsed equipment bag, wrapped in her spare wetsuit, looking like nothing more than ballast.

The rule was simple, and she'd built her whole life around it the way a boat is built around its keel: *never stay longer than a season.* One season, one job, one riverport. Long enough to earn, short enough to leave before anyone learned her habits, her routes, the name she actually answered to in her own head. Twelve years, thirty-one ports, four countries. She owned a duffel bag, a dive kit, a battered brass compass she never used, and nothing else—not a lease, not a debt, not a photograph, not a promise. People who accumulated things accumulated claims. Claims were anchors. Anchors dragged you down, and Oscar Nakamura had spent twelve years making sure that when the water finally took her, it would take nothing it could hold her with.

The compass was the exception, and she didn't count it, because she'd never forgiven it enough to call it hers.

* * *

That evening the barge tied up at the work camp on the north bank, a scatter of corrugated sheds and drying nets under a sky going the color of a bruise. Rain came in off the hills in gray curtains, and the river ran high and brown and muscular, carrying whole trees downstream like it was clearing its throat.

Oscar waited until the camp settled into its evening rhythms—generators, card games, someone's radio playing a love song in a language she almost understood—before she carried her bag into the storage shed and opened it.

The chest came out into the lamplight looking worse and better than it had underwater. Worse, because the wood had begun to check along one stave, the way old wood does when it leaves the water it has spent centuries adjusting to. Better, because the seal showed clean under direct light, and clean light made it stranger, not clearer. A circle. Inside it, branching lines that could be a river system seen from above, or roots, or a family tree drawn by someone who thought families grew like rivers. Around the rim, the unknown script, patient and closed as a fist.

She turned it over. The base bore a second stamp, smaller: a date, or what she assumed was a date, in the same script, and below it a single character repeated three times, evenly spaced, like a refrain.

She tried the key.

It fit the lock the way a word fits a sentence it was always meant for. The mechanism was beautiful—bronze pins, a falling-lever design she'd only ever seen described in her mother's old textbooks—and it turned with a sound like a held breath released. The lid came up on a smell of cold metal and something faintly sweet, resinous, like a temple interior shut for centuries.

Inside: padding of oiled silk gone stiff as bark. And beneath it—

Nothing. No gold. No documents. Just a shallow false bottom, and beneath the false bottom, fitted into a recess cut with obsessive precision, a scrap of waterproofed paper folded into eighths, and a second, smaller object wrapped in waxed linen that she did not unwrap yet, because her attention had snagged on the paper like a fishhook.

She unfolded it with hands that were steadier than she expected them to be.

It was a note. Brief. In English. In handwriting she knew better than her own—knew it the way you know a scar, the particular forward lean of it, the way the letter *r* always trailed off unfinished, as if the writer's mind had already moved to the next word:

*If you're reading this, the key found you before I could. Don't trust the bureau. Don't trust anyone who says the word "preservation." Go upstream. — M*

Twelve years since her mother died—disgraced, discredited, disowned by every institution that had ever praised her—and Oscar had trained herself out of flinching at the sight of that handwriting. Museum catalogs still carried it. Conference proceedings still cited it, usually with footnotes about fraud. She had made herself able to look at it the way you look at a photograph of a stranger.

But this wasn't a catalog. This was a note written to whoever found the chest, and the ink was fresh enough that her thumb smudged it, and the paper was waterproofed in a modern way, with a polymer coating that had not existed two hundred years ago, and the key had been hanging on the outside of the lock like a gift.

Which meant her mother had been here. Or someone with her mother's hand had. Or—

Oscar sat very still in the lamplight of the storage shed and let the implications arrange themselves, and they arranged themselves into a shape she did not like at all.

Because there was a second problem, and the second problem was worse.

The note had been inside the chest. The chest had been inside the temple. The temple had been under forty feet of water until Oscar herself had brought the chest up six hours ago. Therefore the note had been placed in the chest after the chest was recovered—or the chest had been recovered before, by someone else, and re-sunk, and seeded, and left for her specifically.

And if someone had gone to that much trouble to put her mother's handwriting in front of her—

Then someone knew who she was. Knew whose daughter she was. Knew the name she'd buried along with her mother, and had dug it up, and had used it as bait.

She looked at the waxed-linen bundle. Didn't touch it. Some instincts you honor.

Instead she repacked the chest exactly as she'd found it, locked it, wiped it down, and turned to her own dive bag—the one she'd carried aboard, the one that had been sitting unattended in the gear rack all day while she was underwater, while the crew worked, while Deng made notes on his clipboard.

She went through it methodically. Mask, fins, knife, spare regulator, the drybag with her papers under a false name. Everything where she'd left it. Except—

At the bottom, wedged between the spare mask strap and the emergency flare, was a piece of waterproofed paper she had never put there.

Folded once. Her mother's handwriting. Two words, in that same forward-leaning hand, and nothing else:

*They're watching.*

Oscar Nakamura sat down slowly on the gear bench, in the lamplight, in the rain-hammered shed, and listened to the river go by in the dark, and understood that the season was over.

She'd broken her rule without meaning to. She'd stayed somewhere long enough for someone to learn her. And whatever came next—whatever game was being played with her mother's ghost and a dead nation's seal—she was already inside it, the way you're already inside a current before you feel it pull.

She folded both notes together, tucked them against the bronze key over her heart, and began, calmly and completely, to pack.
