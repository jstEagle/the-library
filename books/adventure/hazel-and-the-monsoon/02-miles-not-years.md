## 2. Miles, Not Years

The chest sat on the floor of my rented room like a sleeping animal, and I had been circling it for three days.

I told myself I was waiting for the right buyer. That was a lie I could live with, the way you can live with a stone in your boot if the road is long enough. The truth was that I hadn't opened it again since that first night, when my fingers found the seal stamped into the lead—two rivers braided around a mountain, worn shallow by a century of water—and something in my chest had closed like a fist.

I knew seals. I'd grown up over dinner tables where seals were discussed the way other families discussed weather. My mother kept a drawer of them: wax impressions, brass dies, rubbings taken off temple doors and merchant graves from Champa to Sukhothai. She could read a stamp the way a doctor reads a face. And she had never once, in all her lectures and all her patient explanations, shown me anything like this.

Two rivers. A mountain. A script beneath it that wasn't Thai, wasn't Khmer, wasn't Lao or Burmese or any of the dozen hands I'd learned to recognize just to keep up with her.

It meant nothing. That's what I told myself. It meant nothing, and I would sell it quietly, take my cut, and be three hundred miles downriver before anyone asked questions.

That was the other rule, the one that lived alongside never staying longer than a season: sell fast, travel light, leave nothing behind that anyone could follow. Twelve years of practice. Twelve years of getting very good at not being anywhere.

* * *

My mother died in the dry season, twelve years ago this month, in a hospital in the capital with a view of a drainage canal.

I was nineteen. I had spent the previous two years not speaking to her—not properly speaking, not the way we used to speak, bent over the same map with our heads almost touching. The estrangement, when I try to explain it to people who had mothers they loved plainly, always sounds small. Petty. A girl's grievance inflated by time into a continent.

But grief does its cartography in exaggeration. Every distance gets redrawn.

She had disgraced herself—there was no softer word for it. She stood up at a conference in front of forty of her peers and claimed there had been a nation in these highlands that every treaty, every atlas, every respectable history had agreed to forget, and that she could prove it, and that the proof lay upstream past the last mapped bend. She said the word *Malaya* out loud in a room full of people whose careers depended on maps being finished, complete, settled things. She might as well have stood up and said the river ran backward.

They took her consultancy away first. Then her university post. Then, more slowly, her colleagues, her invitations, her name from the acknowledgments pages of books she had essentially written. She stopped publishing. She started going upstream alone, for weeks, coming back thinner and stranger and more certain, and I—I did what children of the obsessed do. I chose the world that had rejected her, because it was easier to be wrong with everyone than right with no one.

The last thing I ever said to her face was: *You care more about a country that doesn't exist than about me.*

She didn't argue. That was the worst part. She just looked at me with an expression I spent twelve years refusing to identify, and now, standing over a lead chest in a rented room above a machine shop in Klong Suan, I finally had a word for it. It hadn't been hurt. It had been *patience*. The look of someone holding a door open, knowing you'll walk through it eventually, knowing too what walking through it will cost.

She wrote to me after that. Letters, plural—I learned later there were four—but only one reached me before the end, forwarded through three addresses because I'd already begun the habit of moving. I read it once, drunk, on a dock in Kampot, and threw it in the sea. I have never forgiven myself for that, though I've gotten very skilled at not thinking about it, which is a different skill entirely and one I practiced daily.

After she died, I sold everything she'd left me except the compass—the battered brass one she'd put in my hands when I was nine, saying *so you can always find your way back*—and I went to work on the water. Salvage diving suited me. It's a trade for people who don't want roots: you arrive, you go down, you bring things up, you get paid, you leave. The drowned don't ask where you're from. The dead keep no company but their own, and they are excellent listeners.

I measured my life in miles after that. Not years. Years are for people who expect to arrive somewhere. Miles were honest. Four hundred miles down the Mekong's brown spine. Sixty miles up a tributary so narrow the canopy closed overhead like a hand. Eleven miles across a reservoir that had swallowed three villages whole, where on still nights the old church bell of one of them was said to ring under the water, though I dove there twice and never heard it, and part of me was grateful, because I think if I had heard it I would have had to stop moving altogether.

Twelve years. Maybe nine thousand miles. I'd never added them up until the chest came out of the flooded temple, and then, lying awake above the machine shop listening to the compressors cough, I added them, and the number made me feel like a woman who had walked the length of a country and arrived nowhere at all.

* * *

Klong Suan's market opened before dawn, the way river markets do, in that blue half-light when the water looks solid and the boats come sliding out of the mist like thoughts you haven't decided to have yet.

I went early because quiet is bought early. I'd dressed down—faded shirt, no dive watch, the compass left behind in my sock because I trusted no one here, least of all myself—and I carried the chest wrapped in a rice sack, heavy as sin against my hip. The bronze key rode in my breast pocket. I still hadn't admitted to myself why I'd stolen it instead of logging it. Foreman Rattan had held out his hand for it, and I'd watched my own fingers close around it and say *must have dropped it below*, and the lie had come out smooth as silt, and that smoothness had frightened me more than the seal had.

There is a row of brokers along the north quay who deal in the gray market—recovered goods, unprovenanced bronzes, the occasional thing pulled out of the mud that makes their eyes go careful. I picked the third stall, an old man named Somchai with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and hands stained green from decades of handling copper. He'd bought from me before. He didn't ask questions, which I valued, and he paid fairly, which I valued more.

He unwrapped the sack, looked at the chest for a long moment, and then looked at me over his glasses with an expression I didn't like.

"Where," he said, "did this find you?"

"Flooded wat, south of Nakhon. Standard contract."

"Mm." He turned the chest with both hands, slow, respectful, the way you turn a body. When the seal came around to face him he stopped turning it. His thumbs rested on either side of it without touching, the way you'd keep your hands off a burn.

"Standard contract," he repeated.

"Somchai." I kept my voice level. "You've gone strange."

"I've gone *careful*." He set the chest down and stepped back from it slightly, and I watched a man who had handled looted crowns and temple bells and at least one thing he swore was cursed decide that he wanted distance between himself and a box. "Oscar. You know I like you. You know I pay straight. So believe me when I tell you to take this back to the mud you got it from."

"The seal—"

"Is not for sale in my stall." He was already pulling a cloth over the chest, briskly, like covering a birdcage at night. "Not because of price. Because of what comes asking. There are men who buy history and men who bury it, girl, and this—" he tapped the cloth once, "—this belongs to the second kind. Whatever nation owned that stamp, somebody worked very hard to make sure it stayed owned by nobody."

"Then you won't mind if I try elsewhere."

"I'll mind what happens to you." He said it gruffly, pushing the glasses up again, and for a moment he looked older than the river itself. "You've been coming through here six years. Always alone. Always leaving. Some cargo a person shouldn't carry, and yours has been looking heavier every season."

I should have listened. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the river keeps: Oscar Nakamura was warned, and she was too hungry and too curious and too tired of being unmoored to listen.

Because that was the thing I couldn't say to Somchai. The chest wasn't just cargo anymore. From the moment I'd seen the seal, some locked room in me had swung open, and inside it was my mother's voice saying *past the last mapped bend*, and I needed money for fuel and food and silence, yes—but underneath all that, I needed to know. And needing to know is the most dangerous condition a drifter can catch. It's how drifters become seekers. Seekers stop measuring in miles.

"Elsewhere, then," I said, and lifted the chest.

"That's your right." Somchai sighed. "Try the far end of the quay. But if a smooth man in good boots starts talking to you about preservation, walk away, girl. Walk away and keep walking."

* * *

The far end of the quay was quieter, all broken planking and drying nets, and that's where Voss found me—which I understood later was not coincidence, because nothing with Voss ever was.

He was maybe fifty, dressed like a man who had studied photographs of explorers and improved on them: linen jacket, good boots, a panama hat worn with just enough irony. Handsome in the way of well-kept ruins. He had a cane he clearly didn't need, carved from some dark wood, and he used it to gesture at the chest before I'd even set it down.

"You're selling the Malaya piece."

Not a question. The word landed in my stomach like a dropped anchor.

"I'm sorry," I said, "the what?"

He smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the smile of a man who had practiced charm until charm practiced itself. "No need for games. I saw Somchai turn you away—he turns everyone away from that particular item, bless his cautious heart. Word travels down a quay faster than oil." He circled the chest once, slowly, and I noticed his eyes never actually touched mine; they stayed on the lead, on the shape of the seal showing faintly through the rice sack. "May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He knelt, drew back the cloth, and looked at the seal the way my mother used to look at things—with total, hungering attention—and something cold moved through me, because I had seen that expression before, on her face, in the years when the obsession was eating her alive.

"Two rivers braided about a mountain," Voss murmured. "And the old script beneath. Do you know what you're carrying, Ms.—?"

"Nakamura."

"Ms. Nakamura." He rose, brushing off his knees. "A hundred years ago, there was a kingdom in the highlands at the head of this river. Wealthy. Literate. Treaty-signing. It appears in exactly four surviving documents, all fragmentary, all disputed. Then the colonial powers redrew the border, and Malaya"—he said the name lightly, tasting it—"was divided, absorbed, renamed, and finally erased. Not conquered. *Erased.* There's a difference. Conquest leaves ruins. Erasure leaves questions." He smiled again. "And questions, Ms. Nakamura, are the only commodity that appreciates forever."

"You know a lot about a country that doesn't exist."

"I make it my business to know about things that don't exist. They're less crowded." He produced a card from his breast pocket—heavy stock, embossed, just a name and a satellite phone number. VOSS. No first name, no firm, no address. "I'd like to make you an offer. A serious one. But not here, and not in a hurry. Haste is how sellers get robbed, and I suspect you've been robbed enough for one lifetime."

"I haven't agreed to sell."

"No. But you carried it to a market, which means you intend to. And you'll find, if you shop it around, that every legitimate buyer will suddenly develop scruples, and every illegitimate one will suddenly develop fear. I am the only man on this river who will pay you what it's worth, because I am the only man on this river who understands what it *is*." He tipped the panama hat a fraction. "Three days. I'll be at the Grand Meridian. Do think about preservation, Ms. Nakamura. It's such a noble word. It covers so many sins."

He walked away down the quay, cane clicking, and the morning crowd parted around him without seeming to notice they were doing it, the way water parts around a hull.

I stood there holding a chest that had doubled in weight in ten minutes, watching him go, and I ran the conversation back through my head the way I'd run a dive plan, checking for faults, and found one sitting at the bottom like a sunken engine block:

I had never said the seal's design out loud. Two rivers, a mountain. Somchai hadn't described it either—he'd only refused it.

Voss had known what was under the cloth before he knelt down.

Which meant Voss had known about the chest before I ever brought it to market. Which meant either the flooded temple had ears, or someone had been watching me since the day I came up out of the green water with a dead nation's seal in my hands.

I looked down the quay after him. Mist was burning off the water, boats loading fruit and fuel and fish, the ordinary commerce of a morning that no longer felt ordinary at all. Somewhere in that crowd, probably, his people. Somewhere else, possibly, other people—Somchai's *men who bury it*, the ones who come asking.

Nine thousand miles, and everywhere I'd gone, I'd been light. Unattached. Impossible to follow, because there was nothing to follow.

Now there was a chest. Now there was a key in my pocket I'd lied to keep. Now there was a name—*Malaya*—that sounded, when I finally let myself say it silently, exactly the way my mother had said it across a conference hall while her career bled out on the carpet in front of her.

I picked up the chest and carried it back toward my room, and I caught myself doing something I hadn't done in twelve years: I caught myself wishing she were alive, so I could ask her what the hell I'd found.

The ache of that was so familiar it was almost comfortable. That's the trap of being unmoored—you start mistaking the drifting for the destination, the ache for an identity. I had built a whole self out of leaving. And standing there in the noise of the market with a dead nation under my arm, I understood, dimly and unwillingly, that the self had a hole in it shaped exactly like a woman I'd refused to forgive, and that no amount of miles would fill it.

Back in the room, I set the chest down and dug my dive bag out from under the cot to check the key against the lock—one small, controlled act of curiosity, I told myself, nothing more.

The bag was as I'd left it. Almost.

Tucked into the side pocket, folded into a square no bigger than a matchbook, was a scrap of waterproofed paper that I had never put there. I unfolded it with hands that had gone very steady, the way they do at depth, and the handwriting hit me before the words did—the quick left-leaning slant, the loops she never bothered closing, the handwriting I'd know blind, underwater, dead.

My mother's handwriting. On paper that had been in my bag. In my locked room.

The note was one line, and I read it four times before the meaning crawled all the way in:

*Whoever searched your things knows what you carry. Trust the ferryman.*
