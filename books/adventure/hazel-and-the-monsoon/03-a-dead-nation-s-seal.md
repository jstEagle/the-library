## 3. A Dead Nation's Seal

The riverport of Khemmarat smelled of diesel and durian and the sweet rot of fruit left too long in the sun, and Oscar Nakamura had been there eleven days, which was already eight days too long by her own accounting.

She kept the chest under her bunk now, wrapped in a tarp that had once covered an outboard motor, as if canvas could make it anonymous. It couldn't. She'd learned that much in the week since she'd pulled it from the flooded temple—learned it from the way men's eyes slid toward her boat at the fuel dock, from the way the market women who had chattered at her freely for the first three days had gone quiet when she passed, from the way the harbor master's clerk had asked, twice, casually, exactly how long she intended to stay.

Eleven days. In twelve years of drifting since her mother died, Oscar had never stayed anywhere longer than a season. That was the rule, self-imposed and load-bearing: one season, one river, one job, then gone before anyone learned her habits or her face or the name she'd stopped using. The rule had kept her alive through border disputes and salvage wars and two separate occasions on which men with machetes had expressed opinions about her diving claims. The rule was the only structure left in a life she'd built deliberately without any.

And now there was a lead chest in her bunk space stamped with a seal she didn't recognize, and a bronze key warm from her pocket, and the growing, crawling certainty that both of them were going to get her killed.

She sat on the dock behind the boatyard that evening, legs dangling over black water, turning the key between her fingers. It was old work, older than anything else she'd pulled from the temple. The bronze had gone the color of dried tea, and the bit of the key was cut in a pattern she'd never seen—not the warded locks of colonial safes, not the pin tumblers of anything modern. The bow of the key was cast in the shape of a flower she couldn't name: five petals, tightly furled, like something holding itself closed.

*Like me,* she thought, and then hated herself for the sentimentality, and turned the key over again anyway.

The seal on the chest matched it. She'd memorized every line of it by now: a circular device, pressed deep into the lead, showing a river rendered as a sinuous line running between two mountains, and around the rim, in a script of looping curves that was neither Thai nor Lao nor Khmer nor anything else she could read, a ring of characters worn almost smooth. Someone had wanted this chest identified even if its contents were lost. Someone had wanted whoever found it to know it belonged to something.

To what, though? Nations didn't put seals on treasure chests. Governments did. And governments, in Oscar's experience, were the single most dangerous thing a salvage diver could attract.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

The voice came from behind her, low and dry, and Oscar's hand went to the knife at her hip before her head finished turning. An old man stood at the top of the dock steps, thin as a heron, wearing a shirt that had once been white and a pair of spectacles mended with copper wire. He held a bottle wrapped in a banana leaf and an expression of professional curiosity.

"I'm not buying anything," she said.

"Good. I'm not selling." He came down the steps anyway, unhurried, and settled himself on the dock planks a careful arm's length away—the distance of a man who had learned exactly how close strangers could stand. "I'm Jun. I draw maps."

"Nobody draws maps anymore. Satellites do it."

"Satellites draw pictures," Jun said. "Maps are arguments. I lost mine." He unwrapped the bottle, poured a measure into the cap, and offered it to her first, which was either courtesy or a test. She took it. Rice whiskey, harsh enough to make her eyes water, honest enough to be trusted. "You've been asking questions at the market. About old seals. Old scripts."

Oscar said nothing. Asking questions at the market had been a mistake; she'd known it while she was doing it, and had done it anyway, because the alternative was sitting alone with a mystery until it ate her alive. Curiosity was the flaw in all her rules. Her mother had had the same flaw. It was probably the only thing they'd ever shared.

"There's a woman," Jun continued, watching the water, "who comes down from the hills sometimes. Sells orchids. Two years ago she brought me a rubbing she'd taken off a boundary stone upriver, past where the charts go blank, and asked me what it said. I told her the truth, which is that it wasn't in any living script. She asked me whose it was, then. And I told her that too." He took the cap back and drank. "Three weeks later my license was revoked. Fabrication of historical documents, they called it. Inventing a country."

The word landed in Oscar's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it hit bottom.

"What country?" she asked, and her voice came out quieter than she intended.

Jun looked at her then, really looked, the way a man reads water before he commits his boat to it. Whatever he saw made him decide to trust her, or decide she was worth the risk, or simply decide he'd been silent long enough for one lifetime.

"Malaya," he said.

* * *

In Jun's rented room above the boatyard—a room that contained a cot, a kerosene lamp, and four hundred maps rolled into tubes stacked floor to ceiling like the ribs of some enormous wooden whale—he unrolled the oldest chart he owned across the bed and weighted its corners with his boots and the whiskey bottle.

It was a survey map, hand-inked, dated by its style to somewhere around a hundred and ten years ago. The paper had gone amber and brittle at the folds. And there, in faded sepia wash, filling the upper reaches of the river valley Oscar had been diving in for a month, was a territory with borders. Real borders, surveyed and named, with towns marked along the banks and mountain passes inked in confident strokes.

MALAYA, the title block read. Below it, smaller: *Territory ceded and dissolved per the Treaty of Vientiane Annex, Article IX.*

"A client state," Jun said, tracing the border with one cracked fingernail. "Older than the colonies that swallowed it. They had their own dynasty, their own script—you've seen it, don't pretend you haven't—and their own way of keeping records. When the treaty dissolved them, the signatories did something unusual. They didn't just take the land. They took the *past*. Every registry, every archive, every map was collected and destroyed or locked away. Malaya was removed from the atlases within a decade. Within twenty, you couldn't find a scholar who'd admit it had existed. The bureau made sure of that."

"The antiquities bureau."

"The antiquities bureau." Jun's mouth twisted. "Officially, Malaya is a myth. A folk memory. Unsubstantiated. Anyone who produces evidence to the contrary is committing fraud, and fraud against the historical record is a criminal matter." He tapped the chart. "I produced evidence for thirty years. Field surveys. Stone inscriptions. Oral genealogies recorded in nine villages. I built a case a lawyer could have taken to court. The bureau took my license instead, and when that didn't shut me up, they took my teaching post, and when that didn't shut me up, they stopped needing to take anything, because no journal would print me and no university would hire me and a cartographer nobody listens to is just an old man with too much paper."

Oscar stood very still among the map tubes, and the room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a boat tilts when a wave comes from a direction you weren't watching.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said aloud to anyone, the thing she'd carried up from the temple in silence along with the chest: the seal on the lead matched a stamp inside her mother's field notebook. Not the published notebooks—the ones Oscar had memorized as a child, the ones full of potsherds and trade routes and safe, respectable scholarship. The other notebook. The one she'd found three years ago in a storage unit in Chiang Mai, after finally working up the courage to open her mother's remaining effects, the one written in cipher-quick shorthand that Oscar had spent three years failing to crack. On its inside cover, stamped in fading ink, was a river running between two mountains.

Her mother had known. Her mother had been hunting this dead nation in secret, in the last years of her life, while Oscar was learning to hold her breath longer than anyone should have to.

"Why does the bureau burn things?" she asked. "You said—"

"I said they burn whatever carries the seal. Yes." Jun lowered himself onto the cot, and for a moment he looked every one of his years, an old man surrounded by the corpse of his life's work. "There are stories. A village library on the middle reaches, gone in a fire the same winter a collector there started asking about Malayan coinage. A monastery's archive, burned, officially an electrical fault. A French archaeologist, thirty years back, who found a Malayan royal tomb and announced it to the newspapers. His expedition notes were seized as evidence. He died before the trial. Officially of fever." Jun met her eyes. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you, girl. I'm telling you because you have that look. The look of someone who has found something. And I am the last person alive who can tell you what you've found, so you'll forgive me if I want to know what it is before the bureau finds you first."

Oscar thought about lying. She was good at lying; twelve years of drifting taught you to give customs officers a smile and a false manifest and a boat name spelled wrong on purpose. But Jun had lost everything to this question and was still, at the end of it, offering her the truth for free. It felt like theft to answer him with anything less.

So she went back down the stairs, and along the dock, and into her boat, and came back with the chest.

Jun didn't touch it at first. He knelt over it the way a doctor kneels over a patient, hands hovering, reading. His fingers found the seal and traced the river, the mountains, the ring of script, and stopped on the five-petaled flower at the center.

"That's the lotus closed," he murmured. "Their royal device. Open lotus for peace, closed for—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Closed for mourning. Closed for what is hidden and kept." He looked up at her. "Where."

"Flooded temple. Sixty kilometers downriver, maybe seventy. Submerged forty years when they dammed the valley. Everyone says it's just another drowned wat—Buddhist, nineteenth century, nothing special. But the chest was under the altar slab, in a cavity that was sealed with mortar *before* the temple flooded. Somebody hid it there on purpose and never came back for it."

"And the key?"

Oscar hesitated. Then she took it from her pocket and set it beside the chest, and watched Jun's face do something complicated—grief and wonder fighting for the same ground.

"It fits," she said. "I haven't tried it. But it fits. Same bronze, same age, same flower."

"Don't," Jun said sharply, and then, more gently, "not yet. Listen to me. The moment you open that chest, you stop being a salvager who found junk and become a person in possession of Malayan state property. There's no law about that, because officially there's no such thing as Malaya—but the bureau has ways of making laws retroactive, and graves, unfortunately, are also retroactive." He gripped her wrist, his hand surprisingly strong. "Whatever is in there, someone died keeping it hidden. Ask yourself whether they died so that you could dig it up in a riverport with a bureau inspector likely already on the road."

"They're already looking," Oscar said quietly. "The harbor master's clerk. The market women. Someone's been asking about me, and I don't think it's about mooring fees."

Jun released her wrist and sat back on his heels, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Down at the water's edge, a night heron lifted off a piling on soundless wings, and the river went on doing what rivers do, which is carrying everything away except the things that sink.

"Then you have perhaps days," Jun said. "Maybe hours. The bureau has field inspectors stationed at every major crossing, and they pay informants in every port on this river—I know because I used to be one of the people they informed *about*." He began rolling up his ancient chart with hands that trembled slightly, whether from drink or feeling Oscar couldn't tell. "Open it or don't. Run or stay. That's your arithmetic, not mine. But whatever you decide, decide it tonight."

* * *

She decided it at two in the morning, alone in the boat's cabin with the lamp turned low and the cabin door wedged shut with a oarlock, which would stop no one but would at least announce them.

Curiosity overriding caution—that was the story she'd tell herself later, and it was even true, mostly. But underneath the curiosity was something older and less rational. Her mother's handwriting lived inside this mystery somewhere; she could feel it the way divers feel current through a wreck, a pull coming from a direction the light doesn't reach. Twelve years ago she had walked away from her mother's life and everything in it, and had told herself the walking away was freedom. Tonight, kneeling on the deck plates with a bronze key in her fist, Oscar understood that she had never walked away at all. She had just been swimming in circles, and everything she'd swum past was still down there, waiting.

The lock was set into the chest's front face behind a sliding brass plate, and it was small and beautiful and utterly alien: no keyhole she recognized, but a socket shaped like a five-petaled flower, and when she fitted the key into it, the petals of the socket aligned with the petals of the key's bow as if the two had been cast from the same hand in the same hour.

She turned it. The mechanism inside was oiled—*oiled*, after decades underwater, which meant the chest had been maintained, which meant someone had expected it to be opened someday—and the lock gave with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, packed in wool gone grey with age, lay books. Dozens of them. Ledgers bound in oiled leather, their spines stamped with the closed lotus. A scroll case of bamboo. A lacquered box the size of a prayer book. And on top of everything, wrapped in oilcloth, a field notebook with a cracked spine that Oscar recognized before her brain finished processing what her eyes were seeing, because she had grown up watching that notebook disappear into her mother's satchel every morning and reappear every evening smelling of river mud and cigarette smoke.

Her hands were shaking. She made herself unwrap the oilcloth slowly, fold by fold, the way you approach anything that might bolt.

The notebook was her mother's. The handwriting was her mother's—small, slanted, impatient, the letters of a woman always writing faster than the world could happen. And the first page, dated fourteen years ago, read:

*Day 1. If anyone is reading this, it means I failed to keep it hidden and succeeded in leaving a trail, and I hope to God it's Oscar.*

Oscar sat down hard on the deck plates.

The lamp hissed. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere across the port, a dog barked twice and thought better of it, and the night folded itself back around the boat like water closing over a diver.

She did not sleep again that night. She read until the kerosene ran low, and then she read by the gray light coming through the porthole, and what she read was the record of her mother's final years: the secret hunt, the erased nation, the archive at the river's source that the bureau would burn the world to keep buried. Fourteen years of work conducted in shadows, in code, in the gaps of a life her daughter had mistaken for the whole of it.

And on the last written page, mid-sentence, the ink trailing off as though the pen had been set down expecting to be picked up again:

*The elders will only show the source to someone the river itself has marked. Bao understands. If I can reach the falls before the dry season, before they send Seng's people—*

The sentence stopped there. It never resumed.

Oscar closed the notebook and held it against her sternum and breathed, and outside the porthole the sky was turning the color of the inside of a shell, and down at the far end of the dock a car door slammed—once, deliberately, the way doors slam when someone wants to be heard.

Through the porthole, small and framed like a photograph, she watched a tall woman in a government-grey uniform step onto the dock, flanked by two men, and pause to speak with the harbor master's clerk, who pointed.

The pointing finger indicated Oscar's boat.
