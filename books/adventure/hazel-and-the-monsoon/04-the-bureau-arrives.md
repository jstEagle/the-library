## 4. The Bureau Arrives

The rain came in off the river that morning like something with intent, and Oscar was on the *Kestrel's* deck re-caulking a seam when she saw the launch cut through it—white hull, government grey trim, an antenna bristling from its roof like a heron's crest. It moved the way official things move: without hurry, without doubt, as if the water itself had been instructed to part.

She kept her hands moving. Caulk, press, smooth. The seam didn't need her anymore but her hands needed the work, and work was the oldest lie she knew—*I'm busy, I'm nobody, look elsewhere.*

The launch tied up at the customs pontoon two slips down. A woman stepped out first, and even at this distance Oscar could read her the way she read riverbeds: weight forward, eyes already cataloguing. A dark uniform without insignia heavy enough to mean anything, a document case held against her ribs like a child. Behind her came two men who walked like men who had never once been cold or wet by choice.

Oscar finished the seam. Then she went below, took the lead chest out of the bilge locker where it had spent three miserable days wrapped in oilcloth, and put it in the one place no inspector would look: inside the freshwater tank, wedged under the baffle plate, where the drinking water for the whole boat sloshed over it like a blessing. She wiped her hands on her trousers, climbed back into the rain, and made tea on the deck stove because a woman caulking and drinking tea is invisible in any port in the world.

The woman in the dark uniform came down the pontoon at half past nine exactly.

"Oscar Nakamura." Not a question. Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had learned that quiet carried further than shouting on open water. "Inspector Adira Seng. State Antiquities Bureau." She did not offer a hand. She offered instead a laminated card, holding it the way a doctor holds an X-ray.

Oscar looked at it long enough to be polite. "We're not carrying antiquities. Dive charter. Salvage consulting, sometimes."

"I know what you carry." Seng's gaze went past Oscar's shoulder, taking in the *Kestrel* with a single unhurried sweep—the dive gear drying on the rail, the compressor, the chart table visible through the companionway. "A chest. Lead-lined. Recovered from the flooded temple at Wat Phra Nam on the eleventh. Your salvage crew logged forty-one items." She opened the document case and drew out a sheet. "Forty-one. But the temple inventory from the original survey lists forty-two."

"The survey's wrong," Oscar said. "They usually are."

"They usually are," Seng agreed, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "May I see your manifest?"

Oscar handed it over and watched her read it, and hated how steady her own breathing stayed. This was the thing about lying that no one told you: it wasn't hard while you were doing it. It was hard after, in the silence where the truth sat waiting to be missed.

Seng returned the manifest. Then, instead of leaving, she reached into the case again and brought out a photograph, holding it up between two fingers so the rain couldn't touch it.

"Do you know this woman?"

The world did the thing it always did around photographs of her mother—it narrowed, went quiet, went underwater. The picture was old, twenty years at least, taken at some dig site: a woman crouched at the edge of an excavation trench, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth, laughing at whoever held the camera. Laughing. Oscar could count on one hand the times she'd seen that laugh after the disgrace, and every one of them had been for strangers, never for her.

"No," Oscar said.

The word came out wrong. Too fast, too flat, pitched half a tone above her usual voice, and she heard it land on Seng like a dropped tool. The inspector's eyes came up from the photograph and rested on her face for a long, patient moment.

"No?"

"Should I?"

"She was a consultant for this bureau once. Dr. Miriam Nakamura." Seng said the name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Before your time, perhaps."

"I don't keep track of other people's consultants."

"Mm." Seng slid the photograph away. For a moment she only stood in the rain, which she seemed entirely unbothered by, and looked out at the brown swollen river as if it might testify. "The temple site is sealed as of yesterday. All salvage operations suspended pending survey. If anyone approaches you offering to buy unlogged material from that site—" she turned back, and her voice lost nothing to the rain, "—it would be in your interest to report it. The Bureau pays informants well."

"And if I find something myself?"

"Then you'd bring it to me." A pause, precise as a depth gauge settling. "Everyone brings everything to me eventually, Ms. Nakamura. It's just a question of how much of it is still useful when they do."

She left the way she'd come, unhurried, and the two silent men fell in behind her, and the launch pulled away, and Oscar stood on her own deck with rain running off her chin and her heart going like a bilge pump at full throw.

*Dr. Miriam Nakamura.* So they knew. Not about the chest—not yet—but about her. Which meant the seal meant what Jun said it meant, which meant the bureau hadn't come for a missing inventory item at all. They'd come because somewhere in their records, a dead disgraced archaeologist's name had surfaced next to a dead nation's seal, and Inspector Adira Seng had gotten on a boat.

Oscar poured her tea over the side and watched it vanish into the river, and thought: *they're going to search the boat.*

Not today. Today they were being polite, giving her the chance to do the sensible thing, because polite was cheaper than warrants and paperwork. But the clock had started. She had maybe two days before politeness ran out.

She spent the afternoon being loudly, boringly visible—drinking coffee at the dockside stall where everyone could see her, arguing amiably with the harbormaster about mooring fees, buying rope she didn't need. Let the port watch her do nothing suspicious all day. Let Seng's watchers report a woman sulking over fees.

At dusk the rain thickened into the kind of downpour that emptied streets, and Oscar went back aboard, waited until the pontoon lights blurred and the watchman made his first round, then opened the freshwater tank.

The chest came up streaming, heavier than lead had any right to be, and she set it on the chart table under the swinging lamp and stood looking at it for a long time.

The bronze key was warm from her pocket. It fit the lock the way Jun had promised—no forcing, no tricks, just the small deep click of a mechanism built by people who expected their secrets to last. The hasp lifted. The lid came up on a breath of air that smelled of wax and river mud and something older underneath, something dry, like a room closed for a century.

Inside, packed in wool gone grey with age: a lacquered box. A bundle of documents tied with cord, stamped with the same seal—a sun split by a river's course, crowned in lotus. And on top of them all, wrapped in waterproofed silk the way divers wrap things they intend to survive, a book.

Not a ledger. Not a state archive. A field journal. Cloth-bound, corner-battered, its cover stained with the particular ring-marks of a hundred tin cups set down on it. Oscar knew that binding. She had grown up watching that binding get patched with sail thread and candle wax at kitchen tables in six countries.

Her mother's handwriting was on the first page.

*Day 1 — If anyone ever reads this, I've failed twice: once at my work, and once at keeping you out of it.*

Oscar sat down. Her legs decided it without consulting her.

She turned pages by lamplight while the rain hammered the deck overhead, and the journal opened up like a second river running parallel to her mother's life—one Oscar had never seen, flowing through all the years Oscar had spent hating her.

There were the early entries, careful and academic: surveys of colonial-era records, letters copied out in facsimile, references to a treaty signed in a room in 1889 that had struck Malaya from every map in exchange for peace, on the condition that its archive—its entire memory, genealogies and songs and laws and names—be surrendered and destroyed. Except it never was. There was a line in the treaty, buried in a clause about riverine access, that suggested the archive had gone upstream instead. Into the highlands. Into the care of people whose existence the treaty also required the maps to forget.

*The bureau thinks I'm chasing treasure,* her mother wrote in the third year. *Let them. Treasure they understand. What I'm chasing is a library, and libraries frighten them far more.*

And then the entries changed. Fewer citations, more rivers. Sketches of rapids, of karst formations, of a ferryman's face rendered in quick confident pencil with a note beneath: *Bao remembers more than he says. He carried me past the third gorge when the patrol boats were close. He asks nothing, which frightens me more than questions would.*

Oscar touched the pencil marks. Twelve years ago. Bao had been old then; he must have been ancient now, if he lived at all. Somewhere downstream or upstream, in some bend she'd never bothered with, an old man had poled her mother through gorges toward this, and she had never known.

Page after page. Years compressed into ink. Her mother learning Malayan script from a fisherman's granddaughter. Her mother mapping trade routes by rumor. Her mother writing, in the fifth year, in a hand gone smaller and warier: *They've stopped pretending I'm mistaken. Now they pretend I'm mad. It's kinder, for them. Madness can be pensioned off. Truth requires action.*

And then the disgrace—that was here too, laid out in her mother's own flat account of it, the fabricated provenance scandal, the colleagues who recanted, the career dismantled in committee rooms Oscar had been too young and too angry to enter. Her mother had never defended herself publicly. The journal explained why in four words: *To defend it is to confirm it.*

Oscar read until the lamp oil burned low. Outside, the port slept; inside, twelve years of her mother's secret life lay open under her hands, and every page was a small accusation—not of Oscar, never of Oscar, and somehow that was worst of all. There had been room in this life for obsession, for danger, for a hunted clandestine decade of scholarship. Her mother had simply chosen not to make room for her daughter in it. Or—or she had, and Oscar had refused to look. The thought arrived and she pushed it down like something trying to surface from depth.

The last entry began near the end of the twelfth year. The handwriting had changed again—shakier, hurried, written maybe in a fever or a fear she could only guess at:

*I have it now, most of it. The route. The key. The falls behind which—*

It stopped. Mid-sentence. No date after, no closing, no signature. Just the dash, trailing off the edge of the line like a diver's bubble reaching the surface and breaking.

Oscar sat in the cold lamp light with the journal open on her knees and found that she was crying—no warning, no build-up, just suddenly, the way a hull lets go. Twelve years of not crying, hoarded like ballast, all of it coming loose at a sentence fragment. Because this was worse than the disgrace, worse than the funeral where nobody came, worse than the letter she'd never answered. This was her mother alive on every page, funny and stubborn and afraid, hunting something true enough to die for—and dying, apparently, mid-word, with the ending locked somewhere upstream behind a waterfall.

*The falls behind which—*

Behind which what?

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the sparks came. When she could see again, she turned back through the journal slowly, deliberately, a diver checking her gauges, looking for anything she'd skimmed past. And near the end—three entries before the broken one—she found a line she'd read straight over in her grief-blind sweep, small, almost casual, tucked at the bottom of a page of route notes:

*If it's you reading this, little fish: the map is in the only thing I ever gave you. You'll forgive me eventually. Or you won't, and you'll find it anyway, because you were always better at finding things than at forgiving.*

Oscar looked across the cabin, to the shelf above her bunk where a battered brass compass hung on a nail, its glass clouded, its needle long since gone lazy.

The only thing she ever gave you.

The rain kept on. Down the pontoon, a lantern moved—slow, deliberate, making its rounds—and Oscar sat very still in the dark with her mother's journal in her lap, listening to footsteps pass, and understood that whatever she decided next, she had already run out of seasons.
