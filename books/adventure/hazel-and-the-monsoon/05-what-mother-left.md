## 5. What Mother Left

The lamp in the stern cabin burned low, a yellow coin of light swimming in the dark of the impounded boat's hold. Oscar had wedged herself between the fuel drums and the bulkhead where the paint had bubbled and peeled into shapes like drowned continents, and she read by that coin of light with her knees drawn up, the journal open across them like something fragile and alive.

She had not meant to read it all in one night. She had told herself she would take it slowly, a few pages at a time, the way you ration water in the dry season. But her mother's handwriting had a gravity to it—the letters small and slanted, leaning forward as if hurrying somewhere—and every page ended on a question that could only be answered by the next one. So Oscar read. She read until the mosquitoes found the gaps in the netting and the river slapped its slow applause against the hull, and she read until the journal ran out.

It ran out mid-sentence.

*The elders speak of a cave behind the falls where the water goes to be born. I have seen the mouth of it twice, once in flood and once—*

Nothing. The page was half blank, the pen stroke trailing off in a thin gray line like a bird's track across snow. The paper after it was empty, and empty, and empty, and then the back cover, and inside the back cover, folded into quarters so old the creases had gone soft as cloth, a letter addressed in the same forward-leaning hand:

*To my daughter.*

Oscar did not open it.

She held it instead, feeling its weight—or rather its weightlessness, the way a thing can weigh nothing and still press down on you like a slab of stone. Her thumb moved along the fold. The paper was the same waterproofed stock her mother had used for field notes, waxy and pale, tough as skin. Twelve years it had sat folded there, traveling through flooded temples and pawnshops and the bottom of dive bags, waiting for hands steady enough to unfold it.

Her hands were not steady enough. Not tonight.

She put the letter back exactly as she'd found it, refolded along its soft creases, and closed the journal, and sat in the dark listening to the river, which went on saying what it always said: *keep moving, keep moving, keep moving.*

But the journal wasn't finished with her. Because near the end, three pages before the sentence that never finished, her mother had written something else. Oscar had skimmed past it an hour ago, hungry for the story, but now it rose up out of memory with the insistence of a fish breaking water.

*I have left her a map. Not on paper—paper burns, paper rots, paper can be seized. I have left it where no bureau will ever think to look, in the only thing I ever gave her. If she is reading this, if she has come this far, then she already knows what I mean. Forgive me for hiding it there. I wanted it to be somewhere safe, and there was nowhere safer than the place she would carry whether she forgave me or not.*

Oscar sat very still.

The only thing she ever gave her. The words turned over in the dark like stones in a current. Her mother had given her plenty of things, technically—a name, a childhood of dig sites and ferry decks and hotel rooms that smelled of mildew and insect coil, a way of holding her breath so long the world went soft at the edges. But *gave*, in the sense of a gift, chosen and wrapped and offered—there was only one.

The compass.

Oscar reached for her dive bag without deciding to, the way you reach for a railing when the deck lurches. The bag was canvas, army-surplus, older than she was, its straps replaced twice with braided polypropylene. It had been through more rivers than most boats. Everything she owned lived in it or hung from it, and everything she owned fit inside a space smaller than grief, which was convenient, because grief took up the rest.

The compass was in the side pocket, wrapped in a sock. It had been in the side pocket, wrapped in a sock, for twenty-three years. A battered brass compass the size of a pocket watch, its case dented where a nine-year-old had dropped it on a ferry gangway in some delta town whose name she'd never learned, its glass scratched in an arc from the time she'd used it, at eleven, to scrape barnacles off a hull because she'd read somewhere that sailors did that and wanted to be a sailor more than she wanted anything. The bezel no longer turned smoothly; it caught and clicked, caught and clicked, like a joint with gravel in it. The needle had a stutter in it north of east, a little hesitation, a hitch in its breath, and she had never once wondered why. You don't wonder about a gift you haven't forgiven.

Because that was the truth of it, the truth she kept folded up tighter than any letter: she had never forgiven the compass. It had been given to her the week before the disgrace broke, the week before the hearings and the headlines and the colleagues who suddenly couldn't remember her mother's first name. *So you can always find your way home,* her mother had said, crouching on the dock, pressing it into her palms, closing her fingers over it with both of her own hands. And then her mother had gone back upriver, and the finding-home had become a joke Oscar told strangers in bars—the compass that pointed everywhere except home, because home had been repossessed along with the house and the reputation and, eventually, the woman herself.

She carried it anyway. Twenty-three years, four countries, eleven rivers, two arrests, one marriage that lasted the length of a dry season. She carried it the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out: not fondly. Necessarily.

Now she unwrapped it from the sock and set it in the coin of lamplight, and the brass drank the yellow and gave back nothing, just sat there being old and dented and mute.

*In the only thing I ever gave her.*

Her heart was doing something stupid. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather in another province. Her hands were not shaking—that was a lie she would tell Jun later—but they were careful, careful in a way they never were around rope or wreck or razor-edged salvage, careful the way hands get when they're afraid of waking something.

The faceplate was held by a ring of tiny screws, brass, slotted, softened by decades of thumbs. She had no jeweler's screwdriver. She looked around the cabin—at the tool roll hanging by the door, at the spools and shackles and the jar of miscellaneous screws that every boat accumulates the way a tide pool accumulates crabs—and settled on the smallest blade of her dive knife, which was wrong for the job in every measurable way and worked anyway, the way things do when you want them badly enough.

The first screw fought her. The second one stripped halfway and she swore, quietly, in the language of her childhood, which was mostly English with a sediment of three other tongues. The third came free with a click that sounded enormous in the little cabin. She worked her way around the ring, screw by screw, laying them in a row on her knee like a row of tiny brass vertebrae, and when the last one came loose she tipped the faceplate up with the knife's tip, gently, the way you lift a stone to see what lives underneath.

Beneath the dial, where there should have been only the compass card and the pivot and the damp-proofed void of the case, there was a second plate. Thin as foil. Etched.

Oscar stopped breathing—not dramatically, not the controlled suspension of a diver conserving air, but the ordinary animal stoppage of a body confronted with something it doesn't have a category for. She tilted the case toward the lamp. The foil caught the light and threw it back in lines, hair-fine lines, thousands of them, dense as rain on a window.

Lines that were roads. Lines that were rivers. Lines that were contour marks climbing in close-set terraces toward something circled at the top of the plate, a circle drawn with a single confident stroke, and beside the circle, characters in a script she didn't know—Malayan, she thought, it must be Malayan, the dead nation's alphabet, the one Jun had traced for her in spilled beer on a bar top two nights ago—and below that, smaller, in English, in her mother's hand rendered impossibly small and impossibly exact:

*Past the last mapped bend. Follow the green water. — M.*

M. Not *Mom*. Not *Mother*. M, the initial, the way her mother signed field notes, the way she signed letters to colleagues, the way she had signed the letter folded in the journal's back cover. Even hidden under the face of a compass, even speaking directly to the daughter she hadn't seen in years, she was still the archaeologist first. Or—Oscar turned the plate in the light, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome—still protecting her. Even this. Even here. No names, nothing legible to a searcher, everything encoded in a script only three living scholars could read. Her mother had hidden a map inside a gift and encrypted the gift itself.

*Somewhere safe. Nowhere safer than the place she would carry whether she forgave me or not.*

Oscar laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, half a laugh and half the other thing, and she pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth until it stopped.

Because here was the arithmetic she had done a thousand times in a thousand riverports, usually at night, usually drunk enough to be honest: her mother had chosen the work over her. That was the sum, that was the total, that was the number the whole equation reduced to. When the bureau disavowed her, when the universities revoked her, when every door in the lowlands closed, her mother had not come home to her daughter. She had gone *upstream*. Deeper into the hunt. Further from the child waiting in a boarding school outside the capital with a brass compass and a phone number that stopped working.

Twelve years Oscar had carried that sum. It balanced. It had always balanced.

And now, under the faceplate of the gift she'd carried through every one of those twelve years, was proof that her mother had been thinking of her the entire time. Not thinking of her *instead of* the work—thinking of her *inside* it. Hiding the map of her life's great obsession in the one object she knew her daughter would never abandon, even in hatred. Especially in hatred. Choosing, deliberately, with an archaeologist's cold patience, the one vessel guaranteed to survive: not paper, not stone, not a bank vault, but a daughter's grudge.

*Whether she forgave me or not.* Her mother had known. Had counted on it. Had built the map's survival on the very anger Oscar had believed proved she didn't matter.

The needle stuttered in its housing, catching north of east, and Oscar understood the stutter all at once, and understanding it hurt worse than anything the bureau or Voss or twelve lonely years had managed. The micro-plate sat millimeters above the pivot. All these years, the map had been bending the needle. Every reading she had ever taken had been slightly, secretly wrong—pulled a degree or two toward the highlands, toward the source, toward the green water past the last mapped bend. Her mother had tuned the compass so that even broken, even resented, even carried by a daughter who refused to open her letters, it would lean, always, faintly, home.

Oscar set the compass down on the deck planking and put both hands flat on her thighs and breathed, because the alternative was something she had not done since she was seventeen and intended not to do now.

The river said *keep moving*. The lamp guttered. Somewhere across the black water a patrol boat coughed and died, and started again, and idled, patient as a crocodile.

She picked the compass back up. Of course she did. She screwed the faceplate down again, vertebra by vertebra, and when she finished, the instrument sat in her palm looking exactly as it always had—dented, scratched, stubbornly alive—and she looked at it for a long time.

"You were carrying it the whole time," she said aloud, to the cabin, to the river, to the woman mid-sentence in a journal twelve years dead. "You absolute—" The word wouldn't come. There wasn't one. In three languages there wasn't one.

She should sleep. Dawn would bring the harbor master, the impound paperwork, Inspector Seng's questions circling closer, Voss's men probably already asking dockhands about a woman who dove alone. She had a stolen chest under a false panel, a dead nation's seal, a journal worth burning, and now a map etched under glass that two different sets of hunters would kill for. The smart move—the move she'd have advised anyone else to make—was to sink the whole business in the channel and be gone by morning, season over, miles onward, unmoored as ever.

Instead she sat cross-legged on the deck with the compass open in one hand and the journal in her lap, and she studied the etched route until her eyes ached, memorizing it the way her mother had taught her to memorize sites before the drawings were done: bend, channel, falls, bend. Past the last mapped bend. Follow the green water. The line climbed northwest through contours that grew denser and denser, terrain stacked on itself, country where a river would run fast and narrow and mean, and ended at the circled place behind the falls where the water went to be born.

The dry season was six weeks out. Maybe five. After that, the upper channels would drop to a trickle, and no ferryman alive would take a boat above the gorge.

Six weeks. She had spent longer deciding what to eat.

Oscar closed the journal, wrapped the compass back in its sock, and slid it into the side pocket of the dive bag—its pocket, its home of twenty-three years, though it felt different now, occupied differently, the way a room feels different once you've learned what's under its floorboards. Then she lay back against the fuel drums and stared at the overhead where the paint peeled into its drowned continents, and let the last hour replay itself in fragments: the half-blank page, the folded letter she still couldn't open, the foil-thin map shining in the lamplight like a vein of ore.

*Whether she forgave me or not.*

There was a knock. Three knuckles, twice, on the hull near the gangway—soft, deliberate, the knock of someone who didn't want to be heard by anyone but her. She knew the rhythm. Old Bao had knocked that way at her mother's camp gates, according to the journal; the ferryman's knock, patient as water.

Oscar sat up in the dark, the letter's soft creases burning a shape against her thoughts, the map's route already printed on the backs of her eyes, and reached for the lamp.

* * *

Outside, the riverport lay under its usual skin of dark—diesel lamps, fish scales, the wet gleam of the walkways. Bao stood at the foot of the gangway with his hands in his sleeves, ancient and upright, his face unreadable as carved wood, and behind him, half-hidden in the shadow of a mooring post, stood a small figure in an oversized jacket who was absolutely failing to look like they weren't watching her.

"The harbor master says your boat is confiscated," Bao said, in the tone of a man reporting the weather.

"I know."

"And the inspector says you are leaving on the morning packet."

"I know that too."

Bao nodded slowly, as if each fact confirmed something he'd suspected about the world for eighty years. His eyes went to the dive bag on her shoulder—to the side pocket, specifically, where the sock-shaped bulge sat exactly where it had sat for twenty-three years—and something moved in the deep creases of his face that might, in a younger man, have been called a smile.

"Your mother," he said, "carried hers the same way."

Oscar's breath went out of her like a diver's last air.

"Tomorrow," Bao said, turning away up the walkway, "you will tell me why you never wrote back. Then we will talk about how far upstream a boat can go before the dry season." He paused at the edge of the lamplight, and the small figure in the shadows shifted, caught, exposed, unmistakably fourteen years old and unmistakably still holding something that glinted brass in one quick, guilty hand.

"But tonight," the old man added, without looking back, "you should count your pockets. The river sends passengers before it sends permission."
