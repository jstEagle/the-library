## 6. Voss Makes an Offer

Voss had learned long ago that the best lies were built on true foundations, and so he told himself the truth first: he wanted the chest. He wanted the compass. He wanted whatever Oscar Nakamura was carrying in that battered dive bag of hers with the same hunger he'd once felt for a woman named Linnea Nakamura, and he dressed the want in finer clothes every time he examined it. Preservation. History. The rescue of things too precious for the mud that made them.

He sat in the back booth of the Golden Carp, a riverport teahouse that smelled of fried shallots and diesel runoff, and watched her through the slats of the ceiling fan. She'd taken the corner table by the window, the one with sightlines to both doors and the dock beyond—of course she had; her mother used to sit the same way, spine to the wall, eyes on the water—and she was drinking tea she clearly didn't taste while turning a bronze key over in her fingers beneath the table's edge, where she thought no one could see.

Voss saw everything. It was his profession, or it had been, before the profession became something else.

The rain came down in the fat, warm sheets of early monsoon, hammering the corrugated awning outside until conversation required leaning close. Good weather for him. Rain made people careless with their voices and generous with their assumptions. Rain meant the river would rise, and rising rivers moved cargo, and cargo was how Voss had built the small, elegant machine of his life: buy low from divers who didn't know what they'd found, sell high to collectors who didn't care what they'd bought, and never, ever hold anything long enough for it to hold you.

Except this. Except her mother. He'd held that too long, and look what it had cost.

He let her finish her tea. He let her count the room twice—once when she sat down, once when the two bureau men in grey shirts walked past the window without coming in, which was itself information, and which Voss noted with the cold satisfaction of a man whose patience was finally paying interest. Then he rose, straightened his linen jacket, and crossed the floor with the unhurried gait of someone who owned every room he entered, even the ones he didn't.

"Miss Nakamura." He set a second cup on her table, poured from the pot she'd already emptied, and signaled the boy at the counter for another. "You'll forgive the intrusion. In my experience, people who sit facing two exits are usually waiting for someone they don't want to wait for."

"I'm not buying anything," she said.

"Neither am I." He smiled, and meant it to be disarming, and knew from the flatness of her stare that it wasn't. "I'm selling."

"I said—"

"You said you're not buying. I heard you. I'm offering you the rarest commodity on this river, Miss Nakamura: an honest conversation." He turned his cup so the handle faced his right hand, a habit from a life spent in rooms where manners were currency. "You pulled something out of the drowned temple at Wat Phra Saeng three nights ago. A lead chest, roughly forty centimeters, sealed with wax that predates the treaty era. You logged it as ballast scrap, which was clever, because your foreman is a lazy man who can't read seals, and you pocketed a key that doesn't appear anywhere in your salvage manifest."

Her hand went still under the table.

Voss spread his own hands, palms up, the universal gesture of harmlessness, though he knew better than anyone that hands were never harmless—they were just honest about what they intended, or they weren't. "Before you decide what I am, consider what I've just demonstrated. I know what you have. Which means other people know what you have. And the ones who know are not sitting in teahouses offering you tea."

"The bureau."

"The bureau," he agreed pleasantly. "Two men in grey shirts just walked past this window without coming in. That's not restraint, Miss Nakamura. That's confirmation. They've already identified you. They're letting you finish your tea because they want to see who you meet." He leaned forward slightly, lowering his voice into the register he reserved for truths. "I'm the only person in this port who wants you rich instead of ruined. Surely that counts for something."

"And what do you want?"

"What I always want." His eyes caught hers and held them, warm as lamplight, sincere as a forged signature. "History preserved. There are objects in this world that belong in museums, behind glass, catalogued and safe, where scholars can study them and children can be educated by them. What you're carrying belongs there. Not in a dive bag. Not in the hands of a state apparatus that burns what embarrasses it." He paused, letting the word *burns* land exactly where he'd aimed it. "Yes. I know about that too. Ask your cartographer friend what happens to things stamped with that seal."

Something flickered across her face—the briefest contraction, there and gone, but Voss had spent thirty years reading faces across bargaining tables, and he knew a hit when he landed one. So the drunk cartographer had talked. Excellent. Jun was exactly as predictable as he'd been at university, before the maps and the disgrace, and predictability was worth more than gold on a river with no banks.

"Name a number," Oscar said.

"Ah." Voss sat back, savoring it. "Now we negotiate."

* * *

They negotiated for an hour, and it was, Voss thought privately, one of the great performances of his career—hers, not his. She played reluctant. She played insulted. She played a woman doing arithmetic on a napkin, and she did it well enough that a lesser buyer would have believed every minute of it. But Voss had watched her mother bargain the same way in these same hills twenty years ago: concede nothing, reveal nothing, keep the real object out of the conversation entirely and let the buyer convince himself it existed.

Linnea Nakamura had been the finest liar Voss ever met, and he had loved her for it, and he had destroyed her for it, and those two facts lived together in him now like snakes in a basket.

"Five hundred thousand," he said finally, and watched her flinch—not at the size of the number, but at what accepting it would mean. "For the chest, unopened or opened, I don't care which. For the key. And for the compass."

"I never mentioned a compass."

"No," Voss agreed softly, "you didn't."

The silence between them stretched taut as a mooring line in flood current. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming the awning like fingers on a coffin lid. Somewhere down the dock a generator coughed to life, and the lights along the waterfront stuttered and steadied.

Oscar Nakamura looked at him for a long moment, and Voss watched her make the calculation he'd been counting on: not *can I trust this man*, but *how much time does trusting him buy me*. She was going to say yes. Not because she believed him—she'd seen through him inside the first five minutes, he was almost certain of it, and the clarity of her seeing was its own strange pleasure—but because a cornered animal takes the open door even when it can smell the trap behind it.

"I need to think about it," she said.

"Of course." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and left a fold of bills under his cup for the boy. "But think quickly, Miss Nakamura. The river is rising, and the bureau's boats are faster than yours, and I am—" he smiled again, genuinely this time, at the truth of it— "I am the most patient man you will ever meet. Tomorrow evening. This table. Bring the compass and we'll conclude our business like civilized people."

He was halfway to the door when she spoke, and her voice stopped him with its quiet.

"Why her?" she asked. "Why did you really come looking for me? People don't pay half a million for lead and brass."

Voss turned. In the fan-shadowed light, with the rain silvering the window behind her, she looked startlingly like her mother—younger, harder, unsalted by grief yet, but the same bones, the same level stare that made men feel audited.

"Because your mother was the best field archaeologist I ever worked with," he said, choosing each word like a man crossing ice, "and because I owe her family a debt." Every word true. Every word a lie. "Good evening, Miss Nakamura."

* * *

The boat was gone by morning.

Not sunk, not stolen—impounded. Voss learned of it before breakfast, the way he learned everything in riverports, through a lattice of dockhands and fuel sellers and customs clerks each paid a little for a little, so that no single bribe was ever large enough to be corruption and no piece of information ever traceable to its source. The Nakamura woman's boat—a thirty-year-old salvage tug called the *Kestrel*, patched hull, rebuilt engine, the kind of vessel that looked worthless and ran forever—had been seized at dawn by the antiquities bureau. Paperwork violation, the notice said. Unregistered salvage equipment. The notice was signed by an Inspector Adira Seng, and the name meant nothing to Voss, which meant she was new, or careful, or both.

He stood on the upper deck of his own boat, the *Meridian*, coffee going cold in his hand, and watched the Kestrel across the harbor with its new government lock on the wheelhouse door and its new government seal pasted over the old ones. Two grey-shirted men lounged on its deck with the studied idleness of guards who expected to be watched.

Well. He hadn't done it, but he couldn't have designed it better himself. The trap was closing from both directions now, exactly as intended, whether he'd intended it or not. Sell to Voss and she handed him the map to Malaya's source, and whatever conscience he claimed afterward would be his own business. Run instead, and she became a fugitive on a river full of checkpoints, easy to track, easier to catch, desperate enough eventually to deal with anyone—including him—at any price.

Either way, the compass came to him. Either way, the archive route opened like a throat.

Unless she did something stupid. Unless she did something brave. The two were indistinguishable at this distance, and Voss had learned, at terrible cost, that Linnea Nakamura's daughter was constitutionally incapable of telling them apart either.

He thought of the last time he'd seen her mother, up in the high country where the maps went blank. The rain had been falling then too. Linnea had stood in the treeline with her journal under her arm and asked him—one question, quiet, in that voice that made refusal feel like a scientific error—whether he would help her carry it the rest of the way. And he had looked at the cliffs, and the mist, and the carved stones that even then had frightened him more than any law ever had, and he had said the boat couldn't make the passage, and he had steered downstream alone with her supplies still stacked on his deck.

The bureau had reached her within the month. He'd told himself for twenty years that they'd have found her anyway.

He drained the cold coffee and set the cup down with a click.

"Get the tender ready," he told his deckhand. "And find out which dock the ferryman Bao ties up at. The old one. The one who used to run the upper river."

His deckhand blinked. "The upper river's closed, sir. Has been for—"

"I know how long it's been closed." Voss watched a grey shirt on the Kestrel's deck light a cigarette, the smoke thinning into the monsoon grey. "That's rather the point."

If the daughter wouldn't sell, she'd run. And if she ran, she'd need a boat that could pass where patrol boats couldn't—an old man's ferry, a smuggler's channel, a route only three living people remembered. Voss intended to be standing at the end of it when she arrived, holding out the same offer with the same smile, and this time she'd be too tired, too hunted, and too far from any law but the river's to refuse.

He went below to check his charts, and his hand paused, without his permission, on the old survey sheet folded at the bottom of the case—the one with Linnea's annotations in blue pencil, the last route she'd ever plotted, ending mid-river at a bend no map had ever named.

He put it away. He told himself, as he always did, that history deserved to be preserved.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and the river kept rising, patient as everything else that meant to drown him.
