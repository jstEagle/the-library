## 7. The Ferryman

The journal mentioned him twice.

I had read those pages so many times by then that the paper had gone soft at the corners, the ink ghosting through to the other side like a bruise showing through skin. The first mention was clinical, the way my mother was clinical about everything except people: *Ferryman at Klong Tham crossing — Bao — knows the upper channel better than any chart I've seen.* The second mention was different. The second mention had a warmth in it that made my chest hurt in a way I didn't have a name for: *Bao laughed at me today for falling in the shallows. He says the river forgives everyone but the proud. I think he may be the wisest man I've met, and he'd hate to hear me say it.*

Twelve years of drifting, and here was a man who had heard my mother laugh.

I found the crossing on the third day of asking, following a road that gave up halfway and became a footpath, then a suggestion, then a memory of a path through elephant grass taller than my head. The river there ran slow and tea-colored, wide as a lake in the wet season, and on its near bank sat a house on stilts that had been repaired so many times it looked less like architecture than like persistence — new planks bolted over old ones, tin patched with tarpaulin, a roofline that sagged like a tired shoulder. A ferry landing of blackened wood jutted into the current. No ferry.

An old woman mending nets under the house pointed her chin across the water when I asked. "He's there," she said. "He's always there."

*There* turned out to be a man sitting on the end of the landing with his feet in the river, smoking a pipe that had been smoked down to something the size of a thumb. He was small — smaller than me, and I'm not tall — with a chest still barrel-shaped under a shirt gone thin as gauze, and arms that looked like they'd been carved from teak root and left out in the weather for sixty years. His face was all planes and patience. He did not look up when I came down the landing boards. He watched the water instead, the way old men watch water, which is to say the way some people pray.

"You're Bao," I said.

"I'm fishing," he said.

"There's no line in the water."

"Then I'm very good at it."

I stood there a moment, letting the heat press down on me, letting the midges find the sweat at the back of my neck. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling. I had rehearsed this conversation the whole walk from the last village — the opening, the offer, the careful way I'd ask without asking, because a man who ferried my mother would want to know why her daughter stood before him smelling of diesel and dive rot, twelve years too late and carrying a stolen chest's worth of secrets. I had rehearsed it, and now every word of it dried up in my mouth, because he hadn't even looked at me yet, and already I felt like a child caught somewhere she shouldn't be.

"I need to go upstream," I said finally. "Past the last mapped bend. As far as you'll take me."

That got me a look. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of very old men whose bodies have spent their color elsewhere — in rope callus, in scar tissue, in work. He looked at me the way you look at weather coming in off the hills: assessing, unhurried, already deciding what he'd do about it.

"No," he said, and went back to not fishing.

"I can pay."

"No."

"You haven't heard how much."

"It isn't a price problem." He knocked his pipe against the landing post, and ash went into the river and vanished, and I thought, absurdly, *that's what happens to everything you put in the water, girl, remember that.* "It's a direction problem. Nothing goes up past the bend anymore. Not fuel, not cargo, not fools. The bureau patrols the narrows. And the dry season is coming early this year — the birds told me, and the birds are never wrong about water, only about men." He flexed his feet in the current. "You want to go where the river is leaving. That's not a trip. That's a mistake with a schedule."

"My mother—"

"I know whose daughter you are."

The words landed harder than they should have. I hadn't said her name. I hadn't shown him anything. But of course — the jaw, maybe, or the way I stood, or simply the fact that a foreign woman comes walking out of the grass asking after the upper channel, and there's only one person that could ever have been. My mother had walked this same landing twenty years ago, or thirty, and this man had watched her go, and now her ghost had come back wearing my face and my bad posture.

"She's dead," I said. It came out flat. Twelve years of practice makes it flat.

"I know that too." Something moved behind the pale eyes — not softness exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a wound he remembers inflicting, or watching inflicted. "Sit down," he said. "You stand like someone about to run, and I don't argue with runners. I just wait for them to stop."

I sat. The boards were hot enough to sting through my trousers. Below us the river slid past, brown and patient, carrying a dead branch, a plastic bottle, a flower — the whole delta's unsorted mail, going wherever rivers send things.

* * *

We sat for a long time. That was the first thing I learned about Old Bao: he treated silence as a tool, not an absence. He refilled his pipe. He lit it with a match struck on his thumbnail. He watched two egrets argue over a sandbar. And I sat beside him feeling the silence tighten around me like a net being drawn slowly closed, until I understood that this was the negotiation. This was the whole negotiation. He wasn't waiting for my money. He was waiting for whatever I'd say when I couldn't stand the quiet anymore.

It took me eleven minutes. I counted.

"The chest I found," I said. "In the flooded temple at Wat Phra Nam. It has a seal on it. A nation that isn't on maps." I kept my voice low, though there was no one within a mile but the net-mender and the egrets. "My mother's journal was inside. She spent her last years looking for the source. For what's buried there. There's a map — she hid it in a compass she gave me when I was nine, and I've been carrying it for twenty years without knowing." I pulled the compass out and held it between us, brass gone green at the seams, the glass scratched from a hundred dives. "She wrote about you. She said you knew the upper channel better than any chart."

"I did say that," Bao said. "To her. About myself." Smoke curled out of his mouth. "Charts are drawn by men who visit. I stayed."

"Then take me up. Please. Before the dry season closes the channels."

"Why?"

The question was so simple it took me apart. Why. Not *for how much*, not *how far*, not *are you crazy*. Just why, asked in the tone of a man who had ferried a brilliant, stubborn woman into the hills once and had watched what it cost, and who deserved more than a speech about seals and archives.

"Because it was hers," I said. "Because she died for it and nobody knows, and the bureau wants it burned, and a thief named Voss wants it sold, and if I don't get there first, the last thing she ever built gets erased twice — once by the treaty, once by fire." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it. "Because I owe her."

"Do you?" Bao asked, mild as milk.

And there it was. The thing underneath everything, the stone at the bottom of the river that all my drifting had been a long way of not diving down to. Because I didn't owe her. That was the truth, and we both knew it, and the silence came back and sat between us on the hot boards like a third passenger.

There had been a letter. Her last one, folded into the journal's back cover now, but then — twelve years ago — it had come to a hostel in Vientiane, forwarded three times, arriving six weeks after the telegram that said she was gone. I remembered the envelope. I remembered the weight of it in my hand, thin, one page, her handwriting slanting left the way it always did when she wrote fast. I remembered that I could not open it. I remembered telling myself I'd read it when I was somewhere stable, somewhere permanent, and then measuring my life in river miles precisely so that nowhere would ever be permanent, so that the letter could stay sealed forever, so that the last thing my mother ever said to me would remain a question I hadn't answered rather than a goodbye I hadn't earned.

I never wrote back to the letter before it. Either. There were three letters total, in the end. I answered none of them. I told myself she understood — she of all people, who had chosen the work over me a hundred times, who had missed birthdays for dig seasons, who had taught me to hold my breath before she'd taught me to ride a bicycle. I told myself we were the same, she and I, two creatures who loved each other best at a distance, and that silence was our native language.

She had died in the hills with no one but strangers, and her daughter hadn't answered her last letter, and the letter was still sealed, and I had carried it across four borders inside a dead woman's journal like contraband.

"I never wrote back," I said.

Bao didn't move. The river didn't move, or seemed not to; rivers only pretend.

"Any of them," I said. "She sent three. The last one came after she was already dead. I carried it around for years and then I lost track of it and then—" My hands had started shaking, which annoyed me enormously, the body betraying the story the way it always does. "Everyone thinks she abandoned me. The papers said it. The bureau let them say it. And part of me believed it, because believing it meant I didn't have to feel what I actually felt, which was—" I stopped. Started again. The shame came up my throat like floodwater. "Which was that I was angry at her for dying before I could decide to forgive her. That's the ugly truth. I wanted to stay angry. Anger was easier than missing her. So I never opened the letter, because as long as it was sealed, I could keep pretending she was the one who left."

The egrets flew off. The pipe smoke hung straight up in the windless heat. I stared at the water and waited for the old man to tell me what a fool I'd been, and he didn't, and somehow that was worse.

"When your mother came here," Bao said at last, "the first time, she argued with me for two hours about the price. Not to lower it. To raise it. She said I wasn't charging enough to live on, and I said mind your own expedition, and she paid double anyway and left the extra under a stone on my porch, and I bought a new engine with it that I did not need, and I named the boat after her." He tapped the ash again, gently, as if the gesture itself were the point. "You know what she talked about, the whole trip up? Four days on the water. You. How you could hold your breath longer than boys twice your age. How you'd memorized the river distances between towns without anyone teaching you. How she was afraid she was making you into a wanderer because she couldn't stop wandering herself, and how she was writing you a letter to try to explain, and how terrified she was that you wouldn't read it."

The boards under me might as well have been open water. I gripped the edge of the landing.

"So," Bao said. "Did you read it?"

"Not yet."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd suspected for twelve years, which perhaps he had. Perhaps my mother had written to him too. Perhaps everyone in her life had known about the sealed letter except me.

"I'll take you up," he said.

I looked at him. "Just like that? After—"

"After nothing. You sat still for the worst of it and didn't dress it up pretty. That's rarer than money." He began levering himself upright, joint by joint, an operation that took visible time and no complaint, and I moved to help him and he waved me off with a hand like a root. "But hear the terms. One: I pilot, you haul. My shoulders are done pretending. Two: we stop where I say we stop. Three—" he fixed me with those pale eyes, and for a second I saw the man my mother must have seen, the one who laughed at her in the shallows, "—when you open that letter, and you will, because the river doesn't carry secrets upstream, it carries them home — you read it out loud to yourself where I can see you doing it. I ferried her in. I'll not have her daughter arrive at the source still hiding from her."

"That's a strange term for a ferry contract."

"This isn't a ferry contract. Ferries go back and forth." He looked upriver, where the water narrowed between darkening banks and the first hills stood up out of the haze like animals rising from sleep. "This is a going."

* * *

His boat was older than I expected and better than it looked, the way its owner was. Thirty feet of clinker-built hull, repainted so many times the paint had become a geological record — blue over green over rust-red over, at the waterline, a stripe of the original white — with a diesel engine that Bao claimed had been rebuilt eleven times and a canopy of woven palm that smelled of decades of rain. He loaded it while I watched: rice, charcoal, a drum of fuel, coils of rope gone soft with age, a wooden box of tools that rattled like bones. He worked in no hurry whatsoever, and by late afternoon the boat sat low and ready and the light had gone the color of strong tea.

"We leave at dawn," he said. "Before the patrol boats wake up. Sleep aboard — the house is full of my granddaughter's cats and they judge visitors."

I was stowing my dive bag under the canopy when he spoke again, not turning around, hands busy with a mooring line.

"One more thing. Tomorrow we take on no passengers. Whatever climbs aboard at the crossing stays off. I know everyone on this stretch of river, and half of them owe me, and none of them are coming." He tied the line off with two pulls. "So if I find a stranger sleeping among my cargo bags in the morning, that will be a great mystery to us both."

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said.

"Good," said Old Bao. "Neither do I. Dawn, then."

I lay that night on the deck boards under the palm canopy, the compass under my hand and the journal zipped against my ribs, listening to the river breathe against the hull. Upstream, somewhere past the last mapped bend, the hills were already dark. Somewhere in the dark was a cave behind a waterfall, and a nation that wasn't on maps, and the ending of a sentence my mother had left unfinished.

And in the morning, when the light came up gray-gold over the water and Bao grunted awake and began checking his engine with the tenderness of a man touching an old wound, I lifted the canvas over the cargo bags and found, curled up among the rice sacks with both my spare knife and my cigarette lighter tucked innocently beside her like evidence of a successful audit, a skinny kid of fourteen or so, asleep, filthy, and grinning even in her sleep.

Bao looked. Bao sighed. Bao reached for his pipe.

"The river," he said, "decides its passengers."
