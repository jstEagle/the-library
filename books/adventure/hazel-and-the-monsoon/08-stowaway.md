## 8. Stowaway

She woke the way river rats wake: all at once, with her hand already moving toward whatever was nearest and worth taking.

I had my boot on her ankle before she finished sitting up. Not pressing. Just resting there, a fact, a border. She looked down at it, then up at me, and her grin arrived like weather—sudden, unbothered, entirely without apology.

"Morning," she said.

"That's my knife."

"It's a good knife." She turned it over in the light, admiring it as one might admire someone else's horse. "Bad grip wrapping. You should redo it with waxed cord, left-handed wrap if you're left-handed, which you are, because you hold your chopsticks wrong and your oar calluses are on the outside of the palm." She set it down between us on the deck planks, handle toward me, exactly where I'd have put it myself. "Also your lighter's out of fluid. I filled it. There was a stall at the fuel dock."

Bao, crouched over his engine with his back to us, made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed by forty years of practice.

"You went through my things," I said.

"I inventoried your things." The kid stood, unfolding like a fishing rod—all elbows and shins, a head shorter than me, hair chopped raggedly at the jaw as though she'd done it herself with the very knife now lying between us. "There's a difference. A thief takes. An auditor returns." She tapped the canvas bag at her feet. "Everything's back except this."

She produced my brass compass.

She held it flat on her palm the way you'd hold a sleeping bird, and I felt something move through my chest that had no name I wanted to give it. That compass had not been out of my sight in twelve years. It had been underwater with me, slept against my ribs, survived three sinkings and one arrest. And this child had lifted it twice—I would learn later she'd taken it the first night at the ferry dock, while I sat ten feet away drinking tea with Bao—and returned it both times without my knowing.

"How," I said.

"Your jacket pocket has a torn seam on the left side. You always sit with your right elbow on the table, so the left side faces the room." She shrugged. "You're easy. You're actually really easy. Don't take that personally—it's just that you're always watching the water instead of the room."

* * *

Her name was Pip. She offered it an hour later, once she'd established that we weren't going to throw her off at the fuel dock, which we were absolutely going to do, and she wanted to be on record with a name before the throwing-off occurred so that whoever found her could tell her family. She said this cheerfully, eating rice out of Bao's pot with Bao's spoon, sitting in Bao's spot on the gunwale, occupying space in our small world the way water occupies a hull: completely, and without negotiation.

"Fourteen?" Bao asked her.

"Fifteen in the wet season."

"Where are your people?"

She pointed downstream, then upstream, then made a gesture like a bird taking off. "Everywhere I've been is behind me."

I knew that answer. I had given versions of it for twelve years, in eleven ports, to anyone who asked why a woman with an archaeologist's training was hauling salvage out of drowned temples for men who paid in cash and questions never. I knew the exact flavor of it—the way it sounds free and tastes like nothing. Hearing it come out of a mouth half my size did something I didn't appreciate.

"We're dropping you at the next dock," I told her.

"Sure," said Pip.

"The next one."

"Whatever you say."

"There's a dock in two hours."

"Long dock," she agreed, and asked Bao if he had any more of the pickled mustard greens, because the rice was lonely.

* * *

The thing about children on the river is that everyone has a theory about them and no one has a plan. In every port town there are dozens of them—diving for coins thrown off ferries, running cargo along the gunwales, selling single cigarettes to tourists with the confidence of senators. The river raises them the way floodwater raises silt: indiscriminately, and then deposits them somewhere and moves on. I had been one myself for exactly one season after my mother died, before I got old enough and hard enough to stop being pitiable and start being invisible. I remembered what the interval between those two conditions cost.

So when I said *next dock*, some part of me already knew how it would go, because I had been the kid watching the boat pull away, calculating whether the person on it would look back.

Pip spent those two hours making herself useful in the specific way of children who have learned that usefulness is armor. She bailed water that didn't need bailing. She coiled lines better than I coiled lines, which annoyed me. She watched Bao's hands on the engine and asked questions—not the pestering kind, but the kind with weight behind them, the kind that meant she was memorizing. When a patrol boat from the antiquities bureau slid past us heading upstream, black-hulled and flying the state pennant, she was the first to see it and the last to react, going still and casual in a way that told me she'd been stopped by patrols before and had learned exactly what stillness buys.

"They're looking for something," she said, when it was gone.

"They're always looking for something."

"No—" She squinted upstream at the fading wake. "That one was *looking*. Two passes yesterday at the dock, slow, like they were reading names off boats." She looked at me with eyes too old for the rest of her face. "You're carrying something they want."

I said nothing. Bao said nothing. The engine said everything, thumping its patient heartbeat into the brown water, and the current carried us on toward the dock where I was going to leave a child on the bank and feel nothing about it whatsoever.

* * *

The dock belonged to a village called Tha Len, which consisted of a stilted market, a shrine with a fresh garland gone brown, and a dog asleep in the exact center of the landing planks, defying commerce. I told Pip to get her bag. She didn't have a bag. Everything she owned, it emerged, was on her body: the knife wasn't even hers, she'd returned it, remember, and her actual possessions amounted to a plastic sheet, a tin cup, and a length of fishing line coiled around her wrist like a bracelet.

She stepped onto the dock. She stood among the sleeping dog and the fish crates and the morning smell of diesel and drying shrimp paste, and she turned around and looked at me across the gap of dark water between the boat and the planks.

And she smiled. Not the grin—the other thing, the real one underneath, brief and unguarded, the face of an actual fourteen-year-old girl standing on a dock waiting to find out if the world was going to keep her or spend her.

"Thanks for the ride," she said. "Tell the river thanks too."

Bao pushed off.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only inheritance my mother ever left me that I've managed to keep: I looked away first. I got us a hundred meters downstream before I made Bao turn around, and I told him it was because a lone kid on a bureau-watched dock was a liability who might talk, and Bao looked at me with his ancient, terrible patience and said nothing at all, which was worse than arguing, and we went back.

Pip hadn't moved. She was sitting cross-legged at the end of the dock, feeding the dog dried fish she'd presumably acquired through means I chose not to investigate, and when our bow swung back toward her she didn't stand up. She just watched us approach with an expression of profound, infuriating unsurprise.

"Took you four minutes," she said. "I gave you six."

"Get on the boat."

"Which part of that was the river deciding?"

"*Get on the boat.*"

She came aboard, took up her spot on the rice sacks, and immediately fell asleep, mid-morning, in full sun, with the total shamelessness of the young and the exhausted. Her feet were bare and split at the heels. One of her hands stayed curled near her wrist where the fishing line lived, even in sleep, holding on to the only thing she owned.

Bao lit his pipe and watched the banks slide by.

"The river decides its passengers," he said again, and this time it sounded less like philosophy and more like a man telling me that I had just become responsible for something, and that he had known it since the moment he saw her curled among the sacks, grinning in her sleep.

* * *

Here is what I know about solitude, having practiced it professionally for twelve years: it works. That's the trap of it. It genuinely works. You measure your life in miles and the miles add up cleanly and nothing on the ledger owes anything to anybody. No one can be taken from you if no one is kept. My mother taught me that lesson by dying, and I had studied under her absence ever since, top of my class.

But solitude requires empty boats. And as the afternoon came on gold and the channel narrowed into the first fingers of the hill country, I sat across from a sleeping child who had picked my pocket twice to prove she could and returned everything to prove something else—to prove, I think, that she *could stay*, that she was the kind of passenger who leaves things where she finds them—and I felt the first hairline crack run through the structure I'd built.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. Cracks in foundations are supposed to hurt. This one just let a little air in.

She woke at dusk, ate, and without being asked took the second watch, sitting up in the bow with the fishing line off her wrist, trailing a lure she'd tied herself, catching nothing, wanting nothing, keeping watch over strangers because it was the only currency she had and she intended to pay her way.

Upstream, far off, lightning walked along the edge of the hills where the last mapped bend waited, and the monsoon clouds were stacking themselves like cargo no one had declared.

"Hey," Pip said quietly, not turning around. "Those patrol boats. Whatever you're carrying—you should know I'm good at hiding things."

I almost laughed. Almost.

"Go to sleep, Pip."

"I'm on watch."

"Then watch," I said, and lay back, and listened to the river, and did not sleep either—for the first time in twelve years, not because of what was behind me, but because of what was beside me.
