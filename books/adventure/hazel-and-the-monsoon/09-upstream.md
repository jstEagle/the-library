## 9. Upstream

The delta woke before the sun, and it woke angry.

Oscar stood at the wheel of Bao's ferry—the *Sombai*, he called it, which he claimed meant *patience* in a language he refused to name—watching the brown water unspool into a hundred braided channels ahead of them. The monsoon had turned the river into something less like water and more like a moving country, whole provinces of it, sliding past each other in the dark. Debris rode the current: whole trees with their roots clawing at the sky, roof thatch, a wooden shrine door painted with peeling gold, a single rubber boot turning in a slow, patient circle as though waiting for its owner to come back for it.

"You'll want to keep to the eastern channel," Bao said from beneath the canvas awning, where he sat with his legs stretched out and a tin cup of something that smelled like scorched ginger. "The western one eats boats in flood season. Eats them politely, but it eats them."

"You could have mentioned that before we cast off."

"You didn't ask before we cast off." He sipped. "That's a lesson your mother never learned either. She asked questions about everything except the things that would have kept her alive."

Oscar didn't answer that. Behind her, in the shallow hold, Pip was asleep in a nest of coiled rope and fish netting, one arm flung over her face, the stolen—borrowed, returned, stolen again—compass tucked against her chest like a small brass heart. The girl had come aboard the way weather comes aboard: without permission, without apology, and with every sign of intending to stay for a long time.

The delta opened around them as the light came up. Floating markets materialized out of the mist the way they did every morning, as if the river dreamed them into being overnight—long sampans lashed together in drifting boulevards, canopied in woven palm, hung with lanterns that still burned pale orange against the gray. Women paddled among them selling river greens and eels still writhing in their baskets; a man with a voice like a foghorn auctioned cooking oil by the tin. The smell hit Oscar in layers: diesel and durian, woodsmoke and wet rope, the green rot of the flooded banks, and under it all the mineral cold smell of deep water moving fast.

"Stay close," Bao said, taking the wheel from her with the unhurried authority of a man who had been doing this since before she was born. "In the market channels, they don't move for anyone. Not for patrol boats, not for crocodiles, not for the river itself. You yield or you sink."

The first crocodile came off a mudbank not ten minutes later.

It didn't lunge. That was the thing Oscar would remember—the sheer indifference of it. It lay in the shallows like a log that had opinions, its yellow eye tracking the ferry as they passed, its tail giving one slow sweep that sent a wake across the water and rocked a passing sampan full of gourds. The woman poling the sampan didn't even look up.

"Salties come down from the coast in flood," Bao said. "The little ones stay in the reeds. The big ones—" he nodded at a shape sliding beneath the surface off the port bow, a shadow the length of the ferry's hull, "—the big ones have learned the markets. They follow the boats because the boats drop things. Fish guts. Chickens that didn't sell." A pause. "Once, a man."

"Comforting."

"I'm not here to comfort you. I'm here to get you up the river alive. Comfort is a different fare entirely, and it costs more."

They threaded the market for the better part of an hour, the *Sombai* nudging through gaps barely wider than her own beam, Oscar fending off a sampan with a boat hook while its owner shouted cheerful curses at her and Bao shouted back what were clearly older, more traditional curses, the two of them grinning at each other in a way that suggested this happened every morning and probably did. A girl in one of the stalls held up a string of silver fish and mouthed something at Pip, who was awake now and hanging over the rail, and Pip held up three fingers, and coins changed hands across the water, and suddenly they had breakfast: fish grilled over a clay brazier, wrapped in banana leaf, hot enough to blister fingers and good enough to make Oscar forget, briefly, that she was a fugitive.

Briefly.

Because at the far end of the market channel, where the stalls thinned and the brown water opened into the main current again, a patrol boat sat at anchor with its engines idling.

It was bureau green, that particular institutional green that no one chooses voluntarily, with the state crest on its flank and a canvas awning over the stern where two officers sat with rifles across their knees, not aiming them at anything, which was somehow worse. A third officer stood at the bow with a megaphone, and even at this distance Oscar could hear him calling out the morning's business: papers, manifests, names. The floating market flowed around the patrol boat the way water flows around a stone, closing behind it without a ripple, every vendor suddenly very interested in their own goods.

"They're not looking for us," Bao said quietly. "Not yet. They're counting."

"Counting what?"

"Boats going upstream. It's what they always do first. They don't know who they want yet, so they write down everyone." He handed her the last of the fish. "Eat. And get below when we pass. A woman alone at the wheel of a stolen—"

"Borrowed."

"—of a borrowed ferry, with a child stowed in her hold, is a story they write down twice."

Oscar ate the fish. It tasted like smoke and river and the particular sweetness of food eaten while afraid. Then she went below, and pulled the canvas over the hold, and sat in the dark with Pip's breathing and the compass's faint weight in her pocket, and listened to the patrol boat's engine swell and pass and fade, and felt the river carry them north like a current carrying a secret.

* * *

They took on fuel at a depot that wasn't on any chart, at the junction where the river split around an island the shape of a sleeping dog. Bao called it the Dog's Ear and said everyone did, and that the man who ran the depot was called Uncle Nine because he was the ninth of ten brothers and the only one still living, and that he watered his fuel like a barkeep waters his beer, so check the drums yourself and don't stand downwind when he cooks.

The depot was four stilt houses and a corrugated roof over a forest of fuel drums, perched on the island's muddy spine. Chickens owned it absolutely. A dog that might have been part crocodile itself lay in the shade and watched them with professional boredom. Uncle Nine turned out to be a tiny, magnificent old man in a military jacket with no insignia, who embraced Bao like a brother, insulted his boat at length, and then embraced Oscar too, which she was not prepared for and which left her holding a cup of tea she hadn't asked for.

Pip vanished immediately, which was what Pip did. Oscar let her. The girl had a genius for being unremarkable in crowds, and besides, the depot had maybe thirty people on it, half of them related, and the other half too old to chase anyone.

Oscar was checking the third drum for water when she heard the voice.

"You're going the wrong way, you know."

She knew the voice. She'd heard it for two days in the riverport, drifting from the taverns like smoke—measured, amused, with the particular polish of a man who had once been someone and was determined to make that past tense hurt. She turned.

Jun stood at the edge of the fuel shed with a canvas satchel over one shoulder and a bottle in the other hand, and he was, she noted with detached professional interest, drunk in the way that some men are drunk and sober men are not: precisely. His eyes were clear. His voice was level. Only his hands, she saw, were not quite steady, and they were not quite steady because he was afraid, and he had come anyway.

"You followed me," she said.

"I followed the *Sombai*. Old Bao's ferry is the most recognizable thing on this river. It's like following a floating temple." He took a pull from the bottle, winced, and offered it to her. She declined. "Also, I bribed Uncle Nine's nephew, who told me you bought enough diesel for a run to the headwaters and back, which is interesting, because the headwaters are in the Forbidden Territories, and nobody goes there, and the last person who tried to charter a boat for that run was—"

"Stop."

"—a woman named Dr. Elara Nakamura," Jun finished, gently. "Twelve years ago. I drew the charts for her expedition."

The river seemed louder all of a sudden. Or maybe it was just Oscar's blood.

"You're lying."

"I'm a disgraced cartographer, not a liar. There's a difference, and it's mostly that cartographers get punished for it." He set the bottle down on a drum and opened his satchel, and what he took out made the breath go out of her like a punch.

It was a chart. Hand-drawn, hand-inked, on paper gone amber with age, and it was beautiful in the way that only dead things are beautiful—the old Malayan trade routes rendered in a fine brown line, the river system above the last mapped bend drawn in loving, obsessive detail: side channels, portages, seasonal fords, villages marked with tiny symbols, and at the top of the sheet, where a title should be, a seal.

The seal from the chest. The seal from her mother's journal. A sun split by a river's course, ringed in script.

"Where did you get this?"

"I drew it. From memory. Over eleven years." He said it simply, the way a man says *I did the time*. "I was the surveyor on your mother's last expedition, Dr. Nakamura. I drew the charts she used. And when the bureau came for her—when they burned her work and struck her name from the registry and made sure no journal in this country would print her research—I stood up in a hearing room full of men in uniforms and said, the maps are real, Malaya is real, I have drawn its rivers with my own hands." He smiled, and it was not a happy thing. "They took my license. They took my archive. They very nearly took my hands. And then they let me go, because a drunk who insists on a dead country is not a threat, he's a joke. I have spent eleven years being a joke, Ms. Nakamura, and I am very tired of it."

Down at the dock, Pip appeared out of nowhere at Bao's elbow, and Bao looked up at the fuel shed, and his old face did something complicated—recognition, resignation, and a kind of grim amusement, as if the river had sent exactly the passenger he'd expected.

"You want to come aboard," Oscar said. Not a question.

"I want to come aboard." Jun folded the chart with the care of a priest handling a relic and held it out to her. "Because you have the journal, and I have the routes, and neither of us is worth anything without the other. Because your mother's expedition died in the hills and I have spent eleven years trying to understand why, and I think the answer is upstream. And because—" he paused, and for a moment the polish dropped and she saw the man underneath, the one who had stood up in the hearing room, "—I owe her. She carried me out of the gorge when my leg was broken and the bureau's boats were coming, and I repaid her by testifying at the inquiry that the route was sound, because I was afraid, and my testimony was used to bury her. So yes. I want to come aboard. And you want me aboard, whether you know it yet or not."

"I don't trust you."

"Good. Distrust keeps people honest." He picked his bottle back up. "You have until Uncle Nine finishes filling your drums. After that I'll walk back to the port, drink myself to death in roughly a decade, and you'll spend the rest of the journey guessing which channel drowns you. Your choice."

Oscar looked at the chart. The brown ink lines ran up past the last mapped bend like veins, into a country no living surveyor had charted, and she thought of her mother's journal ending mid-sentence, and the compass in her pocket with its micro-etched route, and the fact that every mile upstream was a mile into country she could not read.

The drums finished filling with a gurgle and a knock. Uncle Nine called something cheerful from the dock.

"Sleep on deck," she said. "You touch the journal without asking, you touch the compass, you so much as look at the girl's things, and Bao puts you off at the next crocodile bank."

"A fair contract." Jun bowed, ironically, and ruined it entirely by grinning like a man who had just been given his life back. "You know, you argue exactly like her."

"Shut up and get aboard."

* * *

They ran north through the afternoon, and the river changed around them the way a face changes when a person stops performing.

The delta's braided channels drew together into a single broad current, and the current itself changed character—faster, deeper, carrying a sediment that turned from brown to a strange pale gold where the sun hit it. The banks rose. The floating markets fell behind, and then the last of the stilt villages, and then the last of anything at all except forest: kapok and ironwood standing hip-deep in floodwater, orchids hanging from the branches like small pale faces, the air so thick with moisture and the smell of crushed green things that breathing felt like drinking.

Pip stood at the bow for hours, saying nothing, which for Pip was unprecedented, watching the country change. Once she came back and sat next to Oscar at the wheel without asking, which was also unprecedented, and after a while she said, "The man's chart. The one he showed you. Can I see it?"

"Why?"

"Because I've never seen a map of somewhere that isn't anywhere." She said it with the absolute seriousness of the young, and Oscar found she didn't have an answer, so she unfolded Jun's chart across the deck plating, and the two of them knelt over it in the failing light while Bao steered and the river ran gold and black around them.

"Who drew the little fish?" Pip asked.

"Those aren't fish. Those are the old route markers. See—" Jun's voice, from behind them, quieter than it had been all day, sober now in a way the bottle hadn't managed. "The Malayan river pilots marked the safe channels with river-spirit signs. A fish for a deep channel. A coiled snake for a place with standing waves. A closed eye for a channel that only opens in flood." His finger came down on a stretch of river above the last bend, where the chart filled with closed eyes. "Which means the upper river, in this season, is mostly open. If you know how to read it."

"And if you don't?" Oscar asked.

"Then the river reads you." He said it without drama, which made it worse. "Your mother's expedition had my charts. What they didn't have was a pilot who could read the signs. Bao can. But Bao hasn't been above the bend in thirty years, and the river moves."

"Rivers do that," Oscar said.

"Rivers do that," Jun agreed. "Which is why your mother is dead."

The words dropped into the evening like a stone into still water. Pip looked up sharply. Bao's shoulders didn't move at the wheel, but the engine note changed, just slightly, as if the boat itself had flinched.

Oscar folded the chart along its old creases, carefully, and stood. "We make the bend in three days," she said. "If the flood holds. If the bureau doesn't find us. If Voss doesn't find us first." She looked at the two of them—the disgraced man and the thief-child, her crew, assembled against every instinct she had, on a borrowed ferry going where her mother had gone to die. "So nobody dies of pessimism before we get there. Sleep. We take the night watch in shifts."

She took first watch alone at the wheel, because she was the captain, or because she had made herself the captain by standing at the wheel, which amounted to the same thing on a river. Behind her the others settled into their nests of rope and canvas. Ahead the channel narrowed, and the closed-eye markers began to appear on the banks—carved stones, half-drowned, their old symbols worn but legible, staring out of the floodwater with their patient, ancient patience.

And on the third stone, as the *Sombai* slid past in the dark, the lantern light caught something fresh.

New carving. Weeks old, at most, cutting through the moss and lichen like a wound through old skin. Not a closed eye. Not a fish or a snake.

An arrow, pointing upstream, and beneath it, in characters Jun would later translate with shaking hands, a single word of a dead language that the people who carved it very much intended to be read:

*Come.*

Oscar brought the ferry about, and followed it.
