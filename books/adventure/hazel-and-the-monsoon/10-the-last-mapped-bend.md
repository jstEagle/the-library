## 10. The Last Mapped Bend

Jun had drawn this river eleven times in his life, and every one of those drawings was a lie.

He sat at the bow of Bao's old ferry with his knees drawn up and a pencil stub behind his ear, watching the banks unspool past them like paper being pulled from a roll. The morning was the color of pewter, the monsoon clouds hanging so low that the karst towers ahead of them seemed to grow out of the sky itself rather than the water. Mist moved through the channels in slow ropes. Somewhere upstream, hidden by all that gray, lay the bend where his maps ended—and where, if he was honest with himself, his life had ended too, twelve years ago, in a hearing room that smelled of floor wax and institutional contempt.

Bao poled them through the shallows with the unhurried competence of a man who had been arguing with this river for sixty years and had learned to lose gracefully. Pip crouched at the gunwale, fishing for nothing, her line trailing a bare hook through the brown water because she liked to watch it cut a seam. And Oscar stood amidships with her arms folded, staring upstream as though she could see through the mist to whatever waited there.

Jun knew what waited. He had spent half his career trying to prove it existed and the other half paying for it.

* * *

The charts came out after breakfast, because Jun could not help himself. He kept them in an oilcloth roll lashed inside his bag—copies, all of them, the originals confiscated years ago—and he spread them across the deck planks with the reverence other men reserved for photographs of their children.

"Here," he said, tapping the topmost sheet. "The delta. I drew this when I was twenty-three. Every channel, every sandbar, every floating market from here to the sea. The bureau's survey office called it 'adequate.'" He smiled without warmth. "Adequate. Do you know how long I worked on that? Fourteen months. I interviewed ferrymen. I measured current speeds with a cork and a stopwatch. I once waded chest-deep into a leech marsh at four in the morning because a fisherman swore the channel had shifted a hundred meters east."

"It's very good," Pip said, leaning over it, dripping river water dangerously close to the ink.

"It's very dead," Jun said. "That's the trouble with maps. They're obituaries for rivers. This one was obsolete before the ink dried—the monsoon redrew three channels that same season—but the bureau stamped it anyway, filed it, and forgot it. Rivers don't hold still for governments. Neither do nations." He paused, and then, because the moment demanded it and because he had carried this alone for too long, he pulled the second chart from beneath the first.

This one was different. The lower half was precise, beautiful even—the lowlands rendered in his careful hand, contour lines like fingerprints. But above the last mapped bend, the paper went blank. Not empty. Blank in the way a held breath is blank. And in the center of that emptiness, in pencil so faint you had to tilt it against the light, Jun had written a single word:

*Malaya.*

Oscar went still. He felt her go still, the way you feel a change in current before you see it.

"You've seen the seal," he said quietly. "On the chest. You know what it is now. So let me tell you what it cost me to write that word."

* * *

"I was thirty-one," he said. "Junior cartographer, third grade, which is a polite way of saying nobody important. I'd been sent upriver to survey the highland tributaries—a routine job, six weeks, come back with elevations and channel widths. Routine until I found the stones."

"The warning stones," Oscar said.

Jun looked at her. "You've heard of them."

"My mother's journal mentions them. I didn't know what they were yet. Still don't, not really."

"They're older than any kingdom on record," Jun said. "Carved into the cliff faces along the headwater gorges, in a script no living language claims. I copied four of them. Took rubbings. Sent samples of the stone itself back downriver with my quarterly report, along with a note saying I believed the inscriptions belonged to Malaya—the nation that appears in exactly one treaty document from the colonial archive and then vanishes from history as though it had never been. Except it *had* been. The treaty proves it. You don't sign away a country that doesn't exist."

Pip had abandoned her fishless hook entirely and was sitting cross-legged on the deck, chin in her hands.

"So I said so," Jun continued. "In writing. In an official report. I said the historical record was incomplete, that Malaya had been erased not by time but by decision, and that the decision appeared to have been made by people who were still alive when I made it." He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "I thought I was doing my job. That was the funny part. I genuinely thought a cartographer's job was to draw what is there."

"What happened?" Pip asked, though her voice said she already guessed.

"A hearing," Jun said. "Then another. Then a finding: that I had fabricated evidence, that the stones were natural weathering patterns, that my rubbings were forgeries of my own making—which is a remarkable accusation when you consider that I was accused of forging something the accusers insisted had never existed. You cannot forge a script no one has ever seen. But logic was never the point. The point was that certain names do not go on maps, and certain questions do not get asked, and I had put a forbidden name on government paper in government ink." He rolled the chart closed with hands that remembered how to be steady. "They took my license. They took my originals. They offered me a desk job cataloging drainage ditches if I signed a statement retracting everything. I refused. My wife did not refuse to leave me, which I suppose was its own kind of honesty."

The mist had thickened around the boat while he spoke, beading on the oilcloth, running in threads down the deck. Bao poled on, silent, but Jun noticed the old man's eyes flick toward him once—not pity, exactly. Recognition.

"So when I say I know what that seal means," Jun finished, "I don't mean I read about it in a book. I mean I lost everything I had to a word, and I would write it again tomorrow."

* * *

They passed the bend at midday.

Jun felt it before anyone announced it—a wrongness in the water, a hesitation in the current, the way a sentence hesitates before a difficult clause. Bao brought the ferry alongside a shelf of black rock and shipped his pole, and for a while nobody spoke. On the left bank rose a cliff Jun had never seen but had imagined a thousand times; on the right, the river split around a gravel island furred with bamboo. Ahead, the channel narrowed and climbed toward hills that no survey had ever measured.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked on the single syllable. He cleared his throat and tried again. "There. That's the edge. Everything behind us exists on paper somewhere. Everything ahead—" He gestured at the blank gray distance. "Everything ahead is mine. Ours. Unwritten."

Oscar came forward slowly and stood beside him at the bow. For a long moment she said nothing, just watched the unmapped water slide beneath them, and Jun watched her watch it, and understood that they were looking at different things. He was looking at the end of his disgrace. She was looking at the beginning of her mother's.

"I used to think my mother was a coward," she said at last. "She ran. Whatever happened to her—the scandal, the disgrace, all of it—she just left. Took contracts in worse and worse places, wrote me letters I didn't answer, and died alone in some highland village with her work unfinished and her name ruined." Her jaw tightened. "I built a whole life out of not being her. Never staying. Never caring enough about anything to ruin myself over it. Twelve years of measuring everything in miles instead of years, because miles don't ask anything of you."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going upstream to finish her work," Oscar said simply. "And I'm starting to suspect she wasn't running from anything. She was running *toward* something, and everyone who called her a liar—including me, in my own quiet way—was standing between her and it."

She reached into her dive bag then, and Jun's breath caught, because he knew what lived in that bag. He had known since the night in the riverport when she'd finally opened the lead chest under a lamp in Bao's boathouse and gone white as bone. The field journal. Twelve years of a dead woman's secret hunt, ending mid-sentence, and Oscar had guarded it since the way drowning people guard air.

She held it out to him.

"Read it," she said. "All of it. If we're going to trust each other with what's ahead, you should know what she knew. And Jun—" something moved across her face, grief and apology tangled together, "—you should know you weren't the only one they called a liar."

* * *

He read while the boat climbed the narrowing river, and the world shrank to the size of a page.

Her mother's hand was small and relentless, slanting hard to the right, crowding the margins when the thoughts came faster than the lines. The early entries were almost cheerful—field notes, supply lists, sketches of pottery shards. Then, entry by entry, year by year, the cheerfulness drained out and the obsession set in like a tide. Rubbings of the warning stones. A partial decipherment, tentative, brilliant. References to a sealed colonial file she had bribed a clerk to see and immediately regretted seeing. Mentions of names Jun knew: Old Bao, appearing first as *the ferryman who asks no questions*, later as *my friend, though I don't deserve the word*. And threaded through everything, recurring like a refrain she couldn't stop humming, a girl's name.

*Hazel.*

Jun glanced up. Oscar was watching him read, and her expression was the expression of someone waiting for a verdict.

"She named you Hazel," he said.

"Hazel Nakamura." Oscar's mouth twisted. "I stopped using it when she died. It felt like wearing a costume she'd picked out. Oscar was mine. I found it myself."

"May I keep reading?"

"That's why I gave it to you."

So he read on, into the final years, and the entries grew stranger and more urgent. *The Kaeli keep the memory.* *The source is not water, it is witness.* *If I am taken, the work must not be.* And then, three pages from the end, a passage that made Jun's hands tremble against the paper:

*I have been warned off twice now. Once politely, once otherwise. The bureau knows I'm close. Voss knows I'm closer. I should turn back. I have a daughter who hasn't answered my letters in two years, and maybe that's answer enough, and maybe the kindest thing I can do for both of us is let this die with me.*

*But the river remembers what the world forgot. Someone has to remember with it.*

The last entry broke off mid-sentence, the ink trailing into nothing, as though the pen had been lifted and never set down again.

Jun closed the journal and discovered that the light had changed—that the afternoon had gone gold and then copper while he read, that the mist had burned off the hills, that Pip had fallen asleep against a coil of rope with her mouth open, that Bao had moored the boat in the lee of the cliff without any of them noticing. The unmapped river murmured against the hull. Somewhere above them, invisible in the gathering dusk, birds were making sounds Jun had no names for.

"Well?" Oscar asked.

"I met her once," he said. "Your mother. At a conference, years ago—before my hearing, before hers. She gave a paper on trade-route anomalies in the highlands and got laughed out of the room. I was the only person who asked her a question afterward, and it was a pedantic question about sediment layers, and she answered it anyway, patiently, like it mattered." He shook his head. "I've thought about that woman for fifteen years and told myself I invented her. That anyone stubborn enough to be right about something that big ends up either vindicated or destroyed, and the destroyed ones are just cautionary tales."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was neither." He handed the journal back, carefully, the way you return something borrowed from a shrine. "I think she was a cartographer. Not by license—by nature. She was drawing a map of the truth, and the truth was bigger than the paper, and she kept drawing anyway. That's not destruction, Oscar. That's the whole profession, done properly. We're all of us just people who refuse to leave the blank spaces blank."

Oscar looked down at the journal in her hands, and then out at the darkening, uncharted water, and Jun saw something loosen in her face—something that had been clenched for twelve years and was only now, cautiously, testing whether it could open.

"They took your license," she said. "They took her name. They took—" she touched the compass at her throat, the battered brass thing she hadn't explained yet but wore like a wound, "—they took a lot of things. Seems to me the bureau spends most of its energy taking."

"It does."

"Then let's give something back." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "Starting with whatever's at the end of this river."

* * *

It was full dark when Pip woke, and Bao lit the stern lantern, and they ate rice and dried fish in a circle of yellow light surrounded by a darkness so complete it felt architectural. Jun found himself talking—about his grandfather teaching him stars, about the hearing room, about his wife's garden, which he still dreamed of—and the others listened, and nobody pitied him, and the strange loosened feeling in his chest grew another degree looser.

Later, when the others slept, he sat up with the blank chart across his knees and sharpened his pencil stub with a knife, and above the last mapped bend, in the space where the forbidden word had lived alone for twelve years, he began—at last, in ink—to draw.

He got perhaps ten centimeters of riverbank onto the page before the night produced its first wrong note: a sound from downstream, low and rhythmic, that might have been water and might have been an engine, threading up through the dark gorge toward them.

Jun lifted his head. The lantern flame leaned, as if listening too.

"Oscar," he said softly. "You'll want to wake up for this."
