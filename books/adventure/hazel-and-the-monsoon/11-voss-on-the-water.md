## 11. Voss on the Water

There was a particular pleasure, Voss had learned over thirty years of buying and selling other people's history, in watching a boat that did not know it was being followed.

He stood at the rail of the *Cormorant's Grace* with a cup of cold coffee going bitter in his hand and watched Bao's old ferry crawl upstream through the drowned forest, its wake unspooling behind it like thread off a careless spool. The ferry sat low in the water, heavy with fuel drums and stubbornness, and it moved with the unhurried confidence of something that believed itself invisible among the fishing boats and floating markets. It was not invisible. It had not been invisible since the day Oscar Nakamura walked out of that riverport market with her chin up and her lies tucked under her arm like newspapers.

"Two klicks," said Renn from the wheelhouse. "Maybe two and a half. She's running dark tonight—no lamp."

"She's always running dark," Voss said. "That's what I like about her."

He meant it, too, which surprised him a little. Sentiment was a currency he kept out of circulation; he'd watched it bankrupt better men than himself, men who fell in love with a piece instead of its price. But there was something in the woman worth admiring the way you admired a well-cut stone—the economy of her, the way she moved through the world without leaving purchase for anyone's hooks. He had offered her a fortune on the dock at Kampong Serai and she had looked at him with those flat grey eyes and lied to his face so smoothly that he'd almost applauded. Almost. He'd known it was a lie because he'd taught half the river to lie like that, and she hadn't learned it from him.

She thought he wanted the chest. That was the beauty of it. She would guard the chest the way a mother guards a cradle, and all the while the thing that mattered would sit wherever she actually kept it—in a dive bag, most likely, against her spine where she carried everything she couldn't afford to lose. He'd seen her check that bag eleven times in three days. People always checked the true thing more often than the decoy.

The flooded karst rose around them as the light went down: black towers of limestone standing hip-deep in the brown floodwater, hung with vines, hollowed by ten thousand years of rain into arches and mouths and windows. In daylight they were beautiful in the way of postcards. At night they were a maze built for drowning. Local pilots gave the karst a wide berth after dark and hugged the marked channel, where the bureau's patrol boats ran their lights up and down like patient lighthouse keepers.

Bao's ferry was not hugging the channel.

"There." Renn pointed. The ferry's silhouette slipped between two towers of stone, into the labyrinth, lampless, silent, running on some knowledge older than any chart Voss had ever bought or burned.

Voss set down his coffee. "Follow her in."

Renn gave him a look. Renn was a good man in the way a hammer is a good tool—reliable, blunt, useless for fine work—and the look said what the look always said: *this is how we die.* But he turned the wheel, and the *Cormorant's Grace* leaned into the dark between the stones.

* * *

Voss had been in these hills before. He did not say so, and he had arranged his life so that no one alive could say it for him, but the karst knew him. He felt it in the way the echoes came back wrong off the limestone, in the smell of the water here—colder, greener, with a mineral bite underneath the rot. Thirty years ago he had come up this same river in a longboat with a woman who talked to the water like it was an employee who'd disappointed her, and he had left her on a trailhead in the cloud forest with a handshake and a lie, and he had rowed away before the screaming started.

He did not think about that. He was very good at not thinking about it. He had made not-thinking-about-it into a discipline, almost a craft, the way other men made furniture or trouble.

What he thought about instead was value. The chest was lead-lined, seal-stamped, and worth perhaps four thousand to a collector who liked curiosities. But the chest was not the value. The value was provenance—a documented chain leading back to a nation that officially did not exist, which meant every document inside it became evidence, and evidence of Malaya was the one commodity on this river with no ceiling on its price. There were men in the capital who would pay a house's weight in gold for a single authenticated page. There were men outside the capital who would pay more, and ask fewer questions, and were less likely to be wearing a bureau badge when they did.

And somewhere on that ugly little ferry was a girl carrying twelve years of an archaeologist's field notes, and she didn't even know yet what she was carrying. Or she did, and that was better—that meant the journal had teeth, and things with teeth fetched a premium.

"Contact," Renn murmured. "Starboard side. She's stopped."

The ferry lay still in a pool of black water cupped between four towers, so still that Voss could see the stars doubled in it, one sky above and one below, and the boat sitting in the seam between them. A lantern came alight aboard her—deliberately, he judged, a signal or a trap—and in its small yellow circle he saw the shapes of them: the old ferryman at the stern, the cartographer hunched over something, the girl moving along the gunwale with the quick economical grace he remembered from the market.

And a fourth shape. Small. Quick. Wrong.

A child. There was a child on that boat.

Voss felt something shift in his chest, some old plank settling, and he stepped on it the way you step on a loose board. Children were not his concern. Nothing was his concern except the arithmetic of the next hour. He put the glass to his eye.

The girl—Nakamura—was rigging a line from the ferry's bow to a karst wall, tying off in the dark with hands that knew knots the way priests knew prayers. Anchoring for the night. Sensible. The pool was deep enough to hide in, enclosed enough to defend, and the only approaches were two channels, both narrow enough that a faster boat lost its advantage the moment it entered.

She was smart. He'd counted on her being smart. Smart people guarded the front door.

"The west channel," Voss said. "There's a shelf of rock under the surface on the east approach—I hit it once, years ago. West is deep all the way through if you hug the north wall. Kill the engine at the mouth and drift. We'll come out of the blind spot behind that tower with the split top, and we'll be alongside her before anyone hears water."

"And then?"

"Then I go aboard alone. Quietly. We take the chest and we are gone before the lantern burns down. Nobody bleeds tonight unless somebody insists."

Renn grunted, which from Renn was a treaty of full agreement.

* * *

They drifted in on the flood current with the engine off and the water talking softly against the hull, through a corridor of stone so close that Voss could have touched the walls on either side, under an arch hung with sleeping bats that stirred and clicked but did not fly. The karst swallowed sound. His own breathing seemed loud as surf. And underneath everything, faint as a pulse through a wrist, he heard the river moving somewhere below them—underground channels, the limestone full of them, whole drowned rivers running beneath the rivers. He had forgotten that about this place. The water here went down farther than maps admitted. Everything here went down farther than maps admitted.

They slid out of the channel into the pool, and the ferry lay ahead of them, lantern burning low, and nobody shouted, and no lamp swung toward them, and Voss allowed himself the small cold satisfaction of a plan arriving exactly on schedule.

He boarded alone, as promised, dropping from the *Grace*'s rail to the ferry's deck with a softness that thirty years of practice had refined into habit. The deck was wet and smelled of diesel and fish and, faintly, of someone's cooking—rice and chili, hours old. Home smells. He noted them with distaste. This boat was becoming a home, and homes made people slow.

The chest sat amidships under an oilcloth, exactly where pride would put it: visible, central, guarded by nothing but the assumption that darkness was armor. He crouched beside it, ran his thumb over the lead edging, felt the seal's raised geometry under the cloth. There you are, he thought. There you are, you beautiful dead nation.

He lifted it. It was heavier than he expected—heavier than lead and old wood should account for—and for one absurd moment he imagined the thing full of gold, full of coins, full of the literal treasure he had long since stopped believing in. Then he adjusted his grip and got it to the rail.

That was when the deck creaked aft of him, and a voice said, very quietly, "You're standing on my net."

The child. The small quick wrong shape from the lantern circle, materialized out of the shadow of the wheelhouse with a filleting knife held low and professional and eyes that caught the lantern light like an animal's. Fourteen, maybe. Barefoot. Utterly unafraid, which was either courage or a childhood that had never afforded fear.

"You're the pickpocket," Voss said. "From the market. You lifted my watch."

"I returned your watch." The child—Pip, he'd heard the name shouted once across the docks—tilted her head. "I lifted it to show Nakamura-san you were following us. She didn't believe me. She believes me now, probably." A pause, and the knife came up a few centimeters. "Give back the chest."

"Or what? You'll cut me?" He smiled the smile that had closed a hundred deals. "Child, I've been threatened by men with rifles. Go wake your friends and let the adults transact."

Something flickered across the kid's face—not fear, calculation—and Voss understood one heartbeat too late that the flicker was the point, that she was keeping him looking at her face while her bare feet found the oilcloth he'd left crumpled on the wet deck.

He lunged. She darted. His shoe came down on the cloth and the world tilted—the deck, the chest in his arms, the child, the rail, the black doubled sky—all of it rotating with terrible smoothness, and the chest tore out of his grip and went into the water with hardly a splash, and the child went after it, or was taken by it, the flood current here running harder than it looked, snatching her small body sideways like a hand closing.

"Pip!" The girl's voice, from the bow—Nakamura, awake, fast, already moving.

The child surfaced once in the starlight, gasped, went under. The chest was gone into the dark. None of it mattered. Voss stood at the rail of a stolen moment and watched a fourteen-year-old get pulled toward the underground channels he knew were down there, the ones the water sang about, and he heard himself shout—"Line! Throw her a line!"—in a voice cracked open in a way he hadn't heard since he was young enough to mean things.

Nakamura was already diving.

No hesitation, no stripping of gear, no negotiation with herself—she simply went over the rail and into the black water in her clothes and boots, and the river took her the way it takes everything, and the lantern light showed nothing but rings spreading on the doubled sky.

* * *

They got her back. Of course they got her back; the river was not finished with any of them. The old ferryman threw a coil of rope with the accuracy of sixty years' practice, and the cartographer hauled, and Nakamura came up with a fistful of the child's shirt and a face like something carved, and they dragged them both aboard while Voss stood on his own deck twenty meters off with empty hands and a chest-shaped absence where his fortune used to be.

He should have left then. Every discipline he owned told him to leave: the chest was lost, the alarm was up, the night was spent. Instead he stood at the rail and watched, through the glass, as Nakamura knelt on the ferry's deck with the child coughing floodwater into her lap, and did something Voss could not parse at first because it fit no transaction he recognized.

She held her. That was it. She wrapped both arms around this feral little stranger and held her, rocking slightly, saying something too low for the glass to carry, and the child clutched her shirtfront with both fists like a drowning swimmer, and the old ferryman put a blanket over them both, and the cartographer stood watch with his back to them, giving them privacy on a deck eight meters long.

Nobody asked what the child was doing on deck at midnight. Nobody said *whose fault was this.* Nobody calculated.

Voss lowered the glass. His hands were not quite steady, and he examined them with clinical interest, the way he might examine a tool that had begun to slip.

The chest was gone—sunk, or hidden, or never truly aboard; he suspected now, with the sour clarity of hindsight, that it had been bait all along, weighted and placed, and that the real cargo slept against the girl's spine in a canvas bag she touched eleven times a day. He had lost the night, and the child had nearly paid for his loss, and the woman he'd come to rob had jumped into a flooded karst pool after her without a second's arithmetic.

He thought, unbidden, of a trailhead in the cloud forest, of a handshake and a lie, of a woman who had also once waded into danger for something that wasn't hers to protect. He put the thought down. He stepped on the plank. The plank held, the way it always held, the way it had held for thirty years.

But it had creaked.

"Course?" Renn asked quietly beside him.

Voss looked back once at the ferry, where the lantern was being shuttered and the family—if that was the word, and the word arrived in his mind uninvited and refused to leave—was folding itself back into the dark. Then downstream, at the far southern horizon, a string of lights was working its way up the marked channel: the bureau's patrol boats, methodical as beads on an abacus, coming to count whatever the night had left.

"They'll run for the highlands," he said. "Past the last mapped bend, where the charts go blank and the bureau's boats draw too much water. Which means we stop chasing them." He turned away from the rail, and his voice came out level and cold and entirely his own again. "We go ahead of them. There's a landing at the falls where the river narrows—everything on this water eventually comes through it, and everything that comes through it has to get out of the boat. We'll be waiting."

"And the girl?"

Voss flexed his hands once, feeling the ghost of a creak somewhere in the timbers of himself.

"The girl," he said, "is starting to care whether other people live. That makes her predictable. Caring people can always be led." He smiled at the dark, and the dark did not smile back. "Set the course for the falls, Renn. We're done following. From here on, we wait where the river gets narrow—and when she comes, she'll bring me everything I need, because she won't leave any of them behind."

Behind them the karst receded into the night, black towers dissolving into blacker sky, and somewhere in the flooded labyrinth a lantern went out, and the river ran on in the dark toward the falls, carrying all of them—hunter and hunted alike—toward the place where the water dropped out of the world.
