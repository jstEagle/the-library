## 12. The Drowned Village

The rain found them an hour before the village did.

It came down the valley in a grey wall, bending the palms flat, turning the river into something that moved like a living muscle beneath the hull. Bao stood at the tiller with his collar up and his eyes half-shut against the water, reading the current the way other men read print, and when he said, "There," Oscar almost didn't see it—a break in the treeline, a suggestion of rooftops, the pale geometry of a concrete wall rising out of the flood.

"Reservoir," Jun said from under the tarp where he'd been hunched over the journal for two days straight, copying its route notes onto paper he'd waterproofed with candle wax. "Or what's left of one. They dammed this stretch forty years ago, maybe fifty. The government maps show nothing here."

"The government maps show nothing anywhere," Pip said. She was wedged in the bow with her knees drawn up to her chin, watching the drowned shapes slide past beneath the surface—fence posts, a chimney, the ridge of a roof furred green with algae. Her voice had gone quiet in a way Oscar hadn't heard before. "Look at the houses."

Oscar looked.

They came on the village slowly, because Bao would not hurry, and by then Oscar understood that Bao never hurried at anything except perhaps dying, which he intended to do at his own pace as well. The water stood three meters deep where the main street had been. A minaret—or what had been a minaret, its top sheared off by some long-ago demolition—rose from the flood like a broken tooth. Below it, whole neighborhoods lay suspended in the green murk: doorways without doors, staircases climbing toward balconies that opened onto open water, a schoolyard where a rusted swing set swayed gently in the current, though there was no wind.

Nobody had ever moved the dead town's bones. They'd simply raised the water over them and written the place off the charts, the way you'd write off anything inconvenient.

"It's called Selat Ampang," Bao said. He brought the boat alongside a concrete spillway where the water ran shallow and clear over old paving stones, and began tying off with the unhurried competence of a man who had done it ten thousand times. "Was. My mother was born here."

Oscar turned to look at him. In all the days since the delta—since the patrol boats, since Jun's blackmail and Pip's thieving fingers and the long wet miles upstream—he had offered almost nothing about himself except directions and disapproval. Now he stood looking at the drowned minaret with an expression Oscar couldn't name, something older than grief.

"You knew this place," she said.

"I knew it when it had streets." He gathered his satchel and stepped onto the spillway without waiting for them. "Come. The rain will not wait and neither will Amah Ining, if she is still alive. She is older than I am, which means she is older than God."

* * *

The upper village—the part that had climbed the hillside above the flood line—was inhabited still, barely. A few dozen houses on stilts, roofs patched with corrugated tin and thatch, laundry strung between them going translucent with rain. Chickens muttered under the eaves. Children watched them from a doorway with the frank, unblinking stares of kids who had learned early that strangers meant either trouble or news, and usually trouble.

Bao led them to the largest house, at the top of the hill, and did not knock. He simply called out a word—not Malayan, Oscar realized, or not entirely; it had a shape she didn't recognize, vowels stacked in a way no language she'd salvaged from had ever used—and waited.

The woman who came to the door was small and bent and wrapped in a shawl the color of river mud, and her face was a topography of wrinkles so deep they seemed carved rather than grown. She looked at Bao for a long moment. Then she said something in that same unplaceable tongue, sharp and questioning, and struck him lightly on the arm with a hand like a dried fish.

"She says I have gotten old and ugly," Bao translated, rubbing his arm with dignity. "She is my cousin's widow. She has been saying this for sixty years."

"And you've been coming back for sixty years to hear it," the old woman said—in accented but perfectly serviceable trade-tongue—and then her eyes went past him, to the four soaked strangers dripping on her porch, and changed. Something moved through her face like a fish through shallow water: recognition, calculation, and underneath both, fear.

She looked at Oscar longest. At the compass hanging at her throat, actually—at the battered brass thing Oscar had worn every day for twenty years and never once thought of as remarkable until everyone started trying to take it from her.

"You are the diver," Amah Ining said. "The one who pulled the chest from the temple."

The rain filled the silence. Behind Oscar, she heard Jun go very still.

"How—" she began.

"Bao sent word ahead when he could. Smoke signals, sometimes. Once, a boy with a bicycle and a great deal of stamina." The old woman's mouth twitched. "Word travels faster than boats on this river. It always has. Come inside before you drown standing up. That is the only free thing I will give you today."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke, dried fish, and camphor. Amah Ining fed them rice porridge with salted egg and pickled mustard greens, and refused to answer a single question until everyone had eaten, including Pip, who claimed not to be hungry and then ate three bowls. Only when the bowls were empty and the rain had settled into a steady drumming on the tin roof did she sit back, fold her hands, and look at Oscar with eyes that were still startlingly quick in that ruined face.

"Now. Show me the seal."

Oscar hesitated. Every instinct she'd built in twelve years of drifting said don't—don't show your cards, don't tell your story, don't let anyone know what you carry or why. But Voss knew. Seng knew. Half the riverport probably knew by now. And this old woman had looked at her compass like she recognized it, which was a thing that had never happened before, not once, not anywhere.

She took the rubbing she'd made of the chest's lid from the oilskin wallet inside her jacket and smoothed it on the table.

Amah Ining looked at it for a long time. Her thumb moved over the stamped impression—the crowned wave, the seven stars, the script ringing the edge—and when she spoke again it was in that other language, low and rhythmic, almost like singing. It took Oscar a moment to understand she was praying.

"That is the seal of Malaya," the old woman said at last. "The true one. There were copies, after—forgers made them for tourists, for smugglers who wanted their crates to look important. But this is real. See how the seventh star sits lower than the others? That was deliberate. The seventh star was the people themselves, placed below the crown because the crown served them, not the other way around. When the treaty came and the nation was erased, the first thing the new government did was burn every flag, every document, every stone with that seal on it. They burned for eleven days in the capital. My grandmother watched the smoke from the hills." She pushed the rubbing back across the table. "Where did you find this?"

"A flooded temple. Lowlands, past the third bend. It was sunk in the sanctuary pool with the offerings."

"Of course it was." Amah Ining nodded slowly. "That is what we did, in the last days. What could not be carried uphill was given to the water. The temples kept our secrets better than the mountains did—for a while. The government never learned to dive." For the first time, something like humor crossed her face, dry as dust. "You people have fixed that now, haven't you."

Jun leaned forward. He'd been silent since the porch, and when he spoke his voice had the careful, over-controlled quality Oscar had learned to hear in him—the sound of a man handling something fragile. "Ma'am. With respect. Malaya appears in no census, no treaty index, no map printed in the last hundred years. I spent six years of my life arguing it existed and it cost me everything. If you're telling me—"

"I am telling you," the old woman said, "that the maps were written by the people who erased us. Why would they draw what they destroyed?" She turned back to Oscar. "And you. You wear a Kaeli compass on a cord around your neck like it's a saint's medal. Do you even know what it is?"

Oscar's hand rose to the compass without her permission. "My mother gave it to me. When I was nine."

"Kaeli work," Amah Ining said simply. "See the maker's mark, under the hinge? Three waves and a root. No factory in the world stamps that. Those compasses are made in the high valleys, by hand, one at a time, and they do not leave the river country unless someone carries them out on purpose." Her eyes held Oscar's, and there was no cruelty in them, only a terrible gentleness. "Your mother carried it out on purpose. For you. Think about what that means."

Oscar thought about it. She thought about it all night, lying awake on Amah Ining's floor with the rain hammering the roof and Pip curled against her back for warmth, breathing in the slow rhythm of a child finally sleeping somewhere she felt safe. *Think about what that means.* Her mother, who had vanished into rivers and archives and disgrace while Oscar grew up shuttling between relatives who spoke about her in the past tense. Her mother, who had chosen a brass compass from a hidden people and put it in her daughter's hands at nine years old, years before any of this—years before the chest, the journal, the hunt. As if she'd known even then where the road went, and wanted Oscar to have a piece of home for the journey, whether or not Oscar ever understood it was home.

She didn't sleep much.

* * *

In the morning the rain had slackened to a fine drifting mist, and the village came out to look at them.

Not all at once—that wasn't the way of it. First the children, orbiting closer and closer to the boat until Pip abandoned dignity entirely and started showing them card tricks and the knife-throwing game she played with driftwood splinters, and within an hour had a crowd of devoted followers and had somehow acquired a new shirt, better than her old one, and nobody could establish whether she'd traded for it or stolen it. Then the fishermen, who examined Bao's boat with professional interest and told him his planking was rotten, and Bao agreed with them, and the two parties parted mutually satisfied. Then the women, who brought food, and Jun, who had spent the night redrawing charts by lamplight, found himself surrounded by elders pointing at his maps and correcting them—gently, persistently, in that layered language and in broken trade-tongue—until by midday he had ink on his fingers up to the wrist and an expression Oscar had never seen on him before. He looked like a man being handed back something he'd buried.

"They're naming channels," he said when Oscar sat down beside him. His voice cracked on the second word and he covered it with a cough. "Channels that aren't on any chart I've ever seen. Side passages through the karst, deep-water routes, places the survey teams never went because the government forbade the region after the erasure. Oscar—" He stopped, pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes for a moment. "I spent six years being told I invented a country. Six years. And these people just—know it. All of it. The names of rapids my grandfather sang about."

"Ask them about the source," Oscar said quietly. "About what's upstream."

Jun looked at her. "You saw it too, then. How they stop talking when the highlands come up."

She had. All morning, whenever the conversation drifted upstream—toward the mist-filled gorge visible from the hillside, the cliffs where the river narrowed to a white ribbon—the villagers' voices softened and their eyes slid away, not with embarrassment but with something closer to reverence. The way people spoke of graves. Or churches.

It was Amah Ining who answered, that evening, on her porch, with the mist rising off the drowned town below and the first stars pricking through above the gorge.

She told it slowly, in the firelight, partly in trade-tongue and partly in the old language, with Bao translating the parts she chose to translate. She told how, when the treaty came and the burning started, some of the people did not flee to the cities or scatter into exile. Some went up. Into the high valleys, past the last mapped bend, into country so steep and cloud-wrapped that the government's soldiers lost three expeditions and wrote the region off as worthless. They took what they could carry of the archives—the genealogies, the songs, the treaties, the names—and they built new villages that appeared on no census, and they made a covenant: that the memory of Malaya would be kept, generation upon generation, until the world was ready to receive it back. Or forever, whichever came first.

"There is a community," Amah Ining said. "Upstream, behind the falls, in the high valleys. We call them the Keepers. They keep the river's memory." She said the phrase in the old language too—*the river remembers what the world forgot*—and Oscar felt the words settle into her chest like cold water into a boot, because she had read them before. Carved into a warning stone, in Jun's careful transcription, in her mother's journal.

Her mother had come this way. Her mother had sat on this porch, or one like it, and been told this same story, and had gone upstream anyway, alone, year after year, until the river took her.

"Your mother stayed with me," Amah Ining said, as if following the thought aloud. "Twice. The first time she was young and frightened and lied badly about why she was here. The second time she was not young anymore, and she did not lie at all." The old woman's gaze was steady. "She asked me the same question you are going to ask me. So ask it."

Oscar's mouth was dry. "How do we find them?"

"You do not find them," Amah Ining said. "They find you—if they choose. The falls guard the approaches, and the side channels shift with every flood, and the warning stones turn strangers back more surely than soldiers ever did. People have gone up that gorge looking for the Keepers and come back with nothing but leeches and stories. People have also gone up and not come back at all." She drew her shawl tighter. "But you have three things the others did not have. You have Bao, who has carried letters to the high valleys since before you were born. You have the compass, which the Keepers will know on sight. And you have her blood." She nodded at the compass at Oscar's throat. "Whether that last one helps you or dooms you, I cannot say. Your mother's blood opened doors here. It also closed some."

The fire crackled. Down below, invisible in the dark, the drowned town lay under its sheet of water, streets and staircases and schoolyards holding their breath, keeping whatever secrets the water had been asked to keep.

"One more thing," Amah Ining said, as they rose to sleep. "The bureau has been here. Two months ago. A woman inspector, polite as you please, asking about travelers from downstream, showing photographs." She paused, and her ancient eyes glinted. "One of the photographs was of your mother. The other was of you."

Oscar went cold. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her I had never seen your face in my life," the old woman said, "which is the kind of lie this village has been telling the government for a hundred years. We are very good at it." She smiled then, briefly, and it transformed her whole ruined face. "But she will come back, that one. They always come back. So rest well tonight, diver. Tomorrow you go upstream—and I think you should go before she does."

Above the gorge, the mist was already thickening, swallowing the last of the light, and somewhere beyond it—past the last mapped bend, behind the falls—the river's memory was waiting to decide whether Oscar Nakamura was worth remembering.
