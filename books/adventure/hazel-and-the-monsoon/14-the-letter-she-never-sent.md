## 14. The Letter She Never Sent

The river at night was a different animal.

Oscar had learned that long ago—learned it the way you learn anything on the water, by being out when you shouldn't be and paying for it. By day the river was work: current to read, snags to dodge, fuel to count, mouths to feed. But after midnight, when Bao had banked the fire down to coals and Pip had finally stopped talking and Jun's lantern had guttered out behind his charts, the river became something else entirely. A breathing thing. A thing with opinions.

She sat the middle watch alone, as she always insisted on doing, wedged in the bow of Bao's old ferry boat with her back against the gunwale and her knees drawn up under a canvas sheet that smelled of diesel and mildew and thirty years of other people's crossings. The monsoon pressed down like a wet hand. Rain came and went in slow curtains, hissing across the water, and between the curtains the air hung so thick she could taste it—mud and rot and green things growing too fast, the whole delta exhaling.

Upstream, somewhere past the dark, were the last mapped bend and everything beyond it. The karst country. The forbidden highlands. The place where her mother had gone to die, if the bureau's version was true, or the place where her mother had found something worth dying for, if the journal was.

Oscar had the journal in her lap.

She'd been carrying it against her body for six days now, ever since Voss took the chest and left them the journal without knowing what he'd left them. She kept it wrapped in oilcloth inside her dive bag, and the dive bag went where she went, and when she slept—which was rarely, these days—she slept with one arm through the strap like a woman holding onto a capsized boat. It was not sentiment. She told herself it was not sentiment. It was leverage, intelligence, the only map to the source that couldn't be stolen because it lived in ink and memory both.

But tonight, alone, with the watch to herself and the rain coming down soft as an apology, she ran out of reasons not to open it again.

* * *

She had read the journal twice through already. The first time in the riverport, the night before the bureau impounded her boat, reading by candle stub with the door barred, and she had read it the way you read a stranger's mail—fast, greedy, hunting for information, for coordinates, for anything that would keep her ahead of Seng and Voss and the closing season. The second time on the water, slower, letting the handwriting do its work on her. Her mother's hand had changed over twelve years of pages. Early entries were quick and slanted, all momentum, a young consultant's confident scrawl. Later ones grew smaller, more careful, letters formed with a patience Oscar didn't recognize, as if the woman writing them had learned to make each word last.

There were things in those pages Oscar still couldn't think about directly. The entry from the year of her father's funeral, which her mother had spent not in the lowlands with her daughter but in a flooded valley two hundred miles north, taking soundings. The entry that said, simply, *Oscar turned eleven today. I sent a parcel. I hope the compass arrived*, written in the same hand that had just described, in loving detail, a carved lintel in a drowned temple—the lintel Oscar herself had swum past a hundred times without seeing.

But there was one part of the journal she had never been able to make herself touch. The back cover.

The journal was bound in cracked leather, and the back cover was thicker than the front, and Oscar had known why since the first night—a folded shape under the leather lining, square, the size of a letter. She had put her thumb against it that first night and then taken her thumb away, the way you take your hand off a hot stove. Because she knew what it was. She had known since she was seventeen years old and a bureau clerk handed her a box of her mother's effects and told her, with practiced gentleness, that no letter had been found addressed to her.

No letter had been found. Because her mother had never sent one. Because it was here, folded into the back cover of a journal her mother must have known—if she was the woman Oscar was beginning to understand she was—that someone would find someday. Maybe not Oscar. Maybe anyone. Maybe the river itself, decades on, when the Kaeli or the bureau or the water came for it.

A letter that was never sent but was never destroyed either. Her mother had carried it through every camp and checkpoint and bad season, the way Oscar carried the compass. Two women keeping the same weight in different pockets.

Oscar looked at the back cover for a long time.

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere across the black water a night bird called, three notes descending, and got no answer.

"You wrote it," she said aloud, to no one, to the dark, to the woman twelve years dead. "You wrote it and you kept it and you never gave it to me. Why?"

The river didn't answer. Rivers never did. That was the whole problem with rivers; that was the whole reason she'd loved them since before she could name it. They took everything you gave them and asked for more, and they never once explained themselves.

She unwrapped the oilcloth. She opened the back cover.

* * *

The paper was waterproofed—of course it was; her mother had taught her that trick when she was eight, wax and heat, *a field journal that drowns is a grave marker, Oscar, not a record*—and the fold had gone soft and furred at the creases from years of being handled. Not written and forgotten. Handled. Taken out and refolded and put back, over and over, the way a person worries a stone in their pocket.

Her mother's handwriting. The late hand, the small patient one.

Oscar read it by feel more than sight, tilting the page toward the faint coal-glow of the banked fire, and the words came up out of the dark one line at a time like something surfacing from deep water:

---

*My Hazel,*

*I have started this letter eleven times. I know because I keep the failures, and there are eleven of them in the bottom of my kit, and every one begins with some version of I'm sorry and ends with some version of come find me, and neither of those is the truth, or neither is the whole truth, and you deserve the whole truth even if it arrives late.*

*So here it is.*

*I chose the work over you. Not once. Every day, for years, in small ways that added up to one enormous way, I chose it. I told myself I was doing it for us—for the vindication, for the day your mother's name would mean what it used to mean—but that was a lie I told myself the way the bureau tells lies, comfortably, and often. The truth is that the work asked me a question the day I found the first seal, and I could not look away from it, and looking away was what raising you required, and I was not strong enough to do both. I want to say I regret it. I do regret it. But regret is a small word for what I feel when I think of you at nine, standing on the dock, not waving.*

*I am going to tell you what I found, or what I believe I found, and then I am going to ask you for something no mother should ask, and you are allowed to hate me for it. Hate is a kind of attention. I will take it.*

*Malaya was real. Not the way the maps say—not the treaty fiction, not the colonial sketch that got erased when the borders were redrawn—but real, a nation of river people who kept their own records, their own names, their own law, and who hid all of it rather than let it be burned. There is an archive. I have seen its shadow: a carved door behind a fall of water, warning stones in a script three universities say does not exist. Someone keeps it. Someone has always kept it. And the bureau—I can't write this part plainly, even here, even to you—if they find it, they will burn it, because a nation that was officially never born cannot be allowed to prove it survived. My own institution will hold the matches. I have seen the order. I signed a version of it myself, once, before I understood what I was signing.*

*And so, my Hazel, my girl, my dock-not-waving child: here is the request.*

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, or worse, and the compass has done its work, and you have come all this way carrying a grudge and a gift in the same pocket. Finish it. Find the source. Put my name next to Malaya's in whatever record survives, so that neither of us is a rumor. OR—and hear me, because this is the harder instruction and the truer one—burn it. Burn the journal, melt the compass down for scrap, take the money any broker will pay you for a dead nation's seal, and go live your life somewhere the water runs slow. Go be a person who stays places. Whichever of these lets you live your own life and not mine—that is the right one. I release you from both. I should have released you at nine. Forgive me. Or don't. Either way, I loved you the whole time, badly, from too far away, in the only language I had, which was distance.*

*The river decides its passengers. I read that on a ferry once, painted on the hull in flaking gold, and I thought it was superstition. It isn't. It's the truest sentence I know.*

*Your mother,*
*M.*

---

Oscar read it twice.

The first time she read it the way she'd read the journal—hunting, scanning, the diver's reflex, pulling out the useful parts. *Finish it or burn it.* A choice. After twelve years of being given nothing, her mother had finally given her a choice, and the choice was everything or nothing, and either answer was sanctioned, blessed, released. *I release you from both.* Oscar felt the strange vertigo of it, the ground tilting. All this time she had assumed the work was a debt. Her whole drifting life—every season abandoned, every port slipped out of before the neighbors learned her name, every rule she kept about never staying—she had framed it as loyalty. Finishing the job. Carrying the weight. And her mother, it turned out, had spent her last year trying to take the weight off her. Had written instructions for putting it down.

The second time she read it slowly, and that was the mistake, or the salvation, she'd never be able to say which.

Because halfway through the second reading she stopped being a salvage diver assessing a document, and became a nine-year-old on a dock.

*Standing on the dock, not waving.* Her mother had known. Had seen it—had stood somewhere and watched her daughter refuse to wave and had carried the image for twenty years, had put it in a letter she never sent, had worried it soft at the folds the way Oscar worried the compass. And the phrase *in the only language I had, which was distance* — Oscar read that line four times, and each time it landed harder, because it was true, God help her, it was true of both of them. What else had Oscar's twelve years of drifting been? Distance, chosen freely, worn like armor, called independence. She had become her mother's letter. She was the unsent message, traveling forever, arriving nowhere.

The rain thickened. The coals across the deck pulsed once, dimming, and the words on the page nearly vanished, and Oscar found herself cupping the letter in both hands, hunching over it, physically protecting it from the dark—as if the dark might take it, as if the dark hadn't already taken everything else.

*I loved you the whole time, badly, from too far away.*

Something broke.

It didn't break dramatically. There was no sob, no sound at all, at first. It was more like a hull giving way below the waterline—a seam opening somewhere deep, pressure finding the one weak point, and then the whole structure of her quietly, catastrophically failing. She felt her face change. She felt her breath go wrong. And then she was crying—not the controlled leak she permitted herself sometimes at funerals, not the single angry tear she'd shed at eighteen and sworn off since, but crying the way children cry, ugly and helpless and loud enough that she had to press the letter flat against her mouth to muffle it, so as not to wake Bao, not to wake Pip, not to let the crew see whatever this was, this twelve-year flood finally cresting its levee.

She cried for the dock. She cried for the parcel that arrived late, the compass she'd carried unforgiven for two decades. She cried for the eleven failed drafts rotting in the bottom of a dead woman's kit. She cried because her mother had offered her a way out and the way out had come twelve years too late to matter, and because it mattered anyway, it mattered more than anything, because being released was not the same as being free but it was the first inch of the road there. She cried because *whichever lets you live your own life* meant her mother had looked at the ruin of her own choices and tried, with her last strength, to spare her daughter the same ruin—and Oscar had spent those same twelve years walking straight into the ruin anyway, calling it inheritance.

And underneath all of it, under the grief like silt under floodwater, another feeling moved that she couldn't name yet, something warm and terrible, something like relief. Because the letter wasn't a monument. It wasn't marble. It was a woman's voice, tired and funny and self-lacerating and precise, and it was talking to *her*, and for the length of four pages her mother was alive again and honest in a way she had never once been in life, and Oscar hated how much she wanted it to continue, how she slowed down at the final lines, rationing them, a diver stretching her last air.

*It's the truest sentence I know.* The river decides its passengers.

Bao's paint-flaking gold. Her mother had ridden this boat. Had sat where Oscar was sitting, maybe, in this same bow, in some monsoon of long ago, and read that sentence off the hull and carried it home to a letter. The river was smaller than Oscar had always believed. It was a house with very few rooms, and everyone she had ever loved had passed through all of them.

* * *

The rain stopped sometime before dawn, the way it did, all at once, like a held breath released. The clouds tore open in the east and let through a light that wasn't quite light—gray-green, underwater-colored, the world seen from beneath the surface. Mist stood on the river in columns. Ahead, where the channel narrowed toward the highlands, Oscar could see the first silhouettes of the karst rising out of the fog: towers of limestone dripping with vegetation, going up and up into cloud, and between them the water ran fast and silver and loud.

Loud. She noticed it the way you notice a change in current—first in the body, then in the mind. The river's voice had changed overnight. The lazy delta mutter was gone. What she heard now had edges in it.

Bao appeared beside her without making a sound, the way old boatmen do, a mug of tea already in his hand. He looked at her face—swollen, salt-tracked, wrung out—and he looked at the letter in her lap, and he said nothing about either. He only followed her gaze upstream to the white water showing pale between the towers, and nodded slowly, as if confirming something he'd suspected for days.

"Gorge," he said. "By midmorning. After that, no turning back for anyone." He pressed the tea into her hands. "Drink. Whatever you decided tonight—" his eyes flicked, brief and kind, to the letter "—decide it fast. The river won't wait for grief."

Oscar looked down at the page one last time. Then, carefully, along the original creases, she folded her mother's voice closed—and instead of returning it to the back cover of the journal, she slid it inside her shirt, against her skin, where the compass used to hang.

"Neither will I," she said, and drank the tea, and watched the white water come.
