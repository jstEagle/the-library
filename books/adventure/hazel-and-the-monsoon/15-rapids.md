## 15. Rapids

Pip had stolen from priests, soldiers, and one very angry monkey, and she had never once been afraid the way she was afraid now, standing on the wet stone lip of the gorge with the river roaring below like something that had been insulted.

The water wasn't brown anymore. That was the thing nobody warned you about upstream. In the delta it moved slow and muddy, fat with silt, lazy as a sleeping dog. Here it ran green-white and glassy at the edges, and in the middle it came apart entirely — whole hillsides of water folding over themselves, throwing up spray that hung in the air long after each wave died. The sound got into your teeth. It got into the soles of your feet through the rock.

"Look at it," Jun said beside her. He had his notebook out, which Pip thought was insane. Who takes notes at the mouth of a place like this? "The old charts call it the Throat of Kaeli. Every pilot who ever drew this stretch wrote *impassable in flood* next to it."

"Then why are we here?" Pip asked.

Jun didn't answer, which was an answer.

Bao stood at the bow of the ferry, testing the rope with his big scarred hands, pulling it hand over hand like he was hauling up something heavy from the bottom of the world. He'd said maybe forty words since dawn. His silence was worse than shouting. Old men who talked all the time were just old men. Old men who went quiet before a crossing were measuring their lives against the water and not liking the arithmetic.

Oscar crouched at the stern, checking the lashings for the third time. She hadn't slept — Pip could tell by the way she moved, too smooth, too careful, like a person walking on river ice. The letter she kept inside her shirt made a small stiff shape when she bent. Pip noticed things like that. Noticing things was the only school she'd ever graduated from.

"The channel runs left of the first pillar," Oscar said. "Then hard right where the water piles up against the cliff. After that—"

"After that," Bao said, without turning around, "the river decides."

"I need more than that."

"You need to hold on." He finally looked back at her, and his face under the white stubble was calm in a way that scared Pip more than the noise did. "I have run this crossing eleven times. I have seen it take nine boats. I am still here because I stopped arguing with it somewhere around the fourth time."

* * *

They loaded the boat light. That was the plan, such as it was: everything nonessential lashed high and dry on the ledge above the gorge, carried overland along the fisherman's trail while the boat ran the rapids empty except for Bao at the sweep oar, Oscar on the bow line, and — despite three separate arguments about it, two of which Pip had eavesdropped on through the hull planking last night — Pip herself.

"You shouldn't be aboard," Oscar had said, low, in the dark. "If we swamp, you can't swim the way you claim."

"I can swim better than you claim," Pip had answered from her nest under the tarp, before she remembered she was supposed to be asleep. A long silence followed. Then Oscar had sighed the sigh of someone losing a fight they'd already lost, and said, "Stay behind me. If I say jump, you jump. Don't think. Jump."

Pip had grinned into the dark. Then she'd lain awake half the night listening to the river, thinking about how Oscar never said *if you fall out, swim to shore*. She'd said *jump*. Like jumping would save you. Like there was anywhere to jump *to*.

Now, standing on the deck with the spray coming off the gorge in cold sheets, Pip understood. There was nowhere to jump to. There was only the boat, and the people in it, and the water that wanted all of them.

Bao pushed off.

* * *

The first hundred meters were almost gentle, which Pip learned was the river's idea of a joke. The current took them fast but smooth, the cliffs closing in on either side, walls of black wet stone furred with moss, so tall the morning sky shrank to a bright ragged ribbon overhead. Water fell from the cliffs in threads and ropes, some of it falling so far it turned to mist before it landed. The roar grew until it wasn't sound anymore, it was weather, it was the inside of a drum.

Then the first pillar came, and the gentle part ended forever.

The rock rose out of the middle of the channel like the tooth of something drowned, and the water hit it and split, and the split half they needed was running fast and steep and full of standing waves taller than Pip was. Bao leaned on the sweep and the boat swung left and then the world tilted, and Pip grabbed the line Oscar had tied for her — a rope from the mast step to her own waist, the knot Oscar herself had checked twice — and the river picked the ferry up like a leaf and threw it.

Spray hit like a slap. Cold went straight through her clothes into her bones. She couldn't see. She couldn't hear anything but the water. She heard Oscar shout something — her name, maybe, or *hold*, or nothing words at all, just a voice against the roar — and then the bow dropped into a trough and the stern lifted and for one long impossible second they were falling, actually falling, the whole boat airborne, and Pip's stomach floated up into her throat—

—and then impact, a blow that went through the hull and through her knees and through her teeth, and they were in the trough between waves, wallowing, shipping water, alive.

"Right!" Bao bellowed. "RIGHT! Pull!"

Oscar hauled the bow line and the boat swung, and the cliff came at them — the black wall where the water piled up and curled back on itself, a moving hill of river standing eight feet high and folding over backwards. Bao threaded them along its base so close that the curling lip passed overhead and rained down into the boat, and Pip saw, in one sideways flash, the whirlpool beyond it: a hole in the river the size of a house, spinning slowly, eating foam.

She understood then what the rope at her waist was for. Not to hold her aboard. To keep her body near enough to grab if the boat went and she didn't.

The whirlpool slid past on their right, close enough to touch, close enough that she could feel it pulling — a faint lean in the deck, a whisper of suction, the river showing them its mouth and saying *later, maybe later.*

And that was when the boat cracked.

It wasn't dramatic. No splintering explosion. Just a sound under her feet like a knuckle popping, deep and wrong, and the deck shifted a half inch beneath her, and water came up through the planks between two boards in a thin silver tongue, tasting the inside of the hull.

"She's opened!" Bao shouted. "She's opened her seam!"

"Where?" Oscar was already moving, tearing off her pack, looking for the leak.

"Midships! Below the waterline — I can't reach it!" And then the third wave hit, the biggest one, the one the old charts must have meant when they wrote *impassable*, and the boat went sideways.

Pip went into the water.

* * *

Cold is a strange teacher. It doesn't lecture. It just removes every thought from your head except the one it wants you to have, and the thought it gave Pip was: *the rope.*

The river rolled her like a sock in a wash cycle. Up was a rumor. Down was everywhere. Her ears filled with a pressure-roar and her body was nothing, a rag in a giant's fist. But the rope at her waist pulled taut, and the pull had a direction, and she followed it the way you follow a voice calling your name in a burning house.

Her head broke surface for half a second. Long enough to see: the boat ten feet away, listing, half-swamped; Oscar flat on the deck with both fists locked on the rope, boots braced against the mast step, being dragged forward inch by inch toward the rail; Bao gone from the sweep oar.

Gone.

"B—" she tried to shout, and the river stuffed the word back down her throat.

The rope jerked. Oscar slid another inch. Pip did the fastest arithmetic of her life: Oscar weighed more than her, the water was pulling harder than Oscar could hold, and in about four seconds either the rope would pull Oscar overboard or Oscar would let go and lose Pip to the whirlpool downstream.

There was a third option. It was a stupid option. It was the kind of option that got pickpockets hanged.

Pip dove.

Not toward the boat — toward the crack. Toward the hull, the wounded place, the seam that had opened midships. Underwater, in the green churn, she found it by feel: a gap between planks wide as two fingers, breathing water. She jammed her hand into it. The wood bit her fingers, ground them against the frame, and she screamed bubbles and held on anyway, wedging her fist deeper, becoming the plug.

The boat stopped taking water so fast. The rope stopped jerking quite so hard. Above her, muffled through the hull, she felt rather than heard Oscar haul — short desperate inches, gaining, gaining — and then hands closed on the back of her collar, on her belt, and she was hauled up out of the green dark into air and daylight and Oscar's arms, dripping, gasping, her right hand still buried in the crack of the world.

"Let go," Oscar said. "Pip. Let go, I've got you."

"My hand's stuck—"

"Pip." Oscar's face was an inch from hers, white-lipped, eyes wild, and for the first time since Pip had known her there was no calculation in it at all. Only terror, and underneath the terror something rawer. "I've got *you*. Let go of the boat."

Pip let go.

* * *

They found Bao thirty seconds later, wedged against a boulder in the shallows below the worst of the rapids, one arm cradled wrong against his chest, coughing river water and swearing in three languages, two of which Pip didn't recognize. The sweep oar was splinters. The ferry lay against the rocks downstream, half sunk, groaning, its spine broken — she could see the bend in the keel from the bank, a curve no keel should have.

They dragged him ashore onto a gravel shelf between the cliffs. Jun came scrambling down the fisherman's trail with the dry packs, took one look at the arm, and went the color of old paper.

"Dislocated," Bao said, before anyone else could say it. "Shoulder. Put it back."

"You should rest—"

"It has been out for two minutes. In two hours it will be a fist of fire. Put. It. Back." He lay down on the gravel and looked at the sky and folded his hands over his belly like a man settling in for a nap, which somehow made it worse.

Oscar knelt beside him. "On three?"

"On nothing. When I breathe out."

He breathed out. Oscar pulled and pushed, quick and brutal, and the joint went home with a sound like a door unlocking, and Bao made a noise that was mostly a laugh and lay still afterward, gray-faced, sweat cutting lines through the river grime on his face.

For a while nobody spoke. The gorge roared on, indifferent. Somewhere above them a bird cried once and got swallowed by the noise.

Pip sat on a rock and looked at her hand. Two fingernails were torn to the quick, bleeding freely, and the whole hand was swelling, the knuckles going purple. It hurt so much it was almost interesting. She wrapped it in a strip of her shirt hem with her teeth and her left hand, the way she'd taught herself years ago, alone, because there had never been anyone to do it for her.

A shadow fell across her. Oscar.

She crouched down, took Pip's wrapped hand without asking, and unwound the bandage with a gentleness Pip had never once felt from her. She looked at the ruined nails. She looked at the crack-shaped bruise already rising across Pip's palm where the wood had gripped her.

"You plugged the hull," Oscar said. "With your fist."

"It was there. The hole was there. My hand was there." Pip shrugged, which was her whole philosophy of life boiled down to one motion. "Seemed rude not to introduce them."

Oscar didn't laugh. She sat down on the gravel next to the rock instead, so they were level, eye to eye, and for a long moment she just looked at the river.

"I've been trying to leave you at every dock since the delta," she said.

"I know. You're bad at it. You always find some reason the next dock isn't right."

"I told myself it was because you'd steal us blind. Then it was because the roads weren't safe for a kid alone. Then it was because Bao wanted the company." She shook her head slowly. "That was all true. None of it was the reason."

"What was the reason?"

Oscar was quiet long enough that the gorge almost seemed to quiet with her, holding its breath between waves. "Because everyone I've ever stayed with, I've lost. So I stopped staying. Twelve years of leaving first, so leaving could never happen *to* me." She turned and looked at Pip, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "And then yesterday you went into that water after a rope I tied, and today you put your hand into a breaking boat, and I stood on that deck and understood that if the river had taken you, I wouldn't have gone back to drifting. I'd have gone under. So." She exhaled. "No more docks. No more next dock. You're crew. You're—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she pushed through it, "—you're family, if you'll have a family this bad at boats."

Pip stared at her. Something hot and horrible was happening behind her eyes, something she hadn't allowed since she was small enough to still believe adults fixed things.

"That's the worst recruitment speech I've ever heard," she managed.

"I know."

"I'm in."

Oscar nodded, once, like sealing a deal over a market stall, and then — quickly, roughly, like she was afraid of it — she pulled Pip against her shoulder and held on. Pip let her. The river roared. Her hand throbbed. It was, all things considered, the best moment of her entire criminal career.

* * *

They salvaged what they could. The ferry was dead — Bao walked its length once, touching the broken keel like a doctor at a deathbed, and pronounced it finished. But the cargo was safe on the ledge above, and the fisherman's trail ran on along the gorge, and Bao, flexing his bad arm with a wince, pointed upstream to where the cliffs pinched together and the river vanished into mist.

"There's a village past the narrows," he said. "River people. They'll have a boat to sell, or trade, or lend. And past the village—" he looked at Oscar, and something passed between them that Pip couldn't read, some old knowledge surfacing, "—past the village, the maps end. What's left of them."

Jun had his notebook open again, sketching the gorge from memory, and Pip watched him draw the whirlpool with a strange expression on his face — grief and hunger mixed together, the look of a man seeing a legend stand up and walk.

"We lost the boat," Pip said, hopping down beside Oscar. "We lost most of our supplies getting them dry. Bao's arm is wrecked, my hand is wrecked, and the river ate our spare rope." She grinned, and it hurt her split lip, and she grinned wider. "So what's the plan, Captain?"

Oscar looked upstream, at the mist, at the ribbon of white water vanishing between the black cliffs, at whatever was waiting there at the end of her mother's map. Her hand went briefly to her chest, to the letter hidden against her skin.

"Now," she said, "we walk into the part of the river that doesn't want to be found."

Above the narrows, faint through the roar, came a sound none of them expected: drums.
