## 16. The Forbidden Source

Jun had spent his whole life believing that maps were promises. Draw a line on paper, and the world would honor it. That was the covenant he'd sworn at nineteen, ink-stained and earnest, when the Royal Cartographic Society gave him his license and told him he would spend his years making the unknown legible.

The river had spent the last three days teaching him how much of that promise was a lie.

They came into the headwaters country at dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath the mist—a gray brightening, a softening of the dark, as though the sky itself were unsure whether to commit to morning. Bao cut the engine an hour before they needed to, and none of them asked why. The sound of water had changed. It was no longer the broad, patient murmur of the lowland river but something tighter, hungrier—the noise of a current being squeezed between walls of stone, gathering itself for violence.

Jun stood at the bow with the journal open against his chest, shielding it from the spray, and watched the cliffs assemble themselves out of the fog.

They were not like any cliffs he had charted. The karst country downstream had been all curves and dissolution, limestone eaten soft by ten thousand monsoons, but these were granite—old stone, hard stone, rising sheer out of the water in black faces streaked white with mineral weep. Mist clung to them in ropes and banners. Somewhere above, invisible birds were crying down through the cloud, their calls arriving thin and strange, like voices from a country that had already ended.

"Nobody's mapped this," Pip said beside him. She said it the way other people said *it's cold* or *I'm hungry*—a plain statement of fact, delivered without awe, which somehow made it more awesome. She had her elbows on the gunwale and her chin on her fists, and she was staring up at a wall of rock that must have been four hundred meters high, vanishing into cloud.

"The bureau has satellite imagery," Jun said. "Radar passes. Elevation models." He paused. "None of it is a map."

"What's the difference?"

"A map is an argument," Jun said. "It says: *this matters, this connects, this belongs to a story.* A satellite image just says *this exists.*" He touched the rail. "No one has argued for this place in a hundred years."

Bao grunted from the tiller. It was the grunt he used instead of agreement, or blessing, or warning—all three at once, depending on the hour. This morning it sounded like blessing.

Oscar was aft, coiling line with the automatic precision of someone whose hands needed work the way other people's hands needed rest. She hadn't slept much since the checkpoint. None of them had, really, but she wore it differently—not hollowed out, the way Seng's questions had left Jun feeling, but sharpened. Whetted. As if the inspector's offer had been a grindstone and Oscar had come off it with an edge.

She looked up at him now across the length of the boat, and jerked her chin toward the cliffs. "You feel that?"

He did. He'd been feeling it since the fuel stop two days back, since the last village where the old woman had spoken those few cracked syllables of Malayan and then pressed her hand over her own mouth as if the words might escape and report her. The feeling had grown with every bend: a pressure behind the sternum, like standing too near a waterfall. Like being watched by something patient.

"It feels like a church," Jun said.

"It feels like a trap," Oscar said. But she kept coiling the line, and she didn't tell Bao to turn around, and that was its own kind of answer.

* * *

The first carved stone appeared at midmorning.

Pip saw it first—of course she did; the girl's eyes worked like a salvage lamp, sweeping everything, missing nothing—and shouted for them to look starboard. Bao brought the boat in slow, engine idling, until they drifted close enough to touch it.

It was a slab of granite taller than a man, set upright on the bank where the river narrowed, its face dressed smooth by hands that had known exactly what they were doing. Water had gotten into the carving over the decades, freezing nothing in this climate but working its slow chemistry anyway, so the letters stood out in shadowed relief, deep and deliberate.

Jun knew the script before his mind finished telling him he couldn't possibly.

Malayan script. Not the colonial transcription he'd been stripped of his license for defending—the romanized approximation some treaty clerk had invented to file the nation away—but the true script, the one from the archive fragments, the one he'd spent thirty years studying in forbidden reproductions because no original was permitted to exist. Curved strokes and angular breaks, each character built like a small boat: hull, mast, keel.

His grandfather had drawn these characters for him in the dust behind their house when Jun was seven years old, saying, *Remember these, boy. One day someone will tell you they never existed.*

"Read it," Oscar said quietly. She had come forward without him noticing. Everyone was looking at him. Even Bao had turned on his thwart, and the old man's face held something Jun had never seen there before: hope, worn smooth by long disappointment, like sea glass.

Jun wet his lips. He read aloud, translating as he went, his voice unsteady on the current:

"*The river remembers what the world forgot.*"

He stopped. The mist moved. Somewhere upstream, water fell with a sound like distant applause.

"Keep going," Pip whispered.

"There's more. Below it." He leaned closer, one hand on the cold stone, and traced the second line with his eyes before trusting his voice to it. "*Turn back, or be remembered with it.*"

Silence, except for the river.

"That's not a threat," Bao said at last. His voice was rough gravel, and he spoke slowly, as if the words cost him. "That's an invitation."

"How do you figure?" Oscar asked.

"Because it doesn't say *we will kill you.* It says *you will be remembered.*" The old man looked up at the cliffs, at the mist, at whatever lived inside both. "Everything else in this world wants to forget you, girl. These people are offering the opposite."

Jun ran his fingers along the base of the stone and found what he'd half hoped, half feared: more characters, smaller, older, worn nearly to whispers. He crouched and read them by touch as much as sight. A date system he recognized from the archive fragments. And a name—or the beginning of one—chiseled and then deliberately defaced, gouged away by a chisel wielded with grief rather than anger.

Someone had loved this place enough to try to erase part of it. He thought of his license, revoked. Of his name struck from the society rolls. Of the way the bureau clerk had slid his life's work back across the desk and said, *We don't argue about things that don't exist,* and how Jun had wanted, in that moment, to stop existing himself.

"They've been here a long time," he said, standing. His knees cracked. He was suddenly, acutely aware of how old he was, and how young the stone was not. "Generations. Centuries. And they've been leaving messages the whole time—for anyone who could still read them."

"Can you?" Oscar asked.

"I can," Jun said. And then, because honesty had become a currency among them ever since the checkpoint, since Seng and her poisoned offer, since the letter Oscar wouldn't talk about: "For the first time in thirty years, I'm glad I can."

* * *

There were six more stones before nightfall.

Bao threaded the boat upstream through water that grew swifter and shallower, poling where the engine would have screamed, and at each narrows another stele rose from the bank or stood half-submerged in the shallows, tilted like a drowned congregation. Jun catalogued them in the journal's margins—Oscar had given him the journal that morning, formally, both hands, like handing over a child—and each inscription circled closer to something.

The first stones warned. *Turn back. The water keeps its own. Strangers drink twice and drown once.*

The middle stones instructed. *If you come in sorrow, come slowly. If you come in greed, the river will know you before you know yourself.*

And the last one, found at dusk on a promontory where the gorge bent north and the mist opened like a curtain being drawn, said something else entirely. Jun stood before it for a long time while the others made camp above the waterline, and he copied the characters into the margin with a hand that shook only a little.

*You who carry the words of the erased: you are expected.*

Below it, in fresher cutting, in script that mimicked the old forms but betrayed a modern hand—a student's hand, a careful hand—someone had added a single line within the last decade, maybe the last five years:

*She read this and wept. We brought her home.*

Jun sat down on the wet stone and put his face in his hands.

He knew. There was no question, no ambiguity, no cartographer's caution left to hide behind. *She.* A woman who read Malayan script—who had learned it somewhere, somehow, against every wall the world had built—and who had stood on this stone and wept, and been taken in. Oscar's mother had stood exactly here. Had touched exactly these characters. Had been *expected*, and had been *brought home*, and had walked out again into a world that destroyed her for what she carried.

And now her daughter was mooring a boat below the same stone, carrying the same words, wearing the same expression Jun could see reflected in the black water: grief and dread and a hunger so large it looked like rage.

He didn't tell her that night. He turned the page and let the campfire smoke blur the characters, and hated himself gently, the way you hate an old scar.

* * *

The waterfall announced itself hours before they saw it.

First as a change in the air—a coolness falling through the mist like weather with intentions. Then as a vibration in the hull, in the water, in Jun's back teeth. Then as light: a pale shimmer high on the cliff face ahead, where the gorge narrowed to a throat and the river seemed to simply end, swallowed by rock.

They rounded the final bend at first light on the third day, and there it was.

The falls dropped perhaps eighty meters down a seam in the granite, a single silver column fringed with ferns, and where it met the river it raised a permanent cloud that caught the early sun and broke it into pieces. Behind the falling water—visible only in slivers, when the wind pushed the column aside—the cliff face gaped black. A cave. An entrance. A door.

Jun took out the compass.

He had watched Oscar use it a dozen times now, had studied the micro-etched map beneath its faceplate until he could draw it from memory, and everything converged here: the route past the last mapped bend, the bearing that made no sense against any modern chart, the terminal mark her mother had etched with a jeweler's patience. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed directly at the black gap behind the water.

"The journal," Oscar said. She was beside him, and her voice was very quiet, the voice of someone standing at the edge of something they've chased for twelve years and aren't sure they want to catch.

Jun opened it to the final written pages. Her mother's hand—tight, urgent, the handwriting of a woman running out of time and knowing it—described the approach in terms that matched the stone inscriptions word for word. *The sixth stele marks the landing. The cave lies behind the fall. They will be watching. Come honest or don't come.*

"Come honest," Jun repeated aloud.

"I heard you." Oscar's jaw was tight. "What does that mean, though? Honestly. What does honest even look like, walking into—" She gestured at the falls, the cliffs, the whole vertical cathedral of the place. "Into this?"

"It means," Bao said from the tiller, "that you leave the lying on the boat."

Pip appeared between them, silent as she always was when it mattered, and pointed at the shore below the sixth stele. "There's a beach. Small. And steps." She squinted. "Cut steps. Going up behind the water."

Of course there were. There had probably been steps for three hundred years. The Kaeli had never needed maps; they had built the territory into the memory of anyone worth teaching, and left warnings for everyone else, and waited a century for the world to forget them completely.

Almost completely.

"We should decide now," Jun said, "what we're going to say. Who we are. What we want." He looked around the boat—at Oscar with her mother's compass clenched white-knuckled, at Pip with her quick eyes and quicker hands, at Bao who had carried a woman up this same river decades ago and never said a word about it until shame made him. "Because I've been the man who insisted on the truth and lost everything for it. And I'm telling you: whatever we say in there, we have to mean it. They'll know. People who guard a secret that size for a hundred years—they can smell a lie the way sharks smell blood."

Oscar was silent for a long moment. The falls filled the silence, that vast steady applause.

"My mother wrote that the archive isn't treasure," she said finally. "It's people. Records of people. Names." She looked down at the compass in her palm, and her thumb moved over the faceplate the way you'd touch a scar. "So that's what we say. We came because of her. We came to finish what she started—or to bury it, whichever lets us live our own lives. Those were her words." Her voice caught, barely. "Her exact words."

"You've decided to tell them about the letter," Jun said. It wasn't a question.

"I've decided to stop deciding things alone." She said it flatly, but Jun heard what was underneath it, the thing twelve years of solo diving and solo sleeping and solo leaving had tried to drown: *I'm tired. I want crew. I want family.* "Whatever happens behind that water, it happens to all of us. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Pip said instantly.

Bao grunted—that triple-purpose grunt—and began steering for the little beach below the sixth stele.

Jun closed the journal and held it against his chest, and looked up at the falls, at the black door behind the silver water, at the mist rising off it like breath. Thirty years ago they had taken his license and told him Malaya was a fairy tale, and he had spent three decades half-believing them, carrying his grandfather's dust-drawn alphabet like a wound he couldn't stop picking.

Now he was about to walk into the proof.

The boat grounded on the shingle. The mist settled over them, cool and mineral-sweet. And from somewhere above the falls—from the darkness behind the water, or the cliffs, or the century itself—came a sound that stopped all four of them where they stood.

A bell. Small, bronze-toned, rung three times.

Then a voice, high and clear, speaking Malayan script's living descendant in accents Jun had only ever seen fossilized in documents:

"*We see you. Leave your weapons in the boat. And come honest—as your stone promised, and as she did.*"

Oscar went pale. Bao bowed his head. Pip's hand found Jun's sleeve and gripped it.

Jun stepped onto the shore of the unmapped world, and answered in the language his grandfather had taught him in the dust, the first time in his life he had ever spoken it aloud to anyone but the dead:

"We came for her. And we came honest."
