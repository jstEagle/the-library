## 17. Voss Catches Up

Voss had always believed that a man could smell water before he heard it—that the air itself went sweet and heavy near a fall, the way it did before money changed hands. He stood on the foredeck of the *Kestrel* with his collar up against the spray and felt that sweetness now, thick as syrup, rolling up out of the gorge in cold silver breath. Somewhere ahead, hidden behind three bends of black limestone, the river was falling off the edge of the world.

He had followed them for eleven days. Eleven days of running dark without lights, of cutting his engine at every patrol boat's wake, of watching through night glasses as Bao's old ferry—slow as a funeral barge, stubborn as a mule—threaded channels that should have swallowed her. He had lost them twice in the flooded karst and found them again by the simplest trick in his trade: he had stopped looking for the boat and started looking for the smoke. Old men and children cooked rice at dusk. It was almost too easy. It had been easy with the mother, too, once. That was the thing about people like Oscar Nakamura and her mother before her—they moved through wild country as if the country owed them something. As if honesty were armor.

The Kestrel's engines idled low behind a curtain of hanging vines. Around Voss in the stern crouched six men he'd hired out of the delta ports—quiet professionals, mostly, though two of them were only quiet in the way that knives are quiet. Dara, his foreman, checked the magazines of the rifles for the third time. The boy with the scarred knuckles—Voss had never bothered learning his name, which was itself a kind of discipline—passed around strips of dried fish and said nothing.

"Say it again," Dara murmured. "The layout."

"Behind the third bend there's a pool," Voss said. "Above the pool, the falls. Above the falls, a cave mouth—the map says so, and the map hasn't lied yet." He touched the breast pocket where the micro-etched plate rode against his skin, warm from his body, cool from its brass housing. He had taken the compass off the girl four days ago at gunpoint on the water, in the dark among the karst, and she had given it up with a stillness that still bothered him. Not fear. Something else. Something that looked almost like pity. "There'll be a landing below the pool. Their ferry will be tied there. The chest may be aboard or may not—I don't care about the chest anymore. The compass is the prize. With that plate, I can find the archive myself, and I don't need any of them alive to show me."

"And the woman?"

"The diver?" Voss smiled, and the smile was an old habit, worn smooth as river stone. "Alive if it's convenient. Dead if it isn't. She's not the point. She was never the point."

But that wasn't quite true either, and Voss knew it, and the knowing sat in him like a hook set deep in the gullet. Twelve years ago he had stood in these same hills—in hills so like these that when the mist came down off the ridgeline this morning he had felt his own past settle over him like wet cloth—and he had made a choice. A small choice, the kind that feels reasonable at the time, the kind a man makes with a rifle in one hand and his own survival in the other. He had left Elena Nakamura on a fisherman's trail above a swollen tributary with a fever and half their supplies, because the bureau's boats were coming upriver and someone had to be holding the maps when they arrived. Someone had to be clean. He had told himself she would make it down. He had told himself a great many things.

She hadn't made it down. Or rather—she had, eventually, in the way that ruined women do, walking into the capital two months later with her hair gone white at thirty-nine and her name already being eaten alive in the newspapers, and the bureau had finished what the hills had started. Disgraced her. Stripped her consultancy. Burned her field notes—or claimed to. And Voss had built a career on the ashes, buying and selling the artifacts of dead nations to collectors who liked their history extinct, and telling himself every year that the woman's death was the bureau's doing, not his. That he had merely failed to save her. There was a difference, he'd insisted. There was a difference.

He was about to learn, again, whether he believed it.

* * *

They came around the third bend at first light, engines cut, poling the Kestrel through the last hundred meters of slack water, and the falls announced themselves the way a cathedral announces itself—not heard so much as felt in the ribs. White thunder. A column of mist standing in the gorge like a ghost that refused to leave. And there, exactly as the etched plate promised, a shelf of rock behind the falling water, black-lipped, patient, older than any nation that had ever tried to own it.

Below the pool, tied to a root-buttressed fig, rode the old ferry. Bao's boat. Her paint was peeling, her rail patched with wire, and she sat in the current like something that had been moored there since the beginning of weather.

And on the shore—this Voss had not expected—he saw figures. Brown-skinned, barefoot, wrapped in indigo cloth, standing in a loose line at the tree line with spears that caught the early light along their edges. Not warriors, exactly. Witnesses. They stood the way stone stands, and among them moved a tall elder woman whose voice, even at this distance, carried the cadence of command. Behind them, half-glimpsed through the foliage: rooftops. Thatch. Smoke from morning fires. A village. A living village, here, at the throat of the world, where every map Voss had ever bought or stolen said there was nothing but cloud forest and death.

So the rumors were true. So the mother had been right, all those years ago, when she'd stood in Voss's rented office in the capital with her hands flat on his desk and told him the archive wasn't lost—it was kept. He had laughed at her then. Politely. The way you laugh at a colleague who has begun to embarrass you. He had been laughing at her for twelve years, right up until the moment a teenage pickpocket lifted a brass compass off a drowned temple diver and the whole rotten architecture of his disbelief came down at once.

Dara touched his arm. "They've seen us."

Of course they had. You didn't hide a boat the size of the Kestrel from people who had lived in this gorge since before the treaty that erased their nation. Voss looked at the line of witnesses on the shore and understood that stealth was over, that the hour for subtlety had closed somewhere back in the flooded karst, and that what remained was the oldest transaction in his trade: take, or be taken.

"Rifles visible," he said. "Not raised. We go in slow, we go in smiling, and the moment I have the compass in my hand, everything else is negotiable." He paused. "Everything except the exit. Nobody stays behind me on that trail. Understood?"

Six heads nodded. The boy with the scarred knuckles chambered a round anyway, soft as a kiss, and Voss let it go. Fear was useful. Fear kept hired men honest for exactly as long as a job required.

They crossed the pool with the outboard barely turning over, and the sound of the falls swallowed them whole, and Voss stepped onto the shingle below the fig tree into a world gone strangely quiet—the roar of the water somehow outside the silence rather than part of it, the way a wall stands outside a room.

Oscar Nakamura was waiting for him.

* * *

She stood apart from the others, nearer the water, arms folded, and she did not look surprised. That was the first thing he noticed, and it wrong-footed him more than any ambush could have. The others—Bao leaning on his staff with his shoulder bound in a sling, the cartographer Jun hovering protectively near a canvas satchel, the girl Pip half-hidden behind the ferryman like a cat behind a chair—all of them wore the faces of people who had hoped the past wouldn't follow them. But the diver looked at Voss the way a doctor looks at a symptom. As if she had already diagnosed him.

"You're louder than your reputation," she said.

"My reputation is mostly volume," Voss agreed, and spread his hands, and gave them the smile. The good one. The one that had opened a hundred locked warehouses and closed a hundred suspicious mouths. "I'm not here for blood. I want you to know that. Whatever you've heard about me—"

"I haven't heard anything about you," Oscar said. "That's the strange part. My mother never mentioned you at all."

The sentence went through him like a spear through wet paper. He kept the smile fixed, but something behind it shifted, settled, sank. Of course she hadn't mentioned him. You don't mention the man who left you. You don't give him the dignity of a name in your journal. He had read enough of Elena Nakamura's recovered notes over the years—bought fragments, mostly, salvaged from the bureau's careless bonfires—to know how she wrote about betrayal: precisely, coldly, without names, as if naming it would make it real.

"Maybe not," Voss said smoothly. "But she and I were colleagues once. Close ones. And I've come to finish what she started—which is more than the bureau ever intended to do. Those people—" he tipped his chin at the line of witnesses under the trees, at the elder woman with her silver-streaked hair and her unblinking eyes, "—are sitting on the single greatest historical discovery in a century. A nation's entire memory. Genealogies. Treaties. Songs that exist nowhere else on earth. And your plan, as far as I can tell, is to leave it in a cave." He shook his head, sorrowful, reasonable. "Elena wanted it for the world. I want it for the world. The difference is that I have the resources to actually get it out."

"The world," Bao said, from beside the fig tree, in a voice like gravel poured slowly. "You say that word like it's a place you've been."

Voss ignored him. He was watching Oscar. He had learned long ago that negotiations were won by watching the person who mattered and letting everyone else become furniture. "Here's my offer, and it's generous, because I owe your mother's memory that much. The compass plate—you don't have it, I do, so let's call that settled. What I need is cooperation. Your ferryman knows these waters better than any pilot I could hire. Your cartographer reads the old script. And you—" the smile widened a degree, "—you have her instincts. I saw that in the karst. You dive like she dove. You hold your breath like she held hers. Come with me. Guide me in. When it's done, the archive goes to museums, to archives, to light—and your mother's name comes out of the mud with it. Vindicated. Celebrated. Everything she was denied. All you have to do is stop protecting strangers who would just as soon put a spear in you as shake your hand."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The falls roared. Mist drifted through the clearing like slow smoke, beading on the rifle barrels, silvering the old ferryman's beard.

Then Oscar unfolded her arms.

"You keep saying her name," she said. "Like you knew her."

"I worked with her for six years."

"Then tell me what happened in the hills." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it stayed level, stayed diving-deep, and her eyes never left his face. "Twelve years ago. Upstream of here. Tell me what happened, since you knew her so well."

And Voss—who had rehearsed a dozen versions of this conversation on the long run upriver, who had prepared deflections and charm and the careful strategic confession that stops just short of guilt—heard himself answer honestly, because her stillness left him no room to do otherwise, because some questions are doors that only open inward.

"She got sick," he said. "Fever. We were on the fisherman's trail, three days from anywhere, and the bureau's patrol boats entered the lower gorge. If they'd caught us together, we'd both have gone down—her work, my licenses, everything. So I took the maps and the satellite phone and I went down ahead." He heard the words coming out of him and could not stop them, could not soften them, could not make them anything other than what they were. "I told myself she was strong enough to walk out alone. She wasn't. By the time she reached the lowlands the fever had done its work, and the bureau was waiting, and I—" the smile finally died, and what was underneath it was sixty-one years old and very tired, "—I let them take her. I stood on a hillside and watched their boats come for her and I did nothing, because her ruin was cheaper than mine. That's what happened in the hills. That's the version I've spent twelve years not saying out loud."

The clearing went absolutely still. Even the mist seemed to hang.

Pip made a small sound, quickly smothered. Bao's hand tightened on his staff until the wood creaked. And Oscar Nakamura stood in the roaring quiet and Voss watched something move across her face that he recognized, because he had seen its twin in a mirror every morning for twelve years. It was not shock. Shock would have been kinder. It was confirmation—the sick, settling weight of a suspicion finally granted its shape. She had grown up believing her mother died of disgrace, of obsession, of a life spent chasing ghosts instead of daughters. She had built her whole drifting, season-bound, never-staying life on that foundation. And now, in one breath from a stranger's mouth, the foundation cracked and showed what lay beneath it: not disgrace. Abandonment. Her mother had not simply been destroyed by the state. She had been delivered to it. Left on a mountain by a man who is now standing in front of her daughter offering to sell her the same hill.

"That's why she never named you," Oscar said quietly. "In the journal. There's a hole in the account—two weeks, upstream of here, no entries. I thought she was delirious. She wasn't. She just couldn't stand to write you down."

"Oscar—" Jun started.

"No." She raised one hand, and Jun stopped. She looked at Voss for a long, long moment, and Voss braced himself—for grief, for screaming, for the wild swing of a woman undone. He had seen all of it in his career. He knew how to handle all of it.

What he did not know how to handle was what actually happened, which was that she stepped closer, close enough that he could see the river-light reflected in her eyes, and said, in a voice with no heat in it at all:

"You're going to give me back the compass."

"I'm going to do no such thing."

"Yes," she said, "you are. Because you just confessed to abandoning her in front of witnesses—" she nodded at the treeline, at the elder woman who had not moved, whose expression had not changed, "—and those people have laws older than your contracts. And because you think this ends with you walking out of here rich, and it doesn't. It ends with you understanding what she understood at the end. This place doesn't need finding. It needs leaving alone. And the only question left is whether you're smart enough to accept that, or stubborn enough to make these good people defend it."

Voss looked at her—at this woman who measured her life in river miles, who trusted no one longer than a season, who had every reason in the world to take his offer and buy herself a new existence in some port city where no one knew her mother's name—and he saw, with the clarity that sometimes descends on men at the exact moment it becomes useless, that he had misjudged her completely. She was not her mother. Her mother had believed in the world. This one believed in something smaller and harder and infinitely more dangerous to men like him.

"Guns," Voss said softly.

The six rifles came up as one, and the negotiation ended the way most of his negotiations ended, and none of his mornings ever began: with the truth already spoken and nothing left to trade but force.

"Take the compass from her if she has a copy," he ordered. "Tie the ferryman. Take the chest, the journal, everything paper. And the girl—" he glanced at Pip, frozen behind Bao, "—nobody touches the girl. We're thieves, not animals."

It was Dara who moved first, and it was Dara who learned the day's second lesson, which was that the diver fought the way she dove—low, patient, and with terrifying economy—before two of the others pinned her arms and the boy with the scarred knuckles wrenched the satchel off Jun's shoulder and spilled the journal into the mud. Voss picked it up himself. Water-stained, swollen, handwritten in a slanting script he knew better than his own signature. Twelve years of a dead woman's secret hunt, ending mid-sentence. He had burned pages like this once, on bureau orders, and told himself it was mercy.

"Elder Suri," Oscar said, loud, over the rifles, speaking to the treeline. "Whatever happens next—keep your people back. This is ours to lose."

The old woman inclined her head, once, unreadable.

They marched the crew to the ferry and took her lines, and Voss stood on the shingle with the journal under his arm and the compass plate warm against his chest, and gave the order he had been carrying upriver like a stone in his boot.

"Cut them loose here. No boat, no supplies past what they can carry. The trail over the mountain is the only way down, and it eats people." He looked at Oscar one last time—at the blood at her lip, at the fury banked behind her eyes like a fire in a sealed room. "Your mother walked that trail," he said. "Alone. Consider it a family tradition."

The Kestrel pulled away into the mist with Bao's ferry lashed helplessly astern, and the falls roared on, indifferent, keeping—as the carved stones had promised—the memory of everything the world forgot.

Including, Oscar said to no one, staring at the empty water, the debt he was about to pay.
