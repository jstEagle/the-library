## 18. Left for the River

The engine sound faded for a long time before it stopped being a sound at all.

Oscar stood on the mudbank where Bao's boat had been tied and listened to it go—Voss's boat, Voss's stolen prize, Voss's laughter swallowed by the gorge walls—until there was nothing left but water slapping stone and the enormous indifferent hush of the hills. The rope that had held their mooring lay coiled in the shallows, cut clean. He hadn't even bothered to untie it. That was the detail that would stay with her, she thought. Not the gun, not the men, not the chest going over the rail into his hold. The cut rope. A man who unties a knot might mean to come back. A man who cuts it has already decided you are no one's problem anymore.

"They took everything," Pip said behind her. Her voice was doing something strange—flat, careful, the voice of a girl holding herself together with both hands.

"Not everything," Jun said.

Oscar turned. Jun was crouched by the pack they'd carried up from the boat, the one with the cooking things and the spare line, and he was laying its contents out on a flat rock with the grim precision of a man taking inventory of a wound. Rice. A dented kettle. Half a roll of mosquito coil. One tarp. Two machetes, because Bao had never trusted a man who owned only one.

"No journal," Jun said. "No compass. No charts." He looked up at her, and his face in the grey light of the gorge was older than it had been an hour ago. "But we have each other, and we have the river, and the river is still going the same direction it was yesterday."

"The river," Bao said, "is not the problem."

He was sitting against the bole of a fig tree with his left arm cradled against his chest, and even from ten paces Oscar could see the shoulder was wrong—the whole line of him skewed, the joint sitting high and swollen under his shirt. He'd wrenched it hauling the bowline when Voss's men shoved off, refusing to let go until the rope went taut and dragged him half into the water. Stubborn old man. Brave old man. Both true, and neither useful now.

"Bao." She knelt beside him. "Let me look."

"Later."

"Now."

He let her peel the shirt back from the shoulder, hissing once through his teeth when the fabric caught. The joint was a hard knot of wrongness under skin gone purple-black. Oscar had set bones before—on salvage crews, on dive boats, once on a dredge barge where a winch cable had taken a man's arm nearly off—but shoulders were different. Shoulders fought you.

"It's out," she said. "I can try to put it back, but I need you to breathe through it, and I need you not to hit me."

"I am eighty-one years old," Bao said. "Everything hurts already. Do it."

She did it fast, the way you pull a hook from your own hand—no ceremony, no warning—and Bao made a sound like a door tearing off its hinges and then went very quiet, forehead pressed to the fig bark, breathing in long deliberate pulls while the joint settled back into itself. When he could speak again he said something in the old river dialect, low and filthy, and Pip laughed despite herself, a cracked bright sound, and then covered her mouth as if laughing were also something they'd lost.

"Good," Bao said. "You laugh. Keep that. It will be worth more than rice where we're going."

"Where *are* we going?" Pip asked. She'd sunk down onto the mudbank with her feet in the shallows, and when she pulled them out to shift position Oscar saw why she'd been so still. Both feet, from heel to toe, were ribboned with cuts—the silty shallows here hid broken bamboo and old fish traps and the teeth of drowned trees, and Pip had been standing in them, and walking on them, and saying nothing about any of it. The blood came away in pink threads in the current.

"Pip—" Oscar started.

"I'm fine."

"You've been standing in a butcher's floor and calling it fine."

"I didn't want to be another thing to carry." It came out fast and furious and young, and then Pip looked down at her own feet as if surprised by them, as if the body had finally filed its complaint directly. Her chin trembled once. She killed it. Fourteen years old and already fluent in the language of not being a burden; Oscar knew every word of it, had spoken it herself across twelve years of riverports, and hearing it in someone else's voice was like hearing her own name called in a crowd.

"We're going up," Bao said, before either of them could make it worse. He nodded at the western wall of the gorge, where the cliffs stepped back into terraces of cloud forest, green going grey going white as the mist took them. "There's a trail. Fisherman's trail, my father used it, his father before him. Over the shoulder of the mountain and down to the headwater country on the far side. Three days if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't."

"Can we walk it?" Jun asked. "In this?" He gestured at the sky, which had been building thunderheads since noon, fat and bruise-colored above the ridgeline.

"People have walked it in worse."

"People have also drowned in worse."

Bao smiled without much humor. "Yes. That too."

* * *

They camped that night under the tarp, strung between two trees above the flood line, and Oscar did not sleep so much as stand watch over four people breathing.

Her mind kept running the arithmetic of loss. The chest—her mother's chest, the lead-and-iron weight of it she'd hauled out of a flooded temple what felt like a lifetime ago—gone into Voss's hold. The compass—the brass thing her mother had given her at nine, the gift she'd carried unforgiven for twenty years, the gift that had turned out to carry a map etched beneath its faceplate, a map that had led them here—gone, pried from her grip at gunpoint while Voss smiled his broker's smile and told her, gently, almost kindly, that her mother would have understood. Everything her mother had left her, taken by the man who had abandoned her mother in these same hills.

And yet.

She lay on her back and listened to the camp breathe. Jun snored faintly, a cartographer's snore, precise and regular. Bao slept sitting up against the tree, arm bound in a sling of torn shirt, and even asleep his good hand rested near the machete. And Pip—Pip had curled up close to the fire ring, feet wrapped in strips of Oscar's spare shirt, and she slept the way small animals sleep, all at once and totally, as if sleep were a country she could enter without papers.

Stripped of everything, Oscar thought. And look what's left.

It was more than she'd had a month ago. That was the obscene truth of it, the truth she couldn't stop turning over: a month ago she'd had a boat, a chest, a compass, and no one. Now she had none of those things and three people who had followed her into a gorge at gunpoint, and if you'd asked her which inventory she'd rather lose from, she would have lied to herself before answering honestly.

Rain began around midnight, soft at first, then committed.

* * *

The fisherman's trail, it turned out, was less a trail than an argument the mountain occasionally won.

It climbed from the river in switchbacks so steep that Bao, one-armed, had to be roped to Jun, and even then he climbed like a man ascending stairs in his own house, muttering directions in a dialect Jun scrambled to write phonetically in the margins of nothing—he had no paper left, so he scratched route notes into the memory of his own hands, tracing lines on his forearm with a finger, the way he'd learned maps before he'd ever been allowed to draw them.

By midday the river was a rumor below them, a green gleam through the canopy, and the mist had come down to meet them, and the world contracted to a corridor of wet black earth and hanging moss and the sound of their own breathing. Leeches found them within the hour. Pip picked them off Jun's neck with a pinch of salt from the rice supply, matter-of-fact, surgeon-calm, and Oscar watched her do it and thought: *she has done harder things than this before I ever met her, and no one carried her then either.*

Pip's feet were the problem.

The wrappings had soaked through in the first hour of rain, and by the second day the cuts had opened properly—not dangerous, not yet, but deep enough that every step left a dark print on the wet stone, and the girl had begun to favor her right side in a way that threw her balance off on the narrow ledges. She said nothing. Of course she said nothing. She walked with her jaw set and her eyes on the ground and she kept pace, and she would have kept pace until she fell, because falling would at least end the arithmetic of being a burden.

Oscar saw it happen on the third morning, at a place where the trail crossed a slide of loose shale above a drop into white mist. Pip went first, testing each stone, and halfway across her right foot slid and she caught herself on her hands and stayed there, on all fours on the shale, not moving. Not crying. Just staying, the way a machine stays when a part finally gives.

"Pip."

"I'm resting."

"You're done resting in five seconds. Come here."

"I'm *fine*, Oscar—"

"*Come here.*"

Something in Oscar's voice must have been new, because Pip came—hobbled across the shale with her hands out like a tightrope walker and stood in front of her, swaying slightly, defiant and exhausted and fourteen. Oscar crouched, turned her back.

"Get on."

"That's ridiculous. I can walk."

"You can walk until you fall off a mountain, and then I'll wish I'd carried you. Get on."

For a moment Pip just stared at her, and Oscar saw the whole war behind her eyes—the pride, the fear, the terrible arithmetic, and underneath all of it something smaller and hungrier that wanted very badly to be told yes, you may rest, someone will hold the weight. Then she climbed on, arms around Oscar's shoulders, light as driftwood and shaking slightly, and Oscar stood up with her and walked.

She was heavier than she looked. They always were, the ones who never asked.

The shale gave way to forest, the forest to a ridge of wind-bent rhododendron, and somewhere in the climbing Oscar stopped counting steps and started listening—to Pip's breath evening out against her neck, to Bao ahead calling trail markers in his father's tongue, to Jun behind her reciting bearings under his breath like prayers. The rain came and went. The mist opened once, briefly, onto a view south across miles of unbroken green to where the river they'd lost ran silver through the gorge, and Pip lifted her head from Oscar's shoulder to look at it.

"He's got our boat," she said quietly.

"He's got a boat."

"And the chest. And the compass."

"Yes."

"Aren't you scared? That he'll find the cave first?"

Oscar walked a dozen steps before she answered, choosing the truth carefully, the way you choose footing on shale.

"He has the map," she said. "He doesn't have the reason. Voss wants to sell what's behind that waterfall. My mother spent twelve years trying to find it, and when she found it, she chose to protect it. I'm not going to let a man who left her to die decide how her story ends." She shifted Pip's weight higher. "Besides. He thinks we're finished. Stranded, footless, one-armed, and finished. Let him think it. People who think they've won get careless, and people who get careless leave trails."

"You sound like Bao."

"I sound like someone who's been listening to Bao."

Pip laughed—a real laugh this time, startled out of her—and put her cheek back down against Oscar's shoulder, and they climbed.

* * *

They made the top of the pass at dusk on the third day, and Bao, gray-faced and feverish, pointed with his good arm at the country beyond.

It fell away from them in folds—cloud forest descending into valleys threaded with water, dozens of streams braiding and rebraiding in the blue distance, all of them crawling toward some common gathering place hidden in the haze to the north. Somewhere down there, behind a waterfall marked on a compass faceplate, was a cave. Somewhere down there was the archive, and the people who had kept it alive for a hundred years after every map forgot them. Somewhere down there was the answer to the question her mother had died holding.

"There," Bao said. His voice was roughened by fever, but steady. "Headwater country. Kaeli land, though they won't thank you for the name spoken loud." He looked at Oscar, and his eyes were bright and wet and utterly clear. "Your mother stood on this exact stone. I carried her pack. She looked at that valley the way you're looking at it now, and then she made me promise something."

"What promise?"

"That if she didn't come back down, and if someday her daughter came instead—" he paused, and the fever seemed to fall away from him for a moment, leaving only the old ferryman, the keeper of other people's crossings, "—that I wouldn't let you turn around."

The wind moved through the rhododendrons, a long cold breath from the north, from the source.

"She knew I'd come?"

"Hazel," Bao said gently, using the name she hadn't given him, the name she'd buried with her mother, "mothers know their children the way rivers know the sea. It isn't knowing. It's gravity."

Below them the first stars came out over the valley, and far off, so faint it might have been wind or might have been water, Oscar heard the sound of a waterfall falling somewhere in the dark—and under it, or perhaps only in her own blood, the sense of being watched by something patient, something that had waited a hundred years and could wait one more night.

Pip slipped her hand into Oscar's. Jun checked his invisible bearings one last time. Bao leaned on them all.

"Down," Oscar said. "Before the light goes."

They started down into the valley together.
