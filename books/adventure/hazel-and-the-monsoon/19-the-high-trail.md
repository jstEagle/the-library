## 19. The High Trail

The mountain did not want us on it.

That was the first thing I understood about the high trail, somewhere in the second hour of climbing when the fisherman's path Bao had described turned out to be less a path than a rumor — a suggestion of flat stone between the roots of trees that had never seen a boot they liked. The mountain wanted us gone the way a dog wants a stranger off its porch: not with violence, not at first, but with a long, patient, unfriendly stare.

I'd been on mountains before. Stealing through hill towns in the dry season, sleeping in terraced fields where the farmers pretended not to see me because a thin kid who doesn't ask for anything is easier to ignore than to evict. But those were hills with people stitched all over them — shrines, fences, laundry lines, the smell of somebody's lunch. This was different. This was cloud forest, and the cloud was not weather so much as a country of its own, a gray-green nation that had annexed everything above two thousand feet and governed it with moss.

Moss on the stones. Moss on the branches. Moss, I was fairly sure by the third day, beginning its slow patient work on my boots.

"You're walking too loud," Oscar said behind me, for maybe the hundredth time.

"I'm walking like a person."

"You're walking like a herd of water buffalo wearing bells."

I turned around to tell her exactly what she could do with her advice, and that's when the leech got me.

I felt it before I saw it — that faint wrongness on my ankle, a cold tickle that wasn't quite pain yet. I yelped, sat down hard on a root, and started tearing at my bootlace, and Oscar crouched beside me with the salt tin out of her pack before I'd even finished swearing.

"Hold still." She shook a few grains onto the thing, which was fat and black and glistening against my skin, and it dropped off with what I swear was an offended little wiggle. She flicked it into the ferns. "You check every hour. Behind the knees, waistband, under the arms. They find you when you stop moving, so don't stop moving."

"Great," I said. "A mountain full of worms that only attack you when you rest. Who designed this place?"

"The same god who designed rivers," Oscar said, standing, "and he wasn't trying to be kind to either of us."

But here's the thing about Oscar Nakamura that I was only starting to understand, up there in the wet green dark: she complained like a person who expected nothing from anyone, and then she gave everything anyway. She carried half of Bao's load strapped over her own pack without asking him, because asking would have meant admitting he couldn't manage it, and admitting that would have made it true. She checked my ankles for leeches when I forgot, with the brisk impersonal efficiency of someone doing engine maintenance. And when we made camp that first night — a lean-to of palm fronds against a fallen trunk, a fire that smoked more than burned because everything was soaked — she gave me the driest corner of the tarp without a word, and took the wet one herself.

I didn't thank her. Thanking her would have been like poking a wild animal. But I lay there listening to the rain start again on the leaves, ten thousand small fingers drumming, and I thought: *she's counting us.* Not miles. Us. I could see her doing it in the firelight, her eyes going around our little circle — Jun hunched over his notebook, Bao shivering under two blankets, me — the way a ferryman counts heads before pushing off from shore.

She just didn't know yet that she was doing it.

* * *

Bao's fever came on the second night.

It started as grumbling — the old man complaining that his shoulder, wrenched loose in the rapids and strapped back tight with a sling of torn shirt, ached worse than the day it happened. Then the grumbling went quiet, which was worse. By midnight he was burning. I put my hand on his forehead and snatched it back like I'd touched a stove.

"He needs medicine," I said. "Real medicine. Not tea."

"We have willow bark," Jun said. "And we have three days' walk to anywhere that sells anything else. So we have willow bark."

Jun brewed it in the dented pot over a fire we kept alive under an oilcloth awning, feeding it damp wood with the patience of a man feeding a stubborn animal. He'd gotten good at fires on this trip. We all had. That's the thing nobody tells you about survival — it isn't dramatic. It's the same twelve small jobs, done over and over, until your hands know them better than your head does.

Bao drifted in and out, and when he was under, he talked. Not to us. To somebody else, some other time. Once he said a name I didn't know — "Mei-Lin, mind the line" — and once he said, very clearly, "The river decides," which was his oldest saying, the one he'd used on me the day Oscar wanted to dump me at a dock and he'd refused. And once, near dawn, he gripped Oscar's wrist with a strength that surprised all of us and said:

"Your mother sang. Did you know? On the boat. Badly. Like a cat in a sack."

Oscar didn't answer. Her face did something complicated and then closed like a door. But she stayed kneeling there, holding his hand, until the grip loosened and he sank back into fever-sleep.

I watched her from across the fire and thought about how she'd spent twelve years not thinking about her mother, or saying she hadn't, and how the mountain had stripped all that away in four days. Up here there was nowhere to hide from anything. Not the rain, not the leeches, not the past. The cloud came down inside your clothes and inside your chest, and everything you'd packed away tight got damp and swelled and leaked.

* * *

On the fourth night the clouds tore open for the first time since we'd left the drowned village, and Jun stood up so fast he knocked over the pot.

"Stars," he said, like a man announcing a miracle. Which, I suppose, he was.

We climbed a bare granite shoulder above camp — Oscar carrying Bao partway on her back, me hauling the packs, Jun scrambling ahead like a much younger man than his drinking years suggested. And when we crested the rock, the sky was *full*. I'd seen stars my whole life, from rooftops and riverbanks, but always through haze, through smoke, through the light-leak of towns. Up here the sky was a black so deep it looked wet, and the stars were not scattered across it but *packed*, dense as fish scales, whole rivers of them pouring from one horizon to the other.

"That one." Jun pointed low in the south, to a star that seemed no different from any other. "My grandfather called it the Anchor. You hold it at the bottom of the sky and everything else arranges itself around it. He taught me the stars before he taught me letters. Said maps burn, but the sky can't be confiscated."

He said it lightly, but I heard the weight underneath. Stripped of his license. Called a liar in front of a room full of men with stamps and seals, for insisting on a country that officially did not exist. And now here he was, navigating by his grandfather's stars toward that same impossible country, and if he was afraid he hid it the way he hid everything — under precision.

"Show me," I said.

He looked at me, surprised. Nobody had ever asked Jun to teach them anything; I think people mostly asked him to prove things, which is a different and meaner request. But his face opened, and for the next hour he walked me through the sky — the Anchor, the Two Brothers, the Crooked Canoe that the old Malayan pilots steered by, each constellation with a name in a language that existed now only in old men's mouths and one disgraced cartographer's memory.

"And that?" I pointed to a smudge of light that wasn't quite a star.

"That's not a star at all. That's another galaxy. A hundred billion suns, so far away the light takes longer than history to reach us." He smiled crookedly. "My grandfather said: remember that, girl-boy, whenever the world tells you it's small."

I'm not a girl and I'm not a boy and I'd never told him which, and he'd never asked, and that night I decided Jun could stay on whatever crew I was ever part of, forever, for that alone.

Below us, Oscar sat with Bao's head in her lap, sponging his forehead with cool water, looking up at the sky in short glances like she didn't trust it. When we came down, she was humming. Barely. Three notes, over and over, tuneless as a cat in a sack.

I didn't say anything. Some things you keep quiet the way you keep a coal alive — cupped, unlooked-at, warm.

* * *

The fifth day, Bao couldn't walk.

We tried. We rigged a litter from the tarp and two poles and took turns, but the trail steepened into switchbacks cut into the cliffside, slick with runoff, and twice the litter nearly went over the edge with him in it. The second time, Oscar called a halt with a word I won't repeat, and we stood in the dripping green silence while the decision made itself among us without anyone saying it out loud.

"We're close," Jun said finally, consulting the compass-map and the journal both, holding them side by side under the oilcloth. "Two days, maybe three, if the trail does what the map says it does. There should be a saddle ahead, then the valley of the falls."

"Should be," Oscar said.

"Maps are promises," Jun said. "Not guarantees. But they're the best promises we have."

So we split the loads differently. Oscar took Bao across her back in a carry she showed us — an old diver's trick, she said, for hauling gear up ladders, weight high and close — and I took the double pack, and Jun took the rest, and we went up.

I want to tell you about that climb honestly, because everyone makes mountains sound noble, and this one was not noble. It was misery. My shoulders were raw where the straps sawed. My feet were two soft white wounds inside my boots; I'd stopped looking at them because looking made them real. The leeches found me anyway, every hour, and Oscar stopped every hour to check me too, without being asked, without comment, her fingers quick and impersonal on my ankles and waistband, and something about that — being checked for, counted, included in someone's tally of things to keep alive — undid me a little more each time.

At the worst of it, mid-afternoon on the sixth day, on a stretch of trail barely wider than my foot with the drop falling away into white nothing below, I froze.

Just froze. Legs done. Head spinning. The cloud pressing in like hands on my shoulders, whispering *sit down, sit down, the mountain wins, everyone stops eventually.*

Oscar came back for me. Of course she did. She had Bao on her back, and she still came back, and she didn't give me a speech. She just stood there in the rain with the old man breathing against her neck and said:

"Pip. Look at me. Not down. Me."

I looked at her.

"One step. That's all anybody gets. One step, then another one. Miles are a lie — nobody walks miles. People walk steps."

And I took one. And then, because I am who I am, I said, "You've been measuring in miles your whole life, haven't you? Twelve years of river miles. That's how you kept from counting anything else."

Her jaw tightened. The rain ran off her chin.

"Yeah," she said. "Maybe I'm learning to count different."

Then she turned and carried Bao up the cliff, one step at a time, and I followed her, and I didn't freeze again.

* * *

We made the saddle at dusk on the seventh day, and Bao woke clear-eyed for the first time in three days, which is the cruelest timing the world has ever managed, because waking clear-eyed meant he understood where he was, and how much we'd carried him, and what it had cost.

"You should have left me," he said, propped against a boulder while Jun fed him broth.

"Don't be stupid," Oscar said.

"It would have been sensible."

"We're not sensible people," I said. "Look at us. A fired mapmaker, a pickpocket, a woman who talks to rivers, and a ferryman with a fever, climbing a mountain that hates us, to find a country that doesn't exist. Sensible died back at the delta."

Bao laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing scared all of us, but when it passed he looked around our circle with eyes gone bright and wet and said, "The river decides its passengers. I told you that, girl. I just never told you why." He closed his eyes. "Because sometimes the river puts people in your boat, and you spend the whole trip wondering why, and then one day you look up and realize you'd have sunk without them."

Nobody spoke after that. The fire crackled. Somewhere below us, far down through the cloud, water was falling — we could hear it now, a vast low breath, the sound of the falls Jun's map promised, getting louder with every hour.

* * *

On the eighth morning the cloud lifted like a curtain and showed us the valley.

It opened below the saddle all at once, sudden as a held breath released: a deep green gorge walled in gray karst, mist rising off it in slow columns, and at its heart a waterfall taller than anything I had ever seen — a white rope of it plunging out of a hanging valley, so high the top vanished into cloud, so loud the ground hummed through my boots. And along the gorge walls, half-hidden in the vegetation, shapes. Regular shapes. Terraces. Stone walls. The corner of a roof.

"Somebody lives down there," I whispered.

"Somebody always lived down there," Jun said. His voice was shaking. He had the journal open in his hands, and he was looking from the page to the falls and back, and tears were running down his face without his permission, and he didn't wipe them. "This is it. This is the bend past the edge of every map. We're standing where the world ends."

Oscar stood at the lip of the saddle with the wind pulling at her, and I watched her take out the brass compass — her mother's compass, the one with the map etched beneath the faceplate — and hold it up, and turn slowly, matching the micro-etched lines to the living valley below. Her lips moved. Reading. Checking. Then she lowered it, and I saw her face do the thing it had been refusing to do for eight days and twelve years, the thing the mountain had been prying open joint by joint.

She looked afraid. Not of the falls. Of what finding this place would mean — that her mother had really been here, really been right, really been alone.

"Down," she said simply. "Before the cloud closes again."

We started down into the gorge, single file, Jun first with the journal held open like a talisman, me behind him, Oscar carrying Bao, the waterfall's breath rising up to meet us, cool and enormous, like the whole hidden country inhaling.

And that's when I saw them.

Figures on the terraces below. Still as stones. Watching us come — five small travelers descending out of the cloud into a valley that had erased itself from the world on purpose. One of them raised something long and wooden, and the sunlight slid along it.

"Oscar," I said. "Company."
