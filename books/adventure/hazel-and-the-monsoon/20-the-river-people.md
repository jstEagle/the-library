## 20. The River People

They came out of the mist the way weather comes—suddenly, and from all directions at once.

Oscar heard them before she saw them: a sound like wind moving through bamboo, except there was no bamboo on this stretch of bank, only black water and the towering buttress roots of trees that had never been logged because no map had ever led anyone here to cut them. The sound resolved into voices, low and layered, speaking a language that was almost—but not quite—the trade tongue she'd picked up in a dozen riverports. It had older bones underneath it. She could hear them, the way you can hear bedrock under topsoil.

"Stay in the boat," Bao said quietly. He had not moved from the tiller since they'd entered the narrows below the falls. His fever had broken sometime in the night, but it had left him hollowed out, his hands unsteady, his eyes too bright. "Whatever happens. Stay in the boat."

Pip's hand found Oscar's sleeve. Jun sat very still at the bow, his chart case clutched to his chest like a child holds a blanket, watching the treeline with an expression Oscar had never seen on him before—not fear exactly, but the look of a man standing in front of something he'd argued for his whole life and half-believed he'd invented.

The mist thinned. And the riverbank filled with people.

There were perhaps thirty of them, maybe more hidden in the green dark behind the first rank. They did not look like soldiers, though several carried long spears tipped with iron gone silver-bright from generations of sharpening. They did not look like villagers either, not with the way they held themselves, spaced apart in a loose crescent that would have made any tactician in the lowlands weep with envy. They wore clothing dyed the color of river mud and old leaves, and their faces were painted with lines of white ash that caught what little light filtered through the canopy.

They were every age. That was what struck Oscar first, before anything else—a grandmother with a spear, a boy of six holding his father's hand, a woman heavy with child standing calmly at the edge of the water as if she'd walked down to watch the boats come in, which perhaps she had.

And every one of them was looking at Bao.

An old man stepped forward from the crescent. He was small and straight-backed, with white hair cut close to his skull and a scar that split one eyebrow cleanly in two. He spoke in the trade tongue, and his accent was strange and beautiful, vowels rounded like stones worn smooth.

"You come up the Sorrow Channel," he said. "No one comes up the Sorrow Channel who does not know it is called that."

"We know," Bao said. He rose slowly, one hand braced on the gunwale, and Oscar saw him gather himself the way an old man gathers himself before a long bow. "I am Bao. I ferried the lower river forty years. I carried your people's goods when your people still traded salt for rice at the third bend, before the patrols, before the maps closed." He paused. "I carried a woman here once. Long ago. A scholar of drowned things."

The silence that followed had weight. Water dripped from the oars. Somewhere above them, behind the falls, a bird cried three notes and stopped.

The old man's face did not change. "Many women have come up this river," he said. "Most were sent back. Some stayed." His eyes moved past Bao, past Jun, past Pip, and settled on Oscar with an attention so total it felt physical, like a hand laid flat against her sternum. "You have her face."

Oscar's mouth went dry. "You knew my mother."

"I knew a woman," the old man said. "Whether she was your mother is a question I will ask her myself, when I see her." Something shifted in his voice then, some grain of grief or caution, and Oscar understood before he said it: *she is not here.* "Get out of the boat. Slowly. Leave your weapons in it."

"We don't have weapons," Pip said.

The old man looked at her—at the pickpocket's quick hands, the river-rat grin that Pip couldn't quite suppress even now—and something almost like amusement touched the corners of his mouth. "Everyone has weapons, child. Some are just better hidden than others." He nodded at Pip's empty sleeves. "Yours are very well hidden indeed."

* * *

They took everything, of course. The compass last, and most carefully—an elder woman with knuckles swollen like tree roots lifted it from Oscar's pack, turned it over twice, and passed it to the old man without a word, and Oscar watched it go with her heart hammering against her ribs and her hands flat and open at her sides because that was the deal, that was the price of being allowed to walk instead of being put back in the boat and turned around.

Jun surrendered his charts with the reverence of a priest giving up relics. When the old man unrolled the topmost sheet—the one Jun had been redrawing from memory through the whole journey upstream, Malaya's coastline rendered in ink mixed from lamp-black and river clay—the old man went very still.

"Where did you get this?" he asked.

"I didn't get it anywhere," Jun said. "I made it. From fragments. Trade records, treaty margins, songs my grandfather sang me, a shipping ledger from 1841 with place-names in the columns that no atlas would confirm." His voice cracked slightly, and he steadied it. "I lost my license for insisting it was real. They called it fabrication. They called me a fantasist. I spent eleven years drawing a country everyone told me didn't exist, and I drew it anyway, because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because someone had to remember it existed."

The old man studied him for a long moment. Then he rolled the chart up with both hands, gently, the way you handle something living, and handed it back.

"You drew the eastern coast wrong," he said. "The bay is deeper. But you drew it." He turned away. "Come. Eat first. Questions after. This is the law of the house: no guest answers questions on an empty stomach, because hunger makes liars of us all."

* * *

The village was not where Oscar expected it to be.

She had imagined—she admitted it to herself later, turning it over in the dark like a stone with a cold underside—something dramatic. A hidden valley. Carved gates. The kind of place that announced its own secrecy, that performed concealment the way the bureau's buildings performed power.

Instead the Kaeli lived in houses built on stilts over the flooded shallows where the river spread wide and slow below the falls, houses so woven into the living trees that from twenty meters away they simply dissolved into the forest. Walkways of split bamboo connected them, sagging and soft underfoot, some of them underwater at this season so that people moved between homes by wading, lifting their lamps high. Smoke rose from cooking fires and vanished into the canopy without ever breaking the surface of the leaves. The whole village breathed like one organism, in and out, and if you didn't know it was here—if no one had ever told you—you could stand on the far bank your entire life and never see it.

"They've been here four hundred years," Jun murmured as they crossed a walkway toward the largest house, at the center. "Maybe longer. Right under the survey expeditions. Under the colonial steamers. Under the satellite passes, even—" He shook his head, wonder and something close to anguish fighting in his face. "All those years they told me I was mad. And the whole time there was a nation breathing quietly upriver, hiding in plain water."

"Don't romanticize it yet," Oscar said quietly. "They haven't decided about us."

But the food decided something. It always did. They were fed in the great house on fish baked in banana leaf and a sour herb broth that tasted of the forest floor after rain, and rice from terraces Oscar realized must be carved into hillsides she had looked at that morning and seen only wilderness. Children gathered at the edges of the room to stare at Pip, and Pip stared back with equal frankness until one girl, bold as brass, held up a woven bracelet and mimed trading it, and within ten minutes Pip had somehow acquired three bracelets, given away a hair ribbon, and taught six children a card game involving cheating, which she insisted was the entire point of the game.

Bao ate little. He sat near the fire with his bad shoulder propped on a cushion, and the elders came to him one by one, touching his arm, saying his name in their own language—it sounded different in their mouths, older, a name from before the ferry, before the river had worn him down to what he was—and Oscar watched him receive it like a man being handed back pieces of himself he'd forgotten he'd lost.

It was full dark when the old man came to her.

He found her at the walkway's end, where she'd slipped out to be alone with the sound of the falls. She heard his footsteps on the bamboo—lighter than a man his age should move—and didn't turn.

"My name is Danai," he said. "I was the one who met your mother, thirty-one years ago. I was younger than you are now."

Oscar turned. In the lamplight spilling from the great house, his face was unreadable and ancient and kind, all three at once.

"She came up the Sorrow Channel with a broken engine and a stolen survey permit," Danai said. "Alone. Everyone thought she was bureau. We nearly speared her at the bank." Almost a smile. "She talked so fast, and so rudely, that we decided no bureau agent could possibly be that disrespectful, and let her live."

"That sounds like her," Oscar said, and her voice came out strange, thick. She hadn't spoken of her mother aloud—to anyone—in twelve years, and now the words came easily, which felt like betrayal and relief in the same breath.

"She stayed a season. Then another. She mapped our waterways—for us, not for them; she burned her field copies in front of the council when they asked her to prove her loyalty, and I have never forgotten the look on her face while she did it. Like burning her own hands." Danai looked out at the black water. "She found the archive, Oscar Nakamura. Of course she did. She was the finest scholar of drowned things I ever met, and we are not easy to find. She stood inside it. She read the treaties. She learned enough of our tongue to sing one of our songs badly, which the children still imitate."

The falls roared. Oscar waited.

"And she left," Danai said. "She went back downriver, into the world that erased us, and she told no one. Not her bureau. Not her colleagues. Not—" he glanced at her, careful, precise, "—not her daughter. For twenty years she kept our secret, knowing it would cost her everything she'd built her life on. Knowing that the discovery of a lifetime was sitting in her chest like a coal, and that publishing it would save her name and kill us." He turned to face her fully. "When the bureau finally came for her—when they took her work and broke her name and drove her upriver one last time—she came here. Dying, some said. Hiding, others said. She asked us for nothing except that we stay hidden. She refused our offer of sanctuary. She said a stranger's grave would draw searchers like blood draws sharks." His voice roughened. "So she went back down to die in the world, and she took our secret with her into the ground, and the world called her a fraud, and we lived."

Oscar could not speak. Twelve years she had carried her mother's disgrace like a stone sewn into her chest—had built her whole drifting, rootless life around the shape of it, the shame of it, the certainty that her mother had died chasing glory and gotten nothing. And all that time—

"All that time," Danai said softly, reading her, "you thought she was destroyed by her ambition."

"Yes."

"She was destroyed by her mercy." He said it without drama, the way you state a depth sounding. "There is no monument for that kind of courage. No vindication. She chose us over herself, and she let you believe the worst, because believing the worst kept you safe from the bureau's attention. A disgraced woman's daughter is nobody. A martyr's daughter is a lead line in the water." He paused. "Or so she reasoned. Whether she was right to make that choice for you—" he shrugged, old and honest, "—that is not mine to judge. Mothers make choices. Children live inside them. That is the oldest flood there is."

Oscar gripped the rail of the walkway. The bamboo bit into her palms. She thought of the compass, the micro-etched map, the journal ending mid-sentence, the unsent letter she still hadn't read, folded in the back cover like a wound she wasn't ready to open. She thought of every riverport she'd left before the season turned, every crew she'd abandoned before they could abandon her, every mile she'd measured so she'd never have to measure time.

Her mother had chosen silence for twenty years. Had chosen disgrace. Had chosen, God help her, to let her own daughter hate her rather than paint a target on her back.

"I need to be alone," Oscar said.

Danai inclined his head. "The walkway ends at the meditation platform, over the deep pool. Our dead are fed to that pool. If you cry there, you will not be crying alone." He moved past her, then stopped. "Your mother sang badly," he said. "But she sang. Tomorrow, the council will decide whether you may see what she saw. Sleep if you can."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat on the meditation platform above the deep pool until the lamps in the village went out one by one, and the falls became the only sound in the world, and the stars came out in the gaps in the canopy—stars Jun could have named, stars his grandfather had taught him, and she made a mental note, absurd and tender, to ask him.

Pip found her somewhere past midnight. The girl padded down the walkway barefoot, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, three new bracelets on one wrist, and sat down beside Oscar without asking permission, which was, Oscar was learning, simply how Pip entered rooms.

"The old lady gave me soup," Pip reported. "Even though I ate already. That's how you know you're staying the night." A pause. "Are we staying?"

"I don't know."

"The kids here play a game like my game but with shells instead of cards. They cheat worse than me." Another pause, longer. "You were crying."

"No, I wasn't."

"Okay." Pip pulled the blanket around them both, decisively, an act of territorial annexation. "For the record, I wasn't scared today. But if I had been, it would've helped that you didn't leave."

Oscar looked down at the dark pool where the Kaeli fed their dead, where her mother's secret had lived for twenty years, where tomorrow the council would decide whether to show her the archive that could vindicate her mother or erase her all over again. The river ran beneath her, patient and enormous, remembering everything.

"Pip," she said. "Go to sleep."

But she didn't move, and neither did the girl, and the falls went on roaring their one long word into the dark.

In the morning, Danai came for them at first light, and behind him walked six elders, and in the eldest's hands, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, was something that made Jun rise to his feet with a sound like a man struck.

"It's time," Danai said, "you saw what your mother chose to protect."
