## 22. The Elder's Question

The Kaeli did not keep time the way the lowlands kept it. There were no bells in the village, no sirens, no diesel engines marking the hours. Instead there was the light moving across the terraced gardens, the shift of birdsong from one canopy to another, and the slow turning of the mist that came off the falls each morning like breath off a sleeping animal.

Oscar had been there four days before she understood that she was not waiting for anything. She had spent twelve years waiting—for the next job, the next boat, the next season to end—and now, for the first time since her mother died, no one wanted her anywhere else.

She was mending a net with two of the Kaeli women when Suri found her.

The elder moved through the village the way water moves through a channel—without hurry, without resistance, arriving everywhere she intended to be. She was small, silver-haired, her hands knotted like tree roots, and she had a way of looking at Oscar that made Oscar feel as though she were being read rather than seen. Not judged. Read. The way you'd read a river gauge, checking what was coming downstream.

"The women say your knots are good," Suri said, settling onto the flat stone beside the drying racks. "For a stranger."

"My mother taught me." Oscar said it before she could stop herself, and then heard it—the strangeness of saying *my mother* without bitterness attached, like a word in a language she'd forgotten she spoke.

Suri nodded slowly. "Come walk with me."

They climbed the path behind the village, up through the terraces where taro grew in flooded paddies so green they seemed lit from within, past the carved stones Jun had been photographing for three days straight, muttering translations under his breath like prayers. Above the last terrace the forest closed in, dark and dripping, and the sound of the falls filled everything—not loud anymore, just constant, the way a heartbeat is constant. You stopped hearing it until you thought about it, and then you couldn't stop.

Suri walked in silence. Oscar let her. She had learned that much in four days: silence here was not an absence. It was a fullness. It was how these people held things too large for words.

At last they reached a shelf of rock overlooking the headwaters pool—the source itself, or close enough to it. The river began here, spilling from the cave mouth behind the waterfall in a single clear ribbon, cold and impossibly blue-green, gathering itself for the long journey down through every country that had ever tried to claim it. From up here Oscar could see the whole valley: the village smoke, the terraces, Bao sitting on the dock of the boathouse testing his healing shoulder against a rope, Pip somewhere below shrieking with laughter at something Jun had drawn.

Her crew. Her strange, accidental family, scattered through a hidden nation that wasn't supposed to exist.

"You have been asking questions," Suri said. "To everyone. About the archive. About what will happen to it. About who decides."

"I didn't mean any offense."

"Offense is a lowland thing." Suri smiled faintly. "I am not offended. I am curious. So I will ask you a question, and you will answer it honestly, because you are your mother's daughter and she always answered honestly, even when honesty cost her everything."

Oscar's throat tightened. "What question?"

Suri turned and looked at her fully, and her eyes were old and brown and patient as the stone beneath them.

"What do you actually want, child? Do you want your mother's name cleared? Or do you want to understand your mother?"

The question went through Oscar like a diver's knife through black water—clean, deep, finding things she hadn't known were there.

She opened her mouth to give the easy answer. *Both.* Or the noble answer. *It doesn't matter what I want; it matters what's right for your people.* She had a whole inventory of answers, sorted and ready, the way she kept escape routes sorted and ready in every port. But Suri sat waiting on the warm stone with the patience of someone who had watched rivers carve canyons, and every prepared answer dissolved in her mouth like silt in current.

"I don't know," Oscar said finally.

"Good," said Suri. "That is the first honest thing you've said since you arrived."

* * *

They stayed on the rock shelf while the afternoon light went long and gold across the valley. Suri told her, in pieces, about the woman who had come upriver twenty years ago—or rather, Suri asked questions and let Oscar discover the answers herself, which was somehow worse and better at once.

"She arrived in the dry season, with a fevered guide and half a map," Suri said. "Bao carried her the last three days. He has never told you this?"

"He tells stories about everything except her." Oscar picked at the fraying edge of her sleeve. "He'll talk all night about the monsoon of '89, about crocodiles, about his wife's cooking. Ask him about my mother and he suddenly needs to check an engine."

"Grief makes some men silent and some men talkative. He loved her the way he loves the river—with his back turned, doing the work." Suri folded her hands. "Your mother came to us with the same hunger you carry. She wanted the archive. She wanted proof. She had been disgraced—you know this?"

"I know."

"They said she fabricated her findings. That Malaya was a myth she invented to save a failing career." Suri's voice was quiet but each word landed like a stone dropped in still water. "She stood where you are standing and she asked us to let her take the archive out. Photographs, copies, anything. She said the world needed to know. She said her name needed clearing."

Oscar's heart stumbled. "And you refused."

"We refused. And do you know what she did?" Suri looked at her, and there was something almost mischievous in the old woman's face, something that made her briefly young. "She cried. All night, in the guest house, we could hear her. And in the morning she washed her face, and she came to us, and she said—" Suri paused, and when she continued her voice had changed, softened, taken on the cadence of remembered speech. "She said, *'I've been so busy wanting the world to apologize to me that I never asked what you would lose if it listened.'* And she stayed. Three months. She learned our songs. She helped rebuild the flood wall after the storm year. And when she left, she left the archive exactly as she'd found it, and she never published a word, and she let them go on calling her a fraud for the rest of her life."

The wind came up the valley carrying spray-mist from the falls, fine as breath, beading cold on Oscar's skin.

"Why?" Oscar managed. "Why would she do that? She had everything to gain. She had *me* to think of—I grew up watching people spit on her name. I grew up defending a woman who wouldn't defend herself, and I hated her for it. I thought she was weak. I thought she was ashamed."

"She was not weak." Suri's voice carried an edge now, protective, fierce as a mother bird. "Do you know what strength costs in the lowlands, child? Your mother paid it. She chose us over her own vindication. She chose the living memory of ten thousand erased people over her own good name. And she paid for that choice with everything—including, I think, you."

The words hit the wound precisely, the way only truth can.

"She wrote to me," Oscar said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she pushed through anyway. "Near the end. A letter I never answered. I found it in her journal. She asked me to forgive her, or to finish her work, or to burn it—she said whichever would let me live my own life. And I never answered. She died not knowing if I—" Oscar stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Twelve years of not crying, and this old woman on a rock shelf had undone it in an afternoon.

Suri waited. She was very good at waiting.

When Oscar could speak again, she said, "You're asking whether I'm her. Whether I'll cry all night and then leave the archive alone. Is that it?"

"No," said Suri. "That question answers itself, in time. I am asking you something harder." She leaned forward, and the mist caught in her silver hair like frost. "Your mother came here wanting vindication and left having found understanding. Those are different hungers, and they lead to different doors. Vindication says: *the world was wrong about her.* Understanding says: *now I know who she was.* One of them heals you. The other only wins an argument." She touched Oscar's wrist, her fingers dry and strong as bark. "So I ask again, and this time do not say *I don't know.* What do you want?"

Oscar looked out at the valley. At the smoke rising from cookfires where women were making the flat rice cakes Pip had declared better than anything in any port market. At Jun, hunched over his field desk by the boathouse, redrawing coastlines from memory with the furious joy of a man restored to himself. At Bao on the dock, testing his shoulder, glancing up the trail every few minutes in a way that meant he knew exactly where she was and who she was with.

She thought about the journal. Twelve years of entries in her mother's small, precise handwriting—the same handwriting that had labeled Oscar's childhood specimen jars, that had written *Hazel, age six, first dive today, she was not afraid* in the margin of a field notebook Oscar had found only weeks ago. She thought about how she had opened that journal expecting evidence and found instead a person. Patient. Secretive. Kind to strangers in ways she had never been kind to her own daughter—or perhaps kind in ways Oscar had been too angry to recognize. The compass. The micro-etched map. *The only thing I ever gave her.* Her mother had been leaving her a road home for twenty years, and Oscar had called it a bribe.

"I came for vindication," Oscar said at last. The words came slow, dredged up from somewhere deep, each one heavy as river stone. "I came because the bureau destroyed her and I wanted the world to see it. I wanted to stand in front of everyone who laughed at her and open this archive like a chest wound and say *look, look, she was right, she was always right, and you killed her for it.*"

"And now?"

"Now I've read her journal. Now I've met the people she chose over her own name. Now I know she wasn't ashamed and she wasn't weak—she was the bravest person I ever didn't know." Oscar laughed, a wet, broken sound. "I came looking for a verdict and I found a mother. A whole mother. One who sang songs I've never heard and rebuilt flood walls and carried other people's children across rivers, and who loved me the whole time in a language I refused to speak." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, unashamed of it, or trying to be. "I don't want her name cleared anymore. I want—" She faltered. Started again. "I want to be someone who would have deserved the letter she wrote me. I want to understand her so completely that understanding her teaches me who *I* am. Because I've spent twelve years being the archaeologist's kid, the disgraced woman's daughter, the diver with the famous dead mother—and I don't even use my real name. I've been hiding inside 'Oscar' the way she hid inside the disgrace. And I'm so tired."

The confession hung in the air over the source waters, and the river took it the way the river took everything—without judgment, without echo, carrying it down toward the sea.

Suri was silent for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way an elder nods when a thing has been said correctly.

"When your mother left us," she said, "she gave us a letter. We keep it still, sealed, in the archive. She said it was for her daughter, if her daughter ever found her way here—but that we should only give it when the daughter had stopped asking what the world owed her mother, and started asking what her mother had left *her.*" Suri rose, joints cracking like green branches, and offered her hand. "I think you may be ready soon. Not today. But soon."

Oscar took the hand and stood. Her legs felt strange, lighter, as though she'd set down cargo she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"And the archive?" she asked. "The bureau is coming. Seng. Maybe worse. What happens to your people when the maps find you?"

Suri looked out at the falling water, at the cave mouth behind it where ten thousand names slept in the dark, kept by generations who had chosen memory over safety.

"That," she said, "is a question for tomorrow. Tonight there is a feast, because Bao's shoulder works again and your young thief has finally learned to weave. Come. Your family is waiting."

They started down the trail together, elder and diver, and Oscar felt the phrase settle into her bones like a key into a lock she hadn't known her chest possessed. *Your family.*

They were halfway to the terraces when Pip came running up the path, barefoot, breathless, her face drained of color.

"Oscar." The girl grabbed her arm. "There's a boat coming up the side channel. Bureau markings. It's stopping at the lower landing." Pip swallowed. "It's the inspector. And she's not alone—she brought soldiers."
