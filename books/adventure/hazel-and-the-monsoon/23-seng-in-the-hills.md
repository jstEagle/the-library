## 23. Seng in the Hills

The rain had followed Adira Seng for eleven days, and she had begun to think of it as a companion rather than an inconvenience.

It came down on the canopy above her in a thousand small percussion, drumming the broad leaves until the whole forest seemed to be whispering about her. Her boots were ruined. Her uniform—she had stopped wearing it three days ago, trading the bureau's grey for a fisherman's oilskin bought from a delta trader who hadn't asked questions—smelled of mildew and diesel and something green and rotting that she couldn't name. Her hands, when she looked at them in the rare gaps between trees, were not the hands of a woman who sat behind a desk in the capital. They were blistered, bitten, stained with mud to the wrist.

She was forty-one years old and she had never been this far from a paved road in her life.

Behind her, the two bureau field agents she'd been assigned slogged through the undergrowth with the grim patience of men who had been told this was a routine escort and had long since stopped believing it. Ravi carried the satellite uplink in a waterproof case against his chest like a child. Dev carried the orders themselves, sealed, in a tube of brass that Seng had signed herself, because the bureau required that seizure warrants be countersigned by the ranking officer present, and she was the ranking officer present, and so her name was on the thing that would end—or begin—everything.

*By order of the State Antiquities Bureau, pursuant to Statute 14: all materials bearing the seal of the erased nation are contraband. All such materials are to be seized and destroyed by incineration. All persons found in possession or proximity are subject to detention.*

She had written those words in her own office, at her own desk, under a lamp, four months ago, before she ever heard the name Oscar Nakamura. She had written them the way she wrote everything: carefully, correctly, without feeling. That was what the bureau had made her into—a woman who could write destruction in beautiful bureaucratic prose and sleep soundly after.

She was no longer certain she slept soundly.

* * *

She had tracked them badly, then well, then badly again. The trail upstream read like a confession scrawled by someone running out of ink. A fuel dock where a ferryman's boat had taken on diesel and a disgraced cartographer had climbed aboard with a duffel bag and no apology. A floating market where a teenage girl with quick fingers had been seen buying bandages with money that wasn't hers. A checkpoint clerk who remembered Inspector Seng's own face from the briefing photographs and had stammered out a report of a woman matching Nakamura's description, traveling with an old man whose ferry license predated the clerk's birth.

And everywhere, faint as a watermark, the seal. Rubbings of it pinned to a corkboard in a riverport constabulary. A fragment of lead sheeting confiscated from a broker's warehouse—the broker Voss, whose file Seng had read twice and whose name made her jaw tighten in a way she refused to examine. And once, pressed between the pages of a temple donation ledger, a scrap of waterproofed paper bearing a single line of handwriting that the forensic unit had matched, with trembling enthusiasm, to Dr. Elara Nakamura, deceased twelve years, disgraced posthumously, disavowed by this bureau in a memorandum that Seng had read so many times she could recite its worst sentence from memory:

*Consultant Nakamura's obsession with apocryphal nationalisms has compromised the integrity of this office's relationship with our treaty partners. Her continued association with the Bureau is terminated effective immediately. All research materials in her possession are to be surrendered and destroyed.*

Seng had been twenty-nine when that memo was drafted. She had been in the building. She might have walked past the consultant's office on the fourth floor, past the woman with the river-tan and the tired eyes, without ever knowing her name.

Now she knew the daughter's name too. She knew it the way you know a stone in your shoe.

* * *

On the ninth day, the forest changed.

Seng couldn't have said how, at first. The trees grew taller and older; the understory opened as if the land itself had been tended. Then she saw the first carved stone.

It stood beside the trail like a sentry, waist-high, moss-eaten, its surface crowded with characters she did not recognize—angular, flowing, nothing like the script of any living language she'd studied. Below them, cut more recently, in the trade tongue: **TURN BACK.**

Ravi laughed nervously. Dev said nothing, which was worse.

They passed a second stone an hour later. This one said only: **THE RIVER REMEMBERS.**

By the third day of stones, Seng had stopped pretending to herself that she was following a fugitive. She was being permitted. She could feel it the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck—not hostile, exactly, but weighing. Someone knew they were here. Someone had decided, for reasons of their own, to let them keep walking.

That night she sat awake under her tarp while the agents slept, and she took out the thing she had carried for eleven days without opening it: a photograph.

It was not the official photograph, the one from the briefing file—Nakamura's daughter at a customs desk, caught mid-blink, looking like a criminal because the camera had been instructed to find one. It was the other photograph, the one Seng had pulled herself from the deep archive, from the folder marked with her mother's—no. Not her mother. With *Elara Nakamura's* case number. In it, a younger woman stood on the deck of a riverboat with her arm around a ferryman who could only have been Old Bao, both of them laughing at something outside the frame, and the light was the particular gold of late afternoon in the highlands, and the woman looked—

Happy. That was the word. Unprofessional, unguarded, happy.

Seng had stared at that photograph for a long time in the archive's cold light, and what she had felt was not professional curiosity. It was grief. Grief for a woman she had never met, dead twelve years, whose crime—if it had been a crime—was believing in a country that Seng's own employer had spent a century erasing. Grief, and beneath it, a question she had been carrying since the delta like a swallowed hook:

*What am I doing?*

The orders in the brass tube answered the question. That was the problem. The orders answered it clearly, completely, and correctly, and Seng had built her entire life on the principle that correct things were bearable things. You did your duty. You burned what needed burning. You went home and slept.

She was thirty kilometers from the source of a river that officially had no source worth naming, carrying a warrant to burn the memory of a nation out of the world, and she could not remember why correct things were supposed to be bearable.

* * *

They smelled the village before they saw it.

Woodsmoke first, threaded with something sweeter—rice cooking, maybe, or resin. Then the sound: not drums, not voices, but a low woven hum of ordinary life, water being drawn, wood being split, a child somewhere laughing and being shushed. The trail bent along a ledge above a gorge where the river ran white and loud, and then the trees ended all at once, and there it was.

The village sat in a fold of the mountain like a secret kept so long it had become part of the landscape. Houses of dark timber and thatch, built on stilts above terraced gardens, their roofs green with moss. Smoke rising straight in the windless air. Laundry lines. A granary. A shrine of some kind at the center, its posts carved top to bottom in the same angular script as the warning stones—but here the carvings were not warnings. Even from the ledge, even without reading them, Seng could see the difference. These were celebratory. Names, maybe. Genealogies. A people writing themselves onto their own houses because no map would ever write them anywhere else.

No one ran. No one hid. That was the detail that undid her. There were figures in the fields below—perhaps forty people—and they had simply paused, the way a room pauses when a stranger enters, and turned to look up at the three muddy figures on the ledge. A man set down his hoe. A woman drew a child against her hip. An old man near the shrine folded his hands and waited.

They had known. Of course they had known. They had watched the bureau come once before, twelve years ago, and they had watched it leave, and they had buried whatever needed burying, and now here it was again, and they were not even afraid. They were just—ready. Patient as the river.

"Inspector." Dev's voice at her shoulder, low. "Orders?"

The brass tube was cold in her hand. She had polished it that morning, absurdly, out of habit, the way a soldier polishes a boot before a battle he doesn't believe in.

Her orders were simple. Enter the settlement. Identify and seize all materials bearing the seal of Malaya. Supervise their destruction by incineration—the field incinerator was in Ravi's pack, a squat steel drum that had ridden on her back for nine days like a second spine. Detain any persons obstructing the operation. File a full report.

Her orders were simple, and she had executed simpler ones a hundred times, and her feet would not move.

It wasn't fear. She catalogued her own fear professionally, the way she'd been trained—elevated pulse, damp palms, hypervigilance—and this wasn't it. This was something on the other side of fear. This was standing at the threshold of a house and understanding, suddenly and totally, what was inside it: not contraband. Not a cache. A *home*. Forty people's entire inheritance, every song and genealogy and treaty and name of a nation the treaties had drowned, stacked in a cave or a shrine or a memory, and her signature on the document that would turn it to smoke.

Statute 14 said the seal was contraband. Statute 14 had never met these people.

"Inspector?" Dev again.

She thought about Elara Nakamura, standing in some office very much like Seng's own, being told that her life's work was apocryphal, that her nation was a fantasy, that her materials were to be surrendered and destroyed. She thought about how the memo had called it *compromised integrity*, that bloodless phrase, and how the woman in the photograph had laughed on a boat deck with a ferryman, and how she had died alone in these hills—or near enough alone—with her work erased and her daughter estranged and her name struck from the rolls of the profession she'd given her life to.

She thought: *the machinery that broke her is the machinery I am.*

"I need a moment," Seng said.

Dev glanced at Ravi. Ravi looked at the sky. Field agents learn when to pretend not to hear their superior officers, and both of them pretended very hard.

Seng stepped forward alone, to the edge of the ledge, where the last warning stone stood. This one was larger than the others, older, its base sunk deep in the earth. She crouched and brushed the moss from its face with her thumb, and beneath the ancient script she found the trade-tongue gloss, cut shallow, perhaps a century old:

**THE RIVER REMEMBERS WHAT THE WORLD FORGOT. TURN BACK OR BE REMEMBERED WITH IT.**

Below that, newer, cruder, scratched with a knife: a date from twelve years ago, and two initials, *E.N.*, and beside them a single word in the same hand, half-worn by weather but legible if you knelt, if you wanted it legible:

*Chosen.*

Seng knelt there a long time. The rain had stopped without her noticing. Water dripped from the canopy in slow irregular counts, and down in the village, someone began to sing—a work song, low and unhurried, the melody folding back on itself like the river in the gorge—and the child who had laughed earlier laughed again, and was not shushed this time.

Twelve years ago, Elara Nakamura had stood on this exact spot, at this exact stone, at the threshold of the same choice. She had chosen to go in. And the bureau—the bureau that Seng served, that Seng *was*—had spent twelve years making sure that choice looked like treason.

If Seng walked down that ledge with the warrant, she would finish what the memo started. She would burn the archive, detain the divers and the cartographer and the child, and she would go home and write the report in careful, correct, beautiful prose, and she would sleep—wouldn't she?—soundly.

Or.

There was no *or*. That was the trap she had finally seen clearly, here at the top of the world with the brass tube in her hand. There was no procedure for mercy. There was no form for conscience. The bureau had built a machine with only one setting, and it had fed Elara Nakamura into it, and now it wanted the daughter, and the old man, and the liar cartographer, and the thief child, and forty singing strangers, and it wanted Seng to pull the lever and call it duty.

Her radio crackled. Capital command, checking in on schedule, the voice tinny and impatient through the static: *"Seng, confirm position and status. Have you located the target materials? Confirm."*

Down in the village, the song went on.

Seng looked at the brass tube. She looked at the stone, at the worn little word *chosen*, at the smoke rising straight from the homes of people who had done nothing in a hundred years but remember.

*"Seng, confirm. Do you copy?"*

She lifted the radio, and her thumb rested on the transmit key, and she found—to her complete lack of surprise—that she already knew what she was going to say, and that she had known it since the delta, since the photograph, since the first forged line of the trail.

But before she could speak, a figure detached itself from the tree line at the far side of the clearing—an old woman, straight-backed, silver-haired, walking toward the ledge with the unhurried certainty of someone who has been expecting this meeting her whole life. Behind her, at the village gate, other figures gathered. Among them, unmistakable even at this distance, mud-streaked and hollow-eyed and alive: the Nakamura woman.

Elder Suri stopped at the foot of the ledge and looked up at the inspector of a bureau that had tried to erase her people, and she inclined her head—not in deference, Seng realized, but in invitation.

"You are expected," the old woman said. "Come down. All of you. Leave the fire-drum on the trail."

Seng's thumb hovered over the key. Down in the gorge, the river roared on, remembering everything.
