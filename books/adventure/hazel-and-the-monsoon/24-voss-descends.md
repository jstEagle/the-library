## 24. Voss Descends

The compass had lied to him for three days, and Voss had loved every minute of it.

That was the thing about instruments—people trusted them because they were small and cold and had no reason to deceive. But a compass didn't point north. It pointed at whatever its maker wanted it to point at, and the woman who had etched this one's hidden faceplate had wanted it to point at the source of the river, past the last mapped bend, into country that appeared on no chart that Voss had ever bought, stolen, or burned. For three days his pilot had cursed it, swinging the wheel against the needle as they climbed through channels that narrowed from rivers to streams to something more like a rumor of water, and Voss had stood at the bow with his hands clasped behind his back and let the man curse. The needle knew where it was going. He was only following.

He thought about Emilia Nakamura the way other men thought about God: rarely, unwillingly, and always with the sense of being judged.

Twelve years ago she had stood on a ridge not forty miles from where he now idled his engines in the shadow of the first great cliffs, and she had looked at him with those gray eyes that never seemed to blink enough, and she had said, *You'll leave me here, won't you, Marcus.* Not a question. A diagnosis. She had read him the way she read strata—the layers of a man laid bare by patience—and she had known what he would do before he did it, and she had let him do it anyway, because she understood that some people could not be anything other than what they were.

He had told himself since that it hadn't been abandonment. It had been triage. The bureau was coming upriver with warrants and fire; her permits were forged; her funding had evaporated; and there was a girl back in the lowlands—a thin, watchful child who dove into brown water like it was air—who needed someone to think about her future. Voss had thought about her future. He had sold Emilia's remaining equipment to pay his own debts, kept the one item he knew she'd want found someday, and walked away clean. That was the arithmetic he'd lived by ever since: everyone survives if you subtract the right people.

The cliffs rose out of the mist like the ribs of something drowned. Below them the river ran green and cold, braiding itself around stones the size of houses, and above them the cloud forest hung in wet rags, dripping a constant soft rain even though the sky was clear. Somewhere ahead, behind a waterfall that the compass insisted upon and no map confirmed, there was a cave, and in the cave an archive, and in the archive the complete memory of a nation that the world had agreed to forget.

Voss intended to remember it all the way to the bank.

* * *

He made camp that night on a gravel bar below the falls, and his men made noise, because hired men always make noise when they're frightened and trying not to show it. There were nine of them. Three were river rats from the delta ports who'd run cargo of every legality; four were ex-military from two different countries' discarded wars; one was a Kachin tracker who hadn't spoken since they passed the carved warning stones; and the last was a boy of maybe nineteen called Deng, who had signed on in the riverport because Voss paid double what anyone else did and because nineteen-year-olds believe themselves immortal.

They sat around the fire passing a bottle, and Voss listened to them talk themselves brave.

"The old man at the drowned village said there's nothing up there," Deng offered. "Just ghosts."

"Old men say that everywhere," said Raska, the broad Serb with the shaved head and the rifle he cleaned more often than he ate. "Ghosts are cheaper than admitting you're afraid of the dark."

"There were stones," the tracker said suddenly, and the fire went quiet around him. "Carved stones. Old script." He turned his eyes on Voss, and they were flat and black and certain. "It said the river remembers. It said turn back or be remembered with it." He spat into the flames. "My grandmother talked like that. Places that eat names."

"Your grandmother also believed thunder was a quarrel between brothers," Raska said, and the men laughed, too loudly, the way men laugh when the alternative is listening.

Voss let them laugh. He had learned long ago that fear is a currency, and like any currency it holds its value best when you don't debase it. But that night, lying on his cot with the mosquito net sagging around him like a shroud, he took out the brass compass and turned it in his fingers under the light of a shielded lamp, and he did not feel like a rich man holding a treasure. He felt like a thief holding a bone.

The faceplate came off with a quarter-turn, the way it had the first night, revealing the micro-etched route beneath: the river drawn in lines finer than hair, the last mapped bend, the gorge, the falls, and beyond them a single mark like a starburst where water met stone. Emilia Nakamura had drawn this. Had knelt somewhere with her jeweler's loupe and her steady hands and had engraved a map into brass so fine you needed firelight and youth to read it, and she had hidden it inside the only gift she ever gave her daughter, and she had died within sight of whatever waited at the starburst.

And the daughter had carried it for twelve years without knowing. Carried it through wrecks and salvage yards and a hundred nameless riverports, sleeping with it beside her heart, and never once thought to look beneath the face.

Voss closed the compass. In the morning, he told himself, it would just be an object again. In the morning it would be worth more than he'd made in ten years of brokering other people's pasts, and the woman whose hands had made it would still be dead, and the girl would still be exactly what Voss had made her: unmoored, unattached, easy to subtract.

He almost believed it by dawn.

* * *

They went up the final approach on foot, because the channel above the gravel bar was a white braid of rapids that no hull would survive, and Voss climbed through the cloud forest with the compass in his breast pocket and the tracker ranging ahead, and by midmorning they heard the falls before they saw them—a long rolling drumbeat through the trees, felt in the chest before the ears.

Then the trees opened, and there it was.

Water fell in a single white curtain off a lip of black basalt perhaps thirty meters high, and the pool below it smoked with spray, and rainbows moved in the mist like fish. Behind the curtain—if the compass and the journal fragment and twelve years of obsession were to be believed—there was a cave. And above the pool, terraced into the valley walls where the forest thinned, there were fields. Rice, bright green against the dark slopes. And among the fields, buildings: longhouses of timber and thatch, smoke rising from cooking fires, figures moving.

People. Dozens of them. A village that had never been surveyed, never been taxed, never been put on a map, going about its morning as if the world outside had ended and this was all that remained—which, Voss supposed, in every way that mattered, it was.

"Well," Raska said softly, at his shoulder. "There's your ghost nation."

Voss raised his binoculars. He found the details the way he always did, methodically, greedily: drying racks hung with river fish; a shrine of stacked stones at the head of the terraces, draped in faded cloth; children running between the houses, chasing a dog; and near the water's edge, a boat—an old ferryboat, patched and repatched, its hull scarred with decades of work. Beside it stood four figures, small at this distance but unmistakable in their arrangement. One tall and lean, arms crossed, looking downstream toward him. One slight and quick-footed, perched on the gunwale like a bird that hadn't decided whether to fly. One round-shouldered and old, leaning on a pole. One gesturing at a map spread across the deck.

Nakamura's daughter. The stowaway brat. The ferryman. And the disgraced cartographer whose license Voss himself had quietly arranged to revoke eight years ago, because Jun Osei had asked one question too many about Malayan trade routes at an auction where Voss was selling.

So they had all come after all. The whole ragged little family, assembled at the end of the world, guarding a door they couldn't open and a prize they couldn't keep.

Voss lowered the binoculars and smiled, and the smile felt strange on his face, because underneath it was something that wasn't pleasure at all.

"Deng," he said. "How much explosive did we bring?"

The boy grinned. "Enough to open anything."

"That's not what I asked."

Deng's grin faltered. "Enough to... close anything, too, sir."

"Yes," Voss said. "That's what I thought."

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he did best, which was arithmetic.

Nine rifles against however many spears the village could raise—call it sixty fighting-age adults, poorly armed, but defending their own homes on ground they knew, which historically counted for a great deal. Against that: surprise, firepower, and the simple fact that these people had survived a century precisely by not being found, which meant they had never once fought a battle, and untested courage is a fragile thing. Voss had seen villages burn. He had ordered villages burned, twice, in another life, for employers whose names never reached paper. He knew how fast a community's will collapses when the first house goes up.

But the cave was the problem. The archive was inside the waterfall, and the waterfall was fed by the whole weight of the upper river, and if he simply stormed the village the elders would have hours—maybe less—to move or hide or seal whatever was in there. Stone doors, flooded galleries, a collapse triggered from within; he had no way of knowing what defenses a century of hiding builds into a place. So the explosives weren't primarily for the villagers at all. They were insurance. If the archive could not be taken whole, it would be taken in pieces, and if it could not be taken in pieces, then no one would ever have it, and Voss would go home poorer than he came but richer than the world, which would have lost a nation's memory forever.

There was a version of this, he told himself, where nobody died. He offered it to himself the way he offered everything to everyone: sincerely, and with the price hidden. He would come down to the water at dawn under a white cloth. He would offer terms—payment, passage, protection—for the archive's contents. He would let them believe they were negotiating. And while they negotiated, Deng and the tracker would be placing charges along the cliff path and the terrace approaches, so that when the talking failed, as it always failed, the exits would already belong to him.

He told himself it was business. He was very good at telling himself things. He had told himself for twelve years that Emilia Nakamura had chosen her own hill to die on, and that a man cannot carry another man's—or another woman's—arithmetic.

But that evening, sitting alone above the pool with the falls drumming their endless note, he watched the village light its lamps one by one along the terraces, and he saw the figures come down to the ferryboat, and he saw the tall one—the daughter—stand at the rail for a long time, facing downstream, facing him, though she could not possibly have seen him in the dark.

She was standing watch. She knew he was coming. Of course she knew; she had grown up reading rivers and the men on them, and she had known him since she was nine years old and he had crouched down at some dig site and given her a trowel and told her she dug like her mother. He wondered if she remembered that. He wondered, with a discomfort he refused to name, whether she had ever believed in him the way children believe in the adults who hand them tools.

He thought about Emilia again, and this time the thought arrived whole and unarmored: *she would have hated this.* Not the theft—she'd have expected the theft. She would have hated the explosives. The idea of solving a question of memory with demolition. She used to say that a site destroyed was a book burned twice, once by fire and once by forgetting, and that the second burning was the one that never went out.

Voss turned the compass over in his hands one last time. Then he pocketed it, checked his pistol, and went down to brief his men.

* * *

Below, in the village, they were not sleeping either.

He could see it from the ridge where he made his final circuit: fires kindled at the shrine of stacked stones, elders moving among the longhouses, and spears being made—not forged, there was no time for forging, but *finished*: shafts straightened over coals, blades whetted on river stones until they caught the firelight, young men and women testing their grips with the awkward seriousness of people whose grandparents' weapons had been ceremony for three generations and were suddenly, terribly, tools again. The sound of the whetstones carried across the water, a long rhythmic whisper, like the river itself sharpening.

They were going to meet rifles with spears. They knew what he had and what they had, and they were whetting their blades anyway, because the alternative was to become a story someone else told, and they had spent a hundred years refusing to be anyone's story.

Voss stood in the dark above the falls and felt the dread arrive at last—not fear of losing, he had been poor before and could be poor again, but the older, colder dread of a man who has finally climbed high enough to see the whole shape of the thing he set in motion, and understands that from here forward it belongs to the storm and not to him.

Tomorrow at first light he would walk down under a white cloth with an offer in his mouth and demolition in his pockets, and a girl with her mother's gray eyes would look at him across the water, and she would know—she would *know*, the way her mother had known on the ridge—exactly what he was carrying besides the flag of truce.

The falls went on drumming. The whetstones whispered. Somewhere in the longhouses, children were being held close by people who remembered what erasure meant, and in the cave behind the water, a nation slept in the dark, waiting to learn by morning whether it would be remembered or forgotten, saved or burned, and by whom.

Voss turned back down the slope toward his men, and behind him the mist swallowed the village lamp by lamp, and the river ran on in the dark, keeping, as it had always kept, its own count of debts—

—and the first grey light of dawn found him standing at the water's edge, the white cloth folded over his arm, watching a figure step out onto the ferryboat's deck to meet him.
