## 25. The Dry Season Turns

The rain stopped in the night, and Oscar woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

She had lived twelve years on rivers. She knew their voices the way other people knew their mothers'—the low mutter of a channel at flood, the gossip of water over gravel bars, the deep throat-sound of current shouldering through a gorge. And she knew that silence. It was the sound a river made when it began to think about leaving.

She lay on her sleeping mat in the Kaeli longhouse and listened to the absence. Somewhere below the village, behind the falls, the river that had roared for weeks was drawing itself down into its bones. The monsoon had broken early. Not ended—the clouds still stacked black over the western ridgeline, and the air hung wet as breath against glass—but broken, the way a fever breaks, and every boatman from the delta to the headwaters knew what came after.

The dry season. The falling water. The closing door.

Oscar rose without waking Pip, who slept beside her with one hand curled around the strap of her satchel even now, even here, where there was nothing left to steal but stories. She slipped out onto the longhouse gallery into a morning washed clean and mercilessly bright. Mist stood in the valley like a congregation. Below, the river ran brown and fast—but lower. A hand's width lower than yesterday. Maybe two.

At the edge of the terrace, Bao was already sitting on his heels, watching the water with an expression Oscar had learned to read as grief.

"It's early," she said.

"Early by nine days." He didn't look up. "The river is angry about something. Or tired." He spat, ceremonially, toward the drop. "Either way, it goes down fast now. Three days, maybe four, and the side channels close. Five, and the falls—" he made a gesture, flat palm dropping past his knee, "—the falls become a wall."

Oscar felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into a well. Behind them, up the mountain path, in the cave behind the waterfall, lay the archive. Forty crates of a nation. Genealogies going back further than any colonial ledger. Songs transcribed on bark paper. Treaties signed with thumbprints and river-clay seals. The names of the erased, tens of thousands of them, kept alive by people who had chosen to become invisible rather than let them burn.

And Voss was somewhere out there with a compass that pointed to this place, a case of explosives, and men who followed him because he paid in advance.

"We can't stay," she said. It wasn't a question, and Bao didn't treat it as one.

"No," he agreed. "We can't stay, and we can't fight, and we can't run fast enough on foot with old men and children." He finally turned his head, and his eyes were the same flat river-gray they'd been the day she'd found him on his ferry, unimpressed by her and everything else. "So what does the daughter of Lin Nakamura propose?"

The question sat between them. *The daughter of.* For twelve years Oscar had worn her mother's name like a coat that didn't fit—too heavy in the shoulders, smelling faintly of someone else's life. But standing here, above a falling river, with a nation's memory in a cave and a traitor in the hills, she found she didn't flinch from it anymore.

She looked downstream, where the water was already peeling back from the banks, exposing ribs of red clay and the drowned stumps of trees no map remembered planting. She thought about channels. About the way water moved when it moved in a hurry. About her mother's journal, which she had read so many times now the pages were soft as cloth, and the marginal note her mother had made in the last month of her life, in handwriting gone hurried and small:

*The Kaeli do not fear the dry season. They have always had more than one road.*

* * *

They gathered in the longhouse at midmorning: Elder Suri and three of the elders; Jun with his knees drawn up and a pencil already moving behind his ear; Pip perched on a rice barrel pretending not to listen to everything; Bao near the door where he could feel the weather; Oscar standing, because she couldn't make her legs agree to sit.

"The archive cannot stay," Suri said, before Oscar could speak. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "You are thinking it, diver-girl, and you are right. Voss has the compass. The compass has the falls. Sooner or later he comes, and we are forty elders and two hundred souls with spears sharpened for ceremony, and he is rifles and dynamite." She folded her hands. "So. Say your plan."

"I don't have one yet," Oscar said honestly. "I have a question." She turned to Bao. "Your mother's people—your grandmother's people—they fished these headwaters before the reservoirs, before the bureau, before the maps went blank. When the big water drops, what happens to the channels behind the karst? The ones west of the falls."

Bao's eyebrows climbed slowly, like two animals waking. "Who told you about the karst channels?"

"Your own stories. The night we sheltered in the drowned village, you talked until the fire died. You said the old fishermen never used the main channel in the dry months. You said—" she closed her eyes, pulled the memory up whole, "—you said *the river keeps a servant's entrance.*"

A long silence. Jun had stopped breathing. Pip had stopped pretending not to listen.

"There is a channel," Bao said at last, grudging, as if each word cost him a coin. "My grandmother called it the Serpent's Gut. It runs behind the western karst, underground for half a mile, then opens into a gorge that comes out below the falls. In high water it is suicide. In low water it is—" he searched, "—a bad idea that sometimes works."

"That's all I need," Oscar said. "That's all I've ever needed. A bad idea that sometimes works."

"Oscar." Jun's voice was careful, the voice of a man who had once lost his livelihood to precision. "Half a mile underground. In a dropping river whose levels change by the hour. With crates that can't be wet." He shook his head slowly. "I can draw you the route if you describe it, but I can't draw away the rock."

"You don't have to." Oscar crouched, took a stick of charcoal from Jun's kit, and began to draw on the swept boards of the longhouse floor: the falls, the pool, the cliff line, the cave mouth hidden behind the curtain of water. "Here's what I know about moving things through water. I've spent my whole life pulling things *out* of it. Chests, engines, bodies, bells. Water isn't the enemy. Water is just a road that moves." She drew the channel behind the karst, a dark thread. "The archive leaves tonight. Not by the trail—Voss will be watching the trails, and elders don't move fast over mountains. By water. Down the Serpent's Gut, through the gorge, to the first wide bend below the falls. From there—" she looked up at Suri, "—wherever you decide. Deeper into the hills. Across the border. That's yours to choose. I only get it through the door."

"And who gets it through the door?" Suri asked. "You?"

"Me," Oscar said. "And Bao to read the water. And Pip, because her hands are faster than anyone's and the crates will need lashing and unlashing in the dark. And Jun, because someone has to keep the route in his head in case the map washes off my slate." She paused. "And me again, because half a mile underground means diving the last stretch if the roof comes down too low, and that part is mine. Nobody else dives."

"Diving," Bao repeated flatly. "In the Serpent's Gut. In falling water."

"In falling water," Oscar agreed. "Which is exactly why it works. Flood water fills the Gut to the ceiling and eats anything in it. Low water leaves an air corridor along the top—a foot, maybe two, most of the way. We float the crates through on rafts, on our backs, walking the shallow stretches, swimming the deep ones. It's not safe." She looked around the circle of faces. "But staying is death, running is slower death, and fighting is death with extra steps. This is the only door that closes last."

Suri studied the charcoal marks on the floor for a long time. Around her, the other elders murmured in Malayan, a language that sounded like rain deciding where to fall.

"My grandmother," Suri said finally, "was born in a year the river dropped early, and the village moved the archive by the Gut. It is not legend. It is maintenance." She lifted her chin. "It has been done. Therefore it can be done. Therefore we will do it." She looked at Oscar, and something in her face shifted—the wariness thinning into something almost like trust. "You propose this like a woman who has already decided to die doing it."

"I propose it like a woman who's decided not to bury anyone else," Oscar said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out rough. "I'm done with that math."

Pip slid off the rice barrel. "I'm coming."

"You're fourteen—"

"I'm fourteen and I've been running cargo since I was eleven, and you know it." Pip's chin was up, her jaw set in the particular way that meant argument would cost more than surrender. "Also I'm lighter than everyone. Also I don't panic. Also—" and here her voice cracked, just slightly, the teenager showing through the smuggler, "—also I'm not letting you go under a mountain without me. Don't make me say it twice. It's embarrassing."

Oscar looked at her—at this pickpocket she'd tried to abandon at three separate docks, this girl who had carried her partway up a mountain on torn feet—and felt the strange new weight settle into her chest that she was learning to call family. It weighed more than solitude ever had. It also held better.

"Fine," she said. "But you do exactly what Bao says in the dark. Exactly. Even if it sounds stupid. *Especially* if it sounds stupid."

"Deal," said Pip, who had never once done exactly what anyone said.

* * *

The day went by like water through fingers.

Jun spent it at the cave mouth with Suri's oldest archivists, sealing the crates in waxed hide and banana-leaf bundles, marking each with numbers only he and the elders could read. He worked with a feverish calm, and once, passing Oscar a crate, he said quietly, "Twelve years ago they took my license and told me Malaya was a fairy tale I'd invented. Today I'm carrying the fairy tale down a mountain on my back." His hands were shaking. "I want you to know I've never been less afraid."

Bao spent it at the Gut's entrance, a black slot in the western karst where the river slid in whispering, sounding the depths with a knotted line, muttering to his grandmother's ghost. Twice he came back and changed his estimate of the air corridor. Once he came back and simply sat with his eyes shut for ten minutes, and nobody asked him anything.

Suri spent it with her people, and the sounds that came from the village were the sounds Oscar imagined a nation made when it picked itself up and walked: the soft hammering of crate lids, the low singing of women wrapping the song-scrolls in oilcloth, children being taught which bundle was theirs to carry and to guard with their lives. No wailing. No panic. Just work, and underneath the work, a patience so old it had fossilized into faith.

Oscar spent it preparing herself, and checking the others' gear, and finding reasons to touch each of them on the shoulder as she passed, as if confirming they were real.

At dusk she went alone to the edge of the falls.

The curtain of water had thinned. Where it had been a white wall a week ago, roaring loud enough to swallow gunfire, it was now a lace of streams down black rock, and the cave mouth behind it showed plainly—a dark oval like a mouth about to speak. Through the thinning veil she could see the glint of lamplight inside, where the last crates waited.

Below, the plunge pool had shrunk from a lake to a pond, and its edges showed the bones of the gorge: slick terraces of limestone, ledges, the dark slot of the Serpent's Gut exhaling mist on the far wall. The river was undressing itself, and every hour revealed more rock and offered less cover.

Somewhere out there, Voss was reading the same signs. He had a compass etched by her mother's hand and a mind like a trap, and he knew—as she knew—that a falling river was a clock. If he hit the Gut behind them, they'd be caught in the throat of the mountain with nowhere to go. If he hit the village while they were gone—

"He won't wait for the dry season either," she said aloud, to the water. "Neither do we."

Behind her, footsteps on the rock. She didn't turn; she knew the uneven rhythm, the old-man shuffle with the river still in it.

"Bao says the corridor holds," she said. "Two feet of air, most of the way. One dive section, thirty meters, maybe less by morning."

"I know. I was there." Bao lowered himself onto a ledge with a grunt and looked out at the falling water. "I also came to tell you something I should have told you at the drowned village, and didn't, because I am an old coward with good manners." He was quiet a moment. "The year your mother came upstream with me, it was also a dry season come early. She stood where you're standing. She looked at that cave the way you're looking at it. And she told me, 'Bao, if it ever comes to choosing between the archive and the people who keep it, I already know what I'd choose, and I need you to remember that I chose it on purpose.'"

Oscar's throat tightened. "Did she ever tell you why?"

"She said a nation isn't its paper. It's its grandmothers." He shrugged. "I'm a ferryman. I carry what people bring me. But I've thought about it a long time, and I think she was telling you, through me, across all these years: whatever you decide tomorrow, decide it the way she did. On purpose. Not out of fear."

Oscar watched the water fall apart against the rocks and put itself back together below, over and over, patient as forgiveness.

"Then we move them tonight," she said. "All of them. Before the moon sets."

* * *

They went down to the cave by torchlight, a procession of two hundred souls carrying a nation on their backs.

The Kaeli formed a chain from the village to the cliff base, handing the crates hand to hand down terraces slick with spray, and the sight of it—elders and children and spear-bearing young men passing the weight of a century along like a bucket line at a fire—burned itself into Oscar so deeply she knew she would see it when she closed her eyes for the rest of her life. Nobody sang. Nobody spoke above a murmur. The only sounds were feet on wet stone, the settling creak of hide bindings, and the river, lower now, whispering its warnings.

Inside the cave, lamplight gilded the last forty crates. Suri touched each one as it left, lips moving—not farewell, Oscar realized, but introduction. *This is who we are. Be gentle with them.*

At the Gut's mouth, Bao stood with a torch in one hand and his knotted sounding line in the other, transformed. The stubborn old ferryman was gone; in his place was something older, a fisherman's grandson reading water the way priests read scripture.

"Air corridor holds at two feet to the third bend," he announced. "After the third bend, the roof dips—we swim, crates on rafts, sixty meters. Then it rises again and we walk. Total passage, half a mile. Current is kind tonight. Tomorrow it won't be." He looked at each of them in turn: Oscar dripping with dive gear, Pip lashed into a raft harness, Jun pale but steady with the route locked behind his eyes. "Rules. Nobody lets go of their crate. Nobody passes me. And if the water speaks"—he tapped his ear—"you answer by stopping. Understood?"

Four voices said yes. The torches hissed.

Oscar took one last look back up the gorge, at the thinning falls, at the lamplit cave, at Suri standing on the ledge with the wind of the water moving her clothes like a flag.

Then she turned and stepped into the dark throat of the mountain, and the river took her weight like it had been waiting all along.

The Serpent's Gut swallowed them one by one—torchlight shrinking to a coin, then a spark, then nothing—and the mountain closed over the last light of the archive of Malaya.

Behind them, far downstream, so faint it might have been thunder, might have been engines, the night made a sound that none of them heard, and none of them needed to.

Voss had started moving too.
