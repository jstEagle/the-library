## 26. The Night Before

The nets came down from the drying racks at dusk, and the Kaeli children brought them to the fire circle in armfuls, trailing the smell of river weed and sun-baked hemp. Pip had been mending nets since she was seven years old—back when nets were the difference between eating and not eating—and her fingers knew the work the way they knew lockpicks and purse strings: without asking permission from the rest of her.

She sat cross-legged between a girl of about six named Ila and an old woman whose name Pip still couldn't pronounce right, and she worked the bone needle through a tear the size of her hand. The mesh had to be even. A crooked net caught crooked fish, Bao always said, which was one of those things old men said that turned out to be true about everything.

Around her the village made its evening sounds. Water, always water—the falls talking to themselves behind the curtain of mist, the side channel chuckling over its stones, the great patient breathing of the river below. Children shouting at each other across the terraces. Someone pounding rice in a hollowed log, a slow heartbeat rhythm that Pip could feel through the soles of her feet. Smoke from cookfires carrying the smell of fish and something green and bitter that she'd hated for two days and now, embarrassingly, loved.

Tomorrow everything would change. Tomorrow the monsoon would either hold or break, and if it broke—if the elders' signs were right, the ants moving their eggs uphill, the birds flying low and silent—they would run the archive downriver through channels that were already thinning, and after that nobody knew what came next.

Pip pulled the knot tight and tried not to think about what came next.

"You're doing it wrong," Ila said, watching her hands with the frank cruelty of small children.

"I'm doing it *fast*."

"Fast and wrong."

Pip looked. The girl was right. She'd skipped a strand. She sighed, unpicked three knots, and started again, slower. Somewhere across the fire, Jun laughed at something—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she'd only heard from him since they'd come up into these hills where nobody knew he'd been stripped of his license, where he was just a man who knew maps instead of a man who'd been told his life's work was a lie.

The old woman beside her took the needle from Pip's fingers without asking, made one impossibly quick pass, and handed it back. The tear was closed. It looked like it had never happened.

"That's showing off," Pip said.

The old woman smiled with most of her teeth and went back to her own section of net.

* * *

Bao held court near the biggest fire, as he did every night now, his shoulder bound in a sling that one of the Kaeli healers had made from bark cloth and some kind of resin that smelled like Christmas if Christmas smelled like medicine. He was better. Not good—the fever had broken days ago but left him thinner, older, his hands with a fine tremor he pretended not to notice—but better enough to talk, which for Bao was the main thing.

The children had colonized the ground around him in a crescent, and tonight there were adults too, drifting in from the edges of the firelight, because tonight he was telling stories about *her*. About Oscar's mother. About Hazel.

Pip kept her eyes on the netting and listened with both ears.

"—so she comes up out of the water," Bao was saying, "first time I ever see her dive, and she's got this great slab of temple stone she wants me to help lever off the riverbed, and I say to her, *girl, that stone has been down there four hundred years, it will keep another day*, and she looks at me—" he paused, and Pip glanced up to see him holding his good hand out flat, level, like a blade, "—she looks at me like I have suggested we stop halfway up a hill. That look. You know the look."

A ripple of laughter. Pip didn't laugh. She knew the look. She'd been on the wrong end of it more than once, usually when suggesting they stop halfway up a hill.

"She was young then," Bao said, and his voice changed, softened, the way it did when he talked about the years before any of them. "Younger than your Oscar is now. Twenty-three, twenty-four. She had come down from the capital with a professor who talked too much, and she had this braid down her back thick as a boat rope, and she was—" he searched for the word, turning his cup in his fingers, "—*hungry*. Not for food. For the bottom of things. Some people want to know what's on top of the world. Your professor wanted that. But she—" he tapped his own chest, twice, "—she wanted what was underneath. What got buried. What people decided to forget."

"What did she find?" asked one of the smaller boys, a boy who had never known a world where Malaya appeared on maps and probably couldn't have found his way to the lowlands if you paid him.

"That first year? Mud." Bao grinned. "Mostly mud. And once, a frog the size of my head, which she insisted on drawing in her notebook with all its toes counted. Forty-one toes. She wrote it down like it mattered."

"It might have mattered," said Jun quietly, from the edge of the group. "Frogs are how you read a watershed. Different species, different water."

"See?" Bao spread his good hand toward Jun. "This is why I let this man on my boat. Everyone else thought she was strange. This one would have sat with her counting frog toes."

Pip bent over her net. Her throat had gone tight in a way she didn't want anyone noticing. Because she was picturing it—she couldn't help it—young Hazel Nakamura with her boat-rope braid, mud to the elbows, forty-one frog toes carefully recorded, and somewhere downstream and twelve years away, a nine-year-old girl being handed a brass compass by that same woman and being told, in so many words, *this is what matters*, when what the girl wanted was for her mother to matter less and be home more.

It wasn't fair to think that way. Pip knew it wasn't fair. Knowing a thing wasn't fair had never once stopped her from thinking it.

"And later?" Ila demanded, tugging Bao's sleeve. "Later, when she was older?"

Bao's grin faded into something gentler. "Later," he said, "she was careful. Careful and quiet and always looking over her shoulder. By then she knew what she was looking for, and she knew other people wanted it too, and not for good reasons. She stopped telling people what she found. She'd come to my ferry with her bags wrapped in oilcloth and she'd say, *Bao, take me upstream, and don't ask me until we're past the bend.* And I never asked." He looked into the fire. "I should have asked once or twice. Might have been company for her."

"You carried her here," Pip said. It came out before she could stop it. "Didn't you. To this village."

The fire cracked. Bao looked at her for a long moment, and Pip felt every eye in the crescent turn toward her too, and she wished immediately that she'd kept her mouth shut, but Bao just nodded, slow, like setting down something heavy.

"Twice," he said. "Once with her notebooks, once with nothing but herself and a face like the end of the world. And both times, going back downriver, she was different. Lighter. Like she'd put something down that she'd been carrying a long way." He shook his head. "I used to think it was a man. Then I thought it was treasure. Took me thirty years to understand it was neither."

Across the fire, standing half in shadow with a mug of something hot, Oscar had gone very still.

Pip watched her. Watched the way Oscar's thumb moved against the rim of the mug, around and around, the only part of her that moved. Watched her listen to a dead woman described as young, as hungry, as light, and say nothing, and give nothing away, except that one thumb going around and around like it was trying to wear a hole in the world.

Oscar caught her looking. Pip looked back down at her net fast, heart thumping, and made three perfect stitches in a row out of pure guilt.

* * *

Later, when the fires burned lower and the children had been herded off to sleep, Pip found Jun sitting on the flat rock above the terraces with a board across his knees and a stub of charcoal in his hand, and around him, cross-legged in the dark like moths, four of the older kids—including Ila, who had escaped her bed and clearly intended to keep escaping it.

He was drawing a map.

Not tracing—there was nothing to trace, no chart, no reference, nothing but what lived in his head. Pip crouched at the edge of the lamplight and watched him build a country out of memory. The coastline first, long strokes, sure ones. Then the rivers, branching like a lung. Then the names, in a careful hand, block letters: towns and ports and mountain passes, a whole nation that officially had never existed, laid down line by line on paper that one of the Kaeli women had pressed and dried from bark.

"This isn't for us," Pip said quietly. "Is it. It's for them."

Jun didn't look up. "Maps are arguments," he said. "That's the first thing they teach you, before they teach you anything else. Every map is somebody saying *this is real, this counts, this deserves to be on the page.*" His charcoal whispered along a ridge line. "For a hundred years, the argument has been that this place doesn't count. So." He tapped the paper. "Counterargument."

One of the boys leaned in. "Will you teach us the symbols? The old trade-route marks?"

"All night if you want."

They bent over the board together, and Pip stayed where she was, arms around her knees, feeling the cold of the stone coming up through her clothes and not minding it. This was the thing she'd figured out somewhere on the river between the drowned village and the checkpoint, the thing she hadn't said out loud to anybody: that Jun redrawing Malaya for children who'd never seen it was the same act as Bao telling stories about a dead woman, the same act as Oscar reading that journal by lamplight with her jaw set like she was daring it to hurt her. Everybody up here was doing the same thing. Putting pieces back. Stitching tears in mesh that the whole rest of the world had agreed to leave torn.

Her fingers itched for the compass. She'd lifted it twice, early on—just to prove she could, just to make the point that Oscar guarded her mother's gift like a fortress and Pip could walk straight through walls—and she'd put it back both times, and she'd never told a soul, and she never would. But she thought about it now, the weight of it, the worn brass warm from riding against someone's skin for twenty years. Some things you stole to keep. Some things you stole just to check whether anyone would fight to get them back.

She wondered, not for the first time, which kind she was.

* * *

She found Oscar alone at the edge of the terrace above the falls, sitting with her knees drawn up, looking at nothing, which was what Oscar did instead of sleeping on nights that mattered. The mist drifted up past her in slow white ropes. Below, the river ran black and enormous, and somewhere under all that dark water was a cave, and in the cave was a nation's whole memory in cedar boxes, and tomorrow—or the day after, if the rain held—it would all start moving.

Pip sat down beside her. Not close. One arm's length, which was as close as Oscar ever allowed without flinching.

Neither of them said anything for a while. That was the other thing Pip had learned: silence with Oscar wasn't empty. It was just quiet, like deep water.

"The kids like Bao's stories," Pip said finally.

"Mm."

"He does her voice. When he does the professor, the one who talked too much. It's really bad. It's terrible. He sounds like a goose with opinions."

Something moved at the corner of Oscar's mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where smiles started, on the rare occasions they were permitted.

"He told me she carried frogs home in her pockets," Pip said. "Live ones. To draw them before they escaped."

"She did." Oscar's voice was low, roughened by the mist or the hour. "Our kitchen smelled like pond for a year. My father put his foot down about snakes. Only snakes." She was quiet a moment. "I forgot about the frogs. Isn't that—twelve years, and I forgot about the frogs, and some old ferryman I've known five weeks hands them back to me over a fire."

Pip picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. Now. Say it now, while it was dark and the falls were loud and neither of them had to look at each other properly.

"Oscar."

"Hm."

"After. After we get it downriver. After Seng and Voss and all of it." She made herself keep going, made the words come out plain, no cleverness, no escape hatches. "What happens to us?"

The mist drifted. Down in the dark the river kept on keeping on.

"I mean it," Pip said. "Jun gets his license back, probably. There'll be some committee, they love committees. Bao's got his ferry. Even if it's wrecked, he'll have another inside a month, men like him grow boats like toenails. And I'm—" her voice did something she hated, wobbled, and she flattened it out, "—I'm nobody. I know that. I'm a stowaway you tried to dump at three different docks. But I've been thinking about it anyway, because that's the thing about me, I think about things, and I keep coming back to the same question, which is: are you going to disappear?"

"Pip—"

"No, listen. I've watched you do it. It's what you *do*. A season here, a season there, never a bed twice, never a name twice—I bet you've got three more names besides Oscar, don't you, I bet one of them starts with a P just to mess with me—" Pip heard herself speeding up and forced herself slower. "You measure everything in miles. Miles, not years. Bao told me that's how you talk about your whole life. And miles are great, miles are safe, because miles go *away from*. Nobody can leave you if you leave first. I know how that works. I invented it. I'm younger than you and I invented it."

"Pip." Oscar's voice had an edge now. "Don't."

But Pip was fourteen years old and had survived on nerve, and nerve was all she had tonight, so she turned and looked at Oscar full in the face, mist-wet and firelit, and asked the actual question.

"Are you going to leave us? After?"

The silence went on so long that Pip counted the pulses of the falls. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Oscar looked at the river. Her thumb was moving against her knee, around and around, wearing its little invisible hole in the world.

"There's a version of it," she said at last, slowly, like each word cost freight, "where I hand the journal to a publisher in the capital and walk out the back of the building before the ink dries. New port. New name. New season." She exhaled. "It's a good plan. It's worked for twelve years. Nothing has ever gone wrong with it except everything."

"But?"

Oscar didn't answer *but*. She didn't answer anything. She stood up, unhurried, and looked down at Pip for a moment—an expression Pip couldn't read, complicated as a chart of a coast nobody had surveyed—and then she reached out and, briefly, awkwardly, like someone handling an artifact she was afraid of breaking, squeezed Pip's shoulder. Once. Then she walked back toward the fires, and the mist folded in behind her, and she was gone.

Pip sat alone on the cold rock with the falls thundering their one long word, and hugged her knees, and told herself the shoulder squeeze meant yes. Told herself it meant *of course, obviously, you idiot, where would I go.*

But she'd grown up reading faces the way Jun read stars, and she knew what an unanswered question looked like. It looked exactly like that.

Above the village, far off in the black over the ridgeline, the first wind of the change came down through the cloud forest, and the trees began to talk, and somewhere in the elders' house a bell that hadn't sounded in weeks began, softly, to ring.
