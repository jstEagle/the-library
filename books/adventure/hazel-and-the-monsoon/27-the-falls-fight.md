## 27. The Falls Fight

The bell woke her before the wind did.

Oscar came up out of a shallow sleep with the sound still ringing in her ears—a bronze note, low and patient, the kind of sound that didn't ask to be heard so much as assume it—and lay for a moment in the dark of the elders' guest house listening to the river. It had changed its voice overnight. All week it had been dropping, the monsoon slackening by degrees, and she had lain awake more than one night cataloguing the new sounds: gravel bars emerging, channels thinning, the great drowned throat of the gorge beginning to show its bones. But now, beneath the falls' long thunder, there was something else. A hiss. A pressure. The wind coming down off the cloud forest like water finding a way.

She was dressed and moving before she'd finished the thought.

Outside, the village was already stirring—not the ordinary stir of dawn, cooking fires and net-mending and children's voices, but something older and quieter. Doors opening without lamps lit. Figures moving between houses at a half-run, wrapped against the cold that had come down with the wind. Elder Suri stood at the center of the village green with two of the Kaeli men, and when she saw Oscar she didn't call out. She simply turned her face toward the ridgeline, and Oscar followed her gaze, and understood.

The mist above the falls was wrong.

It should have risen—falls made their own weather, pushed spray up into the light—but this mist was being pressed down, flattened, dragged sideways in long gray banners. And through it, faint but unmistakable in the last of the night, came the sound of engines. Not one engine. Several. Idling low, held just out of sight beyond the lip of the gorge, the way a hunter holds his breath.

"They've been on the ridge since midnight," Suri said. Her voice was calm in the way deep water is calm. "A boy watching the high trail counted their fires. Nine."

"Voss doesn't travel with nine," Oscar said. "He travels with three and buys the rest."

"Then he has bought well." Suri looked at her then, and in the pre-dawn gray her eyes were very old and very steady. "You knew this day would come. You have been sleeping with your boots on for four nights."

Oscar looked down. She had, in fact, been sleeping with her boots on. She hadn't let herself examine why.

* * *

They gathered in the elders' house while the sky went from black to bruise—the whole strange crew of them around the low fire, plus a dozen Kaeli men and women with spears whose points had been freshly whetted and whose faces said they understood exactly what rifles meant. Jun had the journal open on his knees, the micro-etched map beside it, both weighted with river stones. Bao sat propped against the wall with his arm still bound from the rapids, his shoulder healing crooked and slow, and he was sharpening a fish-gaff with the unhurried strokes of a man who had done everything worth doing twice.

"The archive can't be moved fast enough," Jun was saying. "Not all of it. Not even a tenth of it. The side channel Bao knows drops too low after the monsoon—we'd ground out halfway to the delta with a boatload of a nation's memory stuck in the mud for anyone to find."

"So we don't move it," Oscar said.

Everyone looked at her.

"We hold the cave." She heard her own voice come out flat and certain and wondered briefly where it had learned that. "Voss wants the archive intact—that's the whole point of him. He's not going to blow the cave open until he's inside and has what he wants in crates. Which means every minute we keep him out is a minute that matters." She turned to Bao. "The rope bridge across the lower pool. How many ways up to the falls?"

"One," Bao said, "for people who want to live." He set down the gaff. "The bridge, or the wet climb behind the falls, and the wet climb will kill anyone who isn't raised on this river."

"Then the bridge is where he comes." Oscar crouched over the map, tracing it in her mind: the falls, the plunge pool, the single span of braided cable and split bamboo that the Kaeli had crossed for longer than any map remembered. "Jun. The channel above the falls. The one the monsoon swelled—how much water is it carrying right now?"

Jun blinked at her. Then she watched him understand, and it moved through his face like light through water. "The cutbank," he said slowly. "Above the second bend. It's gravel and root—alluvial, nothing stable. If it were breached—" He was already pulling a fresh sheet toward himself, sketching fast, his ruined-license hands suddenly sure as they hadn't been in twenty years. "If it were breached, the channel would take the short line straight into the gorge above the falls. Everything the monsoon's still holding in those high flats would come down at once. Not a flood. A *wall*. It would scour the approach trail, take out the staging ground, put six feet of brown water where Voss's men are standing."

"How long to dig it?"

"With enough hands?" Jun's pencil stopped. His voice dropped. "An hour. Maybe less if the river helps us—it's been wanting to do this for weeks. I've been looking at that cutbank since we arrived thinking, *that's wrong, that's temporary, that won't hold another season.*" He laughed, a small disbelieving sound. "I spent my career drawing rivers as they are. Nobody ever asked me to tell one what to do."

"You're about to," Oscar said. "Take whoever you need. Go now, while it's still dark."

"And me?" Pip said.

Oscar looked at her. Fourteen years old, knees scabbed from the high trail, sitting cross-legged with her chin up and her eyes too bright, and Oscar felt the old reflex rise—the reflex that said *send her away, put her somewhere safe, keep her clean of this*—and found, examining it, that it had no floor under it anymore. There was no somewhere safe. There was only here, and everyone in it.

"There are lines running down from the ridge," Oscar said. "Fuse lines. Voss brought explosives for the cave mouth, which means he'll have laid charges in advance, probably last night, probably along the cliff path and maybe under the bridge anchorage. He'll want to cut our retreat before he shows himself." She held Pip's gaze. "Can you find wire in the dark?"

Pip's grin was quick and sharp and entirely without fear. "Oscar. I grew up picking pockets on a boat where the lanterns swung. Dark is where I'm *good*."

"Bao—"

"I know my post." The old man rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took up the gaff. "The bridge. Nobody crosses who shouldn't." He paused at the door, and looked back at her, and what was in his face was not the stubborn dismissal of the first day on his ferry. It was something older and gentler. "Your mother stood where you're standing once," he said. "Different trouble. Same look. Go and be your mother's daughter, girl. Then go and be yours."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin.

Oscar lay along the rim of the gorge above the plunge pool, wet stone soaking through her shirt, and watched the mist begin to tear. Below her the falls dropped eighty feet in a white roar, and the pool at their base turned slow and green and enormous, and the rope bridge swayed across the lower reach where the current gentled—a fragile stitch of cable and bamboo connecting the village side to the cliff path, sagging like a hammock left out in rain. Somewhere below and behind her, invisible in the spray-shadow, Bao had taken his place at the near anchorage with the gaff and a coil of spare cable and the patience of geology.

Across the gorge, on the far rim, the first of Voss's men appeared.

They came down the cliff path in a loose file, nine of them, moving with the unhurried competence of hired professionals. Two carried rifles slung across their backs. One carried a crate. And behind them, unmistakable even at distance, even through rising spray, walked Voss himself—in his immaculate field jacket, his silver hair combed back, stepping carefully over the wet rock as though the mountain were a hotel corridor he had merely been assigned. He stopped at the head of the bridge and stood looking across at the village, and even at eighty yards Oscar could read the gesture: the little theatrical pause, the man arranging himself for an audience.

He cupped his hands and called across the roar. The words shredded in the noise, but she caught the shape of them—*the archive, no one needs to be hurt, history belongs to everyone*—and felt her jaw tighten. He'd used that same voice on her in a riverport market. He'd used it on her mother, once, in these same hills, and then he had walked away and let the bureau come.

The first charge went off at 6:40 by the village bell.

It wasn't aimed at anything living. It blew the cliff path behind the raiders—behind *themselves*, which told Oscar everything about how confident Voss was—and the crack of it rolled up the gorge and came back doubled, and a plume of dust and shattered limestone climbed into the brightening air. Cutting off retreat. Herding them. Making the bridge the only door in or out of the world.

Which was, she thought grimly, exactly what she wanted. A man who seals his own exits has told you where he plans to stand.

The raiders started across the bridge.

It was built for porters and fishermen, five at a time, and it complained under nine men and a crate—the cables groaning, the bamboo deck flexing, the whole span swinging in a slow sickening arc over the green pool. Halfway across, the lead man stopped and looked down, and whatever he saw in the water made him grab the cables with both hands.

That was when Bao stood up.

He came out of the spray-shadow at the near anchorage like something the river had decided to return, gaff in hand, and planted himself at the head of the bridge—a small ancient figure against the vast white noise of the falls—and began, methodically, to cut the near-side lashings.

Not the cables. Never the cables; the cables were steel and would have taken hours. The lashings—the bamboo cross-ties, the bindings that held the deck planks true, the hundred small knots that kept a rope bridge a road instead of a suggestion. He worked with a fisherman's knife and a fisherman's economy, and the bridge, feeling itself undone, began to twist.

Oscar saw the calculation happen in the lead raider's body: forward or back. Forward was an old man with a gaff. Back was a sealed path and a boss who shot failures. They surged forward.

And Bao, eighty years old, one shoulder ruined, did not retreat a single inch. He hooked the gaff into the cable stays and *leaned*, throwing his whole slight weight against the span's swing, timing it—Oscar could see him timing it, reading the bridge's rhythm the way he read currents—and each time the deck swung toward shore he hauled it back out of true, so that the raiders staggered, grabbed, lost their footing on loosened planks. One man slipped to his knees. Another dropped his rifle into the pool, and the green water took it without a ripple of comment.

Bao was laughing. Oscar could see it even at distance, could see his shoulders shaking—he was holding the bridge and laughing, alone against nine men, because he had ferried this river for sixty years and he had never once in his life stepped aside for anyone.

Then the second charge went off, and it was aimed at the anchorage.

* * *

She heard it before she saw it—the deeper thud, the different note—and her stomach dropped as she looked down to the near bank. The blast had gone off under the cliff shelf beside the anchorage, close enough to shake loose a fall of rock, and through the dust she saw Bao go down, saw the gaff clatter away across the stones, saw two raiders who had reached the near end of the bridge start toward him with the easy violence of men finishing a job.

There was no time to climb down. There was no time for anything. So Oscar did the thing her body decided before her mind caught up: she went over the rim.

The wet route behind the falls—the climb that would kill anyone not raised on this river—was a slick seam of limestone running down through the curtain of falling water. She'd free-climbed worse in flooded quarries, but never half-blind, never with the world roaring white around her, never with her heart trying to climb out through her teeth. She went down it fast and ugly, hands and boots finding holds by faith, the falls' spray hitting her like thrown gravel, and came out of the curtain onto the lower rocks with her palms bleeding and thirty seconds of breath burning in her chest.

The two raiders had reached Bao.

One of them bent to haul him up by the collar. The other raised his rifle butt.

Oscar hit the second one from the side at a dead run.

It was not elegant. She had fought salvage crews and dock thieves and once, memorably, a customs officer in a flooded warehouse, and none of it had ever been elegant—fighting on wet rock was wrestling a river, all leverage and desperation. She drove the man into the stone, got a fistful of his jacket, slammed the back of his skull against limestone hard enough to make the whole morning ring. He folded. The other one dropped Bao and turned, swinging the rifle up, and Oscar caught the barrel and shoved it skyward as it fired—the report slamming her ears into pure tone—and then they were grappling, chest to chest, his weight bearing her back toward the pool's edge, his breath hot and afraid against her face.

Behind him, Bao was getting up.

Slowly. Using the gaff as a cane. Blood sheeting down the side of his face from a scalp wound, his bound shoulder hanging wrong, the old man straightened himself inch by inch against the anchorage post—and then, instead of fleeing, he limped forward and drove the gaff's hook into the back of the raider's belt and *pulled*, and the man's balance went, and Oscar helped it go, and together—an old ferryman and a salvage diver, bloodied and gasping—they put him down on the stones and kept him there.

"Bridge," Bao rasped, pointing with the gaff. "Rest of them. Still coming."

She turned. On the span, the remaining raiders had frozen mid-crossing, because the bridge was dying under them. Bao's knife work and the blast's shaking had undone it past argument: planks were peeling away into the pool, the deck twisting in long ripples, and the men clung to the cables like flies on a wire, forty feet above green water, going nowhere.

And above the falls, the river spoke.

It was not a sound at first. It was a change in the light—a brown gleam in the upper channel, a sudden muscular thickening of the current—and then it was a sound, a deep subterranean grinding, and then it was the wall itself.

Jun's cutbank broke the way he'd promised it would: all at once, and generously. The monsoon's held-back water came down the short line into the gorge in a single brown shoulder of flood, and it hit the falls' upper basin and detonated into spray, and the falls themselves seemed to double—white on white, thunder on thunder, the whole gorge filling with a roar that Oscar felt in her sternum, in her fillings, in the soles of her boots. The surge swept the cliff path clean below Voss's blown charge. It scoured the staging ground where the rest of his men had waited, and she saw figures scrambling for high ground, saw the supply raft torn loose and swallowed, saw the flood take every crate and coil and plan that had been laid on the low rock and hand it all to the river.

On the bridge, the cables, lashed at a near end now buried under churning floodwater, whipped like living things. One by one, unable to hold, the raiders let go—dropping into the pool below the falls, where the Kaeli were already waiting with throw-lines and spears and the unsmiling efficiency of people defending home. Not killing. Collecting.

Which left Voss.

* * *

He had gotten high. Of course he had; he always positioned himself where the story would flatter him. When the flood came he'd retreated to a shelf of rock partway up the far wall, above the ruin of his operation, and now he stood there with two remaining men and the crate, and as the roar subsided into ordinary colossal thunder, he did the thing Oscar had known he would do from the moment she'd seen the mist flatten at dawn.

He pulled a pistol and pointed it down at the bridge anchorage—at Bao, still swaying on his feet, and at Oscar climbing toward them.

"THE COMPASS!" His voice cracked against the gorge walls, stripped of all its market-day silk. "You bring me the compass and the journal, and I walk away, and nobody else bleeds today!"

Oscar stood up straight on the wet rock, water streaming off her, and looked at him across the pool. Her mother had stood somewhere near this spot. Her mother had trusted this voice, and been left in these hills for the bureau to collect. Twelve years of drift, twelve years of measuring life in miles, and it had all funneled down to this: a man with a gun on a rock, shouting economics at her over a waterfall.

"You abandoned her here," Oscar called back, and her voice surprised her by carrying, clear and level under the roar. "In these hills. You let them take her. And you think I'm going to hand you her compass?"

"It's a piece of brass!"

"It's the only thing she ever gave me." And saying it aloud, she found it was true in a way she hadn't known until the words were out. "You wouldn't understand. You've never given anyone anything."

Something moved in Voss's face—rage, or the memory of rage, or the ghost of a younger man who might once have been capable of giving things—and he leveled the pistol at her chest, and the two men beside him started down toward the ruined bridge with the crate between them, choosing the impossible crossing because their employer's pride demanded it.

That was when Pip dropped out of the sky.

Not literally—though the effect was close. She had spent the battle doing what she did best, invisible, and what she had done best of all was find the fuse lines Voss's sappers had run along the cliff path to the remaining charges: the ones set beneath the bridge's far anchorage, the ones meant to drop the whole span into the pool once the raiders were across. She had cut them an hour ago, quietly, with her pickpocket's knife, and coiled the wire, and waited. Now she came down the cliff face above Voss's shelf like a cat descending a curtain, snatched the crate's rope handle out of the carrier's grip mid-step, and *ran*—along the shelf, up through the broken scrub, small and quick and laughing out loud, with a nation's ransom in a wooden box bouncing against her hip and two grown men flailing after her through terrain she'd been scrambling over since she could walk.

And Voss, hearing her, turned—

—which was the mistake. Because turning meant taking the pistol off Oscar, and taking the pistol off Oscar meant she was already moving, up the wet seam of limestone on the far side of the pool, hands bleeding, breath tearing, climbing toward his shelf with everything her mother had ever taught her about water and stone and patience. He fired once and missed wide, the bullet smacking rock somewhere behind her, and then she was over the lip of the shelf and there was no more room for guns or speeches or anything but the two of them and eighty feet of falling water at their backs.

He was stronger. She'd known he would be; she'd planned around it, staying low, staying inside his arms where muscle couldn't extend. He got a hand in her collar and slammed her against the rock and the world went white at the edges, and through the white she heard him panting: *"Your mother begged too, at the end—"*

Everything in her went very quiet and very cold.

She did not hit him the way she wanted to. She hit him the way Bao handled a rope—with timing, with the river's own rhythm, letting his weight commit and then giving it nothing. He lunged; she dropped; his own force carried him past her onto the wet stone, and she took his wrist and twisted the pistol free and sent it spinning into the falls, where the water took it the way the water takes everything, without malice and without return.

They ended at the shelf's edge, locked together, half over it—the full thundering height of the falls pouring past an arm's length away, spray freezing, the pool a green mile below. His foot skidded on the slick lip. Hers held. And in the last instant, as his balance went and his fingers clawed at her soaked jacket, she saw his face up close for the first time in the honest light: not a broker, not a mastermind. Just an old man, terrified, falling.

She caught his wrist.

The shock of her own grip nearly pulled her over after him. She braced, boots screaming on wet stone, arm burning, and held him dangling above the drop while the falls roared their one long word, and below on the bank the Kaeli were running with ropes, and Bao was shouting her name in a voice scraped raw, and she hung on—this man, this betrayer, this architect of her mother's disgrace and death—and held him out of the river because of a choice she made in the space of a heartbeat and would make again in every heartbeat after:

She was not going to become the thing that had hurt her.

The rope came around his chest. Hands pulled. Voss collapsed on the stone coughing and sobbing and clutching at the ground as if it were his mother, and Oscar knelt over him, dripping, shaking, her knuckles torn to the bone, and looked down at him with something that was not mercy and not hatred but simply the end of a very long current, finally reaching the sea.

Below, the flood was settling into the gorge. Above, the mist was lifting off the falls in banners, and through it the morning sun came down the cliff face in bars of gold, and Pip appeared at the top of the shelf with the crate hugged to her chest and her grin gone strange and soft.

"Oscar," she said. "Oscar, there's boats on the river. Coming up from downstream." Her eyes were wide, and not with fear. "Flying bureau colors. And the woman at the front of the first one—" Pip swallowed. "It's the inspector. Seng. And she's not stopping at the checkpoint, Oscar. She's coming *here*."

Oscar rose, and turned to face the water, and the water was already bringing the next thing.
