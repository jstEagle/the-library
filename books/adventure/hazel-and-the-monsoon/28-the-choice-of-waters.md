## 28. The Choice of Waters

The rope bridge sang.

That was the thing about it—after the fighting stopped, after the last rifle clattered into the churning white below and the last of Voss's hired men fled back down the trail with their tails between their legs, the bridge went on singing. A long, keening note from the severed strands on the far side, vibrating in the mist off the falls, like an instrument someone had struck and then walked away from.

Oscar stood at the near end of it, chest heaving, knuckles split and burning, and watched the man who had abandoned her mother dangle over the void.

Voss hung by both hands from the remaining cables, maybe four meters below the deck where the planks had torn away. His fine jacket was soaked through, his silver-streaked hair plastered to his skull, and the compass—the brass compass, her mother's gift, the map that had led them all here—was gone from his pocket, lost somewhere in the roil of the pool beneath the falls or still clenched in a fist that would not stay clenched much longer. His arms trembled. She could see the tremor even at this distance, could see the moment his right hand slipped two centimeters and he screamed—not words, just sound—and caught himself again.

Below him, the river did what the river always did. It didn't care. It boiled white around the fallen stones, dragged branches and foam and one of Voss's men's boots in a slow spiral toward the drop, and thundered against the cliff face with a patience that made human violence look like what it was: brief, loud, and meaningless.

"Oscar." His voice carried strangely across the water, flattened by the roar, stripped of everything smooth. "Oscar, please."

She didn't move.

Behind her, she heard Pip's quick feet on the wet stone, heard Jun shouting something about the channel gate holding, heard Bao's low voice answering—but it all came to her as if through water herself, muffled, distant. The world had narrowed to the width of a fraying cable and the man hanging from it.

*He let them take her.* The thought arrived without heat, which frightened her more than rage would have. *Right here. In these hills. He walked away and let the bureau take her, and she died, and he built a career out of selling what she found.*

"Please," Voss said again. "I know what I did. I know. But not like this. Not—not like this."

His left hand slid. He caught the cable with his wrist, hooked it somehow, hung there gasping, and Oscar saw with terrible clarity how easy this would be. All she had to do was nothing. Stand here. Watch. Let the river have him the way it should have had him twelve years ago, the way it would have if karma were a current instead of a metaphor. No jury. no blood on her hands. Just gravity and water doing the arithmetic that men never seemed to learn.

Pip reached her first, skidding on the slick rock, grabbing her sleeve. "Oscar—" The girl's voice cracked. Her eyes were huge, darting between the bridge and Oscar's face. "The rope. We can rig a line, we can—"

"I know," Oscar said.

Her own voice sounded foreign to her. Flat. She looked down at her hands—split knuckles, Voss's blood or her own mixed with spray—and remembered something she hadn't thought of in years: standing in a morgue corridor in a city whose name she'd let go, seventeen years old, watching two bureau officials sign papers that reduced her mother to an inventory number, and feeling the exact same flatness. The exact same cold, clean temptation to stop feeling anything ever again. She'd given in to it. She'd spent twelve years giving in to it, measuring her life in river miles because miles asked nothing of her and people asked everything.

*You can become the thing that hurt you,* her mother had written once, in a journal entry Oscar had read by lamplight weeks ago, in another life. *It doesn't happen all at once. It happens every time you choose the easier cruelty.*

She hadn't understood it then. She'd read it as philosophy, as the kind of thing archaeologists wrote to make their obsessions sound noble.

She understood it now, standing above a dangling man with a whole empty sky's worth of permission to walk away.

* * *

"Get me the spare line," she said. "And Bao's good hand. Now."

Pip blinked. Then she was moving, fast, shouting back toward the rocks where Bao sat cradling his freshly re-set shoulder, and Oscar turned back to the bridge and started out onto it.

The remaining cables held, barely. The deck planks were gone for three meters where the blast—or the fight, she'd lost track of which had torn them loose—had shredded them, so she straddled the cables themselves and hauled herself along them the way she'd hauled herself along anchor chains a thousand times in a thousand drowned places, except those chains hadn't swayed over a hundred-meter drop into whitewater. The spray hit her face in cold pulses. The falls' roar filled her skull until her own heartbeat was just another drumbeat inside it.

Halfway out, she looked down.

Voss was looking up at her. That was the worst part. His face, stripped of its broker's polish, was just a middle-aged man's face, terrified, and for one vertiginous second she saw him clearly—not the villain, not the betrayer, but a man who had once been young in these same hills, who had once perhaps believed in something, who had chosen survival over loyalty and spent thirty years paying interest on it until the debt came due on a rope bridge in the rain.

"You're insane," he shouted up at her. "You're actually—you could just walk away. Nobody would know. Nobody would *care*—"

"I'd know," Oscar said.

She got the line looped twice around the main cable and dropped the end. It took three tries; the wind off the falls kept pushing it away from him, and each miss cost him a little more grip, a little more color in his face. On the third try his flailing hand caught it and he wrapped it around his forearm and hung there, sobbing with relief, too exhausted to climb.

"Bao!" she called back. "On my count—we pull, you brace!"

Bao couldn't pull with one arm, but he could anchor like a mooring post, and he planted himself against the biggest boulder at the bridgehead while Jun and Pip took the line. Oscar stayed out on the cables, guiding, calling the rhythm—*pull, hold, pull*—and meter by meter they brought Voss up over the gap, over the torn planks, over the edge, until he collapsed onto the stone beside Bao's feet, coughing river water, shaking so hard his teeth chattered audibly even under the falls' thunder.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The bridge sang its broken note. Mist drifted through them all like something breathing.

Voss rolled onto his back and looked up at her. Water ran off his face into his collar. He opened his mouth—to thank her, to bargain, to do whatever version of himself he reached for next—and Oscar crouched down so their eyes were level.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Whatever you're about to say. Don't."

He closed his mouth.

"My mother trusted you." Her voice stayed level, which surprised her; somewhere inside there was a scream twelve years old trying to get out, and she held the door on it, held it the way she'd learned to hold her breath, minute after minute after minute. "She was alone in these hills because of you. Whatever happened after—whatever the bureau did—they could only do it because you weren't there. And you've spent your whole life since selling pieces of what she loved." She paused. "I should hate you. Maybe I do. But I'm not going to be the reason anyone else gets left behind. Not even you. *Especially* not you. Because that's the trick, isn't it? That's how it works. You leave one person, and then it gets easier, and then one day you're the kind of man who blows up a cave full of people who never did anything to anybody."

Voss stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes—not remorse, she suspected, not really, but maybe its shadow, maybe the place where remorse used to live before he renovated it into ambition.

"She talked about you," he rasped. His voice was wrecked. "At the end. Before—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said you dove like her. She said you'd understand the river better than any of us."

Oscar felt the words land somewhere under her ribs and hurt worse than the split knuckles.

She stood up. Turned away. Walked back across the broken bridge without looking down this time, because looking down was a habit for people who hadn't decided yet, and she had decided.

* * *

They came out of the mist in ones and twos, silent as weather.

Oscar heard them before she saw them—a shift in the treeline, a softness where birdsong had been—and then they were simply there, a dozen figures in woven rain-cloaks the color of wet bark, spears in hand, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who owned every stone here and knew exactly which ones were theirs to stand on. Elder Suri walked among them, small and straight-backed, her white hair bound in a coil at her neck, her eyes going from the shattered bridge to the scattered rifles to Voss, crumpled and dripping on the rock.

One of the Kaeli men crouched, retrieved a rifle, and passed it back without a word. Another collected a second, and a third. They did not point them at anyone. They didn't need to.

Suri stopped in front of Oscar. Her gaze was unreadable—not warm, not cold, the way the deep pools below the falls were neither warm nor cold, just true to their own depth.

"He came with fire," Suri said. Her trade-tongue was accented but precise. "Explosives. Men with guns. Against our children's village."

"Yes," Oscar said.

"And you pulled him from the river's judgment."

"The river wasn't judging him," Oscar said. "It was just being the river. Judgment was mine to make, and I made it." She lifted her chin. "He's yours now. By your law. That's why I pulled him up—I'm not going to kill a man, and I'm not going to set him loose to come back with more men. So he stays. However your law says."

Suri studied her for a long moment. Behind her, the Kaeli murmured among themselves in their own language, a sound like water over stones, rising and falling. Voss tried to push himself upright; two spears crossed gently in front of his chest, not striking, just informing.

"You could have let the water take him," Suri said. "No one here would have mourned. Some would have called it the river settling an old account." Her eyes flicked, briefly, toward Bao—toward some history between them that Oscar couldn't read. "Your mother stood nearly where you are standing, once. Different year. Different danger. She also chose the harder mercy." Something softened, barely, at the edges of the old woman's face. "I begin to see where you come from, diver."

Oscar's throat tightened. She said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't crack her open in front of forty strangers and a defeated enemy, and she'd done enough cracking open for one lifetime this month.

Suri turned and gave a short command. The Kaeli moved at once—two of them hauling Voss to his feet with an efficiency that was almost gentle, binding his wrists with braided cord, marching him up the trail toward the village. He went without resistance. Whatever fight had lived in him had drowned under the falls with the compass, and what remained was just a tired, wet old man being led to a reckoning he'd outrun for thirty years.

As they passed her, he stopped. Just for a second. Looked at her with those washed-out eyes.

"Why?" he said. "After everything. Why?"

Oscar looked at him—at the ruins of him—and thought about her mother's journal, the entry she'd read a hundred times by now, the one that ended mid-sentence. Thought about the letter she still hadn't finished reading. Thought about Pip's hand finding hers in the dark of the gorge, and Jun's voice steady in the storm, and Bao humming an old ferry song with his arm in a sling.

"Because somebody pulled me up once," she said. "When I didn't deserve it. When I didn't even want it." She stepped aside, letting the Kaeli take him. "Ask them what your law says. Not me. I'm done deciding what people deserve."

They led him away into the green dark, and the mist closed behind them like a door.

* * *

Afterward, the crew gathered at the bridgehead, and the adrenaline drained out of them all at once, the way water drains from a hull when you finally find the breach.

Jun sat down heavily on a boulder and put his head in his hands. His shirt was torn across the shoulder; there was a bruise blooming along his jaw where one of Voss's men had clipped him, and his fingers, when he lifted them, shook with the delayed tremor of a man who had redirected a swollen channel with a wooden sluice-gate and his bare hands and only realized afterward what the stakes had been.

"The gate held," he said, mostly to himself. "I genuinely did not know if it would hold. I want that noted for posterity. I am a cartographer, not a hydraulic engineer."

"Noted," Pip said, dropping down beside him. She was soaked to the skin, mud to the elbows, a shallow cut along one forearm that she was already picking at in the way that meant she'd forget about it until it infected. Her hair had half-escaped its braid and stuck out around her face like a corona. She looked, Oscar thought, like a small feral god of the flood season. "Also noted: I cut four fuse lines. Four. With a fish knife. While people were shooting at me."

"One of them shot at you?" Oscar said sharply.

"Around at me. Mostly around." Pip grinned, then winced, then grinned again anyway. "Bao held the bridge, though. Did you see? Whole time. One arm. Like a tree."

All of them looked at Bao. The old man sat against his boulder with his sling-bound arm cradled against his chest, his face gray with exhaustion and pain, his eyes closed. For a moment Oscar thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up. Then he spoke, without opening his eyes.

"I held the bridge," he agreed, "because I have held that bridge since before any of you were born, and I will be holding it long after you are dust, and if any of you ever cut my ropes again while standing on them, I will haunt this river specifically and personally." A pause. "Also, someone owes me dry tobacco."

"We'll add it to the list," Jun said. "The list is getting long. Dry tobacco. A new bridge. My license back. World peace—"

"Your license is coming back," Oscar said. "After this. If I have to burn the capital down."

"That," Jun said, opening one eye, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

Pip laughed—a real laugh, bright and startling against the falls' constant thunder—and then it caught in her throat and became something else, and suddenly she was crying, hard and ugly, the way fourteen-year-olds cry when the fear arrives late and all at once. Oscar sat down beside her without thinking about it, without weighing it, without measuring the distance first, and pulled the girl's head onto her shoulder. Pip resisted for exactly one second and then folded, fists gripping Oscar's sleeve, sobbing out the terror of the fuse lines and the gunfire and the moment she'd slipped on the wet planks and seen the drop yawning under her.

"I thought I was going in," she whispered. "I thought—I was gonna go in the water like—"

"Like I almost did," Oscar said. "Yeah. I know. I was watching."

"You caught my ankle."

"Somebody caught mine once too." She smoothed the escaped hair back from Pip's forehead. "Turns out it's a whole tradition."

Above them, the falls roared on, indifferent and magnificent, throwing rainbows through the mist whenever the light broke through. Somewhere up the trail, in a village that had survived centuries by staying invisible, a defeated man was learning what Kaeli law made of arsonists. Somewhere downstream, Inspector Adira Seng was moving through the hills with orders in her pocket and something heavier than orders on her face. And somewhere in the pool beneath the bridge, sinking slowly into silt, a brass compass lay with its micro-etched map of a dead nation pressed beneath its faceplate—lost, or returned, depending on how you counted such things.

Oscar found she didn't mind losing it. The map had done its work. It had brought her here, past the last mapped bend, to the place where her mother's story and her own finally touched.

What mattered was on this side of the water: a crying girl, a shaking cartographer, a stubborn old man demanding tobacco, and a village upstream full of people who had known her mother's name.

Bao opened his eyes and looked at her across the little circle of them, and something passed between them that needed no translation—the look of a man who had ferried her mother up this same river, watching her daughter become someone worth ferrying too.

"Eat," he said. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow, elder Suri sends for us." He paused, and his voice gentled in a way Oscar had rarely heard from him. "There is something in the archive, girl. Something your mother left in keeping. Suri says it has waited long enough."

Oscar's breath stopped.

"What is it?"

Bao smiled, slow and crooked, and closed his eyes again.

"Tomorrow," he said.
