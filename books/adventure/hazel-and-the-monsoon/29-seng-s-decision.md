## 29. Seng's Decision

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Inspector Adira Seng stood at the edge of the Kaeli village with her boots sinking into mud that smelled of wet ash and crushed fern, and she understood, finally and completely, why her orders had been written in a bureau office three hundred miles downstream by men who had never once put their boots in this mud.

She had come up through the hills alone. That was not what the orders said—her orders said *with escort, under seal, upon confirmation of site location, execute Directive Eleven*—but she had left her two junior officers at the checkpoint below with a fabricated radio schedule and a story about surveying landslide damage on the eastern trail, and now there was no one behind her to stop her from doing what she was already certain she would do, which was nothing, or worse than nothing, which was something.

The village did not look like anything worth burning. That was the first obscenity of it. She had spent twenty-two years in the antiquities service, and she had learned to read sites the way other people read faces—the telltale geometry of buried foundations, the scatter of shard and slag that meant industry, the alignment of stones that meant belief. This village had all of it, worn smooth into the ordinary: drying racks hung with river fish, a forge shed with its fire banked for the night, children's footprints pressed into the clay between the houses, a loom with half-woven cloth still stretched on it, abandoned mid-pattern because someone had heard footsteps on the ridge trail and gone to see who was coming. The archive was here somewhere—she could feel it the way she could feel water under limestone—but it lived inside this life, threaded through it, and Directive Eleven had been drafted by men who thought you could burn a thread without burning the cloth.

Her mother had taught her to read sites. That was the thing she could not stop circling, the wound beneath the wound. Not Oscar Nakamura's mother—her own. A schoolteacher in a provincial town that no longer appeared on railway maps, a woman who took Adira out on Sundays with a hand lens and a notebook and taught her that a country was not its borders but its accumulations, everything layered down like sediment, and that destroying any layer was a kind of lie told against everyone who came after. Her mother had died believing the bureau was a good institution staffed by imperfect people. Adira had spent two decades trying to make that true, and every year it got harder, and every year she filed one more report she had softened, one more finding she had buried, and told herself she was protecting the institution from itself, and somewhere along the way she had become the kind of officer who carried Directives like this one in a waterproof tube against her spine and did not flinch when she read them.

She flinched now.

* * *

They had let her walk in. That was the second obscenity, and it unsettled her more than resistance would have. She had come up the ridge trail in the grey light with her sidearm and her sealed orders and the full machinery of the state implicit in her posture, and the sentries—young men and women with spears whose points were iron but whose hafts were polished from generations of hands—had looked at her, looked at each other, and stepped aside. An old woman had brought her tea without being asked. A child had stared at her bureau insignia with frank, unafraid curiosity, the way children stare at animals in a market, and then run off to whatever children do here in the hour after rain.

*They know what I am,* Seng thought, standing with her untouched tea among houses that had stood, hidden, for a hundred years while her bureau's maps called this place empty forest. *And they are not afraid.*

That was worse than fear. Fear she could work with; fear was the raw material of every seizure operation she had ever conducted. But this—the tea offered because refusing hospitality would shame the guest, not because it disarmed her—this was a confidence so complete it made her feel like a rumor, a weather event, something that would pass over and be forgotten by afternoon. She had spent her whole career being feared by looters and tolerated by academics, and she had never once, until this morning, been *irrelevant*, and she found that it burned worse than any accusation ever had.

Elder Suri came to her near midmorning. Seng had seen her the evening before across the checkpoint fire—a small woman, straight-backed, with the particular stillness of people who have spent their lives being watched by mountains—and now she approached without ceremony and sat on the bench beside Seng as though they had arranged it years ago.

"You have paper," Suri said. "I can see the shape of it under your jacket. Paper that is not yours."

Seng did not insult them both by denying it. "Orders," she said. "From my bureau. Concerning this village."

"Concerning what is kept here."

"Yes."

Suri nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd already entered in a ledger. "A man came once, years ago. Also with paper. He burned his paper in our fire." Her eyes were pale brown, unhurried. "He was not a brave man, I think. He was a tired one. There is a difference. Bravery burns things quickly. Tiredness lets them go slow."

"Did you kill him?"

"No." No offense taken, no offense given. "We fed him and walked him to the low trail and he went back down and told his bureau he found nothing, and they believed him, because men who want to find nothing believe it easily." She turned her head and regarded Seng fully. "You will not find nothing. You have already found us. So the question is only what you will carry back down."

"The orders say—" Seng began, and stopped, because reciting Directive Eleven aloud in this place, beside this woman, with the fish racks and the loom and the child's footprints all around her, would be a desecration she would have to carry longer than any consequence the bureau could impose. "The orders say the material is to be destroyed and the site rendered unlocatable."

"Rendered unlocatable," Suri repeated, tasting the phrase. "You have beautiful words for ugly work. We have words too. Ours are older." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and stood looking down the valley toward the falls, where the river's voice never stopped, day or night, a sound Seng had begun to realize the villagers probably didn't hear anymore, the way you don't hear your own heartbeat. "Come," the elder said. "If you are going to decide, decide looking at it. Deciding in the dark is how bureaus think."

* * *

The archive was not what she had prepared for.

She had prepared for a strongroom. For steel and stone, for catalogued crates, for the austere grandeur of state custody. What Elder Suri led her to was a cave behind the falls reached by a rope bridge that swayed over white water, and inside, lit by oil lamps that turned the dripping rock to amber, the memory of Malaya was kept the way grain is kept in a granary—not enshrined but *stored*, alive, in use. Genealogy bundles tied with bark cord, their edges soft from handling. Song cylinders of wax and bamboo, some of them recent, the wax still pale. Treaty copies in three scripts. Land charts drawn on barkcloth, rivers running across them like veins. And names—thousands of names, birthed and married and dead, recorded in a continuous hand across a century of official erasure, because a nation, Seng understood as she stood there with lamp-oil smoke stinging her eyes, is not territory. A nation is a list of people. You can take the territory. The list survives anywhere people keep writing it.

There were children in the cave. Two of them, crouched by a bundle shelf, copying script onto slate as part of some lesson, and when the strangers entered they looked up, and one of them—she couldn't have been more than eight—simply nodded at Suri and went back to her copying, because the presence of outsiders with weapons was apparently a thing that happened here and was survived, generation after generation, and the copying continued.

Seng had seized collections before. She knew the feeling: the cold triumph, the ledger satisfaction, the sense of mattering. Standing in this cave she felt none of it. She felt instead what she imagined a gravedigger feels when the spade strikes a coffin lid—the sudden intimacy with what should have stayed covered, the knowledge that from this moment forward she was a person who had *seen*, and seeing was a debt.

Nakamura was there. Of course she was. The diver stood apart with her crew—the old ferryman bandaged and feverish, the cartographer Jun with ink-stained fingers and the expression of a man watching his life's vindication and his life's ruin occupy the same room, the girl Pip hovering at Nakamura's shoulder like a shadow learning to be a person. Nakamura met Seng's eyes across the lamplight, and there was no triumph in her face either. Only wariness, and underneath it something rawer, something Seng recognized because she had seen it in mirrors on bad mornings: a person waiting to learn whether the world intended to break the last thing they loved.

"I need to speak with her," Seng said to Suri. "Alone."

"She is not yours to command here."

"I'm not commanding. I'm asking."

Suri considered this for a long moment, then inclined her head a fraction, and the crew withdrew up the passage toward the daylight, though Seng noticed the girl Pip planted herself just outside the entrance, visible in silhouette against the falling water, arms crossed, refusing to go further. Good, Seng thought. Someone should be.

* * *

They stood by the genealogy shelves, and Seng took the tube from against her spine, and held it, and did not open it.

"Your mother," she said.

"Don't." The word came out flat and fast, a blade laid on a table.

"I'm going to say it anyway, because you've spent twelve years hearing lies about her, and I am the last person alive who can give you the truth with documentation attached." Seng drew a breath. Twenty-two years of institutional caution told her to stop. Her mother's voice, Sunday-morning and patient, told her to continue. "Ines Nakamura consulted for the bureau for six years. She was our best field analyst. I was a junior clerk when she came in—I shelved her reports. I read every one twice." She opened the tube now, and removed not the orders but a folder beneath them, thin, old, its corners rounded by decades of handling. "When she refused to sign the suppression finding on the Malaya materials, the bureau didn't just dismiss her. They constructed the disgrace. The plagiarism charge was manufactured—I've read the internal review; the 'original source' they cited doesn't exist. The evidence destruction order was backdated. And when she kept working anyway, upstream, alone—" Seng's voice caught, and she was surprised by it, and let it. "When she kept working, they sent someone to facilitate her removal. Permanently. The file is thin because it was meant to be thin. But it exists. I pulled it eleven years ago, the week I made inspector, and I have kept it in my personal safe ever since, waiting for—" She stopped. Even now she couldn't finish that sentence honestly, because the honest ending was *waiting for permission*, and permission had never come, and she had waited eleven years for an institution to grow a conscience, and institutions don't. People do, or they don't.

Nakamura had gone very still. In the lamplight her face was the face of someone underwater—features present, distorted, pressure everywhere. "Sent someone," she repeated.

"The file calls him a 'field facilitator.' It gives no name. It gives a payment code and a date range that ends four days before your mother was reported drowned." Seng held out the folder. "I'm not giving you justice. Justice requires a court, and the men who signed this are mostly dead, and the ones who aren't are retired on pensions. I'm giving you the record. It's the only weapon the bureau ever accidentally forged against itself, and I've kept it oiled for eleven years."

Nakamura took the folder the way people take things when their hands have forgotten how—carefully, as if it might go off. Then she did something Seng had not expected. She sat down. Right there, on the cave floor, amid a nation's memory, and held the folder against her chest with both arms, and shook, silently, the way water shakes before it freezes.

Outside the falls roared. Inside, the child copied her script, wax cylinder by wax cylinder, name by name, keeping the list.

* * *

Afterward—they never spoke of how long afterward—Seng walked back out onto the rope bridge alone, and stood above the white water, and took out Directive Eleven.

It was a single page under seal. Execute, certify, countersign. Her signature at the bottom would close a question that had embarrassed three directors, and her career would crest on it, directorship within five years, the whole ascending staircase she had climbed toward since she was a girl with a hand lens in a vanished railway town.

She thought about her mother, who had believed the bureau was good. She thought about Ines Nakamura, who had known better and worked anyway. She thought about the child copying names on slate, and about the difference Elder Suri had named between bravery and tiredness, and she found she was neither brave nor tired. She was simply finished. Finished pretending the institution and the country were the same thing. Her mother had taught her the country was the sediment—all of it, every layer—and the bureau had spent a century scraping layers off and calling the remainder clean.

She fed the orders into the wind off the falls, page by torn piece, and watched the white water take them, and felt absolutely nothing where she had expected either damnation or relief—only space, a vast cleared space, like a flooded field after the water goes down.

Nakamura was waiting at the far end of the bridge. Behind her, the crew. Behind them, the village, incurious already, returning to its nets and looms.

"There's a condition," Seng said.

"Of course there is."

"You publish the journal. All of it, redacted where it touches these people—that's not my condition, that's theirs, and you'll honor it. But you publish under her name, in full, with the disgrace named as what it was. And when the bureau comes asking me what happened to Directive Eleven, I will tell them I found nothing." She allowed herself the smallest dry movement at the corner of her mouth. "Men who want to find nothing believe it easily."

"And the folder?" Nakamura asked. "What happens when they ask where *that* came from?"

"It won't have a source. Records like that surface. They always surface eventually—it's practically hydrology." Seng looked past her, at the falls, at the mist rising off them into a sky already gathering the next rain. "Your mother died protecting this place. Whatever else you carry, carry that. It's documented. It's true. And it's the only inheritance the bureau ever gave anyone that was worth the postage."

She turned to go down the ridge trail, alone, toward the checkpoint and the fabricated radio schedule and the long unraveling career she had just chosen with both hands. At the edge of the trees she paused, because the girl—Pip—was standing there, blocking the path with the absolute authority of a fourteen-year-old, holding something out in both grimy hands.

Seng's lighter. Lifted from her coat pocket sometime during the past hour, presumably while she wept or nearly did, returned now with ceremonial gravity.

"For burning things," Pip said solemnly. "Or not burning things. Whichever."

Seng took it, and found, absurdly, that she was smiling, and walked down into the hills carrying nothing but a folder-shaped absence, a stolen lighter, and—for the first time in twenty-two years—no orders at all.

Behind her, high above the falls, the river kept the list.
