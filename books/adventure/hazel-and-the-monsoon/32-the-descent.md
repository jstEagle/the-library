## 32. The Descent

The river was leaving before we were.

Pip noticed it first from the bow of Bao's boat, the way you notice a friend has stopped breathing—something in the rhythm gone wrong, something missing that had always been there. For weeks the water had been a living thing, brown and muscular, shouldering the boat along like it couldn't wait to show them what came next. Now it was pulling back. The banks showed fresh scars of mud, pale as bone where the flood had stripped the vegetation away. Rocks that had been drowned secrets all journey up were surfacing now like old men rising from sleep, dripping, blinking at the light.

*Going down is faster,* Bao had said that morning, spitting into the current with the air of a man delivering a funeral oration. *Faster and meaner. The river going down doesn't carry you. It drops you.*

Pip believed him. Pip believed everything Bao said about the river, because everything he'd said so far had turned out to be true, and true things were rarer than coins in her experience.

She sat cross-legged on the foredeck with her feet still wrapped in Jun's spare shirt, torn into strips, because her soles were a map of their own by now—a cartography of blisters and split skin from the high trail, drawn in red. She'd stopped wincing about it two days ago. You could get used to anything, she'd learned. That was the good news and the bad news both.

Ahead, Oscar stood at the rail with the compass in her hand—not reading it, just holding it, the way you'd hold someone's wrist to feel if they were still alive. Behind her, Jun hunched over his redrawn charts, muttering numbers, and Bao worked the tiller with his shoulder still bound from the dislocation, moving like a man made of rope and stubbornness.

They were running out of river. That was the thing nobody said out loud. The dry season was eating the channel from the bottom up, and every hour they didn't move south was an hour the water got shallower, and every hour the water got shallower was an hour closer to the boat cracking open on a sandbar with the whole story still inside it—the journal wrapped in oilcloth under Pip's sleeping mat, the letter for the Kaeli elders already delivered, Voss trussed at the stern under a tarp like cargo nobody wanted to look at.

"What happens to us now?"

Pip had asked it three nights ago, sitting on the warm stone of the village wall while the Kaeli children slept in a pile around Jun's half-finished map. Oscar hadn't answered. She'd looked at the fire instead, and her face had done the thing it did when she was measuring distance to some horizon only she could see.

Pip wasn't going to ask again. But she kept the question folded up inside her chest like a stolen note, and every mile of the descent, it weighed a little more.

* * *

The first race came at noon on the second day, at a place Bao called the Needle's Eye—a gap between two rock ribs where the channel narrowed to less than a boat's width and the falling water ran through it fast and shallow, hissing over gravel that had been ten feet under a month ago.

"Off the deck," Bao ordered. "All of you. If we ground, I don't want bodies in the way."

"I can help steer," Pip said.

"You can help by being small somewhere else."

But Oscar caught Pip's arm as she moved past. "Stay low by the bow. Watch the water ahead—tell me what color it is."

Pip blinked. "Color."

"Green means deep enough. Brown means it's chewing rocks. White means get off." Oscar's eyes were on the gap ahead, but there was something almost like a smile at the corner of her mouth. "My mother taught me that. Green, brown, white."

So Pip crouched at the bow with her heart going like a bird in a box and called the colors as they came, and the Needle's Eye took them in its jaws and shook them once—Bao leaning the whole weight of his ruined shoulder against the tiller, the hull shrieking along something just beneath the surface, a sound like a nail dragged across tin—and then spat them out the far side into deeper green water, everyone laughing in that shaky way people laugh when they've almost died and haven't.

"Brown," Pip reported breathlessly, pointing astern. "It's going brown behind us."

"Then we don't go back," Oscar said. And Pip thought: *she said we.* Small word. Enormous word.

* * *

They portaged twice more before dark.

The first was short—fifty meters of exposed gravel bar where the channel simply gave up and went underground through a limestone throat, reappearing downstream in a boil of cold spring water. They carried everything: fuel drums, food tins, the wrapped shape of Voss, who cursed through his gag until Jun stopped and looked at him with such flat patience that he shut himself up. Pip walked the bar barefoot despite her bandages, because shoes were for people with unbroken skin, and she wanted to feel the riverbed under her—one last time, maybe, the way it used to be before the maps and the bureau and the men with guns. The mud was cool and held the memory of her footprints long after she'd passed. She found herself walking backward for a few steps, just to see them doubled.

The second portage was not short. It was a cliff of mud and root, two hundred meters of climbing with the boat on rollers improvised from drum lids, and by the end of it even Oscar's hands were shaking and Bao had gone gray-lipped and silent, and Pip did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding: Bao was seventy-something years old with one working arm, and the delta was four days away at best, and the river was dropping faster than Jun's charts could predict.

She found Oscar at the top of the portage, sitting on a fallen log with her head back, watching swifts cut the evening sky.

"He's not going to make another one," Pip said quietly. "Another portage. Not like this."

Oscar didn't deny it. That was one of the things Pip had come to trust about her—she never lied to soften a thing. "Jun thinks there's an old logging chute past the bend. If it holds, we ride it down instead of carrying."

"If it doesn't?"

"Then we carry." Oscar looked at her then, really looked, and Pip felt seen straight through, the way she always did, like Oscar had X-ray vision for the things people hid. "You're worried about him."

"I'm worried about all of you." It came out before she could stop it. *You*, singular, hiding inside the plural like a stowaway.

Oscar heard it. Pip watched her hear it. And then Oscar reached over and knocked her knuckles gently against Pip's knee—the closest thing to affection she'd ever offered unprompted—and said, "Get some sleep. Tomorrow's the chute."

* * *

The logging chute nearly killed them, which by the standards of the trip counted as a moderate success.

It was a timber slide cut into the hillside a lifetime ago, rotted planks and rusted bolts, half-collapsed, running steep and crooked down to the river below. Jun found it exactly where the old survey notes said it would be, and stood at its head looking down its length with the expression of a man being asked to post a letter through a keyhole.

"We grease it," Oscar said.

"With what?"

"Palm fat. We've got two tins of cooking fat and no better ideas."

They greased it. They lashed the cargo down, gagged Voss extra thoroughly—"for his own safety," Jun said pleasantly—and Bao took the tiller with his good arm braced against the gunwale, and Oscar put Pip in the bow again because, she said, Pip had the best eyes and the least sense of self-preservation, which was apparently a compliment.

The chute was eleven seconds long. Pip would remember those eleven seconds for the rest of her life: the world tilting, rot and grease-smell and green light flickering through gaps in the planks, Bao roaring something that might have been a prayer or profanity, the hull slamming left, right, left, a bolt-head screeching a white line down the starboard strake—and then the sky opening, the river catching them with a boom that threw everyone to the deck, and silence, and floating.

Pip lay on her back among the scattered cargo and laughed until her ribs hurt. Above her, the karst towers rose into the haze like the backs of sleeping animals, and a brahminy kite turned slow circles in the blue, and for one whole minute nothing in the world was wrong.

Then Oscar's face appeared over hers, upside down, hair dripping, and she was laughing too—actually laughing, the real kind, the kind that crinkled her whole face—and Pip thought: *this. This is what I asked about. Whatever happens next, I want to keep this.*

* * *

They saw the patrol boat on the fourth morning, and everyone's laughter died at once.

It sat anchored across the lower channel where the river widened toward the delta flats, white hull, bureau markings, a machine gun mount covered with a tarp that fooled no one. Inspector Seng stood at the rail. Even at two hundred meters, Pip knew her by the posture—straight-backed, hands clasped, a woman standing at attention to her own conscience.

Bao brought the boat alongside without being told. There was nowhere else to go anyway; the channel past the patrol boat was barely a hand's depth in places, silvered with sandbars like veins under skin.

Seng came aboard alone. Her eyes went first to the tarp, and Pip watched her jaw tighten when Voss's face was uncovered—watched something complicated move behind her eyes, hatred and satisfaction and an old tired grief all fighting for the same chair.

"Silas Voss," she said. "Wanted in three provinces. The charges will include kidnapping, armed assault, destruction of protected heritage sites, and the murder of Dr. Elara Nakamura."

The name landed on the deck like dropped iron. Pip saw Oscar go very still—not flinching, just still, the stillness of deep water.

"You have evidence," Oscar said. It wasn't a question.

"The bureau has evidence it spent twelve years burying." Seng's voice was level, but her hands weren't quite; she pressed them flat against her thighs. "I burned my orders at the source. What I kept were the records. His deposition, the expedition ledgers, the radio logs from the night your mother was left in the hills. It will be enough. He'll stand trial in the capital, publicly. Her name will be read into the record." A pause. "All of it. Including Malaya."

Voss laughed from under the tarp, wet and ugly. "You think a trial changes anything? The archive stays hidden either way. You've traded the truth for a bedtime story—"

Jun stuffed the rag back in his mouth with the brisk tenderness of a man feeding a horse.

Seng signaled her launch. Two junior officers came aboard and took Voss's arms, and here was the thing Pip would tell the Kaeli children someday, the detail she'd keep: as they lifted him, Voss twisted around and looked at Oscar, and whatever he meant to say, Oscar looked back at him first—looked at him the way you'd look at weather, at something vast and finished and beneath notice—and whatever it was died in his throat.

He was rowed away. The patrol boat raised anchor. The wake of its departure rocked them gently, like the river shrugging.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Bao said, "We should eat something," and they ate something, and the world went on.

* * *

That night they camped on a sandbar that hadn't existed a month ago, and Pip couldn't sleep.

The fire was small. Jun had fallen asleep mid-sentence over his charts, pencil still in his fist, and Bao snored with the deep contentment of a man whose debts were paid, and Oscar sat at the edge of the firelight turning the brass compass over and over, its lid clicking open, clicking shut.

Pip got up and sat beside her. Neither of them spoke for a long time. Downriver, the delta breathed—frogs, wind in the nipa palms, the far-off clang of a temple bell in some town that still existed on maps.

"My mother sold me to a boat crew when I was six," Pip said, to the fire. "For passage money and a bag of rice. I don't even remember her face anymore. Just her hands, pushing me forward." She picked at a thread on her bandage. "Every crew since, I've been the first one gone when the money ran out. You learn to leave before you're left. It's the only trick I know."

Oscar said nothing. She'd gotten good at that—listening without filling the silence, a skill Pip suspected she'd learned the hard way too.

"You asked me once why I kept stealing the compass," Pip went on. "On the ferry, at the start. You never let me answer."

"You answered by giving it back twice."

"That was showing off. Here's the answer." Pip pulled her knees up to her chin. "I wanted to know if you'd notice. If anything going missing from you would matter enough to make you look for it. Because I'd decided—I'd decided before I even met you—that you were the kind of person things went missing from and stayed missing. People included." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it and kept going anyway. "And then you dove off a boat after me. Into monsoon water. For me. And I've been waiting ever since for you to remember that you're supposed to leave, because everyone leaves, it's the rule, it's the whole—"

"Pip."

Just her name. Oscar said it the way she said *green, brown, white*—a report on conditions ahead.

"I'm not good at staying," Oscar said. "I want you to know that about me. I'm not going to pretend I'm something I'm not. Twelve years of running doesn't wash off in one season." She opened the compass and held it so the firelight caught the micro-etched map beneath the faceplate, the route her mother had hidden there for a daughter she'd known might never come looking. "But my mother spent her whole life believing I'd find my way back to her. She was right. It took me too long, and she wasn't alive to see it, and I have to live with that." She closed the compass. "I'm not going to be right about everything. But I'm not going to be wrong about you."

Pip discovered she was crying, which was mortifying, and that Oscar had put an arm around her shoulders, which was somehow worse and better at once, and that she didn't want to move, which was the most frightening fact of all.

"The elders offered you a place," Pip managed. "At the village. Suri said—you could stay. Take or leave."

"I know."

"So what are you going to—"

"Look," Oscar said softly, and nodded downriver.

Pip followed her gaze. Far off across the black water, where the delta began, lights were coming on—fishing boats heading out before dawn, lanterns strung along the floating market piers, the whole glittering sprawl of the living world waking up. And above it all, faint but unmistakable, the smell of rain on the wind. The new monsoon, weeks early, gathering somewhere over the sea.

"First rain," Oscar said. "It'll reach the village in a day or two. The river will rise again." She turned the compass in her fingers, and Pip saw that she wasn't looking at the map inside it anymore. She was looking downriver, and then—slowly—back upstream, into the dark hills where the falls thundered and the Kaeli kept the river's memory and a letter waited that said *not as a keeper of secrets, but as herself.*

"Bao says the boat needs two weeks of work before she's seaworthy again," Oscar said. "New strake where the bolt scored her. New rollers. Maybe a proper cabin, if Jun stops drawing maps long enough to hold a hammer."

Pip's heart was doing something dangerous and hopeful. "That sounds like staying."

"It sounds like a start." Oscar knocked her knuckles against Pip's knee again, the same small gesture, and this time Pip understood it completely. "Sleep, stowaway. Tomorrow we go home the long way."

*Home.* Pip lay back on the deck boards afterward and stared up at the wheeling stars and held the word carefully, the way you'd hold a bird you'd finally been allowed to touch—afraid to grip it, afraid to let it go.

Upstream, the rain was coming. She fell asleep listening for it.
