## 34. What Voss Confessed

The detention facility in the capital smelled of mildew and old paper, which struck Adira Seng as fitting. Everything in her life had come to smell of old paper lately—dossiers, orders, the charred edges of the ones she'd burned.

She sat across a scarred steel table from Marcus Voss and waited. Waiting was the bureau's oldest instrument. She had learned it from men who used it like a blade: let the silence do the cutting, let the prisoner fill the quiet with his own confession. But she was not here to cut him. She was here because there was one thread left dangling from the whole tangled knot of Malaya, and it ran through this man's hands.

Voss looked smaller than he had on the water. The fine linen shirt was gone, replaced by a gray detainee's tunic that made his skin look sallow. His hands—those elegant, ringless hands she remembered gesturing smoothly over charts and offers—lay flat on the table as if he'd been told to keep them still. Only his eyes were unchanged: quick, calculating, always three moves ahead of whoever sat opposite him.

Except, Seng thought, he hadn't been three moves ahead at the falls. He had been hanging from a rope bridge over white water, begging a woman half his age to pull him up, and she had pulled him up anyway.

"Inspector." His voice was still smooth. It would always be smooth; some men polished themselves until the polish became the man. "I wondered when you'd come. The public prosecutor's people have been through twice already. They ask about smuggling statutes, antiquities law, the explosives." A thin smile. "You're not here about the explosives."

"No."

"You're here about her mother."

Seng didn't confirm it. She laid a single sheet of paper on the table between them—the transcript request form, unsigned—and watched him read it upside down, the way clever men do.

"Oscar Nakamura," Voss said, testing the name. "Or should I say Hazel? I understand she's reclaimed it now. Hazel Nakamura. It has a certain ring. Her mother would have approved."

Something moved through Seng's chest, cold and slow, like water finding a crack in stone. "You knew Dr. Nakamura well?"

"I worked for her for two years." He said it lightly, but his fingers pressed flat against the table, whitening at the tips. "Before you people disavowed her. Before the burn notices. Before her name became something junior analysts whispered about as a cautionary tale." His eyes came up. "You want to know how she died. Everyone wants to know how she died. The bureau buried the file so deep even I couldn't reach it, and I could reach almost anything."

"I'm not here on the bureau's behalf," Seng said. "My orders regarding the archive are—" She stopped. The word *burned* sat in her throat like ash. "My involvement with the archive is concluded. This is personal."

"Personal." Voss tasted the word. "An inspector with a personal interest. That's how they got your predecessor too, you know. Caring is a liability in your building. I imagine you've noticed."

"I've noticed," Seng said quietly, "that everyone who touched Elara Nakamura's story ended up broken by it. Her daughter. Jun Reyes. Old Bao. You." She leaned forward. "I'd like to know if I'm next, or if there's still time to put the pieces down differently. So tell me what happened in the hills. All of it. And don't polish it."

For a long moment Voss said nothing. Somewhere down the corridor a door clanged, and the sound rolled through the concrete like distant thunder—the monsoon was coming again, even here, even indoors, in the rhythm of the building's bones.

Then he began to talk.

* * *

"It started the way all my ventures start," he said. "With a map that shouldn't exist. Elara came to me in this city, eleven years ago now, with fragments—trade seals, a bronze key, pages of a colonial survey that officially never happened. She'd spent six years assembling proof that Malaya wasn't a myth or a cartographer's error. She wanted financing for an expedition upstream. No institution would touch her. Her own university had already begun distancing itself. So she came to a broker." He smiled without humor. "A broker and a liar. She knew what I was. I think she believed she could use me and discard me before I did any damage. She was smarter than me in every way except one."

"Which was?"

"She thought everyone loved the truth as much as she did." He said it flatly, and for a moment the polish cracked and something older showed underneath—not remorse exactly, Seng thought, but the ghost of it, the place where remorse would have lived in a better-built man. "We went upriver the dry season after the flood year. Bao piloted—I don't know if you've met him. Ancient then. Ancient now. He carried us past the last mapped bend on a boat older than both of us put together. Elara had a compass her daughter had given her—no, wait." He frowned, correcting himself with visible effort. "The other way around. She'd given the girl the compass. She talked about her constantly, in the way of people who talk about someone constantly precisely because they can't talk to them. 'Hazel would dive that channel.' 'Hazel would have spotted the carving.' Eleven years old and already the standard the rest of us failed to meet."

Seng kept her face still. She had read the journal—she had read every redacted line of it twice—and she knew the compass, knew the micro-etched map beneath its faceplate, knew that a nine-year-old had carried her mother's secret route around her neck for twenty years without knowing it. But she wanted to hear it from him. Testimony had a different weight than paper. Paper could be dismissed. A man's voice, breaking slightly in a concrete room, could not.

"The Kaeli found us before we found them," Voss continued. "Of course they did. They'd been watching the river for a hundred years. Carved warning stones, mist, cliffs—you've seen the approaches. Elara negotiated with their elders for three days. Three days, Inspector, sitting in a village that doesn't appear on any map, being shown things that made her weep. Genealogies. Treaties. Songs written down before your bureau's founding charter existed. A complete nation in a cave behind a waterfall." His voice dropped. "And she made a choice. Right there, with me standing beside her, she chose them. She agreed to keep their location sealed. To publish nothing that could lead anyone to the falls. She walked away from vindication—her name, her career, everything the bureau had taken from her—because she understood what exposure would cost them."

"And you?" Seng asked, though she already knew. The shape of it had been forming in her since the rope bridge, since the explosives, since the hired muscle.

"I saw a fortune," Voss said simply. "The greatest antiquities find in a century, and the only person who could authenticate it was standing in front of me promising to bury it. I tried to change her mind for two days. Then I changed tactics." He looked down at his flat, pale hands. "There was a supply run. Downriver for fuel and provisions. I went alone. When I came back up—" He stopped. Started again. "I didn't come back up. I sent word ahead through a contact in the lowlands. A message to the provincial office. Anonymous. Coordinates, dates, the name Elara Nakamura, and the phrase *unregistered excavation of state patrimony.*"

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when weight shifts wrong. Seng had suspected. Reading between the lines of burned files and redacted reports, she had suspected. But suspicion was a fog and this was a wall.

"They came fast," Voss went on, quieter now, as if the words cost more the further he went. "Bureau enforcement, armed, with a seizure order signed by your directorate. Elara was alone at the falls when they arrived—the elders had withdrawn deeper into the hills to deliberate about letting her stay through the wet season. She had hours of warning. Bao reached her first, offered to run her downriver, out, away. She refused." His jaw tightened. "She sent Bao back with a sealed letter and instructions to keep it safe, and she stayed at the cave entrance to delay them. To buy time for the Kaeli to move the most sensitive materials deeper. She stood between your colleagues and that archive and she talked, and stalled, and cited treaty law from a dead nation, and when they finally moved to force the entrance she—" 

He broke off. For the first time since Seng had entered the room, Marcus Voss looked his age.

"She fell," he said. "Or she jumped into the gorge rather than lead them to the lower passage. The official report says accidental drowning during attempted flight. The report I paid a clerk to copy says the enforcement team fired warning shots at the water where she surfaced." He lifted his eyes. "Your bureau killed her, Inspector. Not with bullets. With a seizure order and a burn notice and thirty years of teaching a brilliant woman that her country would erase her the way it erased Malaya. She died defending a nation that no longer existed, from the nation that did."

Seng sat motionless. The mildew-and-paper smell of the room pressed in. She thought of her own desk upstairs, the framed photograph of her mother on the corner of it, the small daily betrayals of institutional life that seemed so reasonable one at a time. She thought of a woman standing at a waterfall, citing dead treaties to men with rifles, buying minutes with her life.

"Why confess now?" she asked, when she could trust her voice. "You've kept this for eleven years. Your lawyers will tell you to keep keeping it."

"Because of the letter," Voss said.

* * *

He asked for water, and Seng poured it herself from the pitcher—a small human gesture that felt strange in her hand, like using the wrong tool for a lock.

"There was a second item," he said, turning the cup slowly. "That day at the falls, before the enforcement team arrived. Elara gave Bao a letter—sealed, wax, addressed in Malayan script to the elders of the Kaeli. Bao couldn't go back up; the patrols were watching the river within a day. So he gave it to me. Me, because I had a fast boat and clean papers and he believed—" a bitter breath of laughter— "he actually believed I cared about her. He asked me to hold it until the river cooled, then carry it up. And I took it. And I kept it. Because a letter in the hand of Elara Nakamura, addressed to the guardians of the archive, might someday be worth something. Leverage. Provenance. Insurance." He set the cup down with a small precise click. "I hid it inside the lining of the chest. The lead chest. The one your divers pulled out of the flooded temple last season."

Seng felt the floor drop away beneath the conversation. "The chest was catalogued. Sealed. It's in evidence storage—"

"With a false bottom," Voss said, "that your technicians haven't found, because the lining is lead over lead and the seam is soldered with the same alloy. I built that compartment myself, years ago, for items I never intended to declare. The letter has been sitting under her daughter's mother's journal for months, Inspector. Ten centimeters of metal between them. I've thought about that more than I've thought about my sentence." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "When they pulled me out of the river at the delta, half-drowned, delivered to you by the very people I'd robbed—I understood something. Elara beat me. Not at the falls, not with the Kaeli, not with her daughter and that ragged little crew. She beat me afterward. From a grave. Because everything I stole from her turned in my hands like a knife by the blade. The chest brought the bureau. The compass led me to the falls and to the rope bridge. And the letter—" he exhaled— "the letter is the only thing I ever held of hers that I was afraid to open. So I never did. Some part of me knew it was the one theft I couldn't survive."

Seng stood. Her chair scraped, loud in the concrete hush.

"Where is the chest now?"

"Evidence annex, sub-basement two. Crate 114. The seal on the letter is intact—wax, pressed with the Malayan rose. You'll know it when you see it." He paused, and when he spoke again the smoothness was gone entirely, leaving something raw and tired. "Inspector. Tell her daughter. Tell Hazel that her mother's last act on this earth was standing between armed men and strangers who had shown her kindness. Tell her the letter exists. What's in it, I don't know—but Elara wrote it the night before she died, and she wrote it calm. Bao told me that. He said she wrote it smiling."

* * *

Sub-basement two smelled of cold concrete and lamp oil, and the lights hummed at a frequency that made Seng's teeth ache. Crate 114 sat on a steel shelf between confiscated Buddha heads and a crate of looted manuscripts awaiting repatriation, and she signed it out with hands that were, she noted distantly, entirely steady.

The chest opened the way it always opened, the bronze key turning with a sound like a held breath released. Journal, wrapping oilcloth, diving slate, coil of waterproofed cord. And then, kneeling on the cold floor with a penlight between her teeth, she found it: a seam in the rear lining so fine her fingertip passed over it twice before catching. She heated a knife blade over her lighter, drew it along the solder, and the false bottom lifted like a page turning.

Inside lay a letter wrapped in waxed silk. The wax seal was unbroken, deep rose-red, stamped with a bloom she recognized now from a hundred fragments and fragments of fragments: the seal of Malaya. On the silk, in her mother's careful hand—or in Elara Nakamura's careful hand, Seng corrected herself, because the distinction mattered tonight—two lines in the old script, and beneath them, smaller, in English:

*To the keepers of the river's memory. From a friend who stayed.*

Seng knelt there a long time, the letter untouched in her palms, the hum of the lights filling her skull. She had burned her orders to protect this archive. She had crossed every line her career had drawn for her. And still the bureau's machinery had kept one last secret from her, folded in lead, ten centimeters from a grieving daughter's inheritance.

The letter was not hers to open. She understood that with total clarity, the way one understands the depth of water by the pressure in the ears. There was exactly one person alive to whom it belonged, and that person was currently upriver, in a village no map admitted to, learning the names of the erased.

Seng wrapped the letter back in its silk, closed the chest, and began, already, to compose the message she would send upstream—one line only, because some news should travel light:

*Come down when the river allows. Your mother left something for the elders. It's waited eleven years. So has the truth of how she died.*

Outside, far to the north, the first thunder of the returning monsoon rolled down the valleys toward the sea, and the rivers began, everywhere at once, to rise.
