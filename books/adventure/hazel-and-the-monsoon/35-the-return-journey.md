## 35. The Return Journey

The river did not remember us. That was the first thing I understood, standing on the delta dock at dawn with the letter sealed in oilcloth against my chest, watching the brown water slide past the pilings without so much as a ripple of recognition. Rivers don't remember. They only carry. Everything else—memory, grief, the names of the erased—is the work of people.

But going up felt different anyway.

I had made this journey once in fear, running from a bureau boat and a broker's smile, measuring every mile behind us against the miles still ahead, counting like a woman paying down a debt. Now the same water ran the other way through my blood. Bao stood at his tiller—the same tiller, on the same patched and stubborn old ferry he'd sworn was done for after the rapids—and steered us into the current with the unhurried certainty of a man returning to a house he'd left that morning rather than one he'd left forty years ago.

"You're smiling," Pip said, suspicious, the way she was suspicious of anything soft.

"I'm not."

"Your face is doing it anyway."

I turned my face to the wind and let her have it. Fourteen-year-olds win arguments by attrition; I had learned that much on this river.

* * *

We had come down out of the hills three weeks earlier, four of us and a prisoner we no longer thought of as a prisoner, and the descent had been all urgency—falling water levels, drying channels, the race to hand Voss into Seng's custody before the delta strangled itself in sandbars. We had run the river like thieves fleeing a scene. Now, provisioned properly for the first time—real rope, real rice, a new outboard Jun had haggled over with the shameless delight of a man who had spent twelve years unable to buy so much as a pencil without justifying it to a licensing board—we went back the way we'd come, and the river unspooled ahead of us like something being read slowly for the second time, when you finally notice what the words were saying.

Bao sat propped against the wheel housing with his arm in a sling he refused to acknowledge, steering one-handed out of pure orneriness. The shoulder was knitting. The doctor in the capital had said six weeks; Bao had said two, and then informed the doctor that he had been dislocating things since before the doctor's grandfather learned to swim, and that bones, like rivers, found their own way home if you didn't fuss at them. He was mostly right. He was always mostly right, which was more than could be said for any of us.

He had changed since the high trail. Not healed—changed. There was a slowness in him now that hadn't been there before, and I don't mean weakness. I mean he had begun telling stories without being asked. On the first night out of the capital, anchored off a mudbank while the mosquitoes sang their thin silver hymns, he told us about my mother—not the disgraced archaeologist, not the name the bureau had dragged through the mud, but a young woman thirty years ago who had paid him double to run her upstream at night and then fallen asleep in his boat with her notebook open on her chest, and how he had read three pages before deciding he would never tell anyone what she was looking for, because some things you protect before you even know why.

"I decided then," he said, looking at the fire instead of at me, "that if she ever came back, I would take her anywhere she asked."

"She never came back," I said.

"No," Bao said. "She sent you." And he smiled at the fire, and I excused myself to check the anchor line, and stayed there checking it for a long time, because the river at night is a kind thing to cry beside—it makes its own noise, and takes its own share of salt.

* * *

Pip had stopped stealing things.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters to her: she had not stopped *being able* to steal things. On our second morning she lifted Jun's tobacco pouch, transferred its contents into an empty tea tin, returned the empty pouch to his pocket, and presented him with the tin at lunch, all without Jun noticing until she explained it to him, twice, with diagrams drawn in spilled rice. It was a demonstration, not a relapse. She was keeping her hand in, the way a musician runs scales. But the compulsive taking—the hungry little thefts she used to commit just to prove the world could be moved by her fingers alone—those had gone quiet somewhere between the gorge and the village, and they hadn't come back.

She sat at the bow now, most days, feet hooked under the rail, mending nets with a speed that embarrassed me, and she talked. She talked about the Kaeli children who had taught her their skipping games. She talked about Elder Suri's hands, how they moved when the old woman spoke Malayan, like birds settling. She talked about whether the village would remember her, whether the kids would have made up new games in the weeks she'd been gone, whether she'd have to relearn everything, and whether that would mean she'd been forgotten, and whether being forgotten was worse than never having been known at all.

I recognized the question. I had carried it for twelve years in a brass compass case.

"They'll remember," I said.

"You don't know that."

"I know what it's like to think someone forgot you," I said, "and find out they were building you a map the whole time." I nodded at the bow where she sat. "People keep places for each other. Even when they're bad at saying so. Especially then."

Pip considered this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to everything, then said, "That's the saddest nice thing anyone's ever said to me," and went back to her netting, and I let her have the last word, because fourteen-year-olds win those too.

* * *

Jun navigated by stars he had no license to use.

That was his joke, and he made it every clear night, standing at the stern with his sextant—an actual brass sextant, bought in the capital with money he pretended not to be gleeful about—and calling out corrections to Bao in the tone of a man reading scripture. His license had been restored, formally, in a ceremony he described as "eleven minutes and an apology written by someone who has never been sorry in their life." He had accepted it. He had also, I noticed, not framed it. When I asked why, he was quiet for a long moment, turning his coffee cup in his hands.

"A license says the state permits me to draw maps," he said finally. "It doesn't say the maps are true. Those are different permissions. One comes from a ministry." He looked up at the sky, at the same stars his grandfather had taught him over a country that no longer existed on paper. "The other one I already have."

He was redrawing Malaya. That was the work of his evenings now—a great sheet of waterproof paper spread across the deck boards, weighted with engine parts and teacups, growing outward from memory: coastlines, trade routes, the names of towns he'd gathered from archives and ferry captains and the songs the Kaeli had sung for us, each name inked in his small precise hand. When it was finished it would go to the elders, the first map of their nation drawn by an outsider in a century, and the first ever drawn *for* them rather than *against* them.

"Some nights I can't sleep," he admitted to me once, in the blue hour before dawn, "because I'm afraid I've remembered a river wrong. A bend, a confluence. And then I think—" he laughed, short and helpless, "—I think, Jun, you spent fifteen years being punished for insisting this place existed. You are allowed to be afraid of getting it wrong. Fear is just love with its sleeves rolled up."

"That's terrible," I said.

"It's the truest thing I know," he said, "and I am a cartographer. Terrible truths are my entire profession."

* * *

We passed the drowned village on the sixth day.

The reservoir had dropped with the season, and where before we had slipped between rooftops, now the whole skeleton of the place stood exposed in the shallows—doorframes, a wellhead, the arch of a shrine rising black from the receded water like ribs. The old woman was still there. Of course she was. She waved us in with the imperiousness of someone who had been waiting, and fed us fish stew on her porch, which was now a hilltop, and asked Bao questions in that half-remembered Malayan of hers, and laughed at his answers, and made Pip show her the knife trick twice.

Before we left she took my wrist in her small dry grip and held it, harder than I expected, and looked at me with eyes gone pale as river fog.

"You're going home," she said. Not a question. In her mouth it wasn't even a prediction. It was a diagnosis.

"We're delivering a letter," I said.

"Mm." She released my wrist and patted it once, the way you pat a child or a dog or a fact you've confirmed. "The river knows the difference. So do you."

I didn't answer. Out on the water, waiting for the others, I said it aloud to myself, testing it against the current: homecoming. The word fit badly, like a coat cut for someone else. I had spent twelve years making sure I owned nothing that could own me back—no lease, no berth, no name I answered to. Homecoming was for people who had somewhere. And yet the letter against my chest had a weight that wasn't paper, and every mile upstream the weight grew warmer, and I began to suspect that home is not a place you leave. It's a direction you learn to read.

* * *

At the bureau checkpoint, the young officer waved us through before Bao had finished cutting the engine.

That was new. On the way up, this same post had been sandbags and suspicion, a boom across the channel and Seng's inspectors boarding us with clipboards and cold eyes. Now the boom hung open, and the officer leaned out of his hut, squinting at Bao, and called, "Ferryman! You're going up again? In the dry season?"

"Delivering mail," Bao said.

The officer looked at me—at me specifically, and I braced for the photo, the questions, the particular bureaucratic hunger of a man who has been told your face matters. Instead he touched two fingers to his cap brim and said, "Safe passage," and turned back to his radio.

Later, anchored below the karst, I dug out the crumpled note Seng had pressed into my hand at the delta dock, the one I hadn't let myself read until we were beyond the last patrol route. It was brief, in her clipped official hand:

*Travel documents filed under scientific survey. No one will stop you. This is not a favor. It is the minimum the bureau owes your mother, and it is not nearly enough, and I know that. — A.S.*

Below that, smaller, as if added after a struggle:

*Tell them the records are safe. Tell them I checked personally.*

I read it three times by lamplight and then folded it away next to the letter, and lay in the dark listening to the water slap the hull, thinking about a woman who had spent her career inside the machine that broke my mother, and who had chosen, at the threshold of the archive, to burn her orders instead. There were kinds of courage I had names for. Hers I was still learning the word for. I suspected it was the quietest kind, and the hardest: the courage of staying inside the thing that hurt you, and changing it from within, one burned order at a time.

* * *

The karst rose around us on the eighth day, green towers dripping in the heat haze, and the channel narrowed, and the current strengthened, and Bao killed the outboard and shipped the oars because the water here deserved to be entered politely.

I knew this stretch. My body knew it before my mind caught up—the particular smell of the deep pools, cold stone and wet fern; the sound the river made falling over the first hidden shelf, a long exhaled hush like a crowd settling. This was where Voss had boarded us in the dark. This was where Pip had gone over the side, and I had gone in after her, and the river had held us both in its cold green fist and, for reasons I still didn't fully understand, given us back.

Pip stood at the bow and pointed at the water. "Right there," she said. "That's where you fished me out. You looked absolutely terrified."

"I did."

"You're not allowed to look terrified. You're the adult."

"I'm reconsidering the arrangement," I said, and she grinned, and the grin did something to my chest that I was learning not to flinch from.

We camped that night in the lee of the cliffs, and none of us slept well, though none of us would say why. The letter was close now. Two days, maybe three, up the fisherman's trail and over the shoulder of the mountain, down into the valley the maps had erased and the mist kept. The letter my mother had written to the Kaeli elders, sealed years ago, carried through fire and theft and a man's treachery, recovered from the wreckage of Voss's hidden cache by Seng's patient record-keepers—her last words to the people she had chosen over the world.

And beneath it, in the oilcloth, folded flat, another page. Mine. The entry my mother had written the week she died, the one I had read aloud to the crew in the archive with my voice breaking on every third word. I had promised myself I wouldn't reread it on the journey. I reread it every night. *My daughter dives like the water is glad to hold her,* it said, in part. *If she ever reads this: I was not hiding from you. I was trying to build a world worth bringing you into. I ran out of time. Forgive me the miles.*

Forgive me the miles.

I lay awake under the unfamiliar stars of the highlands and understood, finally, what the old woman at the drowned village had seen in my wrist. Twelve years I had measured my life in river miles because miles couldn't disappoint you. Miles didn't die. Miles didn't write letters you were too proud and too hurt to answer. But the river had been carrying me the whole time, patiently, the way it carries everything—silt and seeds and the drowned and the living—toward the sea it never stops believing in.

Going up, the current fought us. Going home, it only slowed us down enough to notice where we were.

* * *

On the tenth day we reached the foot of the mountain, and Bao grounded the ferry on the shingle bar below the fisherman's trail, and we unloaded in silence: rice and medicine and Jun's rolled map, and the oilcloth packet with its two letters, riding against my sternum like a second heartbeat.

Bao lowered himself onto a boulder, cradling his healing shoulder, and looked up at the cloud forest swallowing the trail above. "I carried your mother up this path," he said. "Thirty-one years ago. She weighed nothing. Talked the whole way." He smiled at the trees. "You weigh more. You'll talk less. It balances out."

"Can you make the climb?" I asked. "Your shoulder—"

"My shoulder," Bao said with tremendous dignity, "has been consulted, and it says the alternative is waiting at the bottom like luggage." He levered himself up. "Besides. Someone has to introduce you properly. The last time, I brought them an archaeologist." He started up the trail, one-handed, stubborn as the current. "This time I bring them family."

Pip fell in beside me, her torn-and-healed feet finding the stones with a sureness that would have been impossible a season ago. Jun settled the map roll across his back like a pilgrim's burden. Above us the mist parted for a moment, and I saw the falls—white thread in green shadow, the sound reaching us faintly, like something calling from very far away and very near.

The letter pressed warm against my chest.

Halfway up the first switchback, deep in the dripping green, we heard it: voices, ahead of us on the trail, singing. A walking song in Malayan, high and bright, growing closer through the trees.

Pip grabbed my sleeve. "Oscar. Do you hear—"

"I hear it," I said.

They came around the bend as we climbed toward them—the Kaeli children, half a dozen of them, led by a girl with Suri's same unshakeable eyes—and the girl stopped dead at the sight of us, and her mouth opened, and she shouted one word down the mountainside, a word I didn't know yet but would soon, a word that meant *they've come back*, and somewhere below us in the hidden valley, I felt the whole village turn toward the sound like a field of flowers turning toward the sun.

Bao laughed, wheezing, delighted. "Told you," he said. "They keep places."

And the children came running up the trail to meet us, and the mist closed over our heads, and we went home.
