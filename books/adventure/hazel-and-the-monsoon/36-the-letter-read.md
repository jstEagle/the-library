## 36. The Letter Read

The letter had traveled farther than any of them.

That was the thought Oscar kept returning to as she sat in the longhouse of the Kaeli, morning light falling through the woven bamboo walls in thin gold stripes, the sealed paper resting on her open palm. It had been written by a woman who knew she was dying, carried out of these hills hidden inside a man's guilt, buried for years in a broker's strongbox, and now it had come home up the same river its author had once climbed. Paper was patient that way. Paper could wait out shame, out of fear, out of twelve years of a daughter's silence.

The elders had gathered at dawn. Not all of them—Suri had kept it small, the way you keep a fire small when the wind is uncertain. Seven faces, old and older, seated on mats worn smooth by generations. The smell of woodsmoke and lemongrass hung in the air, and somewhere outside a woman was singing to a child, a low looping melody with no words Oscar recognized but a rhythm like water over stones.

Voss had surrendered the letter two days ago at the delta, through Seng, through a chain of custody Oscar hadn't trusted until she'd held it herself and felt the weight of it—lighter than the compass, heavier than the chest. Her mother's handwriting on the outside, just four words in Malayan script that Jun had translated with his voice gone rough:

*For the keepers. Urgent.*

"You do not have to read it aloud," Suri said. She sat across from Oscar, small and straight-backed, her hands folded like closed doors. "Some letters are only for one pair of eyes."

Oscar looked down at the seal—a thumbprint of wax pressed with the same mark that had been stamped into the lead chest a lifetime ago, the river-and-mountain sigil of a country that no longer existed on any map. Her thumbnail found the edge of it.

"I think," Oscar said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "she wrote it for more than one pair of eyes."

Jun shifted beside her. Pip, cross-legged on the mat at Oscar's other side, had gone very still—the stillness of a girl who had learned that some moments you don't move through, you hold. Even Bao, propped against the central post with his shoulder still bound from the rapids, had stopped pretending to nap.

Oscar broke the seal.

* * *

The paper inside was thin, the kind that rustled like insect wings, and her mother's handwriting filled both sides in the tight, slanting script Oscar had spent a childhood watching move across field notebooks by lamplight. She read it first silently, the way you test water with one foot. Then, because the room was waiting and because some things are not meant to be carried alone, she began to read aloud.

*"To the elders of the Kaeli, and to whoever keeps the archive when I am gone—"*

Her own voice sounded strange saying the words, as if her mother had stepped into the room and borrowed her throat.

*"I write this knowing I may not be permitted to leave these hills again. The bureau has my name on a list now, and men like Voss have my maps, and I have made my choices with my eyes open. I regret none of them except the ones I made at home."*

Oscar paused there. Outside, the river spoke against the stones, patient, insistent.

*"You took me in when I came asking questions I had no right to ask, and you answered them anyway, because you are kinder than the world has been to you. You showed me what remembering looks like when an entire world wants you to forget. I will not betray you. I would die before I let them burn what you have kept. But dying is not the same as being finished, and so I must ask one more thing of you, and it is this:"*

Oscar's hands were not steady. Pip's hand found hers without ceremony, small and calloused, and stayed.

*"If my daughter ever finds her way here—and I have left her the means, though she may hate me for how I left them—take her in. Not as a keeper of secrets. Not as payment for my silence or a reward for her courage. Take her in as herself. She will arrive believing she is unlovable, because I taught her that by leaving, and she will arrive measuring everything, because I never taught her how to stop. Be patient with her. She is better than she knows. She has her father's stubbornness and my worst habits and a heart she has spent her whole life armoring, and if you feed her and ask nothing, she will stay longer than she means to."*

A sound escaped Oscar—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else—and she pressed on because stopping would have been drowning.

*"I did not give her the choice I am giving you. I decided for her, before she was born, what her life would be about: the work, the rivers, the past. By the time I understood what I had done, she was already watching me pack. So let this be the correction. Whatever she chooses when she stands among you—stay, go, burn the journal, publish it, forget us all—that choice is hers, whole and unburdened. If she goes, tell her nothing was ever owed. If she stays, tell her she was always welcome. And either way, tell her the thing I could never make myself say while I was alive: that I was proud of her every single day, even the days she hated me, especially those, because hating me meant she was still fighting to be someone other than my shadow."*

*"The river gave me everything I understood about love. Water does not hold on. It holds what passes through it, and lets it pass, and is not diminished. I failed to learn that lesson in time for her. Perhaps you can teach it to her instead."*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The handwriting was the signature; the handwriting had always been the signature, slanting leftward like something leaning into wind.

The last lines were smaller, cramped against the bottom margin as though the writer had run out of paper and time in equal measure:

*"She dives beautifully. Tell her I watched her learn."*

* * *

The longhouse was silent for a long moment after Oscar lowered the page. Somewhere in the rafters a bird resettled itself. The singing woman outside had stopped, or moved on, and in its absence the river filled the world.

Oscar became aware, distantly, that her face was wet. She didn't wipe it. Twelve years ago she had made a private law about this—no tears, not for her, not where anyone could see—and the law had held through the funeral, through the disgrace, through every riverport and salvage job and cold rented room since. It had held right up until a fourteen-year-old pickpocket put her hand in hers, and now it lay in pieces on the mat, and Oscar found she could not muster the will to enforce it.

"She watched me learn," Oscar said. Her voice cracked in the middle like ice underfoot. "I learned to dive in the flooded quarry behind our house. I was seven. I thought—I thought she wasn't even looking. She was reading her notes. I remember being furious about it."

"She was always reading her notes," Bao said softly. His eyes were wet too, and he made no apology for it. "And she was always watching. That woman could run two rivers at once, your mother. One on the surface, one underneath."

Elder Suri rose. It took her a moment—age had bent her like a reed bends, not broken but curved—but when she stood she seemed somehow taller than the room. She crossed to where Oscar sat and lowered herself down beside her, joints cracking, and took the letter from Oscar's hands with a reverence usually reserved for relics.

"We read this letter once before," Suri said. "The night your mother died. We gathered in this room, in this light, and one of ours who had learned the reading of it spoke it aloud to us, because she had asked us to. And then we did what she asked us to do." The old woman's eyes, clouded at the edges, found Oscar's and held them. "We waited."

"Twelve years," Oscar said.

"The river is patient. So are we." Suri folded the letter along its original creases, careful as a surgeon. "But patience is not passivity, child. Every year we sent word downriver, quietly, through traders and ferrymen and one very unreliable bird-seller, asking after a girl named Nakamura who dove wrecks and never stayed anywhere longer than a season. Every year the answer came back: no trace. You were good at not being found. Your mother trained you well, though I suspect she'd have preferred you easier to find."

"I changed my name," Oscar said. The admission came out before she could weigh it. "After the funeral. Hazel was—her name for me. I couldn't carry it. So I became Oscar, and I went to the water, because the water was the only place the grief got quiet."

"And now?"

"Now I'm not sure which one of us is the disguise."

Suri smiled then, and it transformed her face the way light transforms water—suddenly deep, suddenly moving. She turned to face the other elders, and something passed between them, that wordless consultation of people who have made decisions together for longer than most nations last.

Then she turned back, and she took both of Oscar's hands in hers, and her grip was stronger than her frame suggested.

"Hazel Nakamura," she said, and the name went through Oscar like a hook through water. "Daughter of Lin Nakamura, diver and scholar, friend of the Kaeli, keeper of her word unto death. Hear what we offer, and hear also what we do not offer, because the second part matters more."

The longhouse had gone absolutely still. Even the bird had opinions enough to keep.

"We offer you a place among us. A house when you want one, a seat at this fire, a share in the keeping of the archive if you ever wish it, and a family that has survived erasure and intends to survive everything else. We offer it freely, with no debt attached and no term set. Come for a season or a lifetime. Leave and return. The door does not lock from either side."

"And what you do not offer," Suri continued, her voice dropping into something almost fierce, "is obligation. Your mother was explicit, and we honor her in this above all else. You owe us nothing. Not your silence, not your service, not your grief. If you walk out of this village tomorrow and never return, the offer remains standing, undiminished, for the rest of your life. Belief that must be earned daily is not belief. Belonging that can be revoked is not belonging. We know the difference. Erased peoples learn the difference very young."

Oscar tried to speak and found her throat had closed. She tried again.

"Why?" It was the question under every other question, the one she'd been carrying since the flooded temple, since the chest, since the compass. "Why would you want me? You don't know me. You knew her."

"Child." Suri's thumbs pressed gently against the backs of Oscar's hands. "Twelve years ago a woman came up this river carrying nothing but questions and a broken heart, and we took her in, and in the months she was given to us she mended nets, taught our children to swim, argued philosophy with our elders until midnight, and cried exactly twice—both times over a letter she couldn't send. We knew her for one season. We loved her for it. And everything she told us of you—" the old woman's voice caught, briefly, "—everything, we have watched confirmed in the days since you arrived. The way you check the moorings twice. The way you fed Bao's fever tea before you ate yourself. The way you look at Pip when she isn't watching, like a woman counting something precious she's afraid to jostle. We do not need to know you. We recognize you."

Pip made a small sound beside her, quickly swallowed. Jun had removed his spectacles and was polishing them with great concentration, which was what he did instead of weeping. Bao was grinning at the ceiling beams like a man who had bet on the right horse a long time ago.

"I don't know how to stay," Oscar said. It was the truest sentence she had spoken in twelve years. "I've built my whole life around leaving. I'm good at it. It's the one thing I never had to be taught."

"Then you will learn," Suri said simply, "the way you learned to dive. Badly at first. Then less badly. The water teaches everyone eventually—it only asks that you stop fighting it." She released Oscar's hands and rose, and the formality returned to her bearing like a robe drawn back on. "You do not answer today. You do not answer this week. The offer is a stone in the river, not a fish—it does not swim away. Think. Dive. Argue with us at midnight if it helps. But know that whatever you decide, you leave here as family, and you return here as family, and there is no version of the rest of your life in which that is not true."

* * *

They walked down to the water afterward, the four of them, because the longhouse had become too full of feeling to breathe in.

The river ran high and green-gold in the late morning, swollen with the returning rains, and the village moved around its banks with the unhurried industry of people who had never once doubted the water would provide. Children raced leaf boats in the shallows. An old man mended a net with fingers that moved faster than his eyes. Smoke rose from cookfires, carrying the smell of rice and river fish, and somewhere upstream the falls spoke their endless low syllables.

Bao settled onto the mooring post of the repaired boat with the groan of a man whose shoulder had opinions, and looked out at the current with an expression Oscar had learned to read as profound contentment.

"Your mother stood right there," he said, nodding at a flat stone near the bank. "First day I brought her up. Looked at the falls for a long time and said, 'Well, Bao. Now I understand why they hid.'" He chuckled. "Then she tripped on that rock and nearly went in headfirst. Scholarly types. No footing sense."

Pip laughed, and the sound startled a flock of birds out of the fig tree, and for a moment Oscar just stood in the middle of it—the laughter, the birds, the smoke, the water—and let it happen to her.

Jun came to stand at her elbow. He was holding his notebook, the one full of redrawn coastlines and resurrected borders, and he tapped it against his palm thoughtfully.

"For what it's worth," he said, "and I know my opinion was purchased with blackmail and cheap whiskey, I think you should take it."

"Take what?"

"The place. The offer. All of it." He adjusted his spectacles, watching the children's leaf boats race the current. "I spent ten years insisting a dead nation existed, and the whole world called me a liar, and the loneliest part wasn't the disgrace. It was knowing the thing I loved was real and having nowhere to stand beside it. You've been given what I begged the sky for." He glanced at her, wry and gentle. "Don't make my mistake. Don't wait for permission from people who aren't coming."

Oscar looked at him for a long moment. "When did you get wise?"

"About the same time I got old. They travel together. Terrible package deal."

Pip appeared at her other side, and Oscar braced herself, because the girl had that look—the look she got before asking the questions nobody else would ask.

"So," Pip said, kicking at a pebble with elaborate casualness. "They said you don't have to answer yet. But I've been thinking. About seasons." She scuffed the pebble into the water. "You always leave at the end of one. That's your rule. And this season's going to end soon, probably, when the journal goes to press and everything. So I was just wondering—which season are you counting?"

Oscar opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because the honest answer was that she didn't know, and the more honest answer was that for the first time in twelve years, the not-knowing didn't feel like freedom. It felt like a door standing open in a house she was finally allowed to call home, and the terrifying, wonderful truth was that walking through it wouldn't cost her anything at all.

"I'll tell you when I figure it out," she said.

Pip studied her with those quick dark eyes, then nodded slowly, satisfied in the way of a negotiator who knows the deal isn't closed but the terms have shifted. "Okay," she said. "But not too many seasons. I'm just saying. Some of us have plans that involve you."

She bounded off toward the net-menders, all elbows and momentum, and Oscar watched her go and felt the strange new weight settle into her chest—not heavy, exactly. Anchored.

Behind her, the falls went on speaking. Ahead, the river ran down out of sight toward the lowlands, toward the capital, toward presses and headlines and a world about to learn a dead nation's name. And here, in between, was a flat stone where her mother had once tripped, and a fire with a seat saved at it, and a letter in an elder's keeping that said, in a dead woman's slanting hand: *take her in as herself.*

Herself. As if that had been a place she could go all along, and she'd simply never had the map.

Oscar turned back toward the village, toward the smoke and the voices and the longhouse where an old woman waited with no demands and all the time in the world—and stopped.

At the edge of the clearing, half-hidden by the fig tree, stood Elder Suri's youngest granddaughter, a girl of perhaps eight, holding something out in both hands with the solemnity of a state occasion.

It was a diving mask. Old style, cracked at the temple strap, glass fogged with age. Oscar knew it instantly, the way you know your own reflection—she had seen it in a photograph once, in the back pages of the journal, strapped to a young woman's forehead on the deck of a research barge thirty years gone.

"It was your mother's," the girl said, in careful trade-tongue. "Grandmother says it was waiting for you. She says—" the girl frowned, concentrating, delivering the message exactly as instructed, "—she says the river kept it safe, and now the river says it's yours, and Grandmother says you should come see what's scratched inside the strap."

Oscar took the mask with hands that trembled only a little, and turned it over, and there, worn faint into the rubber where only a diver's fingers would find it, were three small words in her mother's hand.

She read them once. Then again. Then she looked up at the longhouse, at the fire, at the open door—and started walking.
