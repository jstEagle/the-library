## 37. Staying Is the Hard Part

Pip had stolen from priests, soldiers, and one very angry monkey, but she had never once been afraid the way she was afraid now.

It was a different kind of fear. Not the sharp kind that came with a patrol boat's searchlight or a hand closing on your wrist in a market crowd—that fear you could run from, and running was half the fun anyway. This one sat low in her stomach like swallowed river water, cold and heavy, and it didn't come from any danger she could name. It came from a morning.

Because that was the thing about mornings here. Every morning since they'd arrived at the village, Pip woke to the same sounds—Bao coughing by the fire, Jun scratching his pen, the Kaeli children laughing at the water's edge—and every morning she counted them. Not out loud. She wasn't stupid. But somewhere in the back of her head there was a tally, the way there had always been a tally, because in fourteen years of living off other people's boats and other people's kindness she had learned one law above all others: *everything ends*. Markets end. Seasons end. Crews end. The trick was never to be the one who got left standing on the dock watching the wake spread out behind somebody else's boat.

And this morning, when she'd woken, she had heard Oscar talking to Elder Suri by the longhouse, their voices low, and she had caught only three words before her whole body went cold.

*"...when I leave..."*

Maybe it meant nothing. Maybe Oscar had been talking about leaving for firewood. But Pip knew how people talked when they were already halfway gone. She'd heard it her whole life—the future tense creeping into people's voices like water into a hull, *next season*, *downriver*, *when this is over*—and she had spent two days telling herself she was wrong, and the telling hadn't worked, and so on the third morning she did what she always did when a thing hurt too much to look at straight.

She ran.

Not far. There was nowhere far to run to; the village clung to its shelf of stone above the falls like a barnacle, and beyond it the cloud forest swallowed everything. But there was one place nobody went after dark unless they had business with the spirits, and that was the ledge above the waterfall where the warning stones stood in their rows of carved Malayan script, and that was where Pip went, barefoot, taking the fisherman's trail in the blue hour before dawn, because if the world was going to end again—if Oscar was going to fold up like a chart and slip away the way everyone did—Pip wanted at least to be the first to know, and to be somewhere the knowing couldn't find her.

The falls were loud at night. Louder than anything. You didn't hear them so much as feel them, a drumming up through the soles of your feet, as if the whole mountain had a pulse and it lived in the water. Mist hung over the ledge in ropes. The warning stones rose black against the grey, and Pip sat down among them, hugging her knees, and told herself she wasn't crying, because river kids didn't cry, and then cried anyway, quietly, the way she did everything.

That was how Oscar found her an hour later. Of course it was. Pip had taught herself to be invisible in six cities and four riverports, and Oscar had found her inside of an hour, which meant either that Pip was losing her touch or that Oscar had learned to read her, and both possibilities were unbearable.

"You left your boots," Oscar said.

"I don't need boots."

"The trail says otherwise. Your feet are still healing."

"My feet are fine." They were not fine. The high trail had taken the skin off them in ribbons, and the Kaeli healer's salve smelled like rotting flowers and worked like a miracle, but they still stung on wet stone, and Oscar knew it, and Pip knew she knew it, and neither of them said so.

Oscar didn't sit down. She stood at the edge of the ledge with her hands in her pockets, looking at the falls the way she looked at water before a dive—measuring it, reading it, the way other people read faces. The mist beaded in her hair. Behind her, far below, the river gathered itself white and threw itself off the lip of the world, and the sound filled up all the space where words should have been.

"Elder Suri asked me something last night," Oscar said finally.

"I don't care."

"She asked me where I'll go. After."

Pip pulled her knees tighter. "I said I don't care."

"I know you said that." Oscar turned. In the pre-dawn grey her face was hard to read, which was unusual, because usually Pip could read her like one of Jun's charts—every current marked, every depth sounded. "You're allowed to not care. But I'm going to tell you anyway, because Bao says I've spent twelve years saying things by not saying them, and he claims it's a disease."

"Is it curable?"

"He seems to think so." Oscar crouched down, slow, the way you'd approach a dog that might bite or might just be scared, until she was level with Pip, close enough that Pip could see the mist caught in her eyelashes. "Suri offered me a place here. Among the Kaeli. A real place, with a real answer expected."

"And what did you say?"

"I said I needed to talk to my crew first."

Pip's breath caught on the word. *Crew.* She covered it by scowling. "I'm cargo. Bao said the river decides its passengers. I'm just cargo that steals things."

"You're cargo that saved us in the gorge," Oscar said. "Cargo that cut fuse lines while men were shooting at her. Cargo that carried water up the high trail for two days when Bao couldn't walk. If that's cargo, then I'm cargo too, and so is Jun, and so is Bao, and we are the most heavily armed shipment of cargo this river has ever seen."

Despite herself—despite everything—Pip felt her mouth twitch. Then she killed the smile, because smiling would mean hoping, and hoping was the one theft she'd never been able to pull off without getting hurt.

"You were talking about leaving," she said. "I heard you. *When I leave.*"

Oscar was quiet for a moment. Below them the falls thundered on, patient as the earth.

"I say that," she said. "I've been saying it my whole life. It's how I keep the world at arm's length—I'm always already going, so nothing can take me by surprise when I go. My mother used to do it too. Or maybe I learned it from watching her leave for expeditions. Either way—" she exhaled, and the exhale seemed to carry years with it, "—it's a habit, Pip. Like biting your nails. And habits lie. They make you think they're who you are."

"They're not?"

"They're not." Oscar reached into her shirt and drew out the brass compass—the battered one, the one that had started everything, its faceplate scarred, its needle swinging lazy and true. She held it between them in her open palm. "You stole this twice, you know. I counted."

Pip's ears went hot. "I put it back."

"Both times. Within the hour. Do you know why I never said anything?"

"Because you didn't notice?"

"Because the second time, I watched you do it. You took it out of my bag, held it, and put it back exactly where it was, and then you sat there looking at it like—" Oscar stopped, and started again. "Like it was the only home anyone had ever let you near. And I thought: she's not stealing it. She's checking it's still there. Because things that stay are the things she's never been able to trust."

Pip stared at the compass. Its needle trembled in the mist-wind, pointing stubbornly north, or what passed for north out here where every map ended.

"That's not true," she said, in the smallest voice she owned.

"No," Oscar agreed gently. "But it took me thirty-seven years and one drowned temple to learn what's true instead. So listen."

She closed her hand around the compass and stood, and held out her other hand to Pip, and Pip, hating herself, wanting desperately not to, took it. Her hand was small and quick and calloused from rope and lockpicks; Oscar's was broad and scarred from line and stone, and together they walked back along the ledge toward the village, toward the smoke of the morning fires beginning to rise, toward the sound of Jun arguing with two Kaeli children about the proper way to draw a coastline.

By the time they reached the longhouse, the crew had assembled without being summoned—Bao sitting wrapped in his blanket with his shoulder still bound, Jun holding his pen like a scepter, Elder Suri standing at the edge of the group with her hands folded, watching with eyes that had seen a century of people arrive and leave. The whole village, nearly. Word traveled fast in a place built on keeping secrets.

Oscar stopped in front of them all. She looked terrified, Pip realized. Actually terrified, more than at the falls fight, more than under Voss's gun—and that frightened Pip worse than anything, because if Oscar was scared, then whatever she was about to say was real.

"I've spent twelve years measuring my life in miles," Oscar said. Her voice carried over the drum of the falls. "River miles. How far from the last place, how far to the next. It was easier than counting anything else—years, losses, people. Miles don't ask anything of you." She looked down at Pip. "Then a pickpocket stowed away on my boat and stole my compass twice and gave it back twice, and a disgraced mapmaker blackmailed his way aboard, and an old man who ferried my mother made me answer a question I'd dodged for twelve years, and somewhere between the delta and this mountain I stopped counting miles. I started counting something else."

"What?" Pip asked. Her voice cracked on it.

"People," Oscar said. "I count the people beside me. And I'm done drifting. I'm done with the rule of never staying longer than a season. Suri has offered me a place here, and I'm going to take it—not as a keeper of secrets, but as myself. And if the rest of you will have me—" her voice did something complicated, "—then I'd like to stop running. For good. That's the promise. Out loud, in front of witnesses, so none of you can ever pretend you didn't hear it."

Bao grunted, which from Bao was applause. Jun wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and pretended he hadn't. Elder Suri smiled like the sun coming up over water.

And Pip—Pip who had run to the falls to grieve in advance, Pip who had spent fourteen years learning that everything ends—felt the cold thing in her stomach crack and come apart, and what rushed in behind it was warm and terrifying and new.

"So you're staying," she said. "Here? With us?"

"With you," Oscar said. "Wherever the river goes. Together. That's the whole plan. It's not much of a plan."

"It's the worst plan I've ever heard," Pip said, and launched herself at Oscar, and hugged her hard enough to hurt, and felt arms close around her that were not going anywhere.

Above them the monsoon clouds were gathering again over the peaks, fat and dark and full of the season's first rain, and far downstream, in the world of maps and bureaus and printed names, something was stirring that none of them yet knew about—a letter, sealed and waiting, that would send them back up this river one more time before the year was out.

But that was tomorrow's water. Tonight, for the first time in her life, Pip unpacked everything she owned, and left it unpacked.
