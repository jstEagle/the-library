## 38. The Monsoon Returns

The first sign was not the rain itself but the smell of it—wet stone and crushed leaves and something older underneath, the way a house smells when you open a window that has been shut for years. Oscar woke before dawn in the guest hut with that smell in her nostrils and her heart already beating too fast, the way it always did at the start of monsoon season, twelve years of lowland seasons teaching her body what her mind had tried to forget: that water was coming, and everything would change.

She stepped outside into a silence so complete she could hear the river breathing.

The sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of a bruise healing—deep violet shading into green—and the air hung heavy as wet wool against her skin. Down in the village, the Kaeli were already moving. She could see them by lamplight, carrying things up from the lower terraces: nets, grain jars, the small shrines that lived near the waterline most of the year and had to be carried to higher ground when the river came home. No one hurried. That was the thing that struck her every time, the thing she still hadn't learned after weeks among them. They moved like people who trusted the water. Like people greeting an old friend who sometimes broke your furniture.

Bao sat on the terrace above the guest huts, wrapped in two blankets despite the heat, his shoulder finally out of its sling though he still favored it when he thought no one was watching. He was watching the sky the way other men watched television.

"You feel it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Feel like I'm nine years old again," Oscar said, sitting beside him. "My mother used to wake me before the first storm of the season. Every year. Dragged me out of bed half-asleep, made me stand on the porch with my arms out until it hit."

"To teach you respect."

"To teach me joy, I think. Respect came later." She turned her hands over, looked at the calluses, the old rope scars, the white line where the coral had opened her forearm off Sattahip. "I forgot the joy part somewhere along the way."

Bao made a sound in his chest, half laugh, half cough. "You didn't forget. You buried it. There's a difference. Buried things come back up. The river taught me that too."

Above them, the first drops came down through the canopy—fat, warm, deliberate, spaced far apart like a drummer counting in. One struck the terrace stones and spread into a dark coin. Another hit the back of Oscar's neck and ran down her spine, and she shivered with something that wasn't cold.

Then the sky opened.

It did not begin raining; it arrived, all at once, a wall of water falling out of the dark, and the world disappeared into noise. Oscar stood up and put her arms out, and for a moment she was nine years old on a concrete porch in a town whose name she'd let go blurry, laughing without meaning to, water hammering her shoulders, her hair plastered flat, Bao's laughter somewhere beside her lost inside the roar.

* * *

The celebration lasted three days.

Oscar had seen flood festivals in the delta towns—drunken, loud affairs held on stilts and barges—but nothing like this. The Kaeli did not celebrate the rain by defying it. They celebrated by joining it. On the second night, when the river had risen past the third terrace and the flooded shallows spread out below the village like a sheet of hammered tin under the moon, they lit lamps in hollowed gourds and set them adrift by the hundred, and the whole drowned valley became a field of slow orange stars. Drums answered from across the water, from households Oscar hadn't known existed until their lights appeared. Songs rose in Malayan, in languages older than Malayan, in the wordless high calls that mothers used to find children in the dark.

Elder Suri found Oscar at the water's edge, watching the lamps.

"In the old telling," Suri said, "the monsoon is not the river rising. It is the river remembering us. All year it runs thin and forgetful, and then the rains come and it remembers every name it has ever carried." She handed Oscar a lamp, unlit. "Your mother heard that telling once. She cried. She tried to hide it. She was very bad at hiding things from people who were watching for them."

"She was bad at hiding things from me too," Oscar said. "I just wasn't watching."

Suri touched her shoulder, briefly, and moved on into the crowd, and Oscar stood holding the unlit lamp until Pip materialized out of the festival noise at her elbow, soaked to the skin, grinning, a stolen sweetmeat in one hand and someone else's sandals on her feet.

"You're doing the face," Pip said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to disappear into your own head and everyone's supposed to pretend not to notice." Pip bit the sweetmeat in half. "Jun does it too. It's a grown-up thing. Bao says you'll grow out of it or drown in it, one of the two."

"And which do you vote for?"

"I'm hedging my bets." Pip nodded at the lamp. "You going to light that thing or just hold it like a weirdo?"

Oscar let Pip light it—of course Pip had matches; Pip had everything—and they set it on the water together, side by side with a hundred others, and watched it drift out to join the stars.

* * *

On the third morning, the rain eased to a steady silver drizzle and the flooded shallows below the village lay calm and glassy, waist-deep over what had been terraced gardens, clear enough to see the drowned shoots swaying green-gold beneath the surface. The Kaeli children were already in it, shrieking, diving for coins the elders tossed from the bank—an old game, Suri said, older than the treaty, a way of teaching small bodies to be unafraid of going under.

Oscar stood on the bank with her dive mask hanging from one hand and watched them, and felt the old ache move through her chest, familiar now, almost comfortable, the way a scar aches before rain.

Her mother had taught her to dive in water exactly like this. Not the sea—the sea came later, the deep cold open water where Oscar had made her living—but here, in monsoon floods, in rice paddies and temple tanks and once, memorably, the flooded basement of a provincial museum while a curator shouted uselessly from the stairs. *Any water is a classroom,* her mother used to say. *The ocean just grades harder.*

"Pip," she said.

Pip looked up from where she was crouched at the waterline, cheating at the coin game with a magnet tied to a string. "That's not what it looks like."

"It's exactly what it looks like. Come here."

Pip came, trailing suspicion the way she trailed river mud. Oscar held up the mask.

"How long can you hold your breath?"

"Long enough."

"That's not an answer."

"Long enough to get away from anybody who's chasing me," Pip amended, "which is the only length that matters."

Oscar laughed despite herself. "Okay. New curriculum." She waded out until the water reached her ribs, then turned and faced the bank. "My mother taught me to dive in water like this. Floodwater. She said if you could read moving, murky, shallow water—if you could learn to trust it—you could dive anywhere. Deep water's easy. It's honest. This stuff lies to you." She patted the surface. "So. Lesson one."

Pip's eyes had gone narrow and bright, the look she got when a lock yielded or a pocket gave up its watch. "You're going to teach me?"

"I'm going to try. You're going to be insufferable about it. Those are the terms."

Pip dropped the magnet rig on the bank and was in the water before Oscar finished speaking.

* * *

The lesson took all morning and most of the afternoon, and it went the way all lessons go with Pip: sideways, loudly, and faster than expected.

Oscar started where her mother had started—not with technique but with listening. She had Pip float on her back in the shallows, eyes closed, and describe what the water was doing. Currents, Oscar said. Feel the seams between them. Water moves in ropes, not sheets; find the ropes and you'll never be surprised. Pip lasted four minutes before declaring it boring, then thirty seconds later gasped and grabbed Oscar's arm because she'd felt a cold seam slide past her ear that she swore hadn't been there before, and after that she listened.

Breath control next. Her mother's method, which Oscar had hated at nine and understood at thirty: exhale slow, slower than feels possible, until the burn in the chest becomes a kind of quiet. *Panic is just breath that won't leave,* her mother said. *Let it leave.* Pip, competitive to the marrow, treated breath-holding as a duel and beat Oscar's time on the fourth attempt by eleven seconds, and crowed about it loud enough that two Kaeli children paddled over to see what the foreigners were killing each other about.

Then the real lesson. Oscar weighted a stone with a scrap of netting and dropped it in chest-deep water over the drowned garden beds, where the current braided and the visibility went to soup, and told Pip to bring it up.

"Why?"

"Because it's down there and you're up here. That's the whole job. Everything else is commentary."

Pip dove badly—too fast, all splash and fury—and came up empty, coughing. Dove again. Empty again. The third time she stayed under so long that Oscar's hands curled into fists at her sides, and when Pip surfaced she was holding the stone and spitting water and wearing an expression Oscar recognized from her own childhood photographs: furious triumph, the face of a person who has just been beaten by something and refused to stay beaten.

"It moved," Pip accused. "The current moved it."

"Currents move everything. That's the test. Not whether you can grab a rock. Whether you can think underwater." Oscar treaded closer, pushed wet hair out of her own eyes. "My mother used to say the water doesn't care how strong you are. It cares whether you're paying attention."

"Was she good? At diving?"

Oscar paused. Twelve years of not talking about her mother, and now the questions came daily, small ones, casual ones, and each one was a door she got to choose to open or keep shut. She was getting better at opening them. It still cost something every time.

"She was better than me," Oscar said. "Everyone always assumed I'd be the diver because of her, but she was the real thing. She could hold position in a four-knot current like she was sitting in a chair. She found things nobody else could find. Not because she was stronger—" she shook her head, water flying— "because she was patient. She'd hang there and let the water tell her where to look. I never learned to be that patient. I learned to be fast instead. Fast gets you paid. Patient gets you found."

"Found what?"

"Anything. Everything. That's how she found Malaya, probably. Everyone else was digging where the maps told them to dig. She just hung in the current and listened." Oscar looked down at the green-gold blur of the drowned gardens. "I've been fast my whole life. Fast boats, fast exits, fast goodbyes." She glanced up. "You noticed."

"I noticed," Pip said, with the terrible gentleness teenagers deploy without knowing it.

"Well." Oscar flicked water at her. "Lesson two. Watch my feet."

* * *

She showed Pip the finning her mother had taught her—the slow scissor that disturbed no silt, the frog kick for tight ruins, the way to hover by breathing alone, lungs as ballast, body as thought. And then, because the Kaeli children had gathered to watch and were heckling in Malayan that Jun, passing on the bank, translated with relish ("They say your student kicks like a startled duck"), Oscar did what her mother had done in the museum basement all those years ago.

She dove.

Not for a stone. For nothing. For the pleasure of it—the old pleasure, the buried one, the one Bao had promised would come back up. She slipped under the surface and the roar of the village fell away and the world went green and gold and murmuring, sun-shafts slanting through the floodwater, the drowned garden shoots reaching up toward her like the fingers of sleepers. She hung there in the hover her mother had taught her, barely moving, letting the current speak. It said: *seam here, colder, going left. Silt cloud where the duck-child kicked. Fish, small school, nervous, meaning something bigger passed recently.* It said, in the voice twelve years of silence had nearly erased: *this. Remember this. This is why.*

When she surfaced, gasping and grinning like an idiot, Pip was staring at her with an expression usually reserved for card tricks.

"Do it again."

"Nope. Your turn. Hover. Just breathe yourself still."

Pip tried. Pip failed spectacularly, bobbing like a cork, swearing with a fluency that made the Kaeli children applaud. Tried again. Failed smaller. On the fifth attempt she went suddenly, impossibly quiet—hung in the water with only her face above the surface, eyes wide with astonishment at herself, and Oscar felt something turn over in her chest that had nothing to do with pride and everything to do with inheritance.

*Any water is a classroom.* Her mother had poured everything she knew into a nine-year-old on a dozen flooded afternoons, never knowing—never able to know—which lessons would take root and which would wash away, and here was the answer, twenty years downstream, standing in a flooded garden in a nation off every map: the lessons took. All of them. They just grew in strange soil.

"Mom would've liked you," Oscar said, before she could stop herself.

Pip's head came up sharply. In months of travel, Oscar had never once said *my mother* aloud to her. The word hung on the water between them.

"Yeah?" Pip managed, carefully casual, failing completely.

"She'd have called you a menace and then taught you everything she knew within a week. That was her whole method. Insult, then devotion." Oscar swallowed. "Come on. Last drill. Race you to the far terrace, and if you kick like a duck one more time I'm telling Suri you stole her sandals."

"These are Jun's sandals."

"Even worse. Race you anyway."

They raced. Pip cheated—Oscar never saw how, but she was certain—and lost anyway, and floated on her back afterward in the golden late light, breath ragged, utterly content, while the drums started up again on the far shore and the lamps began coming out for the evening.

* * *

That night the rain returned, soft and steady, drumming on the thatch, and the whole village gathered under the great roof of the council hall while Elder Suri told the flood-telling properly, all of it, hours of it, the river remembering its names. Oscar sat against the wall with Bao on one side and Pip asleep against her shoulder before the second hour, dead weight and drool, and let herself not move.

Bao leaned close. "Your mother stood right about there," he murmured, nodding at a spot near the fire. "First time I brought her up. Stood in the same place you're sitting. Same face, too. Like she was afraid to believe a place could be this soft with her."

"Was she? Soft, I mean."

"With strangers. Easier with strangers than with family. Some people are like that. The people closest get the worst of them, because it's safe to give them the worst." He looked at Oscar with terrible mildness. "Sound like anyone?"

"I'm working on it."

"Mm." He settled back. "Working on it is all any of us ever manage. She'd have counted that enough."

After the telling, when the hall had emptied and the fire had burned to a red heart, Oscar carried Pip to the guest hut—fourteen years old and heavier asleep than a chest full of lead—and laid her on the mat, and stood a moment in the doorway watching the rain come down silver through the lamplight, filling the night, filling the valley, the river rising to remember them all.

Tomorrow, Jun had said, he wanted to start the new map. He'd been sketching for days, cross-checking his memory against the elders' stories, and the children had claimed the project as their own, and tomorrow he would put the first clean lines on the first fresh paper. Tomorrow, Suri wanted to walk her along the upper terraces and show her where the archive slept behind the falls, now that the water had risen to guard it. Tomorrow, tomorrow. For twelve years Oscar had planned tomorrows as escape routes. These tomorrows felt like something else. Arrivals, maybe. Or returns.

She looked back at Pip, sprawled and snoring, one hand curled loosely around the brass compass even in sleep—she'd taken to carrying it, and Oscar had stopped pretending to mind—and felt the last piece of an old argument inside her quietly settle.

Her mother had given her a compass, and she'd spent her life thinking it pointed at betrayal. It had pointed here the whole time.

Outside, the rain thickened, and far upstream, behind the falls, the river rose over the cave mouth as it had risen for a hundred years—hiding, keeping, remembering—and in the morning, Jun would sharpen his pencils and unroll the blank paper, and ask her the question she could already feel coming: *what mark do you want on it?*
