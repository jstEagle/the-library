## 39. A Map of Our Own

Jun had drawn maps for thirty years, and he had never once been afraid while drawing one.

He was afraid now. Not of the work—the work was the easy part, the ink and the vellum and the steady hand, the arithmetic of distance that lived in his fingers the way prayer lives in a devout man's mouth. He was afraid because this map would be true, and he had spent his whole career learning what happened to men who made true maps.

The long table in Elder Suri's meeting house had been cleared of its rice bowls and its mending and its arguments, and across it Jun had unrolled the last clean sheet of waterproofed paper from Bao's stores—a boatman's chart, meant for marking sandbars, now conscripted into something larger. Around him, cross-legged on the woven mats, sat eleven Kaeli children ranging in age from a girl of six who mostly ate her hair to a solemn boy of fifteen named Rethan who had appointed himself Jun's assistant on the first morning and never resigned.

They had been at it for nine days.

Nine days of walking the river's edge with knotted cords to measure the bends. Nine days of the children arguing over names—*this rapid is called the Teeth of the Grandmother, not the Teeth of the Old Woman, my grandmother says so and she is older than your grandmother.* Nine days of Jun sitting up past midnight with a lamp made from a hollowed gourd, transferring their knowledge onto paper in his careful cartographer's hand, the hand that had once been licensed by a government that called him a fraud and was now, at last, being used for something honest.

"Here." Rethan tapped the stretch below the falls. "You have the channel wrong. In dry season you can walk it. My uncle walked it when he was young, before the water took his foot."

"The water took his foot?"

"There is a hole under the third stone. The river keeps it like a tooth it doesn't want to give back."

Jun adjusted the line. He drew the hole. He drew it as a small circle with hatching, the way soundings were marked on naval charts, and then—because he was no longer bound by any navy's conventions—he drew a small foot beside it, and Rethan laughed for the first time in nine days, and the little ones laughed because he laughed, and somewhere outside a kingfisher went off like a firecracker over the water.

That was the thing about this map that no other map Jun had ever made possessed: it was funny. It had jokes in it. The girl who ate her hair—Ila, her name was Ila—had insisted they mark the swimming hole where her brother had once lost his loincloth to a fish, and Jun had rendered it, after some deliberation, as a small fish wearing a hat. There were warning stones noted in Malayan script with translations beneath in Jun's cramped hand. There was the grave of a ferryman's dog. There was the tree where, every year, the swallows returned on the same week, and beside it Ila had pressed her entire palm in ink, because *the tree should know we were here.*

Maps were supposed to be objective. Jun had believed that once, back when he still believed in institutions. He had sat in examination halls and learned projection systems and the ethics of scale, and he had come out of them believing that a map was a window, clean and clear, and the mapmaker merely the glass.

Then he had insisted Malaya existed, and the glass had been taken from him, and he had spent fifteen years understanding that there was no such thing as an empty map. Every blank space was a decision. Every omission was a hand, wiping.

This map would leave nothing out. That was why his hands shook.

"You're doing the face wrong," said a voice behind him, and Oscar came around the table with two cups of tea and set one at his elbow without asking, which was how she did everything now, as though she had already decided she belonged here and was simply waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

"It's not a face. It's a compass rose."

"It has cheeks."

"It has *shading*."

Oscar leaned over the rose at the map's corner—the great sixteen-pointed star that Jun had drafted and redrafted until the children pronounced it acceptable—and studied it with the flat, assessing look she gave everything: dive sites, strangers, her own reflection. She'd cut her hair since the high trail; it made her look younger and more stubborn, which Jun hadn't thought possible.

"The points are crooked," she said.

"They're *hand-drawn*. Crookedness is proof of life."

"Hm." She drank her tea. Below the meeting house, the river ran low and bright in the dry season light, down to its winter bones, the drowned stones showing their backs like sleeping animals. Somewhere downstream, past the falls and the gorge and the hundred miles of country that had nearly killed all of them, the world was reading Hazel Nakamura's journal and losing its mind about it. Jun had seen a copy at the fuel stop on the way down—cheap paper, bad binding, his friend's mother's handwriting reproduced in smudged type—and had stood in the shop holding it like it might go off.

Malaya was in the encyclopedias now. A footnote, but a footnote with a map attached. Jun had cried about it in a public toilet like a man with a fever, and then washed his face and bought three copies.

"The bureau sent a letter," Oscar said, too casually. "Seng forwarded it. They're offering you a review board. Rehabilitation of license. Full reinstatement."

"I know."

"You're going to say no."

Jun looked at the map. Nine days of truth spread out on the table, the river from source to delta as the people along it actually knew it—not as the state knew it, not as the salvage charts knew it, but as grandmothers and fishermen and one boy with a dead uncle's foot knew it. No institution on earth would certify this document. That was precisely its value.

"I'm going to say *not yet*," he said. "There's a difference. I spent fifteen years wanting them to admit I was right. It turns out I don't need it anymore. But I might want it later, and I've learned to keep things I might want later." He glanced at her. "You taught me that. You kept that compass twenty years."

Something moved through her face—there and gone, like a fish under ice. She didn't talk about the compass much. Voss had taken it at gunpoint at the falls and lost it to the river, and Oscar had gone down after it into the green dark, and come up with it in her fist, bleeding from the ear where the pressure had gotten her, and had said only, *it's mine,* in a voice that had ended the conversation permanently.

"The map's nearly done," she said instead. "What's left?"

"The edges." Jun smoothed the paper. "A map is only honest if it admits where it stops. Most maps pretend the edge of the paper is the edge of the world. I want to write on ours what we don't know. *Beyond this point the channel shifts yearly; ask the elders.* That kind of thing. Cartography as humility."

"And the village?"

And there it was. The question, arriving the way he'd known it would, the way it had been circling the camp for a week like a bird deciding where to land.

Jun set down his pen.

*

The problem was this: Elder Suri had given permission for the map, but not for the mark.

"The river can be known," she had said, four nights ago, in the meeting house with the lamp between them. "The river was always going to be known. You cannot hide a river. But the village—" her old hands had folded together, brown and certain as roots, "—the village survives by not being looked for. Your book told the world the archive exists. Fine. The archive is a story now, and stories travel far from their homes. But our door should stay a rumor."

She had not forbidden it. That was the worst part. She had simply laid the truth on the table between them the way she laid out betel leaves, and let them look at it.

So the map was finished except for one blank space: a bend of the headwaters, beautifully drawn, accurately sounded, with nothing at its center. The village existed everywhere on the paper except where it actually was.

Jun had hated blank spaces his whole life. It was why they'd stripped his license—because he filled them, because he refused to accept that the world had gaps, because he'd stood in front of the Royal Cartographic Society and said *I have evidence* and they had looked at him the way men look at weather they've decided not to believe in. Fifteen years of drinking in riverports over a blank space where a nation used to be.

And now he was being asked to draw one on purpose.

He understood it. That was the terrible thing. He understood it completely. Anonymity was the Kaeli's walls, and walls were not built to insult the people outside them. He had explained it to himself every night. He had almost made peace with it.

Almost.

*

It was Ila who broke the deadlock, because it was always the children, in the end, who saw what the adults were too proud or too frightened to see.

She came to the meeting house on the tenth morning with the hair-eating girl solemnity she deployed for matters of maximum importance, climbed onto the bench beside Jun, and studied the blank space in the middle of the finished map for a long time.

"Why is there a hole in it?" she asked.

"That's where we decided not to put the village."

Ila considered this with the gravity of a judge. "But the village is *there*."

"Yes."

"So the map is lying."

"It's not lying. It's keeping a secret."

"Secrets are just lies you agreed to." She said it without malice, the way children say devastating things, and then pointed at the blank space. "If I made a map of my family and left out my brother, it wouldn't be a map of my family. It would be a map of the part of my family I like."

Jun opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the blank space, which suddenly looked less like prudence and more like a wound.

That afternoon, Oscar found him staring at it.

*

She came at dusk, when the light through the meeting house doorway went the color of strong tea, and she stood looking at the map for a long time without speaking. Jun had learned to read her silences the way he'd once read tide tables—there were silences that meant *leave me alone*, and silences that meant *I'm working something out*, and this one had the particular density of a decision being forged.

"My mother found this place," she said finally. "Twenty years ago. And she kept it secret her whole life. She let them destroy her rather than give it up." Her finger hovered over the blank space without touching it. "I used to think the secret was what killed her. The hiding. The weight of it."

"And now?"

"Now I think the secret is what *kept* her alive as long as it did. She had somewhere worth keeping." Oscar turned. "But Jun—she also wrote it down. Everything. The whole journey, the whole hunt, sealed in a lead chest at the bottom of a flooded temple. She hid the truth and she *also* preserved it. Both at once. She never chose between hiding and telling. She chose who got to hold each part."

Jun felt the idea land in his chest like a dropped anchor.

"The journal went to the world," Oscar said. "Redacted. The locations burned out of it. The world got the *story* of Malaya, and the Kaeli got to keep the *place*. My mother figured that out before I was born." She looked at him. "Your map can do the same thing."

"How?"

"Mark it. The village. Right there, in the middle of your beautiful blank space. But don't write the name. Don't write anything. Just—" she paused, and Jun watched her reach for it, watch her, for once, fail to find the precise word, "—just a compass rose. Small one. Like a signature. So anyone who ever sees this map knows: something is here. Something was *always* here. We know exactly where it is, and we are choosing, deliberately, freely, to leave it unnamed." She smiled, thin and real. "An honest blank space. The first one ever drawn."

Jun stared at her.

Fifteen years ago he had stood before the Society with evidence and been called a liar. The crime they'd convicted him of wasn't forgery—it was *certainty*. He had claimed to know what the maps said could not exist. And now the punishment and the redemption had come around full circle and met each other on this table: he would draw the thing that could not officially exist, and he would refuse to name it, and both acts would be true at once.

Cartography as humility. Cartography as promise.

"Give me the pen," he said.

*

He worked by lamplight while the children slept in the corners of the meeting house, wrapped in the blankets the dry season nights demanded, and the whole crew gathered to watch—Bao leaning on his healed-but-stiff shoulder in the doorway, Pip perched on the table's edge with her chin on her fists, Oscar standing closest, arms crossed, breathing.

In the center of the blank space, small and precise, Jun drew a compass rose. Sixteen points, half the size of the great one at the map's margin. Perfectly straight. He gave it shading at the north point, and a fine double ring, and then, beneath it, in his smallest and most formal hand, he wrote the legend:

*Here stands a community that remembers. Its name belongs to itself.*

No coordinates. No name. No approach route. Just the declaration, the way you'd hang a lantern in a window—not to show the road, but to show the house was inhabited.

Pip read it upside down, lips moving. Then she said, quietly, "People are going to come anyway. Because of the book."

"Some will," Jun said. "And they'll stand at the edge of this map holding it, and they'll know someone lives here who chose not to be found, and maybe—maybe—that will slow them down. Maybe one in ten will turn back out of respect. That's more than zero. Maps have started wars, Pip. Maybe this one starts manners."

Bao laughed from the doorway, a sound like a hull taking gentle ground. "The river decides its passengers," he said. "Maybe the map does too."

Oscar reached out and touched the wet ink before Jun could stop her, and swore, and laughed, and there was a small black smudge of her fingerprint at the southeast point of the little rose, and nobody suggested removing it. It looked, Jun thought, like a signature. Like the map had been touched by everyone it belonged to.

Elder Suri came at moonrise. She had clearly been told—word traveled through the village faster than water through gravel—and she stood over the map for a long, long time, her hands folded, her face unreadable as carved stone.

Then she bent close to the little rose, and read the legend twice, and straightened.

"My grandmother was erased from a census," she said. "Her whole village, struck from the rolls, as if they had never breathed. She used to say: *they cannot erase us from the river, only from the paper.*" She looked up at Jun, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "You have put us back on the paper. Without giving us away. Child, in a hundred years, when the river has changed its bed and every name on this chart is wrong, that little star will still be true."

She pressed her thumb, deliberate and ceremonial, beside Oscar's smudge, and the children, waking, crowded in and did the same, ink to the elbows, laughing, until the compass rose wore the fingerprints of the whole village like a crown.

Jun sat back and looked at what they had made, and understood that he would draw maps for another thirty years if he was lucky, and none of them would ever matter as much as this one, and that was fine. That was more than fine. A man got one true map in a life if he was fortunate. He had his.

Oscar stayed after the others drifted out to their beds. She stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind her, looking at the river, and Jun saw her hand go to her pocket—to the compass, the habit of twenty years—and stop halfway, and change course, and rest instead on the doorframe, holding herself to the spot.

"Jun," she said. "Tomorrow. When we take the boat down to the delta—there's something I need to tell all of you. At the falls, maybe. Where it's quiet."

Something in her voice made him set down the pen.

"Good news or bad?"

"I don't know yet," Oscar said, and the first rain of the new season chose that moment to arrive, fat and warm against the meeting house roof, the monsoon coming home, the river beginning to rise. "Ask me tomorrow."

She went out into the dark, and Jun sat alone with the finished map and listened to the water climb, and wondered what a woman who had just given an erased nation back its place on the page could possibly still have left to say.
