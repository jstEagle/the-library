## 40. Hazel and the Monsoon

The rain came at dawn, soft as an apology.

Hazel stood at the edge of the pool above the falls, where the river gathered itself out of the mist and the limestone and the long dark throat of the mountain, and she listened to it begin. Not the monsoon's full voice—not yet—but the first syllables of it, a whispering on the broad leaves, a ticking in the canopy, a sound like ten thousand small hands opening. The Kaeli said the first rains were the river remembering itself. She had lived her whole life beside water and had never once heard it that way until now.

Behind her, the village was waking. Smoke rose thin and blue from the cooking fires, threading up through the drizzle. Somewhere a woman was singing—a planting song, Jun had told her, older than the treaty, older than the maps, older than the lie that had unmade a nation. The melody wound down through the terraces and out over the water and did not care who heard it. That was the thing about this place, Hazel thought. Everything here was sung as if singing were safe. As if no one had ever come to burn what was spoken aloud.

For twelve years she had not been safe anywhere, and she had called that freedom.

She crouched at the pool's edge and put her fingers in the water. It was colder than the lowland rivers, quick and mineral-bright, tasting of stone when she touched it to her tongue. This was the source—not the trickle the geographers would have wanted, not a single sacred spring, but a whole wet country of seeps and streams braiding together out of the cloud forest, gathering into something that would run two thousand miles to the sea. Her mother had written about it in the journal, in that small slanted hand Hazel knew better than her own heartbeat: *Everyone asks where a river begins as if it could be one place. A river begins everywhere it has ever been.*

Her mother had stood here. That fact still arrived in waves, sometimes gentle, sometimes like drowning. Bao had carried her up the fisherman's trail with her boots tied around his neck because the mud had taken them, and Elder Suri remembered her laughing in the rain, and somewhere below the falls, in the cave behind the water, her mother had sat with the archive and read the names of the erased by lamplight—and then she had walked back down all those miles and gone home to her daughter and never said a word.

*Not as a keeper of secrets,* the letter had said. *As herself.*

"Hazel."

She didn't flinch anymore when Pip used the name. That had taken weeks—weeks of the girl saying it deliberately, slyly, watching for the wince, until the wince stopped coming and Pip lost interest in causing it and just started using it the way she used everyone's name: casually, constantly, like a hand tugging at yours in a crowd.

Pip dropped onto the rocks beside her, knees drawn up, hair already plastered flat by the drizzle. She held out half a rice cake wrapped in banana leaf, and Hazel took it, because refusing food from Pip had turned out to be impossible—the girl treated every accepted bite like a small victory in a war Hazel hadn't known they were fighting.

"You're doing the staring-at-the-water thing," Pip said.

"I'm allowed. I'm named after weather."

"You're named after *nuts*," Pip said. "Jun told me. Hazelnuts. Your mother thought she was being clever." She grinned, mouth full. "Hazel Nakamura. Sounds like a schoolteacher."

"It sounds like someone who owns a cardigan," Hazel agreed.

They ate in silence for a while. Downstream, the falls had swollen overnight; the white rope of them had thickened, and the spray climbed higher into the trees, and the cave behind the water—her mother's cave, the archive's cave, the Kaeli's cave now and forever—was sealed behind its curtain again, hidden the way it had been hidden for a hundred years. Hidden, but not erased. There was a difference. She understood the difference now in her bones, in the way you understand a current by swimming in it.

"Are you going to do it today?" Pip asked. Quietly, without slyness. The girl missed nothing; she had probably known before Hazel did.

"Do what?"

"The thing you keep almost saying when you look at the water. You get this face. Like you're holding your breath underwater even when you're dry."

Hazel looked at her. Fourteen years old, sharp-elbowed, pickpocket's hands, a survivor of more riverports than any child should know, sitting on a rock above the source of everything with rice cake crumbs on her chin. Three months ago Hazel had tried to leave her at a dock. Two months ago she had carried her up a mountain with her feet torn open. Last night Pip had fallen asleep against her shoulder during Bao's story and nobody had moved her, because moving her would have meant admitting how much it mattered.

"I'm going to say my name," Hazel said. "To the river."

Pip nodded slowly, as if this were a perfectly normal errand, which of course it was, here. "You want company or not?"

"I want witnesses," Hazel said. "All of them. Go get the others."

* * *

They came down to the pool in the gray-gold light of the rain-washed morning, and they came the way they always came, which was to say chaotically and all at once.

Bao led, leaning on the carved staff Suri had given him, his shoulder healed crooked and his gait healed stubborn, complaining before he even arrived—"Sixty years I've rowed this river and now I'm expected to climb hills before breakfast, in my day we let old men sleep"—and behind him came Jun with a waterproof roll of drafting paper under one arm and ink stains to his elbows, because Jun could not walk twenty paces without mapping something, and behind Jun came Pip dragging Elder Suri by the hand, and Suri came laughing, letting herself be dragged, wearing the rain like jewelry.

They arranged themselves along the pool's edge without being asked to. That was the thing Hazel kept noticing, the thing that undid her a little every time: they made space for each other without instruction. Bao on the flat stone he liked. Jun cross-legged, already sketching the falls. Pip perched on a root, closest to Hazel, always closest, guarding her flank the way she'd guarded it in the rapids. Suri standing, because elders stand, Suri said, so the young remember someone is watching.

The rain thickened. It came down through the canopy in fat slow drops, and the pool dimpled with them, thousands of small arrivals, and the river below the falls deepened its voice.

"When my mother died," Hazel said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it crack, because she had learned here that a crack is not a breaking—"when my mother died, I stopped using my name. I was nine years old. They buried her under a cloud of disgrace, and people said her name like it was something dirty, and my name sounded too much like hers, so I put it away. I picked Oscar because it was hard and plain and belonged to nobody. I introduced myself to strangers for twelve years with a word that meant nothing to me." She looked at the water. "I measured my life in miles after that. Miles were safe. Miles don't ask you who you are."

Bao grunted softly. Jun's pen had gone still.

"My mother gave me a compass when I was nine," Hazel went on. "The same year. I hated it for twelve years because I thought it was all she loved more than me—the work, the hunt, the dead nation. It wasn't. It was a map home. It was always a map home. And my name—" She stopped. Breathed. The rain ran down her face and she didn't wipe it away. "My name was the last true thing she gave me before the world took her. Hazel. She chose it. She used to tell me it was a plant that grows back after fire. Cut it down, burn it, and it comes up green from the roots. She thought she was being clever." Her throat closed, opened. "She was right."

Suri spoke then, in Malayan first, and Jun translated in a low voice, though Hazel found she barely needed it—she had begun to hear the shape of the language if not the words: *"A name given by love is a boat. It will hold you on any water."*

Hazel knelt at the pool's edge. The water was right there, cold and clear, running over stones the color of dawn, beginning its journey to the sea—to every sea, eventually, to the drowned temple where she'd found the chest, to the delta, to the ports where she'd sold her seasons and forgotten her faces. Everything this water touched, it would touch carrying this.

She cupped her hands, lifted a little of the source, and let it pour back through her fingers.

"Hazel," she said.

Just that. Her own voice, speaking her own name, at the top of the world. It sounded strange and small and then, as the rain answered it, drumming on the leaves, running silver down the rock, swelling the pool—it sounded inevitable. Like something that had been waiting underground for twelve years and had finally, after fire, come up green.

"Hazel," she said again, louder, giving it to the water. "My mother's daughter. Diver. Cartographer's friend. Ferryman's passenger. Stowaway-keeper." Pip snorted. "Keeper of the river's memory, and keeper of nothing that isn't freely given. Hazel. Hazel Halloran. Hazel Nakamura. All of it. Mine."

The river took it downstream. She watched it go—all those miles, all at once, going where she was not going and exactly where she was going, both at once, the way rivers do.

When she turned around, Bao was crying without any shame at all, wiping his face with his sleeve and daring anyone to comment. Jun had drawn something in the margin of his map—a small figure kneeling at the water, and beneath it, in careful letters, *hazel*, lowercase, like a feature of the landscape, like a spring, like a source. Pip was beaming at her with the fierce uncomplicated pride of someone whose person had done well.

And Suri stepped forward and took Hazel's face in her two old hands, and said in English, carefully, having clearly practiced: "Welcome home, Hazel. You were always coming. We only saved your place."

* * *

They left the highlands three days later, when the rains had filled the channels and the side route below the falls ran deep enough for Bao's repaired boat.

Repaired was a generous word. The hull that had cracked in the gorge bore fourteen patches now, some of them Kaeli work—resin and bark-cloth laid in patterns so beautiful Jun had insisted on sketching them into his notes as *vernacular hydrodynamics, unpublished*—and some of them Bao's own splinted planks, lashed with wire and blessed with curses. The boat sat lower in the water than she used to. She leaked a little, philosophically, in the way of old vessels and old men who have decided the alternative is worse. Bao had named her twice in his life, he told them, and both wives had objected to the name, and so the boat remained nameless, and this, he said, was the secret of her longevity.

They loaded her light: nets, fuel drums, Jun's growing atlas, the last gifts of the village—smoked fish, resin, a coil of rope Suri herself had braided, and, wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of Hazel's bag, a single page copied in Elder Suri's hand: the genealogy of the Kaeli, one line of it, the line that ended with her mother's name, entered in the archive the week she died. *Friend of the river. Remembered.* Hazel had asked what it cost to be entered, and Suri had said, nothing, it costs nothing, that is the whole point of remembering.

The whole village came down to the water to see them off. The children raced the boat along the bank shouting questions at Pip, who shouted answers that were mostly lies and entirely delightful. Suri stood on the landing stone with the rain starting again, fine and warm, the new season settling in over the hidden valley, and she raised one hand, and did not wave, and did not need to.

Then the current took them, and the village folded back into the mist, and the river that remembered what the world forgot carried them down toward the world.

It was different water now. Hazel felt it in the way she handled the boat—in the way all of them did. Going upstream, every mile had been a fight, a claim, a theft from the current. Going down, the river did the work, and their job was only to choose among its choices, to read the water and agree with it. Bao steered from the stern with his crooked shoulder and his eternal complaints, calling the channel changes before anyone else saw them, and Jun sat amidships correcting his own charts in real time, muttering happily about errors he alone would ever have the joy of fixing, and Pip worked the bow pole and narrated everything—"crocodile, big one, don't tell Bao, he'll make it a sermon"—and Hazel dove when diving was needed, checked the patched hull in the black green water, hung weightless for a moment looking up at the boat from below: four shapes against the bright torn sky, leaning over the gunwale, watching for her, waiting for her.

She surfaced. They pulled her aboard. Nobody made a speech about it. That was the whole miracle, she thought, wringing out her hair while Pip complained about the drip and Bao complained about the delay and Jun complained about the humidity ruining his ink: the miracle was that there was nothing to announce. Family, it turned out, was not a thing you declared. It was a thing you kept doing.

On the fourth day they passed the drowned village, and the old woman was at her window, and Bao slowed the boat to trade salt for stories, and she pressed Hazel's hand and said a sentence in Malayan that Jun translated with visible difficulty, his voice catching: *"Your mother's name is still warm here."*

On the sixth day they passed the bureau checkpoint, and Inspector Adira Seng came down to the dock in her pressed uniform, and looked at the four of them—this improbable crew, this floating argument against everything her office believed about order—and for a long moment said nothing at all. Then she handed Hazel an envelope. Inside was a single sheet: the bureau's formal restoration of Dr. Elin Nakamura's standing, posthumous, complete, stamped and signed. Below the official text, in Seng's own handwriting, smaller: *The records also show she filed a request, eleven years ago, to add one name to her emergency contact list. Yours. It was denied. I am sorry. It is approved now.* Hazel read it three times. When she looked up, Seng was already walking away, straight-backed, and she did not turn around, and somehow that was the kindest thing anyone had done all year.

On the ninth day they passed the last mapped bend, and Jun stood up in the boat, and cleared his throat, and delivered a short address to the empty air about the historic significance of the moment, and Pip threw a fish at him, and he sat down, delighted.

And on the twelfth day, in the wide brown evening of the delta, with the floating markets lighting their lamps and the smell of charcoal and river fish and diesel all braided together into the smell of the beginning of everything, Bao brought the boat alongside the old ferry dock and killed the engine, and the silence that followed was the comfortable kind, the kind with people in it.

"So," Pip said, swinging her feet off the roof of the cabin where she'd been lying. She didn't look at Hazel. She looked very hard at the water. "This is where you disappear, then."

Hazel looked at her—at the sharp elbows and the river-tough hands and the terrible, hopeful set of her jaw—and thought about miles. Twelve years of them. All the docks she'd slipped away from in the dark, all the crews she'd vanished from before anyone could ask her to stay, all the seasons she'd counted instead of counting anything that mattered. She thought about how easy it would be, even now. The dock was right there. The night was right there. Old habits float, like everything else on a river.

"No," Hazel said.

Pip's head came around fast.

"No," Hazel said again, and heard how the word fit in her mouth, how it had her mother's stubbornness in it and her own, how it sounded like a name she was choosing. "I'm done measuring in miles. I told you at the falls and I'm telling you at the delta and I'll tell you at every bend between, however many times it takes before you believe me. Wherever this boat goes, I go. Wherever you go, I go. That's the arrangement. It's non-negotiable and it's permanent and Bao can witness it because he witnessed everything else."

"He complains while he witnesses," Bao offered from the stern.

"He does," Hazel agreed. "It's his way of loving things."

Pip was quiet for a moment. Then, gruffly, blinking harder than the lamplight could excuse: "Well. Someone has to teach you to cheat at cards properly. You're an embarrassment."

"She is," Jun said. "I've been meaning to raise it."

"There's a list," Bao said. "We vote weekly."

Hazel laughed—out loud, easy, her whole chest involved in it—and the sound went out over the water and mixed with the market noise and the lap of the current against the patched hull, and none of it came back, because rivers don't return what they carry, they just carry it onward, adding to it, all the way to the sea.

Bao restarted the engine. The dock slid away. Ahead the delta opened into the dark in a hundred silver channels, every one of them going somewhere, every one of them theirs to choose, and the first real rain of the new monsoon began to fall—warm, generous, unhurried—on the nameless boat and its named passengers, going wherever the river goes.

Together.
