## 1. Miles, Not Years

The anchor chain came up green with weed, and Sebastian Hartwell counted it as a good morning.

Fourteen hundred meters of chain, three anchors, and a winch drum off a freighter that had run aground on the Serrano Bank two years back. Routine work. Honest work. The kind of job where you knew what you'd find at the bottom before you ever got wet, where the only surprises were rust and barnacles and the occasional startled grouper. He liked surprises about as much as he liked stillness, which was to say not at all, and so he'd built himself a career out of the predictable sea floor.

"Last lift's rigged," called Deshi from the deck of the *Magpie*, hauling the air hose in coils. "You want to check it or trust me?"

"I've seen your knots."

"My knots are beautiful."

"They're decorative. There's a difference." Sebastian shrugged into his harness anyway and went down the ladder to look. Deshi grinned and let him. That was their arrangement: Sebastian checked everything twice, Deshi pretended to be offended, and neither of them said anything about the way Sebastian never quite relaxed until the second check was done.

The barge groaned against its moorings. Behind them, the archipelago sat low and blue on the horizon, a scatter of islands with no names that appeared on most charts. Good salvage country. Bad everything-else country — no fuel depot within eighty miles, no doctor, no harbor deep enough for anything bigger than a coastal trader. Which was exactly why the syndicates hadn't strip-mined it yet, and why Sebastian kept coming back.

He finished checking the rigging, found it sound, and climbed back up without admitting he'd done it.

* * *

Seven years ago this month, his father had gone to sea from a harbor not two hundred miles north of where they now floated, aboard the *Corvina*, a coastal freighter with a cargo manifest that listed machine parts and a crew list that listed eleven men. Neither document survived contact with the truth. The *Corvina* went down in weather that other boats sailed through without shortening sail. No distress call. No wreckage worth the name. Eleven men entered the water of history and did not come out.

Sebastian had been twenty-three. He'd spent the first year searching, the second year searching harder, and by the third year he had understood something that rearranged him permanently: the sea does not return what it takes, but it will sell you glimpses, if you pay in distance. So he paid. He measured his life since then not in years but in nautical miles — four thousand one hundred and sixty, logged in the back of his dive book in a column he never showed anyone. Every mile a payment. Every wreck a chance that the glimpse would be the right one.

Deshi thought it was dedication to the trade. Amara — well, he hadn't met Amara yet, though the harbormaster at Puerto Cielo had given him her name twice, in the tone people use when they're giving you a name for the third time whether you asked again or not.

The miles added up. They always added up. And somewhere in the arithmetic there was supposed to be an answer, though seven years and four thousand miles hadn't produced it yet.

"Boss." Deshi's voice had changed. Sebastian looked up from the dive log and followed his gaze aft.

A gray-hulled patrol boat was coming around the point at cruising speed, flying nothing, saying nothing, the particular silence of officialdom made visible. It swung wide, then eased alongside the *Magpie* with the insolent precision of a vessel that has never once worried about another boat's right-of-way.

A man stood at the rail. Khakis, no insignia, sunglasses, the posture of someone whose fitness was maintained rather than enjoyed.

"Salvage vessel *Magpie*," the man called across the water. "I'm looking for Hartwell."

"You found him," Sebastian said.

"Lieutenant Commander Reyes. Naval liaison office." He didn't offer a hand across the gap, and Sebastian didn't offer one either. "You're working the Serrano Bank?"

"That's public water."

"It is." Reyes let the word sit there, doing its quiet work. "And you're free to work it. I'm here about a different site. There's a wreck forty miles northeast of here, bearing one-one-five from the Cielo light, depth around thirty meters. It's restricted. Survey in progress. No diving, no salvage, no anchoring inside five hundred meters."

"I haven't been near it."

"No," Reyes agreed. "But you've been asking about it."

That landed harder than it should have. Sebastian kept his face level. Three weeks ago, in a bar in Puerto Cielo, he'd bought drinks for a hydrographic tech and asked, casually, about unlisted wrecks in the northern approaches. He hadn't thought much of it. Apparently someone else had.

"Wrecks are my business," Sebastian said. "I ask about all of them."

"This one isn't available to your business." Reyes reached into his jacket and came out with a folded sheet — not a formal order, Sebastian noticed, just paper, the kind of thing that could be denied later. He held it out. Sebastian took it. A chart extract, one square mile, a single cross marked in pencil, coordinates handwritten. No ship name. No date of loss. No reason.

"What's down there?" Sebastian asked.

"Survey in progress," Reyes said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it wasn't, and both of them let it stand because that was how these conversations worked. "The restriction is temporary. When the survey concludes, the site reopens. Until then, any vessel entering the exclusion zone will be boarded, fined, and escorted to port. I'd hate for that to happen to a man with your reputation."

"My reputation."

"For finding things," Reyes said. He put his sunglasses back on, conversation over. "Stay clear of the mark, Hartwell. Some things stay lost for reasons."

The patrol boat peeled away without wake, and the sea closed behind it like a door.

* * *

They finished the lift that afternoon. The winch drum came up caked and dripping, worth maybe six hundred dollars after transport and fees, and Deshi cracked two warm beers and offered one over, and Sebastian took it and drank half and set it down.

"You going to tell me what that was about?" Deshi asked.

"Navy being navy."

"You asked about that wreck."

"I ask about every wreck."

"Yeah." Deshi looked out at the water, at the long gold light going flat and bronze toward evening. "My uncle used to say the ones nobody talks about are the ones somebody paid to keep quiet. He said it like it was a joke."

"Was it?"

"He stopped saying it after a while." Deshi drained his beer and stood. "I'm turning in. Early start tomorrow."

Sebastian stayed on deck after he'd gone. The anchor light came on, a single yellow eye reflected in water gone black and calm. Forty miles northeast, past the edge of the dark, there was a cross drawn in pencil on a piece of paper in his cabin, and a man in khakis had told him some things stay lost for reasons, and the whole performance — the visit, the warning, the chart he could have simply described over the radio — had the unmistakable shape of a man making sure Sebastian knew the navy was watching, which meant someone above the navy wanted to be sure, which meant the wreck mattered to someone who could make lieutenant commanders cross forty miles of open water to deliver a sentence.

Sebastian unfolded the chart extract again under the anchor light. One square mile. One penciled cross. Thirty meters — shallow enough to dive on scuba, deep enough to hide a hull from casual eyes. He held the paper for a long time, feeling the old arithmetic start up in his chest, the miles queuing up to be spent.

Then he folded it away, because tomorrow was a workday, and because a man who chased every rumor drowned in all of them eventually.

It was nearly midnight when the skiff came alongside the *Magpie*, engine cut early, oars for the last fifty meters so quietly that Sebastian only heard it because he hadn't gone below. A single figure stood up in the boat — thin, older, wrapped in a blanket despite the warm night, holding something flat against his chest like a man carrying a Bible into exile.

"I'm looking for Hartwell," the figure said, voice sanded down to gravel. "The diver. They said at the mission you'd be anchored here."

"It's late," Sebastian said.

"It's late for everyone," the old man said, and stepped onto the ladder anyway. "My name is Okonkwo. I don't have much time, and I have something you're going to want — and something you're going to wish I'd left with someone else."
