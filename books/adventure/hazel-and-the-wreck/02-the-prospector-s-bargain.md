## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

Sebastian should have turned him away. That was the professional instinct, the one that had kept him alive through eleven years of salvage work and seven years of something worse than salvage work, which was looking for a man the sea had already decided to keep.

Instead he stepped back from the companionway and let the old man come aboard.

Okonkwo climbed the ladder like a man descending into his own grave — slow, deliberate, each rung a negotiation with his body. Up close, in the cockpit's single working light, Sebastian could see what the darkness had hidden at the dock: the man was dying. Not sick exactly, not yet, but *spent*, the way a battery is spent, the way an engine is spent when it has been run past every redline its makers ever drew. His skin hung loose on his frame. His eyes were the only part of him still running hot.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Sebastian said. "You know my name."

"I know more than your name." Okonkwo lowered himself onto the cockpit bench with a grunt and set a canvas satchel on his knees, both hands flat on top of it, like a man holding down a lid. "I know your father's name too. Elias Hartwell. Master of the *Cormorant Star*. Lost off Vela Atoll, seven years ago this month. Officially."

The word landed like a hook set deep. Sebastian kept his face still. He'd had practice. Seven years of navy clerks and insurance men and sympathetic strangers at dockside bars had taught him that the only safe expression was none at all.

"Officially," he repeated.

"That word is doing a lot of work between us tonight, I think." Okonkwo coughed, wet and long, and waved off the hand Sebastian almost offered. "I was a prospector. Forty years. Gold, mostly, in the islands east of here, before the governments got nervous and the permits got expensive. I found things. That was my gift — finding things other people had buried and meant to stay buried." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible in their clarity. "Seven years ago I was hired to find something on the seafloor. A strongroom. Sealed compartment on a wrecked ship, contents intact. The men who hired me did not tell me whose ship or why it sat where it sat. They told me where. I went down twice. I saw enough."

Sebastian sat down across from him, slowly. The harbor lapped against the hull. Somewhere across the water a generator droned, and a dog barked at nothing, and the world went on being ordinary, which felt suddenly obscene.

"Say the rest," Sebastian said.

"The rest costs you." Okonkwo's hands tightened on the satchel. "Not money. I'm past money; money is for people with futures. It costs you a promise. And I want you to understand, before you make it, that I am not a good man asking a favor. I am a coward finishing a debt. There's a difference, and you'll feel the difference later, when it's inconvenient."

"Mr. Okonkwo—"

"Listen first. Promise after." He coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed he was smaller somehow, as if the coughing had carried pieces of him away. "The ship is a navy vessel. Wartime hull, scuttled — scuttled deliberately, which I didn't understand until later, because ships don't sink themselves. The strongroom holds gold. War gold, moved in the last months, moved by men who never intended to account for it. But gold isn't the cargo that matters." His voice dropped, and Sebastian leaned in without meaning to. "There are documents. Papers sealed in oilcloth, in a case, under the floor plates. Papers about who was on that ship the night she went down. Who was supposed to be on her. Who wasn't."

The harbor noise receded. Sebastian heard his own pulse instead.

"My father's ship went down in those waters," he said carefully. "A different wreck."

"A different *wreck*." Okonkwo smiled, and it was the saddest thing Sebastian had seen in seven years. "Yes. Different wrecks. Same night. Same operation. Same men signing the same orders. You've spent seven years pulling on one thread, Mr. Hartwell, and the whole time there was another thread lying right beside it, and the navy has divers stationed within range of it to this day. Why do you think they warn boats off? Scrap doesn't need guarding."

Sebastian stood up. Walked two steps to the rail. Gripped it. The metal was cold and honest and told him nothing.

He had built a life out of miles. That was the truth of it, the truth he'd admitted to no one, maybe not even himself: after the *Cormorant Star* went down with his father aboard — or didn't go down, or went down carrying a story instead of a man — Sebastian had stopped measuring time in years and started measuring it in nautical miles. Distance traveled equaled grief managed. Every contract, every job, every charter had been another few hundred miles laid over the hole where his father used to be. Miles were countable. Miles ended. Grief didn't, so he'd traded it for the thing that did.

And now a dying stranger on his deck was telling him the mile counter had been pointed the wrong direction the whole time.

"What do you want from me?" he said, not turning around.

"To go down there. To open the strongroom. To take what can be carried and burn what cannot." Okonkwo's voice cracked on the last word, and he let it crack; he seemed done pretending to be strong. "The papers must burn, Mr. Hartwell. All of them. Whatever they say, whoever they name — even if they name your father alive. Even if they name him dead. Especially then."

Now Sebastian turned. "That's insane. If those papers say my father survived—"

"If they say he survived, they also say who killed him." Okonkwo met his stare without flinching. "I've read enough of the world to know how this ends. Men like the ones who own that secret do not surrender it to a diver with a torch. They erase the diver. They erase the boat. They erase the fisherwoman who lent him fuel. And the papers go into another strongroom, or another fire, and nothing changes except the number of graves." He pressed both palms down on the satchel. "Burn them. Let the sea keep the gold. Take only the promise out. Promises float."

"And what do you get?"

Okonkwo's shoulders finally sagged, all at once, like a structure giving up. "Sleep," he said simply. "I found that room for bad men, and I took their money, and I told myself I was just a mapmaker, just a finder, that what men did with found things was their sin and not mine. Forty years I've made that bargain with myself. I don't have forty years left to carry it." He pushed the satchel across the bench toward Sebastian. "So. The map. The location, the approach, the depth, the compartment layout as best I remember it from two dives twenty years younger than I am now. Everything I know. In exchange—"

"Don't." Sebastian held up a hand. He wanted to shout. He wanted to throw the old man off his boat and drink until the harbor spun. He wanted, most of all, to dive tomorrow morning, alone, and tear that strongroom open with his bare hands if the tools failed. Every one of those wants was a trap and he knew it, and knowing it didn't help. "Don't ask me to promise to burn the only proof I might ever get. Ask me anything else."

"I asked the anything else," Okonkwo said quietly. "Forty years ago. Nobody answered."

The generator droned. The dog had gone silent. Sebastian looked at the satchel — canvas, salt-stained, older than his career — and understood that he was already lost. Had been lost since the old man said *officially*. The only choice left was what kind of lost man to be.

"If I take it," Sebastian said, "and I get down there, and the papers say what I think you're afraid they say — that my father walked away from that water, or was made to — you're asking me to destroy the last seven years of my life. To make them mean nothing."

"No." Okonkwo shook his head, slow, patient, a teacher at the end of the lesson. "I'm asking you to make them mean something else. Seven years chasing a grave. Or seven years learning how to be a man who keeps his word to a stranger who gave him nothing but a map and a hard truth. One of those lives is a hole, Mr. Hartwell. The other one is a shape. I'm offering you the shape."

Sebastian laughed — one short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "You rehearsed that."

"On the walk over. Three times." The ghost of a smile. "It sounded better on the dock."

They sat with it. The harbor breathed. Sebastian thought about his father's hands on a wheel, thought about the last voicemail — *fair winds, kiddo, back by the dry season* — thought about seven years of charts with one blank square in them that no amount of mileage would fill.

He put his hand on the satchel.

"Recover what's in the strongroom," he said. "Carry out what can be carried. Burn what can't." He swallowed. "I promise."

Okonkwo exhaled, long and shuddering, like a man surfacing from a very deep dive. He nodded once, formally, as if sealing a contract older than either of them, and then all the heat went out of his eyes at the same moment, like a lamp turned down.

"There's a woman," he said, already slurring slightly, the exhaustion crashing in now that the debt was discharged. "Runs a boat out of the south village. Amara. If anyone can get you to that water and back, it's her. Tell her—" He stopped. Frowned, as if searching for words that had drifted out of reach. "Tell her the finder sent you. She'll understand. Or she won't, and you'll pay her well, and that will be its own kind of understanding."

He slept in the cockpit, upright, refusing the berth. Sebastian covered him with a blanket and sat awake across from him until dawn came gray and thin over the breakwater, listening to the old man breathe — ragged, slowing, ragged, slower.

By full light the breathing stopped.

Sebastian sat with him another hour anyway. Then he opened the satchel.

Inside: a water-stained notebook, a stub of pencil, a folded chart copied by hand in careful, fading ink — coordinates, depths, a sketch of a hull broken open like a ribcage, one compartment marked with a small, fierce X. And beneath the chart, wrapped in oilcloth, a second object Sebastian hadn't expected: a child's drawing, crayon on paper gone soft with age. A boat. Two figures. One tall, one small, holding hands on the deck.

On the back, in a different hand — a hand Sebastian knew better than his own, a hand he'd watched sign birthday cards and cargo manifests and the inside cover of every book in his childhood —

*For S., who measures everything. Someday measure the right thing. — Dad*

Sebastian held the paper very still, the way you hold still over something that might be sleeping.

Then he folded it into his breast pocket, over his heart, where maps belong, and went to find a shovel, and a priest if the island had one, and after that — a woman named Amara.

* * *

The burial was quick and the tide took the dirt's equivalent without complaint. By noon Sebastian was walking the south village seawall with the folded chart in his pocket and the promise sitting on his chest like ballast he hadn't asked for and couldn't jettison.

At the end of the seawall, a woman was repainting the name on a workboat's bow — *Selkie*, blue letters over old blue letters — and she looked up at him with the flat, assessing gaze of someone who had already decided whether to trust him and was waiting for him to prove her wrong.

"You'd be Hartwell," she said. Not a question.

"How?"

"Okonkwo radioed ahead three days ago." She set down the brush. "Said a man would come carrying a debt that wasn't his." Her eyes went to his pocket, then back to his face. "I told him I'd take the job. I haven't told you the price yet."

Sebastian looked at the boat, the paint, the woman, the open water beyond — all of it suddenly, terribly real.

"Name it," he said.
