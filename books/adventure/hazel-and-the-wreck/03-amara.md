## 3. Amara

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside a fishmonger's receipt, and Amara read it three times before she let herself believe the number written at the bottom.

Four hundred. American dollars. Half now, half when the job was done, for two days' work running a stranger out to coordinates she could have found blindfolded.

She sat on the seawall behind her mother's house with the paper trembling in her hands and did the arithmetic she'd been doing every night for five months: Mama's medicine, forty dollars a month if the clinic didn't raise the price again. The boat loan, sixty-two. Tomas's daughter's school fees, which Amara had sworn on a saint's name she would pay until the girl finished school, because a promise to a dead man was the only kind that couldn't be renegotiated. The engine parts she kept not buying. The roof patch she kept postponing.

The number covered all of it. The number covered almost all of it.

Which was exactly what made her suspicious. Nobody paid four hundred dollars for a two-day run to water any pilot in the archipelago knew like the back of their hand — unless they wanted something at the end of it that wasn't fish.

She found him at the dock at dawn, as promised, and she knew him before he said his name. Not by face — the salvage men who passed through all had the same salt-scoured look, same sunburn peeling off their noses, same way of standing with their weight on the balls of their feet like the ground might move without warning. She knew him by the way he looked at the water. Most men looked *across* it. This one looked *into* it, the way you look into a room where something of yours is stored.

"Sebastian Hartwell," he said, and put out a hand.

"Amara Reyes." His grip was dry and careful, a man used to handling things that broke. "You want to go to the Kestrel Shoals."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, quickly filed away. "You know the site?"

"Everyone knows the shoals. Nobody goes there." She let that sit. "Navy charts mark it restricted. Navy patrol comes through twice a week. You're paying me four hundred dollars to take you somewhere I can lose my license, my boat, or both."

"I'm paying you four hundred because I know what it costs." He said it plainly, no charm in it, and she found herself disliking him slightly less for that. Charm she could resist. Honesty was harder. "I'm not asking you to dive. I'm asking you to hold position and keep the engine warm. Two days. Maybe three if the weather turns."

"The weather will turn." She nodded at the sky, where the trade clouds were stacking up wrong, too tall too early. "Monsoon's coming ahead of schedule this year. Ask anyone."

"Then we should leave soon."

*We.* She caught the word and turned it over. He hadn't even noticed he'd used it.

She should have said no. Every instinct she'd sharpened since Tomas went down said no — no strange jobs, no strange men, no runs that ended anywhere but the fish market. Her mother called the sea a landlord who collected rent in sons. Amara had already paid once.

But the letter was still folded in her pocket, and the clinic raised its prices on the first of the month, and Tomas's daughter was eleven now and asked questions Amara couldn't answer without lying.

"Half now," she said. "Cash. And one more thing."

"Name it."

"If I say we're leaving, we're leaving. No argument, no negotiation. You don't get to spend my judgment along with my fuel."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him decide something — watched the calculation move behind his eyes like weather. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to satisfy him.

"Done," he said.

They shook on it, and that was how, three days later, Amara Reyes found herself standing at the helm of her father's old boat with a stranger's money in her lockbox and a stranger's silence sitting in the stern, running south toward water she had promised herself she'd never see again.

* * *

He didn't talk much, which she appreciated. The bad ones always talked — filled the silence with stories about money waiting at the bottom of the sea, as if gold were a lure and she were the sort of fish that rose to it. Hartwell just checked his gear, over and over, with the patience of a man who trusted equipment more than people, and watched the horizon slide past.

It was on the second hour, with the islands falling away astern and nothing ahead but open water going from green to ink, that he finally spoke without being spoken to.

"You've been out here before. To the shoals."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it as one. "I've been everywhere in these waters."

"Not everywhere. You changed course around those coordinates before I even showed them to you. Twice." He turned, and his eyes were the flat gray of deep water under cloud. "You know something about that wreck."

Amara held the wheel steady and told herself the truth was none of his business, which was true, and also told herself she felt nothing about it, which was not.

"My brother ran cargo," she said finally. "Six years ago. Small runs, island to island. Cash work, the kind young men do when the fishing fails and pride won't let them gut sardines next to their mothers." The words came out level. She'd practiced them, though she'd never had an audience before. "One night he took a run he wouldn't tell me about. Big money, he said. Enough to buy the boat outright, enough to fix everything." She adjusted course a degree she didn't need to adjust. "His boat washed up on Pag-asa beach two days later, empty. No bodies. No cargo. The navy closed the file in a week and told us not to ask questions."

Hartwell was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of its transactional edge. "What was your brother's name?"

"Tomas."

"And the run he took. Do you know where?"

"He never said. But he left in the dark heading south, and the only thing south worth that kind of money is—" She stopped herself. She hadn't meant to say it. She hadn't meant to say any of it.

"The Kestrel Shoals," Hartwell finished for her.

The wind picked up, carrying the first smell of rain from the storm line building on the horizon. Amara gripped the wheel and hated him a little, and hated herself more, because some rotten part of her had wanted him to know — had steered the conversation there like a woman walking in her sleep.

"That's why you took the job," he said. Not accusing. Almost gentle.

"That's why I took *your* money." She made her voice hard again, patched the crack. "Don't confuse the two. I'm a pilot, not a partner. You dive your wreck, I drive my boat, and whatever ghosts you're chasing—"

"They're not just yours," he said quietly.

She looked at him then, really looked, and saw what she'd missed at the dock: the grief under the professionalism, worn smooth like a stone carried too long in a pocket. A man measuring himself against something lost.

"Your family," she said slowly. "The shipping company. Hartwell Line. My father used to paint their mark on harbor walls when he was drunk and feeling patriotic. That was your father's company, wasn't it? The one whose ship went down out here seven years ago?"

Something moved through his face like current under ice.

"Navy says it doesn't exist," Amara went on, watching him now the way she watched the water for reefs — reading the danger beneath the surface. "Charts say the shoals are empty. Patrol boats chase everyone away. And a stranger pays me four hundred dollars to anchor over a hole in the ocean that two governments pretend isn't there." She shook her head. "I spent six years thinking my brother drowned in ordinary water. Now I'm starting to think he drowned in *this* water. In whatever happened here."

Ahead of them, dark against the darkening sky, the shoals showed themselves — a broken line of white where swell met reef, and beyond it, somewhere beneath forty fathoms of cold green silence, the thing the navy wouldn't discuss.

Hartwell stood beside her at the rail, close enough that she could see his knuckles whiten on the metal.

"Then we're diving into the same question," he said.

Behind them, far off across the stern wake, a boat that had been holding station at the edge of visibility for the last half hour — too steady, too patient, too far from any fishing ground — began, very quietly, to follow.
