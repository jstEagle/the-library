## 4. The Wreck the Navy Won't Discuss

The chart said forty-one meters to the seabed, and the sea above it lay flat as poured glass, which Sebastian Hartwell did not trust for a second. Flat water in this archipelago was a liar's calm. It meant heat stacked somewhere over the horizon, pressure building, weather with an opinion. He'd learned to read the ocean the way other men read faces — for what it was concealing.

He crouched on the deck of Amara's boat, the *Sarima*, checking his kit for the third time. Regulator purged. Gauge console bungeed flat. Knife on the inside of the calf where a snag couldn't take it off him. Old habits from a hundred wrecks, but his hands were moving faster than they needed to, and he made himself slow down.

Amara watched him from the helm with her arms crossed. She hadn't asked many questions since he'd shown her Okonkwo's map two days ago — just studied it in silence, traced the depth notation with one finger, and named her price without blinking. He'd paid half up front. She'd taken the money like it was medicine she didn't enjoy swallowing.

"Water's clear today," she said. "You'll see it before you're halfway down."

"That's not always good."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

She was thirty-something and built like the work she did, forearms roped with cable-muscle, hair cut short enough that salt dried on her scalp in white crescents. She handled the *Sarima* the way he handled his gear — without ceremony, without wasted motion. He'd worked with dozens of boatmen across three oceans and could count on one hand the ones he'd trust at his back underwater. He wasn't ready to put her on that list yet. But she was close.

"Forty-one meters," she said. "Bottom time maybe twenty minutes if you push it. You go past the safe margin, I'm not coming after you."

"I wouldn't ask you to."

"Good." She killed the engine and the boat swung, settled, went quiet except for the tick of cooling metal. "Because my brother once asked someone to come after him. Nobody did. I don't intend to be the nobody."

Sebastian looked at her. She kept her eyes on the water, jaw set, and gave him nothing else. He filed it away — another door in this woman locked from the inside — and let it be.

He rolled off the gunwale into the sea.

* * *

The descent started ordinary. Warm water, good light, a column of silver baitfish parting around him like a curtain drawn by invisible hands. At twenty meters the color drained out of the world and left blue steel. At thirty-five, the wreck rose up to meet him.

He'd dived freighters, corvettes, a hospital ship still carrying its beds. He knew what sinking did to a hull: the chaos of it, the way ships died ugly, keeled over, broke their backs, tore themselves open on the way down. A drowned ship wore its death like a scar you could read.

This one had been *arranged*.

Sebastian hung in the water ten meters off the hull and felt the hair rise on his arms inside the suit. The vessel sat upright on the sand, level as a building on a foundation. Its decks were clear. Its hatches were dogged shut — all of them, systematically, in sequence. The scuttle valves had been opened in a controlled pattern; he could see the deliberate flooding order written in the corrosion, the way rust bloomed heaviest aft where the engineers would have opened the sea cocks last, holding trim until the very end.

Nobody sinks a ship like that by accident. Nobody sinks a ship like that in a storm. This was a crew standing at stations, doing it by the book, putting their vessel down gently on purpose — and then, presumably, getting off. Or not.

He swam the length of the hull. Two hundred feet, maybe more, a coastal freighter or a light naval auxiliary, her name plate either removed or eaten by decades of growth. But the plating was sound. The frames were sound. Someone had wanted this ship intact on the bottom. Someone had wanted whatever was inside her to stay *inside* her.

His father's voice came back to him unbidden, from some galley table years ago, a lesson delivered over instant coffee: *A scuttled ship is a confession, boy. Ships sink. Men scuttle. Find out which man, and you'll find your answer.*

Sebastian exhaled a long stream of bubbles and followed them up along the superstructure to the main deck.

That's when he saw the strongroom housing.

It sat forward of the engine room where cargo spaces usually lived, but it was wrong for cargo — too small, too reinforced, riveted plates doubled over the standard gauge, with a fresh-looking patch of welds near the frame where someone had done repairs *before* the sinking, not after. The door itself was steel, a full centimeter thick, sealed with a wheel-lock mechanism that had been greased before the ship went down. Even now, seven decades later, he could see the ghost of grease in the threads, dark against the rust.

And stamped into the plate beside the wheel, beneath a skin of barnacles he scraped away with his knife, was a maker's mark.

A circle. Inside it, a stylized wave breaking against a keel.

Sebastian stopped breathing for a moment. The regulator hissed. His heart slammed against the inside of his ribs hard enough that he could hear it in his ears, louder than the sea.

He knew that mark. He'd grown up under it. It had been painted on the doors of his childhood home, embossed on the leather of his father's logbooks, stitched onto the caps of men who came to dinner and never talked about where they'd been. The Hartwell Shipping Company crest — the wave and the keel, designed by his great-grandfather, carried on every hull the family had ever put in the water.

His family's mark. On a sealed door, at the bottom of a navy-restricted wreck, in waters where his father had vanished seven years ago.

The navy liaison's voice came back to him from that dockside office, polite as a scalpel: *Whatever you find out there, Mr. Hartwell, the file is closed. Some things are closed for a reason.* And Sebastian had told himself it was routine bureaucracy, the usual government allergy to salvage claims. Now, hanging weightless in cold blue silence in front of a door with his own name welded into it, he understood there was nothing routine anywhere in this.

He put his gloved hand flat against the steel. Cold came through the glove like a message. Somewhere behind that door was whatever Okonkwo had spent his last breath sending him to find. Gold, the old man had said. Papers. Things that can't be carried must burn. Sebastian hadn't understood the burning part then. Standing here, feeling the weight of the seal under his palm, he was starting to.

He checked his gauge. Twelve minutes of bottom time gone. He photographed the door, the mark, the weld patterns, the hull lines — methodical, professional, because whatever else this was, evidence was evidence, and evidence lived longer than memory. Then he turned for the ascent, and that's when he saw the shadow cross the light.

* * *

He surfaced fifty meters astern of the *Sarima*, and Amara's face told him everything before he got a word out.

"We've got company," she said. "Been sitting out there since you went down. Didn't move, didn't signal. Just sat."

Sebastian turned, treading water, and found it. Three kilometers out, right at the edge where haze ate detail: a boat. White hull, low profile, twin engines idling. Not a fishing boat — no gear, no smell of fish, no nets drying. Not navy — navy came with flags and arrogance and radio calls. This thing sat on the water the way a man sits outside a house he's watching.

"How long?"

"Since before you surfaced. Maybe an hour." Amara's voice was flat, but her knuckles were white on the rail. "They know we're here. They want us to know they know."

Sebastian hauled himself aboard, stripped his mask, and looked again through the binoculars. The white boat hadn't moved. Sunlight glinted off something amidships — glass, or chrome, or a lens.

Syndicate. The word arrived in his head without permission, wearing Okonkwo's dying rasp. The old prospector had been afraid of something in his final week, jumping at engines, sleeping with a machete by his cot. *They watch the water,* he'd said once, feverish. *They've watched it for years. Longer than you've been alive, maybe.* Sebastian had filed it under the ramblings of a dying man. Dying men, he was learning, often spoke the most precisely.

"They'll come closer at dusk," Amara said beside him. "Or send someone small and fast tonight. That's how it works out here. Nobody boards in daylight with witnesses."

"What witnesses?" Sebastian said. "There's no one for forty miles."

"Exactly." She met his eyes. "So. What did you find down there that bought us an audience?"

Sebastian weighed it. Two days ago he'd have said nothing, told her it was scrap and routine, kept his cards against his chest the way he'd kept them against everyone since his father went down. But she'd seen the white boat and named its behavior in one sentence. She knew these waters and the men who hunted them. And there was a look on her face when she mentioned her brother that he recognized from his own mirror.

He handed her the camera instead of answering. Let the pictures do it.

She clicked through them fast, then slower, then stopped on the shot of the door and held the screen close to her face. For a long moment she said nothing. When she looked up, something had changed in her expression — not softness exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman fitting one more piece into a grief she'd been assembling for years.

"The wave and the keel," she said quietly. "My brother used to draw that. In the margins of his charts. He crewed on a Hartwell run, the summer before—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "The summer before he took the job that killed him."

Sebastian stared at her. "Your brother worked for Hartwell Shipping?"

"For your father." Her jaw tightened. "One run. One run, and the boat came back without him, and nobody would tell us anything, and the company closed its island office a month later like it was wiping its hands." She thrust the camera back at him. "So you tell me, Hartwell. What's behind that door? Because I've spent six years being lied to about that mark, and I will dive this wreck myself, alone, at night, before I let another boat carry that secret out of these waters without me."

The wind had come up while they talked, a thin warm wind out of the east that flattened the swell and smelled of rain that hadn't started yet. Amara glanced at it, frowned, and began coiling line with sharp, angry efficiency.

"Monsoon's early," she muttered. "We've got days. Maybe a week of working windows."

Days. Sebastian looked at the white boat, still sitting patient on the horizon, and felt something shift in his chest — the old restless grief, the mile-counting emptiness of seven years, compressing, hardening, taking on shape and weight. Dread, yes. But dread with a direction now. Dread with a door at the end of it, forty-one meters down, marked with his own name.

His father had scuttled a ship here. Or been scuttled with one. Either way, the answer was behind that wheel-lock, and a syndicate with a white boat wanted it to stay there, and the sky was already stacking its storm.

"Then we don't waste the window," Sebastian said. "Get us back to the cove. I need to rig a penetration plan, and you and I need to talk about your brother — all of it, start to finish."

Amara threw the engine over, and the *Sarima* came about, running for shelter with the white boat sliding parallel on the horizon, keeping its distance, keeping its vigil.

Neither of them said what they were both thinking: whatever was behind that door, they were no longer the only ones diving for it.
