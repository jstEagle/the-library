## 5. Monsoon Warning

The sky turned the color of old pewter two days before it should have.

Amara felt it first in her hands — a pressure behind her knuckles like the sea pressing up from underneath, the same ache that had warned her mother of storms before any radio did. She stood at the wheel of the *Sable Tern* and watched the horizon stack itself into bruised layers, and she thought: *too early. The monsoon is two weeks early, and we are forty miles from shelter with a hold full of dive gear and a man who doesn't know these waters.*

"Sebastian." She didn't shout. He was below, checking the compressor, and he came up the ladder fast when she used that tone. He'd learned it in three days. "Look east."

He looked. She watched him do the arithmetic — clouds, swell, the way the wind had backed around to the northeast overnight instead of its usual afternoon swing. He was quick. That was one thing she'd decided about him already. Quick, and careful with his gear, and careless with himself in ways that made her want to shake him.

"That's not a squall," he said.

"No."

"How long?"

"If we run now? We make the cove before dark. If we push for the wreck tomorrow morning like you wanted—" She let it hang. He was smart enough to finish it.

"The wreck keeps," he said finally, and something in his jaw tightened as he said it, some private argument lost. She recognized the look. Her brother had worn it too, the last season, every time the weather closed in and the money got thin. Men who measured their lives in distances always hated being told to stop.

She put the bow northeast and opened the throttle.

* * *

They'd been running an hour when the debris field found them.

It came out of the gray like a reef that had learned to swim — palm trunks, roofing tin, half a fishing platform torn loose from somewhere up the chain of islands, all of it riding the same current they were riding, all of it moving faster than it should. Amara cut the wheel hard to port. A log the size of a canoe slid past the starboard rail close enough to touch.

Then the hull hit something under the surface.

Not a log. Something dense, low, maybe a submerged container off some freighter's deck, riding just proud of the water. The impact came up through her boots and into her teeth. The engine note changed — a flutter, then a grind, then nothing but the wind.

"Tell me that's fouled prop," Sebastian said, already moving.

Amara was already down the hatch, into the bilge, where the smell of diesel and salt told her everything before her eyes adjusted enough to see it. Water coming in along a seam aft of the engine bed. Not gushing. Seeping. The kind of leak that gave you hours, not minutes, if you respected it.

"We're holed?" He crowded the companionway behind her, flashlight beam swinging.

"Cracked. Small. It's honest about itself, at least." She grabbed the wooden plug from its bracket, wedged it in, wrapped the seam with the spare canvas and a hose clamp. Old trick. Ugly. It would hold in flat water and pray in anything else. "Which is why we're not arguing about the cove anymore."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were going to suggest we could still make the wreck site tonight." She looked up at him from the bilge, oil on her wrists, and saw him not deny it. "Sebastian. I've buried people who suggested things like that."

The silence between them went on long enough to fill with rain. The first drops hit the deck above like thrown gravel.

* * *

The cove was a secret her father had shown her when she was nine — a notch in the eastern side of Kepulauan Kecil where the limestone cliffs folded together and hid a pool of green water deep enough to float a boat and shallow enough to anchor easy. From the sea you'd never find it. From inside, the cliffs rose two hundred feet and held the sky out except for a strip of gray directly overhead, and the monsoon could scream all it wanted over the top.

They anchored in four meters, stern to the cliff, and Amara set a second line ashore because she trusted rope more than anchors and had earned that distrust honestly. By full dark the storm arrived properly, wind funneling down through the gap in the cliffs and flattening the rain sideways, and the *Sable Tern* sat in her pocket of calm and shook like a nervous animal.

Sebastian cooked. Rice, tinned fish, chili paste from a jar Amara had brought aboard without explanation and which he had eaten from without comment — another point in his favor. They ate in the cabin with the lamp turned low, listening to the storm try the cliffs and fail.

"My brother piloted a run to that wreck," Amara said.

She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way water comes through a cracked seam — slowly, then all at once.

Sebastian set down his spoon. "Your brother."

"Dani. Two years older than me. Better boat handler than I'll ever be." She kept her eyes on the lamp flame. Easier that way. "Seven years ago. Same season, early storms, same as this. He took a charter — three men, paid cash, wouldn't say what they were after. My mother begged him not to go. He went anyway, because the money was good and because Dani always believed the next run would be the last run."

"The syndicate?"

"I didn't know that word then. I know it now." She'd done her reading since Sebastian had shown her the navy letter, since she'd understood whose wreck they were diving. "The men who chartered him worked for a company called Meridian Salvage. It doesn't exist anymore on paper. But the men who ran it — one name comes up again and again, in the port records, in the stories the old divers tell. Voss."

Something moved across Sebastian's face. Not surprise. Recognition, arriving late and unwelcome.

"Dani brought them here," she said. "To these waters. And he never came back. No wreckage, no body, no distress call. Just gone, and Meridian's lawyers arrived within the week to tell my parents their son must have scuttled the boat for insurance, and would they please sign this settlement or face costs." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced level for seven years; it was load-bearing now. "My father signed. My mother never forgave him for signing. Or herself. Or the sea. She died last year still angry at all three."

"Amara—"

"I'm not telling you this so you'll be sorry." Now she looked at him, and let him see all of it, the anger and the grief and the seven years of holding both at arm's length. "I'm telling you because you deserve to know why I'm really here. You think I took this job for the money. I did need the money. But when you showed me that letter, and I read the coordinates, and I worked out which wreck it was—" She stopped. Started again. "Dani's last run was to those coordinates. I've known it since the day you hired me. I've been waiting for the right moment to tell you, and there isn't one, so I'm telling you in a storm, in a cove, over bad rice."

Sebastian was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind found a new angle and the rain drummed harder against the cabin top.

"My father disappeared seven years ago too," he said. "Same waters. Everyone says the ship went down in a storm, everyone official, anyway. The navy won't even confirm the ship existed." He turned his cup in his hands. "I've spent seven years diving other people's wrecks and measuring my life in nautical miles, because miles don't ask questions. And now the one wreck I'm not supposed to touch has my family's mark on the strongroom door, and your brother ferried the men who scuttled it, and neither of us ended up here by accident."

"No," Amara said. "We ended up here because the sea keeps its accounts. It just takes its time collecting."

He almost smiled. It was a tired thing, but real. "What do you want, Amara? Truly. If we find what's down there — if it's what Okonkwo's map says it is — what do you want out of it?"

She had asked herself the question every night since she'd agreed to this job, lying in her bunk in the village while the roof leaked. She knew the answer the way she knew the channel markers: by heart, by feel, in the dark.

"I want the name Voss to cost him something," she said. "I don't need gold. Gold buys boats and roofs and quiet funerals. I need whatever is in that strongroom to be worth enough that powerful men are still afraid of it after seven years. Because if they're still afraid, then it can hurt them. And if it can hurt them, then Dani's run wasn't just a run. It meant something. It counted."

"And if using it gets us killed?"

"Then we'll be in good company." She reached across the table and took his cup, poured the last of the tea into it, handed it back. An old gesture, her mother's gesture, given and received between people who had decided to trust each other whether or not it was wise. "Alliance, Sebastian. Full honesty, both directions, no more secrets below decks. You tell me what Okonkwo promised you, all of it. I tell you what I know about Meridian's routes and their boats. And when the weather breaks, we dive that wreck together — not you with me driving, not me with you paying. Together."

Sebastian looked at her across the small lamplit space, this stranger who had become, in five days and one storm, the closest thing to family his life had contained in seven years.

"Together," he said.

* * *

She woke near dawn to a silence so complete it felt wrong. The storm had passed over them in the night, the eye or the tail of it, and the cove lay glass-still under a sky rinsed pale gray. Through the porthole she watched Sebastian standing at the rail, already dressed, already looking east toward the open water and the wreck that waited beyond it.

But it was the water she noticed. The light on it. The strange, held-breath calm that every fisher in the archipelago knew and feared, because it wasn't peace — it was the pause between blows, the sea drawing back its fist.

And on the far horizon, where the channel opened to the sea, a shape sat against the brightening sky. Low, dark, motionless. Too far to read a name. Close enough to know it hadn't been there yesterday.

Amara climbed out of the bunk quietly and stood beside him at the rail.

"They found the cove," she said.

Sebastian didn't answer. He was watching the shape, and his hand had gone, without his seeming to notice, to the chart tube lashed beside the wheel — the dead man's map, the promise, the reason any of them were alive to watch the storm come back.

The barometer on the bulkhead began, very softly, to fall.
