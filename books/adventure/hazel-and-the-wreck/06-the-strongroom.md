## 6. The Strongroom

The sea turned the color of old pewter overnight, and Amara said that meant two days, maybe three, before the monsoon's first real blow. So we went down at first light with the *Vela* riding low on her patched hull and my hands shaking in a way I told myself was cold.

It wasn't cold. It was forty meters of black water and a door with my family's name on it.

We anchored over the wreck's shadow — a long dark seam on the bottom where the ship had settled into the sand on her port side, decks canted like a man leaning against a wall to die. I'd spent three dives mapping her from the outside. Now came the part salvage men call penetration and widows call something else.

"Comms check," Amara said through the mask mic, her voice flattened by the regulator's hiss.

"Loud and clear."

"You go in, I hold the line at the corridor mouth. You do not go past your reel. You do not go past half your air. You come back when I say come back." A pause. "Sebastian. Say it back."

"I come back when you say come back."

"Good." And then, quieter, almost lost under the bubbles: "My brother didn't."

I let that sit between us for one breath, because some things deserve it, and then I rolled off the swim step into the green.

* * *

Descent is always the same silence. The world narrows to the roar of your own breathing and the slow brightening of the bottom as your light finds it. The wreck rose up to meet me — rail, davits, a lifeboat cradle empty since before I was born — and I swam along her flank until I found the gash we'd cut on the last dive, just aft of the superstructure, wide enough for a man with gear if he exhaled and turned sideways.

Inside, the ship was a cathedral of rust. Silt hung in the water like breath held for eighty years. My lamp carved a cone out of the dark and the dark took everything else. I ran the reel off the tie-in point at the gash, paying line behind me the way Okonkwo's map had described the route: aft along B-deck, through the crew mess where a coffee urn still stood bolted to the counter like a faithful dog, down the trunk to C-deck, and there — the corridor.

The map had drawn it straight. The ship disagreed. The blast that scuttled her had buckled the frames, and what had been a passageway was now a throat half-closed, deck plating folded up in a steel wave I had to pull myself over, fins scraping, heart hammering against the tank harness. Somewhere behind me a bulkhead ticked and groaned — the ship settling, or the sea deciding. Old wrecks talk. You learn not to answer.

Halfway down the corridor I found the first body of evidence that this wasn't scrap.

A hatch, dogged shut from the inside. Not jammed — *dogged*, all six dogs thrown by hand, by someone who'd had time and light and a reason. I put my glove flat against the steel and felt the wreck breathe around me. Someone had sealed themselves in here, or sealed something in here, and then gone about the business of dying.

Keep moving. The reel clicked out meter after meter. At the corridor's end, exactly where the prospector's trembling ink lines promised, stood the door.

I'd seen it once before, from the outside, through the gash — enough to make me sit on the deck of the *Vela* for an hour unable to speak. Up close it was worse. Better. It was a strongroom door, proper, the kind banks used before the war, and across its face, stamped deep into the steel beneath the coral crust I'd chipped away on the last dive, ran the mark of Hartwell & Sons Shipping — the anchor wrapped in the laurel, the H-S ligature my grandfather had designed at a kitchen table in Southampton and my father had worn stamped on his cufflinks until the day the sea took him.

Seven years I'd measured my life in miles logged away from this archipelago. Every mile had been a vote that there was nothing here for me. The door said otherwise.

I checked my gauge: eighteen hundred psi. Plenty to reach it, work it, and leave. I set my backup light on the deck angled at the lock, took out the hydraulic spreader we'd rigged from the *Vela*'s tool locker, and went to work on a door my great-grandfather's company had installed.

The dogs gave one at a time, each with a shriek of metal that rang down the corridor like a bell in a drowned church. On the fourth, the silt around me bloomed white and I stopped, lamp off, breathing shallow, listening to the wreck complain and settle and go quiet again. In that darkness I thought about my father's hands. Whether they'd touched this steel. Whether he'd been alive when it closed.

Fifth dog. Sixth.

The door swung inward an inch and stopped on its own weight, and the light from my lamp fell through the gap into the room beyond, and what came back to me out of that dark was gold.

Not a gleam. Not a glint. Gold the way dawn comes up — gradual, total, filling the space. Stacked bars in wooden crates gone soft with rot, rows of them, and loose bars tumbled where crates had failed, and under my light the whole floor of the strongroom burned. Eighty years underwater and it hadn't dulled. Gold doesn't care what happens above it. That's the whole horror of gold, standing there in the doorway with my heart going like a pump alarm: it keeps its shine no matter who dies for it.

I should have started loading the lift bag. That was the job. That was the money Amara needed and the reason either of us was floating in a dead ship's throat. Instead I stood in the gap of the door and looked at all that wealth and felt nothing a bank would recognize.

Because there, on a shelf above the bars — dry, impossibly, in a pocket of trapped air the divers' instruments swore couldn't exist — sat a case.

Aluminum, dented, the latch crusted but whole. Military issue. The kind of case that holds documents, or instruments, or the truth. I lifted it down and it was lighter than it had any right to be, and when I thumbed the corroded latch open, the seal inside had held. Waterproof bag. Inside the bag:

A logbook.

Cloth-bound, salt-stained, the kind sold in every chandlery in the archipelago for a few coins. And on the flyleaf, in handwriting I would have known blind, in handwriting I'd seen on birthday cards and harbor forms and the last postcard he ever sent me — *Wish you were taller, kid. The fish here are showing me up* — in that handwriting:

*S. Hartwell. Master.*

My father's initials. My father's hand. My father, whose body had never been found, whose grave was a line on a navy chart marked *lost with all hands*, holding a pen in a flooded strongroom at the bottom of a sea that officially had killed him.

I don't remember kneeling. I was kneeling. The silt was soaking through my suit at the knees and I didn't care, because I had the book open to the last written page and the date on it was three days *after* the sinking. Three days after the navy said everyone was dead, my father had been alive in this room, writing by lamplight in a pocket of air, and the words were small and cramped and urgent:

*They came back for it. Not the navy — the other ones. Voss's people. I've moved what matters. If anyone reads this, the gold is the least of it. The papers are the truth of that night. He wasn't aboard when she went down. He was ashore, and he knew.*

Voss. The name hit like cold water in the ear — the founder, the patriarch, the syndicate that had been circling us since the first dive, whose survey boat wore a flag of convenience and whose divers wore no markings at all. The name Okonkwo had whispered with his last strength: *burn what can't be carried, boy, burn it, he must never read the papers.*

My air gauge said twelve hundred. Amara's voice crackled in my earpiece, thin and distorted: "Sebastian. Time. Come back to the line."

"One minute," I said. "There's something—"

Then the light changed.

Not my light. Above me, far up the flooded corridor, through the gash in the hull, beams swept across the opening — three of them, four, powerful, disciplined, moving the way trained divers move, in formation, silent. The silt haze lit up like a stage. And through my earpiece, on a frequency only the *Vela* and I shared, came a voice that did not belong to Amara, calm and cultured and amused, the voice of a man who had never in his life needed to raise it:

"Mr. Hartwell. Do finish your reading. We've waited seven years; we can spare you five minutes."

The beams below began to descend toward me, steady as tide.

I closed the logbook, shoved it deep inside my suit against my chest, and reached for the reel.
