## 7. What Survived

The syndicate boat came out of the rain like a rumor made solid.

Amara saw it first from the wheelhouse, a gray shape holding station at the edge of the cove, too patient to be a fisherman, too close to be coincidence. She killed the lanterns and stood in the dark, counting. One boat. Then, behind it, the suggestion of a second, running lights doused, riding low.

"Sebastian."

He came up from below with the logbook still wrapped in his shirt, salt water beading in his hair, and she watched him understand it the way she had — not with surprise but with a kind of tired recognition, as if he'd been waiting seven years for someone to finally show up and collect.

"They're between us and the channel," she said.

"How long can we sit here?"

"The tide turns at four. After that we're aground on our own anchor." She checked the barometer bolted by the door, its needle sagging like something exhausted. "The eye won't hold past midnight. When the back side of the storm hits, they'll have to run for harbor or ride it out hove-to. Either way, they stop watching us for an hour. Maybe two."

Sebastian sat down at the chart table and unwrapped the logbook with hands that were steadier than Amara expected them to be. His knuckles were split from the dive, the skin around his wrists raw where the harness had bitten. He looked, she thought, like a man who had carried something heavy up a long stair and was only now letting himself feel the weight.

The book was slate-gray rubberized canvas, military issue, the kind that shed water the way a duck's back did. A brass clasp, green with age but sound. Stamped into the cover, half worn away: *HARTWELL LINE — VESSEL LOG*.

"You don't have to read it tonight," Amara said.

"Yes I do." He worked the clasp. It resisted, then gave with a small click that sounded enormous in the closed cabin. "Whatever's in here is why they scuttled her. Why the navy drew a circle on the chart and told everyone to forget it. Why Okonkwo spent his last breath making me promise." He looked up at her, and in the single lamp's light his eyes were bloodshot and absolutely clear. "And it's why your brother's name is probably written somewhere in these pages. So yes. Tonight."

She hadn't told him about Tomás yet. Not all of it. In the cove, sheltering from the debris strike, she'd given him the shape of it — a brother who ran cargo, a boat that went out heavy and came home as flotsam — but not the name of the ship Tomás had been crewing when he died. She'd held that back the way you hold back a blow, not to spare him but because she wasn't sure yet what it would do to either of them.

Now she pulled the second stool to the table and sat down across from him, close enough that their knees touched, and said, "Read it out loud. If it's going to gut us both, I want to hear it coming."

* * *

The early entries were ordinary, almost heartbreakingly so. Hartwell Line stationery logic: fuel states, weather, cargo manifests in a small precise hand. Sebastian's father wrote numbers the way some men prayed — regularly, reverently, without flourish. Amara found herself picturing him, this stranger whose face she knew only from a photograph taped inside Sebastian's locker: broad, sunburned, squinting at the horizon as if it owed him money.

Then the dates turned, and the handwriting changed.

It started small. Letters pressed harder. Lines that trailed off and resumed mid-thought. The entries grew shorter even as the events they described grew larger.

*"Cargo loaded at night against standing orders. Navy escort withdrew without explanation at 0300. Crew asking questions I cannot answer. Manifest lists machine parts. The crates are wrong for machine parts — too heavy, sealed with naval wax. Someone above me knows what we carry and has decided I am safer not knowing."*

Sebastian read steadily, flatly, the way you'd read a casualty list, and Amara understood he was doing it for her, keeping his voice level so hers could shake if it needed to.

*"We are being followed. Same silhouette three nights running, no lights, no flag. I've signaled port twice. No reply. My own company does not answer my signals. I have begun to think the word 'company' is doing more work than it should."*

Amara's breath caught. She knew that word. Every fisherwoman in the archipelago knew it, though nobody said it above a whisper: *Meridian*. The syndicate that bought wrecks before ships finished sinking, that owned harbormasters the way other men owned dogs, that had offered her money once — real money — to report the positions of boats that never came home. She'd thrown their man off her dock. Tomás had needed the work too badly to be so proud.

*"Tonight I learned the truth and I wish I hadn't. The gold is payment. Has been for years. Payment to certain men in the colonial administration to look away from Meridian's salvage fleet stripping these waters — and worse, to look away from the ships Meridian sinks themselves. Insured hulls. Doctored charts. Our own company chartered us into it blind. We are not carrying treasure. We are carrying the next lie."*

The cabin creaked around them. Rain moved across the deck overhead like gravel poured from a great height. Somewhere out in the dark, the syndicate boats rode their anchors, waiting.

Sebastian turned the page and stopped.

For a long moment he didn't move at all, and Amara thought the storm had finally gotten to him, that he'd gone somewhere inside himself the way divers did when the pressure got bad. Then he slid the book across the table to her, one finger marking the line.

*"Sinking unavoidable. Hull breached aft, engine room flooding, eleven souls aboard. I have ordered abandon ship — but I will not go with them yet. The strongroom can be sealed from inside. Whatever happens to me, the evidence must survive. If any man reads this: my son is Sebastian. Tell him I did not drown. Tell him I chose."*

Amara read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. *I did not drown. I chose.*

"He went down with her," Sebastian said quietly. "On purpose. To seal the room." His voice didn't break; it did something worse, it went thin and careful, a voice walking on ice. "He sealed himself in with the proof so no one could take it out."

"And then?"

Sebastian turned pages faster now, hunting. The final entries were cramped, written in pencil, the hand deteriorating as the air in a sealed compartment must have deteriorated too. Dates that stretched across nine days after the sinking. Nine days a dead man had lived, breathing canned air from the emergency bottles, writing by the light of a chemical stick.

*"Day 4. Heard divers on the hull. Meridian's, by their rig pattern. They know the strongroom exists. They cut through B-deck today. I've jammed the door mechanism and flooded the corridor behind me. Let them dig."*

*"Day 6. They brought cutting charges. Small ones. Cowards — they fear collapsing the compartment and burying the gold they came for. Their caution is my wall. But they will return with better tools. I have perhaps a week."*

*"Day 8. Air is bad. Thinking slowly. A strange peace in it. I keep hearing my father say: a man's name is the only cargo he owns outright. Meridian's founder came out here with nothing and decided the sea owed him everything. He sank my ship to hide his ledger. I will spend my last hours making sure the ledger outlives him."*

*"Day 9. If you are reading this, the door held. Take the documents. Burn nothing except—"*

The entry stopped. No period, no signature. Just the pencil dragging off the ruled line as if the hand had simply set itself down.

Amara realized she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to hurt. She made herself let go.

"Except what?" she asked. Her voice came out rough. "Burn nothing except what? The rest of the page is torn out."

Sebastian ran his thumb along the ragged stub of paper. "Someone got here before Okonkwo," he said. "Or Okonkwo took it. Or—" He stopped. Looked at her. And she watched him arrive, entry by entry, at the same place she'd been sitting for years.

"Tomás," she said. "My brother crewed on a Meridian recovery run eight months after this sinking. The *Vigilant Star*. They told the families it was lost in a squall." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "A squall. With a full salvage outfit aboard and a hold full of equipment. Sebastian — I think Tomás found this book. I think he found it and tore out that page and tried to bring it home, and Meridian sank his boat for it."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the wind had begun to turn, the eye closing, the storm gathering itself for the second act.

Sebastian closed the logbook with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a coffin. When he spoke, the thin careful voice was gone. What replaced it was quiet and level and colder than the water outside.

"Meridian's founder," he said. "He's still alive. Old now, but alive, and his whole empire sits on top of this book. That's what the navy's silence buys. That's what they're blockading this cove to protect." He stood, and Amara saw that grief had done what grief sometimes does — burned off everything in him that hesitated. "They think we're trapped until the tide turns."

"We are trapped until the tide turns."

"No." He was already moving, pulling the chart of the wreck site toward him, his finger finding the deep channel beyond the reef where the syndicate boats couldn't follow. "We're trapped until *they* believe we have nowhere to go. There's one dive left in this storm, Amara. One window while the eye holds. Help me rig the strongroom, and tomorrow we'll give Meridian exactly what they're afraid of."

She looked at the logbook, at her brother's ghost in a torn page, at the gray shapes waiting in the rain.

"What are we giving them?" she asked.

"A grave," Sebastian said. "And a bill."
