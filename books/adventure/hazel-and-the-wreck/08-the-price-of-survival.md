## 8. The Price of Survival

They came aboard at dusk, and they came politely, which was worse than if they'd come with guns.

Three men in a rigid inflatable that had no business being this far from any harbor. The one in the middle was silver-haired and sunburned in the way of men who spent money on boats rather than time on them. He introduced himself as Voss — just Voss — and accepted the tea Amara offered with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

Sebastian kept his hand near the dive knife on the bench beside him and said nothing.

"You've been busy," Voss said. He sat across from Sebastian like a man visiting a nephew. "Three penetration dives in four days. Through flooded compartments on a hull that's been down since before you were licensed. Impressive work."

"Salvage," Sebastian said. "It's what I'm paid for."

"It's what you were *hired* for. Stripping brass and copper off a dead ship." Voss set his cup down with a small ceramic click. "What you've been *doing* is something else entirely."

Amara stood by the wheel, arms folded, her face arranged into the flat patience she wore around customs officials. But Sebastian had learned to read her in the weeks since the cove, and he saw the tension in her shoulders, the way her eyes tracked the two men flanking Voss — one big and soft-looking, one lean with a scar through his eyebrow. The soft one was the muscle. They always were.

"The navy hired me through a broker," Sebastian said. "If there's a problem with my permits—"

"Your permits are fine. Your permits are beautiful." Voss smiled again. "I helped write them."

The monsoon wind worried at the rigging above them, a low moan like something breathing. Below their keel, forty meters down, lay the strongroom, the gold, the logbook wrapped in oilcloth inside Sebastian's dry bag, which was currently sitting under his bunk beneath a false panel Amara had built from scrap teak. He thought about it the way you think about a wound: constantly, involuntarily.

*Hartwell Shipping.* The mark on the strongroom door. His father's handwriting in the logbook, dated three days after the sinking everyone said killed him. Three days after the night seven years ago that Sebastian had measured every mile of his life against.

"What do you want?" he asked.

Voss leaned back. "Now we're talking. I want what's in your bag, Sebastian. May I call you Sebastian? I feel I know you. I knew your father, after all."

The words landed like a hook behind his sternum. Sebastian kept his face still. "You knew him."

"I did business with him. Once." Something moved behind Voss's eyes — not grief, nothing so clean. "He was an honest man. It ruined him."

"He died protecting your secret," Amara said quietly. "That's what the logbook says."

Voss turned to look at her for the first time, and the temperature in the cabin seemed to drop. "Ah. So you've read it."

"Some of it," Sebastian lied.

"Then you know he survived the sinking. You know he scuttled this ship himself — deliberately, with the cargo sealed inside, because he understood what would happen if it ever surfaced. And you know—" Voss paused, choosing the words like a man choosing tools, "—you know who gave the order to run him down when he tried to leave the archipelago."

The cabin swayed. Somewhere below, the sea pressed against the wreck, patient as ever.

"That was you," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. He'd read enough of the logbook to know; he'd simply needed to hear it said.

"That was a different man," Voss said. "Younger. Frightened. The war was over but the money wasn't, and your father was going to walk it all into a naval court because he believed in things." He shook his head slowly. "I've spent thirty years making sure that night stays buried. And now here you are, with his blood and his stubbornness, dragging it back up by the roots."

"So this is the part where you threaten me."

"This is the part where I *offer* you something." Voss spread his hands. "The truth, first. Everything the logbook contains — names, dates, the whole architecture of it. You can have it from me directly, cleanly, without spending months decoding waterlogged pages. And then wealth. Real wealth, Sebastian. A share of what's down there. Enough that you never have to strip another dead ship. Enough that you can stop measuring your life in miles."

Sebastian went very still. "How do you know I measure it in miles?"

"Because I read the same logbook thirty years ago." Voss's voice softened, and for one terrible moment he sounded almost kind. "Your father wrote about you. Did you get that far? About how you used to count the miles between ports instead of the days? He wrote that he hoped you'd grow out of it. That a man should measure his life in mornings, not distances."

Sebastian's throat closed. He looked away, out the porthole, at the gray-green churn of the coming storm. Amara's eyes were on him. He could feel them, steady as a compass bearing.

*Don't take the deal,* her silence said. Or maybe: *Whatever you do, do it with your eyes open.*

"And in exchange?" he managed.

"In exchange, the logbook comes to me. All of it. And the gold stays where it is — or comes to me, I'm flexible — and the file closes forever. Your father becomes what the record already says he is: lost at sea, seven years ago, tragic, unexplained." Voss picked up his tea again. "You keep your life. You keep your boat. You keep her." A nod toward Amara. "You even keep the truth, once I've told it to you myself. All I ask is silence. Silence is cheap, Sebastian. It's the only currency I've ever needed."

* * *

They gave him until morning.

Voss and his men withdrew to their inflatable and anchored fifty meters off — close enough to watch, far enough to make the point that leaving wasn't on the table. Amara doused the cabin lamp and the two of them sat in the dark listening to the wind build.

"He'll kill us either way," she said finally. "You know that."

"I know that."

"A man who ran down his own partner doesn't leave witnesses to a bargain. He leaves witnesses to nothing."

Sebastian turned the dry bag over in his hands. Under his bunk, the decoy — a second bag, weighted, filled with Amara's old charts and a brick of lead shot. He'd built it that afternoon while Voss's boat was still a shadow on the horizon, before the offer, on instinct alone. Some animal part of him had known this conversation was coming.

"He told me the truth is in the logbook," Sebastian said. "But he told it to me himself, free of charge. Which means the truth isn't the leverage. The *book* is. Signatures. Dates. Proof."

"Proof of what?"

"Of who signed the order. Of who else took the money. There were names in those pages, Amara — naval names. Men who are admirals now, maybe. Ministers." He laughed, low and bitter. "Thirty years of silence bought and paid for. No wonder the navy won't discuss the wreck. They're not protecting a secret. They're *in* it."

Amara was quiet a long moment. "So we give him the book."

Sebastian looked up.

"We give him the fake," she said. "Tomorrow. You hand it over, you cry a little, you take his money. And while his divers are congratulating themselves—" her eyes went to the porthole, to the black line of the horizon where the storm was stacking itself up, "—we're already in the water."

"The eye of the system passes over tomorrow afternoon. Four hours, maybe five of calm."

"Enough?"

"For what has to be done?" Sebastian weighed the dry bag in his hands — the real one, the one with his father's last words sealed inside. "It has to be enough."

Because that was the reckoning he'd been circling all night, the thing Voss's offer had forced him to look at straight on: the gold could buy the truth's silence, and the truth could buy his survival, and every road led back to a man selling his father's grave for a comfortable life. Voss had offered him exactly what he'd wanted for seven years — an ending, a number, a bottom to the ocean. And it had taken about ten seconds of holding the logbook to understand he couldn't do it. Not because of honor. Because of the miles.

His father had counted mornings. Sebastian had counted miles. And somewhere between the two of them was a life measured in neither — measured in what you refused to let sink.

"We rig the strongroom to collapse," he said. "Cut the supports on the aft bulkhead, pull the plates, let the whole compartment slide down the shelf into deep water. Forty meters becomes ninety. Nobody strips gold off a wreck at ninety meters in monsoon season. Nobody finds it for fifty years, if ever."

"And the logbook?"

Sebastian looked down at it. His father's handwriting ghosted through the oilcloth against his palm.

"The logbook goes where it was always going," he said. "Into the fire. Okonkwo's promise. Burn what can't be carried."

Amara nodded once, and in the dark her hand found his shoulder — brief, hard, gone again. "Then sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we sell a lie to a murderer, and then we go diving."

Outside, the syndicate's anchor light burned steady in the rising wind, and beyond it the sky was going the color of a bruise.

The eye would pass over at noon. After that, the sea would belong to no one.

Sebastian meant to be underwater when it came.
