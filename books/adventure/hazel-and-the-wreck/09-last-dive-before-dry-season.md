## 9. Last Dive Before Dry Season

**Sebastian**

The eye of the storm was a lie of light.

We sat in the cove and watched it come across the water like a held breath — a column of ragged cloud with a hole punched through its heart, and inside that hole, absurdly, stars. Amara stood at the wheel of the *Mariana's Wake* with her hands loose on the spokes, reading the sky the way other people read a page.

"Forty minutes," she said. "Maybe fifty before the back wall closes. The syndicate boat is holding off the point. They'll wait for the eye too."

"Then we dive first."

She looked at me. In the strange green half-light her face was all angles and salt. "You understand what you're asking. We rig the charges wrong, we bury the gold under two hundred tons of hull, we never see the surface again."

"That's the plan."

"Not the part I'm worried about." She killed the engine. The silence after was enormous. "Sebastian. If we go down there and your father's logbook is in that strongroom, and we sink it — you're sinking him too. Whatever's left of him down there."

I had thought about this for three days. I had thought about it while pretending to bargain with Voss on the radio, feeding him coordinates that were almost true, letting him believe greed was a lever he could pull. I had thought about it in the dark of the bunk while she slept one arm's length away, not touching, because some distances are load-bearing.

"No," I said. "I'm setting him free. There's a difference."

Amara held my eyes a moment longer, then nodded once, and began checking the gear.

* * *

**Amara**

The water inside the eye was the calmest I have ever seen it. Flat as poured glass, warm as bathwater, lit from below by nothing at all. It felt like diving into a held breath, and I knew — the way my grandmother knew weather in her knees — that the breath would not be held long.

Sebastian went down the anchor line ahead of me, torch beam swinging, and I followed into the blue-black.

The *Corvane* rose out of the dark the way it always did: too fast, too whole, a cathedral of rust and shadow lying on its starboard side with its superstructure reaching up like a hand that had been reaching for seven years. My brother had died somewhere near this spot. For three years I had told myself it was an accident of weather and bad luck. Now I knew better, and knowing made the wreck feel less like a grave and more like a wound.

We swam the corridor to the strongroom door. Sebastian worked the cutting charge into the hinge seam with hands that didn't shake, though I could see him making them not shake, and there is a kind of courage in that which I trust more than the kind that comes easy.

The charge went off muffled, a soft concussion I felt in my teeth more than my ears. The door sagged open.

Inside: the gold. Even now, even knowing what it cost, my breath caught. Bars stacked in wooden crates gone soft as bread, gleaming where his torch touched them like something alive and sleeping. And on top of the nearest crate, wrapped in oilcloth, the logbook. His father's handwriting. *Course corrected. Cargo secured below. If anyone reads this, tell my son—*

Sebastian had read the rest already. He'd read it four times by lantern light while the syndicate circled us outside the reef. He didn't read it again. He tucked it inside his suit against his chest, and then we went to work.

The charges were salvage plastic, meant for peeling plating, repurposed for murder. We set them along the main structural frames of the compartment above the strongroom — the deck that, if it failed, would bring the whole midsection down and fold the strongroom into the silt trench beneath the hull. Two hundred tons of steel settling onto forty bars of gold and every document Voss's syndicate had spent seven years killing to keep buried.

Deep water. No salvage. No truth sold to the highest bidder. Just the sea keeping what the sea was owed.

Sebastian ran the detonation line himself, twin leads back to a float canister we'd surface with. He checked each connection twice, then a third time, and when he turned to me his eyes behind the mask were steady.

"Together," he said, and it wasn't about swimming.

* * *

**Sebastian**

We were thirty feet from the anchor line when the back wall of the storm arrived early.

I felt it before I heard it — a pressure change, a shudder through the water like something vast turning over. Then the sound came down through sixty feet of ocean: a long rolling boom, thunder translated into whale-song, and the calm water around us began to move in ways water shouldn't. Currents crossed. Our bubbles tore sideways instead of rising.

Amara grabbed my arm and pointed up. Through the surface I could see the light going wrong — the clean grey of the eye smearing into a bruised, boiling dark.

We kicked for the line. The current fought us like a living thing. My gauge read half a tank and falling fast, because panic eats air the way rust eats steel. Fifty feet from the line. Forty. The *Wake*'s hull appeared overhead, heaving at her anchor, and I saw — with a clarity that had nothing to do with hope — the syndicate's launch sliding around the point into the cove, running lights dead, riding the storm's edge the way only men who don't care about dying ride storms.

They'd waited for the eye's end. They'd known the schedule better than we did.

Voss wanted the logbook. Voss wanted the gold. And right now his divers were probably suiting up on a pitching deck while we hung in the water like bait on a hook.

We broke the surface in chaos — rain coming flat and sideways, the *Wake* rolling hard enough to swing her rail six feet above the swell. Amara got a hand on the ladder; I shoved the float canister with the detonation leads up to her and followed, and we came over the rail into a boat that had become a bucking animal.

"They're here!" I shouted over the wind.

"I see them!" She was already at the wheel, gunning the engine, hauling the anchor with a hydraulic winch that screamed like something butchered. "Canister!"

I clipped the detonator leads to the firing box lashed by the wheel. My hands were shaking now. I let them.

"Say when," she yelled.

The syndicate launch was two hundred yards out and closing, a black shape shouldering through the swells, and I could see figures on her bow — grappling lines coiled, ready. Boarding. They meant to take us in this cove, in this storm, and no one ashore would ever ask questions about two salvagers lost to weather.

"One hundred yards," Amara called. She wasn't afraid. That was the thing I'd learned about her in nine days that felt like nine years: fear lived in her, but it never drove.

I put my thumb on the trigger and looked down through the rain-lashed water, as if I could see through sixty feet and a hull and two hundred tons of history to the strongroom where my father had hidden everything, including the truth.

He'd survived the sinking. He'd carried the gold ashore and brought it back. He'd died protecting it from men like Voss. And the last thing he'd written was not a plea or a confession but an instruction: *Some things are ballast. Drop them, son, or they'll drag you under.*

"Dad," I said, to no one, to everyone, to the water. "Let go."

The *Wake* cleared the reef line. Amara screamed *now*, and I fired the charges.

* * *

**Amara**

The sea answered.

Not with fireworks — with a single deep concussion that came up through the keel and into our bones, and then a slow, vast rolling swell as the *Corvane*'s midsection folded and settled into the trench, taking the strongroom, the gold, the documents, seven years of blood money down into silt and blackness where no syndicate diver would ever cut through two hundred tons of collapsed steel to reach them.

The wave it sent rocked the launch broadside. We watched her heel, lose a man overboard — I saw him go, arms pinwheeling, swallowed — and fall away behind us, her captain choosing survival over pursuit as the monsoon closed its fist around the cove.

Sebastian stood at the rail with the empty firing box in his hands and the logbook dry against his chest, and he was crying, though the rain made it invisible to anyone who wasn't looking. I was looking.

"We did it," I said.

He shook his head slowly. "It's not done. Voss doesn't quit. And there's still—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. "There's still the burning. What couldn't be carried. I promised Okonkwo."

"Then we keep the promise." I put the wheel over and pointed the *Wake* into the storm, toward the village, toward the shallow water where the last of it could burn. "But Sebastian — look at me."

He turned. Rain, tears, salt, all of it running together.

"You just dropped two million pounds of ballast," I said. "How do you feel?"

He thought about it. Really thought, the way he does, like the answer might sink him if he guessed wrong.

"Lighter," he said finally. "Lighter than I've been in seven years."

Ahead of us the lightning walked across the water toward home, and I drove us into it, and neither of us looked back.
