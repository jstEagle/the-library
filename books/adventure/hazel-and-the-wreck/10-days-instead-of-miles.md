## 10. Days Instead of Miles

The syndicate boat left on the third morning after the storm, the way predators leave — without ceremony, without looking back. Sebastian watched its wake flatten against the swell from the seawall in front of Amara's house, a gray smear on a gray horizon, and felt nothing that resembled victory. Only the particular lightness of a man who has set something down and not yet learned how to stand without it.

The *Vela* rode at anchor in the shallows, patched, scarred, still leaking if you let her sit more than two days. He'd have to deal with her eventually. But not today.

Today the navy came.

It was a single rigid-hull inflatable out of the patrol station on the far island, two officers in civilian clothes who might as well have worn their uniforms under their shirts for all the good it did. The older one had a folder under his arm and the expression of a man performing an errand he'd been ordered to perform badly on purpose.

"Sebastian Hartwell." Not a question. "We'd like a word about the *Corvane*."

"The ship you've never heard of," Sebastian said. "The wreck that doesn't exist."

The officer's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. "That's the one." He opened the folder, glanced at its contents, and closed it again without showing either of them a single page. "Our file indicates the vessel was lost with all hands in nineteen forty-four. Our file further indicates that recent salvage activity in the area was unauthorized but ultimately unproductive. Nothing of value recovered. Site unstable. No further action recommended."

"That's what your file says?"

"That's what our file will say by Friday." The younger officer shifted his weight, said nothing, stared very hard at the water. The older one went on. "Mr. Hartwell, seven years ago this office received a distress report from a trawler operating in these waters. The report was filed by a man named Elias Hartwell. It was never followed up. I'm not authorized to tell you why. I'm telling you anyway because the man who signed off on burying that report retired last month, and because you sank a fortune in gold into four hundred meters of open ocean rather than sell it to people who would have used it to keep men like me quiet for another fifty years."

Sebastian's throat worked. "You knew he made it off the *Corvane*."

"We suspected. Now we know. And now nobody official knows anything at all, which is how these things are meant to work." The officer held out his hand. "Your father was owed better than he got. So were you. That's not an apology. It's the closest thing we're allowed to give you."

Sebastian shook it. The officer's grip was dry and brief and final.

They left the way they'd come. Amara appeared beside him only after the inflatable had shrunk to a speck, two cups of coffee in her hands, steam whipping sideways in the trade wind.

"So that's it," she said. "File closed. Ghosts officially dead."

"Officially." He took the coffee. It was terrible — she made coffee the way she did everything else, fast and rough and without apology — and he drank it anyway. "My father spent his last night writing in a logbook so that someday someone would know the truth. And the truth is now a closed file in a drawer that no one will ever open."

"No." Amara looked out at the water. "The truth is that you know. And I know. And the sea knows. That was always the only place it was going to live."

* * *

He burned the documents on the fourth day, on the beach below the village, at low tide, when the wind came steady off the land and pushed everything out toward deep water.

Okonkwo's promise had been specific: recover what's in the strongroom and burn what can't be carried. The gold was gone — given back to the dark, collapsed into the trench side where no syndicate diver and no navy crane would ever reach it. What remained was the rest of the strongroom's contents: the document cases Sebastian had hauled out during the eye of the storm, wrapped in oilcloth, stashed beneath the floorboards of Amara's house like contraband. Shipping manifests. Naval correspondence. Names, dates, signatures — seventy years of proof about who had loaded the *Corvane*, who had scuttled her, who had been paid to look away, and who had survived.

His father's handwriting ran through all of it. Final entries, careful and calm even at the end. *If no one comes, the sea will keep it better than any bank.*

"He was right," Sebastian said aloud, crouched over the fire.

"He was right about the sea," Amara said. She sat beside him on a driftwood log, her brother's knife across her knees, sharpening it in slow strokes. "He was wrong about banks. Banks sink too."

The paper caught reluctantly, then all at once, the way old things do. Seventy years of secrets went up in orange and black, ash lifting over the tideline and streaming out toward the reef like a flock of small gray birds. Sebastian fed the fire page by page and read none of them twice. He had read them once, in the cove, with the syndicate's guns close enough to hear. Once was enough. Some truths aren't for carrying. They're for releasing.

When the last case was empty he raked the coals apart and let the tide come up over them. The sea hissed, swallowed, went flat and indifferent and beautiful.

"You could have sold it," Amara said quietly. "Any piece of it. You'd never have worked again."

"I know."

"And instead you're going to be patching that leaky tub of yours until you die."

"I know." He looked at her. "Worth it."

She turned back to her knife. But she was smiling, and she didn't hide it, and that was new.

* * *

The season turned the way seasons do in the archipelago — not by calendar but by behavior. The monsoon sulked off south. The swells came longer and gentler. The village boats started running the far reefs again, and the fish returned to the shallows in silver schools that turned the water into hammered light.

Sebastian stayed.

He told himself it was practical. The *Vela* needed hull work that couldn't be done anywhere closer than the main harbor, and the harbor yard was a month backed up, and meanwhile Amara had offered him space in her boathouse and the use of her tools, and refusing free drydock space would have been bad seamanship. That was the story. He told it well enough that he almost believed it.

In truth, he stayed because of mornings.

Because of the way the light came up over the eastern islets and turned the lagoon from pewter to jade in the space of ten minutes. Because Amara brought him fish curry on the seawall most days and they ate it without talking, and the silence between them had stopped being a negotiation and become a language. Because an old woman named Ilsa had decided he needed feeding and would not be argued with. Because children had started following him down to the beach to watch him work on the boat, asking questions about diving with the fearless greed of the young, and he answered every one, and found that he liked answering them.

Seven years he had measured his life in miles. Distance run from the place his father vanished. Miles of charter work, miles of salvage contracts, miles of open water put between himself and the question he couldn't answer. He had kept a logbook of his own, once — nautical miles logged in a neat column, thousands upon thousands, a life tallied in departure and arrival with nothing living in between.

He hadn't opened that logbook since the strongroom. He wasn't sure he ever would.

Now he measured differently, and didn't decide to — it simply happened, the way a healed bone stops aching. He measured in tides: two high waters before the *Vela*'s keel paint cured. In catches: three good hauls of tuna with Amara at the helm, working her brother's old grounds for the first time since the loss, both of them silent and steady and neither one falling apart. In repairs: one winch rebuilt, one hull seam glassed, one engine that started on the second pull instead of the fifteenth. In evenings: too many to count, and counting them would have missed the point entirely.

One evening near the end of the season, they sat on the seawall watching the sun go down behind the western islands, and Amara said, without preamble:

"My brother used to say there were two kinds of sailors. The ones who measure in miles, and the ones who measure in days. Miles-people are always leaving. Days-people are always arriving somewhere." She turned the knife over in her hands — his knife, the one she'd carried since the cove, the one she'd finally stopped sharpening. "I thought I'd be a miles-person forever. After Tomas. Keep moving till the moving wore the grief smooth."

"Did it?"

"No." She laughed softly. "But the days did. Turns out grief can't swim. You just have to stay in one place long enough and it drowns."

Sebastian watched the last sliver of sun go. Somewhere out past the reef, four hundred meters down, his father lay beside a fortune in gold and a sealed strongroom, and the sea kept them both the way it keeps everything — completely, and without malice. He had wanted, for seven years, to know what happened. Now he knew, and the knowing had cost him everything he'd carried, and he had never in his life felt so unburdened.

"I'm going to need a new logbook," he said.

Amara raised an eyebrow. "Going back to logging miles?"

"Days." He looked at her. "Just days. One per page. Maybe a line about the weather. Maybe a line about the catch." A pause. "Maybe a line about you."

She bumped her shoulder against his, which from Amara was a sonnet. "Buy two," she said. "I'll need something to write in too."

* * *

The *Vela* went back in the water on a bright morning with no wind worth mentioning, and she floated, and she didn't leak, and Sebastian stood in the shallows with his hands on her hull and felt something he recognized distantly as peace.

They'd run her out to the reef line that afternoon — just the two of them, no cargo, no contract, no map, no promise except the ones people make each other. At the edge of the deep water, where the bottom fell away into blue-black, Amara cut the engine and let her drift.

Below them, invisible, the sea held its dead and its gold and its closed files.

Above them, the sky went on for a long way.

Sebastian leaned on the rail and breathed salt air and thought: *today.* Not the day his father died. Not the day the syndicate retreated or the navy lied or the prospector traded a map for a promise. Just today — one day, whole and sufficient, the first entry in a book with no destination in it.

Amara's hand found his on the rail.

"Where to?" she asked.

And for the first time in seven years, Sebastian Hartwell discovered he didn't need an answer — but then, far off across the water, a white sail appeared on the horizon, running hard before the wind toward their anchorage, flying no flag he knew, and Amara's hand tightened around his as she said, quietly, "That's Tomas's boat. Nobody's sailed her in seven years."
