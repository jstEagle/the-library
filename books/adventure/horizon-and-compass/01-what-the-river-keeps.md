## 1. What the River Keeps

The river had taken the road.

Yara Pemberton stood at the edge of what the survey map, folded soft as cloth in his jacket pocket, insisted was a track down to his father's camp, and looked instead at brown water sliding through the trees with the patience of something that had all the time in the world. The monsoon was three weeks early. It had come up from the coast like an army, swallowing ferries, drowning rice terraces, and now it stood ankle-deep in the forest here, breathing.

He should have come sooner. He knew that the way you know a debt — not as information but as weight. Nine years since he'd last seen Elias Pemberton, four months since the telegram, and now he'd arrived to find even the ground his father had worked on was being erased. The river was settling the estate its own way.

He went in on foot, boots sucking at the mud, following the only marker that still made sense: the concession post. Someone had driven an iron stake into the bank years ago, hung with a rusted plate — PEMBERTON EXPEDITION, RIVER CONCESSION 44-C — and beneath it, in smaller letters that someone had once painted by hand, almost tenderly: *respect the waterline*. His father's handwriting. Of course it was. Even his bureaucracy had been a kind of prayer.

The camp appeared through the trees like something shipwrecked.

The main tent had collapsed on one side, canvas gone green-black with mold, one pole snapped so the whole structure leaned like a drunk against the fig tree that had grown up through its corner. Equipment lay where it had been dropped — or thrown. A coring rig furred with rust. Crates split open, their contents scattered: sample bags, empty; notebooks swollen into bricks of pulp; a typewriter with a sapling growing up through its keys, as if the machine had finally decided to grow words of its own.

Yara walked through it slowly, touching nothing at first. This was the part no one warned you about, he thought — that grief for a parent is mostly inventory. You go through their things and every object asks you the same question: *where were you?*

The answer was in London, then Lisbon, then wherever the grants took him. Anywhere but here.

* * *

The permit office in the district town had been a ceiling fan, a sweating clerk, and a stamp that came down like a verdict.

"Concession 44-C," the clerk said, tapping the file. "Expires in thirty days. Non-renewable after lapse. You're the son?"

"I'm the son."

"Then you have thirty days to clear the site or forfeit everything to the company." He said *the company* the way other men said *the weather* — as a force beyond blame, beyond negotiation. "Dr. Pemberton filed two extensions. Both denied. He kept filing anyway." The clerk almost smiled. "Stubborn man."

Yara signed where he was told to sign. Thirty days. Then the riverbed, whatever it held, belonged to Meridian Salvage & Concession, and Meridian did not respect waterlines.

When he got back to the camp that evening, the light was going amber and low, and he noticed what he'd missed in the grey of morning: the dig site itself, downslope toward the water, had been gutted.

Trenches gaped open, their stratigraphy smeared into a single chaotic stew. Grid stakes pulled up and tossed in a pile like pick-up sticks. And everywhere the signature of the looters — not the careful work of archaeologists testing a hypothesis, but the gouged, greedy wounds of people looking for saleable metal. A test pit had been widened into a crater. Shovel marks crossed shovel marks. Someone had even dug into the midden, into the kitchen refuse of people nine centuries dead, hunting for beads.

Yara crouched at the edge of the largest trench and felt something in his chest turn over. Anger, first — hot and simple, anger at strangers. But underneath it, colder and less comfortable, another thought: *he let this happen.* Elias had known the permit was dying, had known the company would strip the site the moment it lapsed, and instead of publishing, instead of calling his son, instead of doing anything a rational man would do — he had simply stopped. Left the trenches open like unbandaged wounds and walked into the flood season.

The coroner's report said heart failure. The river said otherwise, in the way rivers always did: found him at the waterline, they told Yara, at the site he'd refused to leave.

* * *

It rained that night, hard, the kind of rain that sounds like argument. Yara slept in the one intact corner of the main tent under a tarp, on a cot that still held the ghost-shape of his father's shoulders in the canvas, and woke before dawn to a sound he couldn't name — a deep, resonant groan, like the earth clearing its throat.

The river had risen again. Two meters overnight. Where the lower trenches had been there was now moving water, and where the bank had been there was now nothing, the erosion biting inland with every hour.

And in the grey light, caught between two pulses of the current, something was turning over.

At first he took it for a log. Then it rolled, and the light caught it — not wood-brown but green, that particular sea-green of bronze that has spent centuries underwater, the color of patina and patience. It was roughly the size of a sea chest, banded with iron gone to lace, and it turned slowly in the shallows as if deciding whether to come ashore.

Yara stood hip-deep in the flood before he'd fully decided to move, hands closing on cold metal, feet skidding in the silt. The chest was heavy in the way of things packed dense — not with water, he realized, hauling it toward the bank, because waterlogged things slosh and shift. This was solid. Sealed. Whatever was inside had been shut away deliberately, and recently enough that the seals hadn't rotted.

He got it onto the mud above the waterline and knelt over it, breath ragged, rain starting again in fine needles.

On the lid, stamped deep into the bronze, was a crest.

He knew crests. He'd grown up in a house where heraldry books were bedtime reading, where his father had taught him to read coats of arms the way other fathers taught fishing. This one he did not know — and that was the point. A sun disc split by a river's meander, flanked by two figures standing back to back, and beneath them a script he recognized only as *old*, older than the colonial surveys, older than anything the district archives admitted to.

Because the nation it belonged to did not exist. Not anymore. Not officially. The Karaveshi kingdom — if that's what the script spelled — had been declared extinct by the census of his grandfather's era, its villages struck from the maps, its people redistributed into ledgers under other names. Every text agreed. *Extinct. Assimilated. Gone.*

Every text except the ones his father had spent nine years out here not writing.

Yara sat back on his heels in the rain and looked at the thing between his knees, and the grief that had sat in him like swallowed stone all these months began, very slowly, to change state. Grief curdles, if you leave it. It goes sour, then sharp, then — if you're unlucky, or lucky — it becomes curiosity, which is just grief that has learned to ask questions.

His father had died chasing this chest. Or guarding it. With Elias those might be the same verb.

Yara turned the chest over, looking for a seam, a lock, a keyhole. There was a hasp, sealed with lead, and pressed into the lead — a thumbprint. Small. Deliberate. His father's, he'd have bet the estate he was liquidating.

*You could have called me,* Yara said aloud, to no one, to the rain, to the man-shaped absence in the canvas behind him. *You could have told me any of it.*

The river said nothing. Rivers never did. They just kept what they kept, and gave it up on their own schedule, and this one had given him thirty days, a looted site, and a locked box bearing the crest of a country that wasn't supposed to exist.

He needed a boat. He needed someone who knew this water better than the maps did — because the maps, he was beginning to suspect, were exactly the problem.

Downstream, he'd heard at the permit office, there was a pilot who ran cargo through the flooded channels when everyone else tied off. A woman named Noor. People said she'd known his father.

People said a lot of things about his father. None of them, Yara was learning, were the whole truth.

He put his shoulder to the bronze chest and began dragging it uphill, toward the tent, toward the thirty days already bleeding away, while behind him the river rose and rose, patient as a creditor, keeping whatever else it meant to give him.
