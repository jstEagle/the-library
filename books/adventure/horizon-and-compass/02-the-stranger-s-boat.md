## 2. The Stranger's Boat

Noor had been mending nets on the *Sampaguita*'s deck when the stranger came down the mud-slick stairs to the stilt docks, carrying a bronze chest like a man carries a coffin he isn't sure is empty. She knew before he said a word whose son he was. The river had already told her — the way it does, the way it tells everything eventually, in its own slow grammar of silt and rumor.

He was too clean for the camp. That was her first thought. City shoes ruined by one day of mud, a shirt that still remembered an iron. But his hands were wrong for the rest of him: rope-burned, blistered at the palms, the hands of someone who had spent the night hauling things out of water that did not want to give them up.

"You're Noor," he said. Not a question. "My father kept your name in his ledger. Eleven years of entries."

"And you're the son who never came." She set down her netting needle and looked at him until he shifted his weight. "Yara Pemberton. You have your mother's face and your father's stubbornness, which is a cruel combination, I'm told."

"I need a pilot. Upstream. To the upper concession, past the second bend."

"Everyone needs a pilot past the second bend this time of year." She rose, joints complaining, and nodded at the chest. "And what's that?"

"Salvage," he said. "Mine by permit."

The word landed between them like a fish slapped on planks. *Permit.* Elias Pemberton had held permits for nineteen years and had never once let a piece of paper tell him where the river ended and his conscience began.

"The river's rising," she said instead. "Monsoon's three weeks early. The company boats won't go past Kilometer Nine, and the ones that will charge you more than that chest weighs in gold."

"Name your price."

She almost laughed. He sounded exactly like his father — that flat certainty that everything could be bought if you found the right counter. Elias had never once offered her money. He'd offered her truth, or silence, or the wheel of his boat on long nights when her hands shook too badly to hold anything else. She had hated him for it and come anyway, every time.

"No money," she said, and watched surprise move across his face like weather. "You open that chest on my deck, whatever it holds, and I see it first. Everything. Every map, every letter, every coin. You share it all, or you can swim upstream yourself."

"That's not how salvage law—"

"I don't care how salvage law works. This is my boat and my river, and the river keeps its own laws." She stepped closer, close enough to smell the bronze on him — verdigris and old wax and something underneath, faintly, like incense burned in a closed room. "Your father owed me a debt he never paid. Maybe you'll pay it without knowing you are."

* * *

They left at dawn, or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of wet slate. The monsoon had come early and hungry; the river had swallowed the landing steps at Tanjung Wanas whole, and the marker posts she'd known since girlhood showed only their painted tips, like drowned men waving.

Yara Pemberton sat in the bow with the chest locked between his knees and watched the banks slide by as though reading them. He didn't talk much. She decided she could forgive him that. Talking was a city habit anyway; out here, silence was just another instrument you learned to play.

But she tested him once, because she couldn't help herself.

"Do you know why your father stayed?" she called over the engine noise. "Nineteen years. Everyone thought he was mad. A doctor of archaeology, living in a rotting stilt camp, dredging mud for nothing."

"He wasn't dredging for nothing," Yara said. "There were finds. I've seen the inventory lists. They just... stopped. Twelve years ago, everything stops. No reports filed, no artifacts shipped. He kept paying the permit fees out of his own pocket, though. For years."

"And did you ever ask him why?"

"I asked him plenty of things." His voice went flat and hard, a stone dropped in deep water. "He wrote me letters I never answered. Then he stopped writing, and then he died, and now I'm here settling an estate I didn't want."

Noor steered around a half-submerged fence post — someone's cattle pen, drowned now, the third farm lost to the water this season — and said nothing. There was a particular kind of anger that only children of the devoted carried, she knew. It wasn't rage at cruelty. It was rage at being loved less than a thing. Less than a river, a ruin, a question. She recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting.

The rain started an hour past the second bend, fat warm drops that thickened into a curtain. She tied down the tarp and shouted for him to do the same with the chest, and he shouted back that it was sealed, watertight, "built to survive worse than this," and there was something in his voice — awe, maybe, or its twin, grief — that made her look at him properly for the first time.

*Built to survive.* Whoever had made that chest had meant whatever was inside it to last. Meant it to be found, someday, by someone. Or meant it never to be found at all. With bronze, with seals, with rivers, you could never be sure which.

* * *

They made the upper anchorage by late afternoon, wedged the *Sampaguita* into the lee of a limestone bluff where the current went slack and confused. Through the rain they could see the dig site across the water — or what remained of it. Tarps shredded to ribbons. Scaffolding down. The looters had been thorough and the flood had finished the job, erasing Elias Pemberton's nineteen years into a smear of mud and broken bamboo.

Yara stood at the rail a long time, saying nothing. When he finally turned away, his eyes were red, and he pretended they weren't, and she let him pretend.

"Now," she said. "A deal's a deal. My deck, my light, my eyes first."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and then knelt and set the chest on the boards between them. It was beautiful work, she had to admit. Bronze gone green-gold with age, edges cast with waves and river-serpents, and stamped dead center with a crest: a horizon line beneath a rising sun, split by a single vertical stroke.

"I know that mark," she said quietly. "It's on the boundary stones upriver. Old ones. My grandmother said they marked the Sun-Under-Water people. The nation that drowned."

"Nations don't drown," Yara said. "That's what the histories say. The Kerajaan Air went under in the great flood four hundred years ago. Whole civilization, gone in a season. There's no record of survivors. No descendants. Nothing."

"My grandmother would have argued with your histories."

"Your grandmother," he said, working a slim pry bar into the seal seam, "wasn't a historian."

"No," Noor agreed. "She was a witness. Different trade entirely."

The seal cracked with a sound like a knuckle popping, loud even over the rain. Inside, packed in wax-soaked linen, lay compartments — three of them, nested, each sealed with its own wax plug stamped with the same crest. Yara lifted the first and broke it open with his thumbs.

Maps.

Not treasure. Not gold. Maps, drawn on waterproofed calico in ink that had barely faded, in two hands — one precise and academic, one looser, quicker, annotated in a script Noor recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water. She'd seen that hand before. On charts Elias kept rolled in oilcloth under his bunk. On notes tucked into her payment envelopes, years of them, little navigational secrets: *the channel shifts after the third moon, trust the heron, not the buoy.*

Survey maps. Dozens of them. And every single one marked villages.

Villages that officially did not exist.

She spread them out with hands that weren't quite steady while the rain hammered the tarp overhead. Settlements along the upper reaches — stilt towns, fish weirs, terraced gardens on the flood bluffs, a temple complex rendered in loving detail, occupied, maintained, *inhabited*. Dates in the margins. Recent dates. Last year. Last month. Census counts in Elias's tight academic hand beside population figures in the other, quicker script.

"This can't be right," Yara whispered. "These sites were excavated. Published. Declared uninhabited after the flood stratum. I read the papers. My father probably wrote half of them."

"He wrote all of them," Noor said. Her mouth had gone dry. "And then he stopped writing them. Look at the dates on these maps against the dates on his publications. He surveyed all of this—" she tapped a village cluster, forty souls, sixty, a hundred and twelve, "—and then he buried it. Filed nothing. Reported nothing. Let the world believe the Sun-Under-Water people died four centuries ago."

"Why? Why would he—"

She was already pulling the second compartment free of the wax, already breaking the seal, because some part of her had understood before he had, the way you understand a storm coming by the smell of it. Letters. Bundles of them, tied with cord gone soft as skin.

And on top of the bundle, addressed in that quick familiar hand, a single envelope bearing her own name.

*For Noor, when the chest comes up.*

Yara stared at it. Then at her. Rain drummed. Somewhere upriver, thunder rolled along the bluff like a door being opened very slowly.

"You knew about this," he said. Not angry yet. Worse — afraid. "You knew what he was hiding."

"No," Noor said honestly, lifting the envelope but not opening it, because some doors you knock on first even inside your own head. "I knew he was hiding *something*. I just never knew it had my name on it."

Lightning walked the ridgeline downstream, and in its white flash she saw the rival crew's boat — the company barge, running dark, no lights, coming hard up the channel toward their anchorage through weather no sane pilot would sail.

"They found us," she said, and reached for the wheel.
