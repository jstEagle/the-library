## 3. Monsoon Rising

**Noor**

Noor smelled the other boat before she saw it — diesel and cigarette smoke riding the wind ahead of the rain, the particular stink of a Meridian Concessions crew running their engines hard against the current. She cut her eyes to Yara at the stern, his hands full of rope he wasn't really coiling.

"Company boat," she said. "Two hundred meters and closing."

He looked upriver as if he could argue with it. "We're in open water. They can't—"

"They can do whatever they like until your permit dies. You know that better than I do." She eased the throttle down, buying seconds, thinking fast. The channel here braided through drowned forest, dead trees standing in brown water like the masts of a fleet that had sunk standing up. She knew every one of them by heart. The company pilots didn't. "Sit down. Hold something."

The sky had been bruising all morning — a green-black bruise spreading from the west, the kind of light that made old river people pull their children indoors and bar the doors. The monsoon was coming early this year. Everyone on the water had said so for a week. Everyone except the men who wrote permits in air-conditioned offices, apparently.

The company boat was a flat-deck salvage barge with an aluminum skiff lashed alongside, and it came around the bend with the arrogance of something that had never once been told no by a river. Noor counted six figures on deck. One of them raised a megaphone.

*Concession inspection. Heave to.*

Yara stood up. "This is my father's concession," he shouted back, and even from the wheelhouse Noor could hear the crack in it — grief dressed up as legal argument.

"Not after Friday," the megaphone said, and laughed.

She put the wheel over hard.

* * *

**Yara**

The boat dove between two drowned kapok trees and the world went green and dripping, branches raking the roof, and behind them the megaphone's voice dissolved into engine roar and cursing. Yara braced himself over the bronze chest where it sat lashed amidships, wrapped in tarpaulin, and felt — absurdly, shamefully — like a boy guarding a secret from schoolmasters.

Noor flew the boat through the drowned forest like a woman reading scripture aloud from memory. Twice the hull kissed submerged roots with a sound like knuckles cracking. Once they passed so close to a dead tree that bark peeled off along the gunwale, leaving a pale scar.

"You've run this stretch before," he said.

"I ran it with your father." She didn't look back at him. "Eleven times. He never told you?"

"He never told me anything."

"Yes," she said quietly. "That's what he told me about you."

Before Yara could decide whether that was a wound or an accusation, the sky opened.

It did not begin raining; it fell, all at once, a wall of water so dense the drowned forest vanished, so loud that speech became useless. Noor killed the engine rather than drive blind, and they drifted, rocking, in a gray roaring world. Yara hauled the chest toward him and crouched over it, and that was when the barge's bow materialized out of the rain ten meters away, huge and black, and the impact took the boat's stern like a hand swatting a fly.

* * *

**Noor**

Later, Noor would remember it in pieces: the crack of the hull opening along its seam; Yara's arms locked around the chest; the river pouring in cold and muscular, taking the boat out from under them in under a minute. She remembered kicking free of the wheelhouse as the cabin went under, surfacing in rain so thick she couldn't tell water above from water below. She remembered finding Yara because he was screaming — not for help, but *the chest, the chest* — and swimming to him through the flood with her lungs full of fury.

They got the chest onto a root shelf of one of the great drowned trees, and themselves after it, and clung there while the company barge wallowed nearby, its crew shouting things the storm ate whole. The boat was gone. Everything not lashed down was gone. The river had taken its tithe and asked no permission.

And then lightning walked across the sky close enough to taste metal, and the chest, sitting in a shallow of rainwater on the root shelf, gave a sound like a sigh.

The seam had split. Forty years of river pressure and one bad night, and the seal Elias Pemberton had trusted to outlast everything had finally failed.

"No," Yara said — to the chest, to the storm, to the whole arrangement of the universe. He pulled the lid anyway.

* * *

**Yara**

Rain hammered the bronze. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were documents — dozens of them — and Yara lifted the first bundle into the lightning-flash and read by strobe-light, three words at a time.

Survey maps. He'd expected survey maps; Noor had already found those in the first compartment days ago, villages marked where official maps showed nothing but swamp. But these were letters. Correspondence. His father's handwriting, which he hadn't seen since he was fourteen, marching across the pages in furious, careful lines.

*To the Ministry of Culture: I must formally retract my preliminary report of March 1979. The site designated KP-4 does not represent an abandoned settlement. It is inhabited. The population has maintained continuous occupation for — I am now certain — over four centuries.*

Lightning. Next page.

*To Dr. R. Osei: You are asking me to falsify the record. I am asking you to consider why the record wants falsifying. Every expedition that has approached the upper channels has come back with artifacts in their crates and diseases in their bloodstreams. The people of the delta watched the last survey team die of fever and buried them respectfully, and then moved their village two channels east, and waited for us to forget. They are not lost. They are hiding from us. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire moral content of my profession.*

His hands were shaking. Rain ran off his chin onto the oilcloth.

*To E. Pemberton (my son), if this ever reaches you:*

He stopped breathing.

*I know what you think of me. You have reason. I chose the river over you, year after year, and I let you believe it was ambition, because the truth would have endangered people who trusted me. Here is the truth, then, too late to be any use to either of us: I found a living nation, Yara. Not ruins. People. And the moment I reported it, the concessions would come, the roads would come, the disease and the lawyers and the cameras. So I buried my own discovery. I spent thirty years making myself into the kind of man whose work nobody trusts enough to follow. If you are reading this, then the river has decided otherwise, and I am sorry — sorry that I made you carry my silence without knowing it was a gift, and sorrier still that I couldn't find a way to give it to you gently.*

*The village is real. The people are real. Their name for themselves translates, badly, as the People of the Two Horizons. Whatever you do with what's in this chest, be worthy of them.*

*Your father, who loved you in the only language he had.*

The lightning stopped for a long moment, and Yara sat in the dark with the letter pressed to his chest, and the rain came down on him like forgiveness, which is not the same as absolution, and hurt more.

* * *

**Noor**

Noor watched him read, and understood, and hated how much she understood. She'd found her own letter in that first compartment weeks ago — a debt note, four years old, Elias promising her payment for runs she'd made in the dark, cargo she'd agreed not to ask about. Medicine, it turned out. School supplies. Radio batteries. She had ferried a nation's survival upriver one crate at a time and called it smuggling, and Elias had died owing her eleven months' wages, and she had come on this trip for the money and stayed for something she refused to name.

Now Yara looked up at her across the chest, rain streaming down his face, and his expression was the worst thing she'd ever seen on a grown man: hope and ruin fighting for the same ground.

"There's a living community," he said. "Upstream. My father hid them. This—" he lifted the letters, the maps, forty years of deliberate erasure done by one stubborn man with a pen — "this would rewrite archaeology. This would make my name. All I have to do is publish."

"And drown them," Noor said. "Roads. Loggers. Your name carved on a research station where their grandmothers used to plant rice."

"I know." His voice cracked. "I know, but Noor — he let me hate him. Thirty years. He let me hate him to protect strangers, and I'm holding the proof, and part of me—" he stopped, jaw working, rain doing the work of tears whether he permitted it or not.

Downstream, the company barge had recovered its skiff. Through a break in the rain Noor saw lanterns moving on its deck, men rigging a line, preparing to sweep the floodwater for wreckage. For them. For the chest.

"We have maybe an hour before they find this tree," she said. "And the permits die Friday. And the monsoon just ate our boat." She held out her hand. "So. Do we run upstream to people who owe us nothing, carrying proof that could destroy them — or do we let the company fish this chest out of the river and call it salvage?"

Yara looked at the letters. At the dark water where his father's secrets had floated for forty years. Then he took her hand.

"Upstream," he said. "But we go tonight, and we go empty-handed — and first I need to know exactly how much of this river you can lie to."

Noor grinned despite everything, despite the drowned boat and the coming dawn and the lanterns sweeping closer through the rain.

"Kid," she said, hauling him to his feet as thunder rolled down the channel like a door being opened, "I learned lying to rivers from the best. Now hold on — the two horizons are that way, and the water's rising faster than your conscience."
