## 1. The Weight of Brass

The pawnshop on Calle del Ancla smelled of dust, old varnish, and the particular sadness of things that had once belonged to people who needed money more than memories. Astrid Villalobos knew that smell intimately. He'd been breathing it for three weeks now, one instrument at a time.

"Again?" said Doña Eufemia, peering at him over the counter through spectacles thick enough to start fires. She was seventy if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope and a voice that could carry across a harbor in a gale. "You've sold me everything but your teeth, boy."

"The teeth stay," Astrid said, setting his clarinet on the glass counter between a chipped porcelain saint and a taxidermied pufferfish wearing a tiny sombrero. "A man has to keep some standards."

"That's what you said about the guitar."

"The guitar was different."

"How?"

"It was my mother's." He heard how it sounded even as he said it — defensive, thin, the kind of argument that folded under its own weight. "This is just a clarinet. I bought it myself. Secondhand. It squeaks in the upper register."

Eufemia lifted the clarinet, turned it in the light, and squinted down its length like a captain sighting along a bowsprit. "It's a decent horn."

"It's a decent horn," he agreed.

"You'll want forty."

"I'll want sixty."

"I'll give you fifty and throw in the story that you haggled well, so you can tell it at parties."

Astrid laughed despite himself — a short, surprised sound, the first one he'd managed all day. That was Eufemia's gift. She could strip you of your last possession and somehow make you feel like you'd won something. It was probably why she'd outlasted every other business on the street. That, and the rumor she kept a machete under the counter, which Astrid had never verified because he valued curiosity less than his fingers.

"Fifty," he said, and shook her hand.

She counted out the bills slowly, folding each one with the ceremony of a woman wrapping a body. "That's the last of them, then? The instruments?"

"That's the last."

"What will you do?"

He looked at her. Outside the window, the harbor lay flat and silver in the late afternoon light, fishing boats knocking gently against their moorings, gulls wheeling and complaining overhead like creditors. The whole town ran on the water, lived off the water, buried its dead facing the water. And Astrid Villalobos, son of the most famous salvage man the coast had ever produced, was selling his music one piece at a time to buy rice and pay rent on a room the size of a coffin.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Something. Anything. I'm not picky anymore."

Eufemia made a noise in her throat — the disapproving cluck of a woman who had opinions about young men and no hesitation about sharing them. "Your father would be ashamed."

"My father," Astrid said, "is dead."

"Dead men can still be ashamed. It's one of their few remaining powers."

"He'd be ashamed anyway," Astrid admitted. "He thought music was a hobby for people afraid of real work." He paused. "He also thought pawnshops were where dignity went to drown, so at least I'm disappointing him consistently."

"There it is," Eufemia said. "There's the Villalobos tongue. Your father had it too. Could insult a hurricane right out of the sky, that man." Her voice softened by a degree, which for Eufemia was roughly equivalent to weeping. "I was sorry to hear about him. Truly."

"Three months ago." Astrid took the bills. "Thank you."

"You never came to the funeral."

"I wasn't invited."

"You're his *son*."

"I told you," Astrid said, moving toward the door. "We weren't close."

The bell above the door jangled as he stepped out into the heat, and behind him he heard Eufemia mutter something to the pufferfish, probably. She talked to it when customers left. He'd caught her at it once. The pufferfish, for its part, never gave away whatever she told it.

* * *

He didn't go straight home. Home was a rented room above the chandlery with a mattress that sagged like a tired hammock and a window that faced a wall, and there was only so much of that a man could take before noon. Instead he walked the waterfront, because the waterfront was free, and because — though he'd have denied it under torture — it was the closest thing he had left to his father.

Emilio Villalobos had spent forty years on this water. Forty years hauling wrecks off sandbars, raising cargo from the green dark, charting channels nobody else dared. There were still old-timers at the fish market who'd light up at his name, launch into stories about the time he'd freed the grounded freighter *Santa Inés* in a rising storm, or the time he'd dived a wrecked patrol boat and come up holding a strongbox and a live moray eel, and which one he'd been more excited about remained a matter of scholarly dispute. Emilio Villalobos: half legend, half headache, entirely gone.

And Astrid had learned about his death from a newspaper. A stranger's phone call, technically — a woman from the harbormaster's office, polite and careful, asking if he was the son, saying she was sorry for his loss, saying the service would be Saturday. No letter. No visit. Nothing in his father's famously meticulous handwriting, the handwriting that labeled every jar in every workshop, that had annotated every chart Astrid had ever seen. Three months later, Astrid still couldn't decide which hurt worse: that his father hadn't reached out, or that Astrid hadn't been able to make himself go.

He sat on the sea wall, unwrapped the last of the bread he'd bought that morning, and ate it watching the boats. A trawler came in low and heavy, nets dripping. Two kids fished off the pilings with more optimism than equipment. Life went on around him with the cheerful indifference life always showed.

His hand went to his jacket pocket without his permission. It did that sometimes. Found the shape there, the weight, the small dense circle of brass.

The compass.

His father's compass — the one thing from the estate, such as it was, that had been set aside for him. Not willed exactly; there hadn't been time for lawyers and formalities before the debts surfaced. Just a note from the harbormaster's office: *Your father kept this in his breast pocket. We assumed you'd want it.* Assumed wrong, mostly. What Astrid had wanted was an explanation, an apology, a single sentence in his father's voice explaining why they'd gone eleven years without speaking. What he'd gotten was a compass.

But he carried it anyway. Every day. Told himself it was because it might be worth something — brass, good make, antique. Told himself he'd sell it when things got truly desperate. Knew, in the place where he kept the truths he didn't say out loud, that he would sooner sell his teeth.

He took it out now. Turned it over in his palm. The case was worn smooth at the edges where forty years of a thumb had worried it. The lid opened on a hinge that still moved like silk, and inside, beneath glass gone slightly cloudy with age, the needle swung and settled and pointed — as needles do — north.

Except.

Astrid frowned. He'd noticed it before, in the first weeks after the funeral, and decided he was imagining it. But there it was again. The needle wasn't pointing north. Not quite. It was pointing north and east, deflected maybe fifteen degrees off true, steady as a sermon, as if north itself had relocated somewhere out over the delta.

He tapped the case gently. The needle shivered and returned to its stubborn bearing.

"Magnetic anomaly," he murmured. "Local iron. Something in the seawall." All reasonable explanations. All explanations he'd already tested and discarded — walked ten blocks inland once, specifically, to see if the deviation corrected itself. It hadn't. The needle held its strange bearing like a dog holding a point.

He closed the lid. Put it back in his pocket. Tried to feel nothing about it, and failed, as usual.

* * *

The debt collector found him at sunset, which Astrid would later decide was fitting, because debt collectors are creatures of timing and they always arrive at the exact moment the day has given you everything it's going to give.

He was sitting outside Cantina la Perla Negra with a beer he couldn't afford, nursing it the way you nurse a fire you're trying to stretch through winter, when the shadow fell across his table. Big shadow. Broad-shouldered, narrow-headed, wearing a linen suit that cost more than everything Astrid had pawned that month combined.

"Astrid Villalobos."

Not a question. Debt collectors don't ask questions they already know the answers to; it's bad for morale.

"No," Astrid said.

The man smiled without warmth, pulled out the opposite chair, and sat down uninvited, which told Astrid everything. "My name is Bruno Salcedo. I represent certain financial interests." He laid a leather folder on the table, square and heavy as a brick. "You are Astrid Villalobos. Son of Emilio. Which makes this conversation inevitable, so let's not waste each other's evening pretending otherwise."

Astrid looked at him. Broad face, small eyes, the patient menace of a man who'd done this a thousand times and found it neither pleasant nor unpleasant — just work. Somewhere behind him, the cantina's radio played a bolero, and the bartender polished glasses, and everyone very carefully did not look over.

"Corvo," Astrid said.

Salcedo's eyebrows rose a millimeter. Genuine surprise, almost respect. "You know the name."

"Everyone knows the name. Corvo Salvage. Half the fleet on this coast flies his colors. My father hated him." He took a slow pull of his beer. "One of the few opinions of his I shared."

"Then you'll appreciate the irony." Salcedo opened the folder with two fingers, the way a surgeon opens a patient. "Your father owed us. Notes of credit, drawn over the past two years. Equipment leases, vessel charter, advances against expected recovery. Your father was financing an expedition, Mr. Villalobos. A large one. Upriver, into the delta."

The word landed in Astrid's chest like a dropped anchor. *The delta.* His father had talked about the delta for years — at family dinners, back when there were family dinners, gesturing with a fork, eyes lit up like a boy's. *There's a galleon up there, mijo. The San Telmo. Lost with all her cargo in '06. Everyone thinks she broke up on the outer bars. Everyone's wrong. I have the charts. I have the proof.* Eleven years of silence hadn't erased the memory of that fork-waving certainty.

"He never found anything," Astrid said.

"He found something," Salcedo said. "Or believed he did. Belief is expensive." He rotated the folder so Astrid could read it. Numbers marched down the page in tidy columns, and the numbers were large — larger than Astrid had ever seen attached to his own name, numbers with commas in places commas shouldn't be. "Upon your father's death, these obligations pass to his estate. As his sole heir—"

"I inherited a compass," Astrid said flatly. "And a room I rent. There's no estate. There's nothing."

"There's you." Salcedo folded his hands on the folder, a gesture of terrible patience. "Mr. Corvo is not a cruel man. He's a thorough one. There's a difference, and you'll come to appreciate it. He doesn't want your furniture. He doesn't want your—" his gaze flicked, brief and contemptuous, to Astrid's empty hands, "—clarinets. He wants the debt honored. And he's prepared to be generous about how."

"Generous."

"Thirty days. Thirty days to either settle the balance or present Mr. Corvo with something of equivalent value." Salcedo stood, buttoned his jacket, and left the folder on the table like a stone on a grave. "Your father's expedition notes are in there. Charts, correspondence, the location data he purchased. Mr. Corvo thought you might find them... useful. Family sentiment being what it is."

"And if I can't pay?"

Salcedo smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was worse. "Then Mr. Corvo recovers his losses however he sees fit. He's very fitful about recovering." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Thirty days, Mr. Villalobos. Enjoy the beer. It's the last thing anyone in this town will buy you."

He walked out into the dusk, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be because everywhere would wait for him.

* * *

Astrid sat for a long time after that. The bolero ended. Another began. His beer went warm and he drank it anyway, because wasting it felt like one more loss.

Finally he opened the folder.

His father's handwriting covered page after page — tight, precise, obsessive, the handwriting of a man who trusted ink more than people. Tide tables. Depth soundings. Letters to suppliers Astrid vaguely remembered from childhood, men who'd sat at their kitchen table smelling of tar and tobacco while Emilio pitched them his dream. And in the margins, in the spaces between the columns of figures, little annotations that were unmistakably, painfully personal:

*Ask A. if he remembers the lighthouse at Punta Verde. He was six. He cried until we let him climb it.*

Astrid put the pages down. Looked at the water, which had gone dark and gold at the edges where the last light was leaving.

*Ask A.*

Eleven years of silence, and the man was still writing to him in the margins of his own obsession.

His hand found the compass in his pocket. Drew it out. Opened the lid. In the lamplight spilling from the cantina window, the needle swung, steadied, and pointed its impossible bearing — north and east, out toward the delta, out toward the San Telmo, out toward whatever his father had died chasing.

"Thirty days," Astrid said to the compass, to the water, to the ghost in the margins. "You absolute bastard. Even dead, you're spending my money."

The needle trembled, very slightly, as if amused.

He closed the lid, pocketed it, and went to find out what a man had to do to get a berth on a boat going upriver.
