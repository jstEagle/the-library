## 2. Six Tides of Silence

The pawnshop smelled of brass polish and other people's regrets, and Astrid Villalobos had spent enough mornings in it to know both by heart.

"You're back," said Doña Eufemia, without looking up from her newspaper. She was seventy-something, sharp as a fishhook, and had run the shop since before Astrid was born. "Let me guess. The flute again."

"The flute again."

"You sold it to me Tuesday. You want it back Thursday. You'll sell it to me again Monday. Astrid, mi hijo, at some point we should just skip the middleman and you can live in the back room with the accordion nobody wants."

"That accordion has character."

"The accordion has termites." She set down the paper and studied him over her spectacles. Her eyes, milky at the edges but sharp at the center, went straight to the thing on the counter between them — the compass, sitting in its nest of chipped velvet, brass case worn to a soft gold at the edges where forty years of a man's thumb had rubbed it.

Astrid covered it with his hand. An old reflex. A stupid one, maybe, but his.

"I'm not selling the compass, Doña."

"I didn't ask." She slid the flute's ticket across the counter and pushed a small stack of bills after it. "I asked nothing. I am an old woman minding my own shop. But I'll say this for free, since you keep my flute in circulation: a man who won't sell the one thing he owns that's worth real money is a man who hasn't finished being angry at somebody."

He pocketed the bills and didn't answer, which was its own answer.

His father had bought the compass before Astrid was born — before the expeditions, before the maps, before the long silences that eventually calcified into years. Emiliano Villalobos had carried it up every river on the coast, through every mangrove throat and drowned forest, and when he'd finally washed up back in port for good — older, poorer, and stranger — he'd left it to Astrid in a cardboard box along with a debt ledger and a map fragment so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood.

*The San Telmo*, the fragment said, in his father's cramped hand. Coordinates that didn't match any chart Astrid could find. And beneath them, underlined twice: *upriver, past the second bend, where the water forgets to move.*

Astrid had spent three years telling himself the fragment was the fantasy of a dying man. The debt collectors had spent those same three years telling him it was an asset.

* * *

The news came at the Cantina del Faro, where the radio behind the bar was tuned — as it always was, as it had been for thirty years — to the coastal service band, because half the men drinking there had sons or brothers or old selves out on the water.

Astrid was nursing his second beer and doing arithmetic he already knew the answer to. Corvo Salvage held his father's notes. Fourteen thousand, plus interest that grew like mold. The letter had come that morning, on paper heavy enough to be an insult: *Payment due in six weeks. The company is prepared to discuss alternative arrangements.* Alternative arrangements, in Corvo's language, meant his father's maps, his father's charts, his father's compass — everything with salt in its blood, signed over to a man who'd never been upriver in his life and never needed to be, because upriver came to him.

"—repeat, the research vessel *Almirante* reports no contact with the Villalobos expedition since Tuesday's scheduled broadcast," the radio said.

Astrid's beer stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Coastal authorities characterize the silence as, quote, 'probably equipment failure,' given the expedition's remote position in the upper delta. The elder Villalobos is described as, quote, 'an experienced riverman.' Listeners with information are asked to—"

The bartender turned it down when the news moved on to a fishing quota dispute, and the cantina's noise closed back over the gap like water over a dropped coin. Nobody else had even looked up. Half of them had never heard of Emiliano Villalobos. The other half remembered him as the man who talked to rivers, which in a port town is a polite way of saying the man who came back wrong.

Astrid sat there for a long time.

*Probably equipment failure.* His father's radio had been a war-surplus set he'd maintained with a jeweler's care. The man had once repaired it with a spoon, a candle, and what he'd claimed was a prayer but had probably been solder. Emiliano Villalobos did not have equipment failure. Emiliano Villalobos had weather, and patience, and enemies.

Astrid paid for his beer and walked out into the heat with the compass in his pocket, heavier than brass had any right to be.

* * *

That night, in his room above the chandlery — a room with a bed, a chair, a window that faced the wrong way for the view and the right way for the wind — Astrid laid the compass on the table and watched it lie to him.

That was the thing about the compass. It had always been a little wrong. His father had sworn it was the truest instrument he'd ever owned, but it didn't point north the way other compasses did. It pointed *insistently*, with a small trembling eagerness, as if it knew something. Astrid had assumed, as a boy, that it was simply broken, and had assumed, as a man, that his father had known it was broken and loved it anyway, the way you love an old dog that barks at nothing.

At a quarter past midnight, it began to swing.

Not the lazy drunken wander of a cheap needle. It swung with purpose — a slow, deliberate arc, like a door opening — until it stood fixed on a bearing that Astrid, who had grown up reading charts on his father's knee, recognized in his bones before his head caught up.

Southeast. Up the coast. Toward the delta.

He sat down on the chair. He stood up. He picked the compass up and turned it ninety degrees, and the needle swung patiently back to the same bearing, the way a cat returns to a sunbeam.

"Okay," he said aloud, to the room, to the compass, to nobody. "Okay. You're broken and haunted. Great. Wonderful. Add it to the list."

The list was short and getting shorter. One flute, in hock. One debt, fourteen thousand and climbing. One father, silent on a river that ate radio signals and boats and, occasionally, Villaloboses. One map fragment. One compass that was either broken, haunted, or — and here was the thought he'd been stepping around for three years like a hole in a dock — pointing at something.

*Upriver, past the second bend, where the water forgets to move.*

He thought about his father's face the last time he'd seen it, at the funeral of a mutual friend, both of them standing at opposite ends of a church like two magnets turned the wrong way. His father had started to speak. Astrid had left. That was the whole conversation. That had been most of their conversations, near the end — the silences between them so long and well-maintained they'd achieved a kind of architecture.

He thought about the letter from Corvo, and about the phrase *alternative arrangements*, and about the way the salvage magnate's name was spoken in this port — quietly, the way you speak of weather that's coming.

And he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like being mocked: *the wreck could erase all of it.*

The San Telmo. A treasure galleon that official history said had gone down on the outer banks, sixty leagues from where his father's fragment pointed. Official history had never met Emiliano Villalobos. If the old man was right — if the galleon had been run upriver, scuttled deep in the delta to hide it from privateers, and had lain there for three centuries in water black as ink — then it was worth more than the debt. More than the debt times a hundred. Enough to buy back the flute, the room, the name Villalobos itself, scrubbed clean.

He told himself that was why he was going. He told himself it firmly, the way you tell a dog to stay, and with about the same expectation of being obeyed.

Because underneath the arithmetic, in the dark under the floorboards of the reasoning, there was another thing. A smaller, worse thing. A voice that said: *he's out there and he's quiet and quiet is not how he dies. He dies loudly, at ninety, mid-sentence, arguing with a river. If he's quiet, something is wrong, and if something is wrong, then the last thing you ever gave that man was the back of your coat in a church doorway.*

Astrid looked at the compass, holding its bearing with the patience of something that had waited forty years and could wait until dawn.

"I'm not doing this for him," he told it.

The needle did not move. It didn't need to. It had him where it wanted him.

* * *

He packed the way his father had taught him, which was to say: as if the bag might be dropped from a moving boat, set on fire, or confiscated, in any order. Charts — the ones he owned, which were the common ones, useless past the second bend. The map fragment, wrapped in oilcloth. A diving knife he'd bought at nineteen and never used. The letter from Corvo, because he was not a man who could leave a bill behind, even sailing into the wilderness.

He did not pack the flute. It was still in hock at Doña Eufemia's, and some part of him — the part that was already, without admitting it, writing the end of this story — had decided the flute was collateral. Proof he meant to come back.

At the window, the harbor was black and breathing. Somewhere out on the water, a buoy bell clanked its slow idiot music. Somewhere upriver, past the second bend, a radio sat silent in a camp his father had built, and a man who talked to rivers was not talking.

Astrid closed the bag and set his hand flat on the compass one last time, and felt — or imagined he felt, and decided it didn't matter which — a faint tremor through the brass, like a heartbeat, like a held breath about to be spent.

"Six weeks," he said. "That's what Corvo gave me. The tide tables say there's a boat leaving on the morning tide that takes paying passengers as far as the delta mouth, and after that I'll figure it out, because I always figure it out, because it's the only thing I'm good at."

The compass pointed southeast, toward the water that forgot to move, and said nothing, and waited.

He shouldered the bag. He blew out the lamp.

And if, on his way down the stairs, he allowed himself one sentence he'd never say out loud again — *hold on, you stubborn old riverman, I'm coming, and I still haven't forgiven you, so don't you dare be dead* — well. The stairwell was dark, and the dark keeps what you tell it.

The morning tide waited for no one. But the *Perla*, as it happened, was looking for crew.
