## 3. Crew of Strangers

The *Perla* was the kind of boat that made you want to believe in second chances, mostly because she looked like she'd already needed several.

Astrid found her tied up at the end of a rotting pier on the east side of the harbor, past the fuel depot and the fish cannery that had stopped canning anything but rumors. She was forty feet of patched steel and stubbornness, her hull painted a blue that might once have been proud, her deck cluttered with dive tanks, coiled line, and a winch that groaned in its sleep every time the tide shifted. Her name was stenciled on the stern in flaking gold: *PERLA*. Someone had tried to add a decorative flourish under it, given up halfway through, and left what looked like a small drowning fish.

He stood at the foot of the gangplank for a long moment, compass in his pocket, debt collector's card in his hand, and asked himself — not for the first time, and certainly not for the last — what exactly he thought he was doing.

The answer, as always, was nothing good. But nothing good had ever paid six weeks of interest to a man like Corvo.

"You going to stand there admiring her all day," said a voice from above, "or are you going to come aboard and admire her properly?"

She came down the plank with the easy roll of someone who'd spent more of her life on water than land. She was maybe fifty, broad-shouldered, sun-cured to the color of old rope, with gray shot through her black hair like silver thread through canvas. A wrench hung from her belt the way other people wore sidearms.

"Odalys Reyes," she said, and stuck out a hand calloused enough to file glass. "Captain. You're the one who's been asking around about delta work."

"News travels fast."

"News travels fast everywhere except where you're going." She looked him over with the frank appraisal of a woman pricing a used engine — checking for cracks, listening for knocks. "You're Villalobos's boy."

The name landed in his chest like an anchor dropped in shallow water. "You knew him?"

"I knew *of* him. Everyone in salvage knew of Emilio Villalobos. Man could read water like scripture." She turned and started back up the plank, talking over her shoulder. "Also everyone in salvage knew he owed money to people you don't owe money to, which is why I'm guessing you're here instead of at a bank asking for a polite loan. Come aboard. Mind the third step, it lies."

The third step did lie. It gave way half an inch under his boot with a sound like a small apology, and Astrid decided right then that he liked this boat.

The cabin below was low-ceilinged and warm, smelling of diesel, coffee, and old charts. A table took up most of the space, buried under maps of the delta so annotated they'd gone from documents to palimpsests. A kettle sat on a two-burner stove. In the corner, wedged between a shelf of manuals and a rack of spare parts, was a radio set — and sitting before it, very still, very quiet, was a woman.

She was younger than him by a few years, dark-haired, with the kind of face that seemed built for listening rather than speaking. She didn't turn when they came down. Her hands rested on the edge of the radio's desk, and she was looking at the equipment the way people look at graves.

"That's Marisol," Odalys said, and something in her voice changed — flattened, careful, the tone you use walking near a sleeping animal. "Our ears. Best operator on this coast, which isn't saying much, because there are only four operators on this coast and one of them is a parrot at the cantina."

Marisol turned then. Her eyes found Astrid's and held them, and he had the strange, dislocating feeling of being recognized by someone he'd never met.

"You're his son," she said. Not a question.

"I'm Astrid."

"I know." She stood. Up close she was smaller than she'd seemed, and tenser, like wire under fair insulation. "I'm sorry about your father."

"He's not dead."

"No," she said carefully. "He's not answering."

There was a difference, and both of them heard it, and neither of them said it.

Odalys cleared her throat and poured three cups of coffee without asking, which Astrid would come to understand was simply how things were done aboard the *Perla*: questions were for people with time, and nobody aboard had any. She pushed a cup into his hands.

"So," she said. "Talk. You've been asking after delta crews, you've got no boat, no gear I can see, and either money or desperation, and my guess is the second one wearing the first one's coat. What do you bring me?"

It was a fair question and he hated it. He set down the coffee.

"A compass," he said. "My father's. And a map fragment pointing at a galleon called the San Telmo."

The cabin went quiet in a new way. Even the winch outside seemed to stop groaning.

Marisol's eyes flicked to Odalys — a quick, coded glance, a whole conversation conducted in a tenth of a second — and then back to him. "The San Telmo," she repeated, flatly, the way you'd repeat a word you wanted to seem unimportant.

"You've heard of it."

"Everyone's heard of it. It's a story. Wrecked galleon, lost cargo, somewhere upriver in the delta where the charts go vague and the law goes vaguer. Sailors' nonsense." Odalys said it easily, but she'd stopped moving, and Astrid noticed that sailors who stopped moving were sailors thinking hard. "What makes you think your fragment is real?"

"It was my father's."

"And your father was a careful man or a lucky one?"

"Both, I think. Neither, lately."

Odalys snorted — almost a laugh. "That's the most honest answer I've had all season." She leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, and studied him again, longer this time. "Here's my problem, Villalobos. I've got a boat, four crew, and expenses. I don't run treasure hunts. Treasure hunts are how smart divers die and dumb ones get famous posthumously. I run salvage. Contracts, recoveries, boring profitable work. So convince me why I should point my boat and my people at a story."

"Because Corvo's pointing his fleet at it too."

The name went through the cabin like cold water through a hull. Marisol's hands, resting on the radio desk, curled slightly. Odalys's jaw tightened, and for a moment the pragmatic captain disappeared and something older and harder looked out through her eyes.

"How do you know that?" Odalys asked quietly.

"His man found me yesterday. My father owed him. The notes come due in weeks. And a man doesn't lend that much against a fisherman's promise unless he knows what the fisherman was chasing." Astrid pulled the compass from his pocket and set it on the chart table between them. Brass, dented, older than he was. "And because of this."

"What about it?"

"It points wrong."

Odalys raised an eyebrow. "Compasses do that. It's called variation, and it's why God invented mathematics."

"Not wrong like variation." He opened the lid. The needle swung, settled, swung again — and held, steady and certain, toward the northeast. Toward the delta. Toward open water, away from every heading a harbor compass should favor. "Wrong like it wants something."

Silence. Then Marisol stepped forward, slowly, and looked down at the instrument without touching it. Her face did something complicated — grief and fear and recognition braided together — and Astrid watched it happen and understood, suddenly, that she knew exactly what this compass was, and that knowing had cost her something.

"When did it start doing that?" she asked.

"The night the expedition went silent."

Marisol closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she said, "I was the last voice to hear them."

The words fell into the room and stayed there.

"Your father's expedition," she went on, her voice level with the effort of a woman carrying something heavy across ice. "I relayed their traffic for three weeks. Routine position calls, weather, engine notes. Your father had a good fist on the key. Careful. Always confirmed twice." She paused. "Then eleven nights ago the signal changed. Shorter transmissions. No position. And then nothing. Not static — nothing. There's a difference. Static means the sea's in the way. Nothing means someone turned it off."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "Did he say anything? At the end?"

Marisol looked at him for a long moment, and he saw her weigh something — saw the scales move behind her eyes — and then she said, "No. Nothing worth repeating."

Later — much later, in another chapter of his life — Astrid would remember that pause, and understand what it had cost her, and what she'd hidden inside it. But standing in the hot little cabin, he only felt the truth of her tiredness, and believed her, because grief always believes whoever offers it a place to rest.

"All right," Odalys said briskly, into the silence, because someone had to be. "Here's what I see. A rich madman's fleet heading upriver after a fairy tale. A dead-silent expedition. A haunted compass. And you — no boat, no crew, no plan beyond a map scrap and spite." She picked up the compass, weighed it in her palm like a coin she wasn't sure was gold, and set it back down. "It's the worst proposition I've heard since my brother-in-law offered to sell me a bridge."

"But?" Astrid said.

"But Corvo's men burned my cousin's charter operation down to the waterline two years ago over a salvage dispute, and the courts called it an electrical fire." Odalys's voice stayed pleasant; her eyes did not. "So I have a professional interest in seeing that man disappointed. And—" she tapped the compass, once, lightly, "—I've been on this coast thirty years, and I've seen instruments do funny things near the delta. Magnetic iron in the riverbed, the old-timers say. Or something else. Either way, if your father trusted it, and your father read water like scripture, that's worth more to me than your map fragment."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a *maybe* shaped like a yes. Terms." She held up fingers. "One: I'm captain. On my boat, my word is weather — you don't argue with it, you dress for it. Two: whatever we raise, shares are agreed before anyone touches it, in writing, witnessed. Three: if it goes bad — and treasure voyages have a definition of 'bad' that would frighten a war correspondent — you follow orders like everyone else. Four." She hesitated, just barely. "Four: we look for your father's people while we're at it. If they're alive upriver, we find them. That's not negotiable and it's not in the share agreement. It's just what boats are for."

Astrid felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was tight. "Agreed. All of it."

"Don't agree fast. Fast agreements are how men end up owing me labor."

"I've had enough of owing."

Something passed between them then — not warmth yet, but the ground warmth comes from. Odalys stuck out her hand and shook his, hard, sealing it the old way, and that was that. He was crew of the *Perla*, signed on nothing but a handshake and a shared grudge, which on this coast was worth more than paper.

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide, because Odalys said waiting was for people who thought the world would improve if given time.

Astrid spent the first hours learning the boat by being handed things. Coils of line. Fenders. A bucket with a hole in it that he was told, with a perfectly straight face, was "for morale." The crew was small and strange: Tomás, the engineer, a wide cheerful man who spoke to engines more tenderly than to people; Bruno and Félix, divers and deckhands, brothers-in-law who bickered like weather; and Marisol, who appeared on deck only to check the antenna and otherwise lived in the radio shack like a wren in a niche.

It was late afternoon, the mangroves beginning to rise green and tangled along the banks, when he finally cornered her at the rail. She was drinking tea from a tin cup, watching the water go by with an expression he was starting to recognize as her version of conversation.

"You lied to me," he said. Not angrily. Just setting it on the table between them.

Her cup paused halfway to her lips. "About what?"

"In the cabin. You said my father's last transmissions weren't worth repeating. You decided that in about two seconds. People don't decide things that fast unless they've already decided them long ago."

Marisol looked at the water. A heron lifted off the bank ahead of them, unhurried, ancient.

"You're sharper than your file says," she said.

"I don't have a file."

"Everyone has a file. Yours says: pawnshop regular, sells instruments, keeps one thing he won't sell. Mine says: talks to radios, trusts them more than people, and once kept a secret to protect someone and it went badly." She finally looked at him. "So let's not pretend we met yesterday."

The current carried them a moment in silence. Somewhere aft, Tomás was singing to the generator, something tuneless and affectionate.

"Why did you stay quiet?" Astrid asked. "About my father. About all of it."

"Because the last time I told a grieving man the whole truth quickly, he did something stupid and permanent." Her voice was quiet, even, and it cost her visibly to keep it that way. "Because you walked onto that boat with your father's compass in your pocket and rage in your face, and rage makes men sign on for revenge, and revenge upriver against Corvo is just suicide with better scenery. I wanted to know you before I armed you."

"And now?"

"Now I've watched you all day." She nodded toward the deck, where he'd spent hours hauling line beside strangers, taking orders, laughing once — actually laughing — at Tomás's song. "You work. You listen. You didn't once ask what your share would be." She turned back to the water. "So maybe you're not your father's debts. Maybe you're his charts. We'll see."

It wasn't forgiveness, and it wasn't trust, not fully. But it was a door, unlocked, and Astrid had been locked out of enough doors lately to know the value of one left ajar.

"Tell me one true thing," he said. "Anything. As a down payment."

Marisol considered. Then she reached into her shirt pocket and drew out a folded scrap of paper, soft with handling, and held it out to him without letting go of one corner.

"This is the last thing your father transmitted. Word for word. I wrote it down that night and I haven't slept properly since." Her fingers tightened on the paper. "I'll give you the rest when I'm sure you won't burn the delta down to get to Corvo. This much you should have now."

Astrid took it. Unfolded it. Read it by the last of the light.

Seven words, in his father's careful hand transcribed in hers:

*If anyone asks — I never found it.*

He looked up. "Found what?"

Marisol was already turning away, cup in hand, and the sunset caught her face just long enough for him to see that behind the calm she was afraid — not of Corvo, not of the delta, but of something she knew and he didn't.

"That," she said quietly, "is the sixth tide's problem. Right now we've got five to beat them there."

Ahead, the river widened into the first brown reaches of the delta, and somewhere up that drowned green maze, a compass in his pocket trembled toward its secret like a heart remembering how to beat.
