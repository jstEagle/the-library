## 4. What the Radio Kept

Marisol had three rules about the radio shack aboard the *Perla*: nobody touched the dials, nobody smoked near the paper logs, and nobody asked her what she'd heard on the last night of Emilio Villalobos's expedition.

Astrid Villalobos broke the third rule within an hour of coming aboard.

He didn't ask it directly. He was too proud for that, and too much his father's son — she could see that already, in the way he stood in the doorway of the shack with his arms folded like a man waiting for a verdict rather than requesting one. He'd been aboard two days. He'd learned the names of the winches. He'd made Odalys laugh exactly once, on purpose, and hadn't seemed to notice it was an achievement.

"Reyes says you were the last voice to hear them," he said. "The expedition."

"I was the last voice they reached," Marisol said, not looking up from the log book. "That's not the same thing."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is whether anyone was listening after." She closed the book. The spine was cracked and held together with electrical tape, because radio operators are always given the newest equipment and the oldest furniture. "You want coffee? I have coffee. It's terrible. The filter's more suggestion than apparatus."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a hospitality offer. Take it or leave it."

He took it. That surprised her a little. Most men who carried grief the way Astrid did — slung across both shoulders like a pack frame — refused small kindnesses on principle, as if accepting a cup of coffee might constitute surrender. But he wrapped both hands around the chipped enamel mug and sat down on the crate she kept for visitors, which put him at eye level with the transmitter, and she understood that he knew exactly what crate he was sitting on and what hung above it.

"You've been very polite," he said. "For two days. Politely not telling me something."

"Yes."

"How bad is it?"

Marisol looked at him for a long moment. He had his father's jaw and none of his father's patience; Emilio Villalobos would have charmed the answer out of her over three days and a bottle of rum. Astrid just sat there, steam rising off his terrible coffee, waiting, and there was something in the waiting that was worse than any question.

She got up, unlocked the drawer under the log shelf, and took out the cassette.

* * *

The tape was labeled in her own handwriting: **E.V. — FINAL — DO NOT ERASE**, and beneath that, in smaller letters she'd added later, at three in the morning in a portside boarding house: *I'm sorry.*

"This is against my rules," she said. "All three of them, actually. You're touching nothing, but we're near the dials, and you're definitely asking."

"I'm honored."

"Don't be. I'm breaking them because keeping them was making me sick." She threaded the tape into the deck recorder, the old backup unit, the one with the VU meter that jumped like a nervous bird. "There are four transmissions from the final night. The first three are routine. Position reports, weather griping, your father complaining about the cook. I kept those to myself because they're private and sad and no one else's business. The fourth one is why I've barely slept since."

She pressed play.

Static first — the delta ate signals alive, especially at night, when the atmosphere folded in on itself like a hand closing. Then, out of the static, a voice. Emilio Villalobos sounded exactly as Astrid must have remembered him and nothing like it at all: warm, unhurried, faintly amused by the world even at the edge of it.

*"San Telmo expedition to Perla relay, do you copy? Over."*

Marisol watched Astrid instead of the meter. His hands went still around the mug. His face did the thing faces do when a dead man speaks — a flinch, then a terrible stillness, then the effort of pretending neither happened.

*"Perla relay copies,"* her own voice said on the tape, younger-sounding, tighter. *"Go ahead, San Telmo."*

*"Position unchanged. River's dropped another half-meter, which means the sandbars have moved again, which means Ríos is swearing at the chart in three languages. Tell him next time he can do the survey himself."* A pause, filled with river noise. *"Also tell him the cook situation has resolved itself. He fired the cook. We're eating rice."*

Beside her, Astrid made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost something else entirely.

*"Copy, San Telmo,"* the tape-Marisol said. *"Anything else?"*

And here the voice changed. Not dramatically — Emilio Villalobos was too good a man for drama — but the amusement drained out of it the way water drains from a lock, evenly and completely, and what remained was flat and careful and very quiet.

*"Yes. One thing. For the record, and for whoever ends up hearing this."* A breath. *"Corvo knows."*

The static swelled. On the tape, Marisol's younger voice asked him to repeat, please confirm, say again your last —

*"I said Corvo knows. He has someone inside. Don't ask me how I know; I know the way you know weather coming. Three days ago a floatplane passed us twice at altitude, taking photographs, and yesterday we found fresh cut stakes on the bank where our own markers should be — same survey pattern, different hands. Someone is mapping this river ahead of us and behind us both. And two nights ago my radio man picked up a transmission on Corvo's working frequency, coordinates in the clear, and those coordinates are ours."*

Another pause. Longer. When the voice came back it had dropped to almost a whisper, as if he'd cupped his hand around the microphone.

*"Marisol. If anything happens to us, don't let my son come looking. Do you hear me? Whatever Corvo offers him, whatever debt he waves in front of him — the wreck isn't worth what it costs. Tell him—"*

The tape hissed. Ten seconds of pure static, the sound of the delta swallowing a signal whole, and then the recording ended, because that was where Marisol had stopped it, every time, for eleven nights running. There was more. She had never listened past it. She suspected there wasn't much more — a carrier tone, maybe, or nothing at all — but some part of her had decided that the transmission ended where she ended it, and she was not prepared to find out otherwise.

She pressed stop. The cabin was very quiet except for the tick of the cooling machine and, somewhere above them, Odalys shouting at a winch in language unprintable.

"That's it?" Astrid said. His voice came out sanded down to bare wood. "'Corvo knows.' That's the last thing he ever—"

"There was more after the static. A few seconds. Maybe a word. I never played it through."

"Why not?"

"Because if it was a word, it would have been your name." She said it before she could stop herself, and then, having started, finished: "And I couldn't carry that. Not alone, and not yet."

* * *

What happened next took less than a minute, and Marisol would spend a long time afterward turning it over, the way you turn a stone in your pocket.

Astrid stood up. He set the mug down on the log shelf with exaggerated care, the way angry people handle objects when they're trying not to throw them, and he turned to face her, and for a moment she thought — reasonably, she would argue later — that she was about to be on the receiving end of everything a son carries when he learns someone kept his father's last words from him.

"You knew," he said. "Before I ever signed aboard. Before Odalys recruited me. You knew Corvo was behind all of it — the debt, the silence, all of it — and you let me walk onto this boat thinking my father died lost."

"I let you walk onto this boat thinking your father died doing what he loved, in a place he chose, with a crew he trusted." Her voice stayed level, mostly. "Which was also true."

"You lied to me."

"I withheld. There's a difference, and I've had eleven nights to learn how thin it is." She stood too, because she refused to have this argument from below. "You want the whole confession? Fine. Here it is. Your father owed Corvo money — money Corvo engineered, loaned against a wreck only Emilio could find, so that finding it became survival. I figured that out from the traffic patterns weeks ago. And when the expedition went silent, I did the arithmetic every operator does: if I tell the son the truth, the son either stays home and rots, or comes looking angry, and angry men upstream in Corvo's river die fast. So I told Odalys enough to recruit you and not one word more, and I told myself it was strategy, and it was also cowardice, and I have not been able to tell those two apart since."

"So you used me."

"I *needed* you," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, which infuriated her. "There's a difference there too, and it's thinner than the other one. Your father asked me — on that tape, in the part I couldn't bear to finish — to keep you away. And I disobeyed him. I disobeyed a dying man's last request because I decided I knew better than he did about what his son could survive. So no, I didn't lie to protect the mission. I lied because I couldn't stand the idea of you refusing the voyage and me being the reason. I wanted you here. I told myself it was for the truth, for the record, for justice — pick a noble word — but underneath, Astrid, I wanted you here because your father was the closest thing I had to family and you're what's left of him, and I am apparently the kind of person who lies to herself in complete sentences."

The shack was small enough that the confession had nowhere to go but into him. She watched it land. Watched his anger meet hers halfway and stall there, two currents colliding, neither able to push through.

"My name," he said finally. "After the static. Was it there?"

"I don't know. I've never played it."

"Play it now."

"Astrid—"

"*Play it.*"

Her hands were not steady. She rewound the tape to the ten-second mark, set her finger above the button, and looked at him once more — a look that was asking permission without wanting to admit it needed any.

He nodded.

She pressed play.

The static ran. One second. Two. Five. At the seventh second, buried in the wash like a coin at the bottom of a fountain, there was a shape — not a word, not quite, but a rise and fall of sound that could have been a syllable, could have been wind, could have been the river itself getting the last word. Then the tape ran out onto the take-up reel with a soft plastic click, and it was over, and whatever Emilio Villalobos had said in his final seconds belonged to the static forever.

Astrid exhaled slowly. "Could have been 'run,'" he said. "Could have been 'sorry.' Could have been nothing."

"Could have been 'Marisol,'" she said. "It had that many syllables. I checked. Repeatedly. At hours I'm not proud of."

He almost smiled. It was a battered, reluctant thing, but it was real, and it changed the whole geometry of the little room.

"You know what my father would say about all this?" he said. "He'd say we're both idiots, and then he'd make coffee — good coffee, actual coffee — and he'd listen to that tape forty times until he'd wrung every coordinate and frequency out of it, and then he'd forgive you before I did, because he always forgave faster than was sensible. It drove my mother insane."

"He sounds like he was easy to love."

"He was impossible to love. That's what made it easy." Astrid picked the mug back up, found the coffee cold, drank it anyway. "Here's what happens now. I'm furious with you. I expect that fury to last somewhere between six hours and two days, during which I'll be short with you and insufferable generally, and you should feel free to call me on it. And when it burns off — and it will, because I've done the arithmetic too, and you're the only person on this boat who knows his voice, and I'm going to need that — we start over. Properly. No withholding."

"No withholding," she agreed. "On my side. Yours too. What aren't you telling me, Astrid Villalobos? Because I've been on boats my whole life, and nobody signs aboard a salvage run up a lawless river over a compass unless the compass is doing something a compass shouldn't."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the light was going copper; Odalys had stopped shouting at the winch, which meant either victory or catastrophe.

"Last night," Astrid said quietly, "the needle swung toward the delta on its own. No magnet near it. Nothing. It pointed, Marisol. Like it knew."

She felt the hair rise on her arms, and laughed at herself for it — a radio operator, spooked by a needle, as if she hadn't spent her career listening to voices arrive out of empty air.

"Well," she said. "Then I suppose we'd better get there before it changes its mind."

Above them, boots hit the deck hard, and Odalys Reyes's voice came down the companionway, tight and fast: "Marisol! Get on the horn — smoke on the southern channel, and it's moving against the current!"

They looked at each other. Neither of them moved for exactly one heartbeat — the heartbeat where two people who have just traded betrayals discover they are, irreversibly, on the same side.

Then Marisol reached for the transmitter, and Astrid reached for the door, and the *Perla* began to run.
