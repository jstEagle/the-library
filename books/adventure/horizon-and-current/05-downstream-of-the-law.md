## 5. Downstream of the Law

Odalys Reyes had smuggled a great many things past the customs cutter *Justicia* in her eleven years on the water — untaxed diesel, unlicensed rum, a wedding party of nine including one goat — but she had never before smuggled grief. It sat in her wheelhouse like a paying passenger who refused to disembark, taking up space, asking questions.

"Cut the running lights," she said. "All of them. Yes, even the anchor light, Tomás, it's not an anchor if we're moving."

Tomás killed the lights and the night came down around the *Perla* like a lid. They were running dark through the estuary mouth, engines at a murmur, riding the last of the ebb. Somewhere off the starboard bow, invisible but unmistakably present, the *Justicia* sat at her anchorage with her searchlight folded up like a sleeping bird and her crew, Odalys hoped, playing cards below decks.

"You've done this before," Astrid said. He stood beside her at the wheel, hands jammed in his pockets, wearing the expression of a man who had recently learned that the ocean was larger than his problems and was trying to decide whether that was comforting or insulting.

"Once or twice." She eased the wheel a half-point to port. "The trick is to be boring. Cutters are looking for something interesting. A dark sloop doing nothing at four knots is the least interesting thing on this water."

"And if we're interesting anyway?"

"Then we're fast, we're shallow-drafted, and I know where the sandbars bite." She glanced at him. "Relax. The worst they can do is fine us, and we haven't got any money, which makes us nearly unsinkable from a legal standpoint."

That earned the ghost of a smile. Good. The boy needed to learn that fear was cargo you could stow properly instead of letting it roll around loose below decks breaking things.

* * *

They cleared the cutter's anchorage by the width of two prayers. Odalys counted the minutes aloud under her breath — a habit from her brother, who'd claimed counting kept panic from finding a seat — and when the *Justicia*'s masthead light finally slid behind the headland, she allowed herself one long breath and then rang for full ahead.

The delta opened up around them like a green hand uncurling. Mangrove channels braided and rebraided; the water went from brown to tea-dark to something like black glass. Fireflies stitched the banks together. Somewhere a night heron complained about the state of the world, and Odalys found herself agreeing with it.

Astrid stayed at the wheel long after she told him he could sleep. She let him. There was a particular look men got when they were steering toward their fathers' mistakes, and it was better for everyone if they had something to hold.

Marisol appeared out of the radio shack with three cups of coffee so strong it could have caulked the hull. She handed one up to Astrid without a word, one to Odalys, and then leaned against the rail and looked at the water the way people look at old letters.

"He's quiet," Odalys said, low, nodding at Astrid.

"He's listening," Marisol said. "There's a difference."

"I know there's a difference. I married a listener once. Worst decision of my life; he heard everything and said nothing." She drank. "This one talks, though. Talks like a man building a wall out of words and hoping nobody notices the mortar's wet."

Marisol almost smiled into her cup. "You watch people like you chart channels."

"It's the same skill. Both of them will drown you if you guess wrong." Odalys turned the wheel a spoke. "How well did you know his father?"

For a moment she thought Marisol wouldn't answer. The radio operator had a way of going still that wasn't shyness exactly — more like a woman deciding how much of a map to show you.

"Well enough," Marisol said finally. "He taught me Morse when I was nineteen. Said a good operator hears the truth underneath the message." She looked at the antenna wire humming faintly overhead. "He was a good man who made bad arrangements."

"Aren't they all," Odalys said, and thought of Tomás's gambling debts, of her own second mortgage, of every captain who ever signed a paper with a smile and a knot in his stomach.

* * *

Near midnight they anchored in a side channel Odalys had used since she was a girl, a cut behind a mangrove island where the current forgot about you and the mosquitoes mostly didn't. She set the watches, then climbed to the wheelhouse roof with a thermos of something stronger than coffee and found, to her lack of surprise, that Astrid had already claimed the spot.

"This is my thinking chair," she said.

"There's no chair."

"That's what makes it mine. Move over anyway."

He moved over. They sat shoulder to shoulder in the dark, watching the stars do their slow rotation above the treeline, and Odalys poured two fingers into the thermos cap and handed it over.

"To boring boats," she said.

"To boring boats," he echoed, and drank, and coughed, and she pretended not to enjoy it.

For a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation. Then Astrid asked the question she'd been waiting for since the dock.

"Why are you doing this? Really. Salvage rights on the San Telmo would set you up — I get that. But you're spending fuel, risking your boat, running customs. For a wreck that might not even be there."

Odalys looked at the river sliding past the hull, black and patient.

"You know what Corvo calls his operation?" she said. "*Recuperaciones del Mar.* Recoveries of the Sea. Very elegant. Brochures and everything. What it means is: whatever sinks, Corvo owns, and whatever Corvo owns, Corvo keeps." She turned the cap in her fingers. "Three years ago he put together an expedition to a wreck off the shelf edge. The *Santa Inés*. Deep dive, mixed gas, the works. My brother Rafael was his rigger."

"What happened?"

"The lift bags failed at forty meters. Cargo net snagged, Rafi went down to free it — his job, he was good at it, he'd done it fifty times." Her voice stayed level, which took work; she'd had years of practice making it level. "Something went wrong on the surface. A squall coming in, Corvo wanting the gold on deck before weather, the usual arithmetic. They started hauling before Rafi gave the signal. His line fouled. And the winch operator — Corvo's man, a cousin of somebody — kept hauling."

Astrid had gone very still beside her.

"There was a knife on Rafi's belt," Odalys said. "Standard issue. Cutaway tool, right there on his chest strap. All anyone had to do was signal him to cut loose and swim up. But cutting loose meant abandoning the net, and the net meant money, and Corvo was watching the barometer." She exhaled slowly. "So nobody called it. And my brother, who could hold his breath four minutes and read water like scripture, drowned tangled in a cargo net forty meters down, holding a knife, because a rich man's schedule mattered more than a poor man's lungs."

The river said nothing. Rivers never did; that was why people told them things.

"There was an inquiry," she went on. "Corvo's lawyers described it as 'an unfortunate equipment interaction.' The winch operator got reassigned. Corvo sent me flowers. Orchids. I fed them to the compost and kept the vase, because the vase was good crystal and spite should be practical." She felt Astrid shift beside her, some protest gathering in him, and held up a hand. "I'm not telling you this so you'll be angry on my behalf. I'm telling you so you understand where we're going."

"Which is where?"

"Outside." She pointed east, toward the open water past the last surveyed channel. "The San Telmo lies beyond the territorial line. No coast guard. No admiralty court. No inquiry board with a stamp and a shrug. Out there, salvage law is simple: possession is nine-tenths, and the tenth is whatever you can enforce." She looked at him squarely. "Corvo has guns, divers, a ship with a reinforced bow for ramming, and lawyers on retainer for the day he comes back inside the line. We have a sloop, six souls, and your father's compass. So when people ask me if I'm worried about the law out there, I tell them the truth: the law is a harbor. Lovely place. Full of rules. And we are leaving it."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost the pawnshop bitterness, just for a moment, and underneath it was something younger and more honest.

"My father signed notes with Corvo. Did you know that?"

"I guessed. Men who chase wrecks always owe somebody."

"He didn't chase wrecks for the gold. Or — I don't know. Maybe I never knew him at all." Astrid turned the compass case over in his hands, the brass catching starlight. "Everyone keeps telling me who he was. The debt collectors knew him. Marisol knew him. You probably knew him. And all I've got is a man who missed my mother's funeral and my graduation and every Christmas for eleven years, and a compass that points somewhere it shouldn't."

Odalys considered that. She was not, by trade or temperament, a comforter — her consolations tended to run toward soup and hard tasks — but she recognized a man standing at the rail of his own life wondering how he'd come aboard.

"Here's what I know about fathers," she said. "Rafi and I had one too. Wonderful man. Terrible with money, worse with promises, dead at fifty-one with his affairs in a shoebox full of IOUs. I spent ten years being angry at the shoebox." She tapped the thermos cap against Astrid's, gently. "Then I spent ten years being angry at myself for wasting the ten years. Learn from me. Skip a decade. The anger's real and you're entitled to it, but don't sign a lease. Visit sometimes. Don't live there."

"That's terrible advice," Astrid said. "It's also the best thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"I contain multitudes," Odalys said, and refilled the cap.

* * *

She woke before dawn to the sound of Marisol's key clicking in the shack — the girl kept radio hours like a lighthouse keeps light hours, faithful and slightly unhinged — and climbed down to find her hunched over the receiver with headphones clamped on, scribbling.

"Anything?" Odalys asked.

"Traffic chatter." Marisol didn't look up. "Fishing fleet gossip, a weather advisory, and—" She stopped. Adjusted the dial a hair. Listened.

Odalys watched her face change. Not dramatically — Marisol's face didn't do dramatic — but the stillness in it deepened, the way water goes stiller, not rougher, right before something rises.

"What," Odalys said.

"Corvo's fleet." Marisol pulled the headphones down around her neck. "Harbor gossip says the *Venganza* coaled yesterday. She's provisioning heavy. Dive gear, spare compressors, and — this is the part I don't like — armed security contractors signed aboard as 'marine consultants.'"

"How heavy?"

"Heavy enough that 'consultants' is doing a lot of work." Marisol rubbed her eyes. "And Odalys — the gossip puts them sailing on tomorrow's tide. Same tide as us. Maybe hours behind."

Hours. Odalys did the arithmetic she hated: currents, channels, the smashed buoy Astrid's compass had led them past — someone had been in these waters already, and now the someone's employer was coming with a warship's worth of intent.

"Does Villalobos know yet?"

"Not unless he's developed telepathy overnight. He's asleep in the fo'c'sle."

"Wake him gently," Odalys said, and then, because honesty between captains and crews was worth more than ballast: "Actually, wake him however you like. Just make sure he's standing when you tell him."

Marisol paused at the shack door. "You're not turning back."

It wasn't quite a question, but Odalys answered it anyway. "I spent three years learning what turning back costs. It costs everything, and you still end up where you started, only older." She looked east, where the sky was going the color of a bruise healing. "We're downstream of the law now, Marisol. Whatever justice happens on this voyage, we bring it ourselves. So we'd better be faster than Corvo, smarter than his consultants, and luckier than my brother was."

She rang the engine room to life and felt the *Perla* lean into the current like a dog remembering its purpose.

Behind them, the harbor and its courts and its stamps slept on. Ahead, past the mangroves, past the line on the chart where the colors changed, the black water waited with everything in it — the galleon, the gold, the ghosts, and a man named Corvo coming down the same river with flowers in his heart and knives in his manifest.

Odalys Reyes put her hand on the wheel and steered into the dawn.

Six tides, she reminded herself. Six tides, and then the sea would settle every account itself.
