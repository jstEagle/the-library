## 6. First Tide

The mangroves began an hour after dawn, and by the second hour I understood that the delta was not a place so much as a mood — a green labyrinth that had been quietly deciding, for ten thousand years, whether to let us through.

"Port," said Odalys Reyes, without looking up from her charts.

I looked port. There was nothing there but more mangrove, roots like the knuckles of some enormous drowned hand.

"You're sure?"

"I'm always sure," Odalys said. "It's my least attractive quality and my most useful one." She tapped the chart with two fingers. "The channel bends here. The chart's forty years old, which means the sandbar has moved twice since it was drawn, which means we trust the compass and not the paper."

The compass. My father's compass sat in my palm where it had sat for most of the morning, its brass case warm from my hand, its needle doing something no honest needle should do. It didn't point north. It pointed *forward* — or rather, it pointed along a line that happened to be forward, threading us through the maze as if someone had drawn the route on the water itself in invisible ink and given this little instrument the only copy.

Every time I checked it, I felt the same small chill. It was as if my father were standing behind me, one hand on my shoulder, murmuring *left here, boy, mind the bar.* He had never called me *boy*. He had barely called me at all, in the last fifteen years. And yet here was his handwriting, written in magnetism, guiding his estranged son downriver toward a fortune he'd died chasing.

Or toward something else entirely. With my father, there had always been something else entirely.

"Astrid." Marisol appeared at my elbow with a tin mug of coffee so strong it could have stood upright on its own. "You've been staring at that thing for twenty minutes. Either it's talking to you or you're trying to hypnotize it."

"It's pointing," I said.

"Compasses do that."

"Not like this." I tilted it so she could see. The needle held steady on our heading, dead calm, while the *Perla* yawed and rolled through the channel turns. A normal needle would have swung with every course change. This one held its bearing like a dog holding a scent.

Marisol studied it for a long moment. Her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding how much truth to spend — a small tightening around the eyes, a brief negotiation conducted entirely with herself.

"Your father used it the same way," she said finally. "On the radio, he'd read me headings off it. I thought he was just being poetic. 'Following the needle home,' he said once." She handed me back the mug. "He wasn't poetic. He was literal. That was the whole man in four words, actually."

"He was literal," I agreed. "And absent. And stubborn enough to die in a swamp over a legend."

"And yet here you are," Marisol said gently, "in the same swamp, following the same needle."

I had no answer for that, so I drank the coffee, which punished me appropriately.

* * *

By midday the maze tightened. The channels narrowed until the mangrove branches scraped both rails at once, and Odalys conned the *Perla* from the bow with her engineer, Tomás, working the throttle by hand signals because the wheel was useless in water this shallow. Our hull drew barely a meter; even so, we kissed the mud twice, a soft grinding shudder that made everyone aboard stop breathing until we slid free.

"Sorry, ladies," Tomás muttered to the engine after the second time, patting it like a nervous horse. "Not my fault. The river has opinions."

"The river can keep them to itself," Odalys called back.

I stood at the bow beside her, compass open, calling the bearing. It should have felt absurd — a failed conservatory musician turned debt-ridden salvage hand, navigating a stolen race through a drowned forest with a dead man's brass toy — but instead it felt, uncomfortably, like competence. Like the first useful thing I'd done in years.

"Left channel," I said. "Then hard right past the big red trunk."

Odalys took us through. Behind us, far back now but not far enough, the *Venganza* was somewhere in the wider southern channels, too deep to follow us into the maze, running parallel and waiting for the routes to converge. We'd seen her smoke at dawn: a gray smudge against the sunrise, moving fast.

"How long is our lead?" I asked.

Odalys didn't answer immediately. She was squinting ahead, and whatever she saw made her jaw tighten before she spoke.

"Ask the buoy," she said.

I followed her gaze. Ahead, maybe two hundred meters, where the channel widened into the first of the great lagoons, something red bobbed against the green. A marker buoy — one of the survey buoys my father's expedition had placed on their way upstream, according to the notes Marisol had decoded. Numbered, painted, anchored. The breadcrumbs of a careful man.

Except this one wasn't bobbing. It was lying half-submerged, canted at an angle no buoy should hold, and as we closed the distance I saw why. Its mooring line had been cut — not frayed, cut, the ends white and clean — and someone had stove in its float with what looked like an axe. Painted across the shattered flank, in dripping black letters, was a single word:

**VENGANZA**

Nobody said anything for a moment. The engine idled. Somewhere in the mangroves a bird laughed at us, three descending notes, like the universe clearing its throat.

"Well," said Tomás from the stern. "That's not ominous at all."

Odalys brought us alongside the wrecked buoy and held station while I leaned out and fished the broken float aboard. Up close, the damage told its story plainly. The axe cuts were fresh — the wood inside the gash still pale, still wet. The paint hadn't even finished drying around the letters.

"Hours," I said. "Maybe less. They came through here this morning."

"They came through here *fast*," Odalys corrected. She pointed at the cut mooring line, then at the axe work. "No time wasted salvaging the buoy, no attempt to hide what they'd done. That's not caution, that's contempt. They wanted us to find it." She straightened, and I watched the pragmatist in her do arithmetic I didn't like. "Corvo's telling us he's not six tides behind. He's not even one tide behind. He's *hours*."

"That's impossible," said Marisol. "The *Venganza* draws three meters. She can't take these channels. We watched her swing wide south at dawn."

"She doesn't need to take the channels." Odalys's voice was flat and cold, the voice of a woman reading a bill she'd already expected. "She needs to take the *lagoon*. Everything converges on the lagoon — every channel, every route, ours included. Corvo doesn't have to beat us through the maze. He just has to be waiting where the maze lets go of us."

The bird laughed again. I was beginning to hate that bird.

* * *

We tied up in the lee of a mangrove island to eat and think, which in that order meant eat first and think with our mouths full. Odalys spread her charts on the deck plates and weighted the corners with coffee mugs, a wrench, and — after some ceremony — my father's compass, which she treated with the wary respect of a woman who had seen instruments lie and people die of it.

"Here." Her finger stabbed the chart. "First lagoon. Then the narrows —" the finger dragged east through a scribble of islands "— then the second lagoon, where the old ferry route crosses. After that, the deep water. The San Telmo's trench is another day's run past all of it, if the compass keeps feeding us true."

"And if Corvo is waiting at the second lagoon?" Marisol asked.

"Then we fight," Odalys said simply, "or we turn back, and I don't own enough fuel to turn back."

"We don't fight," I said. "We're seven people on a sloop with a winch and a diving rack. The *Venganza* carries armed men and a cannon mount. I've seen her."

"You've seen her," Odalys repeated, and something flickered across her face — not quite a smile. "So have I. From the water. Two years ago." She said it lightly, but the lightness had edges, and Marisol went very still beside me, and I understood that I had stumbled onto the thing Odalys kept in the locked drawer at the back of her heart. She gathered the charts before I could ask. "Rest while we rest. We run with the afternoon tide."

I didn't rest. I climbed to the bow with the compass instead, and Marisol, predictably, followed.

For a while neither of us spoke. The delta breathed around us — water ticking against the hull, mangrove leaves rattling in a wind that smelled of mud and green rot and, faintly, impossibly, flowers. My father had loved this country. I remembered that suddenly, from before: him at the kitchen table with his charts, telling my mother about the delta the way other men talked about cathedrals. I had forgotten that he loved anything. Forgetting had been easier.

"He knew these channels," I said at last. "That's why the compass works. It's not magic. It's memory. His memory, drawn into the needle somehow — I don't know how, I don't care how. He sailed this route and the instrument remembers it."

"Or it's magic," Marisol said.

"Or it's magic," I conceded. "Either way, it means he came this way alive and whole and planning. Which means everything that happened to him happened further up. Past the lagoons. Past the narrows." I turned the compass over in my hands. "Which means every hour we save here is an hour closer to finding out what happened to him."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into the register she used for truths she'd been carrying a long time.

"Astrid. About the last transmission—"

"Don't." I said it more sharply than I meant to. "Whatever you're about to tell me, don't tell me in a mangrove swamp with Corvo hours behind us. Save it. I only have room for one dread at a time, and right now the dread is a smashed buoy and a word painted in wet paint."

To my surprise, she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright against the green silence. "One dread at a time. Fine. That's the most sensible emotional policy I've heard all year." She bumped her shoulder against mine, and left it there, warm, and neither of us moved away, and the compass lay between us in my open palm, its needle steady as a heartbeat, pointing upriver toward everything we hadn't survived yet.

* * *

We ran with the afternoon tide, and the tide was generous, lifting us over bars that would have stopped us at dawn. By golden hour we were through the first lagoon and into the narrows, and the maze opened its throat and swallowed us, and for three glorious hours the *Perla* flew — engine singing, wake unspooling behind us like thread off a spool, Odalys at the helm grinning like a woman half her age, Tomás conducting the engine with a wrench like a baton.

It was almost fun. That should have warned me.

The narrows spat us out at dusk into the second lagoon, and the lagoon was empty — gloriously, suspiciously empty, water gone bronze and rose with the dying light, not a hull in sight.

"Clear," Tomás reported from the masthead, glasses up. "Nothing on the water. Nothing on the far shore. We're through, captain! Nobody's waiting!"

Odalys allowed herself exactly one nod of satisfaction. "Then we cross under cover of dark and make the deep water by—"

"Captain." Tomás's voice changed. It dropped, the way voices drop when they've seen something they wish they hadn't. "Far shore. The old ferry landing. There's a fire."

I raised my own glasses. Across the mile of bronze water, on the shingle beach where the drowned village's ferry landing still stood on its rotting pilings, a fire burned. Small. Controlled. Deliberate. The kind of fire men light when they want to be found.

And around it, catching the firelight, shapes. Boats pulled up on the sand. Men moving. And among the men, smaller figures, huddled — figures that moved wrong, that flinched, that were herded rather than walking.

Villagers. Hostages. Or something worse.

And pinned to the landing post above them, catching the last of the light, a fresh-painted board with letters I could read even at a mile, because whoever painted them had wanted them read:

**COME ASHORE, VILLALOBOS. YOUR FATHER DID.**

The lagoon went very quiet around us. Even the bird had nothing to say.

Odalys lowered her glasses slowly. "Well," she said, in the voice of a woman recalculating everything, "it seems the race isn't a race anymore."

Behind us, far to the south, faint as a rumor, the *Venganza*'s smoke stood up against the darkening sky.

Ahead of us, the fire waited.

And in my hand, unasked, unprompted, for the first time since we'd entered the delta, my father's compass trembled — the needle shivering like a living thing, like a warning, like a man's voice saying *turn back, boy*, one last time.
