## 7. The Drowned Village

The storm found us an hour before the village did.

I felt it first in the radio — a crackle of static rising like a tide inside the set, the atmosphere itself warning me before the sky bothered to. I was below, wedged into my corner of the Perla's cramped cabin with headphones on and charts spread across my knees, when the needle on the barometer began its slow, stubborn slide. By the time Odalys called down for a weather report, I already had one: ugly, getting uglier, coming straight at us off the open water like something with a grudge.

"Marisol," she shouted through the hatch, "give me bad news."

"You want it honest or survivable?"

There was a pause, and then her laugh, dry as old rope. "Honest."

"Squall line, maybe forty minutes out. Wind'll back around to the northeast and build. We need shelter or we need to be very good at drowning."

Astrid appeared at the hatch then, salt-streaked and squinting, with that look he'd been wearing since the smashed buoy — the one that said *we're behind* even though nobody had said it aloud. Behind him, the mangrove maze opened onto a wide brown bay, and beyond the bay, half-swallowed by water and strangler figs, sat a village.

Or what had been a village. Half its houses stood on stilts above the floodline, weathered gray, their porches hung with drying nets. The other half stood in the water itself — roofs only, doors opening onto nothing but current, whole streets gone to fish. A church tower rose from the shallows at an angle, its bell green with age, and I remember thinking that whoever rang that bell now would be ringing it for themselves.

"That's Puerto Ciego," Odalys said, coming up beside us. She said it the way you say the name of a person you owe money to. "Blind Port. My father traded here, years ago. There's a ferryman — Tomás. If he's still breathing, he'll know every channel in this delta better than Corvo's charts do."

"And if he isn't?" Astrid asked.

"Then we anchor in the open and argue with the storm."

We didn't have to argue with the storm. The Perla slid up the flooded main street — there is a strange feeling, steering a boat down what used to be a road, past doorframes where children must have run, past a window box still holding the dry skeletons of flowers — and tied off against the second story of what had been a cantina. The rain arrived thirty seconds later, all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath the whole way in.

* * *

Tomás was still breathing.

We found him in the church — the dry part of it, the upper gallery where the pews had been hauled up years ago when the water first came for the nave. He was a small man folded into a smaller chair, brown as a walnut, with hands so knuckled and vast they looked borrowed from someone three times his size. A cat with a torn ear sat in his lap like a co-captain. When Odalys said his name, he looked up without surprise, as though he'd been expecting her since Tuesday.

"Odalys Reyes," he said. "You have your mother's way of arriving wet."

"You have your father's way of being alive when everyone said otherwise."

"They said otherwise about this whole village." He gestured at the water lapping quietly at the base of the altar steps. "It's still here. Most of it. Underneath." His eyes moved over us — Odalys, me, Astrid dripping in the doorway, the two crewmen whose names I was still learning — and settled on Astrid last, and stayed there. "You have his jaw," he said.

Astrid went very still. "Whose?"

"The man who passed through here. Weeks back. Expedition boats, radios, maps drawn on napkins. He ate my soup and argued with me about the depth of the outer channel." The old man smiled with real warmth. "He argued the way honest men argue — like losing wouldn't kill him. You're his son. Don't tell me you're not. The river told me before your boat did. It tells me everything."

I watched Astrid take that. Watched him stand in the doorway of a drowned church, rain hammering the roof above us, being told that the river knew his face before he'd spoken a word. Grief does strange things to a person's posture. His shoulders came up like a man bracing for a blow, and then, slowly, came down again.

"He didn't mention a ferryman," Astrid said finally.

"He didn't mention a son either. Men keep their soft things close." Tomás scratched the cat under its torn ear. "Sit. All of you. The soup is still good and the storm is not finished with us, and I have news that will taste worse than anything I can cook."

* * *

The soup was fish and root vegetables and, I suspect, mostly patience. We ate it in the gallery while the storm threw itself against the shutters, and Tomás talked, and with every sentence the warmth went out of the room.

Corvo's men had come through nine days ago. Three boats, armed, flying no colors because they didn't need to — everyone on this water knew the Venganza's silhouette the way farmers know crows. They'd bought fuel, food, diving tanks, rope. Paid well. Paid too well, the way men pay when they don't intend to come back and be asked questions.

"And they took people," Odalys said. Not a question. She'd read it in the old man's pauses before he got there.

Tomás nodded slowly. "Four. From the stilt houses. The Reyes family's boy — no relation to you, child, half this coast is named Reyes — and old Beatriz, and two fishermen who were foolish enough to ask why armed strangers needed hostages." He turned his cup in those enormous hands. "They left word. Corvo's word. Anyone who guides another boat toward the deep channels, anyone who sells supplies to rival crews, anyone who talks — the four don't come home. They're held on one of the barges anchored off the point."

The rain filled the silence. I looked at Astrid. I couldn't help it; I'd been looking at him more than I meant to since the night he heard his father's voice come out of my radio, and I caught myself doing it now, watching the arithmetic happen behind his eyes.

I knew what the answer would be before he said it. That was the worst part. I'd known this man for less than a week and I could already read the ledger in his face, and the ledger said: *six tides. The Venganza is ahead of us. Every hour is a debt.*

"We can't," he said. Quietly. Not cruelly — that was almost harder. "Odalys. Four people. I'm sorry. But there are six tides between us and that wreck, and Corvo has a head start and hostages and we have—" he gestured at the storm, at the flooded street, at all of it, "—this. If we turn back to fight a barge full of mercenaries, we lose. And if we lose, Corvo wins, and then those four are hostages forever anyway, along with everyone else on this coast. The fastest way to save them is to get there first."

"It's a good argument," Odalys said.

"Thank you."

"I taught arguments like that to my brother." Her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "He was good at them too. Right up until the day Corvo cut his lifeline to save a cargo, and every argument in the world didn't bring him up from sixty feet." She stood. In the low gallery ceiling she seemed taller than she was, the way captains do. "We're turning back. One tide. Maybe less, if Tomás knows the barge's watch rotation, and he does, because Tomás knows everything."

"Odalys—"

"Captain's decision, Villalobos. You signed aboard my boat. You can hate me in writing when we're done." She looked around the gallery, at each of us in turn. "Anyone who wants to push on can take the dinghy and my blessing. Anyone who stays helps me. Those are the terms."

Nobody took the dinghy. Even the two crewmen I'd half-expected to bolt just looked at their soup.

Astrid stood too. For a long moment the two of them faced each other across the drowned church, captain and passenger, and the storm banged the shutters like applause from an enemy crowd. Then Astrid exhaled through his nose — I was learning that sound; it meant he'd lost an argument with himself and was trying to make it look voluntary.

"One tide," he said. "And we do it quiet, and we do it fast, and nobody plays hero."

"Of course," Odalys said. "Heroes are expensive."

* * *

I couldn't sleep, which surprised no one, least of all me. Storms always sit in my chest like a second heartbeat. So I climbed down to the Perla to check the radio — an excuse so thin it barely qualified as clothing — and found Astrid already there, sitting on the deck in the rain shadow of the cabin roof, turning his father's compass over in his hands.

The needle wasn't pointing north.

It was trembling, straining, swinging in small urgent arcs toward the east — toward the deep channels, toward the lagoon none of us had seen yet. Toward the wreck. Even here, even in a drowned village with hostages on a barge and a storm overhead, the thing pulled like a live muscle.

"It's been doing that since the buoy," Astrid said, without looking up. "Getting stronger. Like it's impatient." He laughed, short and hollow. "My father spent his whole life chasing this thing and it killed him, probably, and here I am holding it in the rain like it's a gift."

I sat down next to him. Close enough that our shoulders touched, which was either an accident or wasn't.

"My mother used to say the delta keeps two ledgers," I told him. "One for what people take, and one for what they give back. She said you never get to choose which one they read at your funeral."

"Cheerful woman."

"She ran a radio shack alone on the coast for thirty years. Cheerful was a survival strategy." I watched the needle strain eastward. "You were right today, you know. About the tides. About the math."

"But?"

"But math is how Corvo thinks. Hostages are line items. Your father was a line item. My—" I stopped, because that was Marisol's business and not the crew's, and started again. "Odalys is right too. And the fact that both of you are right is exactly why this whole coast is drowning."

Astrid was quiet for a while. Rain ticked on the cabin roof. Somewhere across the flooded street, in one of the stilt houses, a family lit a lamp, and its light lay long and gold across the black water, and I thought: *they stayed. The water took their streets and they stayed.* There was something in that I couldn't yet say, something about Astrid, about all of us, about why I hadn't told him what the transmissions really held.

Soon. Soon I'd have to play him the rest of the tape, the words I'd kept back — *Corvo knows* — and watch whatever we'd built between us bend under it. But not tonight. Tonight the compass trembled in his hands and the lamp burned across the water and he said, finally:

"All right. One tide. We save them fast, and then we race." He glanced sideways at me, and for the first time since the pawnshop story, since the debt, since all of it, there was something in his face that wasn't arithmetic. "Marisol. Why did you really sign onto this voyage? Odalys pays in fish and promises. Nobody crews a suicide run for fish."

I should have lied. I was good at lying; I'd been lying to him for days with a smile and a pot of coffee. Instead I heard myself say, "Because I was the last voice your father heard. And I've been trying to figure out ever since whether that makes me responsible for finishing his sentence or guilty of interrupting it."

He looked at me for a long moment. The compass needle swung between us, east, east, east, patient as gravity.

"Tomorrow," he said softly, "after the barge — you're going to tell me the rest. Whatever you kept back. I've watched you carry it for three days like a stone in your pocket, and I'm asking. Not demanding. Asking."

The rain came down. Across the water the lamp burned. And because I was tired, and because he'd asked instead of demanded, and because some small traitorous part of me wanted to put the stone down — I nodded.

Above us, thunder rolled up the delta like a drumbeat counting down, and I thought of the barge off the point, and the four names Tomás had given us, and the tide that would begin turning at dawn whether we were ready or not.

Six tides. After tonight, five.

And somewhere out in the dark, the Venganza was already moving.
