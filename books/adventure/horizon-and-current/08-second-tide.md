## 8. Second Tide

The tide did not wait for us to feel good about ourselves.

Astrid stood on the Perla's aft deck and watched the last of the freed villagers shuffle down the dock at Puerto Ceniza — the drowned village's sister settlement, a strip of stilt houses and drying nets that smelled of fish and diesel — and he counted them the way you count money you've just set on fire. Nineteen souls. One old ferryman who refused to leave his boat. And Bruno, their engineer, standing on the planks with his tool bag over his shoulder like a man reporting for a shift he'd never asked for.

"You're not staying," Astrid said.

Bruno looked up at him with the patient expression of a man who had been explaining things to fools his whole life and had long since stopped minding. "I am staying."

"We lost half a tide already. We lose you, we lose the pumps, the winch, the—"

"The compressor," Bruno said. "The generator. The dive rig's regulator manifold, which, by the way, I rebuilt twice since Cartagena because someone—" he glanced meaningfully at Odalys at the helm "—insists on running the engine past redline through mangrove channels."

"It got us here, didn't it?" Odalys called down, not turning around.

"It got us here *loudly*."

"Bruno." Astrid came down the ladder two rungs at a time. "Corvo's divers are in the water somewhere behind us. Those villagers have no one. I understand that. But if we don't reach the San Telmo before the Venganza does, none of this matters. Not the villagers, not us, not—"

"Not your father's wreck," Bruno said quietly.

Astrid shut his mouth. It was the first time anyone had said it out loud like that — *your father's wreck* — as though the galleon were a family heirloom instead of sixty feet of black water and dead men's gold. As though it belonged to a man who had chosen Corvo's ledger over his own son's phone number.

"I'm staying because those people will be taken again within a day if there's no one here with a radio and a rifle and a face that says *I have nothing left to lose*," Bruno said. "I'm staying because I'm fifty-eight years old and my knees gave up diving years ago, and everything I know how to do on that boat, I can teach to Marisol in an afternoon. And I'm staying," he added, shouldering the bag higher, "because somebody has to guard nineteen people, and you're all too young and stupid to do it right."

"That's not fair," said Marisol from the companionway.

"No," Bruno agreed. "It's accurate. Different thing."

He put out his hand. Astrid took it. Bruno's grip was dry and hard as driftwood, and he held on one second longer than a handshake required, the way old men do when they're saying something they'll deny later.

"Your father," Bruno said, low, "hired me once. Twenty years ago, a survey job off Cabo Rojo. He paid me late and complained about my invoice and argued with me about torque specs for six straight days. Worst client I ever had." He let go. "I'd have sailed with him again tomorrow. Make sure whatever you find down there is worth what he paid for it."

Astrid didn't trust his voice, so he nodded. Bruno climbed the dock without looking back, and the villagers gathered around him like he was a lighthouse, and Astrid stood there feeling the tide go out of the channel and out of his chest at the same time.

* * *

They made four miles before Astrid broke.

It wasn't dramatic, which was somehow worse. He didn't throw anything. He didn't shout. He just stood at the bow rail doing arithmetic out loud in a flat, ugly voice — *four miles, two knots against the ebb, that's two hours, two hours is a third of the remaining tide, a third of the tide is the whole margin, the whole margin is the race* — until Odalys said, "Either help me read this channel or go below and sulk where I can't hear you," and he went below and sulked, technically, except sulking implies stillness and Astrid could not be still.

He re-stowed lines that were already stowed. He checked the anchor rode twice. He opened Bruno's toolbox and closed it again, because touching another man's tools felt like grave robbery. He found a coffee tin of screws sorted by size into an egg carton — Bruno labeled everything; Bruno labeled his *labels* — and he held the tin and thought about a man choosing to stay behind among strangers rather than watch a son chase a dead man's treasure, and something in Astrid's ribs went tight and sour.

Marisol found him there, sitting on the engine-room step in the dark, holding screws.

"You know," she said, settling beside him, "the first week aboard, I thought Bruno hated me. He corrected my radio procedure so many times I started dreaming in phonetic alphabet. Foxtrot-Alpha-Delta. Then one night I caught him re-soldering my headset jack at two in the morning because the left ear crackled and he'd decided I deserved better." She shrugged. "That's how he loves people. He fixes things near them and pretends it's maintenance."

"He shouldn't have stayed."

"Probably not."

"Half a tide, Marisol. Half a *tide*. You weren't up there. You didn't see the channel markers going under. Every minute we sit here, Corvo's lead diver is swimming closer to—"

"To what?" she asked. Gently. Which was the most infuriating possible way to ask it.

"To the wreck. To the gold. To the only thing that pays the debt that ruined my life."

She was quiet a moment. Then she reached into her jacket pocket and brought out the compass.

His father's compass. Astrid had kept it in his own pocket since Cartagena — slept with it, ate with it, turned it over in his fingers through every argument and every mile — but sometime during the rescue, in the chaos of the village, Marisol had borrowed it without asking, and he hadn't even noticed, and some traitorous part of him registered that fact as intimacy rather than theft.

"Look at it," she said. "Not the needle. Hold it flat. Feel it."

He took it in his palm. Brass, worn smooth at the pivot ring by forty years of a thumb he'd inherited the shape of but not the habits of. And then he felt it — faint, arrhythmic, like a moth against glass. A tremor. The needle wasn't pointing north. It wasn't pointing at the delta mouth or the channel or any direction a compass had business knowing about. It was shivering, quivering in its card, straining toward the bow like a dog at the end of a chain.

"What is that?"

"I don't know," Marisol said. "It started an hour ago. When we cleared the headland. It's been getting stronger ever since." She looked at him, and her eyes in the dim engine-room light were steady and a little frightened and absolutely certain. "Astrid. Your father's compass isn't finding north anymore. It's finding *him*. Or it's finding what he found. Either way — the wreck is close. Close enough that a piece of magnetized brass can feel it through the hull."

"That's not how compasses work."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

They sat with that. The engine thudded overhead — Odalys pushing them back up-channel the moment the villagers were clear, redline be damned, Bruno's ghostly disapproval notwithstanding. Somewhere above, water slapped the hull in that patient delta rhythm, the sound of a clock that didn't care about anyone's schedule.

"Half a tide," Astrid said finally. His voice came out rougher than he meant. "We spent half a tide pulling strangers out of a flooded village, and we lost our engineer, and I stood on that dock counting heads like they were coins, and I hate myself for it, and I'd do it again, and I don't know what that makes me."

"A man," Marisol said. "It makes you a man who hates the cost of being decent. That's not a flaw, Astrid. That's just the price list." She tapped the compass glass with one fingernail. "Besides. Your father spent twenty years and everyone's patience chasing this thing. You're going to catch it in six tides *and* save a village on the way? Give yourself a little credit. He'd be furious. He'd also be proud. In that order."

Astrid laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of him. It was the first time anyone had spoken of his father with warmth instead of dread, and it landed somewhere under his sternum and stayed there.

"He argued about torque specs," Astrid said. "For six days."

"Bruno told you that?"

"On the dock. Said he was the worst client he ever had. Said he'd sail with him again tomorrow."

Marisol smiled, and it transformed her whole face the way it always did — the quiet radio operator vanishing for a moment and someone luminous standing in her place. "That sounds like him. That sounds exactly like the man who sent me a radio check every Sunday at 1900 whether he had anything to report or not. *Perla, Perla, this is San Telmo expedition, status unchanged, weather fine, tell my son—*" She stopped.

The engine room got very small.

"Tell your son what?" Astrid asked. Very quietly.

Marisol looked at the compass trembling in his hand, then at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her decide — he could see her decide — that some debts get paid now or never.

"Tell my son the needle doesn't lie," she said. "Even when the chart does."

Above them, Odalys's voice came down the deck tube, tight and fast: "Marisol! Get up here — the channel splits ahead and the east fork's showing foam. And bring the crazy compass. Something's wrong with my magnetic heading and I want to know if it's the boat or the world."

Marisol stood and pulled Astrid up after her, and they climbed into the grey afternoon light together, and the compass trembled in his palm like a heartbeat, straining east, toward the foam, toward the deep black water where a galleon and a father and a fortune and a choice were all waiting in the dark —

— and on the eastern horizon, thin as a knife stroke, a smudge of smoke that had no business being there.

The Venganza had entered the delta early.
