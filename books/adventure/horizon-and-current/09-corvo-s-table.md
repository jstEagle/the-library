## 9. Corvo's Table

The Venganza carried a dining table made of mahogany taken from a church in Puerto Hondo, and Corvo liked to say that the wood had learned patience the hard way. It had waited two hundred years for its congregation to stop coming, and now it waited on him.

He sat at the head of it, as he sat at the head of everything, and watched his guest attack a plate of snapper like a man who had not eaten well since the harbormaster's office stopped being profitable.

"You'll ruin your appetite," Corvo said, "if you eat it all now."

"I have no appetite to ruin," said Harbormaster Esteban Ruiz, dabbing his mouth with a napkin that cost more than his monthly salary had, back when he'd drawn one honestly. "I have only nerves."

"Nerves are good. Nerves mean you understand the situation." Corvo poured wine into both glasses himself — he did not trust his steward with the good bottle, and he did not trust anyone at all with the second-best one. "A man without nerves at this table is either a fool or a liar, and I've thrown both overboard."

Ruiz laughed. It was a thin sound, the laugh of a man testing ice. "You're joking."

"I'm never joking about the water," Corvo said pleasantly, and Ruiz stopped laughing, and dinner proceeded.

* * *

Corvo was sixty-one years old and built like something the sea had tried to drown and given up on. His hands were the hands of a man who had once worked rope and winch, before he'd learned that other men would work them for him; the knuckles were still swollen from an old break that had healed crooked because there had been no doctor aboard the first ship he'd ever crewed, only a cook with pliers and opinions. He wore white linen at dinner, always, even in the delta heat, even when the sweat mapped the fabric like river deltas of its own. Cleanliness, he believed, was a form of armor. Filth got into a man the way doubt did, through the seams.

He ate slowly. This too was discipline. A man who ate fast could be rushed, and a man who could be rushed could be managed, and Corvo had spent forty years making sure that the only person who managed him was Corvo.

"Tell me about the Villalobos estate," he said, when the fish was down to skeletons.

Ruiz set down his fork. "You know about the estate. You hold the notes."

"I hold the paper. Paper is not knowledge. Knowledge is knowing whether the son has found anything worth dying for yet." Corvo turned his glass by its stem, watching the candlelight swim in the red. "Emiliano Villalobos owed me eleven thousand pesos when he died. Do you know what he bought with my money?"

"The expedition," Ruiz said carefully. "The boats. The—"

"He bought my permission to look for my own galleon." Corvo smiled, and it was the smile he used for photographs and funerals. "That is the joke of it, Esteban. I lent the man the shovel so he could dig where I wanted him digging. Every peso he borrowed brought the San Telmo closer to surfacing, and every peso made him more desperate to find her before the interest ate him alive. Desperation is the finest fuel there is. It burns hotter than greed and it doesn't ask questions."

"And if he'd refused to borrow?"

"Then I'd have ruined him some other way and waited. Men like Emiliano don't refuse. They're the kind who'd rather owe the devil than admit they can't pay God." Corvo lifted his glass toward the porthole, toward the black water sliding past. "He was a good instrument. Stubborn, but honest in his way. He really believed he was going to lift that treasure out and buy back his name."

"And instead?"

"Instead he went quiet." Corvo let the words sit a moment. "Six tides of silence now. Which means either he found her and decided he didn't need me anymore—" he shook his head, "—no. Not Emiliano. He'd have called. He was too honest to steal from me. Or he found trouble, or trouble found him, or—" He stopped. He did not finish sentences that ended in graves unless he chose to. "Either way, the son has the compass. And the son has gone upriver on a sloop called the Perla, captained by a woman named Reyes, and I want to know how you let that happen."

Ruiz's hand paused halfway to the wine. "Let?"

"The harbor logs, Esteban. The customs cutter. The radio traffic. You are my eyes in that port, and my eyes were closed."

"They cleared out on the night tide, dark, no manifest filed. The cutter didn't—"

"The cutter is commanded by a man who eats at your table, which is to say my table, which is to say he should have known." Corvo's voice did not rise. It never rose. Rising was for men who needed volume because they lacked weight. "Reyes. Odalys Reyes. Do you know her?"

"A small operator. Salvage runs, cargo, whatever pays. Her brother—"

"—worked for me. Yes." Corvo looked at the candle flame. "Tomás Reyes. Good diver. Careless with his feelings. He died doing exactly what I paid him to do, which is more than most men manage." He refilled his own glass. "If she's upriver, she's not running salvage. She's running a grudge. Grudges are heavier than treasure, Esteban. They sink ships."

"You think she knows? About Tomás?"

"I think women like Odalys Reyes keep ledgers of their own, and I think hers has my name in it, and I think none of that matters tonight." He waved the thought away like smoke. "What matters is the tide arithmetic. My lead divers say we make the lagoon on the sixth tide if nothing slows us. If the Perla left when I believe she left, she's ahead of us by one tide, maybe two. She's smaller. She draws less water. She can go where the Venganza cannot follow without breaking her keel."

"So we lose the race."

"We are not racing, Esteban. Racing is for horses and fools." Corvo leaned forward, and the candlelight moved across his face and made the old scar under his jaw — a hook of pale tissue from a boarding action thirty years gone — look freshly earned. "We are hunting. A race ends when someone crosses a line. A hunt ends when someone stops moving. The Perla will stop moving. She has to dive, and diving takes time, and time is the one thing I own more of than anyone alive."

* * *

After Ruiz had been ferried back to his launch — wobbling slightly, the way men wobbled after an evening of Corvo's hospitality — Corvo stood alone at the stern rail with the last of the wine and let the delta air work on him.

The river here was wide enough to pretend to be the sea. Mangroves crowded both banks in their silent millions, breathing mud, hiding channels, keeping their own counsel. Somewhere up that black corridor was a galleon full of silver, and somewhere near the galleon, possibly, was the body of the man whose debts had built half of Corvo's fortune.

He did not feel guilt about Emiliano Villalobos. Guilt was a tax paid by men who still believed in accounts above their own. What he felt was something closer to professional irritation: an instrument had broken in his hand, and instruments that broke were usually the ones mishandled, and Corvo did not consider himself careless.

Though — he turned the thought over, examining it the way he examined all things, for flaws — though the boy might be interesting. Astrid. Named for a star by a man who navigated by dead reckoning. Corvo had met him once, years ago, at a dockside auction where Emiliano had been selling a sextant to cover a payment. The boy had been maybe nineteen, standing beside his father with his arms crossed and his jaw set, radiating the particular fury of a child watching a parent shrink. Corvo had offered to buy the boy a drink. The boy had said something sharp. The father had apologized for him.

Corvo remembered thinking: *that one will either save his father or bury him, and it will be close.*

Now the father was buried, or soon would be, and the son was upriver on a stolen tide with a compass that pointed at silver.

"Captain."

His first mate, Braga, came onto the deck with the particular careful tread of a man carrying news he wished belonged to someone else. Braga was enormous and gentle and had once, famously, caught a falling crane load one-handed, and Corvo kept him close the way other men kept lucky coins.

"Speak."

"The radio room copied a transmission. Faint. Someone keying a signal upriver, low power, short bursts. The pattern matches a salvage survey beacon." Braga hesitated. "It also matches the frequency the elder Villalobos used."

Corvo felt something shift in his chest, small and cold, like a coin settling in a pocket. "Position?"

"Roughly. The bearing puts it ahead of us. Two tides, perhaps less." Braga shifted his weight. "There's more. The signal isn't continuous. It comes in bursts, then silence, then bursts again. Like someone transmitting between dives."

Dives. So they had found something, or believed they had, and they were already in the water. Corvo looked up the river, into the dark where the mangroves swallowed the last light, and did the arithmetic he was famous for doing faster than any man alive.

Two tides. Perhaps less. And between him and the lagoon, a sloop full of strangers, a widow of his own making, and a young man with his father's jaw and his father's stubbornness and, if the reports were true, his father's compass.

"Braga," he said. "Wake the divers. All of them. Full kit, live rounds, the works."

"It's the middle of the night, Captain."

"That is generally when I do my best work." Corvo drained the glass and set it upside down on the rail, a habit from his working days — finished, done, no dregs. "And Braga. When we find the Perla, no one boards her but us. Whatever the crew is doing when we arrive, however they're armed, whatever they've found — we go in ourselves. I want to see the boy's face when he understands who built the trap his father died in. Some debts you collect in pesos." He smiled at the dark water, and the dark water gave nothing back. "This one I collect in person."

Braga nodded and went aft, and his footsteps faded, and the engines changed their note beneath Corvo's feet, deepening, quickening, the whole ship leaning into the current like a hound coming off the leash.

Ahead, two tides north, a lamp was burning on a little sloop, and a boy was probably reading his father's chart by it, believing he was outrunning the past.

Corvo watched the mangroves slide past and thought, with genuine warmth, the closest thing to affection he permitted himself:

*Run, boy. It's better for everyone when they run.*
