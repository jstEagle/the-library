## 10. Third Tide

The false target showed itself an hour after sunset, the way liars usually do — confidently, and only when you wanted to believe them.

Odalys found it with the sonar, such as it was. The Perla's depth sounder was older than any of us and twice as opinionated, and it painted a shape on the paper roll that made her go quiet in a way I'd learned to distrust.

"There," she said, tapping the smudge. "Hull form. Big one. Sitting upright on a shelf at nine fathoms."

"Upright," repeated Tomás, our remaining engineer — the *other* engineer, I should say, since we'd left the first one guarding freed villagers upriver like some saint of the mangroves. Tomás had a way of repeating words as though tasting them for poison. "A galleon that sank three hundred years ago and landed *upright*?"

"Sheltered lagoon. Soft mud. It happens."

"It happens," Tomás agreed, "about as often as I happen to win at cards."

"You don't win at cards."

"Exactly my point."

But the compass agreed with the sounder, and the compass had been agreeing with things all week with a stubbornness that bordered on showing off. It sat in my palm now, its brass case warm from my pocket, the needle trembling toward the northeast shore of the lagoon where the sonar smudge lay. My father's compass. My father's direction. I'd stopped asking myself whether that was comfort or manipulation. The answer, I suspected, depended entirely on how the night went.

"We dive tonight," Odalys said. "Moon's down in two hours. Corvo's lead ship was six miles behind us at last light, and Venganza doesn't run dark — he thinks darkness is for people with something to hide."

"Doesn't he?" Marisol asked mildly, from her nest of radio equipment in the corner of the wheelhouse.

"He does," Odalys said. "That's what makes him predictable."

I looked at the water. Black, flat, patient. Somewhere under it, if the sonar and the compass and three hundred miles of river were to be believed, lay the San Telmo and enough gold to erase my name from every ledger Corvo owned. Somewhere under it, possibly, lay answers about my father that I had been circling for weeks the way the needle circled north.

"Gear up," I said.

* * *

Diving at night is a bargain you make with your own imagination. The water gives you a circle of lamplight — three meters across, if you're lucky — and everything outside that circle belongs to whatever your mind decides to put there. Mine, that night, put my father there. Not as a body. As a presence. As the man who'd taught me to swim in the brown river off Puerto Cielo by the simple method of throwing me in and looking disappointed when I complained.

"You're drifting," Marisol's voice said in my earpiece, warm and slightly crackling. She'd insisted on topside watch with the radio, and Odalys had agreed, and I'd told myself I didn't mind, and then spent ten minutes underwater noticing how much I minded.

"I'm not drifting. I'm conducting a survey."

"You're drifting northeast. The wreck is below you."

"Then the wreck is drifting northeast too, and we should both be concerned."

Her laugh came through the earpiece like something worth surfacing for. Below me, my lamp found the mud shelf, and then found the hull.

It was magnificent. That was my first thought, and I've had worse first thoughts. A galleon's ribs rose out of the silt like the skeleton of a cathedral that had lain down to die, timbers black with three centuries, encrusted with a garden of barnacles and pale sponges. The lamp's circle slid along planking the width of my forearm. My heart was doing something undignified against the regulator.

"Odalys," I said. "I've got her. I've got the hull."

Silence on the line. Then Odalys: "Describe it."

"Upright. Intact. Ribs like — like it's posing."

"Any growth on the deck structures?"

I swept the lamp upward and found the deck, or what remained of it, collapsed inward in places, and I started to say something triumphant and then stopped, because the lamp had found the wood grain, and wood grain, after three hundred years underwater, is a thing you notice the way you'd notice a living man at a funeral.

"Marisol," I said carefully. "What year did the San Telmo go down?"

"Seventy-two. 1772. Why?"

"Because this ship has saw marks." I put my glove against the planking. Straight. Clean. Machine-straight. "Circular saw marks."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. I heard Odalys exhale through her nose, the particular exhale of a captain recalculating everything.

"Keep looking," she said finally. "If it's a plant, there'll be more."

There was more.

I swam the length of the hull with my heart sinking faster than I was, and the evidence assembled itself with the tidy cruelty of a legal document. Iron fastenings where a period ship would have used treenails. A mast step cut with a power adze. And scattered across the silt inside the ribs — arranged, I realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the water — a constellation of artifacts. A coin here. A pewter plate there. A bell, sitting upright on the mud like a stage prop between scenes, polished of all things, its surface scrubbed clean enough to read.

I read it.

*San Telmo.*

Painted. The name was *painted* on the bell, in letters that had been applied with a brush by someone in a hurry, and the sea had been eating the paint for — I rubbed it with my thumb — months, not centuries.

"He planted it," I said. My voice came out strange in the earpiece, flattened by the water and by something larger. "My father planted a decoy wreck."

"A decoy," Odalys repeated. "A *ship*. He planted a ship."

"Where do you even *get* a ship?" Tomás asked from topside, where he'd been listening the way he listened to everything, which was with his whole body leaned toward the radio.

"You get a dying hull cheap," Odalys said slowly, "and you tow it somewhere no one asks questions, and you sink it, and you salt it. It's — God help me, it's brilliant. It's exactly what I'd do. It's what Corvo would do, which is the point. You build a lie shaped like the thing your enemy wants most, and you let him break his fleet on it."

"But the San Telmo is real," Marisol said. "The archives are real. The manifest is real."

"The archives are real," Odalys agreed. "Which means the wreck is real, somewhere else, and this —" I could hear her gesturing, though I couldn't see it — "this is the lock on the door. This is what he built so that when Corvo's divers came down with their lamps, they'd find exactly enough to stop looking."

I hung in the water above the false San Telmo, inside the circle of my own lamplight, and I understood that I was floating inside my father's mind. Three hundred years of nothing down here, and then one man, one season, one desperate act of stagecraft — a dead ship dressed in borrowed bones, set on a mud shelf like a lure set on a hook. He had known Corvo would come. He had known it the way I was only now learning to know things: too late, and completely.

"Check inside," Odys said. "If he built a decoy, he'd want it convincing up close. And if he wanted to leave a message for anyone smart enough to catch the saw marks —"

"He'd leave it up close," I finished. "Where only a diver who looked too carefully would ever go."

The hold was a throat of darkness. I went in.

* * *

Inside, the decoy was hollow and honest in the way that only lies are. My lamp found silt, ribs, the ghost of a cargo deck stripped bare. And against the forward bulkhead, wedged into a gap between timbers with the deliberation of a man who had all the time in the world except the time that mattered, a tin.

It was a biscuit tin. A *biscuit tin*. My father, who had once spent four months of expedition budget on a custom waterproof chart case, had sealed his greatest secret in a biscuit tin with solder around the lid, and I recognized the solder work the way you recognize handwriting, because I'd sat on a dock at twelve years old watching him do that exact sloppy, over-generous bead and telling him he used too much, and him telling me that too much was the right amount when the sea was the customer.

I held it in both gloves. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"Astrid?" Marisol's voice, soft. "Your breathing's changed."

"I found something."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind he wanted found." I was already rising, kicking for the pale gray smudge of the surface. "Bring her up. I'm coming up."

* * *

On deck, they gave me the space to open it, which was a kindness, and then crowded in anyway, which was the truth about crews.

The solder cracked under Tomás's knife — he insisted, saying my hands were shaking, and I didn't argue, because they were. Inside: oilcloth, folded twice. Inside the oilcloth: a chart, hand-drawn, dense with my father's small fierce lettering. And beneath the chart, a letter. One page. My name on it.

I didn't read it aloud. Some things you don't share with an audience, even an audience that has bled with you. I turned my back to the wheelhouse lamp and read it three times, and the third time was for the words, and the first two were for the handwriting, which I had not seen in nine years and which looked exactly the same, which broke something in me that I suspect needed breaking.

* * *

*Astrid —*

*If you're reading this, you found the lie, which means you're smarter than the men I built it to fool, which means you're smarter than I ever told you I thought you were. That was my fault. Most of this was my fault.*

*The true position of the San Telmo is on the chart. It's not where the stories put it, because the stories were wrong, and because I corrected them and then buried the correction, because the moment I confirmed the wreck I understood what I'd really found: a debt I couldn't pay and a treasure I couldn't keep. Corvo financed my last two seasons. I thought I was borrowing against my reputation. I was borrowing against the wreck itself, and he knew it before I did. The debt was the hook. The expedition was the line. I was the bait, and I went anyway, because I told myself I could out-swim the water. Pride is just fear wearing a better coat.*

*The decoy will hold Corvo for one tide, maybe two. He pays his divers to be thorough, not smart. When it fails, he'll come for the true position, and he'll come for you, because you're my son and he keeps excellent records.*

*I'm not going to apologize for the wreck. I'm going to apologize for everything else. For leaving. For the nine years. For making you learn who I was from a lawyer's letters instead of from me. I told myself I was protecting you. That was the coat again. I was ashamed, and shame is a coward's way of being proud.*

*The chart is true. The rest of it — the gold, the debt, all of it — is only true if we agree to play. You can refuse the game, m'ijo. That's the one move Corvo never plans for. Nobody plans for a man who doesn't want the prize.*

*Whatever you choose, I was proud of you before you did anything to earn it. That's the part I never sent. That's the part I'm sending now, the coward's way, in a biscuit tin, at the bottom of the sea.*

*Your father,*
*Emilio*

* * *

I stood with the letter until the paper had drunk the damp from the night air and gone soft at the edges. Nobody spoke. Behind me I heard Tomás blow his nose with the delicacy of a man detonating a small charge, and Odalys say, quietly, "Chart," and Marisol say, "Later. Let him have a minute," and Odalys say, "He can have a minute *and* the chart, those aren't in conflict —"

I turned around. Marisol was closest, and she was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I could read it perfectly, because it was the one I was wearing.

"He knew," I said. "The whole time. He knew what Corvo was, and he went anyway, and he built this —" I gestured at the water, at the lie sleeping under it — "to buy time. Not for the gold. For *us*. For whoever came after him."

"He built it to buy time," Marisol said, "and it ran out."

"Then we spend it better than he did." I held up the chart, rolled tight as a closed fist. "There's a true position in here. And Corvo's divers are going to figure out this thing is sawn lumber in about a tide and a half."

Odalys was already moving, snapping orders, and the Perla came alive around us — engine room, anchors, the wheel — and Marisol touched my elbow, just once, and said, "The last line. The part about being proud. Was it —"

"It was the truest thing on the wreck," I said.

She nodded, and went to her radio, and I went below to the chart table where Odalys was unrolling my father's handwriting under the lamp, and that's when we saw it: the true position wasn't written in coordinates at all. It was written in a cipher, a running grid of numbers and river-names in my father's cramped hand, wrapped around the chart's margins like a border of thorns.

"Please tell me," Odalys said, "that you can read your father's mind."

I looked at the numbers, and the river-names, and the small fierce letters, and I thought of Marisol, who had listened to his voice for months and knew his rhythms better than I ever had.

"No," I said. "But I know who can."
