## 11. Love Against the Chart

The storm found them an hour after dark, and it did not knock first.

It came off the sea like something with a grudge, flattening the mangroves along the shore of the little cove where Odalys had wedged the *Perla* between two hummocks of root and mud. Rain hit the deck in sheets so thick that Astrid, standing in the companionway with a mug of coffee going cold in his hands, could not see the bow from the wheelhouse — ten feet of sloop, erased. Thunder rolled across the water in long, patient volleys, like artillery that had all night and intended to use it.

"Nobody's diving tonight," Odalys had said, an hour ago, lashing down the last of the gear with the grim economy of a woman who had lost things to weather before. "Nobody's doing anything but staying tied to this mud and staying alive. Congratulations, we're on vacation."

So they were storm-bound, six tides into a race that had already cost them their engineer and most of their illusions, and the only thing anyone could do was wait.

Astrid went below.

The chart room — which was generous; it was a closet with a desk bolted to the wall and a lamp that flickered when the generator coughed — was already occupied. Marisol sat at the desk with her back to the door, headphones around her neck, the decoy tin open in front of her. Inside it lay his father's true chart: a single sheet of heavy paper, water-stained at one corner, dense with handwriting in three colors of ink.

She didn't hear him come in. She was holding the chart up to the lamp, tilting it, her lips moving slightly as she read. In the lamplight her face had none of the guardedness she wore on deck. She looked, Astrid thought, like someone reading a letter.

He knew the feeling. He'd been reading the same letter for two days.

"You're dripping on the chart," he said.

She startled, then laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if laughter had caught her off guard. "I'm not dripping. I'm atmospherically enhanced."

"You're dripping."

She looked down at the small dark spots she'd left on the desk beside the tin and conceded the point with a shrug. He hung his wet jacket on the hook, took the towel from the rail, and — after a moment's internal negotiation with himself about what the gesture meant — draped it over her shoulders.

She went very still under it.

Then, quietly, without looking at him: "Thank you."

* * *

They worked for an hour before either of them said anything that wasn't about bearings or ink.

It was slow going. His father had been a careful man and a suspicious one, and the chart reflected both virtues. The main channel markers were drawn correctly — Astrid recognized them from the compass runs — but every depth sounding was written in a cipher of his father's own devising, numbers within numbers, and the margins were crowded with notes in a shorthand Marisol half-recognized from the radio logs.

"He used to say the sea keeps no secrets," Marisol murmured, tracing a column of figures with one finger, "but men do. So he wrote everything twice. Once for himself, once for whoever he trusted." She paused. "I don't think he ever finished the second version."

"No," Astrid said. "He didn't trust easily."

"No," she agreed. "He didn't."

The rain hammered. The lamp flickered. Somewhere above them, Odalys shouted something profane and cheerful at the wind, and the hull groaned against its moorings like an old man settling into a chair.

Astrid spread the chart flat and weighted its corners with the tin, two mugs, and a wrench he found in the drawer. "All right," he said. "Numbers within numbers. If I were a paranoid man hiding treasure coordinates from salvage fleets—"

"You'd anchor them to something that couldn't be moved," Marisol said. "Something you'd know would still be there in twenty years."

"The wreck itself."

"The wreck itself." She pulled the chart toward her, and their hands met over the paper, and neither of them moved away quickly enough for it to mean nothing.

* * *

Midnight came and went. The generator coughed, recovered, coughed again. Marisol made tea on the spirit stove because the coffee had run out, and they drank it scalding and terrible, shoulder to shoulder at the little desk, passing the cipher columns back and forth like a conversation they were having in someone else's language.

It was somewhere past two when Astrid cracked the first layer.

"It's dates," he said suddenly, sitting up so fast he nearly upset the tea. "Look — these aren't soundings. They're dates. Days of the year. He's indexing them by date." His finger moved down the column. "This one here — 14th of June. That's—" He stopped.

"That's the day the expedition lost the dinghy," Marisol said quietly. "Off the second marker. I logged the call."

Astrid looked at her. "You were there. On the radio. For all of it."

"I was there for all of it," she said.

The rain filled the silence for a while.

"Tell me about him," Astrid said finally. It came out rougher than he intended. "Not the man who owed money. Not the man who ran. The one you knew."

Marisol set down her mug. She wrapped both hands around her knees — she'd pulled her feet up onto the bench sometime in the last hour, and Astrid realized he couldn't remember when, only that the distance between them had been closing all night by inches neither of them had voted on.

"He was stubborn," she said. "And funny, when he forgot to be careful. He used to send me riddles over the radio instead of position reports, just to make sure I was paying attention. Once he sent me a recipe for fish stew mid-transmission and I spent twenty minutes thinking it was code." She smiled, and it wobbled. "It wasn't code. He just wanted me to have the recipe."

Astrid laughed despite himself — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in days. "That sounds like him. That sounds exactly like a man who'd hide a treasure chart inside a decoy galleon and leave the key in a sealed tin with an apology in it."

"He talked about you," Marisol said.

The laugh died in Astrid's chest.

"Not often. He wasn't — he kept people behind glass, your father. But sometimes, late in the watch, when the static was low, he'd tell me things. That you played piano. That you hated boats as a boy and he never forgave himself for it. That you had his temper and none of his patience for lying." Her voice dropped. "He said you were the best thing he ever did and the worst thing he ever lost, and that he'd earned the losing."

Astrid looked at the chart, at the three-colored ink, at the careful paranoid hand of a dead man who had sent fish stew recipes across forty miles of static because he wanted someone to have them.

"He never said any of that to me," Astrid said. His voice was steady. He was proud of how steady it was. It took everything he had.

"I know," Marisol said. "That's why he said it to me."

* * *

The storm peaked around four.

It came through in a long screaming rush that set the whole cove shuddering, and Odalys pounded down the companionway ladder to check the bilge, swore at the water level, swore at the pump, and swore, more gently, at the two of them huddled over the chart like conspirators.

"If the pump dies, you bail," she told them. "If the bailer dies, you pray. And if either of you has anything to say to each other that isn't about bearings—" she jabbed a finger at the space between them, "—say it now, while we're all stuck in a steel box in a hurricane and nobody can walk away."

She went back up. The hatch banged. The wind screamed.

Marisol and Astrid looked at each other.

"Bearings," Astrid said.

"Bearings," Marisol agreed.

But she didn't look back down at the chart. Neither did he. The lamp flickered between them, guttering with each cough of the generator, and in its unsteady light her face was open in a way he had never seen it — all the guardedness gone, all the careful radio-operator composure washed out like a coastline in a flood tide.

"I loved him," she said. "Your father. Not — not the way you're thinking. Like family. Like the brother I didn't get to choose." Her hands tightened on her knees. "For three years he was the voice on my radio every Tuesday and Friday, and I knew his moods better than my own mother's, and when he stopped transmitting I—" She stopped. Started again. "I should have gone after him. I had a boat. I had charts. I sat in that shack and listened to the silence and told myself it wasn't my business, and then Corvo's men came asking questions and I answered them wrong on purpose, and then you walked into Odalys's cabin and I saw his chin on your face and I thought: there it is. There's the debt I owe."

"You withheld the transmissions," Astrid said. "'Corvo knows.' You let me sail blind."

"Yes." No flinch. "Because if you'd known, you'd have turned back, and I'd have lost the only chance I'll ever get to pay him back. I chose the mission over the truth, and I'd do it again, and I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry, and I don't know how to hold all of that at once except to say it out loud to you in a storm at four in the morning."

The generator coughed. The lamp dimmed, steadied.

Astrid thought about the pawnshop. About the compass he couldn't sell. About resentment braided with grief, the knot he'd carried since chapter one of this whole miserable voyage, and how Marisol had just laid her own knot on the desk beside his and said: *look, mine's worse.*

"You answered Corvo's men wrong on purpose," he said.

"Yes."

"That could have gotten you hurt."

"Yes."

"And you sat in a shack listening to my father's silence for weeks because you thought it was your fault."

"I *knew* it was my fault."

"It wasn't." He said it simply, the way you state a bearing. "He hid the chart from everyone, Marisol. From Corvo, from his crew, from me. He hid it so well that it took his own son and his radio operator and a hurricane and a decoy full of bones to find it. Nobody failed him. He just refused, right up to the end, to let anyone else carry the weight." He touched the chart, the careful ink, the apology never sent. "That was his sickness. It isn't yours."

Marisol's eyes were bright and furious and wet, and she blinked hard, and lost, and didn't seem to mind losing.

"You sound like him," she managed.

"I'm told I have his temper."

"You do. It's terrible. He'd be proud."

And then — because it was four in the morning, and the world was nothing but wind and water and a flickering lamp, and because some doors only open in weather like this — she reached across the desk and took his hand. Not the way you take a hand to steady yourself. The way you take a hand to keep it.

Astrid looked at their hands on the chart, on top of his father's careful cipher, and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since a collector's knock on a pawnshop door.

"We should decode the rest," he said, and made no move whatsoever to take his hand back.

"We absolutely should," Marisol said, and made even less.

* * *

Dawn came gray and rinsed-clean, the storm dragging itself out to sea like a brawler leaving the bar. The cove steamed. Odalys appeared at the hatch with coffee that was somehow worse than the tea, surveyed the two of them still bent over the chart, still close, still holding — well. She surveyed the chart.

"Decoded?" she asked.

"Mostly," Astrid said. "The final bearing anchors to the wreck itself. We need the compass on site to fix it. But the depth's real now — sixty feet, black water, trench floor. And there's this." He tapped a note in the margin, red ink, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper: *IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THE TIN FOUND YOU. TRUST THE NEEDLE. DO NOT TRUST ANYONE WHO ASKS ABOUT CARGO MANIFESTS.*

"Why?" Odalys said.

"Because that's what Corvo's divers will ask," Marisol said quietly. "Manifests. Paper trails. Legal cover for a claim." She looked up, and the tenderness of the night was still in her face, but underneath it now was the radio operator again, sharp and certain. "My father taught me that too," she added, surprising herself. "Check the signal twice."

Odalys drank her terrible coffee and considered them both for a long moment.

"Storm's blown out to sea," she said at last. "Which means the *Venganza* blew through it too, and she's heavier than us — she'll have run for deep water and she'll be coming back up the channel by noon. We've got maybe one tide of lead left." She drained the mug. "Fix your bearing, children. We dive on the next slack water."

She climbed back into the gray morning. Astrid and Marisol stood together over the chart, over the red-ink warning, over everything his father had hidden and everything they had said to each other in the dark.

"Trust the needle," Marisol read softly.

Astrid picked up the compass from the shelf where it had spent the storm silent in its case. As he lifted it, the needle stirred — once, twice — and settled, quivering, pointing not north but out through the hull, toward the lagoon, toward the black water, toward whatever his father had died keeping.

"It's pulling," he said.

Marisol put her hand over his on the brass. Outside, the tide had begun to turn.
