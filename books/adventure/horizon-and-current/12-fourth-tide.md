## 12. Fourth Tide

Odalys Reyes had a rule about rivers: they were like people. The ones that looked calmest were the ones you had to watch.

She stood at the wheel of the *Perla* with her boots planted wide and her coffee going cold in the cup wedged beside the compass binnacle — the dead man's compass, the one that pointed at treasure instead of north, which Odalys considered showing off. Behind her, the morning mist was peeling off the water in long ribbons, and behind *that*, faint as a bruise on the horizon, sat the smoke of the *Venganza*.

"Still there," said Tomás from the bow, where he'd been squinting aft every four minutes for the past hour like a man checking whether his mother-in-law's car was still parked outside.

"I can smell her," Odalys said. "Corvo burns cheap coal. It smells like a dentist's waiting room."

"That's specific."

"My brother hated it too." She said it lightly, the way she said most true things, and then wished she hadn't, because Astrid Villalobos looked up from the chart table with that expression he got — the one that meant he was filing something away to ask about later, at the worst possible moment.

The river widened here into a brown-green lane between walls of mangrove, and the current ran lazy and warm, and if you didn't look over your shoulder you could almost pretend this was a pleasure cruise. Odalys looked over her shoulder anyway. Habit. The *Venganza* was Corvo's lead ship, steel-hulled, twin-screwed, and faster than the *Perla* in open water the way a shark is faster than a sea turtle. In open water they'd already lost two miles to her since dawn. Which meant that somewhere ahead, Odalys needed to find water that wasn't open.

Fortunately, she knew where it was.

Unfortunately, so did her stomach, which had been quietly dreading it since sunrise.

* * *

The shortcut was called the Chorro by the ferrymen, and by everyone else it was called nothing, because nobody who called it anything ever came back to name it. It was a side-channel where the river squeezed itself through a gap in the rock ridge no wider than a church door, dropped eight feet in a quarter mile, and came out the other side foaming and furious and full of rocks that moved around whenever they felt like it. High water, it was suicide. Low water, it was suicide with better visibility.

"We are not going through there," said Bruno, the cook, who had come up from the galley holding a knife and half an onion as though prepared to defend the very concept of sanity. "I have seen the Chorro. I saw it once from the safe side, and I prayed."

"You pray beautifully, Bruno," Odalys said. "Do it while we're moving."

"The *Venganza* won't fit through," Marisol said quietly. She was at the radio bench, headphones around her neck, and she had that stillness she got when she was listening to more than one thing at once. "Her beam is too wide. If we go through, we buy ourselves six hours. Maybe eight."

"And if we don't go through," Astrid said, "we're salvage by nightfall."

Odalys let them argue for another minute because a captain learns when silence is worth more than orders. Then she turned the wheel.

"Hang everything loose down," she said. "Bruno, put the onion away before someone loses an eye. Astrid — up on the foredeck. You've got good eyes; call the water for me. Green means go, white means we're about to die, and I'll be honest with you, today those might sound similar."

Astrid scrambled forward. Marisol caught Odalys's sleeve.

"Odalys. Have you actually run this?"

"No."

Marisol waited. She was very good at waiting. It was one of the things about her that unnerved people until they learned it was also one of the things about her that made her the only person aboard whose silences you could trust.

"I ran half of it," Odalys admitted. "Twelve years ago. With my brother. We went in at flood stage because Rafael never met a bad idea he didn't want to dance with." She flexed her hands on the wheel. "We came out. Most of us did."

"Most?"

"The boat didn't." She smiled, because the alternative was not smiling, and a crew takes its weather from its captain. "Ring the engine room. Tell Chalo I want everything she has and I want it now, and tell him if he swears at me I'll take it as a compliment."

* * *

The mouth of the Chorro announced itself before they saw it: a change in the river's voice, from a murmur to a mutter, then to something that sounded distinctly like argument. The mangroves fell back. Black rock rose on either side, slick and dripping, and between the walls the water turned from brown to a churning, boiling green-white that made even the light look nervous.

Odalys brought the *Perla* in on the seam — the smooth tongue of water that every rapid has, the thread the river leaves for things it hasn't decided to kill yet.

"Green!" Astrid shouted from the bow, arms wide, reading the water like scripture. "Green — hold left — LEFT —"

"I heard you the first time!"

"Green again! Rock, dead ahead, big one, it has friends!"

The first drop took them. Eight feet doesn't sound like much on paper. On paper, neither does drowning. The bow plunged, the whole hull shuddered like a dog shaking off a bath, and cold spray came over the rails in a single green sheet that soaked everyone above deck to the skin. Bruno, who had refused to put the onion away, lost the onion. He would mourn it later.

They were through the first gate and into the second before Odalys allowed herself to breathe, and breathing was a mistake, because the second gate was where the river kept its opinions.

"White!" Astrid screamed. "WHITE WHITE WHITE — hard right —"

Odalys threw the wheel. The *Perla* heeled, her port rail kissing the water, and for one long, glittering second Odalys looked straight down into the rapid's throat — all foam and fang and the grey flicker of submerged stone — and felt the old familiar clarity that came at the exact moment a plan stopped being hers and started being the river's.

Then the current took the stern and swung them through the gap like a key turning a lock, and they were through, plunging down the last chute in a chaos of spray and steam and Bruno praying in three languages, and then —

Quiet.

The river spread out below the ridge, wide and brown and gentle again, wearing the same innocent face it always wore, as though it hadn't just tried to murder them out of what Odalys could only assume was professional pride.

The *Perla* settled. Somewhere below, Chalo could be heard informing the engine room, in colorful terms, exactly what he thought of her performance. Astrid stood at the bow dripping like a landed fish, laughing — actually laughing, a surprised, helpless sound, as though his body had decided grief could wait five minutes. Marisol had not let go of the radio bench the entire time and now carefully unpeeled her fingers one at a time.

"Well," Odalys said, mopping her face with a shirt that was already beyond helping. "That went well."

"You said you'd run half of it," Astrid said, coming aft. "Which half?"

"The part where we lived."

* * *

They anchored that evening in a bend where the banks rose high and the trees closed overhead, hiding their mast line from anyone coming upriver. Odalys posted watches, ate Bruno's stew — which had improved immeasurably since the loss of the onion, though nobody dared say so — and found Astrid sitting alone on the cargo hatch with the compass open in his palm, watching the needle tremble toward the dark northeast where the lagoon waited.

She sat down next to him without asking. Captains get to do that.

"It pulled like this the whole way through the rapids," he said. "Even upside down. Even underwater, probably."

"Rafael would have loved that thing," Odalys said.

"Your brother?"

"Mm." She took the compass gently, turned it over, read the worn brass. "He loved anything that pointed somewhere it shouldn't. He was the reason I'm a salvager. And the reason I hate Corvo, and those are the same reason, if you follow it back far enough."

Astrid was quiet. He was getting better at quiet. When she'd first hauled him aboard he'd been all edges — resentment and grief braided together so tight you couldn't tell which strand was which. Now the braid was loosening. Grief, Odalys had learned, was mostly love with nowhere to go, and Astrid was finally finding places to put his.

"He died on Corvo's expedition," Astrid said. Not a question. "The last one. Before my father's."

"Three years ago. The *Santa Rosa* survey." Odalys watched the needle tremble. "You know how deep salvage works. Divers down on a wreck, air lines running back to the surface, tender on the hose. The diver's life is literally in somebody else's hands. That's the covenant. You cut a man's air line, you might as well cut his throat, and every man who's ever breathed from a bottle knows it."

She stopped. The river muttered against the anchor chain.

"We were on a target — a collier, nothing romantic, coal and rivets. Rafael was down at ninety feet when the storm came up fast, the way storms do here. Corvo ordered the ship to weigh anchor and run for shelter." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced level for three years; it fit like an old boot. "Rafael's tender told him to wait, that they'd bring him up first. Corvo countermanded it. Said the cargo manifest mattered more than the dive gear. Called the divers 'equipment.'"

"Odalys—"

"They recovered the hose afterward. Cut clean. Not torn, not fouled — cut, with a knife, from the surface end, so the ship could get underway without fouling her screws." She handed the compass back. "There was an inquiry. There is always an inquiry. Corvo's lawyers described it as a tragic equipment failure. My brother's widow believed them, because believing them was easier than believing the other thing. I didn't believe them. So now I work on a slow boat with a good crew, and I wait."

"For what?"

"For him to need something." She smiled at the dark water. "Vengeance is a poor cargo, Astrid. It doesn't keep, it doesn't sell, and it weighs the same whether you carry it or not. But purpose — purpose floats. What I want isn't Corvo dead. Dead men stop answering for themselves. What I want is Corvo *caught*. Caught in front of witnesses, with his ledger open and his lies read aloud. I want the law to arrive late and see everything."

"The law's a customs cutter three days behind us."

"Then we leave the lights on for it."

Astrid turned the compass over in his hands. "My father owed him. I used to think that made my father weak. Now I think—" He stopped, jaw working. "I think Corvo makes debts the way fishermen make chum."

"Now you're learning," Odalys said. "Come on. Watch change is in an hour, and if you sleep wet you'll dream about onions."

* * *

It was near midnight when Marisol's hand closed on Odalys's shoulder and shook her awake, and one thing Odalys knew about Marisol was that she never shook anyone awake unless the news couldn't wait for morning.

"Radio," Marisol said. "On the band we've been watching. It's the *Venganza*'s traffic — coded, but the operator's sloppy when he's tired, and it's late."

Odalys was already pulling her boots on. "What's he saying?"

"Two things." Marisol's face was lit blue by the set's dial glow, and Odalys thought, not for the first time, that she had the kind of face you wanted beside you in bad weather. "First: Corvo knows his lead ship lost us at the Chorro. He's furious. He's detached his armed launch to run the shoreline channels and pick up our trail."

"Expected. What's second?"

Marisol hesitated. In three weeks Odalys had never once seen her hesitate.

"Second message isn't from the *Venganza*," she said. "It's from the delta stations. A customs cutter's been sighted running upriver — fast, no escort, running dark. Somebody aboard is in a hurry." She looked up. "Odalys, it's headed for the lagoon. Same heading as us."

Odalys stood in the dark of the cabin a moment, listening to the river and the rain starting on the deck above, and felt the strange sensation of a plan she'd been carrying for three years beginning, very slowly, to turn over like a wreck rising off the bottom.

"Wake Astrid," she said. "And Marisol — whatever you sent them, whatever signal bought us our head start—"

Marisol's eyes didn't flinch. "I sent them wide. Like I said I would."

"Then pray your lie holds," Odalys said, reaching for her oilskin, "because tomorrow we dive, and everything Corvo owns is coming to the party."
