## 13. The Mutiny Question

The argument started over coffee, which was fitting, because everything on the *Perla* that could not be settled by shouting was eventually settled by coffee — or at least postponed by it.

Astrid had been up since before dawn, coiling rope in the gray light and trying not to think about the engineer they'd left behind in the drowned village with a rifle and a promise to catch up. His hands needed the work. His mind kept wandering back anyway, the way a dog wanders back to a bone it knows is bad for it.

So he didn't hear the first part of it. He heard only the middle, delivered in the flat, dangerous tone of a man who has rehearsed his grievances on the way to the galley:

"—and I'm saying we've lost a tide, an engine room's flooded twice, and Tomás is still back there sleeping in a hammock strung between two drowned houses. That's not a crew anymore, that's a countdown."

Rafael stood at the head of the companionway with his arms crossed, and beside him — quieter, worse — was Ines, the diver, her gear bag already packed and sitting at her feet like a small, loyal dog that had decided to leave.

Astrid set down the coil of rope. He'd learned something in the last week about setting things down slowly when you wanted people to watch you do it.

"Okay," he said. "Coffee first."

"This isn't a coffee conversation."

"Everything's a coffee conversation. That's what coffee is *for*." He poured three cups from the dented pot Odalys kept permanently on the burner, the way some ships keep a cat. "Sit down, Rafa. You too, Ines. If you're going to quit my ship, you can at least insult me sitting down."

"It's not your ship," Rafael said, but he sat. That was the thing about Rafael: he argued like a man building a wall, brick by brick, but he always sat where you pointed him. "It's Odalys's ship."

"And she's asleep below decks after running us through rapids that would make a river guide weep, so right now the ship answers to whoever's holding the coffee pot." Astrid handed out the cups. "Which is me. Democracy of caffeine."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. She took her cup and held it without drinking, which Astrid had come to understand was her way of accepting a gift while reserving judgment on the giver.

"We're turning back," Rafael said.

"No," said Astrid.

"You haven't even heard—"

"I know what you're going to say. You're going to say the wreck is cursed. You're going to say Corvo has armed divers and we have a cook with a flare gun and a radio operator with excellent taste in music. You're going to say the odds are suicidal." He blew across his own cup. "And then you're going to say the word *gold*, and your voice is going to do that thing it does, that little crack, because you don't actually believe in the gold anymore either. You just need it to be true because otherwise this whole trip is just grief with extra steps."

The galley went very quiet. Somewhere above them, water slapped the hull in a rhythm that sounded almost patient.

"That was unkind," Rafael said finally.

"Yeah," Astrid admitted. "I've been practicing being a leader. Apparently it starts with being rude before breakfast."

* * *

Here was the truth of it, the truth Astrid had been carrying around since the decoy wreck, since the tin box, since his father's apology written in a hand that shook: he did not believe in the gold either. Not anymore. Not the way Rafael needed him to.

He believed in the compass. He believed in Marisol's face when she decoded the chart by lamplight, ink on her fingers, saying *here, look, here* like the map was a letter addressed to both of them. And he believed — this was newer, stranger — that a man could owe a debt so large that no amount of treasure could cover it, and that the only honest currency left was making sure nobody else got billed.

But you couldn't lead with that. Not yet. A crew held together by grief dissolves; a crew held together by gold fights over the last coin. He needed something else, and he hadn't found the word for it until Rafael sat there glaring into his cup like it had personally betrayed him.

"Tell me about the curse," Astrid said. "Seriously. I want to hear it."

Rafael blinked. "You want—"

"The curse. The one your cousin told you about, or the one from the bar in the drowned village, or wherever curses come from these days. Tell me all of it. I'll wait."

Rafael shifted. For a big man he could fold himself small when embarrassed. "It's not — my cousin didn't tell me. The ferryman did. Old man, half-blind, poling his boat through the dead streets like he owned them. He said the San Telmo went down with a bishop aboard, and the bishop cursed anyone who touched the cargo. Said the water keeps what the Church gave it."

"And you believed him."

"He knew things! He knew how many men Corvo brought through. He knew the channel markers before we said anything about markers."

"He lived there, Rafa. Knowing things is his whole job." Astrid leaned forward. "But fine. Let's say the bishop cursed it. Let's say the water keeps what it's given. Then answer me this: why did my father spend eleven years and every peso he ever earned looking for it? Was he stupid?"

"No," Rafael said, too fast, and then caught himself. "I mean — no offense meant. To him."

"None taken. He was plenty of things, but stupid wasn't one of them." Astrid turned his cup slowly on the table. "He wasn't looking for gold, Rafa. I know that now. I found his chart. I read his letter. He was looking for it because Corvo made him look — built the debt like a trap, tightened it year by year, until finding the wreck was the only door out of the room. My father died in that room. And Corvo is coming up this river right now to collect on a dead man's IOU."

"So we run," Ines said quietly. It was the first thing she'd said, and it landed harder than all of Rafael's bricks. "We turn south tonight. We're small. We're fast. We lose him in the delta and we live."

Astrid looked at her. She was the best diver he'd ever shared water with, calm at forty feet the way other people were calm in armchairs, and she had packed her bag with the neatness of someone who had fled things before and gotten good at it.

"If we run," he said, "where do we run *to*?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Corvo holds paper on half the coast. He owns harbormasters the way other men own hats. We run, and in six months there's a Venganza tender tied up next to whatever boat we're working, and a polite man asking questions, and Odalys's brother's name comes up again, and mine, and yours." He shook his head. "There's no south of this, Ines. There's only through."

"Through means past armed divers."

"Through means past armed divers," he agreed. "Or it means we stop playing their game entirely. Which brings me to the part I actually came up here to say, before Rafa's curse interrupted."

* * *

He stood. It felt theatrical and he hated that, but Marisol had told him once — back when they were still two strangers sharing a radio headset and a suspicion — that people don't follow arguments, they follow posture. *Say it standing or don't bother saying it,* she'd said. *The river taught me that. Water doesn't negotiate.*

"Here's my offer," Astrid said. "Not gold. Gold's a maybe. Gold's a story we tell ourselves while we hold our breath. Here's what I can actually promise."

He counted on his fingers, which looked ridiculous and he let it look ridiculous, because ridiculous was disarming and disarming was useful.

"One: nobody dies for Corvo's ledger. Not on this boat. Not for his debt, not for his map, not for his pride. If it comes down to the cargo or a person, we choose the person, every time, and I will personally throw the cargo overboard if that's what choosing looks like. Two: we go home even. Whatever we find or don't find, whatever's left of this crew when it's over, we split it equal and we split it clean, and no one owes anyone. Three—" he paused, because three was the hard one, the one he'd only figured out somewhere around four in the morning staring at the compass needle trembling toward the lagoon, "—three: if any of you want off at the next safe anchorage, Odalys puts you ashore with your share of what we've spent and not one word of shame. Ever. From anyone. Including me."

Silence. The coffee pot ticked as it cooled.

"That's it?" Rafael said. "That's your speech? No gold?"

"The gold's real, Rafa. Probably. Sixty feet down in black water, guarded by a madman's divers. But I'm not asking you to stay for the gold. I'm asking you to stay because turning back means Corvo wins without firing a shot, and I have watched that man win my whole life, and I am *tired* of watching it."

Ines looked down at her packed bag. Then, slowly, deliberately, she picked it up — and stowed it back in its cubby instead of keeping it at her feet.

"I stayed for my brother's boat," she said. "When Corvo's men stripped it for salvage after the last expedition. I stayed because I thought the wreck would buy it back." She met Astrid's eyes. "I'll stay for number one. Nobody dies for the ledger. Say it again."

"Nobody dies for Corvo's ledger."

"Not even you."

Astrid smiled, and it cost him something, because they both knew what the descent tomorrow might ask. "Especially not me. I'm indispensable. Who else makes the coffee?"

"You make terrible coffee," Rafael muttered. But he drained his cup and set it down with a click that sounded, in the language of the *Perla*'s galley, like a signature on a document. "Fine. Fine. But I want it noted that I objected, formally, for the record, so that when we're all ghosts haunting this river, my ghost gets to say *I told you so*."

"Noted," Astrid said. "Witnessed. Entered into the log. Your ghost has full bragging rights."

* * *

Marisol was waiting on deck when he came up, leaning against the rail with the chart case under her arm and the wind pulling at her hair, pretending she hadn't heard every word through the open hatch, which fooled absolutely no one.

"Well?" she said.

"They're staying."

"I know. I heard the ghost bragging rights part." She fell into step beside him as he walked toward the bow. "That was a good speech. The part about throwing the cargo overboard — did you mean it?"

He thought about the flooded hold. About his father lying beside the chests he'd died protecting, guarding a secret instead of a fortune. About the way the word *debt* had shrunk in his chest over the last hundred miles, from a boulder to a stone to a pebble he could finally close his hand around.

"I meant it," he said. "Ask me again tomorrow, underwater, and check my face."

Ahead, the river widened into the brown-green throat of the delta, and the mangroves thinned, and beyond them — though he couldn't see it yet, not quite, only feel it in the compass against his ribs, ticking like a second heart — the lagoon waited, black and sixty feet deep, holding everything.

Marisol followed his gaze, then reached over and put her hand briefly on the chart case, right over the place where his father's true course was drawn in fading ink.

"Fifth tide's at dusk," she said softly. "We'll be there by then."

Behind them, Rafael's voice floated up from the galley, grumbling to Ines about the coffee, about the curse, about leaders who gave speeches before breakfast — and beneath the grumbling, unmistakable, the sound of a man getting the flare gun down from its rack and checking it, carefully, twice.
