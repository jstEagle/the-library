## 14. Fifth Tide

The river stopped pretending to be a river somewhere past the drowned village. It widened, slowed, and went the color of strong tea left too long in the sun — black water, Odalys called it, water so deep and so dark it swallowed light whole and asked for seconds. Marisol stood at the rail of the *Perla* and watched the banks fall away into mangrove that seemed to go on forever, an unbroken green wall broken only by the occasional heron standing in the shallows like a man waiting for a bus that would never come.

"Sixty feet," Astrid said beside her. He had the compass open in his palm, and the needle was trembling — not swinging, not pointing, just shivering in place like a dog that knows it's almost home. "She said sixty feet."

"She said," Marisol agreed.

"You were there when she said it."

"I was there for all of it." She kept her voice even. That was her job, mostly — keeping voices even, including her own. Nine years as a radio operator taught you that the person on the other end of the static could hear everything you didn't say. Fear traveled down a wire better than words did. So did hope. She'd learned to pack both away in a little box behind her ribs and open it only when the transmission was over.

The compass trembled. The water lay black and patient beneath them.

Somewhere under all of that, the *San Telmo* had been sleeping for two hundred years.

* * *

They made the lagoon an hour before sunset, threading the last of the mangrove with Odalys at the wheel and Tomás hanging off the bow taking soundings with a lead line he'd weighted himself and talked to constantly, the way some men talk to engines and other men talk to saints.

"Forty feet," Tomás called. "Fifty. Fifty-five — hold, hold — sixty!" He hauled the line up and squinted at the tallow stuck to its bottom. "Mud. Good holding mud. If we drop anchor here we'll still be here at the Second Coming."

"That's the plan," Odalys said dryly. "We'll tell the Second Coming about Corvo."

Marisol almost smiled. Almost. There was a version of this moment, she thought, where she could have laughed — the whole crew gathered at the rail, filthy and sunburned and half-starved on canned beans, staring down at water that hid a fortune. In another life they'd be cheering. In another life, half of them wouldn't have scars from the rapids, and their engineer wouldn't be three days back upriver guarding freed villagers with a borrowed rifle and a promise.

In this life, everyone at the rail was quiet.

Astrid was quietest of all. He hadn't spoken much since the decoy wreck — since the tin, since the letter he carried folded against his chest like a second heartbeat. Marisol had read that letter by lamplight during the storm, while rain hammered the deck above and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder over his father's true chart, decoding bearings by candle-flame because the electric lamp had died and neither of them wanted to be the one to suggest going below. *I'm sorry I was a better sailor than a father,* the letter said. *If you're reading this, I ran out of tides.* She had cried, quietly, and Astrid had pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of kindness, and then he'd put his hand over hers on the chart table and neither of them had said anything, which was its own kind of everything.

Now he looked at her over the compass. "You're sure about the signal?"

"I'm sure it will work." She paused. "I'm less sure what 'working' will cost us."

"Explain it again for those of us who speak radio the way Tomás speaks to fish."

"Slowly," Tomás said, wounded.

Marisol held up her hands. "Corvo is following us by listening. He has better equipment than we do — bigger mast, more power. He can hear anything we transmit within a hundred miles. So we let him hear exactly what we want him to hear." She counted it off on her fingers. "Tonight, I send a message in our old expedition code — your father's code, the one only his crew used. A position report. But I give him coordinates twelve miles east of here, past the sandbar shoals, where the channel narrows and the water goes shallow. It'll sound urgent. It'll sound real. He'll split his fleet to cover both approaches, or he'll run wide around the shoals, and either way we buy ourselves the night."

"And if he doesn't bite?"

"Then tomorrow morning we're having a very short conversation with the *Venganza*'s cannons, and honestly, Astrid, I'd rather not rehearse that version."

Odalys snorted from the wheel. "She'll bite. Men like Corvo always bite. They're too clever to believe anyone might be smarter than them, so when someone hands them a lie wrapped in their own arrogance, they take it as a compliment."

"That's either very wise or very bitter," Astrid said.

"I contain multitudes," said Odalys.

* * *

Night came down soft and total. No moon — Marisol had checked the almanac twice, because if you're going to bet your life on darkness you should at least know how much darkness you've bought. The stars came out in such numbers that the sky looked like it was leaking, and the black water below reflected none of it, so the *Perla* seemed to hang between two voids, anchored to nothing.

At the radio shack — a plywood box bolted to the aft deck that Tomás affectionately called "the oven" — Marisol warmed the tubes and waited for full dark. Astrid sat on an ammunition crate across from her, elbows on knees, watching her hands.

"You don't have to stay," she said.

"Yes, I do."

"It's boring. Ninety percent of radio is boredom and the other ten percent is terror. You've had plenty of terror lately. Save yourself the boredom."

"I want to see how you do it," he said simply. "How you talk to ghosts."

She looked at him. He didn't mean the dead, exactly. Or maybe he did. His father had been a voice on her wire for eleven days once, years ago, before any of this — a check-in every evening at seven, weather reports and bad jokes and once, memorably, a full recitation of a poem he'd written about a heron, which had been terrible, and which Marisol had praised anyway, because that was also the job.

"He'd have liked you," she said, before she could stop herself.

Astrid went very still. "You think?"

"He liked stubborn people who pretended not to care." She turned back to the set. "It took one to know one."

The tubes glowed amber. The frequency dial cast a small warm circle of light between them, and outside it the night pressed close. At 21:40 by her watch, Marisol keyed the transmitter and began.

She sent it in the old expedition code first — the letter-substitution cipher his father had invented, half naval procedure and half private joke, with deliberate errors built in that only his own crew would recognize as intentional. Then, for good measure, she repeated the essentials in plain voice on the working frequency, pitched her tone tight with false urgency: *position confirmed, marker buoy intact, commencing survey at dawn, requesting support, repeat, requesting support.* Coordinates twelve miles east. Past the shoals. Into the trap.

She signed off with the old call sign. Her throat was tight. Signing off with that call sign felt like speaking at a funeral.

For a long moment there was nothing but the hiss of the empty band.

Then, faint as breath, from far to the south: a carrier wave. Someone else's transmitter, keying up, listening. Not answering. Just — present. The way a shark is present.

"They heard," Astrid breathed.

"Oh, they heard." Marisol killed her own power and sat back. Her heart was hammering and she hated that it was hammering, because a good operator's heart never hammered, but there it was. "And now they're deciding whether to believe me."

"How long does that take?"

"With men like that? Forever. They'll believe it and suspect it in the same breath. They'll come anyway, because they can't afford not to, and they'll hate themselves for coming, and they'll punish someone for the feeling." She rubbed her eyes. "That's how Corvo works. I listened to him for two years through other people's radios. He never forgives the world for being unpredictable."

* * *

It worked. Of course it worked.

By midnight, Tomás — who had appointed himself unofficial listener-in-chief and refused to sleep — reported engine noise far to the east, faint and wrong-footed, the unmistakable sound of heavy ships feeling their way cautiously along a shallow coast. By two in the morning, the sound had split: one group holding east, one group turning wide south around the shoals, exactly as predicted, exactly as hoped.

Odalys stood at the rail in the starlight and allowed herself one minute of visible satisfaction. Marisol timed it. Sixty-one seconds. Then the captain's face folded shut again and she said, "Too easy," and Marisol's stomach dropped, because she'd been thinking the same thing for twenty minutes and hadn't said it aloud, and now it was said, and saying it made it real.

"Too easy," Astrid echoed. "What does that mean? Concretely. I'm a musician, not a tactician. Use small words."

"It means Corvo believed us," Odalys said slowly, "because he wanted to believe us, and men like him are careful precisely because they know that about themselves. So he hedged. He always hedges." She pointed east, where the engine noise murmured. "Most of his fleet is chasing our ghost. But Corvo doesn't gamble his whole hand. He keeps something back."

"Something like what?"

Marisol answered before Odalys could, because she already knew, had known since the carrier wave, had felt it in her teeth the way you feel a storm coming through a wire. "Divers," she said. "He'll send divers ahead. Quiet ones. No engines, no lights. They'll come up the channels tonight, ahead of his ships, to look with their own eyes before he commits. And if they find us—"

"—they won't need the fleet at all," Odalys finished grimly. "Six armed men in the water are worth more to Corvo right now than six guns on a deck. We can outrun his ships. We cannot outrun swimmers."

Nobody said anything for a while. The lagoon breathed around them — that low tidal sigh of black water moving through mangrove roots, ancient and indifferent.

"So we watch the water," Astrid said finally. "All of us. Watches of two, until dawn. Anything moves out there that isn't a fish, we wake the boat."

"And if it's a crocodile?" Tomás asked.

"Is it a crocodile, Tomás?"

"There are definitely crocodiles."

"Then we wake the boat for crocodiles too," Astrid said, "but we do not shoot the crocodiles, because the crocodiles are neutral parties in this conflict and frankly I respect their position."

Marisol laughed — really laughed, for the first time in days, the laugh surprising her on the way out like a bird startled off a branch. Astrid glanced at her, pleased with himself, and she realized with a small internal jolt that he'd done it on purpose. That he'd been doing it for days. Making jokes at exactly the moments the fear got biggest, not to deny the fear but to give everyone somewhere to stand besides inside it.

His father had done the same thing on the radio. Bad jokes at seven o'clock sharp, especially the nights the weather turned ugly.

*Like father, like son,* she thought, and then: *and neither of them will ever say the true thing out loud, so I suppose I'll have to.*

* * *

Her watch was the last one before dawn, paired with Odalys, and it was during that watch — the sky still black but the black going thin at the edges, the air cool and dense as held breath — that Marisol saw the bubble trail.

She didn't shout. Shouting was for amateurs and people whose hearts hammered. She touched Odalys's arm and pointed, and the two of them watched together as a thin silver seam of bubbles crossed the mouth of the channel maybe forty yards out, moving with terrible patience, rising from the deep channel toward the shallows near the *Perla*'s stern. Where the anchor chain hung. Where a swimmer could climb.

"Divers," Odalys whispered. "Told you."

"You told me."

"We're not waking the boat yet." Odalys's voice was flat calm, the calm of a woman who had buried a brother and decided fear was a luxury item. "Waking the boat tells them exactly where we are and how many we are. Right now they know nothing except that we exist. Let them keep knowing nothing." She moved silently to the locker, came back with two machetes and a signal flare pistol, and handed Marisol the flare gun. "You see a face come over the rail, you put a flare in the sky and then you scream like the devil himself is after you. Understood?"

"Understood."

They waited. The bubbles thinned, spread, vanished. Somewhere below and behind, Marisol knew, men in rubber suits were clinging to the anchor chain itself, resting, listening to the hull above them. She imagined their ears pressed to the steel, hearing the crew shift in their bunks, hearing Tomás snore like a landslide, hearing everything and being heard by no one.

The eastern sky went from black to iron to the color of a bruise healing.

And then, from far out on the water, a single flashlight blinked three times. Paused. Blinked three times again.

Odalys exhaled through her teeth. "Signal. They're reporting back." She raised the flare pistol, sighted along it like a duelist, and fired — not at the flash, but high and wide of it, a screaming red star that burst over the channel and lit the whole lagoon crimson for one impossible second.

In that second, Marisol saw them: four dark heads slipping under the surface in smooth unison, professional, unhurried, unafraid. Retreating. Reporting.

In that same second, far to the east beyond the mangrove, she saw — or thought she saw, or perhaps her radio operator's imagination simply drew her the picture — the running lights of the *Venganza* coming about. Turning. Not toward the decoy coordinates anymore.

Toward them.

"Well," Odalys said, lowering the empty flare pistol. "He bit, all right. Hook, line, and armed divers." She looked at Marisol, and in the fading red afterglow her face was grim and steady. "Wake the boat, Marisol. All of it. Tell Astrid his plan worked perfectly."

"Isn't that good news?"

"It's the best news we're ever going to get," Odalys said, "and we've got until the tide turns to decide what to do with it. After that, Corvo's divers come back — and this time they won't be alone."
