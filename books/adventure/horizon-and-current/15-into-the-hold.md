## 15. Into the Hold

The lagoon at dawn was the color of strong tea left too long in the cup, and Astrid stood at the rail of the *Perla* thinking that if he died down there, at least he would die somewhere beautiful, which was more than most debtors could say.

"Sixty feet," Odalys said beside him, arms crossed, watching the black water with the expression of a woman who had buried one man in it already and did not intend to bury another. "Cold. Dark. The hold of a three-hundred-year-old galleon that has been waiting for you like a spider waits for a fly."

"You're very good at pep talks. Has anyone told you?"

"My brother. Once." She handed him the dive slate without looking at him. "Depth markers every ten meters on your line. You feel anything wrong — current pulling wrong, timber shifting, your own heart lying to you about how much air you have — you come up. No treasure is worth a body we have to explain to nobody, because there's nobody to explain it to out here."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me all week."

"I'm not comforting you. I'm giving you orders." But her hand lingered a half-second on his shoulder before she turned away, and that half-second said everything her mouth refused to.

Marisol was already at the stern, coiling his safety line with the particular care she gave to things that mattered. She'd barely slept. None of them had. The false signal she'd sent at midnight had worked — Odalys's lookout swore the *Venganza* had swung wide toward the eastern channel, chasing a ghost Marisol had invented out of static and nerve — but "worked too well," as Odalys kept saying, was a phrase that sat in the stomach like swallowed glass. Corvo was not a man who stayed fooled. He was a man who got curious.

"Your father dove wrecks for thirty years," Marisol said quietly, not looking up from the line. "He taught you?"

"He tried. I mostly complained about the cold and read adventure novels underwater instead of checking my gauges." Astrid managed something that was almost a smile. "He used to say I had salt water where my sense should be."

"He wasn't wrong, from what I've heard."

"No. He wasn't." The words came out rougher than he intended, and Marisol finally looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop coiling and just look at him for a long moment.

"Astrid." She took his hand, pressed the compass into it — his father's compass, brass worn smooth by forty years of palms, its needle even now trembling faintly toward the center of the lagoon like a dog straining at a leash. "Whatever's down there — gold, bones, answers, all three — you come back up. That's the only order I'm giving you, and I'll deny I ever gave it, because technically I don't outrank you."

"You don't outrank me at all. Nobody does. We're a crew of five people and a boat held together by prayer and rust."

"Then consider it a request from someone who—" She stopped. Started again. "Come back up. That's all."

* * *

The water took him the way it always did: first a slap of cold, then silence, then the strange gentle roar of his own breathing amplified inside the mask until it sounded like the sea itself had learned to speak through him.

He went down the line hand over hand, into the green-black dark, watching the light above shrink to a coin, then a fingernail, then nothing much at all. His lamp carved a small yellow room out of the vastness ahead of him, and in that room floated silt, and fish, and the occasional pale ghost of a branch drifting down from the drowned forest that ringed the lagoon.

At thirty feet the water changed character. Colder. Older. At fifty feet he felt it — not heard it, felt it, a pressure against his chest like a hand pressing flat — and then the San Telmo rose out of the dark beneath him.

She was enormous.

Three centuries of river silt had clothed her hull in a soft grey fur of sediment, and mangrove roots had threaded through her ribs like fingers through the ribs of a sleeping animal, but she was unmistakably, magnificently a galleon — high-pooped, beam-wide, listing gently to port as though she'd merely lain down for a nap in 1690 and simply hadn't gotten around to waking. Barnacles crusted her gunwales. A school of silver fish poured out of a cannon port as his lamp touched it, scattering like spilled coins, and Astrid laughed into his regulator, a soundless bubble-laugh, because of course. Of course the sea had made the joke before he could.

The main hatch gaped amidships, half-collapsed, framed by oak beams furred white with freshwater mold. He checked his gauge: plenty of air, plenty of bottom time if he was disciplined, and he was going to be disciplined, he promised himself, he was going to be the most disciplined diver in the history of undisciplined divers.

He went in.

* * *

The hold was a cathedral built by drunk architects.

Timbers leaned at angles no sober carpenter would bless. Silt lay over everything in soft grey drifts, like snow that had fallen indoors for three hundred years. His lamp threw shadows that moved when they shouldn't, and his own bubbles rose past his face in silver strings, wobbling upward toward some crack in the deck far above where daylight came down in thin, dusty shafts — actual shafts of light, underwater, like something out of the adventure novels he'd read instead of checking gauges.

And there, along the port side, stacked with the patient geometry of a cargo that had been lashed down properly before the ship died: the chests.

Iron-bound. Oak. Dozens of them, some collapsed into their neighbors, some still standing shoulder to shoulder like soldiers at a funeral. One had broken open across the centuries, spilling a slow avalanche of coins into the silt, and his lamp caught them — caught them and held them, gold catching light the way nothing else in nature catches light, warm and deep and utterly indifferent to him.

Astrid hung in the water above a fortune.

It was real. That was the thing that stunned him — not the beauty of it, not the size of it, but the simple, dumb reality of it. All those weeks of arithmetic in pawnshops and debt notices and Corvo's smiling men on docks, all of it had been about this: metal, round and flat, stamped by dead kings. Enough to erase his father's notes twice over. Enough to buy the *Perla* outright and a new engine and Odalys's forgiveness for every reckless thing he'd ever do. Enough to make the whole voyage make sense.

His father had found this. Had stood — or swum — exactly here, where Astrid hovered now, and had seen this same light on this same gold.

And then he had walked away from it.

That thought arrived quietly, the way important thoughts do, and Astrid pushed it away, because there was work to do. He pulled the marker float line from his belt, tied it off near the hatch, and began the survey Odalys had drilled into him: condition of chests, depth of silt, feasibility of lifting. Methodical. Professional. The kind of diving his father would have approved of.

He was measuring the third chest when his lamp swept the far corner of the hold and stopped.

There was modern equipment against the bulkhead. A dive tank, rimmed with rust but recognizable. A weight belt. And beside them, half-buried in silt, something that was neither cargo nor equipment.

Astrid's heart began doing something complicated against his ribs.

He crossed the hold slowly, each kick deliberate, silt rising around him in lazy clouds. The shape resolved by degrees: a human outline, settled into the sediment the way things settle when they've been somewhere a very long time. Diving suit rotted to lace. Boots. And on the wrist of one arm, raised as if in greeting or warning or simply reaching —

— a brass compass, tethered by a lanyard to the bones of a hand.

Astrid stopped kicking.

The water was very cold and very quiet and his breathing sounded enormous in his own ears. He drifted closer, close enough that his lamp lit the face, or what the river had left of it, and he knew. There was no moment of doubt, no dramatic recognition. He simply knew, the way you know your own reflection. The jaw. The set of the brow. Even reduced, even returned almost entirely to the river, it was his father.

Emilio Villalobos had died sixty feet down in the hold of the San Telmo, and no one had known, and Astrid had spent eleven years being angry at a ghost.

* * *

Grief underwater is a strange thing. You cannot sob; the mask forbids it. You can only hang there in the green dark, breathing in and out, in and out, while your eyes sting and your throat closes around the mouthpiece and the silt settles around you like ash.

Astrid hung there for what felt like a year.

Then, because his father had taught him better than to panic, he checked his gauge. Half his air. Fine. Fine. He was fine.

He looked again at the scene, and this time he made himself see it properly — not as a son, but as a diver, because his father would have wanted the reading taken correctly, and because taking the reading correctly was the only thing holding Astrid together.

The dive tank: empty, valve open, positioned by the bulkhead. The weight belt: unbuckled, laid aside deliberately — no diver in trouble discards his belt unless he intends to stay. The compass on the wrist: pointing, even now, even after everything, toward the stack of chests. Not toward the exit. Toward the gold.

No. Astrid corrected himself. Look again.

The compass pointed toward the gold — and just beyond the gold, low in the hull where the frames curved inward, there was a section of planking that didn't match. Newer wood. Carefully fitted. Almost invisible unless you were looking, which no one ever would be, because who looks past a fortune?

His father hadn't been guarding the treasure. His father had been guarding the secret of what *wasn't* the treasure — the decoy chart, the false wreck, the whole elaborate misdirection Astrid had found in the tin box two nights ago. Emilio Villalobos had come back down here — alone, at the end, with an old tank and no partner — to check the concealment one last time. To make sure that when Corvo came looking, Corvo would find exactly what Emilio wanted him to find and nothing else.

Something had gone wrong. An old tank. A bad valve. A heart, maybe, or just the cold, or the dark, or the simple arithmetic of a man in his sixties diving alone at sixty feet. The belt came off because he knew. He knew, and he chose to die here rather than surface and risk leading anyone else to the true hold — or perhaps simply because there was nowhere left to go, and he preferred to spend his last minutes making sure the disguise held.

Dead not of greed. Dead protecting the secret. Dead the way a cartographer dies: keeping the map true.

Astrid reached out and closed his father's hand gently over the compass, tucking the bones in, the way you'd pull a blanket over a sleeping man. It accomplished nothing. It accomplished everything.

"I'm sorry," he said into his regulator, and the words came out as bubbles, and the bubbles rose, and that was all any of us ever manage, he thought. Words that rise. Whether anybody hears them or not.

Eleven years of anger, and the man had been down here the whole time, keeping faith with a promise Astrid had never understood. Not abandoned. Deployed. Sent ahead, like a scout, into a country none of them had meant to visit so soon.

The debt. Absurdly, horribly, the thought arrived anyway: *the debt.* Corvo's notes, the interest compounding like rot, the collectors, the threats. And here was the answer to all of it, sitting in the silt within arm's reach, enough gold to buy back his whole life — and it suddenly weighed nothing. Less than nothing. It was ballast. It was the weight belt his father had unbuckled. Gold was the thing you let go of when you understood where you actually were.

Not yet, though. Not yet. There was a plan half-formed, charges rigged, a choice waiting at the top of the ladder. First he had a job to finish, the last piece of salvage that mattered.

He worked carefully, reverently, freeing the compass from the lanyard — his father would want it traveling, not moored — and gathering the small personal effects: a corroded dive watch, a waterproof pouch that had somehow kept its seal, and inside it, dry as the day it was written, a folded page in his father's cramped handwriting.

Astrid couldn't read it down there. He saved it. Some things you surface with.

His gauge said go. He went — but at the hatch he stopped, turned back, and gave the hold one last look: the gold gleaming dully under his lamp like a sleeping animal that had finally stopped dreaming, the cathedral of leaning timbers, the corner where his father kept his long watch.

"Sixty feet," Astrid said to him. "You always did overdo everything."

And he kicked for the light.

* * *

They pulled him over the rail into a wall of noise — Odalys shouting, Marisol already stripping his fins, somebody shoving hot coffee into his hands that he would later have no memory of drinking. He was shaking. He noted this distantly, as though the shaking belonged to someone else, a colleague he was fond of.

"Talk," Odalys demanded. "Is it there? Is it intact? How many chests, what condition, can we—"

"It's there." His voice came out scraped. "All of it. Intact. Enough to pay Corvo ten times over and buy us each an island."

Odalys grinned — a fierce, hungry, entirely understandable grin — and then she actually looked at him, and the grin died. "Astrid. What else is down there."

Marisol had gone very still, kneeling by the tank rack, her eyes on his face with an expression of dawning, terrible knowledge. She had known his father. She had been the last voice to hear him. She had played Astrid those transmissions with her hands shaking.

"There's a body," Astrid said. "In the hold. It's my father."

Nobody spoke. The lagoon lapped gently against the hull, indifferent, patient, full of gold.

Marisol crossed the deck and took his face in both her hands, and he let her, and for a moment the whole world was her cold fingers and the smell of coffee and diesel and the enormous fact of his father, dead and found and forgiven, all at once.

"He sent the decoy chart to you," she whispered. "Before the end. He made sure you'd find the tin. He was passing it to you, Astrid. The whole time, he was handing it to *you*."

"I know." Astrid closed his hand around the compass — the old one from his pocket, warm now, its needle steady. "I know what he was doing. And I know what I have to do."

He straightened, and looked at Odalys, and at the eastern horizon where, sooner than any of them wanted, the *Venganza* would come around and come hunting.

"Get everyone below," he said. "We need to talk about explosives."

Far off across the black water, faint as a lie, a ship's horn sounded once — and did not sound like it was turning away.
