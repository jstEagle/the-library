## 16. Blood in the Lagoon

Odalys Reyes had been shot at before. She had never been shot at during a sunrise this beautiful, and she found the combination frankly insulting.

The lagoon turned pink and gold around them like a postcard from a country that had never heard of salvage wars. Mist lifted off the black water in slow ribbons. Somewhere in the trees a bird started its morning argument with another bird, and Odalys thought: *enjoy it, little fool. In about ninety seconds neither of you will be able to hear yourselves think.*

"Contact," said Tomás from the bow, flat as a man reading a grocery list. "Port side. Three divers in the water, maybe four. And there—" he pointed with his chin, because his hands were busy racking the slide of an old shotgun he treated better than most people, "—the skiff behind the point."

She saw it. A low gray shape sliding out of the shadow of the mangroves, engine muffled, riding light and mean. Corvo's work. Everything the man owned looked like it had been designed by someone who hated joy.

"Astrid's still down," Marisol called from the radio shack, her voice tight as wire. "Nineteen minutes on his line."

*Twenty minutes of air,* Odalys thought. *One armed skiff inbound. Four divers who swim like they were born in oil.* She did the arithmetic every captain does at moments like this—the kind where all the columns refuse to add up—and then she stopped doing it, because the answer was always the same anyway.

You fight with what you have, where you stand.

"Marisol, get him up. Whatever you have to say into that headset, say it now and make it short." She turned to the deck at large. "Tomás, the shotgun's yours—skiff first, divers second, don't waste shells on water. Chelo, engine room, keep her hot but quiet. If we have to run, I want to run *now*, not in a minute and a half."

"And you?" Chelo asked.

Odalys picked up the rifle she'd kept wrapped in an oilcloth under her bunk for eleven years, ever since a night on the Venganza when a lifeline parted and her brother went down screaming her name into forty fathoms of nothing.

"Me," she said, "I'm going to go say hello to Renco Fuentes."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about revenge, Odalys thought as she crouched behind the Perla's wheelhouse with the sun climbing and the first shots cracking across the water like green branches snapping: it is not a fire. It doesn't burn clean and die down. It's more like rust. It gets into everything. You can paint over it, you can forget it's there, and then one morning you look down and your hands are orange to the wrist.

Renco Fuentes had been Corvo's dive master on the last expedition. He'd been the one holding the other end of the rope.

The official story—Corvo's story, told quietly over drinks in three different ports—was that the cable frayed. Bad luck, deep water, these things happen. But Odalys had pulled that same cable out of the water afterward, and she'd held the end of it in her two hands, and frayed steel looks like a broom. Cut steel looks like a blade had kissed it. She knew the difference. Every salvager knows the difference. It's the first thing they teach you, right after *don't hold your breath coming up.*

Corvo had ordered the line cut to save the cargo winch. Renco had done the cutting. And Renco had looked her in the eye at the memorial, three weeks later, shaken her hand, and said, *He was a good man, your brother. The sea takes the best of us.*

The sea hadn't taken him. A knife and an order had.

Now Renco's skiff came around the point at full throttle, and standing in its bow with a machine pistol held in that lazy, competent way of his was the man himself, older by eleven years, grayer at the temples, still wearing that expression of mild professional regret like a uniform.

"Perla!" he called across the water, over the stutter of gunfire from his divers surfacing among the mangrove roots. "Reyes! Corvo offers terms! Send out the boy and the chart, and everyone else floats home!"

"You've gotten worse at lying!" she shouted back. "There's no floating home from this and we both know it!"

He laughed. That was the worst part. He actually laughed, delighted, like they were old friends at a festival—which, she supposed, in the ugly grammar of their trade, they were.

Then he raised the pistol and shot out the Perla's starboard running light, and the conversation was over.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and maneuvers. This was a brawl conducted across sixty yards of pink water, with the sun rising through gunsmoke like the whole thing was being staged for a painter with poor morals.

Tomás worked the shotgun with the patience of a man shelling nuts. The skiff's engine took a hit and started smoking; Renco's helmsman swung them wide behind a curtain of mangroves. In the shallows, two of Corvo's divers surfaced near the hull and tried to fix something to it—a charge, Odalys guessed, or a grappling hook to drag them off station—and Chelo solved the problem in the traditional manner, by backing the Perla's propwash over them until they let go and thrashed away toward the reeds, alive but thoroughly humiliated.

Odalys barely saw any of it. She was watching the skiff, and the skiff was watching her, and between them stretched eleven years and forty fathoms.

"He's going to board us," Tomás said, reloading. "That's what he wants. He doesn't want to sink us—he wants the wreck intact. He'll come alongside and put men on our deck."

"Then we'll be rude hosts."

"Odalys." Tomás lowered the shotgun, just slightly, and gave her the look he gave her maybe twice a year—the look that said *I've followed you into four bad ideas and I'm asking you to make this one a good one.* "Whatever you're planning, plan it angry later. Plan it smart now."

"I am being extremely smart," she said. "I'm letting him come to me."

And that was true, mostly. Because here was the other thing about rust: it eats judgment. Some cold, sensible corner of her brain noted that walking out onto an open deck to face the man who'd killed her brother was not tactics, it was theater. The rest of her didn't care. Eleven years was long enough to wait for a stage.

The skiff came around the mangroves one more time, slower now, engine coughing, and Renko stood in the bow with his pistol down at his side and his free hand raised—not surrender, invitation.

"Reyes!" he called. "Captain to captain! Five minutes, no weapons, and I give you my word—"

"Your word," Odalys said, stepping out from behind the wheelhouse into the open, rifle slung across her back, hands empty and visible. "That's rich. Let me tell you what your word weighs, Fuentes. I've held it. It weighs exactly as much as a cut cable."

Something flickered across his face. Not guilt—she'd given up expecting guilt the way you give up expecting rain in a drought. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has known for eleven years that this bill would come due and had hoped, professionally, to die first.

"You want to talk about the San Rafael," he said quietly. The gunfire had lulled; even his divers in the reeds seemed to be listening. "Eleven years, Reyes. You think I haven't—"

"Don't." Her voice came out lower than she intended, almost gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "Don't you dare tell me you've thought about it. My brother thought about his daughter's wedding while he drowned. Your thinking doesn't balance anything."

"The order came from above. You know how it works. You cut a line or you lose the cargo, you lose the cargo or Corvo loses the ship, Corvo loses the ship and fifty men don't get paid, and some of those men have children too—"

"There it is." She nodded slowly. "The ledger. Everyone hides behind the ledger. Corvo hides behind it, you hide behind it, and at the bottom of every page there's my brother, written off as *operational loss*." She unslung the rifle and let it hang from one hand, muzzle down. "I'm not going to shoot you, Renco. I want you to understand that. Shooting you makes it clean, and none of this was clean."

For a moment—one strange, suspended moment, with the sunrise gilding the water and both crews frozen in the reeds and on the rails—it almost seemed like it might end there. Like the lawless world might, for once, accept a confession instead of a corpse.

Then Renco's hand moved.

Later, replaying it a thousand times, Odys could never swear whether he was reaching for the pistol or reaching to drop it over the side. Whether the last eleven years had finally caught up with him or whether he simply, in the end, chose the ledger again. His hand went to his hip and her body answered before her conscience could vote, and the rifle came up and spoke once, and Renco Fuentes sat down hard in the bow of his own skiff with a red bloom opening on his shoulder.

Not dead. She'd meant it dead-center chest and her hands, those honest traitors, had pulled the shot wide at the last instant—or maybe they hadn't, maybe she'd aimed there all along and only told herself otherwise after. That was the wound inside the wound: she would never know if she'd shown mercy or missed.

His return shot was not so conflicted. It took her high in the left side, below the collarbone, a punch like a falling anchor, and the sky tilted, and the beautiful stupid sunrise spun, and the last thing she registered before the deck met her back was Tomás roaring and the skiff turning away, trailing smoke, trailing Renco, trailing the conversation they should have finished eleven years ago.

* * *

Pain, Odalys discovered, had a sense of humor. It waited until she was awake and watching Marisol dig the bullet out with forceps boiled in rum before it really introduced itself.

"You're lucky," Marisol said. Sweat stood on her forehead in the cabin heat. "Through and through, no bone. Half an inch lower and we'd be having a very different conversation."

"Lucky," Odalys repeated. The word tasted like the rum they'd poured down her throat as anesthesia, which was to say: like something meant to help that mostly burned.

"You want to hear the funny part?" Tomás offered from the doorway, where he was pretending to stand guard and actually trying not to watch. "Fuentes made it back to the Venganza. Chelo's cousin's radio contact says Corvo's surgeon has him. So you shot him, he shot you, and now you're both going to live. Very tidy. Nobody learns anything."

"That's the sea for you." Odalys hissed as the forceps found their target. "It keeps the receipts and skips the lesson."

Marisol tied off the last stitch and sat back, wiping her hands. For a while neither woman spoke. Through the cabin wall they could hear the lagoon—the water lapping, the birds resuming their interrupted argument, and beneath it all, faint and rhythmic, the sound of Astrid and the others rigging charges on the wreck below, hammers on steel transmitted through sixty feet of black water like a heartbeat from the bottom of the world.

"You went out there to kill him," Marisol said at last. Not accusing. Just setting it on the table between them, the way she set everything.

"I went out there to *end* him. There's a difference." Odalys stared at the overhead beams. "I've had eleven years to imagine it. The moment. And do you know what I felt when the rifle went off? Nothing. No fire, no peace. Just rust flaking off and more rust underneath." She laughed, and it hurt, and she laughed anyway. "My brother used to say I was the patient one. That I'd be the one who grew old gracefully. Look at me now—shot on a beautiful morning, bleeding on my own deck, over a man who wasn't even worth the bullet."

"He was worth the truth," Marisol said. "That's not nothing."

Odalys turned her head and looked at her—the quiet radio operator who'd become, somewhere in the mangroves and the storms and the sleepless nights, the closest thing to family the Perla had left. "And what about you? When this is done. When the wreck goes or Corvo takes it. What's your truth?"

Marisol was quiet a long moment. Outside, the hammering from the deep continued, steady as a pulse.

"That I'm tired of secrets keeping people safe," she said finally. "They don't. They just decide who gets hurt last."

"Well." Odalys reached over with her good arm and gripped the younger woman's wrist, brief and hard. "When Astrid comes up off that wreck, somebody ought to tell him the truth too. All of it. Before Corvo arrives and decides it for him."

As if summoned—as if the world had been eavesdropping, which in Odalys's experience it usually was—footsteps thundered up from the forward hatch, and young Chelo burst into the cabin without knocking, wild-eyed, salt dripping from his hair.

"Skipper—you need to see this. The boy's found something down there. Something big. And—" he swallowed, glancing at the horizon where the mist was burning off, "—there's smoke on the water. Coming up the channel. It's the Venganza. She's entering the lagoon."

Odalys pushed herself upright against the pain, against the stitches pulling, against eleven years of rust finally breaking loose, and looked at Marisol.

"Well then," she said. "Somebody get me topside. It seems the tide's come in early."
