## 17. The Choice at Six Tides

The detonator was smaller than Astrid had expected.

That was the thing nobody told you about decisions that could drown a fortune — they didn't arrive wearing weight. They arrived in your palm like a tin of sardines, a dented box with a plunger and two wires running down through the Perla's deck plates to sixty feet of black water, where Odalys's engineer had spent the last three hours wiring charges along the San Telmo's spine like a surgeon stitching a patient she intended to kill.

Astrid turned it over in his hands. The brass casing was warm from his pocket. His father's compass sat beside him on the coil of rope, needle steady now, pointing straight down — pointing, as it had pointed for two days, at everything he was supposed to want.

"You're going to wear a groove in it," Marisol said.

She had come up beside him at the rail without making a sound, which was her particular gift. The lagoon lay around them like held breath — black water ringed in mangrove so dense it looked poured rather than grown, the air thick enough to chew. Somewhere beneath that water slept four hundred years of silver, emeralds the size of thumbs, gold coin stamped with the faces of dead kings. Enough to erase his debt ten times over. Enough to buy back every instrument he'd ever pawned, the whole shop, the whole street.

Enough to make Corvo the richest man on the coast, and everyone who'd ever crossed him very, very sorry.

"I'm thinking," Astrid said.

"That's what worries me." She leaned on the rail next to him, close enough that their shoulders touched. "You think like your father did. Out loud, in circles, arriving somewhere you already decided to go."

"Is that an insult or a compliment?"

"Yes."

* * *

The Venganza had entered the river mouth an hour after dawn.

They'd all heard her before they saw her — the deep, arrogant throb of engines too big for these waters, the sound of a man who had never once considered whether a channel would hold him. Then she came around the last bend of mangrove, white hull gleaming, gun mounts swept clean and hungry, and the whole lagoon seemed to flinch.

Odalys stood at the wheel of the Perla and watched her come with an expression Astrid had learned to read the way sailors read weather: calm surface, catastrophe underneath.

"There she is," Odalys said quietly. "Six tides I've chased that ship. Now she comes to me."

"Odalys—" Marisol started.

"We're not fighting her. Don't be idiots." Odalys spat over the rail, ceremonial, almost fond. "We're hiding behind the point until Astrid decides what kind of man he is. There's a difference."

They'd tucked the Perla into a cut behind a screen of mangroves, engine cold, canvas down, the crew moving on whispers. From here Astrid could see the Venganza drop anchor in the middle of the lagoon with the unhurried confidence of a predator lying down on top of its kill. A launch went over her side. Armed men. They were heading for the wreck site — heading for the coordinates Marisol's false signal had been meant to pull them away from, except Corvo hadn't swallowed the bait whole. He'd sent divers ahead anyway. Careful, patient, poisonous.

And now there was nothing between Corvo and the treasure but sixty feet of water, a handful of rigged charges, and one indecisive son of a debtor.

* * *

The arithmetic was simple. That was the cruelty of it.

Sign the paper, raise the treasure, split it under salvage law — such as it was out here, where law was whatever floated nearest your gunwale. Corvo took his share plus interest, which by his ledgers meant most of it, but some portion would reach Astrid's hands, and that portion would erase the debt his father had signed, the debt that had driven the old man upriver in the first place, the debt that had gotten him killed face-down in a flooded hold protecting a secret from the very man who'd manufactured the ledger against him.

Gold would free him. That was the whole pitch. Gold would free him, and he could walk away clean, and the compass could go back in a drawer where it belonged instead of dragging him across half a delta by the nose.

Destruction would free everyone else.

That was the part he kept circling back to, the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth. If the San Telmo folded into the trench, Corvo got nothing. Not the silver, not the leverage, not the empire he'd built one drowned crew at a time — because Astrid had seen the manifest pages now, seen the pattern of expeditions that went out full and came back short-handed, always short-handed, always with Corvo holding the insurance and the survivors holding silence. Odalys's brother was in that pattern. Maybe others. Probably others.

If the wreck died, Corvo's whole machine lost its engine.

But so did Astrid. So did the debt — unpaid, unpayable, hanging off him like an anchor with no bottom to rest on. Corvo didn't forgive debts. Corvo collected them, in whatever currency the debtor had left.

"He'll take it out of you," Marisol said, as if she'd been reading his thoughts, which after eleven days aboard she probably could have done in Morse code. "If you sink it. He won't ruin you quickly. That's not how he works. He'll ruin you slowly, and he'll enjoy it."

"I know."

"And you're still going to do it."

Astrid looked at her. Her dark eyes were steady on the Venganza, her jaw set in the way it set when she was frightened and refusing to file the paperwork on it.

"What makes you so sure?"

"Because you haven't asked me once whether we should keep the gold." She turned to him then, and something in her face cracked open — not softness exactly, more like honesty with nowhere left to hide. "Eleven days, Astrid. You've complained about your debt, cursed your father, threatened to sell my radio for parts—"

"It was a joke."

"—and not once have you asked anyone on this boat to help you get rich." She shook her head slowly. "Your father spent his last transmission warning us about Corvo instead of calling for help. You're more his son than you want to be."

* * *

Odalys found them there an hour later, when the sun stood straight overhead and the shadows of the mangroves shrank to nothing, as if the day itself were trying to stop taking sides.

Her arm was bound where the lieutenant's blade had opened it that morning in the lagoon — the fight felt like it belonged to another week — and she moved with the careful economy of a woman rationing her blood. She planted herself in front of Astrid and looked at the detonator in his hands the way a gambler looks at a final card.

"Well?" she said.

"I'm deciding."

"You've been deciding since dawn. The tide turns in forty minutes. When it does, the Venganza's divers will be on the wreck, and after that it stops being a choice and starts being a memory." She glanced at Marisol, then back. "So let me help you decide. My brother Tomas signed onto Corvo's last expedition because we needed the money and Corvo pays well and pays fast. Fourteen men went down to a wreck off the shoals. Twelve came up. And the two who didn't — Tomas and a boy called Ferro — went down again when their line fouled, and Corvo ordered the line cut."

Astrid had heard this before, in fragments, in the dark stretches of the voyage. But Odalys had never said it looking at him like this, direct as a boarding hook.

"Cut," she repeated. "Not jammed. Not frayed. Cut, with a knife, from above, because the cargo net was snagged and Corvo wanted his silver more than he wanted two men who were, by his own arithmetic, replaceable. Ferro was nineteen." She let that sit. "So when you hold that plunger and wonder whether the world will miss Corvo's fortune — understand that I am not a neutral party. I want it gone. I want it gone so badly I can taste seawater. But I'm telling you this because you deserve to decide with your eyes open, not because I've decided for you."

"And if I raise the treasure instead? Take the deal?"

Odalys smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but it was an honest one, which on this delta was worth more.

"Then I'll take my share, bury my brother properly, and spend the rest of my life knowing what money costs. And you'll spend yours learning it." She put one hand briefly on his shoulder — the wounded arm, deliberately, he suspected, because Odalys Reyes did nothing by accident. "Forty minutes, Astrid. Your father chose Corvo's ledger over his own son. Don't you dare choose it over ours."

She walked aft. The deck rocked gently beneath them, the lagoon breathing in and out.

* * *

He went below alone.

The Perla's cabin was low and hot and smelled of diesel and old coffee, and his father's chart — the true chart, the one from the sealed tin — was still spread across the table where they'd decoded it through the storm, weighted at the corners with mugs and a wrench and Marisol's hairbrush. Around the edges, in his father's cramped hand, were notes. Depths. Bearings. And in the lower corner, half-hidden under the wrench, the sentence Astrid had read forty times and still couldn't finish reading calmly:

*I built the decoy so no one would find the real one. Especially not you. Forgive me. Some inheritances are chains.*

Astrid sat down heavily on the bench and put the detonator on the table between the mugs.

The truth he'd been swimming away from for six tides finally rose and surfaced, gasping, in the middle of the cabin: this was never about the debt. The debt was real, and heavy, and Corvo would collect it with interest and malice — but the debt was also the excuse. The excuse to come. The excuse to hope. Because if he raised the treasure, then his father's last voyage meant something legible, something with a number attached, something you could balance. The old man hadn't thrown his life away chasing a ghost; he'd been working. Providing. Even dying, he'd been paying bills.

But that was a lie, and Astrid knew it was a lie, and he was so tired of carrying it.

His father had died protecting a secret *from* greed — not for it. Had spent his final hours in a flooded hold, an old man with a bad heart, standing guard over an empty decoy so that the real wreck might stay asleep and harmless. He hadn't been paying a debt down there. He'd been refusing one.

And Astrid could stand on this deck and sign Corvo's paper and call it survival, call it sense, call it what any reasonable man would do. Or he could put his father's actual last wish ahead of his father's actual last mistake, and let the ledger die with the gold.

The compass sat in his pocket. He took it out and set it on the chart, and the needle swung once, twice, and settled — pointing not north, not toward the wreck, but along the bearing line his father had drawn from the decoy to open water. To the horizon. As if even in brass and glass, the old man was saying: *this way out. Leave it. Go.*

"Yeah," Astrid said aloud, to the empty cabin, to the needle, to nobody. "I hear you. You could've written a shorter letter, though."

From above came Marisol's voice, tight, pitched down the companionway: "Astrid. Launch is coming back. They're bringing something up."

* * *

He climbed into the light in time to see it through his father's borrowed binoculars: the Venganza's launch, riding low, and on its deck a shape under tarpaulin that caught the sun in long yellow flashes even through canvas. A strongbox. Maybe two. Corvo's divers had found the hold, and Corvo's patience had just been rewarded, and every minute Astrid spent being philosophical was a minute the crane on the Venganza's stern spent swinging cargo nets into black water.

"They'll have the main cache by nightfall," Odalys said grimly. "Maybe sooner. Whatever you're going to do—"

"I know."

He stood at the rail with the detonator, and the whole crew gathered without being asked — Marisol at his left, Odalys at his right, the two mutinous crewmen who'd argued for turning back standing a little apart but not leaving, and old Tomás-at-the-engine whose name wasn't really Tomás but who answered to it and had stopped correcting people somewhere around the third rum. Seven of them. Against a gunship. It shouldn't have made him laugh, but it did, a small helpless sound the wind took away.

"If I push this," Astrid said, "there's no share. There's no payout. There's a debt collector named Corvo who will hunt every one of us to the ends of whatever maps he can afford, and I won't pretend otherwise." He looked around at their faces, sunburned, salt-cracked, exhausted. "And if I don't push it, we're rich, and he's rich, and the only difference between us is that we'll feel worse about it."

Silence. Water against the hull. Somewhere in the mangroves a bird screamed once and thought better of it.

Then Tomás-at-the-engine shrugged with magnificent indifference and said, "I've been poor on this boat for nine years. It's got good bones. I'd hate to see it turn into a yacht."

Marisol laughed — wet-eyed, sudden, real — and put her hand over Astrid's hand on the plunger, not pushing, just resting there, an anchor and an answer both.

"Do it for the ones who went down with the line cut," Odalys said quietly. "Do it for your father. Do it because some things should sleep." Her voice roughened. "And then, mi amigo, you run. Because I promise you Corvo will come for this boat like the wrath of God with a bank loan."

Astrid looked down at the detonator one last time. Small. Dented. Lighter than a conscience and heavier than a fortune.

On the Venganza, amidships, a figure in a white suit stood at the rail watching the Perla's hiding place with the unhurried attention of a man who had already sent for the bill. Even at this distance, Astrid would have sworn Corvo smiled.

The tide began to turn. The water in the lagoon shivered, reversed, sighed.

Astrid closed his eyes, saw his father's face the way it had looked in the flooded hold — not accusing, not pleading, just *waiting*, the way the dead wait for the living to catch up —

— and wrapped both hands around the plunger.

"Corvo!" he shouted across the water, absurdly, wonderfully, knowing the man couldn't hear him. "The debt dies today!"

He pushed.
