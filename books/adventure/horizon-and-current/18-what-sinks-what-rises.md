## 18. What Sinks, What Rises

The detonator was a small thing — a wooden box the size of a hymnal, a brass plunger, two wires running down into black water. It fit in my palm like it had been made for it. Like it had been waiting three hundred years for someone foolish enough to hold it.

Below us, sixty feet down in the lagoon's dark heart, the San Telmo lay with her hold full of silver and her dead captain keeping watch over both.

"Say something," Marisol said quietly beside me. "You've been quiet for ten minutes, and you only get quiet like that when you're about to do something stupid or noble."

"Those are usually the same thing on this boat."

"That's what worries me."

I looked at her. Dawn was coming up behind her, gilding the edge of her jaw, catching the salt in her hair. Somewhere out past the mangroves, I could hear the Venganza's engines — Corvo's ship feeling its way through the narrow cut, an hour away, maybe less. An hour to make a fortune. An hour to lose one.

"Astrid." Odalys stood at the rail, arms crossed, her bandaged shoulder held stiff against her side. She'd paid for our dawn in blood already this morning; she wasn't going to flinch at whatever came next. "Whatever you're deciding, decide it fast. The current turns in twenty minutes, and if we're still sitting here when the Venganza rounds the point, none of this matters."

"I know."

"You don't look like you know."

"I'm doing my best impression of a man who knows."

Despite everything — the wound, the blood drying on her shirt, the armed divers who'd come out of the water an hour ago shooting — Odalys almost smiled. "Your father did the same thing. Stood there looking like a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish."

That landed harder than she probably meant it to. My father. Who had spent his last year lying to me by silence, who had died face-down in a flooded hold protecting a secret from men like Corvo, who had left me nothing but debts, a compass, and an apology sealed in a tin box.

And a choice. He'd left me the choice too, though he hadn't known it. The chart in his hand had shown him exactly where the treasure slept. All he'd ever had to do was sell it. Instead he'd built decoys. He'd planted false wrecks and false signals and died keeping the truth from the man who owned his paper.

*Corvo knows,* he'd said on his final transmission. Not *help me.* Not *come find me.* Just that. A warning aimed forward through time, at whoever was listening.

Me. It had always been me.

I looked down at the detonator again. Gold would free me. That was the whole ugly arithmetic of it. Sixty feet below, enough silver to pay Corvo twice over, to buy back every note, every lien, every threat — to walk into any port in the world a rich man instead of a debtor. To be free.

But free *how?* Free because I'd handed Corvo the one thing he wanted more than my father's life? Because I'd dug up the dead and sold them by the bar? Every coin in that hold would have Corvo's fingerprints on it before the week was out, and mine under his, and my father's under mine, all pressed into the same soft metal forever.

Odalys had said it best, three nights ago, running the rapids with the Venganza's wake closing behind us: *"Some ledgers you close by paying. Some you close by burning the book."*

"Marisol," I said. "Get on the radio. One more time."

She raised an eyebrow. "We're a little past the point where a fake signal helps us."

"Not a fake signal. A real one. Open channel. Tell the customs cutter exactly where we are. Coordinates, plain language, no codes. Tell them there's been gunfire on the water and a salvage vessel firing on civilians. Tell them everything."

"They'll take hours to get here."

"They'll take exactly as long as they take. But if Corvo thinks help is coming — if he hears me say it on an open channel — he'll hurry. And a hurried man makes mistakes."

Marisol studied me for a long moment, and then something moved across her face that I couldn't name — pride, maybe, or grief wearing pride's clothes. "You're going to fire them," she said. "The charges."

"I'm going to fire them."

"And the debt?"

"The debt can learn to swim."

* * *

It took four minutes to clear the deck. Four minutes to move everyone off the Perla's port side, away from the water directly above the wreck, away from anything that might come up out of it when sixty tons of old oak and older silver decided to change address. Tomás helped Odalys to the stern rail, arguing gently with her the whole way about whether she could stand unassisted (she could not; she insisted anyway). The freed villagers' boat was long gone upriver, carrying our engineer and our half-tide and our good conscience with it.

Marisol keyed the transmitter and spoke clearly, calmly, like a woman reading a grocery list: coordinates, vessel names, shots fired, request for immediate customs intervention. Then she set the handset down and turned to me.

"Open channel," she confirmed. "He heard it. If he was listening, he heard it."

"He was listening."

"How do you know?"

"Because he's Corvo. Listening is the only honest work he's ever done."

I stood at the rail with the detonator in both hands. Below me the lagoon lay flat and black and patient, holding its treasure the way it had held it for three centuries — without opinion, without appetite, without any of the hunger that living men brought to it.

"Goodbye, Papá," I said quietly. Not to the wreck. To whatever part of him was still down there, standing guard. "You kept it safe. I know now. You kept it safe the whole time, and nobody thanked you, and I'm thanking you. I hope wherever you are, somebody finally told you what you were worth. It was never the silver."

Then I thought — absurdly, in the last second — of the pawnshop, six tides and a lifetime ago. Of the old man behind the counter offering me forty pesos for my trumpet, and me sliding my father's compass back into my pocket because I couldn't say the word *sell* while looking at it. Of how much I'd hated that compass, that morning. How much I'd hated *him.*

How wrong I'd been about nearly everything.

I pushed the plunger down.

* * *

The lagoon did not explode. That was the strange part. I'd expected thunder, a fist of white water, some cinematic violence worthy of the moment. Instead there was a deep, felt-more-than-heard *whump*, like the world clearing its throat, and then the surface of the lagoon simply — changed. It bulged. It shivered. A ring of bubbles rolled outward from the center as if the water itself were exhaling after holding its breath for three hundred years.

And then the San Telmo began to go down.

We saw her through the impossible clarity of the disturbed water — the dive lights we'd left anchored along her hull still burning, still faithful, lighting her going like lamps at a funeral. Her stern lifted, slowly, ponderously, the way an old man rises from a chair he's decided to leave for good. The trench beneath her — the one my father's true chart had marked, the one the charges had been drilled to open — swallowed her bow first. Oak planking that had survived storms, pirates, and my father's stubbornness folded inward with a groan I felt in my teeth.

The silver went with her. Every bar. Every coin. Down into the dark where no ledger could reach it, where no magnate could put a price on it, where the only law was depth and the only owner was time.

The lights went out one by one as the trench closed over her — *pop, pop, pop* — like someone snuffing candles at the end of a vigil.

And then the lagoon was just a lagoon again. Flat. Black. Empty.

Nobody spoke. Tomás took his cap off, which surprised me; I hadn't known he owned a gesture that solemn. Odalys leaned on the rail with her good arm and let out a breath that seemed to carry twenty years with it.

"Well," said Tomás, into the silence. "There goes my retirement plan."

"There goes *everyone's* retirement plan," Odalys said.

"I had a very specific plan involving a hammock."

"You'll have to settle for not being shot."

"Honestly," Tomás considered, "at this stage of the voyage, that's nearly the same thing."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — it came up out of me rough and cracked, half sob, half something lighter, and Marisol put her hand flat against my chest as if to check that the laugh hadn't broken something. Maybe it had. Maybe something needed breaking.

"It's done," she said softly.

"It's done."

Somewhere beyond the mangroves, the Venganza's engines went suddenly, terribly silent.

* * *

"He stopped," Marisol said, thirty seconds later, headphones clamped tight, her face gone pale in the gray light. "Astrid, he's stopped his engines."

Of course he had. Because Corvo had heard the open-channel broadcast, and Corvo had hurried, and a hurried man rounding the point at speed had just heard — or felt, or guessed — the deep whump of his fortune folding itself into a trench. A hurried man makes mistakes. A ruined man makes worse ones.

"He knows," Odalys said. She straightened despite the shoulder, and I saw something settle over her face that I recognized from the night she'd run the rapids: the cold bright calm of a woman who has stopped calculating odds and started settling accounts. "He knows exactly what happened. And there's only one person on this water he can blame."

"We should run," Tomás said. "Current's turning, we've got the shallows, we could—"

"In a sloop with a wounded captain and no engineer?" Odalys shook her head. "He'd run us down inside the hour. No. We stand. We stand, and we make him come to us, and we make it cost."

Through the gap in the mangroves, the Venganza appeared.

She came slow, which was somehow worse than fast — gliding out of the green shadow with her engines idling, her deck crowded with men who had just watched their pay sink into a trench and whose faces, even at distance, had the particular blankness of people waiting to be pointed at something. At her rail, alone, unmoving, stood Corvo.

Even across the water I could feel him looking at me. I don't mean that romantically. I mean it the way you feel weather change.

He didn't shout. I think I'd expected shouting — rage, threats, the theater of a man undone. Instead he simply raised one hand, and the Venganza's launch dropped into the water, and eight men climbed down into it with rifles, and Corvo climbed down last.

"He's coming aboard," Marisol said unnecessarily. Her hand had found mine without either of us arranging it. "Astrid. He's coming *here.*"

"I noticed."

"What do we do?"

I looked at the detonator in my hand, empty now, useless, a hymnal with no hymns in it. I looked at Odalys, who gave me a single nod that meant *your call, but I've already made mine.* I looked at Tomás, who was quietly moving crates around the deck with the air of a man building barricades out of optimism.

What we did not do was run. There was nowhere the launch couldn't follow, and besides — I was done running from this man. I'd been running from him since a collector found me in a pawnshop with forty pesos and a compass I wouldn't sell.

"Put the rifles down," I said. "All of them. We're not giving him a fight. Not today."

Odalys stared at me. "*Not*—"

"He wants a fight. A fight gives him something. A fight lets him take something back, even if it's just blood." I set the dead detonator down on the rail, deliberately, where he'd see it. "He gets nothing. He gets a deck full of people who aren't afraid of him anymore. That's the worst thing I can give him."

Marisol squeezed my hand once and let go. "For the record," she said, "this is either the bravest or stupidest thing you've ever done."

"Both. Usually both."

The launch came alongside with a bump of rubber against hull, and Corvo came up the Perla's ladder like a man ascending a pulpit.

Up close, ruin had not softened him. It had distilled him. His face was composed, almost gentle, the way ice is gentle right up until it takes your fingers. His eyes went past me — to the empty lagoon, to the dead detonator on the rail, to the water where his silver slept beyond anyone's reach — and something flickered behind them that was not grief, because grief requires having loved something.

"Villalobos," he said. My father's name, given to me like a sentence being passed. "Do you have any idea what you've cost me?"

I thought about it honestly.

"A fortune," I said. "But you were never going to keep it anyway. Men like you rent things, Corvo. You never own them. My father understood that about you before I did. It's the only inheritance he left me, and I find—" I looked at Marisol, at Odalys, at Tomás frozen mid-barricade, at the wide bright water where a galleon used to be. "I find it's enough."

Something in Corvo's face went very still, the way water goes still over something rising.

"Enough," he repeated softly. "Well. Let's see how much of it you can keep."

And he stepped fully onto the Perla's deck, and his eight men came up the ladder behind him, and the sun cleared the mangroves on the bloodiest morning the lagoon had ever seen.
