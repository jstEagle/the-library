## 19. Compass Point

### Astrid

The deck of the Perla tilted like a thing deciding whether to die.

Astrid felt it in his boots first — the slow lean as the Venganza's boarding hook bit into the rail and hauled, steel groaning against wood, the two ships grinding together like animals locked at the neck. Then Corvo's men came over the side, four of them, wet and grinning, and the tilt became a slope and the slope became a battlefield.

"Marisol!" he shouted. "The radio — go!"

She was already moving, scrambling aft with the transmitter case clutched to her chest like a child, Odalys covering her retreat with a boathook swung in wide, vicious arcs. Astrid put himself between the boarders and the stern. He had never been a fighting man. His hands knew fretboards and chart tables, not this. But his hands also knew the weight of brass, and there was a compass in his coat pocket that seemed to burn through the wool like a coal.

The first man came at him with a machete held low. Astrid caught the wrist, turned it, and was surprised — genuinely surprised — when the man went down. Grief does strange things to balance. The second man was less polite about it.

They went down together onto the tilting deck, rolling across coils of rope, and somewhere in the tangle of elbows and breath the compass came loose from his pocket. He heard it hit the planks — that particular sound, brass on wood, a sound he'd known since he was nine years old and his father had first pressed it into his palm and said, *It doesn't point north, mijo. It points at what matters.*

The compass skittered.

The deck was canted fifteen degrees now, maybe twenty, and the compass ran along the grain of the boards like a coin down a gutter, spinning, flashing gold in the dawn light, and both of them — Astrid and the man strangling him — stopped at the same instant to watch it go. It was absurd. Two men trying to kill each other, frozen by a three-inch disc of brass sliding toward the scuppers.

"No," Astrid rasped, and lunged.

He caught it at the rail's edge, one finger hooked through the ring, the whole weight of him hanging out over black water. Below, the lagoon churned where the San Telmo had folded into the trench an hour ago, the water still breathing out great sighs of silt and bubbles, the grave of forty million pesos settling into the dark. Corvo stood on his own quarterdeck watching it, and Astrid would remember until he died the look on the man's face — not rage, not yet. Something worse. Arithmetic. A man watching numbers die.

The boarder grabbed his ankle.

Astrid kicked free, rolled back onto the deck, compass fisted against his heart, and found himself face to face with the man again — close enough to count the salt crust in his beard, close enough to see that he was young, younger than Astrid, a hired hand with a machete and a mortgage somewhere, probably, a man whose name would fit on one line of somebody's ledger.

"You don't want this," Astrid told him, and meant all of it: the fight, the wreck, the whole rotten arithmetic.

The man looked past Astrid's shoulder — at the sinking place, at the empty water where a fortune used to be — and something in him deflated like a sail losing wind. He backed away. He stepped over the rail and dropped into the Venganza's tender without another word, and Astrid understood that the battle was already over everywhere except the places that mattered most.

### Marisol

In the stern, wedged behind the wheel housing with the transmitter case open on her knees, Marisol did the only thing she had ever truly been good at. She talked.

"—any station, any station, this is salvage vessel Perla, we are under attack, position the mouth of the Serrano channel, I say again—" Her voice cracked on the third repetition and she hated herself for it and kept going. "—piracy in progress, armed boarding, requesting immediate—"

Static. The eternal static, the delta's static, the static she had lived inside for three years the way other people lived inside weather. She thought of her father's last transmissions, the ones she'd played for Astrid in the storm-bound night — *Corvo knows* — and how a voice on a radio is a promise you make to someone you can't see, and how she had broken that promise once already, to a dead man, and would not break it twice.

Behind her, the deck shook. Someone screamed — one of theirs or one of Corvo's, she couldn't tell, didn't let herself tell. Odalys was holding the midships line alone now, the boathook gone, fighting with a wrench she'd pulled off the engine housing, and Marisol could hear the captain laughing, actually laughing, that wild river laugh, saying something to the boarders like *"You picked the wrong boat, boys, this one's already sunk twice."*

"Any station," Marisol said again, quieter, steadier. "This is Marisol Ferrer. If anyone is listening — I know someone is listening, someone is always listening — please."

And then, cutting through the static like a blade through silk:

*"Perla, Perla, this is customs cutter Almirante Diaz. We have your position. Twenty minutes. Hold on."*

She closed her eyes. Twenty minutes. In twenty minutes anything could happen; in twenty minutes everyone she loved could be at the bottom of the lagoon keeping the treasure company. But a voice had answered. After six tides of silence, after her father's voice dying mid-word on this same band, a voice had answered.

"Hold on," she repeated into the mic, though it wasn't an instruction anymore. It was a prayer she was telling to herself.

Then Corvo crossed the gap between the ships, and everything got simpler and worse.

* * *

### Astrid

He saw it happen almost in slow motion: Corvo stepping off his own rail onto the Perla's canted deck, alone, his men falling back, because men like Corvo do their own killing when the money dies. He was older than Astrid had imagined from the stories — silver at the temples, a salvage magnate's softness gone hard around the jaw — but he moved like a man who had spent thirty years taking things from the sea.

"The letter," Corvo said. Not a greeting. Not even anger yet. "Your father carried a letter of credit under my seal. Where is it?"

"Your seal burned with the Venganza's dignity an hour ago," Astrid said. "Along with your treasure."

"It was never his to find." Corvo's voice stayed level, conversational, which was the most frightening thing about it. "I built his debt the way you build a cofferdam — stone by stone. Every loan, every favor, every port fee he couldn't pay. I made him desperate enough to search and honest enough to succeed. And then he—" For the first time, the level voice bent. "He sank my decoy instead of leading me to it. Do you understand what that cost me?"

"I understand what it cost him." Astrid's hand found the wrench Odalys had dropped. "He died protecting a lie so that people like you would chase shadows. It's the only inheritance he left me, and I intend to honor it."

Corvo came at him.

It was not a duel. Duels are for people with honor and room to swing. It was two men grappling on a sloping deck slick with spray, wrench against forearm, knee against ribs, breath like bellows. Corvo was stronger. Astrid had known he would be, and planned for it the way his father had taught him to plan a river passage: don't fight the current, use it. When Corvo drove him backward, Astrid gave ground gladly, let the slope carry them both, and felt the older man's footing begin to fail on the wet planks.

They broke apart. Circled. The compass, God help him, was still in his fist inside the wrench hand, pressing its brass circle into his palm like a second heartbeat.

"My divers are in the trench right now," Corvo said, circling. "There's gold down there in reach of a grapnel. You've won nothing."

"They're diving into a collapse zone with a lit fuse's worth of unstable wreckage above them," Astrid said. "You sent men into a grave you dug. That's not salvage, Corvo. That's just digging."

Corvo lunged again, and this time Astrid was ready — sidestepped, hooked a leg, and the magnate went down hard on the tilted deck, sliding, catching the base of the mast. And the compass flew free.

Astrid watched it leave his hand the way you watch anything inevitable: with a terrible calm. It arced, caught the dawn, and landed on the planks between the two of them — between Astrid and Corvo, equidistant, needle spinning, pointing at nothing and everything.

Both men moved.

Corvo got there first. Of course he did; he'd spent his life getting there first. He snatched the compass up and held it at arm's length, and for one strange moment the whole battle paused around them — Odalys freezing mid-swing, the boarders stilling, even the sea seeming to hold its breath — while Emilio Corvo, the richest man on the river, examined a dead man's brass compass.

"It doesn't point north," Astrid said, chest heaving. "My father told me that when I was nine. It points at what matters."

"And what does it point at now?" Corvo sneered, turning it in his hand.

"That's the thing," Astrid said. "I think it's been pointing at you."

Corvo looked down at the needle. Whatever he saw there — whether it was north, or the trench, or his own reflection in the glass — held him for exactly one second too long. That second was the sound of engines.

* * *

### Marisol

The Almirante Diaz came around the headland gray and enormous and utterly indifferent, the way justice always arrives: late, bureaucratic, and beautiful anyway. Its wake slapped against both hulls. A loudspeaker crackled words about piracy, jurisdiction, and boarding parties that made Corvo's remaining men evaporate over the rails like morning mist.

Marisol stood at the transmitter, mic still in hand, and laughed — a shaky, disbelieving, entirely undignified laugh — because twenty minutes had become four, because someone had been listening all along, because her father's radio band, the band where the last good thing he ever said had died unfinished, had finally carried something home.

Odalys didn't wait for the cutter's launch.

Marisol saw it happen and understood it completely and would defend it forever: the captain crossing the tilting deck in five strides, wrench still in her fist, backing Emilio Corvo against the mast with three years of grief standing in her shoulders. Her brother had gone down on Corvo's last expedition with a lifeline cut to save cargo. Everyone on the river knew the story. Nobody had ever seen Odalys Reyes tell it.

"You're going to say his name," Odalys said quietly. "Rafael Reyes. You're going to say it, and then you're going to come quietly, or I am going to introduce you to the same water you fed him to."

Corvo, cornered, bleeding from the lip, looked past her — at the cutter, at the launch already pulling alongside, at the arithmetic of his ruin completing itself column by column. And then, incredibly, he smiled at Astrid.

"You could still kill me," he said. "No witnesses will object. The law is whatever you can enforce — isn't that what they teach out here? One wrench, boy. Your captain would thank you. Your father would thank me for the compliment."

Astrid looked at the wrench in Odalys's raised hand. He looked at the compass in Corvo's grip. He thought about his father's body in the flooded hold, arms still wrapped around the decoy's secret, dead for a lie that had nearly worked. He thought about how easy it would be, and how easy was exactly the currency Corvo had traded in his whole life.

"He'd be disappointed in me," Astrid said, "if I became you."

He stepped forward, took the compass gently from Corvo's unresisting fingers, and stepped back.

When the customs officers came aboard, Odalys lowered the wrench herself — slowly, deliberately, letting them see her choose it — and pointed at Corvo with the blunt end. "That one," she said. "For Rafael Reyes, and for whatever number he was on your ledger."

Corvo straightened his collar as they cuffed him, a man managing his image even in ruin. At the rail he paused, and looked back at Astrid one last time.

"The debt survives," he said. "Debts always survive."

"Not this one," Astrid said. "I paid it. In truth. You were there."

The launch pulled away. The Venganza, leaderless, struck its colors to the cutter with a speed that would have been funny on any other morning. And on the wounded, listing, thoroughly alive deck of the Perla, Marisol crossed to Astrid and put her hand over the one holding the compass, and neither of them said anything, because some transmissions don't need a frequency.

Odalys leaned against the mast, finally letting the wound in her side show, and grinned at them both through the blood and spray.

"Well," she said. "Somebody get this bucket upright. We're not sinking in front of the authorities."

Below them, in sixty feet of black water, the San Telmo slept on in the dark with her treasure intact and unreachable, keeping her secret the way the best graves do — and Astrid felt the compass needle tremble once against its glass, as if the old instrument, too, was finally pointing at rest.
