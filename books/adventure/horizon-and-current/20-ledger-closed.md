## 20. Ledger Closed

The customs house in Puerto Vigía smelled of mildew, ink, and forty years of men pretending they had power over the sea.

Astrid sat on a hard wooden bench outside the hearing room, his father's letter folded in his breast pocket, and tried to remember when he'd last been this nervous. Not in the lagoon, with Corvo's divers cutting through black water toward him. Not on the Perla's deck with a detonator in his hand and six tides of other people's lives balanced against his thumb. Those moments had been simple. You acted or you drowned. Here, in a room with a ceiling fan and a stenographer and eleven creditors who between them owned every centavo his father had ever borrowed, the danger was subtler: it was possible to tell the truth and still lose everything.

"You're doing the thing where you chew your lip like it owes you money," said Odalys beside him. She'd come despite the bandage on her shoulder, sitting straight-backed in her one good coat, arms crossed like she was personally guarding the door. "Stop it. You look guilty."

"I am guilty."

"Of what? Sinking a treasure galleon? That's called *initiative*."

"Of wanting it," Astrid said quietly. "For about four hours, I wanted that gold so badly I could taste it. If the Venganza had arrived ten minutes later — if Marisol hadn't been standing next to me —"

"But she was," Odalys said. "And it didn't. The ledger doesn't charge you for the man you almost were. Believe me. I've audited myself enough times to know."

The doors opened. A clerk with a face like a wet envelope beckoned them in.

* * *

The hearing room held a long table behind which sat the creditors' counsel — a silver-haired man named Ferrán whose cufflinks probably cost more than the Perla — and before it, a magistrate named Doña Beatriz Salas, who had the reputation of being the only honest official in three hundred miles of coastline, which meant everyone else hated her and no one dared say so.

Corvo's creditors were an odd congregation. There was the harbormaster's brother-in-law, representing the bribed harbormaster's interests from a safe legal distance. There was a bank man from the capital with soft hands and a softer smile. There were two salvage brokers, a chandler, and a woman from an insurance syndicate who kept looking at Astrid as though he were a weather event she hadn't planned for. Eleven names on the docket. Eleven people who had lent Emilio Villalobos money because Corvo had told them it was good business, backed by the promise of a wreck he'd already found on paper and only needed a fool to dig out of the water.

Ferrán rose first. "Magistrate. The estate of Emilio Villalobos owes this consortium a sum exceeding two hundred thousand pesos, secured against the cargo of the galleon San Telmo. The debtor's son stands in succession to those obligations. We ask simply that the court recognize the security interest and permit salvage recovery to proceed under consortium supervision."

"And the cargo?" Doña Beatriz asked. Her voice was dry as old rope. "Where is it?"

Astrid stood. His legs felt like someone else's legs, borrowed and badly fitted.

"It's at the bottom of a trench," he said, "under sixty feet of collapsed rock, in a lagoon two days upriver, and it is going to stay there."

The room made a sound like a held breath being dropped.

"Explain yourself," said the bank man.

So Astrid did. He told all of it, in order, because he'd learned somewhere in the mangroves that the truth works best unedited — that editing is how lies get in. He told them about the compass and the map fragment. About the debt his father had signed, and how the terms had been engineered — he watched Ferrán's jaw tighten at that word — by a man named Tomás Corvo, who had manufactured the pressure that drove Emilio Villalobos upriver in the first place. He told them about the decoy wreck, the sealed tin, the apology never sent. He told them about the drowned village, the hostages, the engineer who stayed behind, Odalys's shoulder, the firefight at dawn. He told them about the hold of the San Telmo, and what he'd found there besides gold.

He did not tell them everything about his father's body. Some things belong to the water.

"When I found the treasure," he said, "I could have paid every peso in this room. I want to be honest about that. I stood there and I calculated it. It would have covered my father's debts and bought me a life I've never been able to afford." He looked down the table at their faces — greedy, frightened, calculating, human. "But the gold wasn't mine. It wasn't even really my father's. It was bait. Corvo built a trap out of my family's name, and every peso of that cargo was going to pass through his hands on its way to yours, minus whatever he decided to keep, which history suggests would have been most of it. So I fired the charges. And I'd fire them again tomorrow."

Silence. The ceiling fan creaked through it like a slow oar.

Then the insurance woman spoke. "You destroyed collateral worth —"

"I destroyed a lie," Astrid said. "Collateral implies somebody actually intended to pay you. Ask yourselves why Corvo isn't sitting here today making this argument himself."

"He is," said Doña Beatriz, "in a sense. He's sitting in a cell two floors down, awaiting extradition proceedings, thanks to testimony this young man and his crew provided, along with certain radio transmissions obtained by the customs cutter *Justicia*. Which raises a question, Señor Ferrán." She turned her dry gaze on the silver-haired counsel. "Your client's principal is under arrest for conspiracy, bribery, extortion, and the deaths of at least four men including the elder Villalobos. Your consortium's claims derive entirely from instruments he arranged. Would you care to explain to this court why these debts should survive him?"

Ferrán opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "The instruments themselves are lawful —"

"The instruments," Doña Beatriz said, "were designed to force a man to risk his life recovering assets for a criminal enterprise. In this jurisdiction we have a term for contracts procured by extortion." She let the pause stretch until even the fan seemed to lean in. "We call them void."

* * *

It took three more hours to untangle, because law is a tide and tides never recede cleanly. But by late afternoon the shape of it was fixed: the Villalobos debts, secured against a cargo that no longer existed and procured under coercion, were struck down. What remained of his father's estate amounted to a rented room full of charts, a broken sextant Astrid had pawned and then bought back with the last of Odalys's advance, and a compass.

The chandler grumbled. The bank man wrote furiously. The insurance woman stared at Astrid a long moment and then, unexpectedly, nodded — once, small, like a salute. Perhaps she'd read Corvo's ledgers too. Perhaps everyone in that room had known, on some level, what they were lending into, and had chosen not to look, and there was a kind of relief in being forced to stop.

Outside, the harbor light was going amber. Odalys was waiting on the steps, and beside her, leaning against the railing with her radio bag slung over one shoulder, was Marisol.

"Well?" Odalys asked.

"They're gone," Astrid said. He heard his own voice come out strange, lighter than he'd ever heard it. "All of it. Twenty-two years of my father's debt, gone in an afternoon. I'm the poorest free man on this coast."

Marisol smiled. "Congratulations. I know several rich men in cells right now who'd trade places."

He laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in front of the customs house, and realized it might be the first time since the news of his father's silence broke. Grief doesn't leave all at once. It leaves the way fog leaves a harbor: in patches, revealing things you'd forgotten were there.

"Doña Beatriz wants us back Thursday," he said. "There are formalities. Statements. Apparently I'm a material witness in the largest corruption case in the province's history, which means I get to spend a week saying 'and then I sank the treasure' to increasingly important people."

"Poor you," said Odalys. "Come on. The Perla's at anchor and Tomás has been cooking since noon. He says celebration requires at least four dishes and he intends to prove it."

They walked down toward the water together, and Astrid put his hand in his pocket and closed it around the compass. The brass was warm from his body. Somewhere inside it, the needle swung gently, pointing at nothing now — or at everything, depending on how you read it. It had pulled him upriver toward a wreck and a grave, and now it pointed wherever he happened to be facing, patient as a dog that has finally stopped straining at its leash.

At the dock, he stopped. "Give me a minute," he said.

He walked out onto the empty end of the pier, past the coiled rope and the sleeping skiffs, and took out the letter — his father's letter, the apology never sent, salt-stained and softened at the folds from a month of being read. He knew it by heart now. *If you're reading this, I ran out of time to say any of it properly. I was never good at coming home. I thought if I brought back enough gold, it would count as words.*

He read it once more anyway, the way you visit a grave not to learn anything but just to sit a while.

Then he folded it, put it away, and held the compass out over the water.

He didn't drop it. He'd thought he might — he'd imagined this moment on the river, a clean severance, inheritance given back to the sea. But standing here, with the gulls arguing overhead and the smell of Tomás's cooking drifting off the Perla, he understood that wasn't the ending either. The compass wasn't the debt. The debt was paper, and paper burned or drowned or got struck down by a dry-voiced magistrate, and good riddance. The compass was something else. It was the only object in the world his father had touched every day and thought of him every time — he knew that now, from the letter, from the way the needle had been tuned to the delta, to the route home.

Some inheritances aren't meant to be given back. They're meant to be carried differently.

Astrid pocketed the compass and walked back down the pier toward the light in the Perla's cabin windows, toward the voices of the only family he had left, and found himself already wondering — in the way you wonder about a thing you've already decided — how much chart work it would take to refit a salvage sloop for a proper season, and whether a certain radio operator might be persuaded to stay aboard for more than one voyage.

He climbed aboard. Through the cabin window he could see Marisol laughing at something Odalys had said, and Tomás waving him in impatiently, and the whole small lit world of the deck waiting for him to step into it.

The tide was turning. For once, he was exactly where it wanted him.
