## 21. Anchorage

The Perla came home on a Tuesday, which Marisol thought was fitting. Wrecks and miracles both, in her experience, preferred to arrive without ceremony, and Tuesdays were the least ceremonial day the week had to offer.

She stood at the rail as Odalys brought her through the harbor mouth, watching the customs cutter's lights shrink behind them. Somewhere back there, Corvo sat in a hold that smelled of bilge and bureaucracy, his empire of ledgers dissolving into depositions. Somewhere ahead, the yard at Pier Nine waited with its rusted cradle and its owner who charged by the insult as well as the hour.

And beside her, leaning on the rail with salt still crusted in his hair, Astrid Villalobos watched the water the way he'd watched it for six days now — like a man reading a letter he already knew by heart.

"You're going to wear a groove in that rail," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"That's what worries me. You think like you dive. You go down too fast and forget to come up."

He smiled — the small, crooked one that had started appearing somewhere around the fourth tide, after the mutiny question, after she'd held the crew together with coffee and lies about how much fuel they had left. "What should I do instead?"

"Come up," she said. "Breathe. The treasure's at the bottom of a trench. Corvo's in a cell. Your debt is a story we tell over drinks. There is nothing left to solve today."

"There's the boat."

"The boat is solved. The boat is *solved*, Astrid. She has a hole in her hull the size of a church door and a mast held together with prayer and wire, but she is *solved* in the sense that we know exactly what's wrong with her, which is more than either of us can say for ourselves."

That earned a real laugh, short and surprised out of him, and Marisol felt it land somewhere under her ribs where she kept the things she wasn't ready to say yet. She was getting a collection of those. It was becoming a problem.

* * *

Pier Nine belonged to a man named Ferro, who was seventy if he was a day and greeted them by looking at the Perla, then at Odalys, then back at the Perla, and saying: "Who did this?"

"Corvo's divers," Odalys said.

"Corvo." Ferro said the name like he was tasting something spoiled. "The one on the radio all week. The one they arrested."

"The same."

"Good." He spat off the pier with tremendous precision. "Then I'll only charge you half the insult rate. Full rate if I have to look at that transom again."

So began three weeks of the strangest peace Marisol had ever known. By day they worked the boat — Astrid below in the bilges, scraping and grinding and swearing with an inventiveness that made even Ferro pause in respect; Marisol aloft or amidships, re-wiring the radio, replacing the rigging, learning the Perla's moods the way she'd once learned her father's guitar, note by stubborn note. Odalys appeared each morning at seven sharp with bread and orders, disappeared to testify or negotiate or simply sit in the courthouse hallway glaring at lawyers until they confessed things, and returned each evening smelling of ink and vindication.

The crew drifted home one by one, the way crews do. Tomás, who'd argued loudest for turning back at the fifth tide, came back first, sheepish, carrying a crate of oranges as apology. He found Astrid elbow-deep in the engine room and said, "I owe you an argument," and Astrid said, "You were right to make it," and that was somehow the whole conversation, and Tomás stayed. The engineer who'd guarded the villagers sent word he'd taken work on the coastal run but would come paint the hull if they fed him. Even the old ferryman from the drowned village turned up once, delivered a sack of smoked fish, refused payment, looked at the patched hole in the hull, and pronounced it "adequate," which Marisol suspected was the highest praise the man had issued in forty years.

It was, she realized one evening, standing in the galley while rain ticked against the deck above, the first time in her life she'd been part of something that stayed.

* * *

The trouble with warmth, Marisol had learned, was that it made honest things easy to say at exactly the wrong moments.

They were sanding the cabin sole together, side by side in the narrow space, shoulders touching because the space demanded it and neither of them moving away because the space didn't *make* them. Astrid had gone quiet in the way he did when a thought had been working him for days.

"Marisol."

"Mm."

"When my father's transmissions stopped — when you played me that tape in the wheelhouse — you said you loved him like family."

"I did."

"And you failed him. That's what you said. You carried it like ballast the whole way downriver."

Her hands stilled on the sandpaper. Above them, rain drummed. "Yes."

"I've been thinking about failure," he said. "About inheritance. My father left me a compass and a debt, and I spent six tides deciding which one was the real bequest. And I think—" he exhaled, turned the sandpaper over, started again. "I think he left me a third thing. The example of what happens when you carry everything alone. He went up that river with secrets instead of people. He died alone in a hold full of gold he never intended to spend, protecting a lie he never intended to explain. And I nearly did the same thing. At the detonator. With you standing right there."

"Astrid—"

"Let me finish, because if I stop I'll turn it into a joke and it isn't one." His voice was steady but his hands weren't; she could see the fine tremor of them. "I'm not going to sail alone. Not ever again. Not because I can't — I proved I can, badly, expensively — but because alone is how men end up in holds next to fortunes nobody should want. So." He finally looked at her, and there it was, the thing under the thing, laid out on the sanded wood between them like cargo declared at last. "This isn't me asking you to stay aboard. This is me asking whether you'll keep sailing with me specifically. Whether — whether there's an us, past the voyage. Because I've been pretending since the storm night that I wasn't asking, and I'm tired, and you deserve better than a man who asks by not-asking."

Marisol set down the sandpaper very carefully, because her own hands had developed opinions.

"You know what I did during the storm," she said. "While you were decoding your father's chart. I wasn't decoding. I was memorizing you. The way you chewed the pencil. The way you said *hold on* to the chart like it was a person. And I told myself it was grief, transference, some clinical thing, because I'd already lost one Villalobos to a river and I couldn't afford—" Her voice caught. She let it, this time. She was done carrying things quietly. "I couldn't afford to lose another one to silence. So yes, Astrid. Yes. There's an us. There's been an us since the wheelhouse, probably since the pawnshop rumor reached me and I volunteered for this crew knowing whose son you were. I just needed you to ask out loud, because I asked out loud every day for three weeks in every way except words, and you are *beautifully* unobservant for a man who reads currents."

He laughed then, wet-eyed, and pulled her in among the sawdust and the smell of fresh wood, and the Perla rocked gently beneath them like she approved.

* * *

Odalys found them an hour later, still in the cabin, sawdust in their hair, and assessed the situation with the speed of a woman who ran salvage crews.

"So the two of you finally sorted your cargo," she said.

"Something like that," Astrid said, ears going red.

"Good. Saves me a conversation I was dreading." She dropped onto the bench across from them, produced three cups and a bottle of something amber and unlabeled, and poured with the gravity of a ceremony. "Because I have an offer, and I wanted to make it before you two got sentimental enough to say no out of politeness."

"An offer," Marisol said carefully.

"The Perla needs a permanent crew. Not hired hands — I've had hired hands, and hired hands jump ship the moment a job smells like law. I need people with reasons." She raised her cup. "Villalobos. You read water better than any diver I've seen, and you've got a grudge against sunken things staying lost. Marisol — you can pull voices out of static like nobody I've heard, and you lied to a warlord with a radio and made it work. That's not a skill, that's a vocation. Permanent berths. Shares, not wages. Equal thirds, minus Ferro's cut, minus Ferro's insults."

Astrid looked at Marisol. Marisol looked at Astrid. Something passed between them that needed no radio at all.

"What kind of jobs?" he asked.

"Honest ones, mostly. Wrecks that want finding. Cargo that wants delivering. Occasionally," Odalys's eyes glinted, "the kind of salvage where what's being recovered is justice, and the paperwork gets written afterward. My brother's ledger is closed, but there are others open. Corvo wasn't the only one who does business outside the law. He was just the loudest."

"And the danger?" Marisol asked.

"Same as always. Six tides ahead of whoever wants it more." Odalys drank. "But here's the difference. Nobody dies alone on my boat. Not anymore. That's the whole offer. That's the entire contract, written and sealed. We go out together, we come back together, and whatever we find, we split it standing in daylight."

Tomás put his head through the cabin door at exactly the wrong moment, saw the bottle, and invited himself in with the shamelessness of family. Within twenty minutes the whole crew had crowded below — the engineer arrived early with his paint cans, the ferryman's fish got opened, someone found a battered guitar that had been living under the pilot berth since before the voyage — and the little cabin filled with smoke and song and the particular noise of people arguing happily about nothing.

Marisol sat pressed against Astrid's shoulder, guitar going around the circle, and watched Odalys laugh at something Tomás said — really laugh, unguarded, the first time Marisol had seen it — and thought: *this*. Not the trench, not the gold, not even the lagoon at dawn. This smoky, cramped, orange-scented room full of strangers who had become the opposite of strangers.

Found crew. Found family. Same thing, on the water.

* * *

Late that night, when the others had gone ashore or folded themselves into bunks, Marisol climbed to the wheelhouse out of habit and found Astrid already there, at the radio, fiddling with the dial.

"Expecting someone?" she asked.

"No." But he didn't take his hand off the set. "Habit. My father used to do this — sit at the radio at night, just listening to the band. I used to think it was waiting. Now I think it was just... wanting to hear that the world was still talking."

She slid in beside him, and together they swept the frequencies, catching fragments: a freighter reporting weather, a fishing fleet joking in code, the customs station droning its closing announcements. Ordinary voices. The world, still talking.

Then, low on the band, a signal they both recognized — the cadence of a distress pattern, faint, half-swallowed by distance and storm-static somewhere far down the coast. Not theirs. Not their waters. Someone else's night, someone else's six tides beginning.

Astrid's hand hovered over the key. He glanced at Marisol, and she saw it in his face — not the old hunger, not the debt-driven scramble, but something cleaner. A man hearing the world ask.

"Odalys said daylight decisions," Marisol murmured, smiling. "But she also said nobody dies alone."

Astrid reached for the logbook, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote the bearing in a steady hand.
