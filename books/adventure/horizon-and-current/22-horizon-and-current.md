## 22. Horizon and Current

The trench had been waiting for me a year, and it hadn't gotten any friendlier.

I hung at forty feet in water the color of strong tea, watching my bubbles climb toward a sun I could only see as a smeared gold coin overhead. Below me the bottom fell away into the trench where the San Telmo had slept for three centuries and now slept no longer. The charges had folded her hull down into that darkness like a hand closing over a coin, and the sea had done the rest — silt, sand, the patient housekeeping of tides. There was nothing down there now. That was rather the point.

Still, I checked. You spend enough of your life chasing something and you develop a superstition about it, the way Odalys still knocks twice on the Perla's mast before every run even though she'd deny it under oath.

Nothing. Just the trench, breathing its cold breath upward, and somewhere in it — or maybe not even there anymore, maybe scattered by currents into a thousand hiding places — the last of the treasure. Silver pieces of eight going quietly feral in the mud. Emeralds wearing coats of algae. Enough wealth to buy a fleet, or free a man from every debt he'd ever inherited, and all of it exactly where Corvo couldn't reach it, which was to say everywhere and nowhere.

I'd thought about this dive for most of the year. Rehearsed it, if I'm honest, the way you rehearse a difficult conversation. I had the compass in the pouch at my belt, my father's compass, brass worn smooth by a century of thumbs that weren't mine until they were. The plan was simple: go down, set it on the sand above the trench, let him have it back. He'd found the wreck with it. It seemed only right that it should keep the find company.

Simple plans have a way of getting complicated around me. Ask anyone.

* * *

A year is a strange unit of measurement. Long enough for the scar on Odalys's shoulder to fade from angry red to a pale seam she calls her "souvenir from the management." Long enough for the courts to finish with Corvo — four years, actually, once the appeals ran out, but the first year was when the verdict landed, and I remember Marisol crying in the courthouse corridor, which surprised both of us, because neither of us had thought we had any tears left for anything involving that man.

Long enough for the Perla to get a new engine, a new name painted carefully over the old one — Odalys kept *Perla* but added a second word, so now she's the *Perla Libre*, and Odalys says it with the particular satisfaction of a woman who has watched an empire choke on its own ledger.

And long enough for me to learn things I should have learned at fifteen, like how to sleep through a storm, and how to forgive a dead man, and how to be loved without checking the exits.

That last one took the longest.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about coming home from a thing like the lagoon: the world doesn't rearrange itself around your revelation. I surfaced that morning with the San Telmo folding into the deep behind me and Corvo's divers shooting at my friends, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *well, that's settled then* — as if the universe kept books as tidy as Corvo's. As if one good act squared everything.

It didn't. The weeks after were some of the hardest of my life, and I say that as a man who once pawned his grandfather's violin to a shopkeeper who chewed with his mouth open. The debt collectors came anyway — turns out debts don't die just because the collateral does; they linger like the smell of bilge. The lawyers came. The historians came, wanting to know about the wreck, and I told them all the same thing: nothing down there but old wood and older bones, and the sea keeps its own records.

Corvo's men came too, for a while. Not many, and not for long. When a magnate falls, his hired sharks remember very quickly that they were never loyal to him, only to the money, and the money had gone to the bottom of a lagoon with a detonator's signature on it.

But mostly what came, in those weeks, was quiet. And quiet, for a man who'd spent a year running on fear and tide-tables, turned out to be its own kind of difficult.

Marisol found me on the dock at three in the morning, maybe two weeks after we'd limped home, sitting where I used to sit as a boy, watching water do nothing.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what."

"Sitting out here doing arithmetic." She sat down next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was her way of making a point without wasting words on it. "You're adding up everything it cost. Tomás staying behind with the villagers. Odalys's shoulder. Your father. And subtracting it from whatever you think you're worth."

"I don't—"

"Astrid." She said my name the way she used to say *over* on the radio, flat and final, a frequency you didn't argue with. "Your father spent his whole life paying a debt that wasn't real. Corvo invented it. And you've spent a year trying to pay it too, like it was inheritance." She looked out at the black water. "The only thing he actually left you was the compass. So maybe ask yourself what that was for."

"It points at treasure," I said.

"No," she said. "It points. That's different."

I want to tell you I understood her right away. I didn't. I understood her somewhere around dawn, somewhere between the third cup of coffee from the dockside stand and the moment I opened my hand and really looked at the compass — not as a key, not as a debt instrument, not as the last piece of a father who'd chosen a wreck over a son, but as what it had always been. A thing that pointed. A thing that said *this way*, and left the walking to you.

He hadn't chosen the wreck over me. He'd chosen the truth over Corvo, and died keeping it, and left me the compass because he knew — hoped — that someday I'd point it at something better than gold.

Some inheritances take a year to unwrap.

* * *

Which brings us, more or less, to today.

The *Perla Libre* rode easy at anchor over the lagoon, her new engine ticking as it cooled, laundry strung along the rail like flags of a very small and stubborn nation. Odalys stood at the wheel out of pure habit — we weren't going anywhere — and Tomás, who'd come back to us in the spring thinner and quieter and glad, was teaching Rafa, the deckhand we'd picked up in port, how to splice a line properly instead of the way Rafa insisted on doing it, which was fast and wrong.

"Tell him, Okafor," Tomás called down as I shrugged into my tank harness. "Tell him a bad splice is how you lose a whole line."

"A bad splice is how you lose a whole line," I agreed.

"See? The novelist agrees. The novelist knows everything except how to end a book."

"The novelist is working on it," I said, and went over the side.

The water closed over me warm as bathwater, and the noise of the boat fell away, and there was just the green-gold light and my own breath, loud as surf inside the regulator.

I'd dived the lagoon a dozen times since that morning. At first it was grim duty — surveying the collapse, confirming the trench had sealed, making sure nothing of the wreck protruded to catch a salvager's eye. Then it became habit. Then, without my noticing the exact day it changed, it became something else. A visit.

Today I went down past the shelf where the light gave up, down to where the sand met the lip of the trench, and I knelt there in the gloom with my fins stirring up little ghosts of sediment, and I took out the compass.

It looked small in my glove. It had always looked small. Brass case, glass clouded at the edges, needle asleep under its latch. My father had carried it through thirty years of expeditions and one final lie, and I had carried it through pawnshops and mutinies and a lagoon full of gunfire, and between us we had never once used it for the thing it was best at, which was simply telling you which way you were facing.

I popped the latch.

The needle woke, swung once, twice, and settled. Not toward the trench. Not toward any treasure. North. Steady, indifferent, honest north, the way it had pointed for a hundred years before any of us were born and would point for a hundred years after the last of us forgot the San Telmo's name.

I laughed underwater, which produces a sound like a strangled duck and a burst of bubbles, and I don't care who heard it.

Of course. Of course it pointed north. It had never been a treasure compass. That was the story we'd all told about it — Corvo, my father, me — because we were the kind of people who needed instruments to mean money. But it was just a compass. It pointed the way you were already going, and forgave you for it, and waited for you to choose a better heading.

"Here," I said, and my voice came out as a rumble and a shimmer of bubbles rising toward a sun I couldn't see. "You kept your heading. I'm keeping mine."

I set it down on the sand at the lip of the trench, upright, latched open, needle steady. The current moved a few grains of sand against its base, already beginning the work of settling it in, and I swear — and I know what currents do, and I know what wishful thinking does, and I'm telling you anyway — I swear the needle trembled once, the way it used to tremble near the wreck, and went still.

Then I left it there, and I did not look back, because looking back is for people who aren't sure.

* * *

I broke the surface into late afternoon, into the smell of diesel and salt and Tomás's rice cooking, and Marisol was leaning over the rail with her arm out before I'd finished clearing my mask.

"Give me your hand, hero."

"I'm not a hero, I'm a man who blew up a boat."

"You blew up a boat *for the right reasons*. In my experience that's the entire definition." She hauled me up with the efficient strength of someone who'd grown up hauling nets, and then, instead of letting go, kept hold of my hand, dripping, and inspected it like a jeweler. "Well?"

"Well what?"

"Did you do it?"

"I did it."

"And?"

"And it pointed north."

She smiled — that slow smile she saves for things that are actually funny, not the polite one she deploys on customs officers. "Good. It always did. Took you long enough."

"Took me exactly six tides and a year," I said. "Which, for me, is punctual."

Odalys leaned out of the wheelhouse with a mug of coffee she had absolutely poured for herself and was now reluctantly sharing. "If the ceremony's over, dinner's in twenty minutes, and Rafa has been informed that if he splices one more line wrong he'll be eating his ration on the dock."

"I heard that!" Rafa called from the foredeck.

"You were meant to!"

I toweled off on the stern bench while the lagoon went gold and then rose-colored, and Marisol sat down beside me with the particular sigh of a woman ending a working day, and for a while neither of us said anything, because we'd gotten good at that. Two years ago silence between us meant secrets. Now it just meant evening.

She had the letter, sometimes, on nights like this. My father's letter, the one from the tin in the decoy wreck, the apology that arrived too late to be an apology and just in time to be a blessing. She'd read it more times than I had. Tonight she took it out of her shirt pocket — she kept it there the way other people keep cigarettes — and read the last lines aloud, though we both knew them by heart.

*"If you're reading this, I ran out of time to say it properly. I chose wrong, again and again, and I won't insult you by explaining why. Only this: the compass doesn't find treasure. It finds direction. Point it at something worth arriving at."*

"He really wrote that?" Rafa asked, drifting over with his rice bowl. "Like, with a pen?"

"With a pen," Marisol said.

"On a *wreck*?"

"In a tin," I said, "on a fake wreck, surrounded by real skeletons, while a madman hunted him. My father had a flair for the dramatic entrance and the catastrophic exit."

"Runs in the family," Odalys said from the wheelhouse, not looking up from her charts.

"Runs in the family," I agreed, and discovered I could say it without the old taste in my mouth. That was new. That was the year's real work, done quietly, the way barnacles grow: you don't notice it happening, and then one day the hull is covered.

* * *

After dinner — Tomás's rice, Rafa's fish, Odalys's coffee, Marisol's lime-and-chili something-or-other that she refused to write down because "then everyone would make it" — we sat on deck while the lagoon filled with stars, the way it does, all at once, as if someone had tipped over a drawer of them.

"So," Odalys said, in the tone she used for announcing course changes. "The *Venganza*'s old route is opening up again. Coastal run, legitimate cargo, decent pay. Customs wants independent boats on it since Corvo's fleet got broken up. Six-month contract."

Tomás grunted. "Six months of honest work. We'll forget how to dodge cutters."

"We'll manage," Odalys said. "I'm asking for votes. Reyes rules are majority decides."

"I vote yes," Tomás said.

"Yes," said Rafa, who voted yes to everything and had once voted yes to whether he should clean the bilge, a decision he regretted for days.

They all looked at Marisol. She looked at me.

"I vote yes," she said, "if my radio operator stays."

"Your radio operator," I said, "is also your cook, your diver, and the man who fixed the head last Tuesday."

"My radio operator," she said, "is the man I'm not sailing without. Ever again. Those were the terms. You agreed to the terms."

"I agreed to the terms," I said, and took her hand, and across the deck Odalys made a great show of studying her charts, and Tomás made a great show of the stars, and Rafa made no show of anything because Rafa grinned at everything, the absolute menace.

Six months of coastal runs. Honest cargo, honest pay, a crew of five on a boat named for a pearl that had never been for sale. Somewhere south, in a prison yard, a man who had once owned half the delta was learning to live inside a schedule, and I found — testing the thought the way you test a splice, expecting it to fail — that I felt nothing about him at all. Not hate. Not triumph. Just the mild, distant pity you feel for anyone whose compass only ever pointed at one thing.

Mine had pointed at worse, once. That was the difference. Not the instrument. The heading.

Later, when the others had turned in and the anchor light burned its small stubborn circle against the dark, Marisol and I stood at the bow, hip to hip, watching the horizon do its slow trick of being farther away than it looks.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The treasure. What it could have bought."

I considered it honestly, which is the only way she accepts answers.

"I miss the idea of being free of debt," I said. "But I was never going to be free of it that way. Gold was just Corvo's currency. The debt was his invention. Truth paid it off — the courts took care of the rest." I shrugged. "What I have instead is a boat, a crew, a woman who reads dead men's letters, and a horizon that finally goes all the way around. I got the better trade."

"You always were a terrible businessman."

"Terrible," I agreed. "Excellent sailor, though."

"Mediocre," she said fondly, and put her head on my shoulder, and we stood there while the tide turned beneath us, gentle as a sleeping animal, carrying the lagoon, and the trench, and the small brass heart of a compass resting open on the sand, pointing north, pointing nowhere, pointing home.

Out past the anchorage, the current ran strong and clean toward open water, and the horizon held its promise, wider than any debt, wider than any grave, wide enough for the likes of us.

I had a heading. I had a crew. Come morning, we'd sail.
