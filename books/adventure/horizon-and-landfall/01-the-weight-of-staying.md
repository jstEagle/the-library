## 1. The Weight of Staying

The river at Thuan Lok ran the color of old tea, and Emrys Iversen had learned to read it the way other men read faces—what it wanted, what it hid, what it would take if you gave it half a reason. He sat on the gunwale of the *Maren Voss*, his boots dangling over water that moved like something patient and enormous breathing in its sleep, and he watched the charter boat come up from the south with its engines working hard against the current.

He knew before it docked what they'd ask him. They always asked him the same thing this time of year, when the dry season thinned the channels and the salvage work went slack as an old rope. *Go north, Iversen. You know the upper river better than anyone alive.* And he would name a price so high it insulted them, or claim his engine was dying, or simply shake his head and watch their disappointment settle over them like spray.

The charter boat was a converted patrol craft, white paint gone chalky, flying no flag worth noticing. It came alongside the mooring pilings with more confidence than skill, and a man in a linen shirt leaned out and called down to him in trade-accented English.

"You Iversen? The salvager?"

"Depends who's asking."

"The name's Corben. We're running survey equipment up to Khet Landing." The man smiled the way men smile when they've already decided the answer is yes. "They said down at the fuel dock you're the only one who'll take the gorge run this late."

"They said right."

"So you'll do it?"

Emrys looked north, upriver, where the water narrowed between walls of green stone and the monsoon line waited like a held breath. Somewhere past the second bend, past the drowned temple at Sarn Ket, past all the geography he had spent ten years pretending was just geography, there were places his mother had put her hands.

"No," he said.

Corben blinked. "I haven't named a figure yet."

"It doesn't matter what you name." Emrys stood, brushing rust from his palms. "I don't go above Sarn Ket. Ask anyone."

"That's superstition," Corben said, not unkindly. "Whole stretch of good water."

"It is," Emrys agreed. "Good water. Bad country for me."

He walked away before the man could argue, because argument was how these things started—a price offered, a pride stung, a compromise made—and compromises above Sarn Ket had a way of becoming expeditions, and expeditions had a way of becoming years.

* * *

The town of Thuan Lok existed because the river slowed here, and everything the river carried—silt, timber, rumors, wreckage—tended to set itself down. The salvage yard occupied the last flat ground before the banks steepened: a fenced acre of mud and chain hoists, dead engines stacked like vertebrae, three hulls in various states of resurrection under tarps gone gray as fish skin. Emrys had been here four months, which was two months longer than his usual stay, and the owner, a widow named Mrs. Anh with forearms like dock rope, had stopped asking when he planned to leave. She just raised his rent each month by exactly enough to make staying feel like a decision rather than a habit.

That evening she found him in the yard, working a wire brush over a propeller shaft that didn't need it.

"There's a woman looking for you," she said.

"I'm not hiring."

"She's not offering work." Mrs. Anh folded her arms. She had a way of delivering news the way she delivered invoices—flatly, with the expectation of payment. "She came off the afternoon packet boat. Small woman, city clothes wrong for the weather, shoes ruined already. She asked at the fuel dock, then the guesthouse, then here. She says she knows your mother."

The wire brush stopped moving.

The yard around him filled suddenly with small sounds—the tick of cooling metal, a heron arguing somewhere along the reed line, the far diesel mutter of the packet boat turning back downstream. Ten years of practiced stillness, and one sentence could knock it over like a kid kicking a stack of cans.

"What did you tell her?"

"That I'd check whether you were sober enough for visitors." Mrs. Anh shrugged. "You are. Probably. She's sitting at the noodle stall by the customs shed. She ordered two bowls and ate neither."

Emrys set down the brush. His hands, he noticed distantly, had gone cold in the heat.

"Tell her the yard's closed."

"I already told her you'd come." Mrs. Anh turned back toward her house, then paused. "For whatever it's worth, boy—I've watched you sit in this mud for four months like a man waiting for a flood that already happened. Maybe it's time somebody found you."

* * *

He did not go immediately. That was the discipline of it, the architecture of avoidance—he made himself finish the propeller shaft, made himself coil the hoses, made himself perform the whole liturgy of ordinary work while the light went amber and then bruised purple over the water. Only when the yard lamps buzzed on and there was nothing left to pretend to do did he walk the two hundred meters to the noodle stall, hands in pockets, telling himself he could still turn around.

The woman at the stall was younger than he expected—not young, but younger than anyone who'd have business with Halvard Iversen ought to be. She sat with her back straight and both hands wrapped around a teacup she wasn't drinking, watching the street with the fixed attention of someone memorizing exits. When he stopped at the edge of the lamplight, she looked up without surprise.

"Emrys Iversen."

"Not interested in whatever this is."

"My name is Suriya." She didn't stand. Didn't smile. She had the kind of face that had decided smiling was a tool to be used sparingly, like a flare gun. "Sit down or don't. But hear me first, because I've come a long way and I only get to say this once."

Down the street, a dog barked twice and gave up. The river kept on breathing.

"Halvard Iversen's expedition missed its scheduled check-in eleven days ago," Suriya said. "Above the Khet Gorge. There was a second contact window three days ago. Also missed. The institute has been very quiet about it—no distress traffic, no search announcement, nothing filed with the river authority. Which tells you what you need to know about how much they want anyone official looking upstream right now."

Emrys felt the words land somewhere below his ribs, in the place where dread lived, the place he'd built his whole drifting life to keep unfurnished.

"I don't work for the institute," he said. "I don't work for her. I haven't spoken to my mother in—"

"Ten years. Yes. I know." Suriya finally drank her tea, grimaced at the temperature. "I also know you wired money to a supply outfitter in Chet Vinh eight months ago under a name that isn't yours. I know the expedition's satellite uplink was maintained through a service account you opened. I know that for a decade, Dr. Iversen's expeditions have had radios that worked, permits that arrived, and replacement parts that materialized, and that none of it ever appeared in the institute's ledgers." She set the cup down. "You've been her ghost engineer, Mr. Iversen. You just never let her see you doing it."

The lamp above them buzzed. A moth threw itself against the glass, again and again, with the devotion of the damned.

"If she's missing," Emrys said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, rougher, "why hasn't the institute raised alarm? She's their prize. Their name on every press release."

"Because the institute doesn't want a rescue." Suriya's eyes didn't leave his face. "They want discretion. They sent me—someone cheap, deniable, local—to find someone expendable, deniable, local, to go up quietly and find out what happened before anyone else does. And everyone told me the same thing: the only guide who knows that water and isn't on the institute's payroll is a drunk salvager in Thuan Lok who won't go above Sarn Ket."

"I won't."

"I hadn't asked yet."

"You were about to."

Suriya leaned back on the bench. For the first time something moved behind her expression—not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of one careful person measuring another.

"Your mother is somewhere above the gorge with nine people and no communications," she said quietly. "The monsoon tracking shifted last week. Landfall is coming early this year. Whatever's up there—whether it's fever, bandits, a wrecked boat, or something worse—there is a clock on it now, and the clock doesn't care that you two don't speak." She stood, dropped bills on the counter for the untouched bowls, and pulled a damp card from her jacket pocket. "I'm at the guesthouse until the morning packet leaves. If you're on it, we talk terms. If you're not—" she shrugged, "—then I guess the river keeps what it keeps."

She walked off toward the lights of the guesthouse, small and unhurried, her ruined shoes slapping the wet boards.

Emrys stood a long time under the buzzing lamp. The moth had gone quiet or dead. Down at the moorings, the *Maren Voss* rocked against her lines, and beyond her the black water slid north, past Sarn Ket, past the drowned temple, past all the country he had spent ten years refusing, carrying everything it carried toward the sea.

He thought about walking back to the yard. He thought about the propeller shaft, the coiled hoses, the beautiful pointless liturgy of staying.

Then he picked up the card, and his feet, traitorous things, were already moving toward the guesthouse before he'd decided anything at all.
