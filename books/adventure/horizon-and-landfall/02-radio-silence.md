## 2. Radio Silence

The monsoon was still six weeks out, but the river already smelled like it—mud and green rot and the sweet metallic tang of water that had traveled too far through too much. Emrys Iversen stood on the deck of the *Mekhala's* sister ship, a rust-streaked salvage barge called the *Patient Answer*, and watched a dead cow turn slowly in an eddy below the fuel dock at Thung Sala. It had been there three days. Nobody would touch it until the district men came with hooks and a claim ticket, and nobody seemed in any hurry.

He'd been on the lower river for eleven months now. Long enough that the barkeeps knew his drink and didn't know his name. Long enough that he'd stopped flinching when someone said the word *north*. He'd built himself a life with the dimensions of a barge hold: narrow, functional, impossible to lose anything in, because he kept nothing he couldn't carry. Two duffels. A tool kit. A waterproof case of charts that ended, deliberately, at the last bend before the Khet Gorge, as though the river itself had simply ceased to exist above that point, dissolved into cloud like the headwaters on old maps marked *here be dragons*.

The charter request had come that morning, and he had refused it by noon, and he was still refusing it now, at dusk, standing very still on the deck as if motion might undo him.

It wasn't the money. The money had been obscene—a private party, they'd said, needing passage to the upper trading post at Ban Khet, two weeks' round trip, hazard rates doubled for the season. He'd quoted them triple and watched the broker's face do nothing at all, which told him the number didn't matter, which told him who was paying, which was the whole reason he'd said no. Institutions paid like that when they wanted something done quietly and were willing to buy the silence along with the labor. He'd spent ten years learning what that smell was. It came off a man in a pressed shirt standing on a wet dock, offering too much for a simple run north.

North. Where the tributaries braided up into the high valleys where his mother worked. Where her name was spoken in supply posts the way sailors spoke of weather—as a force, impersonal, enormous, something you planned around.

He went below, lit the single bulb over the chart table, and ate rice from a tin plate without tasting it. The *Patient Answer*'s owner, a widow named Chaya who owned four barges and trusted none of them, had left him alone since the funeral of her engineer in March. That was the arrangement: he kept her boats floating, she asked no questions about why a man with his hands and his papers chose to live on the working end of a river instead of in some university town with his name on a building. She thought it was grief. She was not wrong, only incomplete.

The radio crackled at half past nine.

He almost didn't answer it. That was the truth he would carry afterward, the small shameful stone at the bottom of everything: the set hissed and spat static and a voice said *vessel Patient Answer, vessel Patient Answer, come in*, and his hand hovered over the switch for a full five seconds while the dead cow turned in the dark eddy outside and somewhere upstream, above the Khet Gorge, above the line where his charts simply stopped, whatever was happening was happening without him.

He answered because his hand betrayed him. Or because it didn't—that depended on how honest he was willing to be, then or ever.

"Patient Answer. Go ahead."

"Emrys Iversen?" The voice was a woman's, low and unhurried, with the flattened vowels of someone who had learned English in four countries and belonged to none of them. "My name is Suriya. We should talk. Not on this channel."

"I don't know you."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But you know the name Halvard Iversen, and I know where you're moored, and I know you refused a charter this morning that would have taken you within forty kilometers of her base camp. So either you're a coward, Mr. Iversen, or you're careful, and I've read enough about you to bet on careful. Come to the Naga's Rest on the waterfront. Ask for the woman playing cards alone. You have my word you'll walk out again."

"You have my word I'll consider it."

A pause. Static, and beneath the static, faintly, the river.

"Two check-ins," she said. "Her expedition missed two scheduled check-ins. The first one, people make excuses. Equipment. Weather. The second one—" Another pause, shorter, and he understood that the pauses were not hesitation but precision; this was a woman who measured what she gave away. "The second one, people stop making excuses and start making plans. Good night, Mr. Iversen."

The channel went dead. He sat with the tin plate going cold in front of him and the bulb swinging slightly with the current's pull against the hull, and he thought: *I can pretend I didn't hear.* He had gotten very good at pretending, these ten years. Pretending the journal articles with her name on them hadn't needed his fieldwork to exist. Pretending the letters he never opened weren't stacked in a storage unit in a city he avoided. Pretending that distance was a decision rather than a wound he dressed daily and called freedom.

He washed the plate. He checked the bilge pump, which did not need checking. He looked at his charts, all of them ending at the same deliberate blankness, and heard Suriya's voice again: *either you're a coward or you're careful.*

The rain started after midnight, soft and warm, the season's first real taste of what was coming. He lay in his bunk listening to it on the deck overhead and did not sleep, and around three in the morning he admitted to the dark that there was no version of the next ten years in which he stayed on the lower river and called it living.

* * *

The Naga's Rest at dawn was a room of scarred teak and cigarette haze with a view of the brown water and the fuel drums and the dead cow, which had drifted closer overnight as if drawn by the gravity of bad news. Four old men played checkers by the window. A ceiling fan turned with the conviction of something that had long ago stopped believing in its own work.

She was at the corner table, playing solitaire exactly as promised, and she was not what he expected. He had imagined a man, or if a woman, someone harder. Suriya was perhaps fifty, small, neat, wearing a faded batik shirt and reading glasses pushed up into gray-shot hair, and she dealt her cards with the economy of someone who had done every kind of work a river offered and found most of it dull. She didn't look up when he sat down.

"You came," she said. "Good. Losers fold early; winners stay to see the whole hand." She swept the cards together. "Tea?"

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit. Standing men leave early, and this takes as long as it takes." She signaled the boy behind the counter without looking at him, and tea arrived, thick and oversweet, in glasses that had seen decades. "Your mother is Dr. Halvard Iversen."

"Not news."

"The Iversen Expedition, funded by the Corvane Institute for Historical Studies, entered the upper Khet valley on the twenty-third of March. Scheduled contact: weekly, Sundays, 0600, via the relay at Ban Khet. They made contact on the thirty-first. They missed the seventh." She laid two cards face down on the table between them, side by side, like an offering or an accusation. "They missed the fourteenth."

Emrys looked at the cards. Two blanks. He made himself say it plainly. "Radios fail. Batteries die. Rivers eat equipment every day."

"Rivers eat equipment," Suriya agreed. "And expeditions send runners. There are three routes down from the upper valley to the Ban Khet relay, and in nine days, no runner has come down any of them. The last confirmed sighting of the team was a supply boatman who dropped cargo at their forward camp on the second of April. He says the camp was quiet. Too quiet. He says Dr. Iversen thanked him personally and told him not to come back until after landfall." Her eyes came up over the glasses, and they were the color of the river and just as hard to see the bottom of. "Halvard Iversen does not tell boatmen to go away. Halvard Iversen recruits boatmen. I carried her gear myself, once, years ago. She talked me into hauling a generator up a cliff for a bottle of whisky and a story about a temple. She collects people. If she sent everyone away, she collected something she didn't want witnesses near."

The fan turned. The checkers players argued in low voices. Down at the dock, a man in a pressed shirt stepped off the morning ferry, and Emrys felt the back of his neck tighten before he could even say why.

"Who's asking?" he said. "You're not family. You're not government—you'd have brought papers. Institute?"

Suriya smiled without warmth. "I'm asking. Let's leave it there for tonight. What matters is what I know and what you know, and whether those add up to a boat going north." She tapped the two facedown cards. "Here's what I know. The Corvane Institute has chartered every capable vessel above the gorge in the last week—every one except yours, and yours only because you refused. They're paying triple. They've also bought exclusivity at the Ban Khet relay station, which means no other traffic gets scheduled time, which means whoever controls that relay controls what the world hears about the upper valley. And yesterday, a man named Pell Wexler arrived in Thung Sala on the morning flight, carrying diplomatic credentials that don't match his job title, asking questions about you specifically."

The rain had stopped. Through the window the river ran high and fast and indifferent, carrying silt from country he hadn't seen in a decade toward a sea he barely remembered.

"Why me specifically?"

"Because you're the only pilot who knows the gorges above Ban Khet and won't take institute money. Because your mother's expedition is your mother's expedition. And because—" Suriya gathered the two blank cards, squared them, and set them down again, and he realized she was doing it deliberately, making him watch her handle the unknown, "—because whoever arranged this wants the search run by someone with a reason to succeed and a reason to keep his mouth shut afterward. Family is discreet, Mr. Iversen. Family has something to lose."

"And what do you have to lose?"

For the first time, something moved behind her face that wasn't calculation. It was gone quickly, banked like a fire, but he saw it: an old grief, worn smooth with handling.

"A debt," she said. "To a woman who once hauled my generator up a cliff. Now." She stood, dropped coins beside the tea glasses, and put on a canvas jacket that had been mended more times than most jackets are worn. "I'm not asking you to decide here. I'm telling you the shape of it. Somewhere above the Khet Gorge, seventeen people have missed two Sundays. The institute is buying up the river so that whatever happened up there gets handled privately. And the only person who might get there first, ask the right questions, and refuse to be managed—is sitting in this teahouse refusing a charter."

"I refused it because—"

"I know why you refused it." She settled her glasses onto her nose and looked at him fully then, and it was worse than pity, because it was recognition. "Ten years is a long time to sail away from someone, Mr. Iversen. But the river only goes two directions, and you've been pretending otherwise since the day you left. One more thing." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, silhouetted against the bright water. "Wexler booked passage on the first boat that will take him. If it isn't yours, it'll be someone who doesn't know the gorges, doesn't know your mother, and doesn't care what comes back down that river as long as it's quiet. Think about who you'd rather have holding the tiller when the truth comes downstream."

Then she was gone, into the morning, and Emrys sat a long time with the cold sweet tea and the two blank cards lying on the scarred wood where she'd left them, face down, waiting to be turned.

* * *

He walked the docks until noon. It was avoidance dressed as industry, and he knew it, and he did it anyway: checked lines that were taut, coiled rope that was already coiled, argued mildly with Chaya's foreman about a fouled propeller that needed no arguing about. The river did what the river always did. Ferries came and went. Cargo moved south. The dead cow was finally hooked and hauled away by district men who complained about it the entire time, and its absence left a strange clean space in the eddy, a hole in the shape of a thing everyone had gotten used to ignoring.

That was the hour it caught him—not Suriya's arguments, not the missing check-ins, not even Wexler's name, but the empty water where the cow had been. He stood at the rail of the *Patient Answer* and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own chart, that he had spent ten years moored to exactly that: a absence shaped like his mother, so familiar he'd mistaken it for scenery.

He thought about the letters in the storage unit. Eleven of them, unopened, because the twelfth—the one that mattered, the one from the year he left—had said enough. *You were never on the expeditions, Emrys. You were the expeditions.* His mother's handwriting, precise as a survey marker, and the cruelest part was that she'd meant it as praise.

He thought about Sunday mornings, 0600, a girl named Nia Okonkwo—he'd read her name in the expedition roster Suriya had slid across the table before leaving, along with everything else, because of course Suriya had given him the file; she was the kind of woman who armed you and then let you choose—sitting at a relay set waiting for a voice that didn't come. Seventeen people. Two Sundays. A river buying its own silence.

And underneath all of it, older than all of it, the thing he could not pretend anymore: if she was dead up there, and he had been eleven months and forty kilometers away, there was no lower river long enough, no salvage job enough, no distance enough to make that survivable. The avoidance had only ever worked while the danger was hypothetical. Suriya had made it arithmetic.

Chaya found him at dusk, sitting on the hatch cover with the file open on his knees.

"I heard you turned down the north run," she said. She was seventy and built like a capstan and afraid of nothing on the water. "Triple rates. You're either sick or stupid."

"Both, maybe."

"Hm." She lowered herself onto the rail with the groan of old joints and looked out at the brown water, at the far bank dissolving into the blue of evening. "My husband ran north twice a year, forty years. Rice up, timber down. Said the gorges above Khet were the only place he ever felt honest—river too fast for lying." She was quiet a moment. "He drowned there, in the end. Landfall year, big flood. I never blamed the river. Rivers do what rivers are." She turned and looked at him with eyes gone pale as driftwood. "People are different. People choose. You want my boat, take it. You want my blessing, earn it by coming back."

"Suriya said the institute's bought every hull above the gorge."

"Suriya talks too much and knows too much, and both those things have kept me alive longer than sense would have." Chaya stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "The *Patient Answer* is yours. Fuel's short, so you'll want to top at Ban Khet—if the institute hasn't bought that too. And Emrys." She paused at the companionway, a dark shape against the cabin light. "Whatever you're running from up there—it swims faster than you think. Boats help. Running doesn't."

He worked through the night. Charts first: he unrolled the ones he had and then, hands steady in the way of hands doing familiar work over an unsteady heart, drew from memory the reaches above the gorge—the channel shifts, the rock gardens, the blind bends where the current set hard against the cut banks. Ten years since he'd run that water and his pen remembered every meter, which told him something about pretending that he declined to examine. He tested the engines, patched a weeping seam, lashed down spare drums, loaded medical stores he told himself were standard inventory and knew were not.

At 0400 he took out the satellite phone and dialed a number he had sworn never to dial, and let it ring four times, and hung up before it connected, and sat in the dark of the wheelhouse feeling like a man who had shouted into a well and heard nothing come back.

Then he turned on the radio, scanned the bands out of habit, and stopped.

Ban Khet relay was transmitting. Not the scheduled traffic Suriya had described—something else, a looped message, formal and flat, the kind of announcement institutions recorded in advance so no human voice would have to carry it:

*"Attention all vessels. Upper Khet valley closed to navigation pending hydrological survey. Permits suspended. Do not attempt passage above the gorge. Repeat: do not attempt passage above the gorge. This notice supersedes all prior advisories."*

Hydrological survey. In April, ahead of the rains, when no survey crew on earth would choose to be there. He listened to it cycle three times, watching the needle tremble, and felt the dread in his chest finish its slow curdle into something harder and more useful.

They weren't searching. They were closing the door—and calling it the weather.

Emrys switched off the radio, lit the wheelhouse lamp, and began, methodically, to load a boat for waters that officially did not exist. Above the gorge, seventeen people had missed two Sundays. Below it, an institution was buying the silence one permit at a time. And somewhere between those two facts was a stretch of brown water with his name on it, running north whether he approved or not.

By first light, the *Patient Answer* had fuel, charts, and a heading. All she lacked was a captain who'd admit he'd already decided—and a pilot's license the institute hadn't thought to buy.

He was casting off the stern line when the man in the pressed shirt appeared at the foot of the gangway, briefcase in hand, smiling like the morning.

"Mr. Iversen? Pell Wexler, Corvane Institute. I believe we're both going north."
