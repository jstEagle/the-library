## 3. Charter Terms

The boat Ondine Rask kept at the Sengkar wharf was called the *Vela Maru*, and she was ugly in the way that only honest boats are ugly—flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, her hull patched in three places with steel that didn't match the original plating, her cabin roof crowded with solar panels and a rain catchment rigged from salvaged tarpaulin. She sat low in the brown water like a dog that had learned not to flinch.

Emrys found the captain mending a net on the aft deck, though he suspected the net was theater. Some people whittled. Some people smoked. Ondine Rask, by reputation, mended nets she never used, because it gave her hands something to do while she decided whether she liked you.

He stood on the planks of the wharf for a long moment before speaking, letting her look at him. That was the etiquette of the lower river. You did not open with money. Money came later, and badly, if you opened with it.

"You're Iversen," she said without looking up. Her voice had the flattened vowels of someone who'd grown up between ports, belonging nowhere long enough to lose an accent and gain none. "The salvage man."

"Word travels."

"Word travels downriver faster than water." She bit a knot tight, cut the line with her teeth, spat the end into the river. "You want to go up."

It wasn't a question. He hadn't asked anything yet. He felt the first cold thread of it go through him—the sense, familiar from childhood, of being read like a site survey, every layer exposed before he'd chosen what to show.

"I need passage to the head of the Khet Gorge," he said. "Fast as you can make it, but alive. Four passengers, possibly five. Cargo."

"What cargo?"

"Cargo you don't ask about."

She finally looked up then. Her eyes were pale gray, the color of the river in February, and they went over him the way a mechanic goes over an engine—listening for the knock. "No questions about cargo," she said. "What about passengers?"

"Same terms."

"One of them's going to be trouble."

Emrys almost smiled. "You've met them already?"

"I've met everyone already." She set down the net and stood, unfolding to her full height—taller than him, broad-shouldered, with the permanent slight list of a woman who'd spent thirty years compensating for current. "The institute called two days ago. Before they called you, I'd bet. They wanted a boat. I told them I was under repair." She jerked her chin at the *Vela Maru*, seaworthy as sin. "Then I waited to see who else they'd call. And here you are."

The cold thread became a wire. "Why wait?"

"Because whoever goes up this week is either going to come back rich, or isn't going to come back." She said it the way she might have reported the weather, or the price of diesel. "I wanted to know which one you were before I tied myself to your hull."

"And now?"

"Now I still don't know. But you didn't open with money, so you're not entirely stupid, and stupid men don't survive ten years on this river doing salvage work." She wiped her hands on her thighs, leaving brown streaks. "Terms. My boat, my rules. We run days only—no night running above Bend Nine, the channels shift too fast this time of year. I eat at the captain's table or alone; you don't get to choose which. And when I say beach the boat, we beach the boat, even if your mother is drowning."

The word landed exactly where she'd aimed it. Emrys let three seconds pass before answering, because showing that it hurt would have cost more than the delay.

"Agreed," he said. "My term: no radio transmissions about our position or purpose. Not to anyone. If you need to call ahead for fuel or supplies, fine, but nothing about us."

Ondine's eyebrows rose a fraction. It was, he suspected, the most expression she'd shown all morning. "You think someone's listening."

"I think someone's always listening. I just don't know yet who's paying them."

She studied him another moment, then stuck out a hand rough as tree bark. "Half up front. Half when I put you back on this wharf, assuming there's still a wharf." Her grip was brief and crushing. "We leave tomorrow at first light. Sleep aboard if you want; the bunk in the stern doesn't leak much."

* * *

The institute's observer arrived that evening, and he arrived precisely, which told Emrys more than anything else about him.

Pell Wexler came down the wharf road at 6:40 p.m., fifteen minutes after the light began to fail and twenty minutes before full dark, carrying a single soft-sided bag and wearing field clothes so new they still held their creases. Khaki shirt, khaki trousers, boots that had never known mud. He was perhaps fifty, lean, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, and he walked with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry because other people hurried for him.

"You must be Mr. Iversen." He extended his hand across the gap between wharf and deck, and Emrys took it. The handshake was dry, cool, calibrated. "Pell Wexler. Institute Office of Field Liaison."

"Liaison," Emrys repeated. "That's a gentle word."

"It's an accurate one. I liaise." Wexler smiled. It was a good smile, professionally good, the kind that reached the eyes because it had been trained to. "May I come aboard?"

"It's Captain Rask's boat."

"Captain Rask has already agreed." Of course she had; refusing the funding institute's man would have meant losing the charter, and probably worse. Wexler stepped over the gunwale with a sureness that surprised Emrys—he'd expected the man to stumble on the wet decking—and settled himself in the cockpit as though he'd been assigned the seat years ago. "I'll try to be no burden at all. I ask only to observe, take notes for the record, and ensure the expedition's interests are represented."

"The expedition's interests," Emrys said. "Or the institute's?"

Wexler tilted his head, and for a moment the smile thinned into something more honest—something tired, maybe, or merely patient. "Mr. Iversen, may I speak plainly? Your mother's expedition carries the institute's name, its insurance, its liability, and eleven million in equipment grants. When a funded team misses check-ins, the institute has obligations—to the families, to the board, to the governments whose permits made the work possible. Someone must go and see. You were willing. I am willing. There's no contradiction in that."

"No," Emrys agreed. "There wouldn't be. That's what makes people like you useful."

Something flickered behind Wexler's eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his own instrument played back to him by someone who'd found the flaw in it.

"I understand you and Dr. Iversen are estranged," Wexler said, gentler now, and the gentleness was the worst thing yet. "That must make this difficult. I want you to know the institute holds no expectations about—family matters. Your task is navigation and recovery logistics. Mine is documentation. We can stay out of each other's way."

"We can," Emrys said. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When we find them—if we find them—I talk to my mother before you file any report. Whatever you transmit downstream, whatever assessment you send back to your office, she hears it from me first. Not from a liaison's summary."

Wexler considered this for longer than the request deserved, and Emrys understood that the consideration itself was the answer: the man was weighing whether the concession cost him anything, and finding that it cost nothing, because he had never intended to let Emrys stand between him and a transmitter anyway.

"That seems fair," Wexler said warmly. "Family should hear things first." He picked up his bag. "Which bunk is mine?"

* * *

Emrys didn't sleep. He sat in the stern where the bunk didn't leak much and listened to the river work against the hull, and he thought about the shape of the thing he'd agreed to.

Two days ago he had been a man drifting between salvage jobs, taking wrecks off sandbars and engines out of mud, staying south of the monsoon line the way you stayed away from a house where someone had died. Now he had a boat, a captain who knew more than she said, an observer who smiled like a locked door, and a heading north toward the Khet Gorge and whatever silence waited above it.

He turned Wexler over in his mind the way he'd turn a recovered artifact—looking for the seam, the repair, the place where the thing had been broken and made to look whole. *Office of Field Liaison.* He'd dealt with the institute's people before, at a distance, in the years when he'd ghosted his mother's logistics from the outside: supply contracts, permit renewals, the quiet bureaucratic weather that followed her expeditions like gulls follow a trawler. They had been accountants and schedulers, mostly. None of them had ever arrived with creased khakis and a handshake calibrated to the gram.

Liaisons documented. This man assessed. Emrys had watched enough deals go bad on this river to know the difference between a witness and a valuer, and Pell Wexler was a valuer. He was here to determine what the expedition was worth—alive, dead, or silenced—and somewhere upstream, someone was waiting for his number.

And Ondine Rask. He rolled her over too. A captain who turned down institute money and then waited two days at dock for the alternative—that wasn't caution, that was positioning. She knew something about how this would end, or she believed she did, and she'd placed herself where she could profit from whichever way it broke. He couldn't blame her. On the lower river, loyalty was a mooring you paid for by the day.

But she'd shaken his hand, and she hadn't opened with money either, and there was a version of her—buried under the net-mending and the flat gray eyes—who ran rivers other pilots wouldn't, and chose her passengers like she chose her weather windows. He could work with that. He'd have to.

Toward dawn he heard footsteps on the deck, soft, deliberate, and Ondine lowered herself onto the hatch coaming beside him without invitation. She handed him a tin cup of coffee strong enough to strip paint. For a while neither of them spoke. The sky over the eastern bank went from black to bruise to a thin, watery gold.

"Wexler's asleep," she said finally. "Or pretending. Either way, his cabin door's shut."

"He'll report everything he sees."

"He'll report everything he thinks we want him to see. That's different, and it's the only advantage we've got." She drank. "I've carried institute observers before. Three times. Twice they came home and filed reports that matched what happened. Once—" she paused, considering how much to spend, "—once the man came home alone and the rest of the crew signed on to a different boat in a different port and never sailed this river again. I never learned what he wrote. I only learned what it did."

Emrys looked at her. In the half-light her face was all planes and shadow, unreadable as a chart in fog. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you demanded no questions about cargo, and I'm granting them, and in exchange I'm asking one question of my own." She turned to face him fully. "When we get up there and find out what happened to your mother's people—which it will be, *what*, not *whether*—whose side is this boat on? Yours, hers, theirs?" She tipped her chin toward Wexler's closed cabin door. "Because I can carry passengers, Iversen, or I can carry a fight. I can't do both above the gorge. The water won't allow it."

The question hung in the river air, and Emrys found he couldn't answer it honestly, and wouldn't answer it falsely, and so he gave her the only true thing he had.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I'll know before you need to. That's the best I can give you."

Ondine held his gaze for a long moment, weighing it, the way she'd weighed everything since he'd stepped onto her wharf. Then she drained her coffee, stood, and stretched until her spine cracked like green wood.

"First light was an hour ago," she said. "Get your gear stowed. We're burning daylight, and above Bend Nine, daylight is the only thing on this river that's free."

The *Vela Maru* cast off into the current, and the gorge country opened ahead of them like a throat.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the light in Wexler's cabin had been on for some time—and through its small porthole, the observer had watched the whole exchange, making notes.
