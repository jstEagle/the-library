## 4. Upstream

The river changed its mind about them at dawn.

Emrys felt it before he saw it—the boat's rhythm shifting under his boots, Ondine's hands moving on the wheel in small corrections, the engine note climbing half a tone. He came up from the hold where he'd been re-securing the fuel drums and found the water gone from brown to the color of old tea, fast and glassy over stone, and the banks closing in like a book being shut.

"Khet Narrows," Ondine said, not turning around. "From here we read or we drown."

She read the way her mother must have taught her, if anyone had—head tilted, eyes moving across the surface in long sweeps, left to right, like a woman scanning scripture for the verse that would save her. Emrys watched her work and understood that what she was doing wasn't navigation. It was translation. Every boil and seam in the current was a sentence; every V of smooth water spreading downstream from a submerged rock was a word she'd known since before she could write.

"There," she said, pointing with her chin at a line of standing waves. "See how they're breaking even? Even means deep. When they break ragged, something's underneath waiting to take your propeller as an offering." She fed the wheel a quarter turn. "Your mother's people came up this way?"

"My mother's people," Emrys said, "came up this way in air-conditioned launches with institute flags on the bow."

"Mm." Ondine's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "And you?"

"I carried the coolers."

It was true, and it was also a lie, and both of them knew it, and neither of them pressed.

* * *

The gorges narrowed through the morning until the sky was a bright ribbon overhead and the walls rose wet and dripping on either side, ferns hanging out of cracks, water threading down the rock in silver strings. The sound changed too. On the lower river there had been birds, chain saws somewhere far off, the low murmur of a world with people in it. Here there was only the engine and the water and the echo of the engine and the echo of the water, bouncing back and forth between the canyon walls until it seemed the gorge itself was humming.

Pell Wexler sat amidships with his notebook open on his knee, writing nothing. He'd been writing nothing since dawn. Emrys had learned to read him the way Ondine read water: the stillness before the question was the tell.

"You were on the Sengkar dig," Wexler said. It wasn't quite a question. "Ninety-eight. You'd have been—what, nineteen?"

"Eighteen."

"Eighteen, running logistics for a forty-person expedition. That's remarkable."

"It's arithmetic," Emrys said. "Somebody has to count the rice."

Wexler smiled his polite smile, the one that never reached anything below his cheekbones. "The institute's files say otherwise. They say Dr. Iversen's early fieldwork succeeded because someone behind the scenes solved problems no one else saw coming. Fuel contracts. Porters' disputes. A cholera scare in ninety-nine that never made the papers because someone got medicine upriver in four days." He turned a page he hadn't written on. "They say she had a son who was very good at being invisible."

"The files should stick to pottery shards."

"They do. But I'm curious about people." Wexler closed the notebook, which somehow made it worse. "Ten years ago you stopped. Walked away from the whole enterprise. Why does a man walk away from the only thing he's ever been good at?"

Emrys looked upstream, where the river bent and the light fell in a slanted gold column against the far wall, and for a moment he was eighteen again and it was evening at Sengkar and his mother was standing in the trench with the last light on her face, saying *look at this, Emrys, look what the earth kept for us*, and meaning, though she never said it, *look what I kept, look what I earned, look what you only helped carry.*

"She didn't want a partner," he said finally. "She wanted an instrument. And instruments don't get their names on things."

"And so you left."

"And so I drifted." He heard the correction land and watched Wexler hear it too—the difference between leaving and drifting, between a decision and a failure to decide. Ten years of salvage jobs and charter runs and never signing a lease longer than a season. Ten years of staying mobile enough that stopping never became possible.

"Drifting," Wexler repeated softly, tasting it. "And yet here you are. Going back up."

"Here I am," Emrys said, and went forward to check the fuel line he'd already checked twice.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar in the lee of a cliff, and the stars came out hard and close, the way they only did above the monsoon line where no town's glow softened them. Ondine cooked rice and dried fish over a driftwood fire and said nothing, which was her way of saying everything was fine. Wexler turned in early, or lay in his tent pretending to.

Emrys stayed up and let the gorge have him.

Because the geography was doing it deliberately—he was sure of that now. Every bend brought back a bend from another river, another year. There: a gravel bar like the one at Tep Muang where he'd spent a whole summer at eleven, cataloguing potsherds by lamplight while the other boys swam, because his mother had discovered he had a gift for ordering chaos and had put it to work without once asking whether he wanted it. There: a cliff face streaked white with birdlime, like the cliffs above the Kham caves, where he'd rappelled down on a frayed rope because the hired rigger quit and someone had to, and his mother had stood at the top and called down, *careful, Emrys*, and he had understood even then that *careful* meant *don't die, you're useful.*

He had been useful for thirty years. He had been loved, he suspected, the way a good hammer is loved—reliably, gratefully, and never once for itself.

The fire cracked. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, three notes falling, and the gorge gave the call back doubled, tripled, until the dark was full of voices saying the same thing over and over, and none of them his.

* * *

Morning brought the first of the real rapids, and Ondine stopped reading and started praying, if what she did could be called prayer. It looked more like argument. She stood at the wheel muttering to the water—*no, no, yes, there, fine, fine*—and took them through a chute of standing waves with six inches of clearance on either side, and when it was done she exhaled through her nose, once, and said, "Coffee," as if nothing had happened.

Wexler watched all of it with his notebook open now, actually writing, and Emrys understood that the man was mapping something other than the river. He was mapping them. How Ondine handled fear. How Emrys handled questions. What broke loose and what held.

"Your mother's Khet survey," Wexler said over the coffee, conversationally, terribly. "Twenty years ago. You were on it as a child, weren't you? The photographs in the institute archive show a boy at the water's edge. Skinny. Holding a surveying staff taller than he was."

"I don't remember the photographs."

"But you remember the river."

Emrys looked upstream. The gorge ahead bent hard to starboard, and beyond the bend the walls pinched together into a slot of white noise where the whole river forced itself through a gap you could nearly jump across. He remembered the river. He remembered being nine years old and standing exactly where the water in the archive photograph stood, and hearing his mother tell the funders—through him, because his voice carried better than hers, she'd always made him be her voice—that this valley would rewrite the region's history. And he remembered the funders laughing politely, and his mother's hand on his shoulder, gripping, and how the grip had felt less like pride than like proof. *See. Mine. Functional.*

"I remember," he said, "that everything I know about rivers I learned carrying things for her."

"That's not nothing."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It's not nothing. It's just not mine."

Ondine, coiling a line nearby, snorted. Wexler looked at her. She shrugged. "Man's been carrying things his whole life," she said. "I can tell. He checks the tie-downs like they insulted him."

* * *

Past the slot the country opened slightly, then narrowed again, each gorge tighter than the last, the river rising by inches as the tributaries fed it from hidden valleys. By afternoon the water had come up enough that Ondine started measuring it against marks only she could see—a stain on the cliff, a half-drowned boulder—and frowning at what she found.

"Early," she said. "Water's a week early. Maybe more."

"Meaning?" Wexler asked.

"Meaning the monsoon's thinking about landfall sooner than the forecasts say. Meaning our window is shrinking." She glanced at Emrys. "Meaning whatever we're going to find up there, we're going to find it faster than we planned."

That night, anchored in an eddy beneath walls so high the stars didn't reach the water, Emrys took the first watch and heard Wexler's tent zip open in the dark. He didn't turn around. He listened to the man move to the stern, quiet as a man who'd practiced being quiet, and he heard the click of the radio handset, and the low murmur of a voice transmitting in bursts too short and too coded to be weather reports.

*Station calling. Position confirmed. Window narrowing. Advise.*

Emrys stood in the bow with his back turned and the river pulling at the anchor line, and he thought about his mother twenty years ago on this same water, gripping his shoulder, saying *look what the earth kept for us.* The earth kept things. So did men like Pell Wexler. The question—which had been the question his whole life, dressed as a hundred smaller ones—was what you did when keeping and taking wore the same face.

Behind him, the radio clicked off. Footsteps, soft, retreating. A tent zipper, drawn shut like a mouth.

Upstream, past three more days of gorges, his mother was alive or dead, and the river was rising, and the man sleeping ten feet away had just told someone where they were.

Emrys looked at the black water and began, quietly, to plan.
