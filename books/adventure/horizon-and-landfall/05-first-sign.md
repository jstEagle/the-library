## 5. First Sign

The cache was marked on Halvard's own maps, which was the first strange thing about it—her hand, her notation, the tight little hieroglyphs she'd used since Emrys was small enough to sit in a screen tent and sort potsherds by color. *Cache 4, left bank, above the narrows. Resupply for descent party.* He had copied those maps as a boy, tracing her letters until his own handwriting became an echo of hers, and now he stood on a mudbank reading his mother's instructions like scripture he'd stopped believing in but still knew by heart.

Ondine brought the boat in against the current with the ease of a woman parking a car. She didn't kill the engine entirely; she let it idle low, throaty, ready.

"Ten minutes," she said. "If you're not back in ten minutes I come looking, and I don't look quietly."

Wexler stepped ashore first, of course. He wore rubber-soled boots that had never seen real mud and a field jacket with the creases still sharp from folding. He moved up the bank the way a man moves through a museum—interested, careful, uninvolved.

Emrys went up through the cane.

The path to the cache was barely a path—a game trail widened by porters' feet, cut into the red clay of the bluff, shaded by kapok trees whose buttress roots rose out of the ground like the ribs of drowned ships. The air under the canopy was a physical thing, warm and wet and green-smelling, pressing against his skin like a damp cloth. Somewhere above them a hornbill laughed its absurd wooden laugh and fell silent, as if embarrassed.

He smelled the camp before he saw it.

Not rot exactly. Not smoke. Something between—charcoal and diesel and the sweetish chemical tang of spilled fuel, all of it sitting wrong in the wet air, layered over the green instead of rising out of it. A living camp smells like people: rice, sweat, soap if they're lucky. This smelled like a place that had been dressed.

The cache came into view through the cane: a clearing maybe thirty meters across, floored in packed clay, ringed by the forest's slow siege. Two large canvas tents, expedition-grade, khaki-green, still squared away. Crates stacked in neat rows against a tarpaulin lean-to. A fire pit swept clean. A supply drum on its side, and beside it—

Beside it, the scene.

A crate lay broken open, its lid pried up at one corner, medical supplies scattered across the clay with a carelessness too precise to be careless. Ampoules glinted in the leaf-filtered light. A stretcher leaned against a tree at an angle no tired porter would choose. A radio sat on a folding table, antenna snapped—not torn, *snapped*, clean break—and set down facing the river, as though someone wanted it found from the water.

Emrys crouched at the edge of the clearing and did not move for a long moment.

His mother had taught him this without ever meaning to. On digs, when he was nine, ten, eleven, she would walk him through a looted tomb or a vandalized trench and make him read it aloud. *What do you see. What don't you see. What does someone want you to see.* It wasn't archaeology, not really—it was suspicion formalized into method, the same eye she turned on stratigraphy she turned on people, and it had made him nearly impossible to be friends with as a child and very good at this now.

*What do you see.* A ransacked cache. Scattered supplies. Signs of hurried departure.

*What don't you see.* Blood. Drag marks. Personal effects. The thousand small leavings of genuine panic—a dropped hat, a torn sleeve on cane thorn, a boot print skidding in panic-soft clay. Nothing here was panicked. Nothing here was even tired.

*What does someone want you to see.* Devastation. Abandonment. A story told in props.

He stood and walked the clearing slowly, hands clasped behind his back like a man touring his own bad dream.

The crates were stacked in rows—but the rows were wrong. Porters stack by weight and order of need; these were stacked by *appearance*, heavy below, light on top, labels outward, every label facing the same direction. Someone had built this display to be read from a single vantage point: the river approach.

The scattered medical supplies were the worst of it. Real looters take what's valuable—antibiotics, morphine, the little brown bottles that fetch money in any river town. These ampoules lay in a fan, arranged, catching light. And the boxes they'd come from were empty. Not emptied—*empty*. Packed empty, sealed, then opened for the scene. He turned one over. The seal had been broken carefully, blade-work, not tearing. Inside, cotton padding sat undisturbed around the ghost-outline of nothing.

"Emrys." Wexler's voice came through the cane, and Emrys realized he'd been followed—quietly, competently, boots finding the same game trail. Wexler stopped when he saw the clearing and stood very still, and Emrys watched him do the arithmetic every man does when he walks into a room that has been arranged for him: the flicker of recognition, quickly buried.

"You've seen something like this before," Wexler said. Not a question.

"I grew up seeing things like this." Emrys picked up the radio. The battery compartment held batteries—fresh ones, installed recently, terminals bright. A radio abandoned in a hurry doesn't get fresh batteries. "Someone staged this. Recently."

Wexler crouched by the broken crate. His politeness had a different texture out here, thinner, stretched over whatever moved underneath. "Locals?"

"Locals don't stage. Locals take." Emrys set the radio down. "This is theater. Someone wants us—or my mother, or whoever comes next—to believe the expedition broke apart here. Fever, chaos, flight downstream."

"Fever," Wexler repeated, tasting the word.

"It's the story this country tells about dead foreigners. It writes itself. Nobody asks questions after fever."

Ondine arrived at the edge of the trees then, ten minutes gone, machete in hand, and took in the clearing with one long sweep of her eyes. She said nothing for a moment. Then she walked past both of them, straight to the soft ground at the clearing's edge where the canopy opened, and looked down.

"Tire tracks," she said.

Emrys came over. There, pressed into the clay where the trail met the old logging spur—the one that shouldn't exist anymore, the one the forest had been swallowing for twenty years—ran two parallel grooves, deep, crisp-edged, water standing in the treads so freshly that the impressions hadn't yet begun to slump. Tire tracks, wide-set, heavy vehicle. And beside them, boot prints in a pattern he recognized from military manuals and had hoped never to see again outside them: paired, spaced, disciplined. Men walking in step.

"This far upriver," Ondine said flatly, "there are no roads. There is no road within ninety kilometers of here. That truck came up the old logging spur from the east, and that spur hasn't carried a vehicle since before the war."

"How fresh?" Emrys asked.

She crouched, touched the water standing in the tread, rubbed it between finger and thumb. "Rain two nights ago would have softened these edges. They're still knife-sharp. Day. Maybe two."

Two days ago, Emrys had been three hundred kilometers downstream refusing a charter. Two days ago his mother's expedition had missed its second check-in. The timeline closed around him like a fist.

"So someone got here first," Wexler said slowly. "Before us. Looking for the same thing we're looking for."

"Not looking," Emrys said. "Looking leaves messes. This is *message*. Someone came, found what they found or didn't find it, and then built a scene so the next people to arrive would draw the wrong conclusion and go home satisfied." He looked at Wexler directly. "Who else knows where this cache is?"

"The institute has the expedition plan. Standard."

"Standard meaning who, exactly? Names. How many people hold the route?"

Wexler's pause was short, but Emrys had spent a lifetime measuring his mother's pauses, and he knew the species. "Logistics. Security liaison. Project director." A breath. "Me."

The word hung there. Emrys let it hang. He didn't accuse—he'd learned that much from watching his mother lose allies by winning arguments. He just let the silence do its work, and watched Wexler decide not to fill it.

They searched the rest of the clearing properly, because method was the only weapon anyone had handed him that still worked. Behind the lean-to, half-hidden in cane, Emrys found the things the staging hadn't accounted for—the debris that truth sheds and fiction doesn't know to fake. A ration wrapper, foil, foreign manufacture, not expedition stock. A cigarette end, filterless, ground out with a heel, the tobacco dark and harsh-smelling—river-town cheap, soldier's tobacco. And pressed into the clay beneath the tarpaulin's edge, where a man had knelt to work on something low: the clean rectangular outline of a case that had rested there, weighted, for hours. Long enough to leave a bruise in the earth. Something had been set down here, guarded, and then taken away.

Something box-shaped. Something worth kneeling over.

He showed Ondine without showing Wexler. She read it in three seconds and gave him the smallest nod he'd ever been given, the nod of a woman recalculating everything.

Back at the boat, the river ran high and brown and indifferent, carrying its usual freight of silt and secrets toward a sea that kept none of them. Ondine cast off without discussion. Wexler sat in the stern making notes in a waterproof book, writing with the deliberate calm of a man composing a report, and Emrys watched him write and understood that whatever Wexler put in that book would reach other eyes before any of them reached Halvard—if they reached her at all.

He took the bow seat beside Ondine and said, low, under the engine noise.

"You were hired months ago. For this run."

"I was hired for a charter. Terms were terms." Her eyes stayed on the water, on the seam where current met rock, reading it the way his mother read soil. "But I'll tell you what I told myself when I took the job. Nobody pays that kind of money for a rescue unless they already know what happened."

"And you took it anyway."

"I take most jobs anyway." A muscle moved along her jaw. "I have reasons of my own for going up this river, captain's business, and your institute's money buys the passage. But hear me: whoever staged that clearing wanted the next boat to turn around. We didn't turn around. Which means either they'll try again, harder, upstream—or they've already got what they came for, and what's ahead of us isn't a search anymore."

"What is it?"

Ondine fed a little more throttle and the bow lifted toward the narrowing gorge, toward the country where his mother had vanished into silence.

"It's a race," she said. "And somebody else knows the course."

The gorges swallowed them by degrees. The walls drew in, grey limestone streaked black with rain-rot, and the river quickened between them, talking to itself in the language of water over stone. Emrys stood at the bow with his mother's map in a plastic sleeve and felt the geography doing what it always did to him—peeling back the years, putting him twelve years old again on some other river, waiting in camp for her to come down from the ridge, learning early that the people you love best are the ones who leave.

But this time he was going up toward her. This time the leaving had been hers, and the arriving was his, and somewhere above the next bend, past the narrows where Cache 4 had told its beautiful lie, Dr. Halvard Iversen was alive or dead or worse—making decisions, keeping records, holding a truth in her hands that men with tire tracks and disciplined boots had already come once to bury.

He checked the map. Above the narrows, the gorge bent west and the survey marks stopped. Beyond that line, his mother's handwriting simply ended, mid-expedition, mid-sentence, the way a life ends.

"Past here," Ondine called forward, "we're off every map the institute has."

"Good," Emrys said, and meant it, and heard how much it cost him to mean it.

Behind them, faintly, over the water and the engine and the gorge's slow echo, Wexler's pen scratched on. Writing. Always writing.
