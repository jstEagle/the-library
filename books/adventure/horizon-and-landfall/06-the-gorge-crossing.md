## 6. The Gorge Crossing

The river told them before the sky did.

Emrys heard it first as a change in the boat's voice—the diesel note flattening, the hull riding a half-inch lower against water that had thickened overnight with silt from country they hadn't seen yet. He came up from the hold at first light and found Ondine already at the wheel, barefoot, a tin cup of black tea balanced on the binnacle, reading the current the way other people read letters from home.

"Khet's running early," she said, without turning. "Monsoon shifted north two days ago. All that rain up in the headwaters has to come down through us."

"How bad?"

She lifted her chin toward the gorge ahead—a seam of gray-green stone splitting the forest wall, so narrow in places it looked less like a river valley than a wound. Mist poured out of it slowly, like smoke from something smoldering deep inside the earth.

"Bad enough that we portage the upper narrows instead of running them," she said. "Bad enough that we do it today, not tomorrow." She sipped her tea. "The river doesn't negotiate, Iversen. It just announces terms."

Behind him, he heard Wexler come up onto the deck—those soft institute shoes on wet planking, the careful politeness of his footsteps. Emrys had learned to hear him the way you learn to hear a clock in a quiet room.

"Portage means how long?" Wexler asked.

"Depends on your back," Ondine said. "And what you're carrying."

"We're carrying people," Emrys said. "That's the cargo that matters."

Wexler looked at him for a moment too long, and Emrys watched him decide not to say whatever was behind his teeth. The observer smiled his thin, reasonable smile and went forward to check the lines, and Emrys stood at the rail watching the gorge open its mouth around them and thought: *everyone aboard is carrying something they haven't declared.*

Himself included.

* * *

Ondine took them into the lower gorge with the engine at idle and the current doing most of the work, because that was the only honest way to run water this fast. She'd been piloting the Khet since she was nineteen, since before the logging ban, since before the institute had a name worth knowing, and she read its moods the way scripture gets read—by people who believe every word is load-bearing.

The walls rose sheer on either side, fifty meters, eighty, streaked white where springs wept out of the rock. Ferns hung from cracks no wider than a hand. The air cooled ten degrees in the space of a hundred meters, and the sound changed—no longer wind and birdsong but the river's own voice, multiplied, reverberant, a congregation muttering in a stone cathedral.

She knew every bend by heart, but hearts change. That was the thing about rivers. You memorized a woman who was never the same twice.

The first sign was the driftwood. Not scattered—*stacked*, jammed eight feet up the rock face on the outside of the third bend, whole trunks rammed into crevices like spears left standing after a battle. She slowed and studied the stain line above it.

"Flood pulse already coming down," she said. "That wood was floating yesterday."

"How much time?" Emrys asked.

"Six hours to run the narrows clean. After that, the eddies fill and there's nowhere to catch water if we get pushed." She glanced back at him. "So we don't run them. We walk."

Wexler appeared at her shoulder. "Walk? Through there?" He gestured at the cliff walls with the mild incredulity of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a briefcase more than a kilometer.

"There's a porter trail on the east bank. Old trade route. It climbs over the worst of the narrows and comes back down above the confluence." She killed the engine and let the boat swing lazily in an eddy behind a gravel bar. "We winch the boat up the bar, unload everything, carry it over the shoulder, and re-launch below. Two trips. Maybe three."

"That could take all day."

"It will take all day," Ondine said pleasantly. "That's what *all day* is for."

Something moved behind Wexler's eyes—not annoyance exactly. Calculation. She'd seen it before, months ago, in a hotel bar in the capital, when a man with an institute business card slid a contract across the table and said, *We anticipate complications upstream. We want someone reliable on the water.* She had signed it because the money was obscene and because she'd assumed reliability meant engine work and discretion. She was beginning to suspect it meant something else, and she kept the contract folded in a waterproof bag inside her shirt, against her skin, where she could feel it like a splinter.

"Mr. Wexler," she said, "you can stay aboard if you like. The boat will be safe here until the pulse comes through. Then it won't be."

He looked up at the stacked driftwood, at the stain line, and for the first time since the capital she saw him understand something viscerally rather than administratively. It didn't make her trust him. But it made him human for about four seconds, which was four seconds more than before.

"I'll carry," he said.

* * *

The portage was brutal in the way Emrys had known it would be, the way portages always are: not dramatic, just relentless. The trail was a groove worn into the hillside by generations of feet, switchbacking up through dripping forest, roots slick as cable, leeches rising from the leaf litter with the enthusiasm of small congregations. They ferried crates, fuel drums, the dismantled canopy, rope, medical kit, radios—and each man and woman aboard made the crossing twice under loads that rearranged their understanding of their own bodies.

Nia Okonkwo carried like someone who'd grown up hauling water, steady and silent, her breathing measured, her eyes always moving—uphill, downhill, back along the trail. Emrys noticed that. He noticed that she never let Wexler get behind her on the switchbacks, and that she never seemed to notice she was doing it.

Halvard would have liked her, he thought, and then hated himself for the softness of the thought, and then, climbing, let it stay anyway.

At the top of the shoulder the forest opened onto a granite shelf, and the whole gorge lay below them—the river compressed to a white-green torrent between walls close enough to jump across in places, the sound of it enormous, physical, a pressure you felt in the sternum. Everyone stopped there without meaning to. Even Wexler.

"The expedition came through here," Emrys said quietly. "Three weeks ago. This exact trail."

"And three weeks ago it was dry," Ondine said beside him. She'd come up behind him with the last fuel drum strapped to a frame of saplings across her shoulders, and she set it down with a grunt. "Now look at it. Whatever your mother found up there, the river's about to have opinions about it."

"You keep saying *your mother* like that."

"Do I?" Ondine wiped her face with her forearm, leaving a brown smear. "Maybe because everyone else on this boat says *Dr. Iversen* like she's a building. A funding line. You say it like a scar."

Emrys didn't answer. Below them the river threw itself against the narrows and lost, and lost, and lost, and kept throwing itself.

* * *

They were two hours into the second carry when Emrys came back down the trail for the radio crate and found Wexler off the path.

Not far off—twenty meters, in a fold of the hillside where the granite shelf dipped and the forest closed overhead—but off it, crouched with his back to the trail, the expedition's shortwave handset in his hands. The main set was supposed to stay packed. Ondine had been explicit: transmit nothing from the gorge; the walls bounced signal in ways that couldn't be predicted and couldn't be explained.

Wexler wasn't transmitting coordinates. Emrys was close enough now, downwind, boots silent on wet duff, to hear the rhythm of what he was sending—short groups, numbers and letters, none of them place names. Code. And beneath the code, once, unmistakably, spoken low into the handset before the keyed sequence resumed:

"—confirm window holds. Nine days is acceptable. Tell them the party will be *receptive*."

Emrys stood very still. Water dripped somewhere behind him, patient as a metronome. A leech found his ankle and began its work unnoticed.

Nine days. Nobody had said nine days. Nobody had said anything about a window except the monsoon season itself, which spoke in weeks, not days. Which meant Wexler had a schedule from somewhere else—from the institute, from whoever had hired Ondine months ago expecting *exactly this outcome*, from people who had planned for a stranded expedition the way engineers plan for a bridge failure: precisely, in advance, with contingencies.

*Receptive.* The word sat in Emrys's chest like a swallowed stone. Receptive to what? Extraction? Instruction? Or to being handled—collected along with the site's contents, or collected *instead* of them?

Wexler finished his transmission, broke the set down with practiced efficiency, and turned back toward the trail—and stopped. Emrys watched him register the empty spot where the radio crate should have been, watched him do the arithmetic, watched him arrive at the answer and lift his head.

For a moment neither man moved. The river roared below them, filling the silence the way water fills a hull.

"You're back early," Wexler said.

"Took a wrong turn," Emrys said. "Easy to do. Country like this all looks the same unless you know it."

Something passed across Wexler's face—regret, maybe, or its administrative cousin. "We should get the last loads down," he said. "The captain says the water won't wait."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It won't."

They walked back down the trail together, single file, Wexler ahead, both of them smiling at Nia when they reached the landing, both of them lying with their posture, their pace, their ordinary hands. Emrys picked up his load and started uphill again, and the leech dropped off his ankle somewhere in the switchbacks, leaving a small bloodless ache he didn't feel until much later.

Above the narrows, the mist kept pouring out of the gorge, and the river kept announcing its terms, and nine days began counting down from somewhere none of them could see.

* * *

Ondine ran the re-launch at last light, when the pulse had crested and the narrows had settled from murder into menace. She took the loaded boat down the chute with the throttle barely breathing, letting the water carry them, correcting with two fingers on the wheel, and the crew held the gunwales and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the river wouldn't hear.

Below the confluence the valley opened and the current gentled, and she finally exhaled, and that was when Emrys came aft and stood beside her and said, without preamble:

"He transmitted from the shelf. Coded traffic. He mentioned a nine-day window and used the word *receptive*."

Ondine kept her eyes on the channel markers of her own memory—the submerged rock, the deadfall snag, the bend where the current lied. Her hand was perfectly steady on the wheel, which cost her something.

"Months ago," she said, "a man bought my boat for six weeks. Not charter. Bought. Contract said *reliable presence on the upper Khet during anticipated operational period*. I signed it because I'm greedy and stupid in roughly equal measure." She reached into her shirt and drew out the waterproof bag, and held it out to him without looking away from the water. "I've been trying to decide whose side I'm on ever since. Tonight I decided the side that isn't lying to me."

Emrys took the bag. Inside, the contract's language was exactly as sterile as she'd said, and exactly as damning.

"They're not coming to rescue anyone," he said.

"No," Ondine agreed. "Rescue is what they'll call it afterward." She fed the engine a little throttle, and the bow lifted, and the drowned valley unrolled ahead of them in the failing light, dark and patient and full of whatever Halvard Iversen had found. "Question is, Iversen—when they come, and they will come fast now—do we run, or do we make them wish they hadn't?"

Ahead, far upstream, thunder muttered over the headwaters like something clearing its throat.
