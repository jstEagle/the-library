## 7. Camp of the Living

The river told them before anyone did.

Ondine called it two bends out—smoke, thin and wrong, the gray of wet wood rather than dry. Emrys stood at the bow with the binoculars and watched it climb through the canopy in a column too disciplined to be a signal fire and too lazy to be anything else. His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself the way you notice a stranger's tic: with mild, detached disapproval.

"Camp's on the left bank," Ondine said. "High shelf above the flood line. Whoever built it knew what they were doing."

"My mother built it," Emrys said.

He heard Wexler shift behind him, canvas creaking against the gunwale. The observer had been quiet for most of the last day—not the silence of a man with nothing to say but of a man conserving questions like ammunition. Now he came forward, careful of his footing, and stood close enough that Emrys could smell the citric note of his sunscreen, absurdly civilized this far from anywhere.

"You said she was your mother," Wexler said. "Back at the charter office. You said it like a man reporting weather."

"I did."

"And now we're here."

"Now we're here," Emrys agreed, and put the binoculars down.

The camp resolved out of the green as they rounded the bend: three tents under a tarp fly, a drying rack stripped bare, a radio mast leaning at an angle that suggested either storm damage or neglect. A cook fire burned low. And along the near edge of the shelf, facing the water, someone had planted a line of survey stakes with strips of red cloth tied to them—a boundary, or a warning, legible to anyone who had ever worked a dig.

Emrys had worked digs since he was six years old. He read it as both.

"There," Ondine said quietly, and nodded toward the bank.

A woman stood among the stakes. She was smaller than Emrys remembered, or perhaps the river had grown around her, the landscape scaled up by ten years of absence until everything human looked diminished. Gray had taken her hair at the temples. She wore field trousers gone soft with washing and a shirt with the sleeves rolled past her forearms, and she stood with her weight even on both feet, arms loose, watching them come in with the patience of someone who had been watching for some time.

She didn't wave. She didn't call out. She simply stood there while Ondine brought the boat alongside the shelf with a boatman's delicacy, fending off with a pole, and while Wexler climbed out first with his hand extended and his smile already deployed, and while Emrys stepped onto the mud last because he could not make his legs agree to do it sooner.

"Dr. Iversen," Wexler said warmly. "Pell Wexler, Institute liaison. We were very concerned when the check-ins stopped."

"I imagine you were." Her voice was lower than Emrys carried it in memory, roughened by altitude and dust and whatever had happened up here in the weeks since the radios went quiet. She shook Wexler's hand once, briefly, the way you handle something you intend to set back down. Then she looked past him.

Ten years. Emrys had rehearsed this moment in more forms than he would ever admit—in the dark of a bunkhouse on the lower river, in the interval between salvage jobs, in the long gray hours when the current ran slow enough for thinking. In most versions he said something cutting. In a few he said nothing at all. In one humiliating version he apologized.

What actually happened was simpler and worse. She looked at him, and her face did the thing it had always done—the small recalibration behind the eyes, the assessment, the weighing of a specimen—and then settled into something almost gentle, which was somehow the same expression.

"You got my message," Halvard Iversen said.

"I didn't get any message."

"The cache at the second bend. The tire tracks." She turned toward the camp, speaking over her shoulder as though the conversation were already concluded, already filed. "I left the supply manifest pinned under the fuel drum. You'd know what a staged scene looked like. You've known since you were nine."

* * *

The camp interior told its own story, and it was not the story on the surface.

Three tents occupied, four empty with their flies tied shut. A mess table under the tarp with a map weighted down by stones. A medical cot screened off at the far end by a hanging sheet, and beside it, on a folding table, three sets of belongings arranged with terrible neatness: boots paired, glasses folded on top of a folded shirt, a wristwatch whose second hand still moved.

Emrys stopped in front of the table. He had seen death arranged before—river drownings laid out on planks, salvage clients' effects inventoried for insurance—but this was different. This was curated. Someone had made these objects into a sentence, and the sentence read *fever*, and every instinct he owned stood up and objected.

"Who are they?" he asked.

"Vasquez, Lindqvist, and Okonkwo's field partner, Adeyemi." Halvard came to stand beside him, not touching, keeping the distance of a meter the way she kept all distances. "Typhoid, we reported. It moves fast in the wet season. The regional health authority won't investigate; there's no authority within four hundred kilometers."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

Wexler had drifted toward the map table, hands clasped behind his back, reading the terrain with a professional's eye. Emrys watched him clock the empty tents, the stripped drying rack, the absence of half the team, and saw the observer arrive at the same conclusion Emrys had reached on the bank: *the rest moved inland.* Wexler said nothing. That silence had a texture too, and Emrys had spent ten years learning to read silences, mostly his own.

"How many went inland?" Emrys asked.

"Five. Nia Okonkwo, two students, our surveyor, and me—I come back every third day for the radio window." Halvard moved to the stove, poured water from a dented kettle into enamel mugs, and handed them out with the brisk economy of a woman feeding people whether they wanted feeding or not. The gesture hit Emrys somewhere below the ribs. Ten years, and her hands still moved the same way. "You'll want to see the site. Everyone wants to see the site."

"I want to know why three men are dead and their watches are still running."

Halvard paused with the kettle in her hand. Steam rose between them. For a moment—one held breath, one turn of the current below the bank—something moved through her face that was not the archaeologist, not the director, not the legend. Something tired and old and frightened, quickly filed away.

"Because the alternative was six dead instead of three," she said quietly. "And because the man who would have killed them is already in-country, and he reads scenes exactly the way you just did. Drink your tea, Emrys. We have three days of monsoon margin and a discovery that will rewrite the history of this coast, and you came all this way, so you may as well hear it."

* * *

They walked the site at first light, or what passed for light under the cloud deck—a bruised pewter ceiling pressing down through the canopy, the air thick enough to chew.

The path inland climbed through secondary growth, over ground that squelched and breathed. Halvard set a pace that ignored her age and dared comment. Emrys matched it and hated himself for matching it, for falling so easily into the old rhythm, the child pacing the parent, proving fitness, proving worth. Some things the body remembered even when the heart had signed the severance papers.

"You never answered my letters," she said, without turning.

"You never wrote any."

"I wrote eleven."

Emrys stumbled on a root. He recovered, kept walking, and said nothing, because the truth was he had received eleven letters and burned seven unread and read four at three in the morning in a series of rooms he could no longer picture, and answering any of them would have meant admitting he'd read them.

"They're in a box," Halvard continued, in the tone she used for cataloguing sherds. "In my office in Tromsø. If there's ever an office to go back to." She ducked under a fallen trunk and held it, an offer, and Emrys passed beneath without taking the help, and she let the trunk go without comment, and that exchange—help offered, help refused, neither party wounded—was the closest thing to forgiveness either of them had managed in a decade.

Then the trees opened, and Emrys forgot how to breathe.

The city lay in a fold of the valley like a shipwreck on a green sea. Terraces descended in long grassed steps toward a river channel now braided and shallow, and from the terraces rose walls—cut stone, fitted without mortar, draped in vines but unmistakably walls, streets, foundations, the articulated skeleton of a place where thousands of lives had been arranged. At its center, partially excavated, a compound of larger buildings exposed its ribs to the sky: doorways, thresholds, a plaza swept clean by Halvard's crew and floored, in one corner, with rows of opened niches like the drawers of an enormous cabinet.

"The administrative quarter," Halvard said. Her voice changed here—lowered, the way people speak in churches, or libraries. "Pre-colonial. Older than anything the textbooks allow. And those niches—" she pointed with her chin, "—were archives. Sealed clay cylinders, wax plugs, stored dry above the flood line. Four hundred of them recovered. More than a thousand estimated."

"What's in them?"

"Records. Tribute rolls, court proceedings, grain ledgers." She began walking down the terrace steps, and Emrys followed because there was nowhere else in the world to be. "And the last cylinder in the last niche we opened, Emrys, is a deposition. A survivor's account, witnessed and sealed, naming the ships that came, the trade factors who directed them, and the men who financed the factors." She stopped at the bottom and faced him. "Names, Emrys. Dates. Signatures pressed into clay four hundred years ago—and the same names appear in the founding charter of the institute that funded this expedition. The institute that has been trying to bury this valley since before I ever applied for a grant."

The rain arrived then, as if summoned—a soft preliminary hiss in the canopy, gathering itself. Somewhere upslope, thunder cleared its throat.

"Why do you think the check-ins stopped?" Halvard said over the rising sound. "Why do you think three good men are lying under a sheet pretending to be dead? They sent someone, Emrys. Before you. Before Wexler. And when the money can't buy silence, it buys something else, and I have seen what it bought at the second bend." She gripped his forearm—her first touch in ten years, brief and hard as a knot being tied. "I am going to publish this before landfall. Whatever it costs. I need to know, right now, standing on their bones, whether you're going to help me or stand in the doorway like you did at every other crossroads of your life."

The rain came down in earnest. Emrys stood in it, on the stones of a murdered city, looking at the face he had spent ten years outrunning and understood suddenly that he had never been running from her at all. He had been running from this exact question, asked in this exact voice, and it had followed him down every mile of river he'd ever worked.

"I found your cache," he said. "I read the manifest. I'm here."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I've got until I see the cylinders myself."

Something flickered across her face—disappointment, or its twin, respect. She turned back toward the excavation, pulling her hood up, and over her shoulder she said the words that rearranged the entire shape of his journey:

"Then come meet Nia Okonkwo. Because she's the one who staged those deaths—and she's been waiting five days to tell someone why she's afraid we're all next."
