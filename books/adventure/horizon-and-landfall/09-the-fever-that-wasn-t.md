## 9. The Fever That Wasn't

Nia Okonkwo had buried three men in four days, and none of them were dead.

That was the sentence she kept turning over in her mind as she knelt at the edge of the burial ground behind the mess tent, her hands in the wet earth, laying the last of the river stones over the shallow graves. The ground here was too damp for proper graves and too full of roots for dignity, so they had done what the staging required—shallow, hasty, marked with stones that any passing survey team would read as exactly what they were meant to read: fever victims, buried fast by a frightened crew before the sickness could spread.

Except there was no sickness.

She pressed a stone into place and let herself feel the weight of what she'd done. Not the idea of it—she'd been living inside the idea of it for days now, wearing it like a coat—but the weight of it, the actual physical fact of three men she had known for two years, whose coffee orders she knew, whose terrible jokes she'd laughed at, lying under rocks she had placed with her own hands while their pulses ticked away somewhere inland, faint but stubborn, in the care of Dr. Anselm's improvised field clinic.

*None of them are dead.* And still it felt like murder. It was supposed to feel like murder. That was the whole point.

"Nia."

She didn't turn. She knew the voice—it belonged to the son, the one who'd come upriver like an answer to a question nobody had asked aloud. Emrys Iversen. He'd arrived two days ago with the boat pilot and the institute man, Wexler, and since then he had moved through camp the way water moved through limestone: quietly, patiently, finding every crack.

"You're up early," she said, not looking at him.

"So are you." He crouched beside her at the grave line. He didn't reach for the stones or offer to help. He just looked—at the spacing of them, the depth of the disturbed soil, the careful arrangement of the markers—and Nia felt each of his observations land on her skin like flies.

"You did good work," he said finally.

Her stomach dropped. "I don't know what you mean."

"The graves." His voice was level, almost gentle. "The spacing is right. The soil disturbance reads honest. Whoever taught you field methods taught you well." He paused. "It's the effects that bother me."

*The effects.* She kept her hands moving, setting another stone, because if she stopped moving she would have to look at him, and if she looked at him she would have to decide, right now, in this gray hour before the camp woke, whether to keep lying to a man who had come all this way to save people who were pretending not to need saving.

"What about the effects?" she asked, and hated how carefully casual her voice came out.

"Personal kits get burned after death from hemorrhagic fever," Emrys said. "Standard protocol. Nobody wants the contamination." He turned a small object over in his palm—a tin whistle, dented, the kind sold in river towns to tourists and given by fathers to sons. "But you burned the documents and left the personal things. Photographs. Letters. This." He held the whistle out to her. "Fever crews don't grieve that way. Fever crews are terrified. They burn everything."

Nia looked at the whistle and thought of Tomas Ferreira playing it badly on the survey barge, off-key, delighted with himself, his daughter's name scratched into the metal. She thought of how she had packed it into the effects bag with her own hands, deciding that some things should survive even a lie, and understood that this—this single act of sentiment, this refusal to let one small true thing be destroyed—was how the whole architecture would come down.

"You counted the graves," she said.

"I counted everything." He set the whistle down on the grass between them, gently, like returning something borrowed. "Three graves. Three names in the log, dated across five days. But the log entries are in your handwriting, and the dates don't match the deterioration of the ink, and the second entry describes symptoms in an order no clinician would write them." He exhaled. "I'm not the institute, Nia. Whatever you think I am."

"I think you came upriver with Pell Wexler," she said. "I think Wexler carries institute credentials and institute money and asks questions like a man taking inventory. And I think whoever sent him wants this expedition closed the way the last one was closed."

Something moved through his face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. "The last one?"

And there it was. The crack in the story she'd been holding shut with both hands. She should have stood up, brushed off her knees, walked back to her tent, and said nothing further. Instead she heard herself say:

"You really don't know why your mother stopped publishing for six years? You really came all this way and never asked?"

* * *

They walked down to the river because Nia couldn't have the conversation in camp. Walls in a dig site were made of canvas; everything traveled. The Khet ran high and brown with early monsoon silt, dragging whole trees along in its current like it was collecting them, and the sound of it was loud enough to swallow words if they stood close enough to the bank. She chose a fallen fig tree downstream of the tents, where the water noise made eavesdropping a physical impossibility.

Emrys sat on the trunk and waited. That was the thing about him she hadn't expected—everyone else who'd ever asked her questions had leaned forward, hungry, insistent. He leaned back. He let silence do the work. He had his mother's patience and none of her appetite, and Nia found that more frightening than either.

"Halvard lost a crew member in 'nineteen," Nia began, because she had decided somewhere between the graves and the river that the truth was the only cargo left worth carrying. "Rafael Osei. A botanist. Good man. The institute's advance officer at the time—a man named Corben Hale—told her the funding renewal depended on discretion regarding certain findings near the old administrative quarter. Findings that implicated the institute's founding donors in the massacre of 1911." She watched Emrys's hands go still on his knees. "Halvard refused. Quietly. She just... slowed down. Sat on the material. And three weeks later Rafael went into the gorge on a solo collection run and never came back. They found his pack on a gravel bar. No body. The official report said flash flood."

"You think it wasn't."

"I think the flood season was six weeks past," Nia said. "I think Halvard believed it wasn't, and she pulled us all out of the country within a month, and she spent six years building this expedition so carefully, so slowly, with such paranoid redundancy, that everyone at the institute assumed she'd gone timid." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "She wasn't timid. She was baiting a trap. And when we opened the sealed records vault in March and found the correspondence—the actual letters, signed, dated, naming names—Hale's successor was already in-country. We knew within days. There was a man asking questions in the delta towns. Buying information. Showing photographs."

"So the fever."

"So the fever." Nia wrapped her arms around her knees. "Wexler's arrival was the signal. We knew what it meant—an observer shows up right after the discovery, and the next thing that happens is either the evidence disappears or the witnesses do. Sometimes both. So we gave the institute its disaster instead. Three crew members dead of fever. Camp half-abandoned, rest of the team moved inland to the site—which is true, they are inland, they're guarding the records with Anselm and the two strongest porters. And the dead men—" she laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, "the dead men are alive, Mr. Iversen. Tomas has a bad shoulder from a fall on the survey grid. Rafael's cousin Deshi twisted an ankle. Marcus is fine, Marcus is bored out of his mind lying in a hammock pretending to convalesce. We dosed them with antimalarials to explain the lethargy, marked the graves, burned the lab reports, and waited to see what the institute's man would do with a dead crew."

"And what has he done?"

"He hasn't touched the graves," Nia said. "He hasn't asked to see the bodies or the medical records or the burial sites. Do you understand? A man who believed three of his colleagues' employees had died of hemorrhagic fever would do *something*. Grieve. Investigate. Evacuate the healthy staff out of contagion range. Wexler has done none of it. He checks the satellite phone schedule and asks about the records inventory and smiles at everyone." She looked at Emrys directly for the first time since the graves. "He knows they're not dead. Which means he knows it's theater. Which means someone told him—or he already knew the play, because he's seen it performed before."

The river took a floating log and rolled it under, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"My mother knows," Emrys said at last. It wasn't a question.

"Halvard designed it." Nia heard the defensiveness rise in her own voice and couldn't stop it. "Someone had to. You weren't here. None of you were here. When Wexler's confirmation came through, we had forty-eight hours to decide, and Halvard stood in the map tent and said, *they expect us to run or die, so we will do neither—we will be already dead.* And it worked. It bought us time. It got the originals moved inland, it got the duplicates copied onto microfilm and distributed among four people who don't travel together, it got us breathing room." She unfolded herself and stood, because sitting felt too much like surrender. "But it cost—I know what it cost. I wrote those log entries. I dug those graves. I put Tomas's daughter's photograph in the effects bag knowing his wife would see it and believe—" Her breath hitched. "I call her every month from the delta. I've known that woman eight years. And I built her a coffin out of lies and put her husband in it, and he isn't even dead, and somehow that's worse. Somehow that's so much worse than if he were."

Emrys rose too. He didn't touch her shoulder, which she was grateful for; she would have come apart.

"Why tell me?" he asked. "You've held this together for weeks. One more week and landfall closes the river and everyone's story locks into place. Why hand me the thread that unravels it?"

Because, she wanted to say, because you looked at a tin whistle like it was a person. Because you counted ink deterioration on forged log entries at five in the morning instead of sleeping. Because Wexler smiled at me over breakfast and asked how I was holding up after losing so many colleagues, and his concern was so perfectly calibrated that I understood, finally, that we hadn't fooled him at all—we had only confirmed which of us he considered disposable.

What she said was: "Because the plan has a hole in it, and the hole is shaped like you."

"Me."

"Halvard's arithmetic only works if the downriver evacuation is genuine. Someone has to take the wounded—the fake wounded, the real risk—out ahead of landfall, past whatever Wexler arranges, carrying proof that the fever was staged. Because the moment those three men surface alive anywhere south of the gorge, the institute's version dies. You can't claim a cover-up killed them if they're giving depositions in the delta." She hugged her elbows against the river damp. "But Wexler will never allow that boat to leave with passengers he believes are dead. Dead men don't evacuate. The moment he sees Tomas Ferreira boarding a boat, the theater collapses and we're four liars alone in the gorge with a man who has a radio and institutional backing and, I am increasingly sure, a schedule."

"So you need me to figure out what Wexler's actually doing."

"I need you to figure out what Wexler's actually doing," Nia agreed, "before he does it. And I needed you to hear it from me first. Not from Halvard's version, not from the map tent, not from the legend of the woman who out-thinks everyone. From the person who dug the holes." Her eyes stung and she let them. "I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to know that I'll carry it. All of it. Even the parts that work."

Downstream, a fish hawk dropped out of the gray sky and hit the water and came up with nothing, and tried again.

"There's one more thing," Emrys said quietly. "Last night. Wexler was on the sat phone after midnight. I caught maybe ten seconds of it before he saw me. He wasn't talking to the institute's regional office—he used a different cadence, different language. And the one phrase I got clearly was *confirm the gorge party before the window closes.*"

Nia felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth. *The gorge party.* Not the site. Not the records. The *party*—the people. Wexler wasn't reporting on artifacts. He was confirming the location of human beings, and there was a window, and windows close.

"That's not an observer," she said.

"No," Emrys agreed. "That's not an observer."

Above the camp, the first real light of morning came over the ridge and lit the brown river gold, and Nia thought about the three men lying in hammocks inland, alive because of her, and about how many hours might be left in the lie that was keeping them that way.

"We have to move them," she said. "Tonight. Before Wexler's window closes."

* * *

They spent the day performing normalcy, and Nia learned that this was its own kind of labor—smiling at Wexler over rice and dried fish while her mind ran evacuation math; logging sediment samples she had no intention of finishing; watching Halvard across the map tent and receiving, in return, a single long glance that said *you told him* without a word being spoken. Guilt, Nia discovered, was not a weight you could set down once confessed. It simply changed shape. It became vigilance. It became the habit of counting exits.

At dusk she went to the supply tent where the "medical stores" were kept and began, methodically, to repack the stretcher rolls—turning props back into equipment, un-killing the dead one strap at a time. Outside, the river ran high and dark toward the lowlands, carrying its collected trees, rising by inches every day, patient as anything the institute had ever sent.

Tomorrow night, she promised the empty tent, the three of them would be on the water. Alive, visible, undeniable.

She just had to make sure the boat left before anyone confirmed the gorge party.

She heard footsteps behind her and turned, expecting Emrys.

It was Wexler, standing in the tent flap with a lantern, smiling his calibrated smile.

"I hope I'm not interrupting," he said. "Dr. Iversen would like to see you both. Tonight." He stepped aside to let the lamplight fall across the repacked stretchers, and his eyes—polite, unhurried, absolutely awake—moved over them once, like a man taking inventory.
