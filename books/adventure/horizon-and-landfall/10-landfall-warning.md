## 10. Landfall Warning

The rain came in the night the way bad news always came to Emrys Iversen—not all at once but in stages, each one worse than the last. First a hiss on the canvas of the base camp's mess tent, so soft he mistook it for insects. Then a drumming. Then a sound like the whole sky leaning its weight against the ridge above them, and water running through camp in bright braided threads that hadn't existed an hour before.

He stood at the tent flap with a mug of tea going cold in his hand and watched Nia Okonkwo wade past with her arms full of equipment cases, shouting something to the two remaining team members whose names he was still learning. The camp had the look of a body deciding whether to be sick—everything in motion, nothing yet panicked.

Ondine Rask appeared beside him, uninvited as ever, her hair plastered flat and her eyes on the river.

"That's not monsoon rain," she said. "Not yet. This is the overture."

"You can tell that from standing in it?"

"I can tell it from thirty years of standing in it." She tilted her chin toward the gorge downstream, invisible now behind the gray curtain. "The Khet's already up two meters since yesterday. I checked the gauge stake this morning while you were all playing archaeologist. When the real rain arrives—and it will arrive inside ten days, maybe less—that river becomes a brown animal the size of a country. Nothing goes down it. Nothing comes up. You'll be wintering here, if you want to call it winter."

Emrys drank the cold tea anyway. Nine days, then. Maybe eight. He did the arithmetic without wanting to: nine days to move eleven people, an archive of sealed clay tablets and paper records too fragile to get wet, and whatever remained of his mother's certainty, down through a gorge that was already eating its own banks.

Behind him, inside the tent, Pell Wexler cleared his throat.

* * *

Wexler had claimed the camp's only table, spreading a satellite weather printout across it with the crisp economy of a man who had done this briefing in a dozen countries. He'd changed into dry clothes. That was the thing about Wexler that Emrys kept forgetting and kept relearning—the man was never wet, never tired, never ruffled. He arrived at every disaster pre-ironed.

"Satellite pass came through at oh-four-hundred," Wexler said, tapping the printout where a great white spiral sat off the coast like a thumbprint on glass. "The system has strengthened and shifted north-northwest. Previous track put landfall three hundred kilometers south of us. New track puts it—" he moved his finger along a dotted line, "—here. Directly into the upper basin. Forty-eight hours of sustained rainfall, then the secondary systems stacked behind it."

Halvard stood at the far end of the table with her arms crossed, studying the map the way she studied everything—as though it might yield if she stared hard enough. She looked thinner than she had a week ago, Emrys thought. The expedition had been eating its reserves.

"How long," Halvard said.

"To impassable? Based on Ondine's read of the gauge and the catchment above the gorge, I'd say nine days. Call it a margin of error of two." Wexler straightened. "Which is why I'm proposing we move now. Tonight, if we can load in time. Everyone downriver together, before the window closes."

"And the archive?" Halvard asked, in the tone of a woman who already knew the answer and wanted to hear him say it.

"The archive stays." Wexler said it gently, which was somehow worse. "Dr. Iversen—with respect, you've spent six weeks excavating a site that took four hundred years to bury itself. It survived the last hundred monsoons. It will survive this one. We come back after the season, with a proper recovery team, conservation equipment, the full institutional apparatus. What you cannot do is carry three hundred kilograms of fragile ceramic and paper through Class Five water in an open boat. You'll lose the people or you'll lose the records, and if you try to save both you'll lose everything."

Emrys watched his mother's face. He knew every version of it—the lecture face, the triumph face, the face she wore when a trench wall collapsed and she stood in the rubble calculating what could be salvaged. This was none of those. This was stillness, total and deliberate, the stillness of someone listening to a sentence she had been expecting for years.

"A proper recovery team," she repeated.

"Institute personnel. Trained conservators. Security, obviously—the site's significance makes it vulnerable."

"Security." Halvard smiled, and it was not a warm expression. "You mean men with guns, Mr. Wexler. The same caliber of men, I'd imagine, who burned the administrative quarter the first time around."

The tent went quiet except for the rain. Nia froze halfway through setting down a case. One of the graduate students—a boy called Tomas with a bandaged hand—looked between them like a spectator at a match he didn't understand.

Wexler didn't flinch. That was the most frightening thing about him, Emrys decided. Most men, accused of complicity in a century-old massacre cover-up, would at least have the decency to look wounded. Wexler simply folded his hands.

"Dr. Iversen. I have flown twelve thousand kilometers to sit in a leaking tent and negotiate with you in person because my institution believes—sincerely—that your discovery deserves protection. I have eaten your rice. I have carried your crates at the portage. I have not once suggested we abandon the site permanently. I am suggesting we sequence the operation. People first. Records second. That is not a trap. That is logistics."

"It's a very old word wearing new clothes," Halvard said. "My grandfather heard it from men in better suits."

Emrys set down his mug. "I want to see the gauge myself," he said, to no one in particular, and walked out into the rain.

* * *

The river had come up overnight. He could see it without the gauge stake—could see it in the way the current had gone from green to the color of milk coffee, in the deadfall spinning past at walking speed, whole trees traveling upright like drowned men walking home. The bank where they'd moored the boat yesterday was underwater. Ondine had moved her lines twice in the night; he could see the fresh rope-marks in the mud above the new waterline.

She was crouched at the stake when he reached it, reading the notches, her lips moving slightly.

"Well?" he said.

"Two point four meters since yesterday morning. Rising faster than the gauge suggests, which means the tributaries upstream are already in spate." She looked up at him, rain streaming off her jaw. "You want the honest number?"

"I always want the honest number. It's the rest of my family that deals in fiction."

"Seven days. Eight if the system stalls over the coast, and it won't." She stood, knees cracking. "And here's the part your professor friend won't put on his chart. It's not just the flood. It's what the flood does to the gorge. The Khet narrows to forty meters through the Khet Gorge. When this much water hits that constriction, the rapids don't get bigger—they stop being rapids. They become a single hydraulic event. A hole the length of a football field with a recirculation you couldn't swim out of with engines. No pilot runs that. Not me, not anyone."

"So the window is real."

"The window is real. Which means whoever controls the boat controls everything, for about seven days." Ondine wiped her face with the heel of her hand and gave him a long, level look. "You've figured out what he's doing, haven't you."

It wasn't a question, so Emrys didn't answer it. But he turned the shape of it over in his mind as they walked back, and the shape was simple enough to fit in a child's fist: evacuate everyone immediately, leave the records for a proper recovery team, and by the time any proper recovery team could plausibly return, the site would have been visited by men whose training was not in conservation. The flood would take the blame. Rivers made excellent accomplices; they left no fingerprints, only silt.

Or—less charitable, more precise—Wexler himself might not know the details. He might be a man carrying orders he hadn't fully read, executing them with the clean conscience of someone who believed institutions meant well even when their hands came back dirty. Emrys had met that kind before. They were harder to hate and more dangerous to trust.

At the edge of camp he stopped. Wexler was at the radio tent, alone, the handset close to his mouth. Emrys couldn't hear the words over the rain, but he could see the rhythm of transmission—short bursts, pauses, short bursts again. Not conversation. Relay. Someone reading numbers to someone else.

Ondine saw it too. Her hand found Emrys's sleeve—not gripping, just resting there, a pilot's touch, testing which way the wind lay.

"Your mother's going to refuse him," she murmured. "You know that."

"Yes."

"And when she refuses, he doesn't need her permission. He needs the boat." She let go of his sleeve. "Think about that tonight. Sleep with your boots on."

* * *

That evening the camp ate under the mess fly because the mess tent had begun to collect water in its roof like a cupped hand. Rice, dried fish, a sauce Nia had improvised from tamarind and something sharp Emrys couldn't identify. The team ate quickly and quietly, the way people eat when they've started counting meals.

Halvard held court, insofar as a woman holding a tin plate can hold court. She was explaining the administrative quarter to Tomas again—the seal impressions, the tally sticks, the ledger tablets that named, in a dead language and a living shame, the men who had signed the order a century ago. Her voice had the brightness it got when she was close to the center of a thing. Emrys remembered it from childhood digs, from the passenger seat of trucks, from a hundred campfires where she talked past him to whoever would listen. He had spent ten years telling himself he resented that voice. Sitting across the fire from it now, he found the resentment had worn down to something else—something closer to recognition. She wasn't performing. She was alight. Some people are, near the end of a long fuse.

Wexler joined them late, ate efficiently, and then made his proposal again, publicly this time, which Emrys understood was the point. Make the reasonable argument in front of witnesses. Let the unreasonable refusal belong to her.

"We lift at first light," Wexler said. "Everyone. The boat takes eleven people in two runs to the lower staging ground, where the institute has a helicopter arranged—I arranged it before I left, anticipating exactly this contingency. From there, everyone is safe within seventy-two hours. Dr. Iversen, I'm asking you, in front of your team, because they deserve to hear both options stated plainly."

"Both options," Halvard said. "You've stated one option twice."

"The other option is drowning."

"The other option," Halvard said, setting down her plate, "is that these people spent six weeks digging in the rain because they believed the work mattered. And you're asking them to walk away from the only proof that the men who funded their dig murdered the ancestors of everyone in the valley below us. So let me state it plainly, Mr. Wexler, in front of my team: I am not leaving the archive. If the institute wants it, the institute can send its proper recovery team through the flood like everyone else."

Nia's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Tomas looked at the fire. The fourth team member, a surveyor named Anselm, quietly began packing his bowl.

Wexler nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something on a form. "Then I'm obligated to note for the record that the decision to endanger your team rests with you, and that the institute offered extraction for all personnel." He rose, brushed rice from his trousers, and paused at the edge of the firelight. "For what it's worth, Dr. Iversen, I think you're wrong. But I've been wrong before, in weather like this, and the river doesn't care which of us it proves right."

He walked toward the radio tent. Emrys watched him go and felt the trap settle into focus at last—not hidden anymore, not even pretending well, just dressed in rescue clothes and waiting to be walked into. Evacuate the witnesses. Flood the evidence. Blame the monsoon, which after all was nobody's fault.

Nia caught his eye across the fire. She glanced once, minutely, toward the dark line of the storage trench, then back at him. *We should talk*, the glance said. *Before he does.*

Emrys looked at his mother, silhouetted against the flames, already talking again, already brighter, the fuse burning down toward whatever she had decided to be true. Seven days. One boat. Eleven lives and three hundred kilos of proof, and somewhere in the dark a man sending numbers downriver to people Emrys had never met.

He slept with his boots on.
