## 11. Mother's Arithmetic

The rain came at dusk, as it had every evening for three days, and with it the smell of the river changing—mud lifting off the bottom, green rot rising from the banks, the whole waterway turning from a road into a rumor of what it would become. Emrys stood under the tarp strung between two dead trees at the edge of base camp and watched Halvard count.

She counted everything now. Tins of fuel. Cases of records wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed, stacked like cordwood in the excavated administrative hall a kilometer inland. Days on the calendar she'd drawn in grease pencil on the side of the supply crate, each one crossed out with a stroke so hard it dented the wood. Nine strokes now. Eight after tonight.

She counted her people last, always last, and Emrys understood that too. He'd watched her do it across thirty years of digs—supplies first, then specimens, then staff, and somewhere far down the list, past the horizon of her attention, herself.

Tonight she finished counting and looked up and found him watching, and instead of pretending she hadn't, she beckoned him over with two fingers, the way she used to summon him to hold the plumb line when he was nine years old and wanted nothing more than to be anywhere else.

"Sit," she said. "I need your arithmetic."

"I'm told mine's unreliable."

"Your arithmetic is excellent. It's your conclusions I've never trusted." She smoothed the grease-pencil calendar flat against the crate with the heel of her hand. Her knuckles were split from the digging, the nails black-rimmed, and there was a tremor in the fingers he chose not to name yet. "Sit down, Emrys."

He sat on an ammunition case that had held, until last week, someone's idea of emergency rations. The camp behind them was quiet in the way camps get quiet when everyone is listening through canvas walls. Nia Okonkwo's lamp still burned in the mess tent; he could see her shadow moving, cataloguing something, or maybe just unable to sleep. Ondine Rask was down at the boat, running her nightly ritual of checking lines and bilges, talking to the engine in a low voice that wasn't quite prayer.

"Say it," Emrys said. "Whatever you've been building up to since we found you. Say it while the rain covers us."

Halvard almost smiled. In the lamplight spilling from the mess tent her face was all planes and weathering, the same face from a hundred photographs in a hundred journals, except here it was unposed and tired and real. "You've been waiting for me to say it."

"You've been waiting for me to ask."

"We're worse than the river," she said. "It changes course openly."

* * *

She laid it out the way she laid out a trench plan, with objects: a stone for the boat, a stick for the trail, a shell casing for Wexler, who sat apart in his own tent with his own silence and his own radio he no longer pretended wasn't there.

"The site is four hours' walk inland," she said. "The records weigh—" she paused, recalculating, "—two hundred and ten kilograms, packed. The archive cases alone are ninety. The trail is passable now. In five days it won't be. The lowland crossing turns to standing water by day six, and by landfall it's a lake with a current."

"And the boat?"

"The boat takes eleven people. We have eleven people." She said it flatly, but he heard the arithmetic underneath, the one she hadn't spoken yet, the one she'd been carrying around like a stone in her boot. "Eleven people and zero kilograms of archive."

"There's the second boat."

"There is no second boat. There's Ondine's contract and whatever game the person who wrote it is playing, and I don't gamble my people on other people's games." She turned the stick over in her fingers. "So. Two hundred and ten kilograms of proof that the men who funded this dig murdered a city to protect their founders' names—or eleven living people who can testify to what they saw before the fever that wasn't a fever. The river will carry one or the other. Not both. Not in time."

Emrys watched the rain come down harder, drumming the tarp, filling the silences she left the way it filled the wheel ruts on the trail.

"You've already decided," he said.

"I've already calculated. Deciding is what I'm asking you to help me with, which should tell you how much I've changed." She set the stick down. "Take them down, Emrys. All of them. Nia, the students, the wounded, Wexler if he'll go and trussed if he won't. Get them below the gorge before landfall and put them in front of cameras, lawyers, anyone with a microphone. Testimony travels where crates sink. I taught you that."

"You didn't teach me anything about testimony. You taught me stratigraphy." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Everything else I learned watching what you did instead of saying it."

"Then watch this." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the gray coming in at her temples, more of it than the photographs showed, more than he remembered from even a year ago, though a year ago he hadn't been looking. "I stay with the archive. I take it overland to the mission station, transmit what I can, and wait out the monsoon with Father Anselm's people if the transmitter fails. The villagers on the ridge know the trails better than any of us. I'll be slow, not lost."

"Slow," Emrys repeated. "That's the word you're choosing."

"It's the honest one."

"It's the one you use on grant applications when a site is about to be destroyed and you want another season." He stood, because sitting next to her arithmetic made his skin crawl. "You're staying because you think the records are worth more than you are. That's not calculation, Mother. That's the same ledger you've kept your whole life, where everyone else's weight comes first and yours rounds to nothing."

For a moment she said nothing, and the rain filled it, and somewhere down the slope Ondine's engine coughed once and settled.

"You think I don't know what I cost you?" Halvard said quietly. "Ten years, Emrys. Ten years of you making my expeditions possible and my name famous and my coffee cold because I never once looked up from the trench and said the only sentence you were waiting for. And now you think this is more of the same. Me choosing the work over the person standing in front of me." She rose too, slowly, her knee protesting. "But it isn't. That's what you can't see yet. This time the work *is* the person. Every one of those names in those records was somebody's daughter standing in front of somebody who wouldn't look up. If I leave them in the ground unspoken, I become exactly the thing I dug them out of the earth to indict."

"That's conviction. It's not a plan."

"It's both. Conviction without a plan is martyrdom. I have porters, trails, dates, contingencies. What I don't have—" and here her voice did something he had never heard it do, cracked, just slightly, like ice under load, "—is time enough to save everything. So I am asking my son, the best riverman on this water, to take the living down and let me carry the dead."

"No."

* * *

The word landed between them and neither moved to pick it up.

"No," he said again, steadier. "Not because I don't understand the math. Because the math is wrong. You built it backwards."

"Explain."

"You start from the archive because the archive is what you love. Start from the people. Eleven people, one boat, one pilot who can run water nobody else would attempt, and—" he held up a finger, "—one son who has spent ten years learning every smuggling trick on this river because he was running away from you. Two hundred and ten kilograms is nothing. I've moved stolen engines heavier than that through checkpoints that would make Wexler cry. Duplicate the records. Split the cargo. Oilcloth, wax seal, false bottoms in the fuel cases. Send copies down with the team and keep the originals moving overland with you—but you come *out*, Mother. Overland, mission station, transmitter, and then downriver on the far side of landfall with the rest of us. Nobody stays. Nobody is left as collateral."

Halvard stared at him, and he watched her do it—the recalculation, the fast silent working of a mind that had spent fifty years weighing soil and time—and he saw the moment she found the flaw she was looking for and hated it.

"The originals stay with me," she said. "If the copies are intercepted—"

"Then the originals survive. That's the point of duplicates. But if the originals are lost and the copies survive, the truth survives anyway. That's the whole reason you taught yourself archival redundancy, and don't pretend you didn't, I read your methodology chapter twice out of spite."

Something moved through her face then that he couldn't name—pride, maybe, arriving a decade late and unwelcome, or grief for all the years they might have argued like this and hadn't. She looked away first. He couldn't remember her ever looking away first.

"You'd risk the boat," she said. "Wexler will report the cargo. The institute will have eyes below the gorge."

"Wexler is a problem for tomorrow."

"He's a problem for tonight. He heard none of this, which means he suspects all of it."

"Then we give him something to suspect instead." Emrys crouched and began restacking her stones and sticks into a new arrangement, the split-cargo plan taking shape under his hands: boat here, trail there, the duplicate cases marked with chalk, the originals in plain view where a watcher would expect them. "Let him see us loading the archive for the boat. Let him radio his masters that we're doing exactly what they fear. Then at the last hour we swap. He'll have committed to a story, and men like Wexler don't rewrite their reports—they defend them."

"That's devious."

"That's salvage. Same skill set."

Halvard laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut—and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes. When she lowered it, she was, for the first time since the cache, simply his mother: tired, frightened, and glad beyond words to be argued with.

"When did you learn to out-plan me?"

"About year six of not speaking to you. Anger's a good teacher."

They stood together in the rain-shadow of the tarp, the new plan lying between them in stones and sticks, fragile and complete. Down the slope, Nia's lamp went out. Ondine's engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere inland, thunder rolled along the ridge line like furniture being dragged across the sky, and the river answered with a low, gathering note that Emrys felt through his boots.

"Nine days," Halvard said.

"Eight now." He glanced at the grease-pencil calendar. "And one more thing. You cross that lowland crossing by day five or I come back up for you after landfall, and I promise you, Mother, I will find a way, and it will be stupid and expensive and probably fatal, so don't make me."

"Was that affection?"

"That was arithmetic."

She touched his arm—briefly, awkwardly, the gesture of a woman relearning a language she'd let rust—and turned toward her tent. At the flap she stopped, her back to him, rain streaming off the canvas above her head.

"Emrys. The sentence you were waiting for." She didn't turn around. "I should have said it ten years ago, and I should say it now, and I will—when we're all below the gorge. Consider it cargo. I don't ship anything before it's packed properly."

She went inside.

Emrys stood a long moment in the drumming dark, then walked down toward the boat to tell Ondine they'd need false bottoms in the fuel cases by morning—and found her already waiting at the rail, a lantern in her hand and a folded paper in the other, her face unreadable as deep water.

"You'd better hear this," Ondine said. "Before Wexler does."
