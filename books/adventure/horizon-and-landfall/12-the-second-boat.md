## 12. The Second Boat

Ondine Rask had never trusted a river that ran quiet.

The Khet above the gorge was all quiet now—wide, brown, patient, carrying its burden of silt toward a sea it would never reach before the monsoon swallowed the delta whole. She stood at the wheel of the *Maren Voss* with one hand on the brass and the other holding a cigarette she wasn't smoking, and she watched the water the way other people watched faces, reading the small lies in it. A log turning wrong. A seam of current folding where no fold should be. Somewhere upstream, in a camp she could not see, a woman was doing arithmetic with her own life, and a boy who wasn't a boy anymore was refusing the answer.

Ondine had done her own arithmetic months ago, in an office that smelled of carpet cleaner and old money, and she had not liked the sum.

She flicked the cigarette into the river and went below.

* * *

The contract lived in the bilge locker, inside a waterproof pouch, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath a false bottom she'd built herself with a chisel and a bad attitude. She had put it there the way you bury a body you're not sure is dead—deep enough to forget, shallow enough to reach.

She hauled it out and sat on the engine housing with the pouch across her knees. The diesel ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above, Emrys Iversen was arguing with his mother in low, clipped voices that carried over the water the way arguments do when both people are trying not to be overheard. Ondine had heard enough of those in her life to know they were the ones that mattered.

The oilcloth came away in strips. The pouch gave up its secret with a soft exhale of trapped river air.

The paper inside was good paper. That was the first thing she'd noticed, back in that carpeted office, when the man slid it across the desk—good paper, heavy stock, the kind that costs more per sheet than her first boat's entire fuel budget. The institute did not believe in doing anything cheaply, including betrayal.

*MEMORANDUM OF ENGAGEMENT — VESSEL AND PILOTAGE SERVICES*, the header said. Below it, in language so careful it practically apologized for itself: *The Institute may, at its sole discretion, require the Contractor to render assistance in the recovery of personnel and materials from the Upper Khet region, including but not limited to the conveyance of said personnel and materials to locations specified by the Institute's representative.*

Recovery. Conveyance. Locations specified.

She had read it three times that day, in the office, while the man watched her with the pleasant patience of someone who has never once been told no by a river. His name had been something forgettable—Voss? No, that was her boat, named for a woman she'd loved and lost to the sea, which was its own kind of contract. The man's name had been Pell Wexler. She remembered it now the way you remember the name of a storm.

The date on the contract was eleven months old. Eleven months. Before the expedition had ever put a shovel in the ground above the Khet Gorge. Before Halvard Iversen had found whatever it was she'd found. Before the radio went silent, before the fever, before any of it.

Someone inside the institute had hired her before there was anything to recover. Someone had looked at a river, and a dig, and a woman too stubborn to drown, and had seen the ending coming the way Ondine saw a log turning wrong in the current.

She had signed it because the money was obscene and because she'd told herself a story: that she would take their retainer and never be called on, that the clause was boilerplate, that rivers don't deliver on the promises of men in carpeted offices.

She had signed it also because of the second page. The second page was not the institute's. The second page was hers—a private addendum, handwritten, witnessed by a notary in the delta town of Chai Sua who owed her more favors than she could count: *In the event of termination of this engagement by either party, the Contractor shall be conveyed, at the Institute's expense, out of the country, with all effects, to a port of the Contractor's choosing.*

Passage out. That was the whole of it. That was the price she'd written into the deal with her own hand, in ink, before witnesses, because there was exactly one thing in the world Ondine Rask wanted that money could not buy directly, and it was a way off this river and out of this country with her skin intact and a certain sealed file left behind her in a customs office in the capital, gathering dust, gathering interest, waiting for her to be stupid enough to come back and claim it.

She had spent nine years building a life on this river that could be dissolved in an afternoon by a man with a stamp. The contract was her lifeboat. She had built it out of the institute's own greed, and she had told herself that made it clean.

Now she sat on her engine housing with the paper in her hands and the river ticking against the hull, and she understood that the lifeboat had been built for a different drowning than the one that was coming.

* * *

She found Emrys at the stern, skipping stones. Not well. He was a man who did everything with competence except the things he actually cared about, and the stones went flat and dead into the water, one after another, while the real current of his attention ran somewhere else entirely—upstream, toward the camp, toward the woman doing arithmetic.

"Your mother's going to stay," Ondine said. Not a question. She'd seen it in the way Halvard Iversen had stood at the edge of the dig road that afternoon, hands behind her back like a general surveying ground she intended to hold, and in the way the son had gone white around the mouth.

Emrys didn't look at her. "She thinks she can hide the archive and herself both. She thinks the river will cover her."

"The river doesn't cover anyone. It just moves what's on it." Ondine came and stood at the rail beside him. "You should know what you're steering into. All of you."

"Meaning what?"

She took the contract from inside her jacket. She had carried it up from the bilge because some part of her had already decided, and the part of her that hadn't decided yet needed the weight of the paper to argue with.

"Meaning this."

He took it. She watched him read—watched his eyes go down the careful language, watched him arrive at the date, watched him stop there the way she had stopped, the whole world narrowing to a line of type.

"Eleven months," he said.

"Eleven months." She lit the cigarette she'd carried down unlit, and the match flame bent in the river wind. "Someone in that institute of yours—of hers—knew this was coming before your mother ever found a single tablet. They hired the boat before there was a cargo. They built the trap before there was anything to catch in it."

"Who?"

"The signature block's an office, not a name. But the man who handed it to me had a face like a closed ledger and a voice like a hotel lobby." She exhaled smoke. "Wexler."

Emrys looked up sharply. "He's been on this boat for a week."

"I know."

"You've known the whole time."

"I have." She met his eyes and did not flinch, because flinching was for people with somewhere safer to be. "And before you decide what I deserve, you should read the second page. The one in my handwriting."

He turned the page. She watched him read the addendum, watched his face do the arithmetic she had done in that carpeted office—watched him understand that she had sold herself a lifeboat out of a country she could no longer live in, and that the institute had been only too happy to hold the deed.

"Passage out," he said quietly.

"There's a file in the capital with my name in it. Not a criminal file. Worse. A *cooperative* file. I spent four years being useful to people I hated, and the receipt for that usefulness lives in a customs office, and the day I try to leave this country without the right stamp, that file gets read by the wrong person, and everything I've built since—" She stopped. The river filled the silence for her, slapping the hull, patient, indifferent. "The institute promised me a port of my choosing and no questions at the dock. That was my price. That's why I signed."

"And now?"

"Now I'm holding a contract that says I'm supposed to carry you and your mother's people down to a location specified by Pell Wexler, and we both know what's waiting at the location specified." She tapped ash into the water. "Wexler's not a monster, Iversen. That's the problem. He's a man who believes the river can't hold what your mother found without drowning in it. He believes he's the levee. Men like that will flood a valley to save the town and call it mercy."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. The stones lay forgotten at his feet. "You could still do it," he said at last. "Take the contract. Hand us to him. He'd pay you, and pay you well, and you'd have your port and your stamp and your clean exit, and my mother's truth would sink somewhere in the middle gorge and no one would ever—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Why are you showing me this?"

Because, she thought, because I watched you carry your mother's pack up a mountain for six days without one word of complaint; because I watched Nia Okonkwo confess to a murder she didn't commit just to keep the living safe; because I have spent nine years being loyal to nothing but my own survival and I am so tired, so river-tired, of the company I keep.

What she said was: "Because I'm giving notice."

She held out her hand for the contract. He gave it back. She took out her lighter—the old brass one, dented, the only thing she'd saved from the *Maren Voss* before the *Maren Voss* was a name on a wreck report—and she burned the institute's page over the rail, page by page, the heavy stock catching slow and burning bright, the ash spiraling down to the water like black snow. The river took each flake without comment. Rivers were good at that.

The second page—the one in her own hand, with the notary's stamp—she folded and put back inside her jacket.

"This one I keep," she said. "Not because I trust the institute to honor it. Because I trust *you* to honor it better than they ever would have."

Emrys stared at her. "You're asking me for passage out."

"I'm telling you my price. Same as I told them." She ground the cigarette under her heel. "When this is over—whatever this turns into—when the records are wherever they're going and your mother is wherever she's going and Wexler is wherever *he's* going—I want a berth on a boat going out. Any ocean. Any port. You get me past the customs office in the capital with that file still sleeping, and I will run this river for you through whatever the monsoon throws down the gorge, and I will not ask you one question about cargo ever again as long as we both live."

"And if I say no?"

She smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was an honest one, which on this river was rarer. "Then I say no too, and we find out what kind of people we both are. But you won't say no. You've got your mother's face when you're deciding something. She'd take the deal."

"She'd take the deal and then rewrite it," Emrys said.

"Yes," Ondine said. "That's what I'm counting on."

* * *

They told the others at dusk, around the galley table with the lamp turned low because low lamps felt safer than bright ones, though Ondine knew it was superstition and didn't care. Nia Okonkwo took the news of the contract with the stillness of someone recalculating every trust she'd extended in the last week. Halvard Iversen took it with something worse: recognition.

"Eleven months," the old woman said, turning the burned contract's surviving fragment—Ondine had kept the signature block, as evidence, because you didn't burn evidence you might someday need—turning it under the lamp like a tablet from her own dig. "They commissioned the cleanup before the discovery. That means someone read the site survey. Someone knew what was under the administrative quarter before we did." Her voice was steady but her hands were not, and Ondine, who had spent a lifetime reading water, read them easily. "There's a leak inside my own team. There has been from the beginning."

The silence that followed had weight, the way deep water has weight. Ondine watched the faces around the lamp—Nia's guilt, Emrys's dawning fury, Wexler's careful blankness where he sat apart from the others, a man pretending not to count exits—and she felt the boat shift under her, not with the river but with something else, the slow roll of a vessel whose cargo had changed.

"Then we move tonight," Emrys said. "Whatever the plan is, it moves tonight."

Ondine stood, took the lamp from the table, and carried it to the wheelhouse window, where the river lay black and enormous under the last light.

"Then somebody tell me the plan," she said, "because whatever it is, it had better fit through the gorge before the water does."

Above the gorge, far upstream, the first clouds of the monsoon were assembling over the horizon like a fleet, and the river had already begun to rise.
