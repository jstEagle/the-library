## 13. Split Cargo

Nia Okonkwo had learned to count in the dark. It was a skill the site had taught her—counting breaths while crouched behind the fallen stela wall, counting footsteps of the advance man's patrols, counting the hours between when Halvard said *soon* and when soon actually arrived. Now she counted pages.

The administrative quarter's records ran to four hundred and eleven documents, and Halvard Iversen refused to let a single one be photographed carelessly. So they worked by lamplight in the base camp's largest tent, which smelled of mildew and kerosene and the ghost of the three men who had died here pretending to die of fever, and Nia pressed each page flat against a board while Emrys shot it twice—once straight, once at an angle against raking light—and called out numbers in a voice stripped down to its bones.

"Two hundred six."

"Two hundred seven."

Outside, the river spoke its low continuous language, and beneath that, fainter, the sound of Ondine Rask doing something mechanical and profane to her boat's engine. The monsoon was nine days out. Maybe eight. The weather reports came through Wexler's radio now, since he'd taken it over, and every forecast moved the landfall line closer like a hand sliding up a table toward a throat.

"Nia." Halvard stood in the tent flap, silhouetted against the last grey of evening. "You should sleep."

"So should you."

"I will. After." The old woman's face was half shadow, and Nia thought—not for the first time—that she looked less like an archaeologist than like someone standing in the doorway of a house fire, deciding what to carry out. "You've been copying since dawn."

"Someone has to know these documents," Nia said. "Not just have them. *Know* them. If the photographs are lost, if the copies are lost, then the only archive left is whatever lives in someone's head." She tapped her temple. "I'm on document two hundred and seven. Ask me about two hundred and six."

Halvard didn't ask. She came inside instead, lowered herself onto a crate with the careful economy of a body that had stopped negotiating with pain, and watched Emrys work. Mother and son did not speak. They hadn't spoken much in the three days since the arithmetic conversation—the one Nia had overheard through canvas, where Halvard had offered her own life as ballast and Emrys had refused the trade in a voice so quiet it frightened her more than shouting would have. But they worked in the same room now, and Nia understood that this was its own kind of sentence, maybe the truest either of them could manage.

"Two hundred eight," Emrys said.

The plan had been assembled the way a raft is assembled—from whatever floats, lashed with whatever holds. Nia had been present for all of it and trusted almost none of it, and she suspected that was the correct posture. Tonight, after the copying, they would split the cargo: duplicate sets printed from the camera's memory onto the expedition's remaining supply of waterproof paper, one set going downriver hidden in the medical chest with the wounded—two of the porters still couldn't walk unaided, and their evacuation was the story everyone agreed on—and the originals moving overland with Halvard toward the mission station at San Cristóbal, where Father Anselm's transmitter still worked, or so the last reliable word said.

Two routes. Two chances. Neither guaranteed.

"And the third set?" Nia had asked, on the first night of planning.

Ondine Rask had looked up from the contract she'd finally shown them—the institute's money, months in advance, payment for exactly this failure—and said, "There is no third set. There's the set you tell them about and the set you don't. That's how cargo works." Then she'd gone back to cleaning a fuel line with the serenity of a woman who had already decided whose side she was on and considered the matter closed.

Nia did not fully trust Ondine. She did not fully trust Wexler, who was worse, because Wexler wanted to be trusted. And she did not fully trust herself, because she had helped stage three deaths and had not yet decided whether that made her a protector or something else, something with a colder name.

* * *

Wexler found her at the waterline an hour past midnight, when the copying was done and the duplicates were being sealed in oilcloth.

He came quietly, which she noted. A man with nothing to hide announces himself; Pell Wexler moved like the advance man used to move, weight on the outside edges of his feet, hands visible. He sat on the shingle beside her without invitation and looked out at the black water where the gorge began.

"You don't sleep much," he said.

"Occupational hazard."

"Of archaeology?"

"Of being lied to professionally." She kept her eyes on the river. "You want something, Mr. Wexler. You always stand like a man about to ask for something."

A pause long enough to count heartbeats in. Then: "I want to know which set goes downriver."

And there it was. Nia felt the question settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. She turned and looked at him properly—institute tailoring gone soft and salt-stained, ten days upriver without laundry, a face built for conference rooms now weathered into something almost human. He wasn't smiling. That was the thing people got wrong about Pell Wexler, she thought. They expected the smile.

"Why do you want to know?"

"Because if I'm going to choose—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way a man walks across ice. "Because my institution sent me to make sure those records never reached anyone. That was the assignment. I accepted it. I want you to understand that I'm not pretending otherwise." His jaw worked. "But I've read some of them now. The requisition orders. The correspondence between the founding directors and the colonial governor. I helped build databases for that institution for eleven years, Dr. Okonkwo. I know what its archives look like. These documents aren't in any archive I ever touched. Which means someone removed them before I was born, and the removal itself is a confession."

"The removal is a filing decision," Nia said. "That's what they'll call it."

"Yes. And a massacre becomes a labor dispute, and a labor dispute becomes regrettable circumstances attendant upon the period." He said it fluently, wearily, a man reciting liturgy he no longer believed in. "I know the grammar. I wrote some of it." He turned to look at her directly, and his eyes in the dark were very steady. "So I'm asking you, because I think you're the one who actually decides things here, whatever title Halvard gives you. Which set goes downriver? Because if it's the real one—if you're sending the truth into the flood zone in a medicine chest—then I need to know tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

"Why tonight?"

"Because tomorrow I either help you or I radio ahead again," Wexler said simply. "And I'd rather help you. But I'm not going to do it blind, and I'm not going to do it because anyone charmed me. I've been charmed by better liars than this crew."

Nia looked at him for a long moment. The river breathed against the stones. Somewhere upstream, a night bird made its one note.

Here was the problem, laid out clean: she could not verify anything he said. He had seized the radio once already. He had proposed evacuating everyone and leaving the records *for a proper recovery team*, and everyone in camp knew what a proper recovery team meant—it meant a burn barrel and a fresh layer of silt. Every instinct she owned told her that Pell Wexler was the trap, dressed now in doubt and confession, the most convincing costume a trap could wear.

And underneath that: he had read the requisition orders. She had watched him read them, three nights ago, alone by the lamp, for four hours, turning pages with a care that no saboteur fakes. She had seen his face when he reached the letter dated March 1911, the one with the founding director's signature above the phrase *necessary measures regarding the local administration*. Nobody's face does that when they're gathering ammunition. Ammunition doesn't hurt like that.

"You suspect me," Wexler said. It wasn't a question.

"Completely."

"Good. Keep doing it. Just answer the question first."

Nia weighed it. Then she gave him the smallest true thing she could afford. "The wounded go downriver tomorrow. Their medical chest is heavier than it needs to be."

His exhale was slow, controlled, but she heard the relief in it anyway—a man setting down something heavy. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thanking me tells me you're invested, and I haven't decided what your investment costs yet." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "One more thing, Mr. Wexler. If you radio ahead—if one syllable of this reaches anyone before that boat clears the gorge—I will testify that you ordered the staging of those deaths yourself. I'll say you threatened us. I have three witnesses who'll swear to it, and none of us has anything left to lose, which makes us the most credible liars in this whole valley."

Wexler considered her. Something moved behind his face—not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man meeting his own reflection in worse lighting.

"That's the first honest threat anyone's made me in years," he said. "It's almost restful."

* * *

They loaded the boat before dawn, in the blue hour when the river ran silver and cold and the mist stood waist-deep on the water like a congregation waiting.

Nia directed it all, because directing was the one thing she was good at that didn't require believing in anything. The wounded porters—Tomas with his splinted leg, old Bao who coughed blood now and wouldn't say why—were carried down on litters and settled amidships with genuine blankets and genuine kindness, because whatever else this was, they were not props, and Nia insisted everyone treat them accordingly. The medical chest went aboard last. Inside it, beneath the gauze and the ampoules and the splints, wrapped in oilcloth and then in a ground sheet and then in Tomas's spare clothing, lay one hundred and ninety-four pages of duplicated truth.

Emrys checked the wrapping twice. Ondine checked it a third time and then looked at Nia and said, "You'll want to check it a fourth time, so go on," and Nia did, and it was fine, and checking it changed nothing, and she checked it anyway because ritual is what fear does when it can't do anything useful.

Halvard stood apart, at the edge of the camp, watching the river come up grey and enormous out of the gorge. She carried the originals in a leather case strapped across her back like a hump, like a burden she had chosen and would not put down. Her hands were bad now—the tremor had spread from her right to both—and Nia had watched her practice opening the case's buckles twenty times last night until the motion went somewhere below the shaking.

"You're sure about the overland route," Nia said, coming to stand beside her. Not a question either. Everyone was done with questions.

"The logging road holds to the ridge for thirty kilometers, then drops to the mission valley. Leeches, mud, one river ford. Four days if we're slow. Three if we're lucky." Halvard's voice was level, professorial, the voice she used for field reports. "We won't be lucky."

"Then why—"

"Because the originals matter, Nia." For the first time the old woman turned, and in the pre-dawn light her eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely clear. "Copies can be disputed. Copies can be called fabrications, and there will be lawyers—very good ones, paid by very frightened people—who will spend years making that argument. But originals, recovered from a documented stratigraphic context, with chain of custody, with my name attached—" she smiled, thin and terrible, "—my name is worth something to them. Ten thousand citations. Forty years of never being wrong in print. They'll have to destroy me personally to destroy those papers, and I intend to make that expensive."

"And if the flood takes the route? If the ford rises?"

"Then the copies do the work, and I'll have died in good company." She said it the way she might have said *then we redraw the grid square*—field arithmetic, nothing personal. But her hand found Nia's shoulder and gripped it, hard, trembling. "You staged three deaths to keep twelve people alive. Don't tell me you don't understand spending things."

Nia held her gaze. "I understand it. I just haven't forgiven myself for it yet."

"You will or you won't. Both are survivable." Halvard squeezed once and let go. "Get them down the river, Doctor Okonkwo. That's your dig now."

* * *

The boat pulled away at first light with Emrys at the bow and Ondine at the helm and the mist closing behind it like a door. Nia stood on the bank and counted—breaths, seconds, the diminishing sound of the engine—until there was nothing to count but her own pulse.

Then she turned uphill, to the overland party: Halvard, two porters, and herself. She had chosen the overland route in the end, over Emrys's flat objection, over Halvard's raised eyebrow. Someone had to know the documents. Someone had to keep Halvard alive long enough to transmit them. And someone had to watch Wexler, who was coming too—which was its own madness, which Emrys had argued against for an hour, and which Nia had overruled at midnight with a single sentence:

"He's most dangerous where we can't see him. So we see him."

Now Wexler stood at the treeline with a pack on his back that he had clearly never carried before, adjusted badly, and looked at the wall of green rising above the abandoned logging road with the expression of a man reading his own sentence. Halvard was already climbing. The porters followed. The mist was burning off, and the day was going to be hot, and somewhere south of them the monsoon was assembling itself out of the ocean like a verdict.

Wexler fell into step beside Nia on the trail. For a while neither spoke. The leeches found them within the first kilometer, as promised.

"You threatened me well," he said at last, breathing hard on the grade. "Last night. On the bank."

"It wasn't a performance."

"No. That's what impressed me." He wiped sweat, swatted at his neck, missed. "I spent the night thinking about what you said. About being invested." A pause. The forest dripped around them, enormous and indifferent. "Dr. Okonkwo, I need to tell you something before we reach the mission station, and I need you to hear it all the way through before you decide what it means."

Nia's hand found the machete at her belt without her deciding anything. "Say it."

"The extraction team Wexler ordered—" he corrected himself, grimacing at his own name, "—the team *I* ordered, before you disabled the boat. The order went through. Confirmation came back on the last broadcast before Emrys killed the radio." He met her eyes, and his face had gone the color of the fog. "They weren't sent to the gorge to stop the boat."

The trail tilted upward. Above them, Halvard's silhouette climbed steadily into the green, carrying the truth on her back like it weighed nothing, like it didn't weigh everything.

"They were sent to the mission station," Wexler said. "They're already there."
