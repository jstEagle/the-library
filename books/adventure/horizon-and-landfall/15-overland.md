## 15. Overland

The logging road began where the river ended, which is to say it began as a rumor—a seam of gravel and rusted culvert half-swallowed by fern, running northwest into hills that no map in Ondine's cabin bothered to name. Nia found it first, following a survey line on her battered tablet until the screen's ghost of a road resolved into actual ground: two ruts gone soft with forty years of rain, crowned with moss, stitched shut by roots.

"Someone built this," she said, crouching to brush the loam from a concrete marker post. The stencil on it had faded to a bruise-colored shadow. *KM 14.*

"Someone built everything once," Emrys said. "That's never been the problem."

The party was seven. Himself; Halvard; Nia Okonkwo; the surveyor Devi, who walked like a woman counting her own footsteps; two of the site workers, Tomas and Ilya, brothers who had not spoken above a murmur since the gorge; and Wexler, bound by his own arithmetic—he could not reach the institute without them, and they could not trust him farther than a rope's length, so he carried a rope. They had left the boat sunk in the shallows with its radio drowned, and the downriver group gone ahead with the duplicated records sewn into the lining of a stretcher that held a man genuinely feverish, which was its own kind of camouflage. What remained here were the originals: eleven kilograms of lead-sealed administrative clay and paper, wrapped in oilcloth, packed into Halvard's field frame along with her instruments, her notes, her tent, and her refusal to leave any of it behind.

Emrys had tried to take the pack at the trailhead. She had let him lift it, weighed it in his hands for exactly one second, and taken it back with a look that closed the subject like a door.

By the second day, the door was opening whether she liked it or not.

* * *

Leech country announced itself as a smell—wet iron and rotting sweetness, the breath of a forest digesting itself. The old road dipped through a stretch where the canopy closed overhead and the light went green and submarine, and there the leeches waited in the leaf litter, thin as pencil strokes, tasting the vibration of their steps.

They learned the ritual fast. Stop every twenty minutes. Check boots, cuffs, waistbands. Flick the things off before they anchored, because pulling an anchored leech left a wound that bled in this heat like a small betrayal. Devi carried salt in a film canister and doled it out like communion. Tomas got one inside his boot sock and didn't notice for an hour, and when he peeled the sock away the blood ran freely enough that Nia made him sit and bandage it properly, and he sat, obediently, the way men do when they have decided someone else is in charge of the world.

Halvard did not stop for the checks. She stopped when the party stopped, but she would not lift her own trouser cuffs, would not bend, and Emrys understood why before he wanted to: bending cost her something now. There was a hitch in her breathing on the climbs that hadn't been there at base camp, a gray undertone to her skin by afternoon. She covered it with pace, setting a rhythm just short of punishing, and everyone matched her out of respect, and Emrys watched her spend herself like a woman drawing down a final account.

On the third morning he found three leeches on her neck, fat and gorged, while she stood studying the ridgeline through her binoculars.

"Hold still."

"I'm looking at the—" 

"Hold *still*, Mother."

She stilled. He picked them off with his thumbnail, pressed the bites to stop the bleeding, and felt under his fingers how hot her skin was, how thin the architecture beneath it had become. Ten years since he'd touched her. It had taken parasites to arrange it.

"You're checking my pulse," she said.

"I'm checking whether you're dying."

"Not today." She took up the binoculars again. "There's a cut in the trees at the head of that valley. Old skid road. If the mission station is where the mission records say it is, we make it tomorrow night."

"And if your body has other plans?"

"Then you'll carry the archive," she said, "because it matters more than I do," and walked on before he could answer, which was her oldest trick and, he was beginning to understand, her most honest confession.

* * *

The rain came at dusk on the third day, the monsoon's outriders, warm and vertical and endless. The road turned to grease. They camped in the shell of a logging barracks whose roof had mostly kept faith with gravity, and Emrys sat near the doorway watching water sheet off the eaves and listening to Halvard argue with Nia about provenance in the dark interior—her voice thinner than it should have been, but still blade-edged, still certain.

"—the seals are administrative, not commemorative. That's the whole point. Nobody seals a massacre into the grain ledgers unless the ledgers are meant to be the *real* record, the one that survives the official story—"

"Or unless someone hid them there to be found later," Nia said. "Which is what you're doing right now, doctor. With us."

A silence. Then Halvard, quieter: "Yes. And I've thought about what that makes me."

Emrys stayed outside. Some arguments you weren't meant to witness, only to overhear, the way you overhear weather.

Wexler came and stood beside him in the rain-shadow of the doorframe, hands in pockets, watching the dark. He had said little in three days. His city shoes had died on the first morning and he'd wrapped them in strips of tarp and walked anyway, blistered and silent, and Emrys had to admit—if only to himself—that the man hadn't complained once.

"She'll collapse on that ridge tomorrow," Wexler said.

"I know."

"The institute has a medical protocol for field personnel. I memorized it. Rest, hydration, evacuation." He almost smiled. "It doesn't cover conviction."

"No," Emrys said. "Nothing covers that. We've all checked."

Wexler looked at him then, and in the last gray light Emrys saw something he hadn't expected: not calculation, but exhaustion wearing calculation's clothes. A man who had believed the institution was a wall keeping chaos out, and had walked far enough into the country to learn it was a fence keeping witnesses in.

"If she dies out here," Wexler said, "and those records die with her, I want you to know I'll testify to what they contained. From memory if necessary."

"Why?"

"Because I read them." He turned back to the rain. "That was my mistake. I read them."

* * *

The ridge nearly killed her on the fourth day.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the terrible part. No cry, no fall—just a slowing, a stutter in her stride on the switchbacks, and then, near the false summit, she simply sat down on a root mid-step, the way a machine sits down when the fuel is gone, and looked at the pack straps as though she couldn't remember what they were for.

Emrys knelt in front of her. Her pulse was fast and shallow and her lips had a bluish cast, and when she spoke her voice came from somewhere very far away and very calm.

"Take the north bearing. The cut in the trees. You'll see the station bell tower if the mist lifts."

"We're not doing this."

"Do what?"

"Pretend this is a handover." He unslung her pack—the archive, the instruments, all eleven kilograms plus the weight of everything else—and shrugged it onto his own shoulders over his own gear. Then he took her arm and put it across his neck and stood, bringing her up with him, taking most of her weight without making a performance of it. "You're going to walk into that station on your own feet, sit down at their transmitter, and dictate the header yourself, because if anyone else's voice opens that record the descendants will spend fifty years arguing about whether it was coerced. That's your argument, Mother. I've heard it every night for four days. I'm carrying it too. It weighs more than the clay."

She stared at him. Rain ran off her hood between them.

"When did you start listening?" she asked.

"About ten years ago," he said. "I just stopped answering."

They walked. Behind them the party adjusted without being asked—Devi moving up to spell him on the arm, Nia shifting the rope coil to balance the load, Tomas ranging ahead to find the surer footing. Nobody commented. Labor had its own language and they were all speaking it now, the way the river spoke, continuously and without needing to be right.

Twice more that day she faltered, and twice he took the weight back without discussion, and somewhere on the long descending traverse through the leech flats, with salt burning in a hundred small wounds and the rain easing to a fine drifting smoke, he realized she had stopped trying to take the pack back. Not surrender. Something better: delegation. Trust, maybe, or its first draft.

At the last rest stop before dark she ate a full ration, which she hadn't done in two days, and watched him re-wrap the archive in its oilcloth with exaggerated care.

"You carry it like it's alive," she said.

"It is. It's the only witness left that can't be flooded."

Her mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the terrain where one might form. "Your father used to say that about me."

"He said it about the boats," Emrys said. "But yes. About you too."

* * *

They smelled the mission station before they saw it: woodsmoke, real smoke, someone's cookfire, cutting through the green rot of the forest like a sentence through silence. Then, as the mist thinned on the fifth evening, the bell tower rose out of the treeline—lean, shingled, leaning slightly westward after decades of wind—and below it a cluster of low buildings with lamplit windows, and a garden, absurdly, defiantly green, fenced against the forest like a held breath.

Tomas whistled softly. Even Wexler stopped walking.

There were figures in the yard. Two, three, moving toward the gate with lanterns raised—not surprised, Emrys thought, watching them come. Expecting. As if word had traveled faster than feet, as if the forest itself had sent notice ahead: *they're coming, the ones with the dead men's names in a box.*

Halvard straightened beside him. He felt her gather herself the way she used to gather herself before lecture halls, before cameras, before boards that controlled her funding—drawing the failing body upright around the unquenchable spine of purpose. At the gate, an old woman in a faded cassock lifted her lantern and looked at each of them in turn, and stopped on Halvard, and something passed across her face that Emrys couldn't read—recognition, or memory, or warning.

"You're the historian," the woman said. Not a question.

"I am."

"Then come quickly," she said, turning back toward the lamplit buildings, "and come quietly. There was a man here yesterday asking after a survey team. He said he was from the institute." She paused at the threshold, lantern high, and her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"He left his radio on. We've been listening to it. Someone is coming up the logging road tonight."

Behind them, far down the valley, through the rain-smoke and the gathering dark, came a sound none of them wanted to hear: engines. More than one.
