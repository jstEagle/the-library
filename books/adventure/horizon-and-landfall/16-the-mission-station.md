## 16. The Mission Station

The mission station announced itself by sound before sight: a bell, cracked and toneless, rung by wind through the eaves of its tower. Then the building itself rose out of the leech country like something the forest had half-swallowed and then thought better of—whitewashed walls gone the color of old bone, a corrugated roof furred green with moss, mango trees crowding the compound wall as if they had come to reclaim it. A chapel on one side, its steeple leaning. A long low dormitory on the other. Between them, under an awning of rusted tin, the thing Halvard Iversen had walked three days through flooded logging cuts to reach: a transmitter mast, guyed with wire, standing against the bruising sky.

"Father Anselm," she said to the man who came out onto the veranda, because she remembered him from thirty years ago, from another expedition, another lifetime, when he had been a young priest with ink-stained fingers and a radio he let her use to file reports no one read.

He was old now. His hands shook when he took hers. "Halvard Iversen," he said, and his voice was gravel and wonder. "I thought you were dead."

"Not yet." She looked past him at the mast. "Is it working?"

"It works the way I work. Badly, but it works."

Behind her, Emrys set down the pack he had carried for her since the second morning out—the pack she had not asked him to carry, which was its own kind of conversation—and Nia Okonkwo came up the steps with the document cases strapped to her back like a mother carrying twins. The girl's face was gray with exhaustion, her ankles a ruin of leech bites, and still she scanned the compound first, the treeline second, the priest third. Halvard had watched her do this every day since the camp. The girl had learned to count exits the way other people learned to count money.

"You'll want the office," Father Anselm said. "There's a generator. Diesel is—" He made a gesture that meant *scarce*, that meant *pray*.

"We brought some," Emrys said. Of course they had. Her son planned fuel the way other men planned alibis.

* * *

The office smelled of paper rotting slowly in equatorial damp: ledgers from the mission's founding, baptismal records, decades of correspondence bundled and tied with string gone soft as cloth. On the desk sat the transmitter—a wartime set, olive drab, its casing scarred, its dials yellowed like teeth. Father Anselm had kept it oiled through all the years the mission's funding dried up and the diocese forgot them and the river took everything else worth taking. He had kept it, he said, because someone should know how to call for help if help ever mattered again.

Halvard touched the case and felt the tremor start in her own hand and stop there, disciplined, the way she had trained her body to hold steady over forty years of excavation brushes and trowels. Not yet. There would be time enough later for whatever was wrong with her—the dizziness that came now each afternoon like a tide, the pain in her left arm she had mentioned to no one, the way the leech-country march had felt like walking underwater. She had spent her whole life reading strata; she knew what layer was forming underneath her own skin. She simply declined, for the moment, to excavate it.

"The list," she said.

Nia opened the first case and laid out the pages they had copied at the camp by lamplight, page after page of the administrative quarter's sealed correspondence—orders dated a century back, requisitions for ammunition filed as "survey equipment," a governor's letter thanking the institute's founders for their discretion regarding "the regrettable necessity at Khet Bend," and beneath all of it the tally itself, written in a clerk's careful hand: villages emptied, names unrecorded, the river given the work of forgetting.

Three hundred and eleven names recovered from a census fragment. Three hundred and eleven, out of what the orders implied was thousands.

"We go in order," Nia said. She had organized the transmission schedule the way she organized everything, with a rigor that Halvard recognized and, privately, grieved over—rigor like that was usually purchased with fear. "Archives first. They're slow but they're permanent. Then the journalists—we have two confirmed, the one in the capital and Okonkwo—" she paused, only slightly, at her own name, at the cousin she had wired from the gorge crossing, "—in London. Then the descendant communities. The village councils along the lower river. The diaspora associations."

"And last," Halvard said, "the institute itself."

Emrys looked up from the generator he was coaxing into life in the corner. "You're sending it to *them*?"

"I'm sending it to their ethics board, their remaining honest trustees, and their founder's granddaughter, who has been writing letters about repatriation for a decade and being ignored." Halvard smoothed one of the copies flat on the desk. "A truth that arrives only through enemies can be dismissed as attack. A truth that arrives everywhere at once—including through their own mailroom—becomes weather. You cannot argue with weather, Emrys. You can only be in it or out of it."

He held her gaze across the room, and she saw in his face the argument he had been carrying since the camp, since before the camp, since perhaps the day he'd left her expedition a decade ago without goodbye: *you will burn yourself as fuel for this*. He didn't say it. He had stopped saying things like that somewhere in the leech country, when she had refused, for the fourth time, to lighten her load or her pace, and instead of arguing he had simply taken her pack and put it on his own shoulders and kept walking. It was the closest thing to tenderness either of them knew how to speak fluently.

The generator caught, coughed, steadied. The transmitter's dial lamps glowed amber.

"Frequency's clear," Father Anselm said from the doorway. "It usually is, this time of year. Nobody talks much before landfall except the desperate."

"We are the desperate," Halvard said, and sat down at the set, and began.

* * *

She transmitted for six hours.

It was not like speaking. It was like pouring. Each message went out in code groups and plain text both, duplicated, addressed, cross-referenced—the archive in the capital with its catalog numbers; the university collection in the south that had once expelled her mentor and would now, she hoped, choke on its own redemption; the wire service bureau; the woman in London whose byline Nia trusted with her family's name attached; the village councils, where the messages had to be sent in the river dialect, which Halvard spoke badly and Emrys spoke well, so that partway through the afternoon they traded places at the key and she stood behind her son and listened to him send her evidence in the language of the people it belonged to.

That was the moment, she thought afterward—or thought during, in the strange floating way the afternoon had begun to have—that was the moment she understood what they were actually doing. Not publishing. Publishing was a book, a name on a spine, a career. This was something older. This was how truth had always survived the powerful: not preserved but *distributed*, scattered like seed among ten thousand hands, so that no flood, no fire, no institute's lawyers could gather it all up again. The site would drown—she had known that since the monsoon charts changed, since the nine days became eight. The originals might be lost on the overland route; she had sealed them in oilcloth and accepted the odds. But these transmissions, going out now into the humid dark toward three continents—

No one could sink this. No one could buy it back. Every copy that landed was a root.

"Khet Bend council acknowledges," the crackling voice returned at dusk, in the river dialect, and Nia translated with her hand pressed over her mouth. *"We have waited ninety years for someone to say these names aloud. Say them again. We are writing them down."*

Halvard closed her eyes. Somewhere below them, twenty miles and a world away, the river was rising toward the place where those names had been buried twice—once by massacre, once by archive. But tonight they were traveling at the speed of light, in all directions, and the water could not follow.

"Next," she said. "Send the next."

* * *

It was full dark when the tide came in.

She had been fighting it since sundown—the dizziness, the pressure behind her eyes, the pain in her left arm that had graduated from suggestion to statement. She had managed it the way she managed everything, by refusing its existence, by keeping her voice level over the airwaves while the room tilted gently around her like a boat at anchor. Emrys had noticed. She could tell he had noticed, because he had stopped pacing and taken up a position near her chair, casual as furniture, positioned to catch.

"There are four more addressees," Nia said, checking the list. "Then we're done. Then we're done, Dr. Iversen."

"One more after that," Halvard said. "The institute's board. Directly. Plain text. Let them read their founders' handwriting in their own meeting minutes tomorrow morning."

"Halvard." Emrys's voice, low. "Let Nia send it."

"They need to hear it in my voice." She reached for the key. "That's the whole point of a deposition, Emrys. It's sworn. It's personal. It says: I was here, I saw this, I am not afraid of—"

The room swung hard to starboard.

She heard it happen from a distance, the way she heard everything now: the chair going over, her son's shout, Nia's cry, Father Anselm's bell tolling somewhere absurdly in her ears though no wind moved. The floor was cool against her cheek. The generator throbbed like a heart. Someone was saying her name—Emrys, her son, his hands on her face, turning her, and above him the amber dial lamps swam like fish.

"The board," she said. Her mouth worked strangely around the words. "Send it to the board. Plain text. Don't—don't lose the list, Nia, don't you dare lose—"

"We have it," Nia said fiercely, kneeling, gathering her, the girl's hands surprisingly strong. "We have all of it. It's sent. Most of it's sent. Stop talking."

"Four addressees left," Halvard said, because arithmetic was the last thing she owned, because her whole life had been arithmetic—what could be carried, what must be left, what a person was worth weighed against what a person knew—and she would not die—she was not dying, she refused to characterize it as dying—with the ledger unbalanced. "Four. Send the four."

"She needs the mission doctor," Father Anselm was saying, far away. "There's a nurse in the delta village, two hours by boat—"

"No boats," Emrys said, and his voice cracked down the middle like ice in spring. "Not until landfall passes. Not on that river."

*They got out,* Halvard thought. The downriver group, Ondine at the helm, the wounded, the duplicates—they had to have gotten out; the gorge ambush had been yesterday's desperate radio traffic, Ondine's voice like a blade running rapids no sane pilot would attempt. They were at the delta. They were safe. The cargo was scattered. The roots were planted.

Her son's face hovered over her, lit amber, ten years of silence burned off it at last, leaving only the boy from the dig sites, the one who used to sit at the edge of trenches swinging his feet and asking her questions she never answered well enough.

"Emrys," she said. "Listen. If I—"

"Don't." His hands tightened. "Don't you dare give me instructions right now."

"If I—" She smiled, or tried to; her face felt borrowed. "The strata don't lie, my love. I taught you that. Read me. Read what's—"

The tide took her.

But even going under, even as the office and the lamplight and her son's face dissolved into the warm dark, some stubborn surveyor's part of her mind kept the count: four addressees remaining, three hundred and eleven names spoken twice, copies beyond recall on three continents, and the rain just beginning, far away, softly, on the tin roof—the first breath of the monsoon, arriving exactly on schedule, while the truth flew on ahead of it, faster than any water, faster than any flood, into the hands of everyone the institute had never been able to reach.

The last thing she heard was Nia's voice, steady as a metronome, beginning the next transmission.
