## 17. Landfall

Ondine Rask had a rule about weather, the way old pilots have rules about everything: you don't argue with the sky, you just listen for what it's about to do to you.

She'd been listening since dawn, standing at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with her coffee going cold in one hand and the other resting on the throttle like it was a living thing. The air had gone strange overnight—not hot anymore but heavy, pressurized, the way a room feels before glass breaks. The birds had stopped flying upriver two days ago. Now even the insects had gone quiet, and the only sound was the river itself, running brown and fast and full of things it had already begun to take from the banks.

"Landfall," she said to no one, because saying it made it real, and making it real meant she could plan around it instead of pretending it wasn't coming.

Behind her, in the covered hold, the downriver group slept in shifts among crates of dried fish and the false-bottomed fuel drums where Nia Okonkwo had hidden half an archive that could end a hundred-year-old institution. Emrys Iversen was awake—she could tell by the particular quality of his silence, the way he stood at the stern rail watching the water they'd come down, as if the gorge might change its mind about them if he stared hard enough.

The wounded man, Teodor, breathed shallow under a mosquito net. The fever had broken on the second night out of camp, which Ondine counted as the first miracle of a trip that would need several more.

She drank the cold coffee, grimaced, and rang the engine bell twice—the signal they'd agreed on. *Wake up. Something's wrong.*

* * *

What was wrong, she told them an hour later, crouched over her charts in the wheelhouse while rain began to fall in fat, deliberate drops against the canvas, was everything.

"The monsoon's shifted north." She tapped the chart with a pencil stub, tracing the river's course down through the Khet Gorge, that twenty-kilometer throat of granite where the water compressed itself into fury. "Every forecast said it would make landfall south of the delta and push inland over three days. Gentle. Civilized. A polite rain." She looked up at Emrys, at Nia, at Wexler—who stood apart by the door with his hands folded, wearing the expression of a man attending his own sentencing. "It's not doing that. It's come ashore early and it's sitting on the headwaters. Everything that falls up there comes down here."

"How long?" Nia asked. Her voice was steady. Ondine had decided she liked this woman enormously—the kind of person who asked *how long* instead of *oh God*.

"River rises twenty feet in a day when the monsoon sits on the headwaters. Maybe less, if the tributaries all peak together." She let the pencil drop. "We're nine hours above the gorge at cruising speed."

"And we can't go around it," Emrys said. Not a question.

"You can portage around anything if you've got a month and a hundred porters. We have neither." She rolled the chart closed. "So we run the gorge. Today. In rising water."

Nobody spoke. The rain thickened against the canvas, finding its rhythm.

"That's suicide," Wexler said finally, and there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened Ondine more than any threat had. "Even for you."

"Everything worth doing is suicide with better logistics." She stood. "Get your people strapped in. Anything not tied down becomes a weapon. And pray to whatever you pray to that I'm as good as my reputation."

"You're better," Emrys said quietly. "That's what worries me."

* * *

They entered the gorge at midafternoon, under a sky the color of wet slate, and Ondine understood within the first kilometer that every calculation she'd made was already obsolete.

The Khet Gorge in low water was a technical run—boulders, standing waves, two channels that braided and re-braided around midstream islands. She'd run it eleven times. She knew its moods the way she knew the scar on her left forearm: intimately, and without needing to look.

This was not that river.

The water had come up six feet already and was climbing as she watched, the banks vanishing, the boulders that should have been landmarks drowned or barely showing as dark slick backs like sleeping animals. The current had doubled, tripled, gone from muscle to madness. Where the channels braided there was now only one channel, and it was moving at a speed that made the spray fly horizontal.

She flew the boat anyway.

There was no other word for it. The *Kestrel* weighed eleven tons empty and they were riding heavy, and she drove it through that granite throat like a woman threading a needle during an earthquake—reading the water three hundred meters ahead, reading the *shape* of it, the way the surface wrinkled over subsurface rock, the way foam collected in lines that marked the seams between currents. Every pilot learned to read water. What separated the ones who lived from the ones who fed the crocodiles was the ability to read it *fast*, to take in a quarter kilometer of chaos and know in one breathless instant: *there, left of the haystack wave, there's a tongue of green water, follow the green.*

Green water was safe water. Brown water was deep. White water was trying to kill you, and yellow-brown foam meant rocks just under the surface, patient as teeth.

"Left!" she shouted, and threw the wheel over, and the *Kestrel* heeled so far that Nia screamed and Teodor moaned in his sleep, and then they were through the first rapid and into the eddy behind it, wallowing, recovering, the engine roaring as she brought them back into the current.

Emrys appeared beside her, soaked, one hand clamped on the wheel housing. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Ten days on the river had taught him when to be cargo and when to be crew, and right now he was crew: he took the bilge pump handle and started working it, because they'd shipped water over the bow and the *Kestrel* was riding lower than she liked.

"Second rapid's worse," she called over the engine. "It's a slot run in normal water. Right now the whole channel's a slot. If we don't hit it dead center—"

"We'll hit it dead center."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

The ambush came between the second and third rapids, and afterward Ondine would understand that it could not have been otherwise—that Wexler's radio call from the camp, weeks ago now, had set this in motion like a stone rolling downhill, and stones don't stop because you regret dropping them.

She saw the boat first. A gray-hulled patrol craft, institute markings freshly painted over older ones, tucked into the lea of a midstream bluff where the current slackened. Two men on deck. One of them raised something to his shoulder.

"Down!" she roared, and hauled the wheel hard to starboard, putting the bluff between them and the muzzle flash—and even so the first shot cracked off the wheelhouse roof with a sound like the sky splitting, and splinters rained into her hair.

"They're shooting at us?" Nia's voice from below, high with disbelief.

"They're shooting at the *records*," Ondine snarled. "Us is incidental." She risked a glance back. The patrol boat was swinging out of its eddy, coming after them, and its engines were bigger than hers. Of course they were. Institutions always bought bigger engines.

The third rapid lay ahead—a half kilometer of continuous white in ordinary seasons, and now, with the river up, a single churning mile of nothing any sane pilot would attempt. Behind her, gaining, the gray hull. Ahead of her, the water of her nightmares.

She did the arithmetic in four seconds, the way she did all her arithmetic: fast, and without mercy.

They couldn't outrun the patrol boat in open water. They couldn't fight it. They couldn't surrender, because the men aboard had orders to sink the *Kestrel* and everyone in her, and orders like that got followed by people who wanted to keep their pensions.

Which left the rapid.

"Emrys." Her voice came out flat and calm, and she was distantly proud of that. "Get everyone below. Strap them to the frames. Double-lash the drums. Then get back up here, because I need your hands on the bilge pump and I need them *now*."

He looked at her—one long look, the look of a man measuring whether the woman at the wheel had finally tipped over into the madness this river demanded—and then he went, shouting down into the hold, and she heard Nia's voice rise and fall, heard the sounds of straps cinching, of cargo being thrown and lashed, of a wounded man being cradled and tied like the most precious cargo aboard, which he was, they all were, everything aboard was precious, that was the whole point, that was why the gray boat was coming.

Ondine stood alone at the wheel and looked at the rapid.

It looked back.

Somewhere in her chest, terror was doing what terror always did—flooding her, screaming at her, telling her to turn aside, to find the eddy, to take her chances with the guns rather than the water. She let it scream. She'd learned years ago, on another river, in another life, that terror was not the enemy. Terror was information. Terror was the part of her that loved being alive, shouting its love at the top of its lungs.

She thanked it, silently, and then she set it aside like a coat she'd put on again later, and she opened herself to the thing underneath—the cold, clear, singing stillness that came when there was no more deciding to do, only doing.

Pilots had names for it. Old pilots called it *going quiet*. She went quiet.

* * *

The entrance to the third rapid was a V of brown water funneled between two drowned ledges, and the whole weight of a rising river was pouring through it. At the top of the V, the surface stood up in a breaking wave taller than the wheelhouse—water climbing uphill against its own nature, holding its shape out of pure fury.

You did not go through that wave. You went around it, through the seam on its left shoulder where the return current curled back on itself, a ribbon of chaos that was also, impossibly, the only survivable line—and you hit it at exactly the right angle, exactly the right speed, or the river picked up your eleven tons and showed you what it thought of your plans.

Ondine throttled up until the engine howled.

The gray boat was two hundred meters back and closing. Through the rain she saw a man at its rail gesturing, shouting—*they're not following, they can't believe she'll run it*—and she felt something that was not quite laughter move through her.

No sane pilot would attempt it. That was true. That was entirely, beautifully true.

But sanity was a luxury of people with somewhere else to be.

She aimed the *Kestrel* at the seam and held her.

The world became noise and water. The bow climbed, climbed, climbed until the sky was all there was—gray sky, rain like nails, the bluffs tilting away beneath her—and then the wave took them, not gently, not cruelly, but with the vast indifferent strength of a thing that did not know it was killing or saving, and the *Kestrel* went airborne for one endless half-second with eleven tons of boat and archive and human lives hanging in the rain.

Then the bow came down.

It came down wrong—too steep, too far left—and the river seized them sideways, and for three seconds Ondine fought nothing but physics, the wheel useless, the engine screaming in free revolution as the propeller broke water. They surfed. There was no better word. Eleven tons of steel surfing down the face of a standing wave with the bluffs blurring past and the bilge pump hammering and somewhere below her Nia Okonkwo singing—actually singing, a low urgent hymn in a language Ondine didn't know, and she thought: *yes, sing, sing us through, whatever works*—

The bow caught. The propeller bit. The *Kestrel* straightened with a shudder that ran keel to wheelhouse, and suddenly she had steerage again, and the rest of the rapid unrolled before her like a sentence she suddenly knew how to read.

She read it. Left of the haystack. Follow the green. Ferry-glide across the current to dodge the strainer—a whole dead tree lodged against the cliff, waiting to wrap them like a fly in webbing—then hard right, thread the gap between two boulders that were closer together than the boat was wide, close enough that she heard the hull kiss stone, a long grinding shriek along the starboard side that would cost her paint and plating and possibly her livelihood, and then—

Then they were through.

The river widened. The water, still furious, still brown, still climbing, spread itself out and stopped trying to murder them quite so personally. Rain fell. The engine settled into its work rhythm. From below came the sound of people remembering how to breathe.

Ondine realized her hands were locked on the wheel and made them let go, finger by finger. They ached. Everything ached. She was soaked to the skin and shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and she was grinning like a woman possessed.

Behind them, the gray patrol boat sat motionless at the head of the rapid, its wake dying, its crew staring at the water where the *Kestrel* had been.

One of them tried it. She watched it happen—watched the gray hull swing toward the seam, hesitate, commit too late and too far right, and meet the wave broadside. The river took it the way rivers take things: completely, without malice, without ceremony. It rolled once, shed two men into the current—they'd live; the eddy below would spit them out on the bank, bruised and humiliated and alive—and then the boat itself went under, dragged down by its own big beautiful engines, and the river closed over it and kept the receipt.

Ondine watched until there was nothing to watch, and then she turned upstream of her own feelings and found, to her surprise, that the terror was still there—but transfigured now, changed in the fire, become something with a different name. Mastery, maybe. Or just the plain animal joy of having been, for ninety seconds, exactly as good as the moment required.

"Ondine." Emrys at her shoulder, soaked, white-faced, alive. He was looking back at the sinking patrol boat too. "Are they—"

"Two in the water. They'll wash out on the point below. Cold and embarrassed." She wiped rain from her face with a forearm that trembled. "The boat's a total loss. Tell your mother's funders their insurance premiums are about to go up."

Something moved across his face—grief, maybe, or the beginning of grief, for a site drowning upriver, for a mother on an overland road, for everything the water was taking even as it saved them. But he put a hand briefly on her shoulder, and the hand said what the moment required: *thank you*.

"Don't thank me yet," she said. "Look at the banks."

He looked. So did Nia, coming up the ladder with Teodor's pulse under her fingers and wonder all over her face. And they all saw it together: the flood debris riding the current—whole trees, roofs, a fishing platform torn loose and spinning, a cow swimming with terrible patience toward the sea—and the water marks on the cliffs, fresh bands of darker stone showing where the river had already risen eight feet since morning, and rising still.

"It's not done," Ondine said quietly. "Landfall isn't a day. It's a season. By tomorrow this will be twenty feet higher. The delta villages are going under. The lowlands—" She shook her head. "Everything downriver is about to learn what we just learned. The river keeps what it keeps."

Nia gripped the rail, looking north, back the way they'd come, toward the buried city and the overland party and Halvard Iversen walking through leech country with half the truth on her back. "The others," she said. "The mission station. If the flooding reaches the roads—"

"Then we'd better hope they're faster than the water," Ondine said. She put her hands back on the wheel, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the current, running south, running for the delta, running for whatever was left of the world when the sky finished falling. "And we'd better be there when they arrive. Because I've got a contract in my cabin signed by someone inside your institute, and when this is over, somebody's going to answer for it."

Ahead, through the rain, the first lights of the delta towns were coming on—small, stubborn, orange against the gray—and Ondine Rask, terror transfigured, piloted her battered boat toward them and did not slow down.
