## 18. What the Water Takes

The delta announced itself before we saw it—a smell of brackish water and rotting palm, of mud turned over by something vast and indifferent. Ondine brought the boat in on the last of the flood surge, running the braided channels by memory and instinct while the sky came down around us in sheets that had not stopped since the gorge.

I stood at the bow because I could not stand still. The rain had a sound now that it hadn't had upstream: heavier, wetter, the sound of a sky that had finished holding back. Behind me in the covered hold, Nia slept sitting up against the fuel drums, her arms wrapped around a dry bag she hadn't let go of in three days. Two of Halvard's students lay under a tarp, feverish for real this time, their bodies finally paying the toll the staged deaths had only pretended to collect. Wexler sat apart from everyone, hands folded, watching the water as if it might be reasoned with.

"There." Ondine pointed her chin at a smear of tin roofs through the rain. "Kampong Serat. If anyone's still there."

The town was half underwater. Boats were tied to second-story balconies; a mosque's minaret rose from what had been a street. But people were there—dozens of them, sheltering in the schoolhouse on the one hill, and when our boat ground against the submerged steps they came out into the rain to look at us with the flat, assessing stares of people who had already survived the worst thing and were waiting to see if we were it.

An old man with a government radio lanyard still around his neck took charge of us without asking names. He put the sick students on cots, put Nia near them, put Wexler in a corner where he could be watched without being spoken to, and put Emrys Iversen—the son of Dr. Halvard Iversen, though nobody here knew or cared—in front of the radio table, where a map of the river system was pinned to the wall and someone had drawn a thick red line across the upper valley.

"Your mother's route," the old man said. Not a question. Word travels faster than water sometimes. "You should see."

I looked. The red line crossed the logging roads above the Khet Gorge and ended at a mission station marked with a small inked cross. Between the cross and the site, someone had written two words in pencil, in a hand that pressed too hard: *cut off.*

"When?" I asked.

"Yesterday. The bridge at Tanah Merah went in the night. Landslide took the road with it." He tapped the map. "No one goes up. No one comes down. Whatever your people are carrying—" he shrugged, a gesture that contained whole libraries of resignation, "—the river has decided."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in the schoolhouse corridor with my back against the wall and listened to the rain perform its single, endless note, and I made myself do the arithmetic my mother would have done, because someone had to and she wasn't there to do it.

The site was gone. That much was certain now. Pilots flying supply runs over the valley reported the terraces drowned, the excavation trenches filled with brown water, the administrative quarter—we had given it a name by then, the Archive Quarter, as if naming it could make it more real—buried under twenty feet of new river. A century-old massacre hidden by a century-old institution, and now hidden again, this time by weather, which keeps no records and can be bribed by no one.

And the originals—the ledgers, the sealed dispatches, the correspondence Halvard had spent four months coaxing out of the mud with brushes and patience—had been moving overland with her. Overland, on roads that no longer existed, toward a transmitter that might as well have been on the moon.

Which left the transmissions. Three nights' worth, sent from the mission station before the bridge fell: copies scattered to archives in two countries, to a journalist whose name Nia had whispered like a password, to descendant communities on three continents who had been waiting a hundred years for someone to say their dead had names. Fragile things, radio transmissions. Static-eaten, partial, deniable. Everything now rested on whether they had arrived intact, and whether anyone receiving them would believe what they heard.

I thought about my mother's face the last time I'd seen it, lit by the transmitter's glow, her broken hand cradled against her chest as she read the dispatches aloud into the microphone in a voice that never wavered until, halfway through the fourth document, it did—and then she kept reading anyway, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face, because the record mattered more than the reader. She had taught me that without ever once saying it. She had taught me everything except how to stop.

Somewhere past midnight, Nia found me in the corridor. She sat down beside me without asking, the way people do when words have run out between them, and for a long time neither of us spoke.

"The journalist," she said finally. "Maren Voss. She'll have gotten at least part of it. She keeps redundant receivers—she told me once she'd missed too many stories to trust a single signal." Nia turned the dry bag over in her lap. "If anyone got the transmissions, it's her."

"And if the institute gets to her first?"

"They'll try." She said it plainly. She had stopped being afraid of plain speech somewhere between the staged graves and the flooded gorge. "But Wexler said something, back at camp. Before everything. He said the institute doesn't fear discovery, it fears *corroboration*. One transmission is noise. Ten is a pattern. And patterns get investigated."

I looked at her. In the dark her face was all edges and exhaustion. "You're telling me to hope."

"I'm telling you what I know." She leaned her head back against the wall. "Hope is your department. You're the one who came upriver when nobody else would."

* * *

Morning brought a change in the rain—not an end, but a thinning, the monsoon settling into its long middle age. Ondine appeared at the schoolhouse door looking like a woman who had slept exactly as much as she intended, which was none.

"We need to talk about the boat," she said, and led me out onto the veranda where the flood lapped the third step down.

She lit a cigarette against regulations and nobody's objection. "Fuel's low. Hull took damage in the gorge—I patched it, but patchwork and floodwater are a bad marriage. We can go down to the coast, or we can stay here and become very well-acquainted with these people." She exhaled smoke into the dripping eaves. "What I can't do is go back up. Not for her, not for anyone. The river's taken the road, the bridge, and the site. It won't give them back, and it'll take the boat too if I ask it to."

"I'm not asking you to."

"You will," she said. "Or you'll want to. So I'm telling you now so you can start grieving early." She studied me sideways, the way she read water—looking for the undertow beneath the surface. "Your mother knew what she was doing, Iversen. When she split the cargo, she wasn't gambling. She was spending. She spent the originals to buy time for the copies. That's not a plan that fails. That's a plan that costs."

"She's alone up there with a broken hand and a cut-off road."

"No." Ondine flicked ash into the flood. "She's alone up there with villagers who carried her out of the leech country, a mission priest with a shortwave, and a story that belongs to them now. You transmitted those records to *them*, remember? Descendant communities. This isn't your history anymore, or hers, or the institute's. It's theirs. And people don't abandon their own history just because a bridge falls down." She stubbed the cigarette against the rail. "Grieve the site. Grieve the ledgers. But don't you dare grieve her while she's still breathing, because that's a luxury she hasn't earned yet and neither have you."

It was the closest thing to kindness anyone had said to me in weeks. I didn't thank her. She wouldn't have wanted it.

* * *

Wexler found me at midday, which surprised me less than it should have. He had spent three days being deliberately invisible, and invisibility, I was learning, is just another way of positioning yourself.

He sat down across from me at the radio table uninvited. Up close, in daylight, he looked worse than the rest of us—not from hardship but from the particular erosion of a man whose architecture has failed him. His clothes were clean. His eyes were not.

"I've been listening to the bands," he said. "There's chatter. Institute channels, encrypted, but volume tells you something even when content doesn't. They're mobilizing a response team. Legal first, then security." He paused. "They're coming for the narrative, Iversen. Not the documents—they can't un-transmit documents—but the meaning. By next month there will be official statements calling the records forgeries, calling your mother unstable, calling the whole expedition unsanctioned. There are men whose entire careers depend on that version winning."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because they've disowned me." He said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "My access codes died two days ago. My emergency extraction request was acknowledged and then ignored. I am, as far as the institute is concerned, a liability who drowned in the gorge." He almost smiled. "Do you understand what that makes me?"

"A free man," Nia said from the doorway. She'd come in quietly, the dry bag still in her arms.

"It makes me," Wexler corrected, "the last person inside their machine who knows how it thinks. And I am going to tell a journalist everything, in detail, under my own name, because it is the only act available to me that my thirty years there cannot be forgiven for." He looked at me, and behind the institutional polish I saw something rawer—an old man's terror and relief arriving together. "But I need to know the transmissions arrived. I need corroboration before I speak, or I'm just a disgraced functionary making accusations. With the records confirmed, I'm a witness."

"Maren Voss," Nia said. "Delta town called Bandar Laut, two days downstream. She has a house on the waterfront with an antenna disguised as a fishing rod holder. If any receiver caught us, hers did."

Wexler nodded slowly. "Then we go to Bandar Laut."

* * *

We left Kampong Serat the next dawn, Ondine easing the wounded boat down the swollen channels with the tenderness of a woman handling something she loved and resented in equal measure. The delta unspooled around us—mangrove and floating gardens, houses on stilts standing hip-deep in their own reflections, children paddling washtubs between doorways. Life arranged around water, unbothered by catastrophe, because catastrophe here was seasonal and survivable and always, eventually, receded.

I stood at the bow again. It was becoming a habit, standing where the river could see me.

Nia came up beside me and handed me a cup of tea gone lukewarm in the thermos. We drank in silence for a while.

"Do you think she made it out?" she asked. "Before the bridge. Do you think she's alive?"

I thought about my mother's arithmetic, the cold beautiful math of it: *you cannot save both the people and the proof, so divide yourself.* She had sent us downriver carrying copies and stayed upriver carrying originals, and the river had eaten the originals, and maybe her, and the only thing left of either was a voice on a frequency, scattered across three continents like seed.

"I think," I said, "that if she's alive, she's already transmitting. And if she isn't—" I watched the brown water slide past, carrying silt from the drowned valley, from the terraces, from the Archive Quarter, from every grave the institution had ever dug. "Then she's in the record. Which is the only afterlife she ever believed in anyway."

Nia put her hand briefly on my arm. Neither of us said anything else. Some griefs you carry out loud; others you carry the way the river carries its silt—silently, ceaselessly, depositing them somewhere downstream where they might someday become land.

* * *

Bandar Laut appeared at dusk on the second day: a delta town of fish-drying racks and rusting trawlers, built on pilings over water the color of tea. Ondine tied up at a stilt jetty below a shophouse with a blue door, and above the door, hand-painted, fading: *M. VOSS — CORRESPONDENT.*

A light burned in the upstairs window. As we climbed the ladder, I heard it—faint, distorted, fighting through static, but unmistakable to anyone who had spent the last week listening for it. A woman's voice, clipped and professional, playing back on a reel-to-reel through an open window.

"—dispatch 41, dated the ninth, signed H. Iversen, expedition lead. 'The administrative quarter yielded sealed correspondence today. The signatories include the founding trustees of the Meridian Institute. The orders concern the suppression of the river communities in the year—'"

The recording stuttered, skipped, held.

Nia stopped on the ladder, her knuckles white on the rail. Wexler, below her, had gone absolutely still, his face turned up toward the window like a man hearing a verdict read.

The blue door opened before we knocked. A tall woman with grey-streaked hair and a receiver's headset around her neck looked at the three of us—at Nia, whom she clearly recognized; at Wexler, whom she clearly recognized differently; at me, whose face apparently answered some question she'd been asking the static for days.

"You're late," Maren Voss said. "I've had your mother's voice on tape for six days and no one to confirm she's real." She stepped aside to let us in. "Come tell me everything. And then—" her eyes went to Wexler, sharp as a blade finding the joint, "—you can start from the beginning. I have a feeling yours is the confession the whole world's been waiting for."

Behind her, on the reel-to-reel, my mother's voice read on into the static—patient, precise, unkillable—naming the dead one by one, as if the flood had never happened at all.
