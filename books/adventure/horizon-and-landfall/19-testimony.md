## 19. Testimony

The delta town was called Sengkar by the maps and nothing at all by the river, which passed through it twice a day on its way to becoming sea. Wexler arrived in it the way water arrives—inevitably, without dignity, carried along by forces he had once believed he commanded.

He had been stranded eleven days.

The institute's last transmission to his field unit had come through on a borrowed handset with a cracked screen, four lines of corporate prose that managed to say everything by saying nothing: *Mr. Wexler's engagement has concluded. All field operations are suspended pending internal review. Personnel are advised that the Institute assumes no liability for actions taken outside authorized parameters.*

He had read it nine times before he understood it was not a reprimand. It was an amputation. Somewhere in a glass tower above a city he would likely never see again, lawyers had drawn a circle around him and stepped back from its edge, and now he stood inside it alone, a man officially unaffiliated with the very institution whose name he had spoken for nineteen years like a prayer.

The town smelled of diesel and drying fish and the green rot of things the flood had left behind. Its single street ran parallel to the water on stilts, and beneath the stilts the mud held the monsoon's leavings: a child's sandal, half a canoe, a refrigerator with its door open to the sky like a mouth mid-sentence. The river had risen twenty feet in a day and fallen almost as fast, and it had taken the site, the gorge, the pursuit boat Ondine Rask had drowned in white water, and—Wexler suspected—the originals of every document Halvard Iversen had ever touched. It had not taken the transmissions. That was the arithmetic he could not stop performing. Three continents of archives and newsrooms and descendant communities now held copies of what he had been sent upriver to bury, and no flood could reach them, and no lawyer could reach them, and no amount of institutional forgetting could reach them.

He sat most mornings at a table outside the Chao Lay guesthouse, drinking coffee that tasted of smoke and watching the water go by, and the survivors watched him back.

They did not speak to him. Nia Okonkwo had made sure of that in the first days, positioning herself between Wexler and the others like a wall built quickly out of whatever came to hand. But they watched. The wounded man, Teodorczuk, whose arm hung in a sling of knotted bedsheet. The two students who had come downriver hidden among crates of duplicated records, still flinching at engine noise. And Emrys Iversen, who moved through the town's small commerce of boats and batteries and boiled water with the competence of a man who had finally stopped pretending he wanted to be anywhere else.

Wexler understood their watching. He had spent his career being watched by people deciding what to do about him. What he had never done was sit still for it—to let the looking run its course without reaching for leverage, without drafting the memo, without making the call that turned a problem into a procedure. Procedure had been his whole life. He was discovering, in this mud-flat town at the end of the world, how much of himself had been procedure, and how little remained when you drained it out.

* * *

The journalist arrived on the ninth day, on a mail boat flying no flag, a compact woman in her fifties with sun-cracked hands and a canvas bag she never let out of her reach. Her name was Ampai Srisawat, and Nia greeted her at the landing like family—which, Wexler learned, she nearly was; Ampai had mentored Nia's mother through a journalism program in another country, another decade, another set of institutions that needed breaking.

Ampai set up in the guesthouse's back room, which had one window, one fan, and a table scarred by decades of card games. She interviewed the survivors one at a time, for hours, and Wexler heard fragments through the thin wall: dates repeated and confirmed, names spelled aloud, the patient archaeology of testimony. She did not rush anyone. She did not perform sympathy. She asked the same question three different ways until the answers agreed with each other, and when they didn't, she asked a fourth time.

On the eleventh evening, Nia Okonkwo came and stood at the end of Wexler's table while he ate rice he wasn't tasting.

"He wants to talk to you," she said. "Ampai does."

Wexler set down his spoon. "I'm surprised she'd want anything from me."

"She doesn't, particularly." Nia's voice was flat as the horizon. "She thinks your testimony is worth less than the paper it would ruin. She thinks you're a man who sold other people's graves for a salary. She told me that herself, this morning, over breakfast."

"And yet."

"And yet." Nia looked out at the water, where the light was doing something amber and merciless. "She also says a confession means more coming from the man who wrote the orders than from any of us who survived them. She says the institution will call us hysterical, and biased, and grief-mad, and that the only voice they can't dismiss is the voice they trained themselves." She turned back to him, and her eyes were dry and very tired. "So she'll take yours. But understand something, Wexler. If you lie in that room—if you soften one date, if you shift one name—I will know, and she will know, and we will put you back outside where you've been standing all week. This isn't redemption on offer. It's a door. You can walk through it or watch it close."

"I understand," Wexler said.

"Do you?" For the first time something moved behind her composure—not anger, exactly; something older and sadder. "Because I helped stage three deaths to protect people from men like you, and I have to live with that forever, and I don't get to call it a door. I have to call it what it is. Whatever you do in that room tomorrow, you'll have to call it what it is too."

She left him with his cold rice and the sound of the river working at the pilings, patient as a lawyer.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat on the guesthouse steps instead, in the dark that the tropics pour like ink, and took inventory of himself the way he had once inventoried field assets.

Nineteen years. He had joined the Meridian Institute at thirty-one, young enough to believe the letterhead meant something, old enough to know better and choose anyway. He had told himself the story every institution tells its enforcers: that the work mattered, that the funding was the work, that someone had to manage the messy human variables so the science could proceed untainted. He had believed—or had performed believing so long that the performance calcified into conviction—that Halvard Iversen's discovery was a threat because publication without verification was irresponsible. That the dead men at base camp were a tragedy requiring discretion. That the records naming the founders were forgeries, probably, or misread, certainly, and even if they weren't, a century-old massacre was history, and history belonged in archives, not headlines.

He had said all of it to Emrys Iversen on the boat, somewhere in the narrows above the second confluence, and Emrys had listened with a patience that Wexler recognized only later as contempt wearing courtesy's face.

Now, on the steps, in the dark, he found the story would not tell itself anymore. That was the thing about defeat that no one warned you: it didn't break you. It just stopped the machinery, and in the silence you could hear what the machinery had been drowning out. He thought of the administrative quarter under the roots, the sealed clay cylinders, the ledger entries in a dead administrative script that named the founding shareholders of a company that had become, through mergers and rebrandings and the slow laundering of blood into philanthropy, the Meridian Institute. He had held one of those cylinders. He had felt its weight. He had told himself it was probably a forgery planted by rivals, and the telling had cost him something he could not name at the time and could name now: it had cost him the version of himself that still checked.

The night air moved off the water, warm and smelling of salt and silt. Somewhere across the channel a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything. Wexler thought about the word *liability*, which the institute's lawyers had applied to him, and the word *asset*, which they had applied to him for nineteen years, and understood they were the same word pronounced differently depending on which side of the balance sheet you fell.

In the morning, he walked through the door.

* * *

The back room was smaller with Ampai in it. She had arranged two chairs facing each other across the scarred table, a recorder between them, a notebook open but untouched. The fan turned overhead with the slow resignation of a machine past caring. Through the window Wexler could see the river, high and brown and going somewhere without him.

"Before we start," Ampai said, settling across from him, "you should know what I think of you."

"I imagine it's accurate."

"It is." She clicked the recorder on. "You're the kind of man institutions build on purpose. You're not corrupt; corruption requires imagination. You're obedient, which is worse, because obedience scales. Everything wrong in this story happened because men like you did their jobs well." She let that sit in the humming air. "And because of that, your testimony is the most valuable thing in this town. So we're going to do this properly. Full name, for the record."

"Pell Marcus Wexler. Director of Field Operations and Institutional Security, Meridian Institute for Historical Research." The title sounded obscene in the little room, like a uniform worn to a funeral. "Formerly," he added.

"Formerly." She made a note she didn't need to make. "Begin wherever you like. I'll interrupt when I need to."

He began, unexpectedly, with the charter contract—with Ondine Rask's signature on a document dated eight months before the expedition went silent, hired through intermediaries by someone inside the institute who had planned for catastrophe the way farmers plan for rain. He gave Ampai the intermediary's name. He described the contingency protocols, the extraction team staged at the gorge, the radio codes he had used, and he spelled each code out slowly while she wrote. He described the supply cache staged to look looted. He described, in a voice he was surprised to find steady, the conversation in which he had proposed evacuating the team immediately and leaving the records "for a proper recovery team"—and then explained, unprompted, what *proper recovery team* had meant in the operational language he had helped write: a team that arrives after the evidence has been removed, catalogues its absence, and files a report concluding there was never anything to find.

Ampai let him run. When he faltered—and he faltered twice, both times at the human costs, both times catching himself before sentiment could look like strategy—she poured water from a dented thermos and waited.

It was near noon when she asked the question he had known was coming since the previous night, maybe since the boat, maybe since the first memo he had ever drafted that made a person disappear into a paragraph.

"Why?" she said. "Not the institution's why. Yours. You're a competent man. Competent men see consequences. You must have seen, months ago, that this could end the way it ended. Why did you keep going?"

Wexler looked at the recorder turning their voices into something that would outlive them both, and he made himself answer honestly, because Nia Okonkwo was right, because the door only worked if you called things what they were.

"Because I thought the alternative was chaos," he said. "That's the truest thing I can give you. I believed—right up until the water came through the gorge and proved me wrong—that some truths were load-bearing, and if you pulled them out, everything built on top would come down. Descendant communities suing. Governments reopening claims. Donors fleeing. A century of scholarship tainted by association. I told myself I was protecting the edifice." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, aligning it with nothing. "But an edifice is just a weight, Ms. Srisawat. It isn't a reason. And the people underneath it—" His voice did something he hadn't authorized. He let it. "There were three men at that camp. I read the incident report I helped shape. Fever, it said. I signed off on the language. I knew, when I signed, that fever was a word we used for problems we preferred dead to diagnosed. I signed anyway. That's my testimony. Not what I did. That I knew, and signed anyway, and would have kept signing, if a pilot named Ondine Rask hadn't decided her contract was worth less than her soul."

Ampai wrote for a long moment. Then: "Are you prepared to repeat all of this under oath, if hearings are convened? To name names? Including the ones above you?"

"The ones above me disowned me by satellite," Wexler said. "They cut me loose before the flood crested. I'm the cheapest possible witness—they've already priced me at zero." He almost smiled; it came out as something else. "Which means, for the first time in nineteen years, I'm free to be accurate."

* * *

He testified for two more days. On the third morning, Ampai packed her recorder, shook his hand with the impersonal firmness of a surgeon closing, and caught the mail boat south with copies of everything, duplicated in triplicate, distributed to hands Wexler was not permitted to know. He stood at the landing and watched the boat diminish, carrying his voice toward cities he had once flown into on expense accounts, and felt—there was no other word for it, though he distrusted it profoundly—lighter.

The survivors' attitude toward him shifted by degrees, the way the tide shifts: imperceptibly, then all at once. Teodorczuk nodded to him on the street. One of the students asked, without preamble, whether the institute's insurance would cover the equipment they'd lost, and Wexler spent an hour explaining, in detail, exactly how to file claims designed to fail, and then how to file the appeals that wouldn't. It was the first useful thing he had done with his expertise in nineteen years, and it embarrassed him how good it felt.

On the fifth evening after Ampai's departure, Emrys Iversen found him at his usual table and sat down across from him without asking, which was, Wexler understood, either an insult or an honor, possibly both.

"You know she's alive," Emrys said. No greeting. His mother's son to the bone.

"I assumed. The overland route was hard, not fatal. Your mother doesn't die in weather she's studied."

"A fishing crew brought her down the coast two days ago. Broken hand, half-starved, carried the last stretch on a stretcher made of outrigger poles." Emrys looked out at the water. "Villagers from upriver found her. They'd already read the transmissions—someone had printed them, carried them inland. They knew her name before she could give it. They called her *their* historian." He let that land. "She's asking to see you."

Wexler set down his cup. "Why?"

"To finish it, I think. She says a record isn't complete until everyone in it has spoken—including the opposition. She wants your account of the incident reports. In her own archive. Under her own hand." Emrys met his eyes at last, and there was no mercy in the look, and no cruelty either—only the terrible fairness of a man who had stopped running. "She'll never forgive you. Neither will I. But she'll listen to you, and that's more than the institute ever did for anyone. Boat leaves at dawn, if you're coming."

Wexler looked downriver, toward the gorge, toward the drowned valley where a city slept under twenty feet of new water with all its secrets finally, irrevocably, elsewhere. Then he looked at the man across the table, waiting without hurry, as though the answer hardly mattered—as though the asking itself were the point.

"Dawn," Wexler said. "I'll be there."
