## 21. Depositions

The hearing room had been a customs house once, back when the river trade meant something more than tourists and salvage barges. You could still see it in the bones of the place—the high windows salted with grime, the brass fittings gone green at the joints, the floorboards worn into shallow channels where clerks had walked the same paths for a hundred years. Now the channels were filling with lawyers.

Emrys sat in the third row because the first two were for counsel and the press and he had spent enough of his life in neither. The room smelled of paper and old varnish and, faintly, of the river itself, which ran gray-green beyond the windows and never let this city forget what it was built on.

Four months since landfall. Four months since the water took the valley and everything in it that could be burned or buried or denied. He had expected the silence afterward to feel like defeat. Instead it felt like held breath—everyone waiting to see whether the transmissions would surface, whether they would hold, whether anyone would dare call them forgeries when three continents' worth of archivists and journalists and descendant communities had received identical copies on the same night from a mission station transmitter older than most of the people now testifying.

The copies held. That was the thing no one at the institute had understood, back when they'd thought evidence was a thing you could drown: Halvard had not built an archive. She had built a fleet. And you cannot sink a fleet by sinking any one ship.

"State your name for the record," said the chairwoman, a dry-voiced woman named Ferro who had once mapped the delta herself and treated every witness with the same flat courtesy, as if they were all sediment samples.

"Nia Okonkwo. Graduate researcher, Khet Valley expedition."

Emrys watched her from behind, the set of her shoulders as she took the stand. She wore a blazer that fit her badly—borrowed, he guessed, from someone with broader taste for formality—and she kept her hands flat on the rail like a woman steadying herself against current. When she spoke, her voice was clear and did not shake, though he knew what it cost. He had heard it shake, in the weeks after, in the delta town, at two in the morning over cold tea, when she told him again about the three men and the fever that wasn't, about the arithmetic of fear that had made staging their deaths seem like mercy.

She testified for two hours. She described the administrative quarter beneath the silt, the sealed record vaults, the ledgers naming the institute's founding trustees—not donors, founders—in the ordering of a massacre a century ago, the clearing of the river cities so the land could pass quietly into concession hands. She described the advance man who had arrived before them, the staged cache, the tire tracks above the gorge. She described Wexler's radio call, and then, without being asked, she described how Wexler had chosen, in the end, to corroborate rather than deny.

"He believed he was protecting an institution," she said. "When the institution disowned him, he stopped believing it. That's all I can tell you about him. It's more than he'd tell you himself."

* * *

They broke at midday, and Emrys walked down to the quay because sitting still indoors had become a discipline he practiced rather than a natural state. The river was high with early meltwater, brown and muscular, pushing barges against their moorings. A boy was fishing off the pilings with a hand line, patient as the tide.

"You look like a man deciding something," Ondine said, materializing beside him the way she always did, soundless on the boards despite her size, coffee in each hand. She passed him one without asking. It was terrible coffee. It had been terrible for four months and he had drunk it every day.

"I'm not deciding anything. That's the point."

"Mm." She looked out at the water. "You've been offered the Corvane charter twice. The Andes survey. There's a woman from the museum who's been circling your boarding house like a gull." She ticked them off on thick fingers. "You've said no to all of them. Saying no is also a decision, little pilot. Sometimes the loudest kind."

He drank the coffee. Below them the boy hauled in nothing, rebaited, cast again.

It was true, what she said. The offers had come the way offers come after a story breaks—first cautiously, then in a flood, everyone wanting the man who had been there, the son of the woman who had done it, the guide who could find anything. Documentary producers. University departments. A foundation that wanted to fund a "responsible recovery initiative" in the drowned valley, as if responsibility were a thing you could purchase retroactively, as if the valley were lost rather than given.

He had declined all of them. Not with speeches. With the same sentence, each time: *I'm not taking charters north right now.* And each time he said it, he noticed it cost less than the last time. That was the strange arithmetic nobody warned you about—that avoidance has weight, but so does refusal, and only one of them lightens as you carry it.

"Halvard called again," Ondine said.

"I know. I was there."

"She wants you to consult on the testimony. The descendant communities want someone who knows the river to help map what was lost. Not lead. Map."

"I know what she wants." He turned the cup in his hands. "She wants me standing next to her in a room full of cameras so the world can see the reconciliation arc complete itself. Mother and son, healed by history. It's a good ending. It's just not mine yet."

Ondine snorted. "And what is yours?"

He didn't answer, and she didn't press, because that was the contract between them, unspoken since the second boat: no questions about cargo or passengers. Some things you carried until you chose to set them down.

* * *

The afternoon session belonged to the descendants.

They came in ones and twos, and then in families, from the delta towns and the hill settlements and from overseas, people whose grandparents had been counted in those ledgers as line items—laborers relocated, villages cleared, deaths recorded in a colonial administrator's neat hand as *attrition, environmental*. One by one they took the stand and read names aloud. Not arguments. Names. An old woman read for eleven minutes without stopping, and the room learned what a century sounds like when spoken at walking pace.

Emrys had heard the records before. He had helped carry them, duplicated them, hidden them in the false bilge of Ondine's boat among the wounded. But hearing the numbers recited by living mouths, in languages the original clerks had never bothered to learn, transformed them. On paper they were evidence. Spoken, they were attendance. The dead, marked present.

Halfway through, he realized his hands were shaking, and made himself watch anyway, because looking away had been his profession for ten years and he was retired.

Halvard sat at the petitioners' table with her right hand bandaged and splinted—the hand the villagers hadn't been able to fully save, the hand she'd broken hauling the last crate of originals through leech country toward a transmitter she reached too late to save the originals themselves, though it hadn't mattered, because the copies had already flown. She listened to the names with her chin lifted slightly, the way she used to listen to stratigraphy reports, and Emrys understood suddenly that this was the happiest she had ever been in his memory. Not triumphant. *Present.* Ten years ago she had looked through him; on the river she had finally looked at him; here she looked past both of them, at the room, at the century, at the accounting finally coming due.

When the session adjourned, she found him by the windows.

"They're dissolving the endowment," she said, without greeting. They had gotten past greetings somewhere around the portage. "Ferro announced it an hour ago. The founding trust—wound down, assets transferred to a reparations commission seated by the descendant councils. Not charity. Transfer. They used the word *transfer*."

"I heard."

"You don't look pleased."

He watched the river. "I keep thinking about the site."

"The site is mud," Halvard said. "The site was always going to be mud. Every site is mud eventually. That's the whole lesson of the profession, Emrys—we are custodians of things on their way to being lost. The only question worth asking is what we do while we have them." She flexed the splinted hand absently, a habit now. "We published nothing and we published everything. You taught me that, if you can believe it. The boy who wouldn't stop running taught the great Dr. Iversen that some cargo travels better scattered."

"That's not what I taught you."

"No," she admitted. "You taught me that loyalty isn't obedience. The rest I worked out myself, mostly out of spite." Almost a smile. Then, quieter: "Come to the mapping sessions. The communities are building a registry of the drowned valley—every district, every family line, from the records and from memory. They asked for you by name. Not your mother's name. Yours."

"Why mine?"

"Because you know the river the way the pilots' grandfathers knew it, and because you're the only person involved who never wanted anything from it." She said it plainly, the way she stated dates and depths, and somehow that made it land harder than praise would have. "Think about it. That's all I'll say. I've spent my life mistaking pressure for persuasion. I'm done with both."

She left him at the window, and the river went on doing what rivers do, which is refuse to be the same river twice.

* * *

That evening the museum woman found him anyway, at the dockside tavern where Ondine held court over a table of river pilots arguing about channel markers. The woman was earnest and well-funded and offered him, in the space of four minutes, directorship of a new expedition, a vessel, a crew, and a mandate. *Recovery of the remaining artifacts from the flood zone,* she called it. Emrys listened the way you listen to weather reports for places you're not going.

"Thank you," he said, when she finished. "No."

"You haven't even considered—"

"I've considered everything," he said, and found he wasn't angry, which surprised him. "That's why it's no. Everything your expedition would pull out of that valley would arrive with our fingerprints already on it. Every artifact would testify about us instead of them. Let the mud keep what it took. The descendants have the records. They have the names. If they ever want the ground opened, it should be their hands on the spades, and it shouldn't be me standing there being photographed."

"And what will you do instead?"

The question hung there, and Emrys felt for the old reflex—the itch to be elsewhere, the next charter, the next horizon, motion as anesthesia. It rose in him like water in a lock, and then, gently, it settled. Because the truth was he wasn't restless anymore. He was just *here*, and here had started to feel less like stopping and more like standing.

"I'm going to take a job," he said. "A small one. Upriver."

The woman blinked. "Upriver? Into the—"

"Not into anything. Just upriver. There's a community two hundred kilometers north that needs a supply run, and a schoolteacher who wants her students to see the gorge before the new dam surveys close it. Ondine's taking the boat. Nia's coming to map the shoreline for the registry—shallow water, no excavation, just lines on charts that belong to the families, not to any institute." He shrugged. "Cargo and passengers. No questions."

"That's not a career, Mr. Iversen. That's a ferry run."

"Yeah," Emrys said, and this time he did smile. "I've heard it's honest work."

Ondine, from across the room, raised her terrible coffee an inch in salute, having heard every word, because she heard everything, and turned back to her argument about channel markers.

Outside, the river moved in the dark, high and patient, carrying its silt down from the drowned valley toward the sea—carrying, as it always had, everything the mountains let go of, keeping nothing, delivering everything, indifferent and indispensable, going where it was going.

Emrys finished his drink, paid, and went home to pack for a journey that was, for the first time in ten years, not away from anything at all.
