## 1. The Weight of Water

The river was lying to him again.

Quentin Ashgrove could feel it through the deck plates, a tremor in the Marguerite's bones that said the water ahead was thinner than it looked. From the wheelhouse window the channel between Gullwing Shoal and the eastern bank appeared broad and brown and generous, the way low water always did at this hour, when the morning light came slantwise off the hills and turned every sandbar into something soft and gold. But the river was a liar with a beautiful face, and Quentin had spent thirty-one years learning not to trust her when she smiled.

He eased the throttle back a quarter notch. The old boat's engine dropped from its working growl to something more like a purr, and he felt the hull settle, felt the waterline rise by inches as she stopped clawing forward and began instead to *think* about where she was going. That was the trick of the shoals. You didn't muscle through them. You persuaded them. You made the river believe you were no threat to it, just another piece of driftwood with opinions.

"Depth," he said, though there was nobody on the deck behind him to answer but the sounding line itself, coiled on its brass hook by the starboard door.

He took it himself, one-handed, without slowing his scan of the water. The lead came up streaked with gray clay and a thread of green weed. Four fathoms in the channel's throat, which meant three and a half where it mattered, which meant the Marguerite — drawing five feet even, six with her holds full — would clear the bar by less than a boot's width if he held the line exactly where the current wanted him to go.

Which was, of course, nowhere near where he intended to go.

The current here ran hard against the eastern point, curling around it like a hand trying to drag him onto the clay shelf that lay submerged and patient beneath the surface, waiting for boats the way spiders wait for flies. Every year it took two or three. Every year the salvage men came down from Port Calloway with their grappling hooks and their cheerful talk about luck, and every year Quentin read about it in the shipping notices and thought: *not luck. Arithmetic.* The river kept its own ledger, and it never forgave a debt, and neither did he.

He swung the wheel two spokes to port and gave her a half notch of steam. The Marguerite leaned into the turn like an old woman leaning into a wind she'd argued with all her life, and for a moment her bow wave went quiet, and then the shoal was under them — he could see it now, a pale smear in the brown, close enough that a man leaning over the rail could have spat on it — and then it was behind them, and the channel opened out, and the engine climbed back into its full voice, and Quentin let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

Thirty-one years. He had run this stretch of river in flood and in drought, in fog so thick the pilothouse windows might have been painted over, in the first gray light of the dry season when the bars shifted overnight like furniture moved by a drunk. He knew the water the way other men knew their mothers' faces, and he trusted it about as far.

That was the whole secret, if anyone had ever asked him, and no one ever had. Not skill. Not courage. Just distrust, applied patiently, over decades. The river rewarded the man who assumed it was always about to kill him. It ate everyone else.

*Keep moving,* his father had told him, once, the only piece of inheritance worth having. *A boat that stops starts dying. A man who stops starts belonging. And belonging, boy, is how they get you.*

His father had been dead twenty-two years and still talked too much.

Quentin wiped his palms on his trousers and checked the barometer bolted beside the wheel. It had fallen overnight — not dramatically, not yet, but steadily, the needle drifting down like a leaf deciding to fall. The monsoon was still weeks out, somewhere over the ocean gathering its strength, but the air already had that weight to it, that wet-wool heaviness that made the hills look closer than they were. By the shipping almanac's reckoning they had nineteen days of reliable weather above the falls before the upper river turned into something no chart could help you survive.

Nineteen days. Two more runs to the head of navigation and back, if the freight contracts held, and then he'd tie up at Calloway and let the season do what seasons do.

He didn't mind the waiting, he told himself. Waiting wasn't stopping. Waiting was just moving very slowly in place.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually did, at Harker's Landing.

It was past noon when he brought the Marguerite in against the rotting pilings, and the landing looked the way it always looked in the dry months — gaunt, exposed, its mud banks cracked into a mosaic of dried polygons like the skin of some enormous sleeping thing. The town behind it consisted of a chandlery, a fuel depot, a saloon with a false front, and forty people who had all, at one time or another, tried to sell Quentin something.

He had cargo to drop — machine parts for the mine road, crated and stenciled, and a passenger manifest with exactly zero names on it, because Quentin Ashgrove did not carry passengers. Passengers asked questions. Passengers had destinations, and destinations were a kind of debt. Freight didn't care where it was going. Freight was honest.

He was supervising the crane swing when he saw the man come down the gangway ramp, and Quentin's stomach performed the small cold contraction it reserved for exactly one category of human being.

The man wore a gray suit that had been good five years ago, and a straw hat with a sweat-stained band, and he carried a leather folio under his arm the way a priest carries scripture. He walked with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never once been thrown off a dock, and he stopped at the foot of the ramp and waited, because he knew — they always knew — that the captain would have to come to him eventually.

Quentin considered, briefly, weighing anchor and leaving with the crate half-slung. But the mine contract was a third of his dry-season income, and the foreman at the depot was watching, and a man's reputation on this river was the only collateral he'd ever had. So he finished the lift, signed the chit, and walked down the ramp with his hands loose at his sides and his face arranged into the mild, unremarkable expression that had gotten him out of more trouble than any gun ever had.

"Captain Ashgrove." The man tipped his hat brim. He had a narrow face and small, quick eyes, the eyes of a bookkeeper or a ferret. "Milo Rench. Acting on behalf of the Consolidated River & Trade Bank."

"I don't bank," Quentin said.

"Everyone banks." Rench smiled. "Some of us just don't know it yet. May I?" He gestured at the folio without opening it, a conjurer's courtesy. "Your vessel, the Marguerite, carries an encumbrance. An old one. A repair note drawn against her hull, eleven years ago, at the Calloway yard. Principal plus accumulated interest comes to a figure I suspect you'll find educational."

Quentin remembered the repair. Eleven years ago, ice damage, a torn plating seam below the waterline, and a winter with no work and no choices. He remembered signing. He remembered telling himself he'd clear it inside two seasons.

"I've paid on that note," he said.

"You've paid *around* it. Payments made to the yard directly, payments made to a factor who subsequently absconded, payments made in kind against freight discounts none of which were ever properly endorsed against principal." Rench opened the folio at last, and the pages inside were covered in a clerk's precise hand, columns of figures marching down like soldiers. "The bank has recently acquired certain paper. Old paper. Neglected paper. And the bank is given to understand that the Marguerite is currently generating revenue."

"She's generating nothing. She's a hole in the water I pour money into."

"Then she should be easy to surrender."

For a moment the dock seemed very quiet. The crane operator up on the deck paused with his load half-slung, and the foreman by the depot found something fascinating to study in the middle distance, and the river went on muttering against the pilings below them, indifferent as arithmetic.

"No," Quentin said.

Rench sighed, and it was a performance, a fine silk sigh, the sigh of a reasonable man confronted with unreasonable things. "Captain. I'm not your enemy. I'm a mirror. I show you what you owe. What you do about it is your own affair. But I will say this—" and here he closed the folio, gently, lovingly, "—the bank has options. Attachment proceedings. A writ of seizure executed at any port of call. We could take her while she sleeps, Captain. Boats are so vulnerable when they sleep."

"You'd need a court order."

"Courts are downstream of money, Captain. Everything is downstream of money. Surely a man of your experience knows that the law, like the river, only flows one direction." Rench put his hat back on straight and stepped back from the ramp, and his little ferret eyes did something Quentin had seen before on other men's faces in other years — a flicker of genuine appetite, quickly buried. "I'll be making my report. Do think about terms. The bank prefers cooperation, but the bank can afford preference."

He walked back up the ramp without hurrying, and Quentin stood at the foot of it in the flat white heat of the afternoon and felt the old familiar thing wake up in his chest — not fear exactly, fear was too clean a word for it. It was the feeling of a door closing somewhere far away, of a circle tightening, of the world reminding him that a man alone on a river was never actually alone, that debts followed you like wakes follow a hull, long after the reason for them has sunk out of sight.

Eleven years. Eleven years of runs, of shoals, of nights spent nursing the engine through weather that should have killed them both, and the debt hadn't shrunk. Debts didn't shrink. They grew, quietly, in the dark, like roots under a house.

*Keep moving,* his father said in his skull. *Owe no one.*

Too late for that. Too late by eleven years and a winter he couldn't take back.

* * *

He cast off at dusk, against his own better judgment, because staying the night at Harker's Landing suddenly felt like standing still in open country during a firefight. The Marguerite backed off the pilings with her stern lamp burning red, and the landing shrank behind them into a smudge of lamplight and woodsmoke, and Quentin took the wheel himself because he didn't trust his hands to be idle.

The river at dusk was a different animal than the river at noon. The light died out of it in layers — first the gold, then the copper, then the long gray-green twilight in which the water became simply a presence, a vast breathing darkness on either side of the channel, and the only truth left was the one in front of your bow. He ran slow, on the edge of the current where the deep water lived, and the fireflies came out along the banks in their thousands, stitching the darkness together with cold green sparks.

Up in the wheelhouse it was cool, and the compass card glowed faintly under its gimbal, and the engine spoke its steady sentence below his feet — *I am here, I am here, I am here* — and for a little while the day's arithmetic receded, and there was only the boat and the water and the man between them, which was the only arrangement Quentin had ever trusted.

He thought about his brother, and stopped thinking about his brother, in the practiced way of a man closing a drawer he'd learned not to open on the water.

Instead he thought about the barometer. Down another tick since noon. The air had weight in it, and the sunset had gone a strange bruised color behind the western hills, banded like a mussel shell, and old pilots said a sky like that meant rain coming from a long way off, rain with intentions.

Nineteen days, give or take. He'd make his runs, square what he could square, and be tied up safe when the sky finally broke.

He was reaching for the throttle to ease her around the bend above Widow's Reach when the light caught it — a shape on the downstream end of the landing dock, sitting on a bollard like it had grown there. A man. Sitting motionless in the last gray light, with a canvas bag at his feet and something folded in his lap, and as the Marguerite slid past, close in toward the channel, the man stood up.

He swayed when he stood. Quentin saw it even at fifty yards, the way the figure listed like a mast with a broken stay. And then the man raised one arm — not waving, exactly. More like hailing. Like a man signaling a ship from a shore he'd been wrecked on a long time ago.

Quentin's thumb rested on the whistle cord.

Every rule he had said keep going. No passengers. No strangers after dark. No debts that weren't denominated in freight and coin. The river rewarded distrust; it ate everyone else. His father's voice, dead twenty-two years and still talking: *a man who stops starts belonging.*

But the man on the bollard swayed again, and this time his knees went, and he caught himself on the capstan housing, and even across the water Quentin could hear the sound he made — not a shout, not a cry for help, but something worse. Something quieter. A kind of question.

The Marguerite's bell rang once, soft, in the evening air, and Quentin swore at himself under his breath, and rang it again.
