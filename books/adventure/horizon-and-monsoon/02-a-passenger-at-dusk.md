## 2. A Passenger at Dusk

The Marguerite sat low in the water that evening, riding the last of the day's heat like a tired animal, and Quentin Ashgrove stood at her rail with a tin cup of coffee gone cold in his hand, watching the sun go down over Cutter's Landing the way he watched everything — from a slight distance, as if it might turn on him.

The river was doing its evening trick again. All day it had been brown and busy, hauling silt and sticks and the occasional drowned pig past the pilings, but now, with the light failing, it had gone the color of hammered pewter, smooth on top and full of secrets underneath. A kingfisher rattled down the channel and was gone. Somewhere behind the fish sheds, someone was frying onions, and the smell came out over the water in greasy ribbons that made his stomach remember it hadn't been fed since morning.

He'd tied up at Cutter's Landing for one reason only: diesel. The fuel barge ran Tuesdays and Fridays, and today was Friday, and by Saturday noon he intended to be forty miles downstream where nobody knew his name and nobody wanted to. That was the arithmetic of his life, simple as tide tables. Stop long enough to take on what you need. Never long enough for anyone to learn your habits.

Three days was already too long. Three days of patching the starboard seam, three days of the harbormaster's clerk asking friendly questions about where he wintered, three days of old Mrs. Calloway at the chandlery telling him she remembered when his mother used to run this same stretch in a boat not much bigger than the Marguerite, God rest her. He didn't want to be remembered. Being remembered was how debts found you.

And debts had found him anyway, because they always did.

He could see the man from the fuel office coming down the plank walk now, and Quentin's shoulders set themselves before his brain caught up. Broad man, checked shirt, the rolling gait of somebody who got paid by results. Not the harbormaster. Not the chandlery. The wrong kind of visitor entirely.

"Ashgrove." The man stopped at the foot of the gangplank without coming aboard, which Quentin noted and appreciated. Men who stayed off your deck were men who still thought there was a chance of getting paid. "Name's Dreever. I'm acting for Halloway Brothers, factors."

"I don't know any Halloway Brothers."

"You don't have to. You know Silas Merrick, though. Or you did, four years back, up the Sable Forks, when you co-signed a note on an engine block."

Quentin drank the cold coffee. It tasted like the bottom of a rain barrel. "Silas Merrick is dead."

"Dead men's notes don't die, Captain. That's the whole beauty of paper." Dreever smiled with the lower half of his face only. "Two hundred and forty dollars, plus interest, plus my fee for finding you. And I'm good at finding people. You've been hard to find, I'll give you that. Four years. Most men I catch inside a season."

"Then consider yourself bested, and go home."

"I considered letting it go, actually. Then I heard you were taking on fuel, which means you're going somewhere, which means you're earning, which means—" he spread his hands, "—you can pay. I'll take fifty now and the rest against your next port. I'll even write it down nice."

The trouble with stopping, Quentin thought, was exactly this. A moving man owed nothing because no one could catch him. A stopped man was a target painted on the water. His father had told him that once, drunk, on a night Quentin had spent thirty years trying to forget, and like most things his father said it had been cruel and true in equal measure.

"I'll give you twenty," Quentin said. "Cash. And you'll tell Halloway Brothers that's all there is, or they can sue a man who'll be two hundred miles away by Sunday."

Dreever chewed on that, looking past him at the Marguerite — at her patched seams, her rust-streaked stack, the wheelhouse glass gone cloudy with salt. Judging what she was worth. Judging what Quentin was worth. "Thirty," he said finally, "and I don't come back before the dry season."

"Twenty-five, and you never come back at all."

They shook on it, because on the river a handshake was still worth something, though less every year. Quentin fetched the money from the lockbox under his bunk — counting it out twice, feeling each bill leave him like blood — and Dreever pocketed it, touched his hat brim, and rolled away up the plank walk into the onion-smelling dusk.

Quentin stood at the rail until he was gone, then a while longer.

*Never stop,* he told himself. *Never owe. Never belong.* He'd written it nowhere, because writing things down was another way of being found, but he kept it the way other men kept scripture. And yet here he was, lighter by twenty-five dollars, tied to a dock in a town where a woman remembered his mother, and tomorrow he would still be here, because the starboard seam needed one more day of pitch and canvas, and a man who sank his boat to keep a rule was a fool twice over.

The river didn't care about rules. That was the other thing his father never understood. The river took whoever it took.

* * *

The stranger came down to the landing as the light went out of the sky.

Quentin noticed him first as a problem of silhouettes — a figure standing too still at the head of the ramp while the fishing crews clattered past with their crates, a stillness that didn't fit the hour. Everyone else at Cutter's Landing at dusk was in motion, tired motion, the motion of men going home to food. This man stood like a post. Then he swayed, caught himself on the ramp rail, and began to descend, and Quentin saw that every step cost him something.

By the time he reached the foot of the gangplank, Quentin had already decided not to like him. That was procedure. Strangers who sought you out at dusk, alone, walking like their bones hurt, were either dying or dangerous, and in Quentin's experience the two categories overlapped more than anyone liked to admit.

"Captain." The voice was wrecked — gravel dragged through fever. Up close the man resolved into details: sixty-odd years, maybe less worn down to look it; a beard gone half grey, half mud; eyes too bright, the wet shine of a lamp burning its oil too fast. His clothes had been good once, canvas and brass buttons, now stiff with dried sweat and river clay. Prospector, Quentin thought. Upriver man. The kind who came down out of the hills twice a decade with gold dust or malaria, usually both. "Captain Ashgrove?"

"No."

The man blinked. "The harbor clerk said—"

"The harbor clerk says a lot of things." Quentin set his empty cup on the rail. "Boat's not hiring crew, not selling passage, not running charters. Whatever you want, the answer's ahead of the question."

"It's not passage I want." The stranger gripped the rail, and Quentin saw his knuckles whiten around it, saw the effort that went into staying upright. "It's distance. Yours."

That was a strange way to put it, and Quentin had heard strangers say strange things at dusk for thirty years. He said nothing, which on the Marguerite was an invitation, and the man took it.

"There's a place," the stranger said. "Upstream. Past the licensed limit — past where your papers let you go, I know how far your papers let you go, I've read the charts." A thin smile moved through the beard. "You're the only boat at this landing rated for the upper narrows. I checked. I've been checking for six days."

"Then you know the answer's no. The upper narrows are closed to licensed traffic above Stone Latch. Company water. Meridian permits only, and I don't hold one."

"I know you don't. That's why I'm here." The man fumbled at his chest, at a lanyard under his shirt, and brought up a packet wrapped in oilskin, folded flat and tied with waxed cord. He held it out across the gap between dock and deck like a man offering communion. "Payment. In full. For the trip."

Quentin looked at the packet and did not touch it.

"What is it?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters plenty. I've been offered payment before. Once it was a sack of guano. Once it was a woman's wedding ring, and I still think about that woman. Paper's the least of it — paper can be anything. Stolen survey sheets. Warrants. Love letters that'll hang somebody." He nodded at the oilskin. "So. What is it?"

The stranger's bright eyes held his, and for a moment the fever in them steadied into something harder and older. "A map," he said. "To the headwaters. To the source itself, past the falls, past everything your charts call *unsurveyed*, which is a lie the Company prints to keep people out." He turned the packet slowly in his fingers. "There's a valley up there, Captain. There's a valley up there that would make every man on this river rich, and there are men who killed three of my partners to make sure nobody ever sees it but them. I'm the fourth partner. I'm the last one left. And I am dying, and I need to get home before I do."

The word *home* landed strangely, coming from a man whose clothes smelled of woodsmoke and sickness and a hundred miles of walking. Quentin looked at the packet. Oilskin meant the owner expected water. Waxed cord meant he expected time. Both together meant a man who had planned for his own death the way other men planned for winter.

"Home," Quentin repeated. "Where's home for a man like you?"

"Up there." The stranger tipped his chin toward the darkening north, where the river ran out of sight between hills that were already just black shapes against a bruised sky. "Or near enough. There's someone I have to reach. Someone who has to get what's in this packet, whether I reach her or not."

*Her.* Quentin filed that away and hated himself for filing it. Curiosity was the first hook, and hooks were how moving men became stopped men.

"Look," he said, and made his voice flat and kind at once, the voice he used on drowning men, "I'll tell you what I tell everyone. I run cargo downriver. Diesel, salt, nails, whatever pays. I don't run passengers, I don't run contraband, and I sure as hell don't run unlicensed water. Above Stone Latch there's Company gunboats, Company checkpoints, and Company lawyers, and none of the three care whether you're dying. Take that packet to the magistrate at Fort Amity if it's evidence. Take it to a newspaper. Don't take it to me."

"They killed my partners, Captain." The words came out level, which somehow made them worse. "Not robbed. Killed. Tom Osgood they hung from his own windlass and called it suicide. The Delacour brothers they burned in their cabin and called it an accident, and the magistrate wrote *accident* in his book because the magistrate eats from Meridian's hand. There is no magistrate for me. There's no newspaper that isn't bought. There's a river, and a boat, and you." He swayed again, caught the rail, held on. "I've walked eleven days. I ate my boots on the eighth. I'm not asking you to fight anyone. I'm asking you to carry a sick old man upstream and look the other way at the checkpoints, same as you'd do for a crate of untaxed tobacco."

"That's the second time tonight someone's asked me to owe somebody," Quentin said. "Bad luck comes in pairs, my mother used to say. So does begging."

"Your mother sounds like she was right about most things."

Something moved in Quentin's chest at that — small, unwelcome, quickly smothered. He stepped back from the rail. "Answer's no. Go to the mission house, they'll feed you. Go to Dr. Prasad's, he'll bleed you or dose you, whichever you believe in. But get off my dock."

The stranger looked at him for a long moment. The fever-shine in his eyes had dimmed, and what was left underneath was worse — a flat, patient exhaustion, the look of a man who had been refused so many times that refusal had become a kind of weather. He lowered the oilskin packet, folded it away inside his shirt, and nodded once.

"I'll ask the next boat, then."

"There is no next boat. Nothing else at this landing is rated above the Latch, and nothing else will be till the dry season."

"Then I'll wait for the dry season." He smiled, and it was terrible, that smile — the smile of a man pricing his own remaining days against a calendar. "Maybe I'll make it. Maybe the fever will do what the Company couldn't." He turned, and his legs betrayed him on the second step.

Quentin saw it happen the way you see anything inevitable — too late to stop it, early enough to feel responsible. The man's knee buckled, his hand slipped off the rail slick with evening damp, and Elias Vane — though Quentin didn't yet know the name — went down onto the planks of the dock with a sound like a dropped sandbag. The oilskin packet skidded free and lay in the lamplight between them, plain as a confession.

For three full seconds, Quentin did nothing. That was the creed working. *Keep moving. Owe no one.* A man collapsed on a public dock was the harbormaster's problem, the mission's problem, the river's problem. Every minute he stood there he became part of it, and parts of things were what he had sworn off being since he was nineteen years old and had learned what it cost to be part of a family.

But the man wasn't moving. Wasn't groaning, even. Just lying there in the yellow pool of the dock lamp, breathing in shallow, tearing pulls, one hand still curled as if around a packet that wasn't there anymore.

Quentin swore — a long, quiet, comprehensive oath in the deckhand's dialect he'd learned before he learned letters — and swung down the gangplank.

He knelt. Up close the smell of the man hit him: sickness, yes, but under it woodsmoke and pine pitch and something mineral, cave-cold, the smell of deep rock. His skin, where Quentin's fingers found his jaw, was burning. Pulse quick and thin as a bird's. The eyes opened halfway and focused on Quentin with what seemed, absurdly, like satisfaction.

"Told you," Vane whispered. "No next boat."

"Shut up." Quentin got an arm under the man's shoulders — lighter than he should have been, a frame gone hollow as a reed — and hauled him upright. Vane's head lolled, then steadied. "Mission house is a mile. You won't walk it. So you'll sleep in my forward hold tonight, on a coil of rope, and tomorrow you'll be gone, and we'll both pretend this never happened. That's not passage. That's charity, and charity ends at sunrise. Do you hear me?"

Vane's mouth moved. It took two tries to get the words out.

"Heard you the first time, Captain." A breath. A ghost of that terrible smile. "Everybody always does."

Quentin got him up the gangplank step by step, the man's boots dragging, the dock lamp throwing their doubled shadow long across the Marguerite's deck. Behind them the packet lay where it had fallen, oilskin gleaming in the lamplight like something alive.

He went back for it. He told himself it was so the harbormaster wouldn't find it and start asking questions, and that was even true, mostly. He carried it aboard and set it on the chart table in the wheelhouse, where the lamplight caught the waxed cord and the neat, sailorly knots, and he stood looking at it for a long time while somewhere below him a dying man coughed himself toward morning.

Then he blew out the lamp, because whatever was in the packet, he decided, he was better off not seeing it by its own light.

Tomorrow it would be gone, and so would its owner, and the Marguerite would point downriver at dawn like she always did, toward open water and clean distances.

Below his feet, faint through the deck boards, Elias Vane said a name in his sleep. It sounded like *Sera*.
