## 3. The Prospector's Bargain

The fever broke near midnight and came back with the dawn, the way fevers do on this river — like a tide that has learned your schedule and means to keep it.

Quentin had not slept. He told himself it was because he'd been keeping watch on the Marguerite, because a man who left his boat unwatched at a public dock deserved whatever walked off with her, and there was some truth in that. The *Marguerite* was the only thing he owned outright, free of lien and ledger, and he guarded her the way other men guarded their children or their reputations — jealously, without apology, and with the constant low hum of expectation that the world would try to take her if he blinked.

But the larger truth was the stranger in his cabin, breathing.

Elias Vane lay on the bunk beneath the porthole, and the first grey light of morning came through the glass and laid itself across the man's face like a sheet being drawn up by careful hands. His skin had gone the color of old tallow. The fever painted two bright spots high on his cheeks, and his lips were cracked and dark, and every breath came with a small wet sound at the bottom of it, like water finding its way into a hull that should have been sound.

Quentin sat in the pilot's chair he'd dragged from the wheelhouse, boots crossed at the ankle, a tin cup of coffee going cold in his hand. He watched the man breathe and counted the breaths without meaning to. It was a habit from another life, from a sickroom he did not let himself visit in daylight, and he shut the door on it as soon as he noticed what he was doing.

Around noon, Vane opened his eyes.

They were remarkable eyes for a dying man — pale grey-green, river-colored, and still sharp at the center, still taking inventory. They found Quentin immediately, tracked across the low cabin ceiling, and settled on him with an expression that was not gratitude and not fear but something more unsettling: recognition. As if Vane had known all along exactly what kind of man would be sitting in that chair when he woke.

"You didn't leave," Vane said. His voice was gravel dragged through water.

"High water's three weeks out. Nowhere I need to be."

"Liar." A ghost of a smile moved the cracked lips. "You're always somewhere you need to be. Men like you are always running toward the next empty place."

Quentin drank his cold coffee rather than answer. It was true, and they both knew it was true, and there was no profit in arguing with a man whose pulse he could see beating in the hollow of his throat.

"I'm not carrying you upriver," Quentin said. "I said so last night and I'll say it again when you're well enough to hear it properly. Past Marrow Bend the channel's unlicensed. Company water. They impound boats for less than what you're asking, and they don't pay salvage on what they take."

"The Company." Vane said the word the way other men spat. He worked himself up onto one elbow, and the effort cost him visibly — a fine tremor ran through his arm, and his breath went ragged — but he held the position, propped and swaying, like a post the flood hadn't managed to pull yet. "How much do you know about the Meridian Company, Captain Ashgrove?"

"Enough. They own the upper licenses. They own the dredges, the timber rights, the assay office, half the magistrates, and the gunboats to make it stick. I run cargo below the line and I keep my nose on my own side of the glass. That's how I've kept my boat and my skin for eleven years."

"And before that?"

"That's none of yours."

Vane nodded slowly, as if Quentin had answered anyway. "Eleven years," he murmured. "That would have been right after. Right after your brother."

The cabin went very quiet. Somewhere on the dock above, a winch groaned; gulls argued over something rotten; the river slapped the hull in its patient, indifferent rhythm. Quentin set the coffee cup down on the chart table with exaggerated care, because the alternative was to throw it.

"You talked in your sleep," Vane said. "Not much. A name. You said *hold on* and then you said a name, and then you said *the water's too fast*. That's all. I wasn't prying." He paused. "I know what it is to say a name over water."

Quentin looked at him for a long moment. The anger was there, hot and immediate, and underneath the anger something worse — the cold sliding feeling of being seen by a stranger, of having a door he'd bricked over thirty years ago kicked open by a dying prospector's fever-talk. He made himself breathe. He made himself pick the cup back up.

"Don't do that again," he said quietly.

"No," Vane agreed. "I won't need to. Because in a day, maybe two, I'll be past doing anything at all, and everything I have to say will fit in the time I've got left. So listen now, while listening is cheap."

He swung his legs off the bunk. It took him the better part of a minute and Quentin did not help him, partly out of stubbornness and partly because he suspected the old man would rather die than be helped. Vane ended sitting upright on the edge of the mattress, hands flat on his knees, swaying gently with the motion of the river against the hull, a man relearning his sea legs for a voyage of about forty-eight hours.

"Three years ago," Vane said, "I found the headwaters."

Quentin kept his face still. Every boatman on the river had heard the stories — the source valley above the great falls, where the whole watershed gathered itself out of the rock, where the gold ran coarse enough to pan with your hands, where the Company's surveys mysteriously stopped and their gunboats mysteriously didn't. Half the stories were drunk talk. The other half were bait for fools. But every one of them, drunk or baited, circled the same forbidden ground, and every man who claimed to have been there had either come back rich and silent or not come back at all.

"There's a valley," Vane went on, "a long green fold in the mountains where the river is born twice — once out of the glacier, once out of the springs along the east wall. Terraced slopes. Old stone weirs across the channels, built by hands, hundreds of years of hands. Fruit trees gone wild. And gold, yes, gold enough, but that's the least of it. The least of it, Captain. What's there is proof. Proof of who lived there, and proof of how they were made to leave, and proof that every deed the Meridian Company holds on that valley is ink over blood."

"Proof," Quentin repeated. "Paper proof?"

"Paper, stone, and witness." Vane's eyes had gone bright, and not only with fever. "There were four of us. Me and three partners. We spent two seasons mapping it properly — survey-grade work, chain and transit, the kind that stands up in a courtroom. We found the old boundary stones the Riverkin set before any Company charter existed. We found the original community register, hidden in a dry cave above the weir pool. Names, births, deaths, land holdings, going back generations. And we found the deeds the Company filed — and the dates on those deeds don't match the dates on the removal orders, and the signatures don't match the men who supposedly signed them. Forged, Captain. Forged root and branch. The whole legal foundation of the upper licenses is a lie with a wax seal on it."

"And your partners?"

Vane was quiet for a moment. Outside, the gulls had gone. The river kept talking to the hull.

"Joss died in March. Fell from a tramway trestle above Corben's Landing. The Company called it an accident. Joss had worked trestles for twenty years and never dropped a tool, let alone himself. Halloran went in June. Found floating under the fish market dock with his pockets full of stones. Ruled a suicide. Halloran couldn't swim a stroke but he loved life like a miser loves coin — he wouldn't have spent it, not that way." Vane's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "And Merrick. Merrick they took last month. Walked into his rooming house in Port Calloway and never walked out. No body. No ruling. Nothing. Just an empty room and a landlord who suddenly remembered an errand in another town."

"So you ran."

"I carried." Vane touched his chest, where beneath the blanket something flat and rectangular shifted — the oilskin packet, tied with waxed cord, that he'd offered on the dock the night before. "Everything. The map. My journal. Copies of the forged deeds beside the originals. The register pages. Two seasons of work and three dead friends, wrapped in oilskin and riding against my heart. And the Company knows I have it. That's why the fever isn't the thing killing me, Captain — though it's doing its best. There's a man named Aldous Crane. Company surveyor. Polite as a churchwarden and thorough as rot. He signed the fire permit for the records annex in Corben's Landing, and he's signed other things too, and he has been one step behind me since Merrick disappeared. One step. I've been faster because I know the water and he doesn't. But I'm done being fast."

He said it simply, without drama, the way a man reports the weather. Quentin found his hands had closed around the arms of the chair.

"So here is the bargain." Vane leaned forward, and the effort of it put sweat on his forehead, and he ignored it. "Two promises. Not payment — promises. I haven't got money worth the name and we both know it. First: carry me upriver. Not all the way — I know better than to ask for the falls, and I couldn't survive them anyway. Carry me as far as Stone Latch, past the licensed limit, as far as you dare. I want to die looking at country the Company hasn't paved. That's the first promise, and it's a selfish one, and I'll not pretend otherwise."

"And the second?"

"The packet goes to my daughter." Something moved through Vane's face then, the first crack in the level voice — grief, or shame, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Sera Vane. She works the salvage yard in Port Calloway — Kessler's yard, on the mudflats south of the breakwater. Dark-haired. Got her mother's mouth and my stubbornness, God help her. She hasn't seen me in nine years and she has no reason to want to. Tell her nothing soft on my behalf. Don't dress me up. Just put it in her hands and tell her it's from her father, and let her do with it what she will. Burn it, sell it, use it. Hers. All of it hers. That's the second promise."

Quentin sat back. He looked at the dying man perched on the edge of the bunk, swaying with the river, holding his whole burned-down life against his chest, and he felt the trap close around him with the gentle, inevitable pressure of rising water.

Because it was a trap. He could see the shape of it plainly. The first promise was the dangerous one — Company water, gunboat patrols, a boat full of contraband paper and a dying man, all of it dressed up as a favor between strangers. The second promise was worse in a quieter way. Deliveries he understood. Freight was freight. But a packet like this one, delivered to a daughter who wanted nothing from its sender — that wasn't freight. That was a debt that would follow him. That was belonging, in its most corrosive form: someone else's unfinished business, taken aboard and made his own.

His creed was simple and he'd paid for it honestly, over years, in the only currency that mattered. Keep moving. Owe no one. Stop for no one. The river forgave everything except mooring.

"I'll take the delivery," Quentin said. "Port Calloway's on my route anyway. I'll put the packet in Sera Vane's hands myself, unopened, and tell her what you told me to tell her. That I'll do."

Vane's eyes narrowed. Fever-bright, river-colored, and sharp as a surveyor's needle. "And the first promise?"

"Stone Latch is Company water. I told you—"

"You told me what it costs. I'm telling you what it's worth." Vane pushed himself straighter, and for a moment the tremor left him entirely, and he looked — impossibly — like a man standing on his own deck in good weather. "Captain Ashgrove. I am going to die within two days whether this boat moves or doesn't. The only question your answer changes is where. If you say no, I die in this bunk in a licensed port, and Crane's people find the packet on a dead man in a rented berth, and two seasons of truth go into the same dark water my partners did. If you say yes, I die looking at unmapped hills, and the packet goes home to my daughter, and maybe — maybe — the valley gets its name back someday. That's the whole arithmetic. I can't make it simpler and I won't make it prettier."

"It's not the arithmetic I don't like. It's the interest." Quentin stood, finally, and crossed to the porthole, where the brown-green water slid past the glass, patient and endless. "Men like me stay alive by owing nothing. You're asking me to owe a dead man. Dead men collect forever."

"Then don't owe me." Vane's voice had gone softer. "Owe them. Owe Joss and Halloran and Merrick. Or don't owe anyone at all — just carry a passenger as far as Stone Latch and charge him double for the trouble, and tell yourself whatever story lets you sleep. I don't care what you call it, Captain. I care that the boat moves."

For a long time Quentin said nothing. Through the porthole he watched a barge train crawl downriver, riding deep with Company ore, flying the black-and-white pennant from its lead mast, escorted by a patrol launch with a mounted gun that swept the banks in slow, bored arcs. Eleven years of keeping his nose on his own side of the glass. Eleven years of watching that pennant go by and telling himself the river was big enough for everyone's silence.

He thought of a name spoken over fast water. He shut the door on it. It stayed open a crack, the way it always did.

"Stone Latch," he said at last. "No farther. If the river drops or the patrols thicken, I turn around and you get your view from wherever we happen to be. And the packet comes off this boat at Port Calloway into Sera Vane's actual hands — not a mailbox, not a neighbor, not a note pinned to a door. Her hands. If she throws it in the mudflats, that's her business and mine ends there. Those are my terms and they don't move."

Vane exhaled — a long, rattling breath that seemed to empty him of something heavier than air. He extended his hand across the space between them: broad, scarred, steady despite everything, the hand of a man who had gripped rock and rope and cold water for fifty years.

"Those are fair terms," he said. "Better than I hoped."

Quentin took the hand. It was burning hot and dry as paper, and it gripped him with startling strength, and for one strange moment he felt the whole weight of the bargain pass through that grip — the map, the dead partners, the daughter on the mudflats, the valley with its hundred-year weirs — settling onto his shoulders like ballast loaded wrong, slightly off-center, the kind of load that makes a boat handle differently in rough water.

"A promise made too easily is worth nothing," Quentin heard himself say. "So understand me. I'm making this one hard, and I'll keep it hard, and I'll hate every mile of it. That's the best warranty you're getting."

"It's the only kind worth having." Vane released his hand and sagged back against the bulkhead, spent, smiling faintly. "You'll do, Captain. I knew it on the dock. You've got the look of a man who keeps his word out of spite."

"Out of habit," Quentin corrected. "Spite costs extra."

Vane laughed — a short, cracked sound that turned into coughing, and the coughing bent him double, and Quentin fetched the water flask and held it to the old man's lips without quite deciding to. When the fit passed, Vane lay back on the bunk, gray and wrung out, and his fingers found the oilskin packet beneath the blanket and rested on it, the way a man rests his hand on a sleeping child.

"She'll hate me," he said, to the ceiling. "Sera. She should. Nine years is nine years. But she'll open it. She's her mother's daughter — she'll want to know the shape of a thing before she decides what it weighs." His eyes drifted closed. "Kessler's yard. South of the breakwater. Ask for the woman who can price a drowned engine by ear."

By evening he was sleeping again, shallow and fast, the tide coming back in. Quentin stood a long while in the doorway of his own cabin, feeling like a guest in it, then climbed to the wheelhouse and lit the running lamps and studied the river going dark.

Above Marrow Bend the channel markers gave out. Above Stone Latch, the charts went blank and the Company gunboats began. Nineteen days, perhaps twenty, until the monsoon made the upper river a rumor. He had fuel for the run, water for eight days, and a cargo hold full of nothing but a dying man and a packet of paper that three men had already died for.

Keep moving, he told himself. Owe no one.

It sounded, for the first time in eleven years, like a rule he was breaking rather than keeping.

He rang down for cold grub, checked the fuel lines twice, and at first light cast off the *Marguerite* from the dock and turned her bow upriver — past the last honest marker, past the line on the chart where his world ended — while behind him, far downriver and closing like weather, the first grey shapes of a Company patrol launch rounded Corben's Point.

They were headed for the port he'd just left. Quentin watched them in the glass for a long minute, doing the arithmetic of wakes and distances, and did not like the sum.

He opened the throttle.
