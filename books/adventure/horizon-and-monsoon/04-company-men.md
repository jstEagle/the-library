## 4. Company Men

The grey boat came upriver at half past nine in the morning, when the light on the water was still soft enough to flatter anything — even a gunboat dressed as a survey launch.

Quentin saw it first from the wheelhouse of the *Marguerite*, where he stood with one hand on the throttle and the other around a cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago. The vessel sat low in the water, painted the color of weathered slate, with a canvas awning aft that tried very hard to look civilian and did not succeed. No pleasure craft carried that much freeboard reinforcement. No survey crew mounted its lamps like searchlights. And no honest boat ran without a name on the stern.

He watched it come for a long minute before he moved. That was a habit older than his river license: see everything, touch nothing, decide last. The grey boat held its course a moment longer than courtesy required — holding straight at the *Marguerite* as if she were a fixed point on the chart — then swung wide and came alongside with a wash that rocked the old steamer against her mooring lines.

Two men stepped across. Not jumped, not climbed — *stepped*, the way men step who have done it a hundred times onto boats they had no business boarding. They wore matching coats, grey wool cut well, buttoned to the throat despite the heat. Their boots were wrong for the river. Too stiff. Boots made for office floors and courthouse steps, not for decks that rolled.

The taller one smiled before he reached the rail. Quentin noted the smile the way he'd note a snag marker: there, visible, and meaning something underneath.

"Captain Ashgrove?" The man's voice was pleasant, pitched to carry no farther than the wheelhouse door. "Meridian Company. My name is Halloway. This is my associate, Mr. Breck." The shorter man said nothing. He had already stopped looking at Quentin and started looking at the boat.

"Passengers or cargo?" Quentin asked. He leaned in the doorway of the wheelhouse with his coffee cup, letting his weight settle casual, letting the morning do its work on them. "I don't run freight for suits, and I don't sell tickets by the hour."

"Neither, I'm happy to say." Halloway produced a leather folio and opened it with a small flourish, showing a card stamped with the Meridian seal — the river rendered as a straight line, which told Quentin more about the Company than any pamphlet could. A river drawn by men who wished it were a road. "We're making inquiries. Routine ones. There's been a theft of Company property — survey documents, instruments, papers of some sensitivity. We believe the party responsible may be traveling upriver, possibly by commercial packet." His eyes traveled over the *Marguerite* with professional patience. "Yours is the only boat licensed to run this stretch above Calloway Landing. So here we are."

"Here you are," Quentin agreed. He drank his cold coffee. It tasted like the bottom of the river smelled. "When did this theft happen?"

"Three nights ago. At the Company station below the falls."

"Three nights ago I was tied up at Calloway with a cracked packing gland," Quentin said. "Ask your harbor master. He'll tell you I didn't move until yesterday noon, and then only as far as this shoal anchorage, because the water's too low to push past the middle bar before ten o'clock." He shrugged, a small economical motion he'd practiced until it looked like nothing at all. "Your thief would've had to swim."

Halloway's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Harbor masters can be checked, Captain. Of course we will."

"Check them all," Quentin said cheerfully. "It's what they're for."

Breck had drifted along the deck while his partner talked. Quentin tracked him in the corner of his vision the way he tracked floating debris — not staring, never staring, but never losing it either. Breck moved quietly for a big man. He paused at the cargo hatch. He glanced down the companionway stairs that led to the passenger cabin. He looked at the woodpile, at the fuel drums lashed amidships, at the tarpaulin over the spare gear, with the unhurried attention of a man counting things.

And under the tarpaulin, wedged between the spare gear and the boiler casing where the warmth might hold back the worst of the chills, Elias Vane lay shivering through the second day of his fever.

Quentin had told himself, tying up here last night, that he'd put the old man ashore at first light. Let him take his chances at the landing, let the mission church or the Company clinic or whatever passed for charity on this stretch deal with him. That had been the plan. The plan had died at dawn when Quentin had gone down to wake him and found him gray-lipped and muttering, hands working at the blanket like a man rowing a boat that wasn't there. You could not put a dying man off on a dock. Even Quentin Ashgrove, who owed no one, who stopped for nothing, whose whole creed fit on a coin — keep moving, owe no one — could not quite manage that arithmetic. Not yet. Not today.

So Vane lay under the tarpaulin, burning, and the Company's man was looking at the tarpaulin.

"You carry passengers, though," Halloway said. He'd turned slightly, following his partner's gaze without appearing to. These two were better than most. "Licensed limit is Calloway Landing, isn't it? Anything above that requires a special permit. Company water, above the falls."

"My passengers go where my passengers pay to go," Quentin said. "Where they're going is their business."

"It becomes the Company's business when it involves stolen property." Halloway said it lightly, almost apologetically, a man regretting the necessity of unpleasantness. "You'll understand our position. The documents in question concern land surveys. Valuable ones. There are parties — private parties — who would pay a great deal for them, and the Company takes a dim view of that sort of commerce." He paused, and let the pause do work. "There's also a reward. Substantial. For information leading to recovery. Sometimes information is easier to come by than property, Captain. Easier to carry, too. Doesn't weigh anything at all."

"There it is," Quentin said, and laughed — a real-sounding laugh, short and dry, the kind that invited a man to share the joke. "You think I'm holding your thief. Or his loot. Or both." He set the empty cup on the sill of the wheelhouse window and spread his hands. "Mr. Halloway, I'm going to tell you something about rivermen, since you're new to this stretch. We're greedy, we're dishonest, and we'd sell our mothers for a fair price — but we're not stupid. Stolen Company documents are the one cargo on this river hotter than gunpowder. Anybody dumb enough to carry them deserves what happens to him, and anybody smart enough to steal them wouldn't trust a stranger's boat. Your thief is walking. Or he's dead in a ditch somewhere with his pockets turned out. Either way, he's not on the *Marguerite*."

It was a good speech. Quentin knew it was a good speech because he'd built it the way he built everything — for the water it would have to float on. Every word true except the ones that mattered.

Halloway considered him. Up close, the man's pleasantness had seams in it, fine as hairline cracks in porcelain. His eyes were patient the way a heron is patient. "You have a gift, Captain," he said. "For talking. I imagine it serves you well with inspectors and customs men."

"It serves me fine."

"I'd imagine so." Halloway closed the folio. "Well. We'll take a look around, if you don't mind. Formality. Mr. Breck is thorough; you'll forgive him. Then we'll be out of your hair, and you can get on with — whatever it is you get on with."

"Now hold on." Quentin pushed off the doorframe, and let a little edge come into his voice, the right amount, measured like ballast. "You want to search my boat, you'll need more than a card and a smile. This is a licensed vessel. I know my rights, and I know yours, and yours end at my rail unless you've got a magistrate's paper. Do you have a magistrate's paper?"

"We don't."

"Then you can stand on the dock and admire her from there. She's a handsome boat. People admire her all day for free."

Something flickered behind Halloway's eyes — not anger. Recalculation. Quentin had seen that flicker before, in other men, other years, and he knew what it meant: *this one knows the rules, so we change the game.* But Halloway only spread his hands in graceful concession. "Of course. Forgive me. Old habits. On Company water we forget ourselves sometimes." He turned toward the rail, then stopped, as if remembering something trivial. "One thing more. A description, in case it helps. The man we're looking for — he's older. Sixties. Prospector type, sunburned down to the bone, hands like tree roots. He'd have been carrying an oilskin packet, yellowed, tied with cord. Possibly feverish — he took a bad fall during the theft, we understand. Might be hurt. Might be sick." He said it all mildly, a man reading a laundry list. "If a man like that comes asking you for passage, Captain — a sick old prospector, desperate, offering good money — you'd send for us, wouldn't you?"

The morning went very quiet around Quentin. The river kept on doing what rivers do, slapping the hull, worrying the mooring lines, indifferent as God. Somewhere under the tarpaulin twenty feet away, Elias Vane coughed — a thin, tearing sound, muffled by canvas and luck.

Quentin did not look at the tarpaulin. Looking was a confession; every child on the river knew that. He looked instead at Halloway, and smiled, and shook his head slowly, and said, "Prospectors come through every season, broke and sick and full of stories about gold in the headwaters. If one of them offers me money, I'll take it, same as any man on this river. That's not a crime yet. When it is, you let me know."

"A pragmatic answer."

"The only kind worth giving."

Halloway nodded, and for a moment his face did something complicated and brief, like weather changing behind glass. "My father ran a steamboat, you know. Before the Company consolidated the lines. He used to say a riverman's word was worth more than his bond." He stepped to the rail. "Times change, I suppose. Thank you for your time, Captain Ashgrove. I suspect we'll meet again. The river's narrow up here. Everyone passes everyone, sooner or later."

"That's the truth of it," Quentin said. "Safe travels."

They went down the gangway. The grey launch backed water, swung, and slid away downstream, its wake unrolling long and straight behind it, a ruled line drawn across the brown water — the only truly straight thing on the entire river.

Quentin stood in the wheelhouse doorway until the boat was a smudge, and then a while after that, which was longer than he needed to and he knew it.

His hands were steady. They were always steady; that was the first thing anyone ever learned about him, that Quentin Ashgrove's hands didn't shake, not in the shoals, not in the storm, not now. But underneath the steadiness something had set its hook, small and cold, the way a fish feels the point of the barb before it understands it's caught. They hadn't believed him. That was the thing. Men who believed you thanked you and left. Men who didn't believe you remembered your name, your boat, your route, and came back with more men and better questions. Halloway had memorized him. Quentin had felt it happen, the way you feel a leech attach — no pain, just a faint wrongness, a knowledge that something else was now drinking from your life.

And the description. *Feverish. Oilskin packet.* They weren't guessing. They knew Vane had come this way, or they knew enough that the rest was arithmetic, and arithmetic always finished. Which meant the clock on this stretch of river had started running whether Quentin liked it or not.

He went below.

Vane lay where Quentin had stowed him, sunk in the blankets, and the fever had carved him down another pound since morning — the cheeks hollowed, the lips cracked, the eyes moving fast beneath lids gone thin as onion skin. The oilskin packet was tucked inside his shirt, against his chest, where he'd sworn to keep it with the stubbornness of a man guarding the last thing he owned. His hands had found it even in sleep. They gripped it now, knuckles waxy and yellow.

Quentin crouched beside him and poured water between his lips, a little at a time, the way you'd prime a failing pump. Vane surfaced partway, blinked at the overhead beams, and focused with effort.

"They came," he rasped. It wasn't a question. He'd heard, or dreamed it, or simply known the way dying men know things.

"Some company men wanted directions," Quentin said. "I gave them the scenic route."

Vane's mouth twitched — almost a laugh, aborted by a cough that bent him double and left him gasping. When he could speak again, his voice had dropped to a thread. "You lied to them."

"I misdirected them. Lying's for amateurs."

"They'll come back." Vane's hand crawled out of the blanket and closed on Quentin's wrist with a strength that surprised them both. His eyes were clear for the first time in a day, and terrible with what was in them. "Listen to me, Captain. They don't stop. That's the whole of the Meridian Company — they don't stop, they don't forget, they don't forgive. I watched them put three men in the river for less than what's against my chest. Partners. Friends." His grip trembled. "You should put me off at the next landing. Throw the packet in the water. Save yourself."

Quentin looked down at the hand on his wrist. Old, ruined, strong as a root. He thought about saying yes. It would have been easy. It would have been the sane thing, the safe thing, the thing his whole life had trained him to do: cut loose the weight, keep the channel deep, keep moving.

Instead he heard himself say, "Nobody throws anything off my boat but bilge water. Drink this."

Vane stared at him. Something moved across the ruined face — gratitude, or grief, or the beginning of the bargain he'd been building toward since the dock, the one Quentin didn't yet know he was going to make.

Outside, far downstream, the grey launch's wake had long since flattened into the current. But Quentin could still see the line of it in his mind, straight as a rule, running upriver toward him through all that wandering brown water.

Straight lines on a river meant only one thing. Somebody coming.

* * *

He cast off at noon, earlier than the bar allowed, because staying anchored in one place suddenly felt like standing in a shooting gallery. The *Marguerite* ground over the middle shoal with six inches to spare — he heard the bar whisper along her keel, felt her shudder and climb — and then the deep channel took her and she swung upstream into the green country above Calloway Landing, where the banks narrowed and the hills came down closer to the water and every bend hid the next mile.

Company water, Halloway had called it. Above the falls, everything belonged to somebody, and the somebody wasn't Quentin, and the somebody kept ledgers.

He stood at the wheel and let the river talk to him through the hull, reading the water the way other men read print — the smooth tongue of the current, the dark wrinkle of a submerged log, the pale seam where two flows met and argued. It was the only reading he'd ever trusted. Water didn't lie. Water didn't send men in grey coats with pleasant voices. Water just told you what was true and expected you to act on it.

What the water told him now was simple: he was carrying a dying man, a stolen map, and a lie he'd told too well, up a river that was about to become a trap with the net already closing from below.

And the worst of it — the part he shoved down deep where he kept the brother, and the fire, and every other thing he'd outrun by never stopping — the worst of it was that some restless, unkillable piece of him was curious. About the map. About the headwaters. About what the Meridian Company wanted buried so badly it would burn partners and forge papers and send grey boats after one sick old man.

Curiosity, on the river, was how men drowned.

Quentin opened the throttle another notch and told himself it was just the low water making him hurry.

Behind him, faint through the deck planks, Elias Vane began to speak to someone who wasn't there — a woman's name, repeated softly, over and over, like a prayer worn smooth by use.

*Sera. Sera. Sera.*

Quentin pretended not to hear, and drove the boat harder into the narrowing green miles, and did not look back.
