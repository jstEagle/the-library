## 5. Last Words on the Levee

Years afterward, when people asked him about it — and for a long time nobody did, because Quentin Ashgrove was not a man who invited questions — he would say that Elias Vane died at dawn on a mud levee below the Stone Latch narrows, with his boots still on and his hands folded like a man waiting to be paid.

That was true, as far as it went. What he never said, what he kept folded up inside himself the way he kept everything, was that he had heard every word. That he had sat down on an oil drum beside a dying stranger in the grey hour before sunrise and listened the way you listen to a confession — not because you want to carry it, but because by then there is no honest way to put it back down.

He had moored the Marguerite against the levee at dusk, running dark, because the Company gunboats liked to work the narrows at first light and he wanted to be through them before they woke. He had meant to leave again within the hour. He had told himself the feverish old man in his cabin was cargo, nothing more, a debt to be discharged at Port Calloway and forgotten by the time the river bent.

Then, somewhere past midnight, Vane had stopped raving and started talking. And Quentin, who had spent thirty years perfecting the art of not listening — to creditors, to preachers, to the small voices of men drowning just off his bow — had found himself sitting very still with a lamp turned low, doing nothing but hearing.

*

The fever broke around two in the morning, or seemed to break; later Quentin understood it had only paused, the way a storm pauses over open water, gathering itself for one last run. Vane opened his eyes and looked at the cabin ceiling as if reading something written there, and said, without any greeting at all:

"You're still here."

"I'm still here," Quentin said.

"Men like you are always still here. That's your whole trick." Vane's voice was gravel dragged through water. "You're here, and then you're gone, and nobody ever gets a hook into you. I used to be that man."

"You don't know me."

"No," Vane agreed. "But I know the shape of you. I wore that shape for twenty years. It fits well until it doesn't, and then it fits like a coffin." He coughed, a long wet tearing sound, and when it passed he lay breathing shallowly, his eyes bright with fever-light. "Turn the lamp. I want to see the river."

Quentin turned the lamp toward the porthole, and the glass went gold, and beyond it the black water slid past the levee, carrying its usual freight of moonlight and silt and things drowned long ago.

"There," Vane said softly. "You hear it?"

"Hear what?"

"The river. Under the engine noise. It talks. Always has. My father fished these waters before the Company came, before the surveys, before the paper men arrived with their inkwells and their guns. He said the river remembers every name ever spoken along its banks." A thin smile. "Superstition. But I've been listening forty years, Captain, and I'll tell you what I heard."

Quentin should have stood up then. Should have gone forward, checked the fuel line, done anything with his hands. Instead he said, "Tell me," and hated how easily it came out of him, like water finding a crack.

*

Vane talked until nearly four.

He talked about the source valley first, because that was where all his roads led. He described it the way some men describe a woman they wronged — with too much detail, with tenderness and accusation tangled together. Terraces cut into the eastern slope, stone-faced, older than any deed. Weirs across the headwater channels built of fitted rock that no mortar held, fitted so precisely the water itself seemed to be the only thing binding them. Orchards gone wild, their rootstock still bearing fruit no one harvested. A ferry landing rotted down to its pilings. Carved names on the rocks above the falls, generations of them, a ledger written in stone by people who had never needed paper to prove they belonged.

"The Riverkin," Vane said. "That's what the Company called them — kin of the river, as if that made them less than men instead of more. They'd worked those terraces since before anyone surveyed anything. The valley fed them, and they fed the valley, and the water ran clean out of the source because they understood you don't fight a monsoon, you make room for it. Their weirs weren't walls. They were invitations. Come in, rise up, do your work, and go home again."

"And Meridian wanted it," Quentin said.

"Meridian wanted the fall of the water. Forty meters of drop between the source basin and the lower gorge — enough power to run every mill and smelter in three provinces. All they had to do was dam the headwaters and drown the terraces." Vane's hand found the edge of the bunk and gripped it. "Only problem was the people already living on the land. So they solved it the way paper men solve things. They forged the deeds."

"How do you know?"

"Because I helped survey it." The words came out flat and terrible, a stone dropped down a well. "Twenty-two years ago. I was young and greedy and I told myself the maps didn't matter, that lines on paper were just lines on paper. Aldous Crane drew the boundaries. I carried the chain and drove the stakes. And when the Riverkin elders produced their own records — carved stones, oral histories going back nine generations, witnesses from every landing on the upper river — Crane smiled at them and filed our documents anyway. Signed, sealed, witnessed. The law looked at the paper and the paper won. Paper always wins, Captain. That's the whole religion of men like us."

Quentin said nothing. Outside, the river talked on.

"They gave them a week to leave," Vane went on, quieter now. "A week. Some went. Some wouldn't. And on the last night—" His breath caught. For a moment Quentin thought the fever had taken him back under, but his eyes were clear, horribly clear, fixed on the middle distance of a memory he had clearly walked through ten thousand times. "On the last night they burned the ferry landing. Not to kill anyone. To make the crossing impossible. There were families on the far bank — children, old ones — and the ones on this side couldn't get to them, and the ones on the far bank couldn't get out, and the water was coming up with the rains. The Company men stood on this bank with lanterns and watched. I know because I was standing among them. I held a lantern."

His voice failed there, briefly. When it returned it was smaller, scraped thinner.

"My partners knew. Halloran and Beck and little Ferro, the boy who ran our cook fire. They'd found copies of the original deeds — real ones, predating every document Crane ever signed — hidden in the mission archive at Fort Amity. They wrote to me. They said, Elias, we can undo it, we can put this right. And I—" He laughed, a dry broken sound with no humor anywhere in it. "I was afraid. Twenty years afraid. I told them to burn the letters and keep their heads down. They didn't listen. Good men never listen when it matters most."

"What happened to them?"

"March. Six weeks ago. Halloran was found in the sluice channel below the mill works. Beck was found in his own boat, and the boat was found burned to the waterline, and the coroner wrote *accidental fire* in a hand I recognized as Crane's clerk's. Ferro they never found at all. The river keeps what it keeps." Vane turned his head on the pillow and looked at Quentin directly, and his eyes were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a long drop, leaning into it. "They died because of a map, Captain. Because I drew the true boundaries of that valley on a single sheet of oilskin, with the old weir-lines marked and the terrace dates and the names of the elders who could testify — everything Crane's forgeries couldn't survive contact with. I kept it hidden twenty-two years like a coward keeps a knife under the floorboards. And now I'm dying, and the knife is yours."

*

Quentin remembered standing up then. Remembered saying, in the voice he used for haggling and for lying, "I agreed to deliver a packet. That's all. I'm not a courier for crusades, and I'm sure as hell not a martyr."

And he remembered Vane smiling at him — actually smiling, with something that might have been pity — and saying, "No. You're not. That's why I chose you. Martyrs die for causes. Couriers just have to stay alive longer than the people chasing them. Can you do that much, Captain? Can you stay alive?"

It was, Quentin would think later, the only time in his life anyone had flattered him by asking for less.

*

Dawn came up grey-gold over the levee, mist standing on the water in slow columns, and Vane asked to be carried outside.

Quentin told himself it was practicality — the cabin air was foul, the man was dying anyway, and a corpse was a complication best managed in daylight with witnesses nearby. He got Vane under the arms and half-carried, half-dragged him up the companionway and laid him on the levee grass where the ground was dry, propped against a mooring post, facing the river. The old man weighed almost nothing. Fever had eaten him down to a frame with regrets inside it.

For a while neither of them spoke. The mist burned off. Somewhere upstream a boat horn sounded, far away, and Vane lifted his head toward the sound like an animal scenting rain.

"She'll hate me," he said.

"Who?"

"Sera. My daughter." His mouth worked around the name as if testing whether it still fit. "She was six when I left. I told her I'd be back by her birthday. Then I told myself the same lie every year for nineteen years. She'll have built a wall around herself by now, high as the Company's ledgers. She'll take the packet and she'll burn it unread, and she'll be right to."

"Then why send it?"

"Because being right isn't the same as being free." Vane's breathing had gone ragged, each inhale a negotiation. "I carried this thing alone my whole life and it ate me hollow. I won't hand her the guilt. I'm handing her the truth. There's a difference, even if she can't see it yet. Even if I couldn't see it until it was too late to matter." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended he was grey-lipped and shaking. "Captain. Look at me."

Quentin looked.

"I watched you lie to those Company men at the dock. Smooth as poured concrete. You enjoyed it." A ghost of the smile again. "You'll need that. They'll come asking. Crane's people don't stop at the county line and they don't forget a face. When they come — and they will come — you tell them whatever keeps your boat floating. Lie beautifully. I give you my blessing. Just don't lie to yourself about what you're carrying, once you carry it."

"I'm carrying a packet to Port Calloway. That's the whole of it."

"Yes," Vane said gently. "That's what I said too, once. About a survey chain."

The sun cleared the far tree line then, and the light came down the river in a long bronze sheet, and Vane turned his face into it and closed his eyes. His breathing slowed. Quentin checked his watch, an old habit, marking time the way he marked everything — impersonally, professionally — and saw it was 6:14, and thought absurdly that he should cast off soon, that the narrows waited, that low water wouldn't hold forever.

"Elias," he said, because it seemed suddenly important that the man hear his own name spoken by someone who wasn't the river. "Elias."

The old man's eyes opened halfway. They were fixed on the water, on the light lying on it, and there was something in his face that was no longer pain — something loosened, unburdened, almost surprised.

"She used to stand on the dock," he whispered. "Every evening. Waiting. Little red coat. She'd wave at every boat that came in, in case it was mine." His lips moved. "Sera—"

The word didn't finish. Or perhaps it did; perhaps it simply stopped needing a mouth. The river took the rest, the way the river takes everything, patiently, without malice, keeping what it keeps.

Quentin sat beside him for a long time after that. Long enough for the sun to climb clear of the mist. Long enough for his own hands, resting on his knees, to stop trembling — though he noted, with a kind of cold professional interest, that they had been trembling at all.

Then he stood, brushed the levee dirt from his trousers, and went to dig the grave, because leaving a body on a public levee was the kind of stopping that brought constables, and constables brought questions, and questions were how men like him drowned.

*

He buried Vane above the flood line, under a cottonwood, with his boots on, as promised. No marker. Markers were how the Company found things. He said no words over the grave because he didn't believe in words over graves, and because the only words available to him felt stolen — grief on loan from a family that hadn't been consulted.

But before he filled the earth in, he took the oilskin packet from his coat pocket and held it a moment. It was lighter than he expected. Everything that ruins a life usually is.

*Just a delivery,* he told himself. *Port Calloway, a girl in a red coat grown into a woman who hates you, hand it off, cast off, gone. Owe nothing. Belong nowhere.*

The lie sat comfortably in his mouth. It was an old model, well maintained, and it would run for days yet.

He filled the grave, washed his hands in the river — the river that remembers every name, the dead man's voice said somewhere behind his ribs, and he told it to shut up — and walked back to the Marguerite.

Sera. The name stayed with him the whole day, through the narrows, through the long green miles, through the dusk when he finally allowed himself to look at a chart and measure the distance to Port Calloway. Two days' run, if the weather held. Three if the Company was watching, and the Company was always watching.

Two days, and then he'd be rid of it. Rid of the packet, the promise, the dying man's eyes, the little red coat on the dock waving at boats that were never his father's.

He repeated it to himself like a prayer worn smooth by use, and steered into the darkening water, and did not notice — or told himself he did not notice — that he was already memorizing the shape of the girl he was about to disappoint.

Port Calloway lay ahead, and with it a salvage yard, a locked gate, and a woman who wanted nothing from the dead.

Quentin Ashgrove, who had spent his whole life learning what stopping cost, was about to learn what delivering cost more.
