## 6. Port Calloway

Port Calloway smelled of rust and brine and the particular sweetness of rotting rope that had given up on being useful. Quentin brought the *Marguerite* in on the morning tide with her paint peeling and her engine coughing the last hundred yards, and he tied off at the public wharf where the pilings wore their mooring scars like old tattoos — rings within rings, every boat that had ever leaned against them leaving its mark.

He hadn't wanted to come here. That was the truth of it, though he'd have lied about it to anyone who asked, including himself if he'd been careless enough to catch his own eye in a mirror. Delivering Elias Vane's packet meant standing still long enough for something to be asked of him, and Quentin Ashgrove had built forty-seven years of life on the principle that nothing asked of him could follow him downstream.

But Vane had died at dawn three days back, on the levee above Greywater Bend, with the river running gold-pink behind him and his hand gripping Quentin's sleeve like a man holding a rail in a storm. His last word had been a name. *Sera.* He'd said it twice, the second time softer, the way you say a thing when you've stopped expecting it to save you.

Quentin had buried him above the high-water line under a cairn of river stones, because the law required a report and a magistrate and a week of paperwork, and the river required only stones, and Quentin had learned long ago which authority moved faster. He'd said no words over the grave. The man had been a stranger. But he'd stacked the stones carefully, each one seated so the next rain couldn't shift it, and he'd caught himself doing it and hated how much care there was in his hands.

Now the packet sat in his coat pocket, oilskin-wrapped, tied with waxed cord, warm from riding against his ribs for three days. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than anything he'd ever carried.

Port Calloway was a salvage town, half drowned and half dug out, its streets graded with the confidence of people who expected the river to take them again someday and had decided not to take it personally. Houses stood on stilts of varying optimism. Boats rotted in yards like beached animals. And everywhere, everywhere, the iron: winches, chains, boiler plate, propeller shafts furred with barnacle, stacked in hills that men had spent decades building out of other men's disasters.

The salvage yard was easy to find because Vane had told him — told him in those last fevered hours when his voice kept sliding between the present and some older country. *She works the yard on Cordage Street. The Kestrel woman's old place. Tell her — * and then he'd coughed, and whatever came after *tell her* had gone into the river with everything else.

Cordage Street ran along the town's spine, and the yard at its end announced itself by sound before sight: the ringing of a hammer on metal, arrhythmic, patient, the sound of someone who worked alone and liked it that way. Quentin recognized the sound the way he recognized weather. It was his own sound, made by someone else.

He came through the gate — no gate, really, just a gap in a fence of welded bedframes — and saw her before she saw him.

Sera Vane was up on the shoulder of a derelict tug, cutting free a brass fitting with a wrench that she handled like an extension of her arm. She was maybe thirty, built solid the way dockworkers and river pilots are built, with dark hair tied back in a knot that was losing its argument and forearms scarred with the small white history of metal work. She wore canvas gloves split at the thumbs and boots that had been resoled past the point of dignity. When she moved, she moved with economy — no wasted reach, no second grip. She knew exactly what her body could do and asked nothing more of it.

She looked, Quentin thought, nothing like Elias Vane and exactly like him. Same deep-set eyes, same jaw that looked carved for disagreement. Genetics was a cruel current; it carried things downstream whether anyone wanted them delivered or not.

A dog — some brindled, rib-showing mix with the temperament of a customs official — lifted its head from a pile of inner tubes and regarded Quentin without alarm or welcome.

"Help you?" she called down, not stopping work. Her voice had gravel in it, the kind earned rather than born with.

"I'm looking for Sera Vane."

The wrench stopped. Not slowly — all at once, the way a clock stops. She turned her head and looked at him fully for the first time, and Quentin watched her do the arithmetic strangers always did: his river-browned face, the salt-stiff collar of his coat, the way he stood with his weight ready to leave.

"You found her." She climbed down with a fluid drop from the last four feet, landing soft despite the boots. "Who's asking?"

"Quentin Ashgrove. I run a boat. The *Marguerite*, out of Greywater."

"And?"

"And your father hired passage on her."

Something crossed her face — quick, controlled, gone before he could name it. She pulled off one glove finger by finger, taking her time, making him wait, and Quentin understood the tactic because he used it himself. Silence as a toll.

"My father," she said, "is dead. Has been dead to me for nineteen years. So either you're a bill collector, in which case you're wasting a trip, or you're selling something, in which case also wasting a trip."

"He's dead now too. Three days ago. Above Greywater Bend."

The second glove came off slower than the first. She set both on a bollard with exaggerated care, and Quentin recognized that too — the performance of calm, hands busy so they wouldn't shake.

"How?"

"Fever. The bad kind. He was already sick when he came to my boat."

"Of course he was." She laughed, and it was the worst laugh Quentin had heard in years — no humor in it at all, just air escaping a sealed place. "He was always already sick. Sick on drink, sick on schemes, sick on promises. Nineteen years, Captain Ashgrove. Do you know what nineteen years is? That's my whole adult life. That's every birthday. That's my mother dying — did he tell you about my mother? No, of course not. He never told anyone anything except the parts that made him look brave."

"He didn't tell me much of anything," Quentin said. "He was mostly delirious."

"Then why are you here?"

And there it was. The question Quentin had been turning over for three days of river, through shoal water and night watches and the long gray miles where the banks blurred and the only honest conversation was between him and the channel markers. *Why are you here?*

Because a dying man had gripped his sleeve. Because a name had been the last word. Because the packet rode against his ribs like a coal that wouldn't cool. Because somewhere in the stacking of those grave stones he had inherited something he didn't want and couldn't put down.

"Because he paid me," Quentin said, and it was true, and it was also the smallest possible version of the truth, and he watched her hear the smallness of it.

"Paid you to do what?"

"To deliver this." He took out the packet. Oilskin, cord-tied, river-stained at one corner. He held it out flat on his palm like a man offering feed to a horse he suspected might bite. "He said it was for you. He didn't tell me what's in it. I haven't opened it."

"I don't want it."

"You don't know what it is."

"I know what it is." Her chin came up. "It's him. It's the last thing he'll ever make anybody carry for him. Nineteen years of silence and then he dies and suddenly there's a package with my name on it, and some stranger on my property wanting me to take it like it means something. You know what it means? It means he got to die feeling like he'd taken care of it. Like wrapping a thing in oilskin settles a debt. Well, it doesn't. Debts get paid in years, Captain. In showing up. In letters that come before the dying."

The dog had risen and come to stand at her hip, watching Quentin with its customs-official stare.

Quentin had faced storms, sandbars, Company inspectors, and once a man with a knife over a disputed cargo of salt. None of it had prepared him for the specific difficulty of a woman telling him the truth about a man he'd barely known but had, in three days of fever-talk and dawn light, somehow begun to grieve.

"You're right," he said.

Her mouth opened for the next sentence and closed again. She hadn't prepared for agreement. Nobody ever did; Quentin knew that better than most, having spent a lifetime disarming people with candor.

"He owed you nineteen years and he tried to settle it with a parcel," Quentin went on. "That's a coward's arithmetic. I'm not going to argue it. But I'll tell you what I know, since I'm the only man alive who knows it, and then you can do what you want with the packet and with me."

She folded her arms. "Talk fast. I've got a fitting to cut before the light goes."

"He came aboard at dusk, two days below Greywater. Feverish, soaked, carrying nothing but that. He offered it as payment to go upstream past the licensed limit — past where I'm allowed to go, past where anybody's allowed to go anymore, thanks to the Meridian Company's survey closures. I refused. He collapsed on the dock. I took him aboard anyway, which was stupid, and I told myself it was because leaving a man to die on a levee is bad for business, which was a lie, and I knew it was a lie while I was telling it."

"Why is that a lie?"

"Because I don't stop," Quentin said. "It's the whole of my creed. Keep moving, owe no one, belong nowhere. Stopping is how boats get seized and men get buried. I stopped for him. First time in eleven years I've stopped for anything that wasn't weather."

"Why?"

"I've been asking myself that since Greywater." He looked at the packet in his hand. "I think it's because he talked in his fever about a valley. About water coming out of the ground clean enough to see your face in. And about men who burned a ferry landing to take it. And I've run this river my whole life, Mrs. Vane—"

"Sera. I never married. Marriage is a thing people do when they believe someone will stay."

The words landed on Quentin with uncomfortable precision, and he let them sit there rather than flinch away from them.

"—and I've run this river my whole life," he continued, "and I never once asked what was under the Company's survey flags. I read charts. Charts say *here be depth and hazard*. They don't say *here be theft*. Your father's fever-talk was the first time anybody made me wonder what the charts were leaving out."

"So he infected you with it too." She wasn't mocking him, quite. There was something wearier underneath — recognition, maybe. "The righteousness. He had that. Even drunk, even broke, he'd talk about the headwaters like a preacher. My mother used to say he loved that river more than us, and I used to think she was being bitter. Then I got old enough to check the record and found she was being generous. He loved the river instead of us. We weren't even in the competition."

"Did he write? At all, in nineteen years?"

"One letter." Her voice flattened. "When I was sixteen. Four sentences. Said he'd found something worth finding, said he'd send for me when it was safe, signed it *E.V.* No address. I kept it for six years and then I burned it in the forge, and I want you to appreciate that I remember the exact date, because burning it felt like surgery. Necessary and awful and final."

Quentin looked at the packet. Oilskin. Waxed cord. A dead man's last letter, probably — or worse, knowing prospectors, a map, a claim, another *something worth finding*. Another promise that would arrive nineteen years late and expect to be welcomed.

He should offer it again and let her refuse, and then walk away with a clear conscience and a clear boat. That was the script. That was the transaction complete: delivery attempted, refused, obligation discharged. He could be back on the *Marguerite* by midafternoon and gone by dark, and Port Calloway would become one more town he knew the smell of and nothing else.

Instead he heard himself say: "Then don't take it from him. Take it from me."

"What difference does that make?"

"All the difference there is. If you take it from him, it's his last manipulation and you're right to burn it. If you take it from me, it's a favor between two people who owe each other nothing — which is the only kind of favor worth anything. I carried this thing three days upriver against my own rules, and I'd like the carrying to mean something, and it can't mean anything unless it ends somewhere. You can hate him. You can hate me. But let the thing itself be finished. Unfinished things follow you. Believe me. I'm an expert in unfinished things."

She stared at him. The light was going amber behind the stacks of scrap iron, and it caught the sweat on her temple and lit the dust in the air between them like slow sparks.

"You talk like a man reading scripture he doesn't believe in," she said finally.

"I talk like a man who memorized the scripture because it was the only book on the boat."

The dog sat down. Some negotiation had concluded at the animal level, apparently.

Sera Vane looked at the packet for a long moment. Quentin watched her want it and hate wanting it — watched the war happen in real time across a face that was clearly accustomed to winning such wars quickly and cleanly. Nineteen years of refusal, and here was a stranger making refusal feel like cowardice, which was the dirtiest trick available.

"No," she said. "Take it away. Take it back to the river. Drop it in the deepest hole you know. That's where everything of his belongs."

"Fair enough." And Quentin did the thing that forty-seven years of self-sufficiency had perfected: he stepped forward and set the packet down on the bollard beside her gloves, gently, deliberately, in plain view, and straightened, and stepped back. "You're right. I won't force it on you. A man's got no business delivering what nobody wants."

"That's not delivering. That's dumping."

"It's leaving," Quentin said. "There's a difference. Dumping is getting rid of something. Leaving is trusting it to be found." He turned toward the gate. "I sail on tomorrow's tide. If it's still sitting there when I go, I'll consider the matter settled and myself unburdened, and neither of us will speak of it again. If it's not — well. Then I suppose we'll both find out what's in it eventually, one way or another."

"You manipulative river rat," she said, and there was — faintly, unwillingly, at the very bottom of it — something that was almost respect. "You're worse than he was."

"No," Quentin said, not turning around. "I'm exactly like he was. That's what frightens me."

He walked out through the gap in the fence of welded bedframes, past the dog, who gave him a single approving grunt that Quentin chose to interpret as diplomatic. Behind him he heard nothing — no footsteps, no voice calling him back, no splash of oilskin hitting bilgewater. Just the yard settling into evening, the tick of cooling metal, the far-off clang of someone else's hammer on someone else's rust.

Back at the wharf, the *Marguerite* rode low and patient against the pilings. Quentin climbed aboard, lit the lamp in the wheelhouse, and sat down at the chart table without opening any charts. He poured two fingers of the cheap rye he kept for medicinal emergencies and drank it looking at the darkening town.

He had left the packet. He had, by his own accounting, done the thing cleanly: obligation discharged, freedom preserved, the whole affair finished except for the small matter of walking back at dawn to see whether the bollard was empty or not.

Except.

Except that he had seen her face when she said *nineteen years*. Except that he had heard the date she remembered for burning a letter, which was not the memory of a woman who had finished grieving. Except that Elias Vane had died saying her name the way men say the thing they'd give anything to unsay, and Quentin had stacked the stones on the grave, and the stones had been careful, and care was a debt collector that never stopped calling.

He looked at his hands on the chart table, brown and scarred and steady, and thought: *you left it there because you knew she'd pick it up. You left it there because you couldn't bear to open it yourself and you couldn't bear to bury it unopened, and so you made her grief do your deciding for you.*

The lamp hissed. Out on the black water, a Meridian patrol boat went by with its running lights arranged in the smug geometry of an organization that owned the river's permission slips, and Quentin watched it pass and felt, for the first time, the hook set deep — the one that had gone in back at the dock when grey-coated men started asking questions, the one he'd been pretending was just a scratch.

Tomorrow's tide. One night between now and then.

He turned down the lamp and lay in his bunk listening to the hull breathe against the pilings, and he did not sleep well, and near morning he dreamed of a valley full of clean water and a girl of sixteen burning a letter in a forge, and woke with the word *Sera* in his mouth like a stone he'd been sleeping on.

At first light he went back up Cordage Street.

The bollard was empty. The gloves were gone. And Sera Vane was standing at the gate with the oilskin packet in both hands, unopened, held out from her body like something live and angry, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and awake, and she said:

"It's still sealed. I sat with it all night and I couldn't open it and I couldn't burn it and I couldn't put it down. So you're going to help me decide, Captain Ashgrove, or so help me I'll throw you and it both in the harbor."

Behind her, the salvage yard caught the early sun, ten thousand pieces of other people's wrecks shining like a field of broken mirrors — and Quentin, who had spent his whole life keeping moving, felt the ground under him shift toward staying.
