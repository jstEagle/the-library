## 7. The Unopened Packet

The salvage yard went quiet the way it always did after sundown — not silent, but hushed, the way a church is hushed. Sera Vane sat on an upturned hull with a rag in one hand and a cold cup of chicory in the other, and she listened to the river doing what rivers do: talking to itself about everything and nothing.

She had told him no.

She had told the boat captain — Ashgrove, Quentin Ashgrove, a name like a fence post driven into hard ground — that she wanted nothing from Elias Vane, and she had meant it in the way you mean things at noon, in daylight, with your hands busy and your voice level. The packet had come out of his coat anyway. He'd set it on the workbench between them like a man laying down a card he knew would lose, said something about promises being cheaper than debts but lasting longer, and walked back down the levee with his collar up against the evening wind.

*Leave it,* she had almost called after him. *Take it with you.*

She hadn't called anything. That was the trouble with her, her whole life: she never threw things away, not rope too rotten to hold a skiff, not chain too rusted to bear weight, not a father's name spoken once a year by strangers who'd known him better than she had.

The oilskin packet sat on the workbench now, under the hanging lantern, looking exactly as unremarkable as any of the hundred bundles that came through Calloway's salvage yards in a season. Brown paper gone soft with handling. Tied with waxed cord, knotted twice. Water-stained at one corner where some river had gotten ambitious years ago and lost.

Sera looked at it for a long time.

Around her the yard ticked and settled. Copper boiler plates cooling. A crane cable swaying on its hook with a sound like a slow clock. Somewhere down the row, old Marta's dog turned over in its sleep beneath the hull of a paddle-wheeler that would never float again. The lamp above the bench hissed faintly, and the light it threw was the color of weak tea, and every time the wind moved the flame the packet's shadow leaned toward her and then away, leaned toward her and then away, like something asking permission.

"You're nothing," she told it. "You're paper."

Paper was how it had all started, though. She'd learned that young. Paper was how men owned things they'd never seen — deeds and titles and bills of lading, papers with seals on them that could take a house off a family's shoulders from three hundred miles away. Her mother had died holding papers, waiting on papers, writing letters to offices that answered with smaller papers. If there was a God of the river towns, Sera thought, He wrote in ink and never read His mail.

She got up. She made herself walk the length of the yard and check the tie-downs on the *Cormorant*'s salvaged engine, which needed checking the way a healthy man needs checking on, just to have somewhere to put her hands. She checked it twice. She came back.

The packet was still there. Of course it was still there. Things didn't leave her; that was her curse and her trade both. She kept what others discarded, and she was kept in turn.

Her father had been gone nineteen years. She did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you count your own pulse. Nineteen years since a tall man with river mud to his knees had stood in this same yard — smaller then, this yard, and so was she, seven years old and missing two teeth — and promised her he was coming back before the rains. She remembered the exact color of the water behind him. She remembered that he'd smelled of tar and tobacco and something green, some far-upstream greenness she'd never smelled again until this evening when Ashgrove leaned close to hand over the bundle and brought the memory off his coat like static.

Nineteen years. And now a stranger said he was dead, and dead men sent packages.

Sera picked up the packet. It weighed less than a hammer head, more than a promise. The cord resisted her thumbnail; she used Marta's shears, and even then the waxed line parted grudgingly, with a small sound like a held breath let go.

Inside: oilskin folded over itself three times, tied again, and inside *that*, dry as a Sunday sermon, three things.

A map. A journal. A letter.

* * *

The map she spread under the lantern first, because maps were honest in a way people weren't. This one had been drawn by hand on drafting linen, the kind surveyors used, and it was beautiful the way instruments are beautiful — every contour line laid down with a patience she recognized and resented, because she'd inherited that patience and spent her whole life pretending otherwise. The river ran up the sheet in blue-black ink, fattening as it climbed, gathering tributaries the way a story gathers lies. Someone had marked depths in a fine slanted script. Someone had marked shoals, falls, a gorge with soundings penciled in margins like marginal prayers.

And near the top, where the river braided into threads and lost its name among them, someone had drawn a valley. Not named — *unnamed*, deliberately, the space left blank except for a small symbol she didn't recognize: concentric arcs, like ripples around a stone, or terraces cut into a hillside, drawn four times along the eastern wall.

In the lower corner, in that fine slanted hand, a signature: *E. Vane, surveyor.*

His handwriting. She'd never seen it before in her life and she knew it instantly, the way you know your own eyes in a mirror. The way the V crossed high. The little flourish on the E that looked like a man starting to wave and thinking better of it.

She put the map down carefully, faceup, and smoothed it flat with both palms, and found her hands were shaking, and stopped them.

The journal next. Cheap bound ledger, half its pages filled, the cover stained with what she chose to believe was coffee. She opened it at random and the smell came up off the paper — mildew, pipe smoke, and under it that green far-upstream smell again, stronger here, soaked into the fibers.

The entries were dated. They began eleven years ago. She read standing up, because sitting felt like surrender.

They began as survey notes — depths, bearings, gravel bars, the dry litany of a working man's craft. But threaded through them, getting denser as the pages turned, was another kind of record entirely. Names. Dates. Parcels.

*Meridian filed on the east bank today. Deed says the land passed through a R. Olembe in '41. No such sale ever happened. Old Tomas (deckhand) remembers the ferry landing there — says families worked those terraces longer than anyone can count. I checked the county abstracts myself. The chain of title is a fiction. Crane signed the survey certifying boundaries that match no boundary any elder remembers.*

Crane. She turned back a page, then forward. *Aldous Crane* appeared a dozen times, always in the same careful, damning neutrality — a man recording facts he intended someday to be believed. *Crane presented the removal order. Crane's crew burned the landing at Stone Latch. I counted eleven structures going up. I did not stay to count what else.* And later, in a hand gone shakier: *They know I copied the originals. Harrow's clerk warned me. If anything happens to me this book is the truth and the map is the way to prove it. The valley is real. The people are real. The deeds are paper, and paper burns.*

Sera set the journal down.

The lantern hissed. The dog down the row sighed in its sleep. Out on the black river a night boat went by with its running lights doubled in the water, going somewhere, carrying somebody's ordinary cargo, and Sera Vane stood in her yard holding eleven years of her father's conscience and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, why he had never come home.

It wasn't because he'd forgotten her.

It was because he'd known what following him home would cost.

That was worse. That was so much worse than forgetting that she had to grip the edge of the workbench and breathe through it, breath by breath, the way you ride out a cramp, while the yard swam and steadied and swam again. All those years she'd built her anger like a seawall — solid, load-bearing, the thing that held her up — and now here was one thin letter threatening the whole structure, and she hated her father for it, freshly, completely, and loved him in the same instant, and the two feelings ground against each other like millstones with her heart between.

She picked up the letter last.

* * *

It was short. She could tell that before unfolding it — one page, folded in thirds, the creases worn soft as cloth from being refolded many times. He had carried it a long while. He had taken it out and put it away and taken it out again, over years, over miles, and each folding had pressed the paper a little closer to powder.

*Sera.*

Just that, to start. Just her name, in that hand with the high-crossed V, and her vision blurred immediately and she blinked it clear and read on.

*I don't ask you to forgive me. Forgiveness is a debt and I've spent my life owing, and I won't add mine to yours. But you should know what I found, because it belongs to you as much as to anyone, and I was too much of a coward to bring it to you in person, and too stubborn to die quietly with it in my coat.*

*There is a valley at the source of our river. People lived there before any deed, before any company, before the names on the charts. Meridian erased them with forged papers and called it law. I helped draw the charts. That is my sin, and it is not washed by what I did after, only measured against it.*

*If you are reading this, a captain named Ashgrove kept his word. Trust him further than he trusts himself. He is a man who has mistaken loneliness for freedom, and I say that with love, having made the same mistake for sixty years.*

*The rains will come. They always come. What the water takes, the water keeps. Be keeper of what matters, not of what lasts. Lasting is easy. Anything cruel lasts.*

*Your father,*

*Elias*

She read it again. Standing, then sitting down hard on the upturned hull without noticing she'd moved. Then a third time, aloud, barely, her lips shaping the words in the empty yard, because some things don't go all the way in until they've been through the mouth.

*A man who has mistaken loneliness for freedom.*

"Damn you," she said softly, to the letter, to the dark upstream, to the tall man with mud on his knees who had left a seven-year-old girl a promise instead of a truth. "Damn you for knowing me. You weren't here long enough to know me."

But he had. That was the unbearable part. He'd known her from across nineteen years and a hundred miles of river, known her well enough to leave her not money, not apology, but *work* — because he'd guessed, correctly, that she was built the same way he was, that she could no more sit still under a wrong thing than she could sleep in a burning house.

Grief, she discovered, was not a still thing. Everyone said grief was heavy, and it was, but heaviness moves. Heavy things roll downhill. Hers rolled through her now, gathering speed, changing shape as it went — from sorrow into fury, from fury into something harder and cleaner that had no name yet but felt like purpose feels, like a winch taking up slack, like an engine catching on the third try.

She folded the letter along its soft old creases. She rolled the map. She squared the journal. She wrapped all three back in the oilskin, tied the cord the way it had been tied, and then untied it, because hiding it again felt like a lie, and slung the bundle inside her coat against her ribs, where she could feel it with every breath.

Then she looked down the levee toward the water.

* * *

The *Marguerite* rode at the commercial dock with a single stern lantern burning, and steam was already up — she could see it feathering off the escape valve, pale against the stars, hear the low idle of a boat readying to leave. Of course he was leaving. He'd delivered the packet, kept his word, paid his debt; staying past that wasn't in his grammar. She'd watched him work the deck earlier, quick and economical, touching nothing he didn't need, a man who treated attachment like a shoal to be sounded and avoided.

She went down the levee fast, boots ringing on the planks, past the shuttered chandlery, past a sleeping barge, the river-smell thickening around her — tar, fish, wet rope, the green undertone of the far current. The bundle knocked against her ribs with every step like a second heartbeat.

Ashgrove was on the afterdeck, coiling a line with the automatic precision of long habit. He heard her before he saw her — most rivermen did — and turned, and in the lantern light his face did something complicated and brief: surprise, then resignation, then a careful blankness laid over both like paint over rust.

"Packet's delivered," he said. "We're square."

"I read it." She came up the gangplank without asking. "All of it. The journal too. There's a name in it — Aldous Crane. Meridian's surveyor. My father documented the forgeries, entry by entry, and named the man who signed them."

"That sounds like a problem for a magistrate." He went back to his coil. The line ran through his hands smooth as water.

"The records annex in Fort Amity burned last month. Did you know that?"

His hands paused. Only for half a beat, but she caught it, and she understood that he'd already heard, already done the same arithmetic she had, and hated himself for the answer.

"No," he said.

"Liar," she said, without heat. "The originals are ash, Captain. Everything except what's in this oilskin. Which means my father's journal isn't evidence anymore. It's the *only* evidence. And you were about to steam upriver with it stowed on your boat, or dump it, or sell it, or whatever it is men like you do with things that weigh too much."

He set the coil down. Straightened. Looked at her fully for the first time, and she saw what her father must have seen in him: a man built entirely out of motion, so that stillness looked on him like a wound.

"I made one promise," Ashgrove said. "To a dying man. One. I keep the ones I make and I don't make extras. Extras are how boats sink."

"Then I'm not asking you to promise anything." She pulled the oilskin bundle from her coat and held it between them, and the lantern flame leaned, and the shadow of the packet swung across his chest like a pendulum. "I'm telling you what's true. In nineteen days, maybe less, the monsoon makes the upper river unnavigable. After that, nobody reaches that valley until the dry season — months. Months for Meridian to finish what the fire started. Months for Crane to file new papers on blank land, and papers, Captain, are the only thing that man respects." She heard her own voice steady and was grateful for it; somewhere her father's blood was doing the work her fear couldn't. "I'm going upriver. Tonight if this boat goes, alone if it doesn't. I'll buy passage. I'll crew. I'll bail bilge with my hands if I have to. You don't owe me a promise. You owe my father a fair price for silence, and silence is what you'll be selling if you cast off without me."

The river talked below them. The escape valve breathed its white feather. Somewhere aft, a fender knocked the piling — once, twice — patient as a clock.

Ashgrove looked at the bundle a long moment. Then at her. And she watched him weigh it, all of it, the way he'd weigh a channel in fog — depth against draft, risk against reach — and she saw the exact moment the arithmetic failed him, the moment the sum came out wrong no matter how he arranged the numbers, because some cargo changes the boat that carries it.

"Nineteen days," he said finally, not quite to her. More to the river, or to the dying man whose voice was surely still in his ears. Then his eyes came back to hers, gray and unwilling and already committed. "Passage to the source costs double. And you stand watches."

Sera Vane put the oilskin back inside her coat, against her heart, where it belonged.

"Double," she agreed, "and I'll take the middle watch. Get your lines in, Captain. We're burning tide."

Above the dock the stars were going out one by one behind a wall of cloud building in the northwest — high, vast, silent, the monsoon's outriders already marching down the horizon — and the *Marguerite*'s bell rang once in the wind like something waking up.
