## 8. Terms of Passage

The Marguerite rode low on the morning tide, and Quentin Ashgrove stood at her wheel counting the ways she could kill him.

That was how he thought of it — not superstition, but arithmetic. The port-side valve that wept when the pressure climbed past a certain mark. The hairline crack in the starboard paddle shaft he'd been meaning to weld since March. The way she listed two degrees to leeward when her fuel tanks ran below half, which they did now, because Port Calloway's diesel was cut with something that smelled like turpentine and burned like a grudge. He knew his boat the way some men knew their sins: intimately, without excuse, and with no intention of giving either up.

He was still doing this arithmetic when Sera Vane came down the gangplank with a canvas satchel over one shoulder and an expression that made him stop counting.

"You're casting off," she said. Not a question.

"Tide's turning in twenty minutes." He kept his hands on the wheel spokes, brass worn to a dull shine under his palms. "River won't wait."

"Neither will I." She stopped at the rail's edge, just short of stepping aboard, as if the deck were a border between countries. "I want passage."

Quentin looked at her. She was maybe thirty, built like someone who'd spent years hauling things heavier than herself — salvage-yard shoulders, rope-scarred hands, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of copper wire, because apparently in Port Calloway even your ponytail had to earn its keep. Her eyes were her father's eyes, and that was the problem. Elias Vane had looked at him the same way three days ago from the dock at Marrow Bend: fever-bright, desperate, certain that Quentin Ashgrove was the kind of man who could be bargained with if you found the right currency.

"I delivered the packet," Quentin said. "That was the arrangement. That's where my part ends."

"It doesn't end there." She stepped aboard before he could decide whether to allow it, and the Marguerite dipped slightly under her weight, accepting her the way she accepted everything — without comment. "I read it. All of it. The map, the journal, the letter." Her voice caught on *letter*, just barely, then steadied itself through what sounded like sheer force of will. "My father didn't die of fever, Captain. Or he did, but the fever had help. Meridian killed his partners and burned their survey camp, and the journal proves the headwaters sit on land they stole with forged deeds. And you know it, because you carried him and you listened and you took his money anyway."

"I took his money for delivery," Quentin said. "Which I completed."

"And now I'm buying more."

"No."

She hadn't flinched at the word. Most people flinched. They negotiated around it, circled it, treated it like a door slamming rather than a wall standing. Sera Vane just set her satchel down on his deck planks with a thud that said she'd been expecting the word and had packed accordingly.

"Before you say no twice," she said, "hear the terms."

"There are no terms. There's a river, and there's a season, and there's a boat that barely floats, and none of those things care about your father's—"

"The county records office is in Fort Amity." She said it flatly, like laying a card face-up. "Downriver. Two days' run, maybe less with the current behind us. That's all I'm asking. Not the source. Not the headwaters. Not whatever death march you and my father were planning upstream. Fort Amity. Where the original land grants are filed. Where a clerk can pull the 1871 deeds and hold them next to whatever Meridian filed with the territorial office, and any half-awake magistrate can see the signatures don't match."

Quentin felt the current shift under the hull before he saw it — the tide beginning its slow reversal, tugging the Marguerite's bow toward open water the way it always did, patient as gravity. He'd been running rivers for twenty-two years. He knew every kind of passenger there was: the freight speculators who haggled over tonnage rates until sunset; the missionaries who prayed loudly enough to spook the fish; the Company men with their grey coats and their questions shaped like pleasantries. He'd ferried them all and belonged to none of them, and his creed fit in a sentence he'd carved somewhere inside himself so deep he couldn't remember carving it: *keep moving, owe no one.*

Fort Amity was downriver. Downriver was easy water. Two days, current at his back, and then he could point the Marguerite's bow at the horizon and let the whole ugly business sink behind him like ballast cut loose.

He hated that it tempted him. That was the tell. A clean refusal came easy; this one tasted like iron.

"Say it plainly," he said. "What you're actually asking."

"I'm asking you to carry me to the law." Her jaw tightened. "I'm asking you to take the only evidence my father bled out on a levee to protect, and put it in front of the one institution in this territory that can make Meridian answer for it. That's the whole request. It fits in a sentence too."

*Keep moving, owe no one.* He turned the creed over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "And when the law looks at those deeds and shrugs? When the judge in Fort Amity plays cards with the Meridian factor every Thursday and rules however the cards fall? What then? You'll want passage back upriver. Then to the territorial capital. Then wherever the next door slams shut. I've seen this before. Justice isn't a destination, Miss Vane. It's a debt that compounds."

"Then I'll pay it," she said. "Compounding interest and all."

* * *

They argued for another hour while the tide went slack, and Quentin learned things about Sera Vane that her father's journal hadn't told him.

He learned that she'd been eight when Elias left — old enough to remember his face, young enough to have believed him when he said he'd be back by the spring floods. He learned that she'd waited four springs. He learned that her mother had died in the fifth year, of something slow and poor and treatable, and that Sera had gone to work the salvage yards at fourteen because grief doesn't cover rent. He learned all this not because she offered it but because it leaked out of her in fragments whenever the argument touched anything tender, the way water finds seams in a hull.

"You think I don't understand wanting to be free of it?" she said at one point, pacing his deck like a woman measuring a cell. "I spent twenty years being free of that man. Free of his promises, his letters that stopped coming, his name that I only heard when strangers asked if I was *related to*. You know what I did yesterday, when you handed me that packet? I nearly threw it in the harbor. That would have been freedom. That would have been the end of it."

"But you opened it instead."

"I opened it instead." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the raw sound of a wound being reopened on purpose. "Because I'm my father's daughter, God help me, and I needed to know. And now I know, and knowing isn't a thing you can put back in the oilskin. So don't stand there and tell me about the cost of carrying things, Captain. You've been carrying his map for a week. I've been carrying his absence for twenty years, and neither of us gets to set it down."

The words landed harder than she probably intended, or exactly as hard as she intended — with her, Quentin was learning, the distinction didn't exist. Because she was right, and he knew it, and the knowledge sat in his chest like swallowed river stone.

His brother had drowned when Quentin was nineteen. Everyone said it was nobody's fault — flash flood, bad crossing, the river taking its tax like it always did. But Quentin had been the one who wanted to push on that day, who'd argued the ford was passable, who'd watched the water take Daniel and understood, in the space of one screaming minute, that moving forward could cost everything and stopping could cost nothing and he had chosen wrong. So he'd built the opposite life. Never stop. Never belong. Never be the one whose judgment drowns somebody. For twenty-two years the creed had held. It had kept him alive and unencumbered and alone, and he'd called the aloneness freedom right up until a dying prospector paid him in maps and guilt, and now his dead brother's ghost and a stranger's dead father were both standing on his deck demanding he choose again.

"Two days to Fort Amity," he said finally. "Current's with us. I can have you there by Thursday."

Sera went very still, the way people do when they've braced for a blow and received a gift instead. "You're agreeing?"

"I'm stating a price." Quentin crossed to the rail and looked down at the brown water sliding past the hull, refusing to look at her while he said it, because looking would make it real. "Passage to Fort Amity and back to Calloway, forty dollars, payable now. You eat at my table, you work if I need hands, and you do exactly as I say on the water. My boat, my rules. And when it's done—" he turned, and made himself meet her eyes, and hated the earnestness in them, "—when it's done, we're finished. You file your papers, you fight your Company, and I go my way. No debts. No obligations. Nothing owed in either direction. Are those terms acceptable?"

Something flickered across her face — disappointment, maybe, or recognition. She'd heard the same speech from her father once, probably. Men like them, Quentin and Elias Vane, always negotiating the exits before entering the room.

"Acceptable," she said, and counted forty dollars out of the satchel in bills soft as cloth.

* * *

They cast off on the turn of the tide, and the Marguerite slid downriver with Fort Amity two days ahead and trouble, Quentin suspected, considerably closer than that.

By noon he'd put her through the lower shoals, the channel narrowing between clay banks where the current ran green and fast, and Sera Vane proved less useless on deck than he'd expected. She coiled line like a stevedore. She read water without being taught — pointed out the submerged snag off the eastern point before he'd marked it, and when he raised an eyebrow she shrugged and said, "Salvage yard. You learn to see what's under the surface or you lose fingers." He decided not to tell her that was the most sensible thing anyone had said on his deck in a year.

But the ease of the morning bothered him. The river was too quiet. The fishing skiffs that usually worked the lower bends were pulled up on the banks, nets drying, their owners nowhere visible. At Miller's Reach, where a fuel barge always moored, the berth stood empty, and a Company survey stake — new, white-painted, planted within the week — stood on the levee like a small pale finger pointing at his boat.

"They're marking the channel," Sera said, coming to stand beside him at the wheel. "For the dam surveys."

"They're marking everything." Quentin kept his voice level. "That's what ownership looks like before it's official. Stakes and paint and men with clipboards. By the time the guns show up, the paperwork's already won."

"Then we beat the paperwork to Fort Amity."

"The paperwork has a head start." He nodded downstream, toward a smudge of smoke rising off the water around the next bend. Woodsmoke, he thought at first. Then he caught the chemical undertone beneath it — lamp oil, maybe, or something hotter — and his stomach dropped with a certainty he couldn't yet justify. "That's not a cook fire."

Sera saw it a moment later as the Marguerite rounded the bend: a riverboat moored at the Fort Amity landing, listing hard to starboard, black smoke still bleeding from her midsection where the deckhouse had burned to the frame. Men were passing buckets along a human chain from the water, moving with the weary rhythm of people fighting a fire that had already won. Painted on the charred bow, still legible under the soot, were the words *Circuit Records Transport*.

Quentin brought the Marguerite alongside without being told to, threw lines to men on the landing, and stood at his rail watching the smoke rise into a sky that had begun, far to the south, to stack itself with the first grey towers of monsoon cloud.

Beside him, Sera Vane stared at the burning boat, and her hand went slowly to the satchel at her hip, and her face went through grief and fury and arrived somewhere colder than either.

"That's why the records office felt safe," she said quietly. "Because the records were supposed to come to us."

Quentin looked at the ruined transport, at the bucket chain, at the white survey stakes marching down the levee like a fence being built around the future, and felt his creed — *keep moving, owe no one* — begin, very quietly, to crack along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"Miss Vane," he said, "I think we need to talk about what happens when the law burns faster than we can reach it."
