## 9. Records and Ruin

The county records office at Fort Amity was not what Sera had imagined.

She had pictured something grander, she supposed — a courthouse with columns, marble steps worn smooth by a century of petitioners, the law made visible and heavy. What she found instead was a squat brick building wedged between a chandlery and a funeral parlor, its shutters bleached grey by forty monsoons, its sign — *AMITY COUNTY DEEDS, SURVEYS & LAND REGISTRY* — painted over an older sign that had never quite been sanded away. She could still read the ghost letters beneath: *FURS, GUM, & CATTLE*.

The river had built this town for trade, and the law had arrived later, like a tenant moving into someone else's house.

Quentin stayed with the *Marguerite* at the public moorings. He'd argued about it all the way downriver from Port Calloway — two days of arguing, off and on, the way weather argues with itself — and in the end they'd split the difference. He would come as far as the door. He would not come inside.

"I don't do rooms where men keep paper on other men," he'd said, coiling line with more force than the work required. "Paper's how rivers get owned."

"Paper's also how theft gets proven," Sera had answered, and he'd looked at her for a long moment with those flat grey eyes of his, and said nothing, which from Quentin Ashgrove was a kind of surrender.

So now it was just her, climbing three warped wooden steps into air that smelled of dust and lamp oil and old varnish, the oilskin packet riding against her ribs beneath her coat like a second heartbeat.

* * *

Inside, the annex was dim and cool and wrong.

She understood it was wrong before she could name why. The clerk's counter stood to her left, oak-topped, tidy. Shelves ran back into shadow, row after row of ledger spines, boxes labeled in faded copperplate. A ceiling fan turned with the slow indifference of something that had been turning since before anyone present was born. Everything was in order.

Everything except the smell.

Under the dust and varnish there was char. Faint, but unmistakable — the sweet-sour ghost of burnt paper, soaked so deep into the brick that no amount of airing would ever lift it out. It was the smell of a thing that had happened here, recently, and been very carefully cleaned up after.

A man sat behind the counter, middle-aged, spectacles, ink-stained fingers, reading a newspaper he lowered with the wariness of someone who had learned that visitors to a records office were rarely bearers of good news.

"Help you?" His voice was dry as his shelves.

"I'm looking for land records," Sera said. "Deeds. Original deeds, if you have them — pre-annexation grants, upper river. The headwaters district."

Something moved behind the man's eyes. It was small and quick, a fish turning under ice, and Sera had spent enough of her life around people hiding things to know what it looked like.

"The headwaters district," he repeated.

"That's right. Roughly from Stone Latch north. I want the original grant documents. Chain of title. Surveys, if they exist."

He set down the newspaper with exaggerated care, aligning its edges with the counter's edge, buying himself time the way some men bought it with silence or drink. "Those records," he said, "were held in the north annex."

"And where is the north annex?"

He didn't answer at once. Instead he came around the counter — slowly, joints protesting — and led her through a door behind the shelves, down a short corridor that smelled increasingly of wet ash and lime, and stopped before a second door, iron-banded, padlocked.

He unlocked it and swung it wide, and stood aside, and let her look.

The room beyond had been fire. That was the only honest way to describe it. The walls were blackened to shoulder height; above that, smoke shadows fanned upward like the wings of something enormous pinned to the ceiling. The shelving was gone — not collapsed, not toppled, *gone*, reduced to a drift of grey ash and twisted bracket-iron along the floor. Water stains sheeted down from a scorched hatch in the roof, long dried into continents of mineral bloom. Someone had shoveled. Someone had swept. Someone had hauled out the worst of it and washed the floor and locked the door, and the room had been left like that — a wound dressed but never stitched.

"When?" Sera asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"Eleven weeks ago," the clerk said. "Night of the fourteenth."

Eleven weeks. Her father had died — she counted backward, and her stomach tightened — eleven weeks and six days ago. Five days after Elias Vane had walked into Port Calloway with a fever and a packet full of proof.

"You're telling me," she said carefully, "that every original deed for the headwaters district burned five days after my father—"

She stopped. The clerk's face did something complicated.

"After your—" He glanced past her, down the corridor, toward the open front door where daylight lay in a pale stripe. Then he stepped into the ruined room and pulled the door mostly shut behind them both, so that they stood together in the dark and the ash-smell, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"You're Vane's girl."

It wasn't a question. Sera felt the floor tilt slightly under her, the way a deck tilts. "Who told you that?"

"No one told me anything." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, though they didn't need polishing; it was a man's way of putting distance between himself and his own next sentence. "But a woman comes in asking for headwaters deeds eleven weeks after the annex burns, and mentions a father, and there was a prospector named Vane who used to come through here twice a year, every year, for twenty years, always asking the same question you're asking." He put the spectacles back on. "You have his jaw."

Sera said nothing. Her hand had gone, without her deciding it, to the shape under her coat.

"The fire report," the clerk said, "was filed as accidental. Faulty flue from the chimney of the building behind us — the saddlery, it was then. Sparks, wind, dry season." He recited it flatly, a catechism. "That's what the report says."

"But you don't believe it."

"I believe," he said, "that the saddlery chimney hadn't been used in nine years, because the saddlery closed nine years ago. I believe the fire started in the center of this room, not near any wall, because char doesn't lie even when reports do. And I believe that whoever set it knew exactly which shelves to stand beside." He looked around the black room with an expression that was not grief exactly, and not fear either, but some alloy of the two that had been smelting in him for eleven weeks. "Survey rolls first. Grant books second. They burned what proved ownership and what proved *dis*ownership, miss. Both. You burn a deed to steal land. You burn a survey to hide that the land was already somebody's."

The words settled over Sera like cold water.

Her father's journal — she had read it four times now, cover to cover, until she could find any passage blind — had named names. Aldous Crane, Meridian Company surveyor. The forged signatures. The Riverkin families removed from the source valley on the strength of deeds signed by men who had never seen the valley, dated years before the ink existed. Elias Vane had written it all down because he had helped do it, and because guilt is a kind of ledger too, and his had run thirty years unbalanced.

But a journal could be called a madman's scrawl. A dead man's grudge. Copies could be dismissed as copies.

The originals in this room could not have been.

And now they were ash.

"They got here first," she said quietly.

"They always get here first." The clerk — his name tag, she noticed for the first time, read *R. OLLISTER* — moved to the window slit high in the wall, the only light, and stood with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a graveside. "Miss Vane, I've kept these records for nineteen years. My father kept them before me. There are families up and down this river whose whole legal existence lives in that ash. Fishermen who can prove their grandfathers held water rights. Farmers. Ferry folk." He paused. "Riverkin."

"You knew about the Riverkin."

"I filed their papers myself, years ago. Removal notices, compensation schedules — such as they were." His mouth thinned. "I file what comes across my counter. That's the job. You don't ask the ledger if it's moral." He turned to face her, and in the grey light from the slit his eyes were very tired. "But I'll tell you what I know that isn't in any ledger, and then I'll tell you the part that should frighten you, and then, Miss Vane, I'm going to ask you to leave, because we have spoken too long already and I have a wife and a bad heart and no appetite for martyrdom."

"Tell me anyway."

* * *

What R. Ollister knew, in the end, was less than she wanted and more than she expected.

He knew that the fire brigade had taken forty minutes to arrive for a blaze eight streets from their station — a delay he had personally noted and personally been told to strike from his own incident log by a county commissioner whose brother-in-law held a Meridian shipping contract. He knew that the insurance claim had been paid within three weeks, unusually fast, to a holding company whose registered address matched, character for character, a Meridian subsidiary listed in the shipping manifests that crossed his counter monthly. He knew that in the eleven weeks since the fire, four separate parties had come asking after headwaters records — two lawyers, a man claiming to be a historian, and a woman with a Company pin hidden under her collar who had asked, oddly, whether *any duplicates* of the old surveys might exist elsewhere.

"And I told them all the same thing," Ollister said. "That everything burned. Which was true, as far as it went."

"As far as it went," Sera repeated.

The clerk looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she would think about for years afterward: he reached into his waistcoat pocket and drew out a small brass key, no longer than her little finger, and held it in his palm between them, and did not give it to her.

"There was a practice," he said, "in my father's day. Before photostat machines, before the Company started 'assisting' with our filing. When a document came through this office that was likely to be disputed — contested grants, boundary fights, anything the lawyers would circle like gulls — the clerk of record made a fair copy in his own hand and lodged it privately. Off the register. Not fraud, exactly. Insurance. My father called them his *quiet papers*. I kept up the habit for my first few years, before I learned to be careful." His thumb moved over the key. "There is a deposit box at the Amity Trust & Safekeeping, two streets east, rented under a name that isn't mine. In it are fair copies of perhaps sixty documents. Among them—" his voice dropped further, until Sera had to lean in, close enough to smell the ink and the fear on him, "—the complete grant roll for the headwaters district, as it stood before the fire. Including three deeds bearing the signature of Aldous Crane, Meridian Company surveyor, executed on dates that Crane himself, in his own field notes of that same year — which I also copied, because I am a thorough man and a coward, and the two are not incompatible — recorded himself as being *two hundred miles downriver* at Fort Harrow."

The room seemed to tilt. Sera gripped the edge of a scorched shelf bracket to steady herself, and the char came off on her fingers, black as the truth.

"You have proof," she whispered. "You've had proof for eleven weeks."

"I have proof that has killed better men than me," Ollister said. "Vane's partners had proof too, I'd wager. Ask yourself where they are." He closed his fist around the key. "I am not a brave man, Miss Vane. I want that understood. I am a filing clerk. But I watched them carry out my father's life's work in buckets of ash, and I have slept badly for eleven weeks, and there is a limit to how long a man can be a coward before the cowardice starts costing more than the courage would have."

"What changed?"

"A woman with a Company pin asked me last week whether duplicates existed anywhere else." He smiled without humor. "Which means they believe duplicates might exist somewhere else. Which means, sooner or later, they will stop asking clerks and start asking box-rental houses. I would give it a month. Perhaps less."

He pressed the key into her hand. Her fingers closed around it and it was warm from his pocket, warm as a living thing.

"Box forty-one," he said. "The rental name is *Harrow*. Don't ask me why; the joke was my father's, and it's too late to change it. Go tomorrow morning, early, before the street wakes. Sign nothing. Look at no one. And Miss Vane—" he caught her arm as she turned, and his grip was stronger than his bad heart suggested, "—once you take those papers, you are no longer a daughter asking questions. You are evidence walking around on legs. The Company does not subpoena evidence. Do you understand me? They *destroy* it. Ask the north annex."

"I understand."

"Do you? Because the young man waiting outside on your boat — Ashgrove, the pilot, everyone knows his boat — he has the look of a man who survives by never standing still. Good instinct. Hold onto it. But understand that from tomorrow, *staying ahead* is no longer enough. Papers like these don't protect themselves and they don't stay hidden. They have to go somewhere. They have to become something. A case. A hearing. A judge." He released her arm and stepped back into the dark of the burned room, and the last thing Sera saw before he shut the door was his face, half-lit from the window slit, looking older than the building around him.

"And that," R. Ollister said, "is the part that should frighten you. Because I have watched the courts of this county for nineteen years, Miss Vane, and I will tell you what the ash already knows: the law here is not a wall between the strong and the weak. It is a gate. And Meridian holds the key."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold and long, and Fort Amity smelled of tar and frying fish and the green rot of the riverbank. Sera walked the three blocks back to the moorings with the brass key biting into her palm where her fist clenched around it, and she did not feel afraid, which frightened her more than anything Ollister had said.

Quentin was on deck, checking the fuel lines with the methodical suspicion he gave all machinery. He glanced up as she came down the gangplank, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down the wrench.

"Well?" he said.

"It burned." She climbed aboard. "Eleven weeks ago. Every original record for the headwaters district. Deeds, surveys, grant rolls — all of it. Filed as a chimney accident from a building that hadn't had a fire in nine years."

Quentin went very still, the way he did on the water when the current shifted under the hull. "Crane?"

"The fire permit was countersigned by the county commission. But the inspection report — the one that declared the site safe to demolish and clear —" she let it hang, "— was signed by Aldous Crane. Meridian Company, surveyor. He signed the paperwork on his own arson, Quentin. He didn't even use a false name."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Down the moorings a barge horn sounded, low and mournful, and somewhere a child was laughing, and the ordinary world went on around the two of them as if the ground hadn't just moved.

"So that's it, then," Quentin said finally, and his voice was quiet and hard. "The originals are ash. Your father's journal is a dead man's word. We ran the narrows, we dodged the gunboats, we hid a map in a coffin, and the law's answer is a pile of cinders and a signature." He shook his head slowly. "Told you. Paper's how rivers get owned. Now you've seen how they get stolen, too."

"The originals are ash," Sera agreed. She opened her fist, and the brass key lay in her palm, catching the last of the light. "But not everything burned."

Quentin looked at the key. Then at her. And Sera watched the calculation move behind his eyes — she was learning to read him, current by current — the pilot's instinct that said *cast off, go downriver, outrun it*, warring with something newer and less welcome that had been growing in him since Port Calloway.

"Where?" he said at last.

"Amity Trust and Safekeeping. Box forty-one. Tomorrow, at opening." She closed her hand again. "Fair copies. Sixty documents. Three deeds signed by Crane on dates he provably wasn't there — proven by his own field notes."

The silence stretched. A rope creaked against a cleat. Somewhere behind Quentin's stillness, Sera could see the exact moment the argument lost — not because he agreed, but because he had stopped pretending he could leave without her, and she had stopped pretending she could stop.

"Tomorrow at opening," Quentin said, in the voice of a man signing on for a voyage he already knows will nearly kill him. "Early. Before the street wakes." He turned back to the fuel lines, then paused, wrench in hand, and added without looking at her:

"We'll need to be gone from this mooring by noon either way. If the Company burned an annex to bury your father's truth, they've got someone watching this town for whoever comes digging it up." He nodded, almost imperceptibly, toward the far bank — where, among the moored skiffs and drying nets, a grey-hulled launch sat at anchor with no cargo, no crew visible, and no reason at all to be there.

Sera followed his gaze, and the warmth of the key in her palm suddenly felt like a brand.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we dig fast."
