## 10. Gunboat Wake

The narrows began where the river forgot its manners.

Quentin had run the Serrano Narrows forty times in eleven years, and every time it introduced itself like a stranger — all elbows and grudges, the current pinching between limestone walls that leaned close enough to read the water stains like handwriting. The Marguerite drew too much for comfort and not enough for honesty; she was a boat that survived on his attention alone, and he gave it to her now in full measure, one hand on the wheel, one on the throttle, his eyes moving in the old rhythm: bank, bend, boil, bank.

Morning fog still clung to the cut in ropes and rags. It burned off slow, the sun coming through it like a lamp behind muslin, and in that half-light Quentin saw the wake.

It came around the lower bend a mile back — a long white seam drawn across the grey water, straight as a ruled line, too disciplined for driftwood, too fast for freight. He knew before he raised the glasses. He'd known since Stone Latch, since the inspector with the soft hands had run his palm along the Marguerite's rail like a man appraising a horse he intended to buy cheap. Some boats you passed and were forgotten by nightfall. Others you marked.

He raised the binoculars anyway. Confirmation was a discipline, and discipline was most of what he owned.

Grey hull. Low freeboard. Twin screws churning a wake that didn't wander. A Meridian patrol boat, the kind they built in pairs up at the works — one for show, painted pretty for the shareholders' photographs, and one for this: bare, black-funneled, a machine for being somewhere it wasn't invited. This was the second kind. There was a mount on her foredeck under a tarp, and tarps on foredecks always covered something worth covering.

"Company's come calling," he said to the wheelhouse, because saying things aloud kept them ordinary.

Sera was below, sleeping in the berth she'd claimed without asking, curled around the oilskin packet like a dog around a bone it hadn't decided whether to bury or eat. Tomas sat on the stern bench mending a net he never fished with, his hands moving in old patterns while his eyes did other work entirely. Quentin didn't trust those eyes yet. He wasn't sure he ever would. But the man could read water, and right now Quentin wanted every reader he had.

"Tomas."

The old deckhand appeared at the door, wiping his palms on his thighs. He looked back downriver, followed Quentin's glance, and something moved across his face — not surprise. Recognition. That was worse.

"You know her?" Quentin asked.

"I know the shape." Tomas's voice was flat as slate. "Meridian runs three of those above the falls. That'll be the *Vigilant*. She used to escort the removal barges." He said the last word the way a man says the name of a disease he's carried.

"She's gaining."

"She's got two hundred horsepower and no cargo. You've got a boat full of books and bad decisions." Tomas squinted. "She's not running you down, Captain. Not yet. She's pacing you."

That was the truth of it, and Quentin felt it settle into his chest like cold ballast. A gunboat that wanted to board you came alongside with a hail and a hook. A gunboat that wanted to *know* you hung back at the edge of visibility and wrote down everything — your speed, your draft, your stops, your habits. Somewhere behind that tarp-covered mount a man with a notebook was turning Quentin Ashgrove from a rumor into a file.

*They're marked now.* The thought arrived without drama, the way the worst thoughts do.

"All right," Quentin said. "Then we stop being interesting."

---

The narrows ran nine miles from the Lower Gate to the Weeping Cliffs, and in those nine miles the river dropped eleven feet and changed its mind four hundred times. Charts called the middle stretch the Braidwater, where the current split itself around a graveyard of shifting sandbars and reassembled downstream, sullen and quick. No pilot ran the Braidwater at speed. No pilot ran it at all if he could help it. The bars moved after every flood, and the river kept its own ledger of them, written in gravel, revised nightly.

Quentin had spent three seasons keeping a rival ledger.

It lived in his head, which was the only safe place for it — ink could be seized, but memory died with a man, and Quentin had every intention of dying old and inconvenient. He knew the Braidwater the way some men knew their sins: intimately, uselessly, and better than anyone who might come looking.

"Take the wheel," he told Tomas. "Hold her center-channel until I say. Then do exactly what I say and nothing else."

Tomas took the spokes without comment. Through the open door Quentin heard Sera come up the companionway, and then she was beside him at the glass, hair uncombed, the packet's corner showing from inside her jacket like a knife handle.

"How long has it been there?"

"Since the bend. Maybe longer."

"Can we outrun it?"

"In the narrows? Nobody outruns anything. You only survive it." He handed her the glasses. "Look at her bow wave. See how clean it is? She's drawing six feet if she's drawing an inch. This channel averages five."

Sera lowered the glasses slowly. "So she can't follow us in."

"So she *shouldn't*. But Company men have been following things they shouldn't since before either of us was born." He watched the grey shape grow against the fog-burn, patient as arithmetic. "What they can't do is guess where the deep water goes. And right now, neither can anyone else."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm about to do something stupid," Quentin said, "and I'd take it kindly if you held onto something."

---

The first fork of the Braidwater opened ahead like a wound — the main channel swinging left, broad and brown and honest-looking, and to the right a narrower throat between two gravel spits where the water ran green and quick over hidden shoulders of sand. Every instinct a pilot had said *left*. The chart said *left*. The drowned trees leaning out of the left channel's margin said *left*, in the way drowned trees always testified to past mistakes.

Quentin turned right.

The Marguerite's hull groaned as the current grabbed her quarter and tried to argue. Tomas held her straight without being told twice, and Quentin stood at the starboard window reading the water the way other men read scripture — the darker green meant depth, the olive shimmer meant sand two feet down, the place where the surface wrinkled without wind meant a bar rising to meet him, and he threaded all of it with the throttle breathing under his hand, fast where the channel funneled, slow where it widened, the boat answering him like a thought.

Behind them, faint through the fog-ruins, the *Vigilant*'s engines changed pitch. Slowing. Hesitating at the fork.

"They're stopping," Sera said.

"They're arguing." Quentin allowed himself one thin breath of satisfaction. "Their charts show the right-hand channel closing out in '09. It doesn't. It shifted east in the flood of '11 and nobody paid to survey it again, because nobody with money needed to come through here." He eased the wheel a spoke. "Charts are just maps of what somebody bothered to learn. Out here, ignorance is a wall, and today I'm standing behind it."

"That sounds like a philosophy."

"It's a business model."

The channel narrowed until the banks were close enough to hear the water combing through the grass roots, and then it bent hard around a bar whose crest broke the surface in a long amber curl, and Quentin brought the Marguerite around it so tight her wash slapped the gravel and rang off the canyon wall. For ninety seconds there was nothing in the world but water and wheel and the animal concentration that was, if he was honest, the closest thing to happiness he trusted.

Then the channel opened, braided back into the main stem, and the narrows spat them out beneath the Weeping Cliffs, where groundwater bled down the limestone in a hundred silver threads and the air went cool and mineral-sweet.

Quentin looked back.

The Braidwater lay empty behind them, a maze of light and gravel with no grey hull in it. Far downstream, at the mouth of the left channel, a smudge of smoke hung where the *Vigilant* had turned away — or pretended to.

"We lost her," Sera said.

"No." Quentin cut the throttle to a crawl and let the Marguerite drift, listening. "We lost her *sight* of us. Different animal."

Tomas spat over the rail. "They'll put a boat above the cliffs. Watch the exits. That's how they work — they don't chase, they wait. Cheaper."

"And you know this how?"

The old man's hands went still on the net. "I told you. I ran supplies for them once. Removal crews, checkpoint barges. You learn their habits the way you learn a fever's." He met Quentin's eyes, and there was no apology in his face, only the tired fact of himself. "You want me sorry, Captain, I'm sorry. But sorry doesn't tell you where they'll be tonight. Knowing does."

Quentin let the silence sit a moment, testing it. Then he nodded once and turned back to the river, because the river was the thing in front of him, and the thing in front of you is the thing that kills you first.

---

They made the anchorage below the Weeping Cliffs an hour before dusk, tucking the Marguerite into a willow-choked slough where the overhang hid her silhouette from the channel. Quentin killed the engine and the silence came down like a lid. Somewhere in the willows a heron complained about them and gave up.

He cooked without appetite — rice, dried fish, the bitter greens Sera had gathered without being asked, because she was the sort of person who gathered things, evidence and greens and grudges alike. They ate on the stern bench in the last copper light, and the river slid past the slough's mouth, brown and gold and indifferent.

"There," Sera said quietly.

Not a boat. A light — high on the cliff road that switchbacked along the narrows' rim, a lantern moving with the deliberate pace of a man walking a post. It stopped at the point overlooking the slough's mouth. It stayed there.

"Watching the exit," Tomas said. "Told you."

"He can't see us in here."

"He can see anywhere we *go*." Tomas tore his bread and chewed. "And when we move, he'll signal, and whatever's waiting downstream will be told our heading, our speed, our draft. They'll know us by morning. Name, owner, port of registry, what we had for supper." He glanced sideways. "Well. What *you* had for supper."

Quentin set down his plate. The thrill of the Braidwater — that hot, singing thing in his blood, the old joy of out-thinking the river and everyone on it — had gone quiet somewhere in the last hour, and what remained underneath was colder and heavier. He knew this feeling. He'd felt it once before, years ago, in a different narrows, in a different life, the night he understood that running was no longer a choice but a condition.

*Never stop, never belong.* His creed, worn smooth as a river stone. It had kept him alive. It had also, he was beginning to suspect, kept him useless.

"They're not going to lose interest," Sera said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"Because of the journal. Because of what my father wrote." Her hands were wrapped around a tin cup she wasn't drinking from. "Because if Meridian lets us reach the headwaters with proof of what they did, it isn't just a scandal. It's the whole company. The dam, the leases, the land — all of it built on paper Crane signed with someone else's name underneath."

"Yes."

"Then they won't send one gunboat next time."

"No," Quentin said again. "Next time they'll send enough."

The lantern on the cliff point went dark. In its place, far downriver where the narrows gave out onto the long reach, another light answered — brief, red, deliberate. Signal to signal. A conversation happening over their heads in a language of fire, and every word of it about them.

Sera looked at him across the small fire, and in the coals' light her face was young and stubborn and afraid in the specific way of people who have decided to be brave anyway. "So what do we do?"

Quentin looked out at the dark water, at the red ember of the distant signal fading, and felt the dread arrive fully now, taking up residence, unpacking its bags. The trick had worked. The trick always worked. But tricks bought hours, not safety, and somewhere below them in the dark the river was already carrying word of the *Marguerite* faster than any boat could steam — down through the narrows, down the long reach, into the ears of men who solved problems with tarps and mounts and notebooks.

There was only one direction left that the Company didn't own, and it was up. Above the falls, into the country where charts failed and the river remembered things the law had agreed to forget.

"We go up," he said. "Past the licensed limit. Past the falls, if we have to." He heard himself say it and marveled, distantly, at how easily the promise came — how a man could spend a lifetime refusing to carry anyone's burdens and then find himself volunteering for the heaviest one on the river.

Sera searched his face. "You said you'd never run above the line."

"I say a lot of things." Quentin fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Tomorrow we make for the upper reach before first light, before that man on the point finishes his coffee. And Sera—" he waited until she looked at him, really looked, "—whatever's waiting for us downriver, it's already awake. From tonight on, we're not hiding anymore. We're racing."

Above the willows the stars came out hard and cold, and the river ran on through the dark, whispering toward the sea, carrying everything it knew.
