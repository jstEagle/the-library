## 11. What the Journal Says

The Marguerite lay moored in a backwater below the narrows, tied to a drowned willow that had learned to live with wet feet. Quentin had chosen the spot for its silence — no traffic lane, no Company patrol schedule, nothing but frogs and the slow tick of the hull cooling after the day's heat. He told himself it was prudence. He was beginning to suspect it was something closer to dread.

Sera Vane sat across the cabin table from him with the oilskin packet between them like a third passenger.

They had not spoken much since the records annex. Since the smell of char that still clung to her sleeves, since the clerk's whisper — *Crane signed the permit himself* — had turned every map and ledger on this river into evidence of something worse than greed. Quentin had run boats through gunfire before. He had never run one through a silence like this.

"Open it," Sera said.

"It's your father's."

"It's been yours longer than it was ever mine." Her voice didn't crack; she'd built it too well for that. But her hands, when she pushed the packet toward him, were not steady. "You carried it up half a river. You earned the right to be there when it speaks."

Quentin wiped his palms on his trousers — an old habit from engine work, though there was no grease on them now, only sweat — and worked the oilskin's knots loose. The canvas had been wrapped and rewrapped so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. Inside: the map he'd glimpsed once, by dying firelight, and beneath it a journal bound in river-calf leather, swollen with damp years, its spine stitched twice where it had broken and been mended.

And a letter, folded small, addressed in a careful hand: *For Sera.*

She took the letter without a word and turned away toward the lamp by the stern window, giving herself the privacy of a woman who had learned not to ask for it out loud.

Quentin opened the journal.

* * *

The first pages were surveyor's shorthand, all bearings and elevations, the tidy arithmetic of a man measuring a world he did not yet love. Elias Vane had been twenty-three when he'd signed on with the Meridian Company's upper-river survey, and he had written like a man who believed the Company when it said the headwaters were empty.

*Day 41. Ran the line above the falls. No settlements marked on the Company chart. Found a stone weir across the east channel, old work, fitted without mortar. Who builds weirs where there are no people? Noted for removal if it obstructs the dam line.*

Quentin read that last sentence twice. *Noted for removal.* As if the weir itself were a squatter.

He looked up. Sera stood at the window with her back to him, the letter held close to the lamp, and he could see by the set of her shoulders that whatever her father had written her was heavier than anything in the journal. He went back to the pages.

*Day 44. Met people today. The chart says this valley is vacant. The chart is a lie.*

The handwriting changed here — looser, faster, as if the man writing it could no longer afford the patience for neatness.

*They call themselves Riverkin. They have fished these channels for — they don't count years the way we do, but their oldest weir predates the fort at Amity, and Amity is older than Meridian by two generations. There is a woman, Odessa's grandmother, I think, who showed me the fish-ladders cut into the rock. Cut. Into rock. With tools and time and more patience than any Company has ever had.*

*The valley is terraced to the treeline. Orchards gone wild. Graves with names on them going back further than any deed in the provincial registry. And Meridian's map shows this as unimproved wilderness. Unimproved. I have seen what improvement looks like. It looks like Port Calloway.*

Quentin heard Sera turn. She came back to the table and sat, and her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, which somehow made it worse.

"He knew them," she said. "Before all of it. He knew them."

"He surveyed for them," Quentin said. "That's different."

"No." She reached across and turned the journal back three pages, her finger stopping under a line. "Read that again."

*Day 47. The elders asked me, through Odessa — she speaks trade-tongue — why the Company wants the water. I told them about the dam, the power, the progress. Odessa listened and then said: you cannot own a thing that owns you. I laughed. I should not have laughed. She wasn't being poetic. She was explaining the local law, and it is older and better than ours.*

"She was right," Sera said quietly. "Whoever she was."

Quentin didn't answer. He was thinking about the woman named Odessa he'd ferried once, years ago, downstream from the falls country — a pilot with river-eyes who had read his charts upside down and found two errors in them. He put the thought away. It would keep.

* * *

The lamplight guttered once when the river breeze found the cabin door, and in that flicker Sera saw her father's handwriting swim like fish under ice. She had read the letter four times now. It was short. That was the cruelest part — a whole life of absence compressed into half a page, ending with words she could not yet decide whether to believe:

*I did not leave because I stopped loving you. I left because I stayed too long at things that mattered less. If you are reading this, then a man named Ashgrove kept his word, which will surprise us both.*

She hated him for that line. She hated that she almost smiled at it.

"Keep reading," she said, because if she stopped she would have to talk about the letter, and she was not ready. "Out loud, if you can. I want to hear how he says it."

Quentin cleared his throat like a man starting a cold engine. Then he read.

*"Month six. The deeds came through today. Crane brought them himself, riding a launch with the Company flag and a case of good whiskey, and he read the names off the abstract like a litany: the valley conveyed to Meridian Consolidated by lawful purchase from the rightful owners. Rightful owners. I know these people. I have eaten at their fires. There was no purchase. There were papers signed by men who cannot write, witnessed by men who were not present, notarized by a justice of the peace who has never been within eighty miles of this water. I said as much. Crane smiled at me the way a knife smiles, and told me my signature was on the original survey, and that surveys do not lie even when surveyors do."*

Quentin's voice caught on that. Sera watched him wrestle it flat.

*"They came at night. Of course they came at night. Removal crews, Tomas's old outfit — I recognized the launch engines. The Kin were given until dawn to take what they could carry. Some of them carried nothing. Old Wren stood on his own doorstep and recited the names of everyone buried on that hillside, and when he ran out of names he started over, and they carried him out mid-list."*

Sera's hand had found the edge of the table and gripped it.

*"I wrote to the magistrate. My letter came back unopened. I wrote to the territorial office. A clerk wrote back to inform me that the matter fell outside his jurisdiction, and that my continued correspondence was noted. Noted. Everyone notes everything and no one does anything. So I did the only thing left. I copied everything. Deeds, dates, signatures, the survey field notes that prove the weirs predate the Company's own charter. I hid the originals where no clerk will ever look, and I kept copies, and I have spent eleven years being called a thief by the men who actually stole."*

The cabin was very quiet. Out in the dark, a night bird called across the water, three notes descending, like something letting go.

"They burned the annex," Sera said. "Eleven years later, they're still burning things."

"Burning paper is cheaper than answering it." Quentin turned a page. His jaw was tight. "Your father wasn't a thief. He was a witness. That's a more dangerous trade."

"There's more." She leaned over and touched the next entry, dated nearly a year after the removal. "This is where he stops writing like a surveyor."

* * *

Quentin read the passage she pointed to, and understood why she'd hesitated.

*"I went back. Twice, before the checkpoints went up. The valley in the rain season is the most beautiful place I have ever failed. The terraces have gone to bramble but the stone holds. The weirs still hold. Water does not need permission to remember its shape. I stood on the east bank where Wren used to fish and I thought: they can steal the land from the living, but the dead keep their address. Every grave on that hillside is a deed. Every weir is a signature. Someday someone will read this valley the way you read a document, and understand that it was never vacant, only robbed."*

Quentin set the journal down. His hands felt strange, as if the book had passed something into them.

"I've run this river twenty-two years," he said. "I've seen the Company charts. Headwaters marked *uninhabited*. I believed the paper." He shook his head slowly. "Paper's just paper until somebody bleeds on it."

"That's what he learned." Sera pulled the journal back toward her, gently, the way you'd take a sleeping child from a stranger's arms. "Too late. But he learned it." She turned to the final entries — sparse now, the handwriting of a sick man rationing his strength. The last full page was dated eight months ago.

*"If anyone finds this: the source valley belongs to the Riverkin, by tenancy older than any crown, by graves, by weirs, by water. Meridian's title is forgery stacked on forgery, and Aldous Crane's name is on every layer of it. I am done running from what I helped measure. Let whoever reads this finish what I couldn't. And let them tell my daughter that her father's last honest act was to stop lying."*

Below that, one more line, added later in a hand gone thin and shaky:

*Sera. Forgive me or don't. But know whose daughter you are.*

Sera closed the journal with both hands, as if it might otherwise fly apart.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed. The willow branches dragged softly along the hull. Quentin found himself thinking of his brother, of the debt of stillness he'd never paid, and he understood suddenly why Elias Vane had hired a moving man to carry his dying body upriver: because only someone who had spent a lifetime leaving could be trusted to understand what it cost to finally arrive.

"The elders," Quentin said at last. "Some of them survived. Your father wrote that Odessa woman's granddaughter — or the woman herself —"

"Odessa." Sera looked up sharply. "There was a Riverkin pilot at the falls landing, years back. The boatmen used to say she could run the gorge blindfolded. Everyone assumed she was dead, or gone."

"River folk don't die," Quentin said. "They just move upstream where the charts stop." He was already reaching for the river guide wedged above his bunk, already flipping past the licensed limits to the blank space where the paper gave up and the water went on. "If anyone's left up there who can read those weirs the way your father hoped —"

A sound from the deck stopped him. Footsteps, soft ones, deliberate — the tread of a man who wanted to be heard coming. Then a knock at the cabin door, and Tomas's voice, low and rough as rope:

"Captain. Miss Vane. Hate to interrupt." A pause, long enough to mean something. "But there's a lantern signal coming off the point yonder. Three flashes, pause, two. And captain — I've seen that code before." Another pause, longer. "I used to answer it myself."

Quentin met Sera's eyes across the table, across the open journal, across eleven years of other people's crimes.

"Tomas," he called out, keeping his voice level, "you'd better come in and tell us exactly where you've seen it."
