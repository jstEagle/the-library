## 12. The Informant

The rain had followed them down from the narrows, thin and persistent, ticking against the Marguerite's cabin roof like a man counting out coins he didn't want to spend. Sera sat at the little table beneath the lamp, her father's journal open in front of her, though she hadn't turned a page in twenty minutes. She had stopped reading the words and started reading the silences between them — the places where Elias Vane's handwriting went small and careful, as if he were afraid of being overheard by the paper itself.

*They came on a Tuesday,* one entry said. *I was upriver. I heard it from three miles off.*

She knew what he'd heard. She'd read enough now to know.

The deck boards outside creaked — the particular slow, deliberate creak of a man who wanted to be heard coming. Sera closed the journal and laid her hand flat on its cover, the way you'd quiet a dog.

Tomas appeared in the doorway with his knock already half-finished, an old habit of a man who'd spent forty years entering rooms that weren't his. He was past sixty, built like something the river had worn down rather than worn out, his forearms roped with old rope-scars, his face a topography of sun and squint. He held two enamel mugs of chicory coffee, and he set one down in front of her without asking, which was either courtesy or strategy.

"You read that thing like it's going to bite," he said.

"It might."

He lowered himself onto the bench across from her with a sound like a hull settling. For a while neither of them drank. The lamp hissed. Somewhere forward, Quentin's boots crossed the deck twice, unhurried, then stopped — she could feel him standing there in the dark beyond the doorway, listening, the way he always listened, a man who trusted nothing that happened behind his back.

"Tomas." She said his name plainly, because she'd learned in the salvage yards that plainness got you further than politeness. "You've been circling me for two days. Say what you came to say."

The old man looked into his mug. When he spoke, his voice had changed — dropped some of the deckhand's easy gravel and found something underneath it, older and heavier, like silt under moving water.

"I ran supplies for Meridian," he said. "Eleven years ago. Removal crews, they called them. I told myself it was just cargo. Tents, chain, stoves, barbed wire. A man can tell himself anything if the pay's good and nobody makes him look at what the cargo's *for*."

Sera didn't move. The rain ticked on. Her hand stayed flat on the oilskin cover of the journal, and under her palm she felt the shape of everything her father had written about those crews, all of it pressing up through the leather like heat through a stove lid.

"Go on," she said.

"Stone Latch checkpoint. The weir landing above the falls. The portage trails through the hills where no chart shows a path." He ticked the names off on scarred fingers, and each name landed in Sera's chest like a stone dropped down a well. "I know which inspectors take bribes and which ones just like to hear themselves talk. I know the hand signals the launch pilots use to each other. I know how Crane's men run a search — they start at the bow because they want you watching the bow." He looked up, and his eyes were wet-bright and steady. "I can get you past every checkpoint on this river. Every one. And I'm telling you now because if I wait until you need me, you'll think I'm buying my way out of something instead of paying into it."

"And aren't you?" Sera asked. It wasn't cruelty. It was the question she genuinely needed answered.

Tomas took it without flinching, which she noted, and respected.

"Both," he said. "A man's never just one thing, girl. That's the first lie the Company ever sold anybody — that people are simple. Simple people are easy to move."

*Easy to move.* The phrase went through her like cold water. She thought of the journal again, of the Riverkin families her father had described, loaded onto barges at gunpoint with their terraced gardens burning behind them. *Simple people are easy to move.* Eleven years ago, Tomas had been one of the hands doing the moving.

Outside, the boots on the deck resumed, and Quentin filled the doorway without entering — he had a way of arriving that made doorways seem like things he tolerated rather than used. His eyes went from Tomas to the closed journal to Sera's face, reading all three in order, and whatever he found there made his jaw set in the particular way she was beginning to recognize: not anger, but arithmetic. A man calculating risk.

"How much of that did you hear?" Tomas asked.

"All of it. Walls on this boat are thinner than your excuses." Quentin stepped in, took the third mug from the shelf, poured himself coffee from the pot on the stove, and drank standing up, as if sitting would mean staying, and staying would mean this conversation mattered. "You ran supplies for Meridian's removal crews."

"That's what I said."

"For eleven years?"

"Six years running, five years trying to forget it. Neither worked."

Quentin set the mug down. "And now you want to guide us through their checkpoints. Out of the goodness of a guilty heart."

"Out of exactly that," Tomas said, unoffended. "Guilty hearts are the only ones that work for free."

Sera watched the two men look at each other across the little table, and she understood that what passed between them wasn't hostility so much as recognition — two men who had both made their livings on this river by not looking too hard at what floated past them. Quentin had said as much to her once, in the dead calm below the falls, when the engine lay open between them like a patient on a table: *Every boat on this river has carried something for Meridian. Most of us just never asked what.* He'd said it lightly, the way he said most true things, and she'd let him get away with the lightness because she'd needed him light just then.

She didn't let him get away with it now.

"He's offering what we don't have," she said. "We're marked, Quentin. You said it yourself after the narrows — that gunboat knows our lines, our draft, our whistle. At Stone Latch they'll be looking for us specifically. You can lie as well as any man alive, but lying gets harder every mile upstream, and we both know it."

"Lying's not the problem." Quentin's voice stayed level, but she saw his thumb press white against the mug's rim. "The problem is trusting a man whose loyalty has a price tag hanging off it backwards. Men who've been bought once stay bought, or they turn again. Either way, you're not holding the money."

"And what's your alternative?" Sera kept her voice gentle, which somehow made it worse for him; she could see that. "Run the checkpoints blind? Hide the map in another coffin and pray the inspector's feeling sentimental?"

"The coffin worked."

"The coffin worked because the inspector was lazy. The next one won't be. You know it, I know it, and—" she nodded at Tomas— "he knows it better than either of us."

Tomas raised his mug slightly, a toast to being agreed with, and Quentin looked at him the way he looked at shoal water: measuring depth, suspecting rocks.

"Tell me about the night," Quentin said suddenly.

Tomas blinked. "What night?"

"The night you're paying for. The one that put that look on your face. If I'm going to bet this woman's life and my boat on your guilt, I want to know what it's made of."

The lamp hissed. Rain ticked. Sera realized she was holding her breath.

Tomas set his mug down with great care, as if it might break, or as if he might.

"There's a bend below the Riverkin ferry landing," he said. "Deep water, good holding ground. We anchored there the last night — six barges, my boat among them, loaded with fencing and flood lamps. Orders were to have the landing cleared by morning. There were maybe two hundred people there. Families. They'd been served papers full of words none of them could read, in a language the papers weren't even printed in properly — the Company's clerks wrote the notices in trade-tongue and the Riverkin signed with marks, and the marks meant nothing, and everybody on both sides of the paper knew it."

His hands had gone still on the table. Sera noticed they were trembling anyway, very slightly, like water finding its level.

"We lit the flood lamps at midnight. Not to help them pack. To keep them from leaving by water. The Company didn't want them scattering into the hills — scattered people come back. So we strung the lamps across the channel and sat there all night with the light burning on the water, and the whole village stood on the bank with what they could carry, watching us watch them. An old man came out on the landing and sang for a while. Nobody stopped him. I don't know if that was mercy or if the song was part of somebody's schedule."

Tomas's voice caught — not broke, but caught, like a cleat snagging a line.

"Toward dawn a woman tried to cross with two children in a dugout. Small boat, dark hull, going right along the edge of the lamp line where the shadow was. My pilot — Company man, name of Rusk — swung a searchlight on her. Just to make her stop. That's all it was supposed to do." He swallowed. "She panicked in the light. Swung away from it, straight out into the current. The dugout swamped at the bend. We had boats in the water inside a minute. We pulled the children out. Both of them, alive. We never found her."

The rain filled the silence, patient, indifferent.

"The children," Sera said quietly. "What happened to the children?"

"They were taken upriver to a Company mission school. That's what the paperwork called it." Tomas met her eyes. "I carried the requisition order myself. Blankets, clothing, two children, one lot. That's how it was written. One lot."

Nobody spoke. Down the length of the boat, the Marguerite's timbers settled and groaned around them, the old vessel breathing in its sleep, and Sera thought: *this is what my father heard from three miles off.* This was the Tuesday. This was the sound. And somewhere in the years after, Elias Vane had sat in mining camps and saloons writing these pages in handwriting gone small and careful, carrying the same weight Tomas was carrying now, except her father had chosen exile and Tomas had chosen decks and cargo runs and forty years of not talking about it.

Quentin drained his coffee and set the mug down. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost none of its caution, but something underneath it had shifted — Sera heard it the way you hear a current change under a boat, felt more than seen.

"You understand I can't trust you," he said. "Not fully. Not ever, probably. Trust isn't a thing you get back once you've spent it on Company work."

"I'm not asking for trust," Tomas said. "I'm asking for a job. Point me at a channel and I'll take you through it. Watch me the whole way. Tie a line to my belt if it makes you sleep better. But don't pretend you've got a better option, Captain, because I've been listening to you plan for two days, and every plan you've got ends with the word *luck* in it."

That earned the ghost of a smile from Quentin — gone almost before it arrived, but Sera caught it, and she suspected Tomas did too.

"Here's how it works," Quentin said. "You guide. You don't decide. You don't signal anyone, you don't speak to anyone ashore, and if we're boarded, you stand where I can see your hands. First sign of Company colors in your eyes that I don't like, and you'll be swimming to whatever shore's nearest, and I won't slow down."

"Fair terms."

"And one more." Quentin leaned forward, and the lamplight carved his face into planes and shadows. "If we get to that valley — if we actually make it — you look those elders in the eye and you tell them what you told us tonight. All of it. Not for absolution. For the record. They've had eleven years of the Company deciding what the record says. They can have one honest sentence from the other side."

Tomas was quiet a long moment. Then he nodded, once, slow, like a man accepting a sentence rather than a bargain.

"They won't want to hear it."

"No," Quentin said. "They won't. Do it anyway."

* * *

After Tomas left, Sera stayed at the table with the lamp turned low, and Quentin lingered in the doorway, which was unlike him. He was a man who left rooms cleanly, the way he left towns.

"You think I made the wrong call," she said.

"I think you made the only call. Those are different things." He rubbed the back of his neck, a rare tell. "My brother used to say that. *Only call's not the same as right call, Quent.* He was usually the wrong call himself." Something moved behind his eyes and was gone, filed away with the rest of what he didn't say about himself.

"Then why agree?" she asked.

Quentin looked out the doorway at the rain, at the black river sliding past, at the far shore where the hills were already rising toward the country they were sailing into — country with checkpoints on it, and gunboats, and a valley at the end of it that two hundred people had been forced to leave in the dark.

"Because he's right about the luck," Quentin said. "And because a man who carries a thing that long — eleven years, and it still sits on him like ballast — that man's not bought. Bought men travel light." He pushed off from the doorframe. "Get some sleep. Stone Latch by tomorrow evening, if the river behaves."

"And if it doesn't?"

He paused at the top of the steps, half-turned, rain-light silvering his shoulder.

"Then we find out what Tomas remembers," he said, "and whether it's worth what it cost him."

She heard his boots cross the deck, unhurried, and fade. Sera opened the journal again, to the page she'd been stuck on, and read the last line once more by the low flame:

*I knew the pilot of the supply boats. He was kinder than the rest. Kindness changes nothing, but I remember it anyway, and I hate that I remember it.*

She looked up, toward the bow, where an old man was taking the first watch in the rain, and she wondered which of them her father had meant to forgive.

Then the engine-room bell rang twice — Quentin's signal, sharp and short — and through the rain ahead, far down the black channel, a white light swept the water once, twice, and held.

Company colors. Stationary. Directly across the river at Stone Latch.

And it was not yet tomorrow evening.
