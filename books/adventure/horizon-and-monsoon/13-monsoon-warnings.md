## 13. Monsoon Warnings

The radio had a voice like a man reading his own eulogy.

Quentin stood in the wheelhouse of the *Marguerite* with one hand on the throttle and the other on the brass rim of the wireless, listening to the weather bulletin crackle out of Fort Amity through a wall of static. The announcer's tone never changed — flat, unhurried, the voice of a man who had said these words every season for twenty years and would say them long after everyone listening was drowned or gone — and that was what made it terrible. The sea does not raise its voice either.

*"—depression now tracking northwest across the open water, sustained winds of ninety kilometers per hour and strengthening. Landfall projected for the nineteenth of the month. Repeat: landfall projected for the nineteenth. All licensed traffic above the Stone Latch narrows is advised to conclude operations no later than—"*

Static swallowed the date. Quentin didn't need it. He'd been running this river since he was fourteen years old, since before the Company put numbers on the shoals and names on the rapids, and he carried the calendar in his body the way other men carried watches. Nineteen days. Maybe sixteen if the depression fed on warm water the way they sometimes did. After that, the upper river would not be a river. It would be a brown argument between the sky and the land, and nothing with an engine would win it.

He turned the wireless down until the voice became a fly against glass.

Behind him, the wheelhouse door hung open to the morning, and the morning was wrong. He'd felt it for two days without naming it — a heaviness in the air like wet wool laid over the shoulders, a stillness in the heat that wasn't peace but held breath. The light came down thick and yellow, as though filtered through tea. Downriver behind them, where the burned records annex still sat in his mind like a coal under the floorboards, the horizon had gone the color of old pewter.

Nineteen days. And above them lay two hundred kilometers of narrows, three checkpoints, the falls — the falls, God help them — and whatever waited beyond the last charted bend.

"Bad news?"

Sera Vane stood at the rail below the wheelhouse window, arms crossed, watching the water slide past. She'd taken to standing there in the early hours, when the mist still clung to the banks in ropes and the kingfishers were working the shallows. She wore her father's canvas jacket now. It fit her badly at the shoulders and she hadn't once mentioned why she wore it, and Quentin hadn't once asked.

"Monsoon's making landfall in nineteen days," he said.

She turned. In the yellow light her face looked carved rather than drawn, all planes and shadow, her mother's jaw and her father's stubbornness fighting for the same territory. "And?"

"And after that, everything above the falls is closed. Not by law. By physics." He came down the companionway steps and joined her at the rail, close enough to smell the river on the wind — mud and rotting vegetation and, underneath, that faint mineral tang that meant the high country was already drinking rain. "The gorges fill. The shoals disappear, which sounds like good news until you understand that disappearing shoals means disappearing channel. The current runs four times its dry-season speed. There are stretches where the water comes through the narrows standing eight feet higher than my pilothouse windows."

"You've run it in flood."

It wasn't a question. She'd learned to read him the way he read water — from the surface, by inference, trusting the shape of things beneath.

"Once," he said. "Deliberately, I mean. Once." He watched a log turn slowly in an eddy off the port bow, gray and patient as a drowned man. "I was younger and the boat was lighter and there was a doctor on the far bank and a boy on mine who was bleeding out. I ran the gorge at half flood and I made it, and the doctor saved him, and I have never once thought of it as anything but the stupidest thing I've ever done." He paused. "I did it because stopping meant dying. That's the only arithmetic that justifies it."

"And now?"

"Now we're not dying. We're choosing."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked back at the river, and her voice, when it came, was quieter than he'd ever heard it. "My father chose too. He chose to leave. Every year of my childhood, he chose it again, and each time he told himself it was arithmetic." Her knuckles whitened on the rail. "So don't talk to me about choosing like it's clean."

He had no answer for that, so he gave her none, which was its own kind of answer, and she seemed to accept it. They stood together while the *Marguerite* worked upstream against the current, her engine knocking its steady heartbeat through the deck plates, and the pewter horizon crept closer behind them like something following at a respectful distance.

* * *

He called the meeting at noon, in the shade of the cargo awning amidships, because a captain who sets a deadline owes his crew the courtesy of saying it to their faces.

They were five now, counting himself. Sera, sitting straight-backed on a crate of rivets with the oilskin packet's empty twin — the decoy case, wrapped and weighted — resting on her knees like a sleeping cat. Old Ferro, his engineer, who had served with him eleven years and trusted him exactly as far as the pay did, which was to say completely, because Quentin had never missed a payday and never would. The Mwangi brothers, Doss and little Anselm, deckhands and line-handlers and, when the occasion demanded, liars of modest talent. And Tomas.

Tomas sat apart, as he always did, on the coil of spare hawser near the stern rail. He was sixty if he was a day, built like driftwood, with hands that told the history of forty years on this river in scar tissue. He'd come aboard at Port Calloway offering to guide them past the checkpoints, and Quentin had taken him on because Sera vouched for him and because a man who admitted his shame out loud was rarer than a man without any. He still didn't trust him. Trust, in Quentin's experience, was a bridge you crossed only after you'd tested every plank yourself.

"Here's the arithmetic," Quentin said, and laid it out the way he laid out everything: plainly, without decoration, because decoration was for people with time. "Landfall in nineteen days. The upper river closes after that — closes hard, closes for the season. Above us sits the Stone Latch checkpoint, then the narrows, then the falls, then country no chart shows honestly. If we're going to reach the source valley, we cross Stone Latch within ten days. Eleven at the outside. After that we run the narrows ahead of the first swells, and if the swells catch us in the gorge, we die, and worse than dying, we fail."

"Failing's worse than dying?" Anselm, the younger brother, always the one to poke at the edges of a thing.

"For this, yes." Quentin heard his own voice come out harder than he'd intended, and made himself continue. "There are people in that valley who've been waiting thirty years for someone to carry the truth to them. If we drown in the gorge with the journal in the hold, Meridian wins twice — once by killing us, and once by writing the story afterward. So. Ten days to Stone Latch. Anyone who wants off at the next landing gets their wages and no hard feelings. I won't think less of you." He would, a little, but that was the price of saying it kindly.

Ferro spat over the rail, a ritual as reliable as sunrise. "Ten days. You'll want the injectors cleaned and the fuel polished before the narrows regardless. I'll do it whether we go or stop, so don't flatter yourself that your deadline changes my work."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

Doss shrugged, which for Doss was a signature on a contract. Anselm grinned and said something in the river cant that made his brother elbow him, and that was settled.

Which left Tomas.

The old man had been looking at the water the whole time Quentin spoke, and now he looked up, and his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of a sky before hail. "You know what's above Stone Latch besides the checkpoint," he said. Not a question either. This river was full of men who didn't ask questions because questions could be denied later.

"The narrows."

"The narrows," Tomas agreed. "And in the narrows, this time of year, the Company keeps a gunboat. Not for smugglers." He said it flatly, without drama, the way a man reports weather. "For anyone asking after the headwaters. I ran supplies for the removal crews, Captain. I know what they post up there, and I know how they post it. Your ten days is generous. Your ten days is also a lie you're telling yourself, because Crane will have word of this boat by now, and Crane doesn't wait for deadlines."

Silence took the deck. Somewhere below, the engine knocked on, indifferent.

Quentin found that his hands had curled into fists against his thighs, and made them uncurl. "Then we go faster."

"Going faster is how boats die in the narrows."

"Going slower is how boats get boarded." He met the old man's gaze and held it. "I've spent my whole life believing that the answer to trouble is motion. More motion, faster motion, motion in a direction nobody expects. I'm not going to stop believing it now because the trouble has a gunboat."

Tomas held his look for a moment longer, then nodded, slow, like a man agreeing to terms he'd already read the fine print of. "Then pray your god is a pilot," he said, and went back to watching the water.

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep, which was itself an alarm bell, because Quentin Ashgrove slept the way other men breathed — automatically, anywhere, in any condition, a skill learned young and kept sharp. He lay in his bunk beneath the wheelhouse and listened to the river and did the arithmetic again, and again, and each time the sum came out worse.

Nineteen days. Two hundred kilometers. A checkpoint, a gunboat, a gorge, and a waterfall that no engine could climb. And somewhere behind all of it, patient as the pewter horizon, Aldous Crane.

He got up, lit the lamp, and took out the charts.

He spread them on the chart table — the good ones, the ones he drew himself, because the licensed surveys were lies of omission, missing sandbars the way official histories miss massacres. His own hand was all over them: soundings in pencil, corrections in ink, marginal notes in a shorthand only he could read. *Bar shifts left after high water. Watch for deadfall at the willow point. Do not trust the green buoy; it moved in '09 and nobody fixed it.*

He traced the route north with one finger. Stone Latch. The narrows. The falls. And beyond the falls, a blank space where the paper went white and the contour lines gave up, and in that blank space, a valley full of people who had once terraced the hillsides and built stone weirs and named every bend of the river in a language the surveyors hadn't bothered to record.

His finger stopped at the edge of the blank space and rested there.

When had it stopped being a delivery?

That was the question he'd been refusing to ask since Port Calloway, and it sat in the lamplight now like a stowaway finally come up on deck. Because a delivery had an end. You carried the parcel, you handed it over, you wiped your hands and cast off and the river took you away clean. That was the creed. Keep moving. Owe no one. Never stop, never belong, because belonging was a debt and debts were anchors and anchors were how boats ended up rusting at some town dock with their names peeling, owned by the place instead of owning the current.

But somewhere between the burned annex and the coffin at Stone Latch — no, further back, further even than that, maybe back to a feverish man collapsing on a dock with an oilskin packet in his fist — the parcel had become a promise, and the promise had become a plan, and the plan had become a thing he checked on at night the way a man checks on a fire he's decided to keep burning.

He cared whether they made it.

Not in the professional way, the way he cared about any passage, any cargo, any contract honored. He cared the way you care about a person. About persons. About a woman in a borrowed jacket who read her father's letter three times and then started planning a war. About an engineer who cleaned injectors whether or not the cause was worthy. About two brothers who joked in cant to keep fear small. Even, God help him, about a guilty old man on a hawser coil whose shame Quentin recognized the way you recognize your own face in dark glass.

Commitment had arrived uninvited. It had come aboard like a passenger at dusk, quiet and feverish, and he had let it stay, and now it was dying on his deck and asking him for promises, and he knew — he knew with the certainty of a man who has watched this exact pattern take other pilots apart — that promises were how moving men became stopped men.

His brother had taught him that. His brother, whose name he kept in the same locked drawer as the grief, whose grave was in a town Quentin had never returned to because returning would have meant admitting the moving hadn't saved him either.

Quentin closed the charts. He blew out the lamp. He lay back down in the dark and listened to the engine knock and the river breathe, and he made himself a bargain the way he made all his bargains: narrowly, precisely, with the exit marked.

*Nineteen days,* he told the darkness. *We make the valley, we deliver the truth, we see it safe. And then the river drops, and I cast off, and I owe no one.*

The darkness, being a river man, said nothing. But somewhere in the hull, in the timbers, in the old wood and iron of the *Marguerite*, he thought he felt the boat disagree — the way she always disagreed, patiently, with the patience of a vessel that had carried him through twenty years of never-stopping and knew better than he did what he was really running from.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin.

Quentin was at the wheel before first light, as always, taking the boat up the long straight reach below the Latch hills, when Sera climbed into the wheelhouse carrying two cups of coffee that tasted, as always, like boiled rope and salvation. She handed him his without a word and stood beside him at the window, and for a while neither of them spoke, because the morning was doing the speaking for them: the hills ahead rising black-green out of the mist, the water running smooth and deep and deceptively kind, the air gone heavy as a held breath.

"There," Sera said suddenly.

She was pointing north, where the river bent toward the first of the Latch hills. At first Quentin saw nothing but the ordinary traffic of a working morning — a wood raft, a line of cormorants drying their wings on a snag. Then he saw what she'd seen: a smudge of smoke rising from the tree line above the far bank. Not cooking smoke. Cooking smoke rose thin and domestic. This rose in a fat, deliberate column, straight up in the windless air, the way a signal rises.

Or a beacon.

Quentin set down the coffee untouched and reached for his glasses. Through the lenses the smudge resolved, and beneath it, glinting where the first sun caught it, something else — a shape moored in the lee of the point, angular and gray, too new for fishing, too armed for anything honest.

A launch. Flying no colors at all, which on this river meant colors you weren't meant to see yet.

"Stone Latch," he said quietly, "is eleven hours ahead of us."

Sera lowered her own borrowed glasses. "Is that—"

"That's a welcome party." He rang down for more revolutions, and the *Marguerite* answered the way she always answered, faithful and steady, driving them north toward the smoke and the gray shape and the ten days that had just become nine. Behind them the pewter horizon had begun, very faintly, to grumble.
