## 14. Checkpoint at Stone Latch

The river narrowed at Stone Latch the way a throat narrows before a word it doesn't want to say. On either side the cliffs rose grey and dripping, seamed with fern, and between them the water ran green-black and quick, funneling every boat on the upper reach through a single gate of stone where the Company had built its checkpoint out of timber and authority and gunmetal.

Quentin saw it from two miles off — you always did, the flag first, that grey-and-white pennant snapping above the palisade like a bird that had sold its song — and he felt the old familiar tightening in his chest, the one he'd learned to breathe around the way a man breathes around a cracked rib.

"Stone Latch," he said, mostly to himself.

Sera came up from below, wiping her hands on a rag. She'd been oiling the valve stems again, though they didn't need it, the way she'd been doing everything twice since the narrows, since the gunboat wake had faded astern and neither of them had said what they both knew: *they're marked now.* "That's it? That's the famous checkpoint?"

"That's it." He eased the throttle back a notch. The *Marguerite* grumbled, lowering her voice as if she understood too. "They'll board us. Two inspectors, maybe three, plus whatever soldiers are drinking coffee behind that wall this week. They'll want papers, cargo manifest, passenger list. They'll walk every inch of her."

"And we tell them—"

"We tell them exactly what's true, because truth is easier to carry than a lie. You're my paying passenger, bound for the upper trading posts. I'm carrying freight." He glanced at her. "The question is whether the map travels with us or not."

She didn't answer right away. Below them, the current was already picking up its pace toward the gate, eager or obedient, hard to say which. Sera leaned on the rail and looked at the checkpoint the way she looked at most things — like something she intended to take apart.

"There's a coffin," she said.

Quentin blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Manifest. I checked it last night when I couldn't sleep. You're carrying a coffin upriver. Shipped from Port Calloway, consigned to a family at Miller's Bend." She turned to face him, and there was something in her expression he was beginning to recognize — the particular stillness she got when an idea finished assembling itself behind her eyes. "A sealed coffin, Quentin. Nobody opens a sealed coffin. Not even Company men. There are superstitions about that sort of thing even among people who burn villages for a land deed."

He stared at her. Then at the coffin itself, lashed amidships under tarpaulin where it had ridden for three days without either of them giving it a second thought — a long dark box of planed hardwood, bound in iron, shipped by a grieving family who wanted their dead buried in hill country soil instead of the mudflats.

"You want to put your father's map," Quentin said slowly, "inside a stranger's father."

"It's not a stranger's father. It's a man going home." Her voice was steady but not cold; that was the thing about Sera Vane that kept catching him off guard. Even her hardest ideas arrived wearing grief. "It's the safest place on this boat. Safer than any hiding place we could build, because we didn't build it. We just have to be brave enough to do it and calm enough never to mention it."

* * *

They did it at dawn, while the mist still stood waist-deep on the water and the checkpoint was a smudge of lantern light ahead.

The coffin's lid was screwed down with sixteen screws, and Sera counted them as she backed each one out with a borrowed driver, laying them in order on a cloth spread across the deck so they could go back exactly where they'd come from. Quentin held the lantern low and tried not to look inside, and then made himself look, because a man who couldn't look at death had no business running a river.

The dead man lay in his Sunday coat, hands folded, face composed into the particular dignity that embalmers give the departed. He'd been a big man once; the illness or the years had hollowed him, leaving cheekbones like river stones under skin gone the color of birch bark.

"I'm sorry about this," Sera murmured to him, and meant it, Quentin could hear that she meant it. She wrapped the oilskin packet in oilcloth and then in a wool shirt, and slid it beneath the body along the man's left side, in the hollow where a living arm would rest. "You'll get him back safe. I promise. And nobody will know you carried more than yourself."

Quentin found he couldn't speak. There was something about the sight of her — kneeling over a dead man in the grey light, whispering promises to him as if he could keep them — that put a crack in him he hadn't planned for. His father had been buried in a box like that, or near enough. He hadn't gone. He'd been downriver, running cargo, keeping moving, owing no one. The memory arrived the way these things did, uninvited and fully armed, and he set it down again the way you set down something hot.

"Screws," he said, instead of any of that.

She drove them home in reverse order, seated each one flush, and wiped the heads with a rag so the bright metal wouldn't show. By the time the sun cleared the eastern cliff the coffin looked exactly as it had looked for three days, and the only evidence of what they'd done was the faint smell of lamp oil on their hands, which they washed away at the rail like two conspirators scrubbing a murder.

"Tomas," Quentin called.

The old deckhand appeared from the galley, mug in hand, eyes already on the checkpoint. He'd been quiet since the narrows — quieter than usual, which for Tomas was saying something, a silence with weight in it, ballast in the hold. "Captain."

"When they board, you're fixing the starboard winch. It's been grinding for a week." It hadn't, but Tomas nodded as if it had. "Loud work. Lots of swearing. Understood?"

"Understood." A pause. The mug steamed in the cold air. "Captain — those men down there. They'll have a list. Names." He said it carefully, the way a man walks across ice he's heard cracking. "Might be worth knowing how you mean to answer questions about me."

Quentin studied him. "You've run this checkpoint before."

"A time or two." Tomas drank his coffee and didn't meet his eyes. "Different flag, same pole."

Whatever that meant — and Quentin suspected he knew exactly what it meant, had suspected since the night Tomas admitted he'd once hauled supplies for men whose job was clearing rivers of the people who lived on them — there wasn't time to pull the thread. The current had them now. Stone Latch rose up on both sides, wet stone streaked white with bird lime, and the gate opened ahead: pilings, chain, a floating platform where two grey-coated figures waited beside a swivel-mounted rifle that pretended to be decorative.

"Easy water," Quentin said, to the boat, to himself, to everyone. "Easy water all the way through."

* * *

The inspector who came aboard introduced himself as Inspector Brack, and he had the kind of face that had been assembled for patience — heavy jaw, pale eyes, a mouth that looked like it had forgotten other shapes. His partner stayed on the platform with the soldiers, which Quentin noted and disliked: it meant Brack was the talker and the platform was the verdict.

"Papers, Captain."

Quentin handed them over. License, registry, cargo manifest, passenger declaration. All genuine, all in order, because the first rule of lying well was to make sure everything they could check was true.

Brack read slowly. That was the technique, Quentin knew — the slow read, the silence afterward, letting the inspected fill the quiet with confession. He'd used it himself on debt collectors once, long ago, in another life. He leaned against his own wheelhouse and watched the cliffs and let the silence belong to the river.

"Freight," Brack said finally. "Coffin."

"One coffin. Consigned to the Okonkwo family, Miller's Bend. Mother's brother, coming home to be buried." True, all of it. He'd verified the shipping order himself months ago when he took the contract.

"Anyone die aboard recently?" Brack asked it casually, eyes still on the paper, and Quentin felt the hook go in — felt it, named it, breathed around it.

"Not aboard. But it's the season for it. Fever's been walking the lower towns since the equinox." He let a little tiredness into his voice, the honest kind, the kind that couldn't be faked because it wasn't fake. "Half my passengers this month have been going somewhere to bury somebody or escaping somewhere that's about to."

Brack looked up. The pale eyes were very still. "And your passenger?"

Sera stood at the rail with a tin cup of tea, dressed in the plain working clothes she'd adopted for the trip, hair tied back, looking precisely like what she was pretending to be: nobody worth a second glance. "Sera Vane," she said, before Quentin could answer. "Booked passage to the upper posts. I'm a typesetter. I'm told there's a printing office at Fort Amity that needs steady hands, and I'm told the upper country pays better for anyone who can spell."

"Typesetter," Brack repeated.

"Movable type, ink, and headaches," Sera said, and sipped her tea. It was good, Quentin thought. It was better than good. She sounded bored.

Brack consulted his list. Quentin watched him do it — watched the finger slide down the column of names, watched it stop, watched the pause stretch half a second too long — and felt the fraying begin, the smooth weave of his composure catching on something rough underneath.

"Vane," Brack said. "Uncommon name."

"Common enough where I'm from."

"Where's that?"

"Downriver of wherever you're standing." She smiled when she said it, a small dry smile, and Brack almost smiled back, and Quentin understood suddenly that his heart was going too fast and that the terror was real and enormous and that none of it could be allowed to show, not in his hands, not in his breathing, not anywhere. This was the craft of it. Terror managed like a boat in floodwater: you didn't fight the current, you read it, you gave it just enough of yourself to steer.

Brack folded the papers and handed them back. "We'll need to look at your freight."

"Course you will." Quentin pushed off the wheelhouse and led him aft, unhurried, narrating as he went because narration was ballast — it filled the hold of the moment with ordinary words. "Tarps are lashed against the rain. Manifest's posted at the gangway. Winch is broken, so mind your boots, my deckhand's been cursing at it all morning."

Tomas, bless him, chose that moment to swear magnificently at the winch, a string of blasphemies so inventive that one of the soldiers on the platform laughed, and the tension shifted, just slightly, like weight moving across a deck.

They uncovered the coffin. Brack walked around it once. He put one gloved hand flat on the lid, and Quentin watched that hand and thought about sixteen screws and a wool shirt and a dead man keeping faith with the living, and he kept his face doing nothing at all, which was the hardest work he'd ever done, harder than any shoal, any gorge, any night water.

"Sealed," Brack said.

"Shipped sealed from Calloway. Undertaker's seal's on the hasp if you want to break it and explain to the family why their brother arrived opened."

Something moved behind the pale eyes. Not suspicion exactly — arithmetic. Brack was weighing the trouble of breaking a sealed coffin against the trouble of being wrong, and Quentin could see the scales tipping, could see the whole fragile structure of the next thirty seconds balanced on a bureaucrat's reluctance to touch the dead.

Then Brack lifted his hand.

"Company regulation requires inspection of all freight," he said, "excepting human remains in sealed transit, per Article Eleven." He said it with the flat satisfaction of a man reciting the verse that saves him effort. "You're clear, Captain."

"Much obliged."

They covered the coffin again. Brack stepped onto the platform, the chain came down, the pennant snapped its grey-and-white salute to nothing, and the *Marguerite* slid through the gate into the upper river with her secret riding quietly among the dead.

* * *

It wasn't until the cliffs fell away behind them and the valley opened into drowned forest and long brown reaches that Quentin let his shoulders come down from around his ears. He became aware, then, of Sera beside him at the wheel, and of the fact that his hands were trembling — fine, fast, unmistakable, the way rigging trembles after a storm it was never rated for.

"Well," she said.

"Don't." He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, short and unsteady. "Don't talk to me for a minute. I'm busy being terrified retroactively."

"He stopped at your name on the list. Did you see it? Before mine. His finger stopped on *Ashgrove*."

"I saw it."

"Why would the Company care about your name, Quentin?"

Because they'd boarded him at dusk in chapter four of his life, he almost said. Because a grey-coated man had asked him about a thief of survey documents and Quentin had lied smoothly and felt the hook set. Because names went on lists and lists went in files and files lived forever in Company offices, growing teeth.

"Old business," he said. "Debts. The kind that follow a man upriver."

Sera looked at him for a long moment with those eyes that took things apart, and mercifully she let it go — or seemed to. She went forward to help Tomas stow the tarps, and Quentin stood alone at the wheel with the current pushing them toward the high country and the monsoon waiting somewhere over the horizon like a debt of its own.

It was Tomas who broke the quiet, later, when the light went long and gold across the water. He came up to the wheelhouse with two cups of coffee, handed one over, and stood looking back the way they'd come, at the invisible checkpoint already lost in the haze.

"They'll write it down," Tomas said. "What happened today. Coffin, passenger, captain. It goes in a ledger, and the ledger goes to someone." He drank. "There's a man the ledgers go to, up here. Surveyor. Name of Crane."

Quentin's hands went still on the wheel.

"I served under him once," Tomas said, to the river, to the past, to no one. "You'll want to know that before he finds you. Instead of after."

Ahead, the upper river bent north into hills that no chart Quentin owned had ever mapped correctly, and the water there, when the light hit it, ran the color of old iron.
