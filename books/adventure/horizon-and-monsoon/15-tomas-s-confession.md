## 15. Tomas's Confession

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the river lay flat as poured tin, holding the last of the stars in its skin. Tomas could not sleep in weather like this. Still water was worse than rough water. Rough water gave a man something to fight, and fighting kept the past on its side of the hull. But when the Marguerite sat becalmed in some narrow bend of the upper river, engine ticking as it cooled, the silence came up through the deck plates and into his bones, and in that silence he could hear everything: the oars, the dogs, the screaming that had no words in it.

He climbed out of his bunk quietly, out of habit, though there was no one left aboard who would wake at the sound of an old man moving. He took the coffee pot from the galley stove — cold, but he drank it anyway, because bitter was better than empty — and carried it up to the aft deck where the night still hung like wet cloth.

She was already there.

Sera Vane sat on the rail with her back against the stanchion, knees drawn up, a blanket around her shoulders that she had clearly taken from Quentin's bunk without asking, which Tomas approved of more than he could say. She was looking upstream, toward the falls they could not yet hear but both of them knew were coming, the way you know a storm is coming by the color of the light before it changes.

"You sleep like my father," she said, without turning. "Which is to say not at all."

"Your father slept fine." The words came out of him before he could weigh them, and once they were out he understood there was no taking them back, and no reason to try. "Elias Vane could sleep anywhere. On gravel. In a canoe full of fish guts. Slept like a man who'd made peace with whatever was chasing him." He lowered himself onto the hatch cover across from her, joints announcing every year of their acquaintance. "It's me who never learned."

"What chases you?"

Tomas looked at the coffee in his cup. It had gone the color of the river at flood stage, which was fitting, because it was the river at flood stage he needed to tell her about, or rather the night before it, the night the water ran red-brown with ash instead of silt.

"You've read your father's journal," he said. "You know about the removal crews."

"I know what Meridian did. I know what the deeds were."

"The deeds are paper. Paper is easy to hate, because paper doesn't have a face." He set the cup down on the deck between them, deliberately, the way a man lays down a knife before a conversation that might require one. "I have a face, Sera. I was on those crews."

* * *

She didn't move. He had expected anger, and part of him wanted it — wanted her to stand up and call him a name and go below, so that the worst thing that could happen would already have happened. But she only pulled the blanket tighter and said, "Tell me," in a voice that was her father's voice exactly, and that was somehow worse.

"There were four boats," he began. "That's how it started. Four flatboats out of Fort Amity, flying the Meridian pennant, loaded with fencing wire and lumber and forty men. I signed on for the wage, same as any fool. Eight dollars a day and meat twice, which in that year was a fortune to a man who'd been eating beans since spring. They told us we were going upriver to build a survey station. That was the first lie, and it was such a small one, Sera. That's the thing nobody tells you about lies. The big ones never come alone. They come dressed as small ones, and by the time you see the size of what you're carrying, you're already miles upriver with it."

The sky was going grey in the east now, the way it does over water first, a thinning of the dark rather than a dawning. A heron lifted off the far bank, unhurried, ancient, indifferent.

"The Riverkin landing was called Serai Ford. You won't find it on any chart Meridian ever printed, but it was older than any chart. Three hundred years, the elders claimed, maybe more. A ferry crossing where the river braided around a gravel bar, and stone weirs upstream that their grandfathers' grandfathers had built to raise the water for the terraces. There were maybe two hundred people living along that stretch. Houses of timber and thatch, drying racks heavy with fish, a longhouse for the winter stories. Children everywhere. That's what I remember most, God help me — how many children there were, running the banks like the banks belonged to them. Because they did."

His hands had begun to shake, and he let them. He had spent twenty years letting them shake in private, in bunks and outhouses and the dark behind engine rooms, and he was tired of privacy.

"We anchored below the ford the first evening and the crew chief — a man named Brack, big, soft-spoken, the dangerous kind of quiet — went up under a white rag to talk. He came back down and told us the elders had refused the Company's offer. Refused it politely, which I think angered him more than cursing would have. And then he told us the schedule had changed. We weren't building a survey station. We were clearing 'encumbrances.' His word. Encumbrances. Two hundred human beings, and the man said it the way you'd say driftwood."

Sera's breath had gone shallow. Tomas watched her hands whiten on her knees and kept talking, because stopping now would be mercy for him and none for her, and this night was not about him being forgiven. It was about him being known.

"They moved at three in the morning. That was Brack's design — catch people asleep, get them on the barges before the valley woke enough to organize. But the Riverkin weren't asleep. Or if they were, the dogs woke them, because the dogs started first, all along the bank, a sound I can still hear when the engine idles too long. By the time our boats grounded on the gravel bar there were lanterns everywhere, and men with clubs and a few with rifles — Brack's own picked men, not us hired hands, though don't mistake me, we were there, we laid hands on people, I laid hands on people — and the shouting started, and then the burning."

"The ferry landing," Sera said quietly. "My father wrote about the ferry landing."

"He would have seen it from the water." Tomas nodded slowly. "They fired it first. The landing, the drying racks, the longhouse. Kerosene and pitch-pine, and the wind was coming downriver that night, off the mountains, so the flames leaned into the village like the fire itself had been hired. I stood on the gravel bar holding a coil of rope I never used and watched a building older than any country burn, and inside my head there was a voice saying *walk away, walk into the river, swim, drown, anything*, and my legs stayed where they were. That's the truth of cowardice, girl. It isn't running. Running is honest. Cowardice is standing perfectly still with a coil of rope in your hands."

* * *

A fish rose somewhere mid-channel, a soft circular sound, and was gone. The east had gone the color of pewter. Tomas got up, refilled his cup from the pot, and found his legs would carry him back to the hatch cover, which surprised him a little; some nights they wouldn't.

"The barges filled fast," he went on. "Too fast. Brack had counted on two trips and the arithmetic of panic gave him three boatloads in the first hour — old people, mothers, men with their arms tied by their own neighbors because they'd tried to fight and couldn't be allowed to. And here is the part I have never said aloud to another living soul, and I need you to understand that before I say it."

Sera looked at him. Her eyes in the half-light were her father's eyes, and he made himself hold them.

"When they counted heads at the bar, the children didn't add up. Forty-one children on the manifests of the households — Brack kept manifests, the bureaucratic devil, he counted everything — and thirty-three on the barges. Eight missing. And then someone pointed across the channel, and we all looked, and there they were."

He stopped. Not for effect. Because the memory had a gate in it, and the gate only opened so far at a time, and he had to breathe against it the way you breathe against a cramp.

"On the far bank. Eight of them, the eldest maybe ten years old. They'd been sleeping at the fishing camp on the far side — the Riverkin sent the children over during the salmon runs sometimes, to learn the far-water techniques, it was a whole tradition, a whole tenderness — and they had woken to see their world on fire across the water, and they had run to the bank, and now they stood there in the lantern-light calling across the channel. Calling for their mothers. And their mothers were on our barges, tied hand and foot by regulation, screaming back at them in a language I didn't know and have been sorry every day since that I never learned."

The grey had become a pale gold along the eastern hills. Somewhere below decks, the Marguerite ticked and settled, faithful and oblivious.

"And the river," Tomas said. "That's the cruelty I can't get past. The river was high that week. Spring swell, snowmelt off the headwaters. No boatman alive would have crossed that channel at that flow with eight children in a loaded skiff — and we had no skiff anyway, and Brack wasn't going to delay the schedule for a rescue, because the schedule was the whole architecture of the lie. So the barges pushed off downstream with the parents screaming, and the children stayed on the far bank, and I want to tell you someone stayed with them. I want to tell you we left a man, food, a fire. We didn't. Brack said they'd be collected on the return run. The return run was nine days later, after we'd strung wire along the north terrace, and when we came back up—"

He drank the cold coffee. It did nothing. Nothing did anything; that was the discovery of his sixties, that nothing did anything, and you carried it anyway.

"—the bank was empty. That's all I know. Empty. Whether they walked out, whether some fisher family took them in, whether the river—" His voice failed on the shape of it, and he let it fail, and let the silence sit there between them like a third person. "I have told myself every version. Twenty years of telling myself versions. And the honest answer is that I chose not to know, because not knowing was lighter than knowing, and I have never in my life put down anything heavy if I could find a way to carry it crooked instead."

* * *

Sera uncurled at last. She stood, and for one lurching moment Tomas thought she was going to strike him, and he found he wanted it, found his cheek practically offering itself. Instead she walked to the rail and stood looking at the water, and when she spoke, her voice was strange and level, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"My father was there."

"Yes."

"Not as a crewman. As a witness. He wrote about it — I thought it was secondhand, something he'd heard later. It wasn't, was it. He saw it."

"He was moored above the ford that night with a load of survey gear. He came down in his canoe with a lantern and he shouted at Brack until Brack's men threatened to shoot him, and then he did the only other thing he could do. He poled across to the far bank." Tomas's mouth bent, not quite a smile, grief wearing a smile's clothes. "Your father spent that night on the children's side of the river, Sera. Whatever else Elias Vane was — the leaving, the years, all of it — that night he was on the right bank. He sat with those eight children till dawn and there wasn't a damned thing he could do for them either, and I watched him do it, and I have hated myself ever since for the difference between us. He crossed the water. I held a rope."

"That's why he never came home." She said it to the river, not to him. "That's why he couldn't. He knew what he'd seen and he knew no court would hear it, and he couldn't sit at my mother's table making small talk with that in his chest."

"Or he was a coward too," Tomas said. "We're a plentiful breed out here. Don't make saints of us. He ran from that valley the same as I did — I just ran slower, so I'm still running."

"No." She turned, and the early light caught her face, and Tomas saw that she was crying without any noise at all, tears simply present, like dew. "No. He sent the journal. He sent it to me. Twenty years late, but he aimed it true. And you—" she came toward him, and he flinched, and she ignored it, and crouched so that her eyes were level with his, an old man and a young woman on a steel deck while the river ran silver-gold beneath them, "you just aimed it too. Right now. At me. So don't tell me what you are, Tomas. I'll decide."

He bowed his head. Something broke in him then — not loudly, nothing so kind as loudness — a hairline fracture running through a wall he'd mortared brick by brick for two decades, and he felt the first cold draft of morning move through it.

"I can guide you past the checkpoints," he heard himself say, and was astonished, because he had not decided to say it; it had decided itself, out of the crack in the wall. "Above the falls. I know which channels Meridian watches and which they think no fool would try. I know the hand signals the patrol launches use to each other. I know where Crane's men water their boats and when the Stone Latch inspectors change shift. Twenty years of running supplies for the Company taught me the shape of its fist, and I am done pretending the knowledge weighs nothing."

"Why?" she asked. Simple, terrible. "Why help us?"

"Because absolution isn't given, girl. I asked God for it for years and God, whoever He is out here, had the good sense to stay silent. Absolution is built. Stone by stone, like a weir. And I don't deserve to build it, and that has never once mattered, because the river doesn't ask if you deserve the current. It just asks if you'll row."

She straightened. For a long moment she studied him the way her father must once have studied a seam of ore — testing, weighing, deciding what was real.

Then the sound came.

Faint, downstream, around the last bend — the low diesel throb of a launch running against the current at cruising speed, disciplined, unhurried, the sound of an organization that owned the river and knew it. Tomas was on his feet before his mind finished the thought, coffee cup forgotten, twenty years of instinct snapping taut like a hawser.

"That's not a fisherman's engine," he said. "And it's not patrol schedule — Stone Latch doesn't rotate for six hours yet." He looked at Sera, and the dread in his face was old and fluent. "Someone's coming aboard. Someone polite. Get the map case hidden, and whatever you do — when they ask you questions, and they will — don't look at me. I've lied to better men than the ones Crane sends. But I've never once lied with your father's daughter standing where I could see her face."

The launch rounded the bend into the gold morning light, grey hull, company pennant snapping, and began to close on the Marguerite.
