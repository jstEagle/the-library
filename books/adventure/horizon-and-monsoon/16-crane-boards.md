## 16. Crane Boards

The launch came up the channel at midmorning, white-hulled and unhurried, flying no colors at all — which was itself a kind of flag, because only men who owned everything they needed to own could afford to fly nothing. Quentin saw it first as a smudge against the haze above the falls, then as a shape, then as a fact: forty feet of polished mahogany and riveted steel, twin screws turning the brown water to cream, cutting a wake so clean it looked ironed.

He stood at the Marguerite's rail with a mug of chicory going cold in his hand and watched it come, and he did not like what he saw, and he did not let that show anywhere on his body.

They had made good time out of Stone Latch. The coffin was lashed under tarpaulin amidships where it had ridden since the checkpoint, bound for a widow's family up past the falls — or so the manifest said, and so Sera's map had ridden inside its false bottom for two days now, sleeping beside a dead man who had never existed. The trick had worked at Stone Latch. The inspectors had lifted the lid, frowned at the pine box and the sandbags dressed as a body, and waved them through with their gloves pinching their noses. Quentin had laughed about it that night, quietly, over cards. He wasn't laughing now.

Because the launch did not slow at the shoal line the way an honest boat would. It came straight across water that should have torn its running gear off, threading a channel that existed on no chart Quentin had ever bought or bribed his way into, and it did this without hesitation, which meant whoever was steering had either surveyed these narrows himself or was reading from someone who had. And there was only one surveyor on this river whose charts were better than Quentin's memory.

*Crane.*

The name arrived in his mind the way weather arrives — not as information but as pressure.

"Quentin." Sera came up from below, hair still damp from the basin, a wrench tucked through her belt loop out of habit rather than need. She followed his gaze downriver and went still in a way he was learning to read. Not afraid. *Bracing.* "That's—"

"I know."

"The launch from the checkpoint. It turned back after we—"

"I know, Sera." He set the mug down on the rail cap. "Go below. Take the journal with you."

"No."

He looked at her. She looked back, chin up, arms crossing, every line of her saying she had inherited her stubbornness honestly and would defend it to the death. Two weeks ago he'd have ordered her below and expected obedience, because he was captain and this was his deck. But two weeks ago she hadn't been crew. Somewhere between Port Calloway and the flooded gorge she had stopped being cargo and become something else, something he hadn't invited aboard and couldn't put ashore, and captains do not order crew below when the crew is right.

"Then stay behind me," he said. "And say nothing unless I ask you a question directly. Can you do that?"

She nodded once.

Below decks, Tomas was mending net — always mending net, the man's hands incapable of idleness, guilt needing occupation the way a fever needs a body. Odessa had gone ahead at dawn in her own shallow skiff, promising to meet them above the falls by the hidden channel, and Quentin felt her absence now like a missing tooth. The Riverkin pilot read water the way Crane read paper, and he would have given a month's earnings to have either of them standing beside him in the next ten minutes.

He had neither. He had himself, and the Marguerite, and whatever face he chose to wear.

The launch swung wide, throttled down with a sound like a man clearing his throat, and came alongside with a bump so gentle it might have been an apology. Three crewmen in Company grey took lines without being asked, and Quentin let them, because refusing small courtesies is how a man tells you he's frightened of large ones.

The man who stepped onto the Marguerite's deck was not what Quentin had expected, though he couldn't have said what he had expected instead. Aldous Crane was fifty-ish and narrow, built like a drafting instrument, in a linen suit that had been white once and was now the color of old bone. He wore round spectacles and carried a leather document case under one arm the way other men carry rifles — close, familiar, an extension of the arm. His hair was silvering at the temples in a way that looked arranged. When he smiled, which he did immediately and warmly, the smile reached his eyes without warming them, the way lamplight reaches the far corner of a room without heating it.

"Captain Ashgrove." He extended a hand. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated to exactly the pressure courtesy required and not a grain more. "Aldous Crane. Meridian Land and Survey. I hope you'll forgive the intrusion — your wake is famous, but I confess I've never had occasion to admire your vessel at rest."

"She's never at rest long," Quentin said. "Can I offer you coffee? It's chicory, I'm afraid. Real coffee doesn't come above the delta anymore."

"Chicory is honest," Crane said, as if this settled some philosophical question. "Yes, thank you."

Quentin poured two cups from the deck pot and handed one over. Sera stood by the wheelhouse door, silent, watchful, the wrench still through her belt. Crane glanced at her — one glance, brief and total, the glance of a man taking inventory — and returned his attention to Quentin with the smoothness of a card mechanic.

"You're heading up past the falls," Crane said. It was not a question, and he did not dress it as one.

"I'm heading wherever the river lets me," Quentin said. "After that, wherever my passengers have paid me to go. You know how it is. A boat's got to earn."

"A boat has indeed got to earn." Crane sipped the chicory, found it acceptable, sipped again. "And that's rather why I've come, Captain. To help you earn. Efficiently." He opened the document case, and Quentin watched his hands — watched them the way he watched a gambler's hands, a stranger's hands, any hands that might be holding something — and they produced only a single sheet of heavy paper, folded once, and laid it on the rail cap between them.

It was a bank draft. Quentin didn't touch it. He read the figure instead, twice, because the first reading seemed like a misprint.

"That's a great deal of money for a cup of chicory," he said.

"It isn't for the chicory." Crane removed his spectacles, breathed on each lens, polished them with a handkerchief that appeared from nowhere, and replaced them. All of it performed with the unhurried grace of a ritual he'd done ten thousand times, and Quentin understood suddenly that the whole performance — the launch, the draft, the spectacles — was choreography, rehearsed, deployed on every boat and in every office where a man needed to be made to feel that refusal was unthinkable. "It's for a packet of documents that came into circulation recently. Survey materials. Company property, taken by a thief — a dying thief, we believe, which is the saddest kind, because they take things out of spite rather than profit." He smiled again, apologetically. "You'll understand our position. An organization can't function if its records walk off. It would be like a ship's master letting his charts blow overboard."

"Would be," Quentin agreed. "Good thing I keep mine lashed down."

"Mm." Crane looked out at the river, at the falls' distant white sound, at the green hills closing in on both banks like a held breath. "Captain, may I speak plainly? I find plainness saves everyone such weariness."

"You may try."

"There is a coffin on your deck."

The words landed on the morning like a dropped anchor. Quentin kept his face where it was — mild, faintly bored, the face of a man hearing about weather. Inside, cold moved through him, quick and thorough, the way floodwater moves through a hull. *Stone Latch,* he thought. *Someone talked at Stone Latch. Or before. Or since.*

"Two coffins, actually," Quentin said. "Spare lumber in the second one. This far upriver you bury people fast and you bury them often, and a captain who can't build a box isn't worth his berth. The one under the tarp belongs to the Halloran family. Old man Halloran. Wanted to go home above the falls." He shook his head sadly. "You know how it is with the dead. They always want to travel."

"They do," Crane said softly. "More than the living, sometimes." He turned from the view and regarded Quentin fully, and for the first time the warmth drained entirely out of his manner, leaving something underneath that was not anger and not menace exactly but *attention* — the pure, patient attention of a man who surveys land for a living and sees, in every acre, what could be taken from it. "I'm not accusing you of anything, Captain. I want to be clear about that. Accusation requires evidence, and evidence requires effort, and effort is expensive. What I'm offering instead is simplicity. You give me the packet — today, here, no witnesses, no paperwork — and you take the draft, and you continue upriver, and you deliver your coffin, and you go on being the man who keeps moving and owes no one. That last part is your reputation, I understand. Never stops. Never belongs. It's a fine creed. Very clean. Very *navigable*." He used the word gently, almost tenderly. "I'd hate to see it snag on something."

Quentin drank his chicory. It had gone bitter and he drank it anyway, giving himself the three seconds the swallow cost him.

There it was, then. Not just the map — the whole architecture of him, named aloud by a stranger in a bone-colored suit: the creed he'd built his life on, held up and examined like a chart with a flaw in it. And the worst of it, the part that sat in him like a swallowed hook, was that Crane was right about the shape of the thing. Keep moving. Owe no one. It *was* navigable. It was designed to be. He had drafted it himself, years ago, in the aftermath of a night he did not think about, in ink he did not look at, and he had steered by it ever since, and it had kept him alive and alone and free, and free was the word he used for it, and had always used, and would use again tomorrow.

But Crane thought the creed was a weakness to be purchased. That was the surveyor's error. He read men the way he read land — as territory, as acreage, as something with a price per unit — and he had priced Quentin's freedom correctly and Quentin's heart not at all, because he could not see that the heart had changed its boundaries somewhere between Port Calloway and the flooded gorge, quietly, without filing papers, the way rivers do.

"Mr. Crane," Quentin said, "let me tell you what I know about packets."

"Please."

"I know that a man came downriver to me three months ago with a fever eating him alive and a story in his teeth. I know he paid me with a promise, which is the only currency a dying man has left. I know he died on my deck at dawn with the birds going crazy in the reeds, and I know what his last word was, and it wasn't *Meridian*." He set the empty cup down with a small, deliberate click. "Now. Maybe there's a packet involved in all that and maybe there isn't. But here's what I'll tell you for free, surveyor, since you came all this way. Whatever that man carried, he carried it out of those hills on his back for twenty years, through country that killed better men than him, and he carried it because somebody burned his partners in a warehouse and called it an accident. If I had such a packet — and I'm not saying I do — I wouldn't sell it to the men who lit the match. Not for that." He nodded at the draft without looking at it. "Not for ten of them."

Something flickered behind Crane's spectacles. Not surprise — Quentin doubted the man surprised easily — but recalculation. The look of a surveyor finding the ground less stable than his notes promised.

"You misunderstand the Company's aims," Crane said, and his voice remained pleasant, remained reasonable, and that pleasantness was now the most frightening thing on the deck. "We are not arsonists, Captain. We are builders. There is a difference, though history blurs it. The headwaters project will bring power to forty thousand people who currently burn dung and driftwood. It will bring roads, clinics, telegraph lines. The valley will be developed humanely. That phrase will follow me around for the rest of my life, I suspect, so I may as well begin practicing it." He picked up the draft, considered it, and slid it back into the document case with a sigh that sounded almost genuine. "But you're a moving man. Builders and movers rarely understand one another. It's a pity."

"It is," Quentin agreed.

Crane snapped the case shut. For a moment he simply stood there, and the morning stood with him, and the sound of the falls came thin and constant across the water like something whispering behind a door. Then he smiled his lamp-lit smile again, and offered his dry hand, and Quentin took it, because declining would have been a message too, and the message Quentin wanted to send was *unmoved.*

"Thank you for the chicory, Captain. Give my regards to Miss Vane."

The name went through the morning like a knife through wet canvas.

Quentin did not move. Did not blink. Somewhere behind his sternum his pulse tried to bolt and he shut the gate on it with a will born of thirty years of poker tables and customs houses and gunboat wakes. *He knows. Of course he knows. He's known since Stone Latch, maybe since Calloway. The coffin, the daughter, the dead man's name — he's been carrying the whole manifest in his head this entire conversation, offering money for a thing he already knows the location of, because the offer was never about the map.*

The offer was about watching Quentin's face when he heard the name. About measuring what the girl was worth to him. About learning whether the moving man had, without noticing it, acquired something he could no longer afford to lose.

"Miss Vane," Quentin repeated, flat as a shoal. "My passenger. Booked passage to the upper settlements. Good with an engine. Why?"

"No reason at all." Crane stepped back to the rail, where the grey crewmen waited with the lines. "An old man of my acquaintance knew a Vane, years ago. Prospector. It's a common enough name on this river." He paused, one foot on the rail, and looked back, and for an instant the lamplight in his eyes guttered and Quentin saw the actual flame beneath it — and the flame was afraid. Faintly, deeply, professionally afraid, the fear of a man whose signature was on documents that were beginning to surface like drowned bodies. "The river gives everything back eventually, Captain. That's the trouble with water. It has no respect for ownership."

Then he was over the rail and into the launch, and the lines came off, and the twin screws chewed the brown water, and the white hull drew away downriver with that same ironed wake, unhurried, unflagged, certain.

Quentin stood at the rail until the launch was a smudge and the smudge was haze. Sera came to stand beside him. She didn't ask. She had heard enough; the deck had carried every word.

"He knows about the coffin," she said finally.

"He knows about everything. The coffin was the point. He wanted me to know he knows." Quentin turned the empty chicory cup in his fingers. "The money was theater. The name was the play."

"Why tell us? Why show his hand at all?"

"Because a man who shows you his hand wants you to think about folding." He looked upriver, toward the falls, toward the hidden channels where Odessa was waiting, toward the green hills and whatever slept behind them. "Or he wants you thinking about your hand instead of his. Either way—" He stopped.

Either way, the engine had been idle for two days, and they had been at the checkpoint, and men in grey had come aboard with clipboards and oily rags and reasons to open every hatch.

The thought arrived whole, cold, and complete, the way the worst thoughts do.

"Sera," Quentin said quietly. "Get below. Check the fuel line. Now."

She was already moving.
