## 17. Dead Calm Above the Falls

The sound reached us before the falls did—a long, low grinding in the air, like the river clearing its throat. Sera stood at the rail of the *Marguerite* and felt it through her boots, a tremor that wasn't the engine's doing.

"Sound like thunder," Tomas said beside her, squinting upriver. "But it don't never stop."

"How far?"

"Half a day, maybe less with this current." He spat over the side, an old habit she'd begun to read as punctuation rather than contempt. "Falls are called the Widow's Teeth. Water goes over in three channels. Middle one's the killer—looks smooth as glass right up until it ain't."

Quentin emerged from the engine hatch with grease to his elbows and a look on his face that Sera had learned to distrust more than anger. Anger he showed you. This was something quieter, held close, like a coal carried in a bare hand.

"Engine's running rough," he said. "Not failing. Rough."

"You said that an hour ago," Sera said.

"I said it an hour ago because it was true an hour ago." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being gray sometime last year. "It's getting worse. I want to put in below the falls and have a proper look before we try anything stupid."

"We're not trying anything stupid?"

"Not until after I've looked."

Tomas laughed, short and dry. "Captain's idea of stupid has a wide border."

They tied off at a shelf of rock and gravel maybe two hundred yards below where the river began its long fall—the Widow's Teeth, white water braided across a quarter mile of broken stone, throwing up a mist that hung in the air like breath in winter. The pool below was deep and strangely still, ringed by hills so steep and green they seemed to lean in, listening. There was no landing here, no dock, no smoke from any chimney. Just the boat, the water, and the roar.

Sera helped Quentin haul the engine cover while Tomas kept watch with a rifle he claimed he barely remembered how to use, which Sera had decided was either modesty or a lie, and either way she preferred not to test it.

The engine bay smelled of hot metal and diesel and old rain. Quentin went in shoulder-deep, flashlight between his teeth, and for a long time said nothing at all. Sera crouched at the edge of the hatch, watching his boots and the small of his back and the way his breathing changed—not the breathing of a man working, but of a man reading.

Then he came out, slow, and sat on the deck plates, and looked at his hands.

"What?" she asked.

He didn't answer right away. He picked up the rag, folded it, unfolded it. "Fuel line's been cut. Not worn through—cut. Clean slice, three-quarters through, angled so it wouldn't fail all at once. It would seep for hours, run the engine lean, make it stumble, lose power exactly when—" He stopped.

"When we needed it most," Sera finished.

"Below the falls. In the narrows. Anywhere the river could finish the job the knife started." He looked up at her, and in the flat light under the hatch awning his face looked older, carved rather than lived-in. "This happened at Stone Latch. While the inspectors were walking my decks telling me what an honest man I seemed."

"The coffin," Sera said. "They searched everything else twice."

"They searched everything else twice because they wanted us to think they were searching for the map." He stood, joints cracking, and stared down into the engine bay like a man staring into a grave he'd been asked to dig himself. "They weren't. Or not only. They wanted to leave us a gift."

"So Crane knows exactly where we'll die."

"Crane knows exactly where we'll *stop*." Quentin's voice was very even, which she knew by now was worse than loud. "There's a difference. A dead prospector's daughter and a smuggler captain wrecked above the falls—that's an accident. That's the river's doing. Nobody has to write a report about the river."

The mist drifted over them, fine and cold, beading on the rails, on Sera's forearms, in her hair. She thought of her father's journal in its oilskin, wrapped now in waxed canvas inside a false bottom of the chart drawer, split from the deed copies Tomas had helped her hide in the coffin shipment that had gone on ahead—and felt the distance between here and there, between alive and drowned, measured in nothing but a sliced fuel line and a man's patience.

"How bad?" she asked. "To fix."

"I've got spare line. Splice is easy." He almost smiled. "The hard part is that whoever cut it knew my engine better than most men who've worked on it. Knew where the line runs, knew the angle, knew how long it would take to show symptoms. That's not an inspector with a knife. That's someone who's been aboard before."

"Tomas?"

"I've thought about it. Timing's wrong—he was on watch at the bow during the inspection, in front of God and everybody." Quentin rubbed the back of his neck. "Which leaves the other possibility, and I don't like it much."

"Which is?"

"That somebody paid one of my own crew when we laid over at Calloway. Or that Crane has a man who's been closer to this boat than either of us ever noticed."

Sera looked out at the water. The pool below the falls lay flat as hammered tin, holding the green of the hills upside down, and it struck her suddenly how much the calm resembled the surface of things—her father's silences, Quentin's charm, the Company's polite letters and lawful searches. Everything dangerous ran underneath.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon.

Quentin crawled back into the bay and Sera handed him tools, learning without being taught which wrench he meant by the shape of his hand when he reached. The splice took longer than he'd promised because the fitting threads were scored—someone had gone at them with a file, another small signature of malice—and he had to reseat the whole assembly, swearing in three languages she recognized and one she didn't.

Tomas walked the perimeter twice, then a third time, and came back with his jaw set wrong.

"There's a trail," he said. "Up the east bank. Old one, Riverkin-made by the look of the cuts, but somebody's used it lately. Fresh boot prints going down toward the water. Big man, heavy on the left heel."

"How fresh?" Quentin asked, not looking up from the engine.

"Rain last night would've softened anything older than this morning."

The three of them stood in a row at the rail, looking at the hills that leaned in and listened, and for the first time since Sera had boarded the *Marguerite* as a paying passenger instead of a stowaway of circumstance, the silence of the place felt less like emptiness and more like attention.

"He's watching us," she said.

"Or he's already gone to report." Quentin closed the hatch. "Either way, we're not moving tonight. I'm not taking a freshly spliced fuel line into the narrows in the dark to prove a point."

"So we sit here," Tomas said, "in a bowl, under a knife-line of sky, with a man on the hill counting our heads."

"Yes," Quentin said. "We sit here. And tomorrow, if the splice holds, we go up through the middle channel like idiots, or we find Odessa's people and ask for the hidden way. But tonight we eat, we sleep in shifts, and nobody stands alone at the rail looking romantic."

Sera almost laughed. Almost.

* * *

Night came down soft and enormous.

They anchored mid-pool, away from the bank, engines cold, one lantern burning low in the wheelhouse so the darkness around them stayed honest. The falls filled the whole world with their sound—you couldn't hear anything else, not footsteps, not voices at a distance, not even your own thoughts unless you shouted them inward. Sera found it both terrible and kind. Whatever was coming for them would come unseen; but whatever grief rose in her would also go unheard, and there was a strange mercy in that.

She found Quentin on the stern deck, sitting against the rail with a tin cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, looking up at a sky rinsed clean by the coming monsoon—stars sharp as nailheads, a thin moon like a paring of bone.

"Shift change isn't till midnight," he said, without turning.

"I know. I couldn't sleep." She sat down across from him, knees nearly touching. "The engine. You've fixed it before. Worse."

"Worse, yes." He turned the cup in his hands. "Fixed engines in war, in flood, in a typhoon once off the southern capes. Engines are honest. They break for reasons."

"But?"

"But I keep thinking about who held the knife." He finally looked at her. "You want to hear something ugly? For about an hour this afternoon, I suspected every soul on this boat, including you. Including myself—whether some part of me, some old coward part, cut that line myself so I'd have a reason to turn back. A reason that wasn't a choice. The river made me do it."

Sera studied him. "Did you?"

"No." He said it simply, and she believed him, and was surprised how much the believing cost her—how much she'd already invested in this hard, ironic, self-sufficient man whose creed was *keep moving, owe no one*, and who had somehow, without signing anything, become the keeper of her father's last promises. "But I thought it. And thinking it means I know how a man gets bought. It doesn't take evil. It takes tired. Somebody on this crew is tired, and scared, and Meridian counted on exactly that."

The falls roared on. Somewhere in the black hills, a bird cried out once, thin and high, and was swallowed.

"My father used to say the river forgives everything except stopping," Sera said. "I hated him for it. All those years he was gone, I built this picture of him choosing the water over me—over Mother—every single day. And then I open his packet and find out he was carrying a whole valley's worth of guilt around in his chest, and I don't know whether to grieve him or be furious at him, and mostly I do both at once, and it's exhausting."

"And now you're doing the same thing he did," Quentin said quietly. "Carrying it upriver."

"I'm not abandoning anyone."

"No. You're just finding out what it costs to stay with something." He set the cup down. "That's not a criticism. I'm the expert on leaving. Ask my brother."

She went still. "You've never mentioned a brother."

"No," Quentin agreed. "I haven't."

The mist moved through the lantern light like slow smoke. Below them the pool held the stars, and above them the Widow's Teeth ground their endless stone, and between the two sounds—the deep water and the falling water—Sera felt the boat hanging, the way she herself had hung her whole life between a father's absence and a mother's silence.

"His name was Merrick," Quentin said at last. "Younger by two years. We ran cargo together, back when I still thought a crew was a family you got to choose. There was a fever season, up the Sable Reach. He took sick at a trading post, and the post doctor said wait out the storm, and I—" His jaw worked. "I had a contract. Penalties for late delivery. I told myself the post had doctors, supplies, that he'd be safer ashore than bouncing downriver in a fever. I told myself a lot of sentences that started with *I*. I sailed. He was buried by the time I came back through, six weeks later."

"Oh," Sera said. It was all she had.

"After that I made the rule. Keep moving, owe no one. Because owing someone means staying, and staying means being there when the fever comes, and being there means you can fail." He laughed, without any humor in it at all. "You see the arithmetic? If I never belong anywhere, I can never fail anyone again. It's not a creed, Sera. It's a scar wearing a uniform."

The words hung in the wet air. She reached over and, before she could think better of it, put her hand over his where it rested on the deck plates—grease-creased, scarred, trembling very slightly, though she pretended not to notice and he pretended not to tremble.

"Merrick would be proud of you," she said. "Of this. Carrying a dying stranger upriver. Staying."

"Don't canonize me yet. I accepted only the delivery, remember. You bullied me into the rest."

"I did no such thing. I bought passage like a respectable salvage-yard woman."

"Like a pirate," he said, and there it was—the ghost of the charm, but warmer now, less armored, and underneath it something raw and new that neither of them named.

They sat until the moon slid behind the eastern ridge, saying little, the falls speaking for them both. And it was in that silence, near midnight, that Tomas came aft, quick and quiet, and crouched between them with his eyes on the dark hills.

"Lantern," he whispered. "East ridge. Two flashes, pause, one flash."

Quentin was on his feet before the words finished. "Signal code?"

"Company pattern. I've seen it before." Tomas's voice was flat, but his hands, Sera noticed, were not steady. "Somebody up there is talking to somebody downriver."

Quentin stared at the ridge where the light had died, and Sera watched him do the arithmetic she could see he hated: a sabotaged engine, a watcher on the trail, a signal passed in the night—and somewhere below, in the narrows or beyond, whatever that signal was calling for was already moving toward them.

"Splice holds?" she asked.

"It holds."

"Then we don't wait for whatever's coming." She heard her own voice come out level, her father's stubbornness and her mother's iron braided together, and realized she no longer knew where one ended and the other began. "First light, Captain. Take us up."

Quentin looked at her a long moment—at the oilskin-wrapped inheritance sleeping below their feet, at the falls grinding their patient stone, at the dark hills that had stopped listening and started reporting.

"First light," he said. "God help us, first light."
