## 18. Night Fishing, Night Talking

### Quentin

The engine room smelled of scorched copper and river rot, and Quentin Ashgrove had been kneeling in it for six hours when he finally admitted the truth to himself: whoever had done this had known exactly where to cut.

He sat back on his heels and wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth. The fuel line hadn't failed. It had been *helped* — a clean slice through the primary feed, hidden under a wrap of tarred canvas so neat it would have passed any casual inspection. The spare line in the locker was gone too. And the water pump's packing gland had been backed off half a turn, just enough to weep. Not sabotage meant to kill them. Sabotage meant to *strand* them. Someone wanted the Marguerite sitting helpless below the falls until the Company came down from the hills to collect what they'd been sent to collect.

Quentin climbed out into the evening air and stood on the afterdeck, flexing his stiff fingers. The gorge walls rose on either side of them like the inside of a throat, purple now with dusk, the great falls somewhere above thundering their constant sermon. Below, the river ran slow and deep and green, pooled up behind the falls' natural bar, holding its breath.

Sera was at the rail with a hand line, fishing without much hope. She'd strung a lantern over her shoulder and the light made a small gold island of her in all that gathering dark.

"Parts won't come," he said. "Not before Thursday, maybe not before Sunday. The launch that runs supplies from Stone Latch only comes twice a week."

She didn't turn around. "Then we wait."

"We wait." He said it like a sentence being handed down.

*Never stop,* his father had told him, back when his father still told him anything. *A boat that stops moving starts taking on the river's opinions.* Quentin had lived by it thirty years — owed no man, anchored nowhere, and here he was, beached by a knife stroke in a fuel line, waiting on a supply launch, caring whether a woman he'd known three weeks got her justice or got herself killed.

He picked up the second hand line leaning against the wheelhouse and baited the hook with a strip of salt pork.

"Move over," he said. "If we're going to sit still, we might as well eat."

* * *

### Sera

The fish came up silver and furious, smallmouth bass fat off the pool's quiet water, and by full dark they had four of them gutted and frying in the galley pan with whatever Quentin could find — wild garlic leaves, a heel of dried pepper, river salt. They ate on the deck because the cabin was too hot and too close, and because out here the falls filled the silence so neither of them had to.

Sera found she liked the way he ate: quickly, efficiently, but watching the water between bites, the way a man watches a thing he trusts and distrusts in equal measure.

"You've run this river your whole life," she said.

"Most of it."

"But you don't belong anywhere on it. I've watched you talk to people at every landing — they know you, they wave you in, they'd give you anything you asked. And you never stay past dawn."

He turned his tin plate slowly, chasing the last of the oil. The lantern hissed. Somewhere across the pool a night bird called once and got no answer.

"I had a brother," Quentin said.

Sera went very still. In three weeks of running rivers and dodging gunboats, he had given her nothing of himself but competence. She hadn't realized how hungry she'd been for the rest.

"Jules. Younger than me by two years. Our mother died when he was nine and our father was a boatman like me, always on the water, so Jules and I mostly raised each other on deck." He spoke evenly, the way men speak about things they've rehearsed. "When I was nineteen I took my first solo contract — hauling freight above the marshes, good money, six weeks. Jules wanted to come. He was fourteen and all elbows and questions, and I told him no. Told him the marsh run was no place for a boy. Told him I'd be back before the figs ripened and we'd go together next season, just the two of us, wherever he wanted."

"And?"

"And I stayed away eight weeks. Freight contracts stretch. A buyer in one port wants a favor, another port offers double, and every time you say yes you're saying *not yet* to everything you left behind." His jaw worked. "There was fever on our home landing that summer. It went through the waterfront in eleven days. By the time I got back, they'd buried him beside our mother in a churchyard I couldn't find again if you paid me."

The falls thundered. Sera set her plate down.

"He asked you to come along," she said quietly, "and you chose the horizon."

"I chose the money. Same thing, most days." He looked at her then, and there was nothing smooth in his face at all — none of the dockside charm he wore like oilskin against the rain. Just a tired man with an old wound he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for a spine. "I've been moving ever since. You tell yourself motion is freedom. Mostly it's just a way of never having to look at what you left. Because if you stop—" He stopped himself. Picked the plate back up. "Anyway. That's why I don't stay past dawn."

Sera thought of the packet in her seabag, oilskin-wrapped, carried two hundred miles against her will and then entirely by it. She thought of a fourteen-year-old boy asking to come along.

"My father used to promise summers," she said.

Quentin looked up.

"Every time he shipped out, it was *next summer, Sera-girl, next summer I'll take you up to the headwaters, show you where the river is born.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I must have heard it forty times. I had it memorized like a prayer. I'd pack a bag in June. Actual bags, actual clothes folded, sitting by the door. My mother would find them and unpack them without saying anything, year after year, until one year I stopped packing."

The lantern flame bent in a breath of wind off the falls.

"The last time I saw him I was twelve. He was on the levee with his kit over his shoulder and he crouched down and did the whole thing — *next summer, Sera-girl* — and I looked at him and I knew. Twelve years old and I knew it was a lie, and I let him believe I believed it anyway, because I didn't want him walking away knowing I'd stopped loving him. So I hugged him and I lied right back, and that was the last thing we ever gave each other. Two lies on a levee." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I stopped waiting that day. Not because I stopped hoping — hoping's not something you choose. I stopped *waiting*. There's a difference. Waiting means your life doesn't start until someone comes back. I decided mine already started, with or without him."

Quentin was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"And then a stranger dies on your dock and hands you his life in an oilskin packet."

"And I spent three weeks telling myself I was doing it for the map. For the valley. For justice." She stared into the firebox embers. "But the first night I opened that journal and read his handwriting — his actual handwriting, after twenty years — I sat in this wheelhouse and cried like the bag was still packed by the door. Like some part of me never stopped waiting after all, and it hated me for it."

* * *

### Quentin

He should have said something wise. He wasn't built for wise; he was built for reading water, and water at least tells you plainly where the rocks are.

So instead he said, "Show me the letter again."

She hesitated — it was hers, the one piece of Elias Vane that belonged to no one else — but she dug it out of the journal's back cover and handed it over, and Quentin read it by lantern light while the falls preached on.

*My Sera. If you are reading this then I ran out of summers. I know what I took from you and I know a letter cannot pay it back...*

It was a short letter. It didn't excuse itself. It named what he'd done — taken Meridian's coin, signed his name to surveys he knew were false, helped drive families off land that had held them for generations — and it named why he'd finally run the other way, and it asked for nothing except that she deliver the truth to the valley if he couldn't.

"He wrote it the night before the ambush," Sera said. "Tomas said he was up all hours. Ink on his fingers when they broke camp."

Quentin folded it carefully and gave it back. "You know what I keep thinking? Jules would have liked this. All of it — the gunboats, the coffin trick at Stone Latch, running the narrows with Company wake on our stern. He was fourteen and all elbows and questions. He'd have loved every minute and driven me mad doing it."

"That's not grief talking anymore," Sera said. "That's something else."

"It's the same thing wearing different clothes." He fed a stick into the firebox. "You spend enough years running from a grave, you start looking for it everywhere. Maybe that's what this is. Maybe I'm trying to save him by saving everyone else's fathers and daughters, and maybe that's a fool's arithmetic. But when I burned through those narrows with Crane's wake behind us, it was the first time in twenty years I wasn't running *from* something. I was running *toward* it. Toward you, if I'm honest, though God knows I didn't plan on honesty tonight."

Sera looked at him across the small fire, and the lantern light caught the gray coming in early at his temples, and the scar on his knuckles, and the eyes of a man who had mapped every mile of a river to avoid mapping himself.

"Next summer," she said softly.

"What?"

"That's what he always said. *Next summer.*" Her mouth curved, sad and fierce at once. "Well. This one's spoken for."

* * *

### Sera

They fished a little longer after the fire burned low, not because they needed fish but because the lines gave their hands something honest to do while the conversation went on in a lower register — about her mother, dead of the same stubbornness she'd inherited; about the ports Quentin loved best, ranked absurdly by the quality of their coffee; about whether a river could forgive what was done on its banks or simply carried it forever downstream.

It was near midnight when Sera noticed the light.

High on the eastern gorge wall, where the old Riverkin path switchbacked up through the scrub — the path Odessa had warned them no chart showed — a lantern blinked. Once. Twice. Then dark.

Then, unmistakably, three times.

"Not a signal anyone taught me," Quentin murmured, rising slowly, all the softness gone out of him and replaced by that coiled readiness she'd seen at Stone Latch. "Company signals run in pairs. Riverkin run in threes."

"Odessa?"

"Maybe. Or someone who knows her signals." He was already moving toward the wheelhouse, checking the darkness beyond the pool with a practiced sweep. "Either way, nobody climbs that path at midnight to admire the view. Get the journal and the deed copies somewhere deep. And Sera—"

She waited.

He glanced back at her, firelight and lantern light fighting over his face, and whatever he'd been about to say — some captain's order, some wall going back up — he set aside.

"Whatever comes down that path," he said, "you're not alone on this deck anymore. Neither am I."

Above them, on the black shoulder of the gorge, the lantern blinked three times again — closer now, descending — and the falls went on thundering like a heart that refused to be calmed.
