## 19. The Riverkin Ferryman

The falls announced themselves long before they could be seen. Quentin heard them first as a change in the river's voice — a low, continuous thunder rolling down through the gorge like the breathing of something enormous asleep beneath the water. Then came the mist, hanging above the treeline in a pale column that caught the late light and turned it to tarnished silver. The birds changed too: the herons and kingfishers that had kept pace with the Marguerite all morning peeled away, unwilling to go further, and the air filled instead with the cries of swifts riding the updraft off the cataract.

Quentin stood at the wheel with his hands easy on the spokes and let the sound work on him the way it had worked on him for thirty years. Every riverman knew the falls. Every chart marked them with the same blunt annotation — *unnavigable* — and every chart was right. Below them, the whole weight of the upper watershed gathered itself and went over a shelf of black basalt in three great curtains of water, and the spray rose two hundred feet and drifted for a mile downwind, raining gently on country that never saw a cloud.

He cut the engine to half and let the current carry them the last stretch, into the lee of a bluff where the water lay green and slow as bottle glass. Here the river widened into a drowned forest — dead trees standing to their shoulders in floodwater, their bark stripped white, their branches hung with grey curtains of moss that moved without wind. It was a place that looked abandoned even by abandonment. Sera came up from below with the coffee pot and poured two cups without being asked, and Tomas sat on the cargo hatch with his elbows on his knees, watching the dead trees slide past with an expression Quentin couldn't read and didn't try to.

"End of the line," Sera said. "By the charts."

"By any chart ever drawn," Quentin said. "The Company's surveyors came this far in '09, took one look at the falls, and turned around. Everything above is blank paper."

"Except it isn't," Sera said. "It's just unrecorded. There's a difference."

She said it quietly, but he felt the weight of it settle somewhere under his ribs. Above the falls there were people. There had always been people. The journal had made that much plain — the Riverkin, the terraces, the weirs, the ferry landing that Tomas had helped burn. And if the journal was right about anything else, then somewhere above that thundering wall of water was a valley full of elders who might or might not welcome the daughter of Elias Vane and the man who'd carried his map.

The problem, as ever, was not people. The problem was water.

"There has to be a way up," Sera said. "My father got up. He got down, too, at least once — enough times to father me and then leave." Her mouth did something bitter when she said *leave*, and Quentin watched her fold it away. "The journal mentions a portage. Carved steps. But a portage means hauling everything overland through country Meridian is already searching, and we'd be visible from the ridge lines for two days."

"A portage is a last resort," Quentin agreed. "A portage is what you do when you've run out of friends."

"And if we haven't run out of friends?"

He looked at her. She was watching the drowned forest with an expression he was beginning to know — the look she got when she was three moves ahead of him and waiting to see how long it took him to catch up.

"Tomas," she said, without turning her head. "You ran supplies for the removal crews. You knew these waters before the Company did. Who moved the Riverkin's boats?"

Tomas didn't answer right away. He turned his cup in his hands, and the steam curled up past his face, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and several he hadn't earned yet.

"The Riverkin moved their own boats," he said finally. "Always did. They had pilots who could read water no surveyor ever charted. Channels behind the falls — side channels, flood channels, channels that only run in high water, channels that only run in low. The Company maps show rock. The Riverkin maps show time." He paused. "There was a woman. Odessa. She piloted the supply barges through the narrows when I ran the landing. She could take a loaded barge through water that would fold a gunboat in half. If anyone's still up there who remembers the old channels—"

"She's still up there," Sera said. "Someone has to be. People don't stop being from a place just because someone burned the dock."

* * *

They found the first sign of her an hour later, and they found it because Quentin almost put the Marguerite's bow through it.

He'd been working the boat up the drowned forest's eastern edge, feeling along the bluff for any break in the rock, when the depth line came back shallow — far too shallow, four feet where the chart promised twenty — and he threw the wheel over hard and backed water, heart hammering, and only when the boat swung did he see what he'd nearly hit.

A stake. Driven into the riverbed, old and dark with years, its top carved — not marked, *carved* — with a spiral pattern worn nearly smooth. And beyond it, half-hidden by the overhang of the bluff, a gap in the rock face barely wider than the Marguerite herself, curtained by hanging moss so thick it looked like solid stone until you were close enough to see the black water sliding through it without a ripple.

"That's not natural," Sera said, coming to stand beside him at the rail.

"It's maintained," Quentin said. "Look at the edges. Those rocks have been cut. Someone quarried that channel open." He stared at it the way he'd stare at a door in a cliff face. "Thirty years on this river and I never knew this existed."

"Because you never needed to," Sera said. "And neither did the Company. That's rather the point, isn't it? A road you can't see is worth more than a fortress."

Tomas had come forward, and when he saw the carved stake he went very still, and then he took off his hat, which was more than Quentin had ever seen him do for a living person.

"That's a pilot's mark," he said. "Riverkin pilots staked their channels. Each family had their own spiral. You'd know whose water you were entering by the carving." His voice had gone rough. "I haven't seen one of those stakes in twenty-two years."

"Whose spiral is it?" Sera asked.

"I don't know. They were all different. But—" He stopped. Squinted. "There's a second mark. Under the spiral. Look — a fish, and a hook through it. That was Odessa's. She told me once, drunk on palm wine at the landing, that her family's sign was the hooked fish, because her grandmother used to say the river gives you what you hook yourself." He put his hat back on slowly. "That's her stake. Or her mother's. Or—"

The moss curtain across the channel entrance moved.

Not with the wind — there wasn't any wind down here in the lee of the bluff. It moved the way a curtain moves when someone behind it has decided to let you see, and it swung aside on a rope, and the channel beyond opened up into the rock like the throat of the river itself: forty feet of black, glass-still water running between walls of wet stone, roofed overhead by interlocking branches, lit from above in broken coins of gold.

And standing in a small wooden skiff at the mouth of it, one hand on a sweep oar, was a woman.

She was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered and deep-chested, with skin like wet driftwood and hair gone entirely to iron-grey, braided tight against her skull. She wore a man's oilskin coat cut down and patched, and she held the sweep with the casual authority of someone holding a limb she'd been born with. She looked at the Marguerite — at the hull, at the wheelhouse, at the three of them standing at the rail — with an expression of such complete, unhurried assessment that Quentin felt, absurdly, like a boy caught poling a stolen skiff.

Then her eyes found Tomas, and everything in her face closed like a fist.

"You," she said. Her voice carried across the still water without effort. "Tomas Reyes. Still walking around with your lungs full of air, I see."

"Odessa." Tomas's voice cracked on the name. He stepped to the rail. "I don't—"

"No," she said. "You don't. Whatever you were going to say, save it. I've heard it. Everyone who helped them has said it, sooner or later, usually after the third drink. 'I didn't know.' 'I thought it was freight.' 'They said it was legal.'" She spat over the side of her skiff, delicately, with terrible precision. "The water remembers what you helped carry, Tomas. So do I."

Sera stepped forward before Tomas could say another word, and Quentin watched her do it — watched her plant both hands on the rail and meet the old woman's stare across ten feet of black water, and understood that whatever happened next would happen between these two, and that he was witness, not participant.

"My name is Sera Vane," she said. "Elias Vane was my father."

Something moved behind Odessa's eyes — not softening; nothing so simple. Recalculation. The stare sharpened, weighed, and held.

"Elias Vane." She said the name the way you'd handle a knife you weren't sure was clean. "The prospector. The one who walked out of the valley twenty-two years ago and never walked back. The one whose partners are buried up there under cairns we built, because nobody else would touch them." She let that sit. "And you're what he left instead of himself."

"He's dead," Sera said. "Three weeks ago, on a dock in Calloway. He sent me his journal. And the deeds — the forged ones. The ones Meridian used to take this valley." Her voice didn't rise, but it gathered, the way water gathers before it goes over a ledge. "We're taking them to whoever still claims this river. Which I'm beginning to understand is you."

Odessa was silent for a long moment. The swifts cried overhead. Somewhere behind her, deep in the channel, water dripped from stone with the regularity of a clock.

"You want passage," she said at last. Not a question.

"We need passage," Quentin said, and stepped up beside Sera. "Above the falls. Through whatever water you'll trust us to. My boat draws four feet, my engine's sound, and I've run rivers since before you lost that valley — but I won't pretend I know yours. No one does. That's why we're asking, not demanding."

Her gaze swung to him, and he weathered it the way he'd learned to weather a falling barometer: steadily, without flinching, giving back nothing he couldn't afford to lose.

"An honest captain," she said. "That's rarer than an honest river. And what do you get out of it, Captain? Passage costs. Nothing on this river is free — least of all what I'm selling."

"What I get," Quentin said, "is to finish a job I promised a dying man I'd start. What I *want*—" he hesitated, and the honesty cost him visibly, "—what I want is to not be hunted anymore. Meridian's marked us. Gunboats below, Crane above. Your channel is the only door they can't follow us through, because they don't know it exists. That's selfish enough for you?"

For the first time, something that was almost humor moved at the corner of Odessa's mouth. It vanished quickly, but it had been there.

"It's a start." She shipped her oar and let the skiff drift a foot closer. "But hear me, all of you, because I'll only say this once. This channel is not a favor. It's a grave. My grandmother cut it open with fire and hammer over eleven winters, so that when the soldiers came — and they came, twice, before your Company ever signed its name — the valley could be evacuated by water while the men held the landing. Every bend in it has a name. Every pool holds somebody's bones or somebody's wedding offerings. When I take you through it, I am not showing you a shortcut. I am opening a vein."

She looked at each of them in turn — Sera, Quentin, and last, longest, at Tomas, who bore it without looking away, which Quentin granted him was something.

"So here is my price. Not coin. Coin I can get from the Company, and cheaper than my dignity." She raised one finger. "Swear it. Swear that whatever happens — whether the judge rules for us or against us, whether Crane burns the valley or the monsoon takes it — the source stays unowned. No deed, no claim, no fence, no toll. Not Meridian's, not yours, not the crown's, not the church's. The valley belongs to itself, the way the rain belongs to itself. Swear that, and I'll take you up through water no chart shows. Break that oath, and the river will collect. We have ways of collecting. We've had twenty-two years to practice."

The silence stretched. Water dripped. A swift screamed somewhere in the gold light above.

Quentin found that his mouth was dry. It wasn't the size of the oath that frightened him — oaths were cheap, he'd sworn a hundred and meant maybe ten. It was that for the first time in thirty years, he wanted to mean one. And wanting to mean a thing, he had learned young, was how you got hurt.

He thought of his brother. He thought of the rule he'd built out of that grief — keep moving, owe no one, belong to nothing — and he thought of how thoroughly the last month had dismantled it, plank by plank, until here he stood at the mouth of a hidden channel being asked to belong to something larger than a boat.

Sera spoke first.

"I swear it," she said simply. "On my father's name, which I'm only now learning how to carry. The valley stays unowned."

Tomas swore next, hoarsely, and Odessa received it with a nod that gave back nothing.

Then they all looked at Quentin.

He gripped the rail. The black water slid past below him, patient as a debt collector, and the mist off the falls drifted down through the trees like something breathing.

"I'm a riverman," he said. "I don't own water. Nobody owns water — that's the first thing the river teaches you and the last thing men forget. So yes. I swear it. The source stays unowned. Not mine, not theirs, not anyone's." He paused, and heard himself add, quietly, "And I'll burn any map that says different."

Odessa studied him for a long moment, and whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it, because she nodded once, sharply, like a lock seating home.

"Then come," she said, and turned her skiff into the channel. "Keep to my wake exactly. Where I go left, you go left. Where I stop, you stop. And Captain—" she glanced back, iron eyes glinting under the branches, "—when I tell you the water ahead doesn't exist, believe me. It exists. It just doesn't exist for anyone who hasn't been invited."

The moss curtain fell shut behind the Marguerite like a held breath released, and the black water carried them forward into the throat of the river, toward the falls, toward the valley, toward everything that waited above.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, Quentin thought he heard engines — far downstream, many of them, running hard against the current.

Crane had found the river.
