## 20. Portage of Ghosts

The path began where the river ended.

That was how Odessa described it, standing at the base of the falls with her arms crossed against the spray, watching the water fall in a white curtain that seemed to go down forever and come up as mist. "Above the falls there is no river for boats," she said. "There is only the path. My grandmother carried freight on it when the steamboats still came. She said the path remembers everyone who walks it."

Sera had thought that was poetry. Standing at the foot of it now, a switchbacking thread stitched into the cliff face, she understood it was inventory.

They had stripped the *Marguerite* to her bones. Quentin had been ruthless about it — the engine's carburetor wrapped in oilcloth, the tools, the spare fuel in five-gallon tins, the food, the ammunition he didn't talk about but counted twice, the journal and the deed copies sealed in the tin case that rode against Sera's ribs on a strap cut from an old belting leather. Everything else stayed aboard, lashed under tarps, guarded by nothing but the boat's own stubbornness and Odessa's cousin, a Riverkin boy named Pell who looked fifteen and handled a winch like he'd been born to one.

"Two days up," Odessa said. "Three if the rain comes early. We sleep in the shelters. You carry what you can and you leave what you can't." Her eyes went down the line of them — Quentin, Sera, Tomas, two of Odessa's kin whose names Sera was still learning — and settled, without any particular malice, on Tomas. "Leave what you can't."

Tomas said nothing. He'd said very little since the night of his confession, and what little he said came out sideways, like a man walking past a dog he'd once kicked.

They went up.

* * *

The first hour was stone and breath. The path climbed through a notch in the cliff where the rock had been cut — not eroded, Sera realized, staring at the regular faces of the blocks, but *cut*, fitted, the seams so tight that moss grew across them like skin over an old scar. Steps rose ahead of her, each one dished in the center by generations of feet, and she put her boots where ten thousand other boots had worn the stone smooth and thought: nobody told me people built stairs here. Nobody told me anybody lived above the falls at all.

The maps hadn't shown it. Of course the maps hadn't shown it. The maps were Meridian's.

By midmorning the gorge had closed around them into green shadow. Water ran everywhere — not the river below but smaller voices, seeps and springs threading down the cliff face, feeding ferns that hung from cracks like green lace. The air smelled of wet granite and crushed leaves and something sweeter underneath, some flowering vine she couldn't see. Her shoulders burned under the pack straps. The carburetor, split between her load and Quentin's, weighed like a sin.

Quentin climbed ahead of her, setting a pace that was almost gentle by his standards. She'd watched him read the path the way he read water — three steps ahead, always, his eyes flicking to handholds and loose rock, adjusting the line of the whole caravan with small words: *left of the root, mind the slab, drink now while we're in shade.* He never stopped moving. That was the thing about Quentin Ashgrove she'd understood first and understood least: motion wasn't his habit, it was his architecture. Take away the going and she suspected there'd be very little man left standing.

She was beginning to want to test that theory, which frightened her more than the gunboats had.

"Rest at the bend," Odessa called back, and the word moved down the line like a ripple.

The bend opened onto a shelf of flat rock above the falls' roar, wide enough for all of them, shaded by a fig tree growing straight out of a crack no wider than Sera's hand. Someone had built a low wall along the outer edge — fitted stones again, waist-high, a railing older than any railing had a right to be. Beyond it the gorge dropped away and the river showed itself as a single braided ribbon of brown and white, impossibly far down.

Sera set down her pack and her legs nearly refused to hold the letting-go. She sat against the wall and drank and let the spray-cool air move over her face, and that was when she saw the names.

They were cut into the top course of the wall. Not scratched — cut, with chisels, by hands that knew stone, each name in its own small panel, ranked along the rail like passengers at a railhead waiting for a boat that had stopped coming years ago.

*MARU. KESTI. ODILE FERRY-DAUGHTER. THE WEIR-BUILDERS OF THE THIRD TERRACE. JONAH PELL.*

Dozens of them. Hundreds, when she stood and followed the wall around the bend. Some panels held single names; some held clusters, families; one long stretch held nothing but dates, decades of them, and beside each date a mark she didn't understand until Odessa came and stood beside her and touched one.

"Flood marks," Odessa said. "High water, every year, cut by whoever kept this wall. My grandmother's mother kept it last. See —" her finger moved to the final date, and beneath it, cut shallower, hurried, a single word in the same alphabet but a different hand: *GONE.*

"What happened here?" Sera asked, though the journal had already told her. Reading a thing and standing inside it were different countries.

"The Company came with papers," Odessa said. Her voice was level in the way water is level right before it goes over a drop. "Papers saying the valley belonged to them. Papers saying my people had never been here at all. You cannot argue with paper if no one will look at your stones." She tapped the wall. "So they wrote on the stones anyway. So the stones would remember what the judges would not."

Sera ran her fingers along the panels. The cuts were clean, deliberate, made by people who expected the wall to outlast the lie. She thought of her father's journal in its tin case against her ribs, the careful draftsman's lettering, the same stubbornness in a different medium. He had learned this somewhere. He had learned to write for people who weren't listening yet.

"Your father walked this path," Odessa said quietly, behind her.

Sera turned. Odessa was looking not at her but up the trail, at the next flight of steps climbing into shadow.

"There are more walls above. And a shelter with a lintel the young men used to sign, when they finished their first season of weir work. If Elias Vane worked the river here — and your journal says he did, before he was anyone's prospector — then his name is on one of them."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the path gave up its dead slowly, the way a river gives up drowned things — one at a time, when it chooses.

A second wall at another bend, its flood-marks ending in the same year, the same shallow *GONE*. A flight of steps where the center of each tread was polished glass-smooth and the edges still held chisel-tooth. A terrace, half-swallowed by jungle, where stone channels still funneled spring water into a basin someone had carved with a spout shaped like a bird's open beak; Sera filled her flask there and the water tasted cold and mineral and old.

And shelters. That was the word Odessa used, and it was the right one — not ruins, though ruin had gotten its hands into everything, but shelters: low stone buildings roofed once in split cedar, now open to the sky, their doorways tall enough for a loaded carrier to pass through without stooping. In one, soot still blackened the ceiling corner above a hearth, and someone had left, on a shelf cut into the wall, a clay lamp with a wick burned down to nothing, waiting all these years for a flame that hadn't come back.

Sera stood in the doorway of that one a long time. Through the empty roof she could see evening coming down into the gorge, the light going amber, the first stars pricking through above the falls' perpetual cloud of mist. Somewhere below, faint as memory, the river went on talking to itself.

Her father had slept in rooms like this. Before Port Calloway, before the salvage yards and the silence and the twenty years of nothing, before she'd built herself a father out of absence and anger because an absent man could be anything and she'd chosen to make him a coward — before all that, he had been a young man who carried loads up these stairs and signed his name on a lintel and slept under a roof his neighbors raised with him.

She had spent her whole life knowing what Elias Vane took from her. It had not once occurred to her to ask what the world had taken from him first.

"Tomas says we camp at the next shelter," Quentin said from behind her. He'd come up quiet, the way he did, and he stood looking at the hearth with an expression she couldn't file — something older than curiosity. "He knows these paths better than he lets on."

"I know," Sera said.

"It bothers me."

"I know that too." She turned to face him. In the blue dusk his face was all angles and watchfulness, a man perpetually braced for the river to turn on him. "But he's carrying more than his share and he hasn't complained once, and when we passed the second wall he stopped and stood there like a man at a grave. Whatever Tomas owes this place, he's paying it in sweat. That's more than most people ever pay."

Quentin was silent. Below them, someone struck a flint and a cookfire bloomed, small and orange against the darkening stone.

"You sound like you're defending him," he said finally.

"I sound like I've spent twenty years deciding what people are without asking them." She shouldered past him gently, out into the evening. "Come on, Captain. Your carburetor won't carry itself."

* * *

The lintel shelter sat in a fold of the hill where two springs met, and the elders' path crossed a paved courtyard gone to moss, and there — Odessa stopped at the doorway and simply pointed, and stepped aside, and let Sera walk to it alone.

The lintel was a single beam of dark hardwood, and the names on it were younger than the wall's, cut in a looser hand, crowded, overlapping, generations of finished apprentices reaching up on their toes to leave their proof. Moss had taken some. Rain had softened others to shadows. But near the left end, at shoulder height, where the wood had been sheltered by the eave's last surviving plank, a name stood clear:

*ELIAS VANE.*

Not Vane the prospector. Not Vane the thief of survey documents, the Company's wanted man, the fevered stranger who died on a levee at dawn whispering a girl's name. Under the name, in the same confident strokes, a mark: a stylized weir, three lines and a curve, the same mark repeated beside half the names on the beam. Weir-builder. Third terrace, maybe, or fourth. A young man with stone dust on his hands and a place among these people and a future here, before whatever happened happened, before exile or flight or grief walked him down off this mountain and out of every story Sera had ever been told.

She put her fingers on the letters. The wood was rain-cold and hard as iron.

"He cut deep," Odessa said softly, beside her now. "See how the others of that season have weathered faster? He wanted it to last. Or he was angry when he cut it. Sometimes those are the same stroke."

Sera laughed — a broken, wet sound she didn't recognize as hers until it was already out. "He couldn't even write me a letter that said what he meant. Twenty years, and the only honest sentence in the whole packet was my name at the bottom. But this—" she pressed her palm flat against the carving, "—this he meant."

Below the courtyard, the cookfire smoked. Quentin sat apart from it on a fallen block, cleaning the carburetor parts with a rag and a patience he showed to machines and nothing else alive. Tomas was hauling water. The mist off the falls had risen with the night until the whole gorge breathed it, and the stars swam overhead like fish in cloudy water.

Sera stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then she took out her knife — her father's knife, actually, salvaged from his kit, the one possession of his she'd kept without admitting she was keeping it — and she knelt, and beneath *ELIAS VANE,* in small letters that shook only a little, she cut:

*HIS DAUGHTER CAME BACK.*

It wasn't true yet. The valley was still a day above them, the Company still between them and justice, the monsoon still gathering somewhere over the sea like a debt coming due. But stone was patient. Stone could wait for the truth to catch up.

Odessa read it upside down without touching it. Something moved through her face — not warmth exactly, but the loosening of a knot held a long time. "My grandmother said the path remembers everyone who walks it," she said. "Now it remembers you."

* * *

Sera woke once in the night, in the shelter with the others, firelight banked to coals, and heard voices low by the courtyard wall. Quentin and Odessa, planning tomorrow's climb. And a third voice, lower, rougher — Tomas.

She should have gone back to sleep. Instead she lay still and listened, and caught only fragments through the mist and the mutter of coals: *...the launch... ...they'll be at the weir by now... ...if Crane's offering passage, it's because...*

Then silence, and footsteps going different directions, and the coals settling.

She stared at the shelter's empty roof, at the stars swimming there, and felt the tin case against her ribs and her father's name on the lintel behind her in the dark, and understood with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with the mountain air that the hardest part of the path might not be the climbing at all.

In the morning, she decided, she would tell Quentin what she'd heard.

If morning came before the weir did.
