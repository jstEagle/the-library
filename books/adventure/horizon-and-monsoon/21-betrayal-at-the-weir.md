## 21. Betrayal at the Weir

The weir came into view an hour past dawn, a long gray seam of stone laid across the river by hands that had been dust for two generations. Water spilled over it in a lace curtain, and the mist off the fall hung in the gorge like breath held too long. Quentin cut the Marguerite's throttle to half and felt the deck settle beneath him, the old boat easing the way a horse eases when it smells home—or trouble. He'd learned long ago that those two smells were often the same.

"Slow water past the weir," Odessa said from beside him at the wheel, her voice low. She had not slept; he could see it in the way she held her jaw, as if her teeth were holding words prisoner. "We can shoot the spillway on the left channel if we draw less than four feet. Your boat draws four and a half."

"Then we portage the heavy gear around the right bank and run her light," Quentin said.

Odessa's eyes moved to him, dark and unhurried. "Or we stop. Take on water, rest the crew. The weir pool is deep and calm. My people used to camp there."

"We don't stop," Quentin said. It was not a creed he announced usually; it was simply the shape of his life, like a scar. But saying it aloud now, with Sera somewhere below sorting papers and Tomas coiling line on the foredeck, it sounded less like wisdom than like fear wearing its work clothes.

"You stopped for my father's map," Odessa said quietly. "You stopped for the valley."

"I stopped because the river made me." He didn't look at her. "There's a difference."

* * *

They took the right bank anyway, warping the Marguerite through the slow eddy behind the weir's abutment, lines out to the old mooring stones—Riverkin stones, Odessa said, drilled and ringed before any Company charter existed. The work went easy. Too easy, some part of Quentin noted, the part of him that had survived thirty years on this river by distrusting calm water and calm mornings alike.

Sera came up on deck with the map case slung across her body, the oilskin gleaming dully under her jacket. She'd taken to carrying it that way since Stone Latch, sleeping with it, eating with it, as if it were a child she couldn't set down without it crying. Her hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth Tomas had given her at the last village landing—a small kindness, Quentin had thought at the time. He thought about it again now and couldn't say why the memory snagged.

"Tomas says there's a trader track over the ridge from here," she said. "Two days to Fort Amity if we cut across instead of running down through the narrows."

"Tomas says a lot of things."

"He carried our water up from the weir pool yesterday. Hauled stone at the camp. He hasn't complained once."

"No," Quentin agreed. "He hasn't."

He watched the old deckhand then, across the length of the deck. Tomas was fifty feet away, bent over the anchor winch, oiling it with the thoroughness of a man who loved machinery or was bidding it farewell. His hands moved with the certainty of long habit. Forty years on the river, he'd told them. Ran everything from log rafts to mail boats. And for three of those years, Quentin now knew, he had run supplies for Meridian's removal crews—kerosene, chain, barbed wire—up to the landings where the Riverkin waited to be driven off their own ground.

Tomas looked up and caught him watching. The old man smiled, gap-toothed, unbothered, and raised his oil can in a small salute.

Quentin raised two fingers back and turned to Sera. "How many copies did you make?"

"Three sets. Journal pages, deed copies, the letter. One set stays with me. One went into the coffin at Stone Latch—that one's gone upriver ahead of us, God help whoever opens it. And one—" she patted the case against her ribs "—is here."

"And the original map?"

She hesitated. Just a fraction. A hitch in the breath, a flicker of the eyes toward the cabin door and back. Quentin had spent a lifetime reading passengers' faces for lies about cargo and customs fees; he knew what a fraction of hesitation weighed.

"The original is where it needs to be," she said.

* * *

They ate a cold lunch on deck because Odessa wouldn't allow cooking smoke above the treeline. Rice and dried fish and the last of the guava jam, eaten sitting on the hatch covers with their backs against the pilot house. The weir murmured its white noise beyond the rail. Somewhere upslope a bird called three notes and got no answer.

It was Tomas who broke the quiet, as it so often was. "Captain. There's a thing I should show you. On the far side of the weir—the old ferry landing. Stones there with markings. Odessa's people's writing. Might matter, for your judge."

Odessa stiffened almost imperceptibly beside Quentin. "The landing is watched," she said. "It has been watched since the burning."

"It's watched by nobody now but ghosts," Tomas said easily. "I crossed there forty years, girl. I know every stone. Captain wants evidence for his judge, yes? Evidence sits at that landing. Half a morning's walk."

Quentin looked at Sera. Sera looked at the map case. And something in Quentin's chest—the animal that had kept him alive through shoals and gunboats and Company men with polite voices—lay its ears back.

"Show me on the chart first," he said.

Tomas spread the chart on the hatch cover without hesitation, pointing out the landing, the trail, the ridge line. His finger was steady. His eyes were steady. Everything about him was steady, and that was exactly what was wrong. Quentin had known frightened men and honest men and men carrying guilt like ballast, and Tomas had been all three at different hours of this voyage. But this morning he was none of them. This morning he was a man performing steadiness, and Quentin had seen that performance once before—from a Meridian inspector at Stone Latch who'd been told exactly which questions to ask.

"Tomorrow," Quentin said. "We'll walk it tomorrow, after I've checked the starboard shaft bearing. Today we stay aboard."

Something crossed Tomas's face then, quick as a fish under a boat. Disappointment? Relief? Quentin couldn't name it, and that frightened him more than either.

* * *

Night came down soft and rainless, the last dry night, maybe, for a season. They anchored in the weir pool with two lines ashore and a watch rotation Quentin set himself: Odessa till midnight, himself till dawn. Sera rolled into her blanket in the pilot house, the map case buckled to her wrist with a lanyard she'd rigged herself.

Quentin sat at the rail with the river talking to itself below him and let himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding.

If someone wanted the documents, tonight was the night. The valley camp was a day behind them. Crane's gunboat was a day ahead, somewhere below the falls, blocked from following them up through the gorge channels only Odessa knew. But a launch—small, shallow, quiet—could have been waiting at the weir for days. Could have been waiting since Stone Latch. Since the moment someone aboard told it where they'd be.

He didn't want it to be Tomas. That was the truth of it, plain and ugly. The old man hauled stone and oiled winches and gave Sera red cloth for her hair and told stories about the river when it ran free, and Quentin had let himself believe that guilt, honestly carried, could be trusted. That a man ashamed enough would never sell you twice.

But shame was a currency too. And Meridian paid in more than money.

Near midnight, Odessa touched his shoulder and went below. The pool lay black and still. Across the water, the far bank was a wall of darker black, and beyond it the ridge stood against the stars.

He heard the click at 2:40 by his pocket watch. A single metallic sound from the foredeck, small as a beetle in a wall. Then darkness bloomed at the tip of the point downstream—a lantern, uncovered for three seconds, hooded again. Three seconds. Long enough.

Quentin didn't move. He sat very still at the rail and felt the hook go in—not surprise, because some part of him had known since the chart that morning, but grief, cold and specific, the grief of being right about a man you'd wanted to be wrong about.

He waited until the stars wheeled another hand's width. Then he went below, tapped twice on the pilot house door, and woke Sera with a hand gentle on her shoulder and a voice pitched below the water's murmur.

"They're coming," he said. "Get the real case."

* * *

She didn't ask how he knew. That was the thing he'd remember afterward—she didn't waste a single question on it. She just unbuckled the lanyard, slid the case from under her jacket, and set it on the chart table between them, and in the lamplight her face was pale and set and older than her years.

"How long have you known?" she asked.

"Since the chart this morning. Knowing isn't proof. Proof walked out on deck ten minutes ago and lit a lantern."

"Tomas." She said the name the way you press on a bruise to see if it still hurts. It hurt. He could see it hurt. "He carried our water. He told me about the children on the far bank. He cried, Quentin. Men don't fake that."

"Men fake everything," Quentin said. "It's the one trade older than boating." He opened the case while he talked, fast fingers on the brass catches. "Here's what happens now. They'll come aboard quiet, sometime before dawn, thinking we're asleep. They'll take the case off your wrist or out of the pilot house—they'll know where it is, because he told them. We're going to let them find it."

Sera stared at him. "Let them—"

"The case they get will hold paper. Good paper. I've been copying journal pages in my own hand since Port Calloway, and the deed copies are close enough to fool a man reading by lantern light in a hurry. The dates are wrong in three places. The names are wrong in two. If Crane reads carefully, he'll know. If he reads like a man who just won, he'll believe it—and he'll act on it." Quentin looked up. "Which way does the false route send him?"

Understanding moved across her face like weather. "The decoy journal says the deed originals were never burned in the annex fire. Says Vane mailed a set to a safe deposit in Fort Amity under a false name." She exhaled slowly. "You've been building this since the records office burned."

"I've been building it since Company men boarded my boat and asked polite questions," Quentin said. "I lie for a living, Sera. It was time the lying worked for us." He buckled the case shut and held it out to her. "Wear it. Sleep with it on your wrist like always. When they come, you fight them for it—hard, but not too hard. Let them take it. And whatever you hear, whatever you feel, you do not call out my name."

Her hand closed around the case. For a moment she was trembling—he felt it through the oilskin—and then the trembling stopped, deliberately, the way a river stops at a weir before it goes over.

"He cried," she said again, quieter. Not arguing. Mourning.

"Yes," Quentin said. "And then he lit a lantern. Both things can be true. That's what makes betrayal what it is—if he were only a monster, it wouldn't cost anything." He touched her shoulder once, brief as a stamp on a document. "Now sleep, or pretend to. Dawn's going to need us both awake."

* * *

They came at 4:10, in that hour when even the river seems to sleep. Quentin heard them the way he heard everything—by absence: the absence of the night bird, the absence of current-sound off the point where a hull could slide without wake. Two boats, maybe three, paddles wrapped in cloth. He lay in his bunk in the dark and counted heartbeats and did not reach for the shotgun under the floorboards, because the whole design of the night depended on no shots.

The grapnel came over the stern rail soft as a fishing line. Boots on deck—soft-soled, three pairs, four. A whisper in a voice he didn't know. Then the pilot house door, eased open on hinges Sera had oiled two days ago, which now betrayed them with silence.

He heard her wake. Heard the sharp intake, the scramble, the crack of her elbow against the chart table—real pain, or good acting, or both. Heard a man's low voice say, "Just the case, miss, and nobody's hurt," and heard Sera answer with a scream she strangled down to a snarl, and heard the scuffle—her boots skidding on boards, the lanyard snapping, the case torn free. Then the rush of bodies going over the rail, the splash of paddles resuming, fast now, urgent, and gone.

Gone downriver, toward the narrows, toward Crane, carrying thirty pages of Quentin Ashgrove's best handwriting and three wrong dates.

From his bunk, Quentin listened to Sera breathing hard in the pilot house. He listened to the river resume its ordinary voice. And he listened, longer than he wanted to, for the sound of Tomas—whether the old man had gone over the rail with the raiders or slipped away down the bank or was standing somewhere in the dark aboard, having done the worst thing of his life and found it lighter than he feared.

What he heard, after a long time, was the creak of the foredeck hatch. Footsteps, hesitant, stopping outside the pilot house. A long silence. Then footsteps retreating, uneven, an old man's walk suddenly old, down the gangway plank, onto the bank, into the trees toward the old ferry landing.

Going to meet his payment, Quentin thought. Or going to stand where the children once stood on the far bank, and find out what kind of man the lantern made him.

He rose, lit the lamp, and found Sera sitting on the chart table's edge, rubbing her elbow, her eyes bright and dry and furious.

"He took it," she said.

"He took exactly what we gave him." Quentin poured coffee from the ever-warm pot and put a cup in her hands. "By tomorrow night, Crane will have read his prize. And if he believes it, he'll turn his whole operation toward Fort Amity and a safe deposit box that doesn't exist—which buys us the days we need to reach the judge with the real papers. And if he doesn't believe it—" he drank, grimacing at the chicory burn "—then he'll know we know, and he'll come at us angry, and we'll be glad of Odessa's channels and a head start."

Sera looked at the doorway through which Tomas had gone, and her jaw tightened.

"When this is over," she said, "I want to know why. I want him to tell me why the water-hauling wasn't enough. Why the stone-hauling wasn't enough."

"Some debts a man doesn't think he can pay," Quentin said. "So he keeps paying the wrong ones." He set down his cup. "Get your real case. Wake Odessa. We're running the spillway at first light, light boat, no stops."

Outside, the east had begun to gray above the weir, and the mist off the fall was turning the color of pewter. Somewhere downriver, a launch was hurrying a lie into Aldous Crane's hands, and the monsoon was nineteen days out and closing like a door.

Quentin stood at the wheel and rang the engine bell softly, twice, and the Marguerite woke beneath him, faithful as ever, asking nothing except which way, and how fast, and for how long.

For the first time in thirty years, he knew the answers to all three.
