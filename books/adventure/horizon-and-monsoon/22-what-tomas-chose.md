## 22. What Tomas Chose

The lantern sat in Tomas's hands like a bird with a broken wing.

It was an old thing, older than him — brass gone green at the seams, glass smoked amber from forty years of oil and rain. He had carried it on his first run for the Company, back when he still told himself he was only ferrying crates. He had carried it the night the Riverkin landing burned, though he'd kept it shuttered then, hidden under his coat like a sin too heavy to lift. And tonight, standing on the shingle below the weir with the pre-monsoon wind worrying his collar, he had turned it toward the water and given Crane what Crane wanted: three long flashes, two short, the signal they'd agreed on in a smoke-filled office that smelled of ink and money.

*The map is here. Come take it.*

They had come. They had taken it. And now Tomas stood among men who were not surveyors, not inspectors, not anything so clean as thieves, and he understood — slowly, the way cold water finds its way through a boot seam — exactly what he had done.

* * *

Crane's launch had come up the narrows running dark, engine muffled, the way boats moved when they did not wish to be remembered passing. Six men came ashore. Not grey coats — plain canvas jackets, river-muddied, faces that gave nothing back when you looked at them. Tomas knew the type. He had ferried their kind before, years ago, up to the checkpoints above Stone Latch, and he had learned never to ask their names because men without names could do things names would have to answer for.

The decoy case had been where Sera Vane wanted it found — lashed under the Marguerite's stern bench, wrapped in oilskin, fat with paper. One of the nameless men cut it free with a knife that made no sound against the cord. Another held a lantern low over the deck planks while a third went through the boat's stores with quick, practiced hands, lifting tins, checking compartments, running fingers along seams the way a doctor runs them along a rib.

Tomas watched from the shingle, and his heart performed a strange arithmetic. *Let them find nothing,* he prayed, and was ashamed of how much of the prayer was for himself.

Aldous Crane came last, stepping off the launch onto the wet stones with the delicacy of a man who disliked rivers touching him. He was thinner than his reputation, Tomas thought. The Company men spoke of Crane the way sailors spoke of weather — as a force, impersonal and vast. In person he was a narrow man in a dry coat, spectacles beaded with spray, holding a document case against his chest like a hymnal.

He opened the recovered case right there on the stones, by lantern light, gloved fingers moving through the papers inside with terrible patience.

Tomas watched his face.

He watched it go from hunger to satisfaction to something else — something slower, colder, working its way up behind the eyes like silt behind a dam. Because the papers were good. That was the devil of it. Sera Vane had built her decoy well: old charts, copied pages, a journal fragment in Elias Vane's cramped hand, even a convincing forgery of a land deed aged with tea and river damp. It looked real. It looked *exactly* real enough.

And Aldous Crane, who had signed the fire permit at the records annex, who had spent twenty years making false things true through sheer administrative will, looked at those papers and knew within a minute that they were lies.

"Mr. Ferreira." Crane said it quietly, without looking up. He pronounced the whole name, all of it, the way no one had since Tomas's mother died. "Come here, please."

* * *

The wind came down the gorge in long breaths, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't started yet but was gathering itself somewhere over the high country, patient as a debt. Below the weir the water ran brown and fast, swollen already with the first melt off the shoulders of the mountains, and somewhere upstream — Tomas could feel it rather than hear it — the Marguerite lay dark against the bank, engines cold, waiting out the hours until Odessa's channel opened.

Waiting to see if the lantern would tell them the truth.

"Twenty-two years," Crane said. He closed the case. He did not raise his voice; Tomas understood that this was worse than shouting. "You ran supplies for the removal crews in '61. You carried the payroll to the checkpoint men at Stone Latch for nine years after that. You have been on the Company's books longer than some of my surveyors have been alive." He finally looked at Tomas, and his eyes behind the wet spectacles were pale and exact, like instruments. "So you will understand why I am confused."

Tomas said nothing. His mouth had gone dry as August mud.

"The papers in this case are fabrications." Crane held one up between two fingers — the forged deed, its tea-stained edges fluttering in the wind. "Good ones. Recent ones. Which means the woman knew we were coming. Which means someone told her we were coming." A pause, thin as a knife laid flat. "Or someone helped her arrange what we should find."

"I don't know anything about—"

"You signaled us." Crane's voice stayed gentle, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing on the whole dark river. "Three long, two short. Your signal, Mr. Ferreira. Given by your hand, with your lantern. So either you betrayed us, or you betrayed them, and you are about to tell me which, and you are going to tell me in a way I can believe."

One of the nameless men drifted closer. Not threateningly. Just closer, the way water rises around a man standing on a shoal.

And there it was — the moment, the hinge, the door swinging on its rusted pin. Tomas felt it open in his chest, and everything he had spent thirty years keeping shut behind it began to swing out into the wind.

Because he had done this before. Not the lantern, not the betrayal — but the standing-aside. The looking-away. He had stood on the far bank in '61 with his engine idling and watched the Riverkin landing burn, and he had told himself the Company men were only enforcing lawful orders, and he had told himself the families crowding onto the east shore would be compensated, resettled, made whole somehow, someday. He had watched children on the far bank calling across the water to parents who were being held back by men with rifles, and he had put his wheel over and steered away, because the current was wrong for crossing and the Company's schedule was tight and he had a wife in Port Calloway and debts of his own.

He had been telling himself stories for thirty years. Stories were how men like him survived. Stories were the ballast that kept a guilty hull upright.

But tonight, standing on these stones with Crane's quiet voice in his ears, Tomas finally heard the story for what it was. Because the Company had displaced people before. Displaced was a word with paperwork behind it, trucks and rations and resettlement ledgers, ugly but *accountable*, ugly but survivable. What he saw in Crane's face now, in the stillness of the nameless men, in the way none of them had asked a single question about witnesses —

That was not displacement.

That was erasure.

* * *

"Where is the rest of it?" Crane asked. "Where are the originals?"

"I told you everything I know," Tomas said. "The case was under the bench. That's what I signaled. That's all."

"Yes," Crane said softly. "You see, that's precisely the problem."

He turned away and spoke to the tallest of the nameless men, low and unhurried, and though Tomas caught only fragments, the fragments were enough. *No loose ends.* Enough. *The gorge above the falls — the flood channel — bodies come down the river every monsoon anyway.* Enough. *The pilot woman, the girl, the captain — after the gorge, if the river doesn't—*

Enough.

Tomas's knees went strange. The world tilted a degree or two and righted itself. He thought of the Marguerite lying dark upstream, Quentin Ashgrove asleep or half-asleep in the pilothouse with his hat over his eyes, Sera Vane curled in the cabin with her father's true journal against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Odessa somewhere in the hills, knowing channels no chart showed. All of them believing the worst was past, believing the decoy had bought them time.

Believing Tomas was theirs.

He looked at the lantern in his hands. Three long, two short had meant *come and take it*. But a signal code is just a language, and every language can lie in both directions. There was another pattern — an old one, a riverman's one, older than the Company, older than the checkpoints. One flash, long, then darkness, then one flash again. *Danger. Turn back. Do not come.*

Every pilot on this river knew it. Every pilot, from here to the delta, had learned it at their mother's knee or their first master's elbow. It was the one signal no Company man had ever bothered to learn, because it was not a signal for offices. It was a signal for people who loved the river more than they feared what rode upon it.

Crane was still talking to his men. The tall one was nodding. Somewhere above the weir, thunder cleared its throat in the high country, a long rolling syllable, the monsoon rehearsing.

Tomas's hands were shaking. He noticed this with a strange detachment, as if the hands belonged to a younger man, the man he might have been if he had trembled like this in '61 and let the trembling win. The brass was cold. The wick was low. He would need to turn the key, feed it oil, cup the flame with his body against the wind.

Any of the six men could see him do it. Any of them could read a raised lantern as easily as a raised gun.

Unless he didn't raise it.

Unless he set it down on the stones, casually, the way a tired old deckhand sets down a tool at the end of a shift. Unless he walked a few steps along the shingle, muttering, stretching his bad knee, positioning himself — a screen of his own thin body between the lantern and the launch, between the flame and the men who watched. Unless he knelt, fumbled, cursed softly at the wick, played the fool one last time, the doddering old supply-runner, the harmless traitor who had already given them everything he had.

And beneath the shadow of his own stooped shoulders, turned the key, and gave the river its truth.

* * *

"Mr. Ferreira." Crane had turned back. "Before we conclude our business. The woman — the daughter. Where does she keep the journal on her person? Think carefully. If you truly know nothing further, say so, and you'll be released at Fort Amity with the others. If you're withholding—"

"I'm not withholding anything." And God forgive him, it wasn't even a lie anymore, because in the moment he decided, he had stopped belonging to them. Whatever he knew, he knew it for the other side now. "I'm an old man who made a bad signal. That's all I am."

Crane studied him for a long moment. Rain touched Tomas's cheek — the first of it, fine as sifted flour, the vanguard of the season. Behind Crane, the tall man said something Tomas didn't catch, and Crane's mouth thinned.

"Keep him with us," Crane said. "If the river gives us the boat, we may need a pilot who knows Ashgrove's habits." He paused at the edge of the shingle, one foot on the launch's gunwale, and looked back, and for a single unguarded instant Tomas saw what lived underneath the surveyor's calm: fear. A small, sharp, human fear, the fear of a man who has gambled a career and a signature on a flooded gorge in monsoon season and knows, in the place where his instruments cannot reach, that the river does not honor deeds.

Then it was gone, filed away behind the spectacles, and Crane stepped down into the launch.

They put Tomas amidships, between two of the nameless men, and the engine woke with a low murmur and swung the bow upstream. The rain thickened. The dark came down like a lid.

And Tomas, wedged between strangers with his wrists loose and his heart hammering out a signal of its own, began to work the problem that would decide whether he died as the man who burned a landing or the man who saved a boat.

The lantern sat beside him on the thwart. They hadn't taken it. Why would they? It was Company property now, evidence, a trophy of a successful operation. It sat there in the rain with its wick drowned and its brass going cold, and Tomas let his hand rest on it, lightly, the way a man rests a hand on a sleeping dog.

Upstream, around the first bend, the gorge waited — black walls, white water, the flooded throat of the whole river. Odessa meant to take the Marguerite through it at dawn, through channels no chart showed, ahead of the swell. Crane meant to follow. Crane meant to follow *through*, and the nameless men meant to make sure that whatever came out the bottom of the gorge came out without witnesses.

Tomas closed his eyes. He saw the far bank in '61. He saw the children calling across the water. He saw his own hands on his own wheel, steady, obedient, damned.

"No," he said aloud, very quietly, into the rain. "Not again. Not this time. Not them."

The man to his left stirred. "Something?"

"Just praying," Tomas said. "Old habit."

The man grunted and looked away, upstream, toward the bend and the blackness beyond it.

Tomas's thumb found the lantern's key. He began, slowly, patiently, with the tenderness of a man tuning an instrument he had neglected for thirty years, to work the wick dry. He had one chance, maybe two, before the gorge swallowed them all. When the launch rounded the bend and the Marguerite's sentry — and Odessa would post a sentry, she was too careful not to — saw a light flicker on these dark waters, it would read it one way or the other.

Company signal: three long, two short. *We have it. Come.*

Riverman's signal: one long flash, darkness, one long again. *Danger. Turn back. Run.*

His thumb rested on the key. The rain fell harder. Around the bend, faint through the drumming dark, he could just make out the shape of the high ground where the Marguerite lay hidden, and his old heart broke and healed itself in the same beat, the way it had always done, the way it would do tonight or not at all.

The launch leaned into the current. The gorge opened its black throat ahead.

Tomas turned the key.
