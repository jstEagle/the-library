## 23. Running the Flooded Gorge

The river told us before the sky did.

I'd been listening to it all morning from the engine room hatch — a sound like gravel shifting in a giant's fist, deep under the usual chatter of current. Old-timers called it the river clearing its throat. I called it what it was: the monsoon's advance guard, days of rain fallen on high country we couldn't see, coming down to meet us in the one place on the whole river where there was no room for either of us to be wrong.

"The Gorge won't hold this water long," Odessa said. She stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes, the way she did everything — as if touching things hard was a habit she'd outgrown. "By tomorrow it'll be a chute with a body count. Today it's only mostly lethal."

"Mostly," I said. "That's my favorite kind."

She didn't smile. Riverkin didn't waste smiles on jokes made to keep hands steady. She just looked at me with those flat dark eyes that had read every channel above the falls without a chart, and said, "You run the valves. I run the boat. You do not touch the wheel, Quentin Ashgrove, no matter what you see. Swear it."

"I don't swear much."

"You swore the valley stays unowned. You can swear this too." A pause, and something almost gentle moved through her voice. "A man who has never been humbled by water is a man who hasn't met enough of it yet. The Gorge will introduce you properly. Let me do the talking."

Sera stood at the rail with the oilskin case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, her father's journal inside it, wrapped in waxed canvas against spray that hadn't even started yet. Tomas was forward with a boathook he looked like he didn't trust himself to hold. The Marguerite sat low in the water, riding heavy with coal and nerve.

I went below and put my hands on the valve wheels I'd been turning since I was fifteen years old, when my father first let me feed steam to a boat half this size, and I waited for the river to make its argument.

* * *

You hear the Gorge before you see it. The walls begin as hills, then bluffs, then cliffs of black shale closing in until the sky becomes a ribbon overhead and the whole world narrows to wet stone and white noise. The channel between the falls and the low country runs four miles through rock that the river has been arguing with since before there were men to name it, and in ordinary season it's a hard run — standing waves at the Squeeze, a midstream fang called the Deacon's Tooth that has eaten more hulls than rust ever will.

This was not ordinary season.

The water was up nine feet by Odessa's marks — she knew them the way a woman knows the scars on her own hands — and nine feet on the Gorge doesn't mean nine feet deeper. It means faster. It means the eddies behind the rocks fill and turn and become whirlpools with shoulders. It means the standing waves at the Squeeze stand ten feet tall and lean back over you like a door about to close.

"Valves!" Odessa shouted down the speaking tube, and her voice came through flattened by wind and roar. "Half ahead — no more! She'll want to run, but running is dying down here!"

I gave her half. The Marguerite leaned into the current and the current leaned back, and the deck began to tremble the way a horse trembles when it smells something it can't name.

Through the engine room's open grating I could see slices of the walls going by — black stone streaked with old flood lines, terraces cut by hands I now knew were Riverkin, stairways worn into the rock where ferrymen had hauled boats for two hundred years. Ghost stairs. They were underwater now, all of them, and that fact sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. This river had been a road once, a lived-in road, and we were running it like thieves through a burning house.

"Tooth!" Odessa's voice. "Hard to starboard — give me everything you have for ten seconds, then take it ALL away!"

I spun the throttle wheel full over. The Marguerite surged, groaned, swung her bow left of the Deacon's Tooth — a fin of shale standing clear of the flood like a shark's dorsal — and then Odessa screamed *kill it* and I slammed the valves shut and the boat went dead in the water, drifting sideways toward the fang with the current shoving her stern around like a hand moving furniture.

That's the trick nobody who hasn't run white water believes: sometimes the only way past a rock is to stop fighting the river entirely and let it carry you, spinning, trusting that the spin ends where the pilot says it ends. Odessa had said it would end. I watched the Tooth slide past our hull close enough that I could have spat on it, close enough to see the pale scuff-marks of other boats' paint ground into the stone like chalk ghosts, and then the current finished its turn and set us true and I fed the steam back in by inches.

My hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the effort of not doing anything.

"Humbled yet?" the tube crackled.

"Ask me at the Squeeze."

* * *

The Squeeze is where the gorge pinches to less than half its width and the whole river has to fit through a gap it hates. In low water it's a chute with a standing wave train you can thread if your nerves are soldered to your spine. In flood, the wave train becomes a single monster — a hydraulic backwash where the water piles against the downstream constriction, curls, and swallows itself, over and over, a hole in the river big enough to drown a barge. Boats that go into the hole nose-down and never come up right side out.

Odessa brought us to the head of it and held us there, steering into the current just fast enough to stand still, hovering above our own deaths while she read the water.

I came up on deck for that. I couldn't watch from below. Some things a man should see coming.

The hole was alive. That's the only word for it. Ten feet of green water standing on end, folding backward on itself with a sound like continuous thunder, and along its flanks the boils turned slow circles, and foam lay on the surface downstream in long gray rafts like the river's shed skin.

"There," Odessa said, pointing not at the hole but at its right shoulder, where a tongue of smoother water ran along the cliff base. "The seam. Two boat-widths wide, maybe less today. We go down the seam, quartering across the face of the wave, and we do not stop rowing the wheel for anything. If we broach—" she shrugged, and the shrug said everything the word *broach* means in a place like this.

"If we broach," Sera said quietly, "the journal drowns with us."

"No," Odessa said. "If we broach, the journal drowns with us, but the valley doesn't. Remember that. It makes the dying easier." And then, to me: "Full ahead on my mark. Not before. Ashgrove — your boat answers you better than any I've steered. But she answers me better still, because I know what she's afraid of. Mark—"

The Marguerite went in.

I have run rapids since I could walk. I have shot the Narrows at Calloway in spring melt, crossed bars in fog, taken a loaded coal barge down a flooded cutoff with the water over the towhead. None of it was this. The seam took us and the world became noise and cold and a single line of green water that Odessa held us on with corrections so small and so constant they were less like steering than like prayer — a hundred small mercies a second. The wave rose off our port beam, taller than the wheelhouse, its crest feathering and curling, close enough that spray came over the rail in solid sheets and Sera and I clung to the same stanchion like children. The hull groaned. Somewhere below me a seam in the planking spoke up, a thin whistle of complaint, and I felt it in my teeth because I knew every voice that boat owned.

Then the wave leaned.

It leaned over us the way a door leans when someone big puts their shoulder to it, and for one full second the Marguerite was in shadow — actual shadow, cast by standing water — and I heard Odessa say, very calmly, in her own language, something short and rhythmic, and I understood later that it was a ferryman's verse, older than the Company, older than the deeds, older than the theft. A verse for asking permission.

The wave changed its mind.

It broke behind us instead of on top of us, and the shockwave of it lifted our stern like a hand under the keel and threw us down the seam and out into the boil, spinning, shipping water, alive. I was on the valves before I knew I'd moved, giving her steam to answer the spin, and Odessa was already correcting, already reading the next hundred yards of chaos, and neither of us said a word because there were no words that weren't wasted breath.

We came out of the Gorge four miles later into widening water and gray light, both banks softening back into forest, and the roar falling away behind us like a crowd losing interest. The Marguerite limped. I could feel it in the engine's note — a new rattle, high and wrong, probably a cracked stay or a sprung bearing from the pounding. But she floated. She moved. She answered.

Odessa stepped back from the wheel and let go of the spokes one finger at a time, and I saw that her hands were bleeding where the wood had bitten them, and that she was smiling — the first smile I'd seen on her, small and fierce and gone again in a moment.

"Now you've met enough of it," she said.

I looked at my own hands on the throttle wheel. Steady now. They hadn't been steady once in four miles, and the boat had survived anyway, because steadiness had never been the thing keeping us alive. Something else had. Something that sounded like an old verse in a drowned language, spoken by a woman whose people had run this water for generations before anyone thought to chart it.

All my life I'd believed mastery was the creed: know your boat, know your river, owe nothing, need nothing, and nothing can take you by surprise. Standing there dripping on my own deck, I understood that I'd survived the Gorge precisely by surrendering the wheel — by trusting a knowledge that wasn't mine, carried by a woman whose people the maps had erased. Mastery and humility, fused in white water, like two metals hammered into one blade. I didn't feel diminished by it. I felt, for the first time in twenty years, honest.

Sera appeared beside me with a coil of rope over her shoulder and her hair plastered flat, and she looked at me looking at Odessa, and she said, "You're different."

"I'm wet."

"You're different," she said again, and went forward to check the bow line, and I let it stand, because she was right.

* * *

We tied up that evening in a slot canyon Odessa knew — a side slough behind a curtain of hanging vine, invisible from the main channel, a hiding place her family had used during the removal years when Meridian patrols worked the river. While Tomas and I rigged a canvas over the engine hatch and I got a lamp on the new rattle, Odessa climbed the slough wall and came back down with news in her stride before she had words for it.

"Smoke," she said. "Downriver, around the bend. Cook smoke, not campfire. And engine oil." Her eyes found mine. "Somebody's building a fire they expect to cook on for days. Nobody camps below the Gorge in flood season unless they're waiting for something."

"Waiting for the water to drop," Sera said slowly. "Or waiting for us to come back down. Or—" she stopped.

"Or they've got a launch that can handle the swell and they're coming up behind us," I finished. My hands, steady all afternoon, wanted to shake again, and I let them, alone, inside the engine room where nobody could see. "Crane wouldn't risk the Gorge."

"Crane hires men who don't know enough to be afraid of it," Odessa said. "Flooded, it kills the ignorant first. But it lets some through. The river's fair that way."

I killed the lamp and stood in the dark listening to the Marguerite breathe — the tick of cooling metal, the slap of water against a hull that had come through the worst water on the river by inches and mercy. Four miles of white chaos had taught me in an afternoon what twenty years of moving had refused to teach me: that I couldn't do this alone, that I no longer wanted to, and that the boat I loved was carrying something heavier than coal now — a dead man's truth, a living woman's fury, a valley's claim older than any deed Crane's company could forge or burn.

And then, out of the dusk downriver, thin and distorted by the canyon walls, came a sound that froze every one of us where we stood: the flat metallic bark of a hail-speaker, a Company loudhailer, calling across the water in a voice polished smooth as courtroom oak.

"*Vessel in the slough. This is Aldous Crane, surveyor for the Meridian Company. Captain Ashgrove — I know you can hear me. We should talk.*"

Odessa looked at me in the dark. Sera's hand found the oilskin case at her chest.

"He followed us through the Gorge," I said. "God help us — he followed us through."
