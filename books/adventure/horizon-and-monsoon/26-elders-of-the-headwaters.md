## 26. Elders of the Headwaters

Odessa had not stood on the old ferry landing in eleven years, and the boards remembered her feet.

That was the thing about Riverkin wood — her grandmother had said it a hundred times, planing a hull or mending a weir gate: *wood keeps what water teaches it.* The planks beneath her sandals were silvered and split, furred with moss at the seams, but they still held the faint give of a landing built to flex with the river rather than fight it. Her feet found the worn hollows without looking. Eleven years, and her body still knew this place the way a tongue knows a lost tooth.

She had come up from the hidden channel that morning with word ahead of the strangers, as courtesy demanded. Now she stood at the landward end of the landing with the elders arrayed behind her, and she watched two figures descend the carved stairs through the drizzle, and she felt the whole weight of what she had done settle onto her shoulders like wet rope.

*You vouched for them,* she reminded herself. *Before you knew if they were worth the vouching.*

The tall one moved like a boatman — weight low, eyes on the footing, hands loose and ready. Quentin Ashgrove. She'd taken his measure on the water already, in the dead calm below the falls, when his engine lay open like a gutted fish and he'd let a stranger pilot him through channels no chart admitted existed. He hadn't flinched at the oath. He hadn't tried to bargain it down, either, which was more than she could say for most men who wanted something from the headwaters.

The woman was harder to read. Sera Vane came down the stairs with her chin up and her jaw set, and Odessa saw the elders see it too — saw them mark the set of her shoulders, the way she looked at the terraces rising above the landing not with a prospector's hunger and not with a surveyor's cold arithmetic, but with something closer to grief. As if she recognized the shape of what had been broken.

*Elias Vane's daughter.* Odessa turned the fact over in her mind like a stone from the riverbed, all its surfaces unfamiliar. The name Vane meant one thing to the elders, and it was not a gentle thing. It meant the surveys. It meant the man who had walked these banks with Meridian's instruments before the burning, measuring a home for its theft. That he had repented — that he had spent his last years carrying the truth in an oilskin packet like a coal he couldn't put down — that story had not yet been told here. And Odessa did not know yet whether telling it would buy the strangers a hearing or cost them everything.

Behind her, someone shifted. Old Maren, whose voice carried further than any hail-speaker Odessa had ever heard. Maren, who had been a girl when the Company boats came, who had watched the ferry landing burn from the far bank with the children of half the valley screaming beside her. Maren, who had earned the right to hate anyone she chose.

"Eleven years," Maren said, not loudly, and every elder heard it anyway. "Eleven years since a stranger stood on this wood."

"Not quite true," Odessa said. "Tomas Ferro stood here. Nine years back, running Company tin."

"And we sent him away hungry," Maren said, "and I have never once regretted it." A dry sound that might have been a laugh, from somewhere in the line. "This is different, Odessa. You know it's different."

"I know," Odessa said. "That's why I'm standing here and you're standing behind me."

* * *

They received the strangers in the long house above the second terrace, because ceremony mattered even — especially — when your heart wasn't in it. Odessa had argued for that herself. Send them away from the landing and you told them they were filth; bring them into the long house and you told them they were guests; make them wait outside in the rain while the council debated, and you told them exactly where they stood. So that was what they did. Quentin Ashgrove and Sera Vane stood under the eave of the drying shed for the better part of an hour while the rain thickened, and Odessa watched them through the slats, and what she saw made her more certain and more afraid in equal measure.

Ashgrove didn't pace. That alone marked him. Men who lived by moving could not bear stillness, and this man bore it like ballast. He stood with his arms folded, watching the terraces, and once — only once — Odessa saw him reach out and touch the nearest weir stone, flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. Checking whether it was alive. Whether anything downstream of him was alive.

Sera Vane paced. Three steps, turn, three steps. But she kept her eyes on the long house door the entire time, and when the door finally opened she was moving toward it before the herald finished speaking.

Inside, the fire was small and the room was dim and the elders sat in a crescent around it, and Odessa took her place at the crescent's open end — neither inside the circle nor outside it, which was where her oath had put her. Guide to strangers. Sworn to the valley's remaining unowned. Belonging fully to neither side of the fire.

Maren spoke first, as everyone had known she would.

"You come into our house," she said. "Say why."

"We come—" Ashgrove began.

"The woman." Maren's eyes didn't leave Sera. "The man can stand there and look decorative. You. Daughter of Elias Vane. Say why you've come."

Odessa watched Sera take that blow. Watched her recognize it as a blow — the name arriving before the person, the father's sin preceding the daughter down eleven years of silence. And then Odessa watched her do the thing that decided everything, though none of them knew it yet.

She didn't defend him.

"My father surveyed this valley for the people who stole it," Sera said. "I've read his journal. Every page. He names what he did and he names what was done, and he spent the rest of his life trying to carry the truth back here, and he died on a dock in Port Lasseter without making it. I brought it the rest of the way." She reached into her coat and drew out the oilskin packet, and held it out — not to Maren, not to any single elder, but to the fire, to the crescent, to the room. "It's yours. It was always going to be yours. I'm just the courier my father couldn't afford to hire."

Silence, except the rain on the thatch.

It was old Ruven — third from the left, keeper of the seed stores, a man who spoke perhaps forty words in a season — who finally said, "And what do you want for it?"

"Nothing," Sera said.

"Everyone wants something."

"Then want me to want nothing," Sera said, "and be disappointed. I want my father's name read aloud beside those carvings on the rocks, and I want the people who forged the deeds to answer for it, and I want this valley to still belong to no one when I'm dead. That's not nothing. But none of it comes out of your pocket."

* * *

The council went behind the woven screen to deliberate, which took until the fire had burned down to coals, and Odessa used the time badly. She found herself standing beside Ashgrove at the wall, both of them pretending to study the old net-hung hooks, and he said, quietly, "You didn't have to do this. Bring us up. You could have left us below the falls to rot with the engine."

"Yes," Odessa said.

"Why didn't you?"

She considered lying, found she didn't have the energy for it. "Because I ran supplies past the checkpoints for three years before I ever piloted a stranger anywhere, and in three years I learned one thing about the Company. They don't stop. Not for weather, not for bodies, not for sense. If I left you below the falls, Crane's crews would find you in a week, and whatever you're carrying would be ash, and so would you." She turned to face him. "I didn't save you, Captain. I relocated a problem to where it could be solved properly."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "Honesty. From a pilot. The river's changing."

"The river always changes," Odessa said. "That's the whole lesson. Most people drown refusing to learn it."

Before he could answer, the screen rattled back, and Maren came out alone, and Odessa's stomach dropped, because Maren coming out alone meant the vote had been close, and close votes in the long house were settled by whoever spoke last and loudest, and Maren's voice had never once been last or loudest in favor of mercy.

"The packet stays," Maren said. "The documents will be copied by our own hands and hidden in three places, and no stranger will know where." Her eyes found Sera. "Your father's name will be read at the stones, when the rains allow. That debt we honor." Then her gaze swung to Odessa, and it was heavy as an anchor. "But the outsiders go. When the storm breaks. Downriver, past the falls, and they don't come back. That was the finding."

"That was *your* finding," Odessa said.

"It was the finding of seven against five," Maren said. "Count again if you doubt me. And you—" her voice dropped into the register she used for correcting children and correcting rivers, which was the same register— "you swore the valley stays unowned. You did not swear to fill it with fugitives and gunboat trouble. There's a difference, girl, and you're standing on the wrong side of it."

* * *

They gave the strangers the drying shed for the night, because even a hostile council did not put guests out into a building monsoon, and Odessa lay awake in the long house listening to the rain hammer the thatch and hating arithmetic. Seven against five. She turned the numbers over and over. Somewhere past midnight she gave up, pulled her oilskin on, and crossed the flooded walkway to the shed.

She found them awake, of course. Boat people slept like cats — light, and ready to bolt. Ashgrove had a lamp lit low and Sera sat cross-legged with the journal open on her knees, though she closed it when Odessa ducked under the lintel.

"You heard," Odessa said.

"We heard," Sera said. No bitterness in it. That was somehow worse.

"Seven against five isn't the valley," Odessa said. "Seven against five is fear wearing the valley's clothes. Maren watched this place burn. Half the council has kin scattered from the removals who never came back. When you've lost everything to strangers with papers, every stranger with papers looks like the first wave of the next flood." She crouched by the lamp. "I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you what it is."

"And you?" Ashgrove asked. "Where do you stand?"

"I stand where I swore to stand." Odessa held his gaze. "The valley stays unowned. That oath doesn't say unvisited. Your judge at Fort Amity — if he voids the deeds, if the titles die, then no one can ever dam this river, and everything Maren fears becomes impossible instead of just postponed. Your fight *is* the valley's fight. The council can't see it because grief doesn't read documents." She rose. "So here's what happens tomorrow. I speak for you again. I spend whatever credit I have left, and after eleven years of hauling their cargo past the checkpoints, I have some. And if the vote goes against me a second time—" she shrugged, rain dripping off her hood— "then I'll guide you downriver myself, past the falls, and consider my oath kept and my judgment ruined."

"And if it goes for you?" Sera asked.

"Then you stay through the storm, and when it breaks, you go to Fort Amity and bring back the law — the real law, not the rented kind — or don't come back at all. Those are the terms. Hospitality across old wounds, Captain. It's the only kind we have left."

At the lintel she paused, and looked back at the two of them in the lamplight — the boatman who touched weir stones like sleeping animals, and the courier who had given away her inheritance without blinking — and she thought of her grandmother's saying, the one about wood keeping what water teaches it.

These planks had held. Eleven years of empty river, and the landing still flexed instead of breaking.

Maybe the valley could too.

Outside, the rain suddenly slackened — not ending, but shifting, the wind swinging hard from the south, and Odessa stood in the dark reading it the way her grandmother had taught her, and what she read made her blood run cold.

That wasn't a storm breaking.

That was the leading edge of the monsoon proper — weeks early, riding a wind that should not arrive for another six days. And somewhere downriver, in that same wind, Aldous Crane was climbing.

She turned back toward the shed, toward the lamplight, toward the two strangers whose fate she had hitched to her own.

"Change of plans," she said, ducking back inside. "Nobody's waiting for anything. We need to talk to the council tonight."
