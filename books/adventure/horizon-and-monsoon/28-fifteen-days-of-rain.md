## 28. Fifteen Days of Rain

The monsoon came three days early, the way debts come — announced long in advance and still a surprise when it arrives.

Quentin felt it first in his teeth, an old riverman's ache that had never once lied to him. He was on the Marguerite's deck at gray dawn, checking lashings he'd already checked twice, when the wind changed its mind about everything. It had blown upriver for a week, warm and patient, carrying the smell of far-off water. Now it swung around without ceremony and began to push down from the heights, cold and certain, and every leaf in the valley turned over to show its pale underside like a crowd of people looking away.

"Here it comes," Odessa said beside him. She'd come out onto the deck without him hearing her, which still unsettled him after two weeks of knowing her. Riverkin moved quiet as current.

"How bad?"

She considered the sky with the flat attention of someone reading a ledger she'd memorized years ago. "Fifteen days, maybe sixteen. The upper gorge will run brown by tomorrow night. After that, nothing goes up or down until it's done." She glanced at him. "You wanted a deadline. This is one you can't argue with."

He grunted. Below them, the camp was already moving — Sera's voice carrying across the terraces, organizing, directing, the way she organized everything now, as if command were something she'd inherited along with the journal. The elders moved among the younger ones with slower gestures but no less authority. Tarps came off supply caches. Stone hammers were passed hand to hand. A child ran past with an armful of rope nearly as long as she was tall, solemn as a clerk delivering writs.

The rain arrived not as a wall but as a decision — a fine silver hiss moving up the valley floor, swallowing the terraces one by one, and then it was on them and the world contracted to the size of a tarp's shadow.

* * *

They dug in the way the old ones taught them, which was the way Quentin would never have thought of on his own: not against the rain but into it.

"The water is not your enemy," Elder Maren said the first morning, standing in the downpour without a hat, her voice pitched to carry. She was the oldest of them, small and bent as a root, with hands that looked carved from the same grey stone they worked. "Your enemy is the water going where it should not. So we give it roads."

The roads were the weirs. Quentin had seen them before — the low stone terraces stepping down the valley's throat, so overgrown he'd taken them for natural shelves. Now, under Maren's direction, he understood them differently. They were arguments, centuries old, made of fitted rock: *flow here, rest there, feed this channel, spare that bank.* The monsoon didn't destroy the valley. It fed it. The valley only needed to remember how to drink.

Which meant work. Endless, cold, specific work.

The lower weir had failed decades ago — Tomas knew the night it happened, though he didn't say so yet — and a gap yawned in it wide enough to pass a canoe. If the gap stayed open through the flood, the water would carve a new channel straight through the eastern terraces, and the terraces would go, and the slopes above them would follow, and the valley would spend fifty years forgetting itself all over again.

So they filled the gap. Stone by stone, in the rain, day after day.

Quentin learned the work the way he learned rivers: by being wrong in front of people who weren't cruel about it. His first stones went in proud and loose, and Maren's grandson, a boy of maybe twelve named Pell, pulled them out again without comment and showed him how each rock needed a partner, a weight leaning against a weight, nothing standing alone. *Like people,* the boy didn't say, because Riverkin children apparently didn't editorialize. Quentin heard it anyway.

Sera worked beside him, mud to her elbows, hair plastered flat, laughing at something Pell said — actually laughing, a sound Quentin hadn't heard from her in the early weeks, when grief sat on her like ballast. He found himself watching her more than was efficient. He told himself it was because she was lifting stones wrong. She wasn't lifting stones wrong.

"You're staring," she said, not looking up.

"Checking your form."

"My form is better than yours."

It was. He went back to work.

* * *

Tomas hauled stone.

That was all anyone could say of him for the first four days, and it became, without anyone deciding it, enough. The old deckhand had arrived in the valley carrying his guilt the way mules carry ore — silently, visibly, under obvious strain — and he had set it down nowhere except into the work. He carried the largest stones. He carried them alone when two men would have been sensible. He waded into the flooded channel to seat the foundation rocks while the current tried to peel him off the riverbed, and when they pulled him out shivering he went back in before his tea was finished.

"He's trying to drown the past," Odessa observed quietly, watching from the high terrace.

"Doesn't work," Quentin said. "I've tried."

"No," she agreed. "But sometimes the past drowns a little too, if you hold it under long enough."

On the fifth day the elders gave him what he hadn't asked for. Maren came down to the weir in the evening rain, stood beside him where he knelt in the shallows fitting a capstone, and watched him work for a long time.

"You were at the burning landing," she said. Not a question.

Tomas's hands went still on the stone. Rain ticked against his shoulders. "Yes, ma'am."

"I was there too." Her voice was level as still water. "I carried my sister's boy across the ford while the boats burned. He was four." She crouched, laid her own hands on the capstone, helped him seat it. "You brought flour to the removal camps afterward. I know. We knew even then who did what, and who did what they were told."

"I could have—"

"You could have many things. So could we all." She patted the stone twice, the way you'd pat a finished thing. "This rock doesn't care what you did. It cares whether you set it true. Set it true, Tomas. That's the whole of the absolution available to any of us, and it's more than most get."

She walked back up the terrace. Tomas knelt in the rain a while longer, and Quentin, working ten feet away, pretended not to see an old man press his forehead against wet granite like a man praying to something that might finally answer.

* * *

The nights were for shelter, and the shelter made strangers into something else.

They crowded into the great hall — a longhouse built of timber and stone on the highest terrace, its roof steep as a church's, blackened beams overhead older than any deed or forgery. Fires burned at both ends. Wet clothes steamed on lines strung the length of the room. Children slept in piles like puppies. And the adults talked, because there was nothing else to do, and the talking wore away at people the way the water wore at stone.

Quentin learned the Riverkin stories by firelight: the ferry landing that had served three generations, the songs sung to time the crossing through the falls narrows, the year the fish ran so thick you could walk across the shallows on their backs. He learned that Odessa had piloted the last legal ferry at fourteen, and the first illegal one at fifteen, running supplies to families hiding in the high forest during the removals. He learned that Pell's father had been born in the valley, left as a baby, and died downriver without ever seeing it again — and that Pell had been brought back last spring to learn the stones before the ones who remembered them were gone.

In exchange, they asked about him, and for once he answered.

"The Marguerite was my brother's name," he said one night, surprising himself. The fire popped. Sera looked up from the journal she was copying by lamplight. "Not my wife's, not a lover's. My brother. Daniel. He drowned when we were boys, on a boat our father was piloting drunk. I decided somewhere in the aftermath that the river takes what stops moving, and I have been proving it ever since." He turned his cup in his hands. "Named the boat after him so he'd keep moving too. Sentimental, probably. I don't recommend it."

"It's not sentimental," Sera said quietly. "It's a promise. There's a difference."

The elders listened to all of it without comment, the way they seemed to listen to everything, filing it away like water finding its level. But that night, Maren's sister — a tiny fierce woman called Brey who had lost her husband to the removal crews — brought Quentin an extra blanket without being asked, and draped it over his shoulders with a briskness that permitted no thanks, and he understood he had been admitted to something whose membership requirements he would never fully know.

* * *

The storm peaked on the eighth day.

The river rose eleven feet in six hours. From the high terraces they watched the gorge below the falls turn into a single brown muscle, flexing, throwing spray forty feet up the cliff walls. The sound was beyond sound — a pressure, a presence, something you felt in the chest cavity and the backs of the eyes. Quentin had run flooded water his whole life and had never stood above it like this, watching it do what it wanted, and he felt something he couldn't immediately name and finally identified, with some embarrassment, as awe.

The weirs held.

Not perfectly — a section of the middle terrace shifted on the second night of peak flow, and half the camp spent a miserable midnight hour in driving rain passing stone hand to hand by lantern light, Sera ankle-deep in the spillway with her arms locked around a boulder while Pell wedged timbers beneath it. But they held. When dawn came grey and exhausted, the water ran where the stones told it to run, and the eastern terraces stood green and unbroken above the flood, and old Brey stood at the weir's edge laughing at the river like a woman scolding a dog that had finally learned to sit.

"We'll lose the top course," Maren said, surveying the damage with professional calm. "We'll rebuild it in the dry season. That's the arrangement. Every year it tests us, every year we answer." She looked at Quentin. "Your boat took water?"

"A little in the bilge. She's lashed double. She'll complain but she won't die."

"She sounds like you."

From Sera, later, wringing out her braids by the fire: "You realize you've stopped talking about leaving."

"I talk about leaving constantly."

"You talk about *when*. You used to talk about *whether*." She hung her braids by the fire and sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders touched, which neither of them moved away from. "There's a difference."

He looked at her. Firelight, steam, the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke and the green breath of the valley beyond the walls. Somewhere outside, the rain kept up its endless work, patient as geology.

"You're keeping score," he said.

"Somebody has to. You're terrible at noticing things about yourself." She leaned her head briefly against his shoulder — a touch so brief it might have been accident, except that nothing she did was ever accident — and then got up to check the drying lines, and Quentin sat alone by the fire feeling something shift in his chest the way the middle terrace had shifted: not broken, just rearranged, settling into a new configuration that would hold if the foundations were true.

* * *

On the eleventh day the rain thinned to a steady whisper, and Odessa came down from the watch post on the northern rim with rain streaming off her oilskin and an expression Quentin had learned to read as trouble.

"Smoke," she said. "Downriver side of the gorge. Not ours."

Quentin climbed with her to the rim, Sera following, and the three of them stood under the dripping canopy and looked south along the river's brown ribbon. Far off — miles, but visible in the washed clarity the storm left behind — a smudge of grey rose against the green of the far bank. Camp smoke. More than one fire. And on the water below it, small with distance but unmistakable to a man who'd spent thirty years reading vessels at any range, the angular silhouettes of boats at anchor.

"Meridian can't run the gorge in this," Sera said. "You said yourself the water's still up."

"They can't run it," Quentin said slowly. "But they don't have to. Those are supply boats. They're staging." He counted again, lips moving. "Six hulls. Maybe seven. That's not a survey crew, Sera. That's an expedition."

Odessa was silent a moment, her face unreadable, rain gathering on the brim of her hood. "Crane," she said at last. "He waited out the storm on the lower river. Now he moves the moment it breaks." She turned to look back at the valley behind them — the terraces, the smoke rising thin and domestic from the longhouse chimney, the sound of children's voices carrying faintly up the slope. "The dry season starts in four days. The river drops fast after a monsoon like this one. In a week, that gorge will be runnable again."

"Then we have a week," Quentin said.

Below them, the camp went on with its evening — fires lit, stones hauled, the weirs holding their ancient argument against the water. Tomas was at the lower weir still, working late as always, and Quentin watched him for a moment, wondering what an old man who had already betrayed them once would do when the boats he'd once supplied came up the gorge with guns aboard.

The smoke on the horizon did not move. It didn't need to. It only waited, the way the monsoon had waited, the way debts wait — announced long in advance, and arriving anyway.
