## 29. Crane Ascends

The rain began at the mouth of the gorge and did not stop.

Aldous Crane stood on the foredeck of the *Meridian Standard No. 4* with his collar turned up and his survey case strapped across his chest like a bandolier, watching the water rise around the pilings of the last Company checkpoint as though the river were climbing to meet him. Behind him, three gunboats rode low in the current, their decks crowded with men in grey coats who had been told they were going upriver to secure a land claim and who understood, in the wordless way of hired men, that securing meant something else this far from any courthouse.

Nineteen days. That was what the radio reports had said when he'd left Fort Amity — nineteen days until the monsoon made landfall, and every man with a barometer had told him the same thing: you cannot go up now, Mr. Crane. The upper river will be unnavigable after. The gorges will run brown and blind. The falls will drown their own thunder.

He had thanked them for their concern and ordered the boats loaded at dawn.

Ambition was a kind of arithmetic, and Crane had always been good at arithmetic. Harrow's stay order would not survive federal review — or it might, but review took months, and months were worth more than money to a man like Meridian's board. If the valley could be surveyed, staked, and physically held before the rains cut the river closed behind him, then the law, whatever it decided, would be ruling on an accomplished fact. Companies had built empires on less. He had built his reputation on less: a boundary moved here, a signature dated there, a community reclassified from *resident* to *obstacle* with the clean stroke of a pen.

That was the trade he had made twenty years ago and never regretted — not once, not until lately — that ink was cheaper than blood, and someone else always spilled the blood.

"Mr. Crane." The boat captain, a slab-faced man named Dreever, stood at his elbow shouting over the engines. "Water's up two feet since Stone Latch. Pilot says we can make the narrows today if we push, but coming back—"

"There is no coming back, Captain. Not until the dry season."

Dreever looked at him the way men look at a superior who has just announced his own funeral. "And the crews?"

"The crews knew the terms. Hazard pay doubles past the falls."

"Hazard pay doesn't spend underwater, sir."

Crane almost smiled. "No. But it spends widows' mouths full, which is what it's for. Push on."

* * *

He went below to the survey cabin because standing on deck had become unbearable, though he would have shot any man who suggested so. The cabin was four feet wide and smelled of lamp oil and damp paper, and it was the only place on the river where Aldous Crane felt he existed.

He unlocked the case and took out the field notebooks — his own, twenty years of them, bound in oilcloth and numbered in his own hand. Every surveyor kept them. They were the raw truth beneath the finished maps: the sightings that didn't close, the measurements taken twice, the notes in the margin where the neat geometry of a claim ran up against the stubborn facts of the ground. A surveyor's notebooks were his conscience, if he had one, and his alibi, if he needed one.

He had needed one only twice.

The first time had been the headwaters itself, twenty years ago. He had been young then, hungry in the way that makes a man quick rather than careful, and the Meridian contract had been the largest of his career. He remembered the valley still — better than he wanted to. Terraced slopes gone soft with moss. Stone weirs across the tributary streams, fitted without mortar, older than any deed in the provincial registry. Fruit trees growing wild out of the ruins of orchards. And the people — the Riverkin, the registry called them, though they called themselves nothing he'd bothered to write down — standing along the banks watching his theodolite the way one watches weather.

His notebook from those weeks contained everything the finished survey omitted. It contained the weirs, measured and sketched, with his own annotation in a young man's confident hand: *Construction predates earliest registered title by a century at minimum. Continuous maintenance evident. Recommend further investigation before title proceedings.*

He had written *recommend further investigation*, and then he had accepted a bonus to write the opposite in the final report. The deeds were already drawn; his job had been to make the ground agree with them. So he had dated his observations loosely, buried the weirs in a footnote about "aboriginal works of no legal significance," and signed the certification page that converted a living valley into vacant land.

Vacant. He turned the page and found the population column he had filled in himself: *Permanent inhabitants: none of record.*

None of record. As though a record were the world.

The second time had been last month, when the old prospector Vane surfaced with his journal and his copies, and Crane had been asked — politely, in a room with no windows — to confirm whether the documents were genuine. He had read Vane's field notes by lamplight while men waited to hear his answer, and he had known within a page that they were genuine, because Vane had surveyed the same ground he had, honestly, and honest surveys of that valley all said the same thing. He had said so. He had watched the decision move through the room like water through a lock, and he had signed the fire permit for the records annex two days later without being asked twice, because the alternative to signing was becoming a witness, and witnesses to Meridian decisions had a way of becoming casualties.

Now the annex was ash, and the ash had his name on the permit, and the journal was somewhere ahead of him on this river, in the hands of a boat captain who didn't stop and a daughter who should have stayed in her salvage yard.

Crane sat in the swaying cabin with the lamp swinging above him and read his own handwriting from twenty years ago until the letters stopped looking like words.

*Recommend further investigation.*

He had been the investigation. That was the joke he did not laugh at. Every question his younger self had raised, he had spent two decades answering in the most thorough way possible — with forged dates, with burned records, with murdered partners, and now with three gunboats and sixty armed men gambling against a monsoon. He had investigated that valley more completely than any man alive, and the finding, accumulated year by year, was that he was the crime.

A knock. Dreever again, or one of the enforcers — Hask, the broad one with the rifle slung muzzle-down out of some borrowed notion of propriety.

"We're passing the burned landing, sir. Thought you'd want to see."

Crane closed the notebook. "I've seen it."

"You saw it twenty years ago, maybe. This is fresh."

* * *

It was fresh. The Riverkin ferry landing — the one Tomas had spoken of once, drunk, and never spoken of again — still smoked faintly in the drizzle, black ribs of pilots' timber standing in the shallows like the skeleton of something that had drowned standing up. The mud around it was churned by boots. A cooking pot lay in the reeds, dented, half-buried. Someone had scratched marks into the charred planking of the landing — names, Crane realized, or prayers, or both; the Riverkin carved names into everything, the way other people planted flags.

Hask stood beside him on the deck, breathing through his mouth. "Crews are asking questions, Mr. Crane. The pilot especially. He grew up downriver. He says his grandmother ferried from a landing like this."

"Tell the crews we're securing Company property against squatters."

"They'll ask whose property it was before the Company."

"Tell them," Crane said, "that the question is above their pay grade. It is certainly above mine."

That was true, and saying it aloud did something strange to his chest. Above mine. Twenty years ago he had believed there was nothing above his pay grade except the board, and the board was only weather — impersonal, distant, something a clever man learned to sail under. Now he stood on a gunboat in the rain looking at a burned ferry landing and understood that the weather had been him. He was the board, at this altitude, at this hour. Every decision between here and the source would be his alone, and there was no one above him left to blame, and the rain kept falling, and the river kept rising, and his own signature kept surfacing everywhere he looked like a body that would not stay sunk.

"Sir?" Hask said. "You want us to put men ashore? Sweep the bank?"

"No." Too fast. He moderated his voice. "No. We don't have time to sweep banks. The monsoon is nine days out, perhaps fewer in these hills. We go straight to the source. Everything else is weather."

* * *

They made the narrows at dusk, and the narrows had changed.

Crane had charts — good ones, his own work among them — and the charts showed a channel forty meters wide below the overhanging bluffs, with a gravel bar on the eastern bend marked as a hazard at low water. What the charts showed and what the river offered were now different countries. The channel had swallowed the bar whole. The water ran high, brown, muscular, carrying entire trees end-over-end like things it had grown tired of holding. Rain came off the bluffs in sheets that turned the world to grey silk. The pilot worked the wheel in silence, reading water the way Crane read deeds, and twice he backed the engines hard and let a floating log pass where the chart said open channel, and neither time did he explain himself, and Crane did not ask.

From the stern he heard one of the enforcers vomiting, and another laughing at him, and the laughter had a cracked edge that Crane recognized. He had heard that laughter before, in other men, on other rivers, always shortly before the moment when discipline became the only structure holding a crew together — and shortly after the moment when it failed.

In the survey cabin that night, unable to sleep with the engines thudding and the rain hammering the deck above like a fist demanding entry, he opened the second notebook. The recent one. His own survey of the upper gorge, done six years ago when the dam project first revived, when the board had begun to speak of the headwaters in the present tense again.

He had surveyed the gorge honestly, that time. There had seemed no reason not to; the forgery was old and settled, and honesty in the details flattered the lie at the center. But on the final page, in the section reserved for hydrological recommendations, his younger-middle-aged self had written a paragraph he had forgotten entirely:

*Note: proposed dam site at the source basin sits above a natural floodplain of unusual capacity. In monsoon conditions, impoundment failure would release the entire headwater volume through the lower gorge within hours. Villages at Stone Latch and Port Calloway fall within the inundation zone. Recommend the board commission a full risk study before construction proceeds.*

The board had commissioned no such study. The board had shelved the dam and pursued instead the quieter theft — titles, evictions, the slow bureaucratic drowning that leaves no wreckage. And Crane had let the paragraph sleep in its oilcloth box for six years, a warning addressed to no one, filed by the very man it should have convicted.

He read it three times by lamplight while the river rose outside the hull.

Then he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since Fort Amity. Sixty armed men, three boats, nine days of grace, a gorge that was already running at flood stage, and at the end of it a valley defended — the scouts' last report had used the word *fortified*, absurdly, of farmers and ferrymen — by people whose grandparents his signature had dispossessed. Against all of it: a stay order, a judge with Meridian debts, a burned annex, and a journal that might at this moment be lying in a circuit court's evidence locker or might be waiting for him at the source, whichever way the wind of chance had blown it.

If he turned back now, the board would understand. Men turned back from weather; it was forgivable. But turning back meant the valley stayed unheld through the dry season, and an unheld valley with public sympathy and a federal review pending was a valley lost. Ambition, which had carried him up every river of his life, sat in the cabin with him now and said nothing, and its silence was worse than argument, because he understood at last that ambition had never been a voice at all. It had been an absence — of doubt, of mercy, of the willingness to count costs — and the absence was filling up.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the rain, and for the first time in twenty years Aldous Crane prayed, though he did not know to whom, and would not have called it prayer. It was more a sentence repeated against the dark, the way a man repeats a coordinate to keep from losing it:

Let the map be ash. Let the journal be ash. Let me find nothing at the top of this river but water.

He did not believe he would get his wish. Men like him never did. That was the whole architecture of the punishment: you climbed toward the thing you stole, and at the summit you found your own signature waiting, and you had to read it aloud.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, and with it came the sound of engines that were not theirs.

The lookout's shout brought Crane onto the wet deck with his glass already raised. Far downstream, half-lost in the rain-haze where the narrows widened toward Stone Latch, a boat was running the flooded channel — small, single-screw, riding light, taking water that would founder anything heavier. Even at that distance he knew her silhouette; he had studied the harbor registries until he could draw her from memory. The Marguerite. The captain who never stopped, coming back upriver against the current and the season, with the Vane woman aboard and God knew what in her hold.

Beside him, Hask lowered his own glass. "That's them, isn't it. Ashgrove's boat."

"It's them."

"She can't outrun us in this. Shall we signal the lead gunboat to run her down?"

Crane kept the glass at his eye a moment longer. Through the rain-haze he watched the little boat take the flooded channel at an angle no chart would recommend, and he thought: *that man has read this water the way I never read anything, and he is betting his life on the reading.* Then he thought of the journal, and the weirs, and the paragraph in his own notebook, and the names carved into the burned landing, and he lowered the glass.

"No," he said. "Not in the narrows, not in this water. We'd lose as many as we drowned, and drowned men make martyrs, and martyrs make headlines." He turned for the cabin, and heard his own voice come out level and cold, the professional instrument, the signature-hand. "But log the sighting, Captain. Course and speed. Because she's going where we're going — and at the top of this river, Mr. Hask, one of us is going to arrive."

Behind them the rain thickened over the gorge, and the river rose another inch, patient as a ledger, keeping its accounts.
