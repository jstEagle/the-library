## 30. The First Skirmish

The rain came in the night before the boats did, and Quentin Ashgrove woke to the sound of the valley breathing.

He had slept badly in the weeks since they'd hauled the Marguerite's bones above the falls — not from fear, exactly, though there was plenty of that to go around, but from the strangeness of sleeping ashore. Twenty years he had fallen asleep to the river's voice filtered through a hull, water talking to wood, wood answering. Here the sound was unfiltered. The whole basin was a bowl of noise: rain on leaves, rain on stone, rain on the terraced gardens the Riverkin had cleared and re-cleared since the first of them came back, and beneath it all the deep arterial pulse of the headwaters themselves, that vast slow heartbeat under everything, older than any deed or company or name.

He lay in the lean-to Odessa's people had built him against the old ferry house wall — the same wall, Tomas said, that had burned the night of the removal, its stones still smoke-dark if you knew where to look — and he listened to the valley breathe, and he thought: *nineteen days became twelve, and twelve became this.*

The monsoon had come early. It always did, the elders said, when the mountain wanted something.

* * *

By grey light he was at the weir.

It was the thing he'd come to love most about the valley, which surprised him; he'd expected to love the river itself, the source streams braiding down out of the cloud forest, but it was the weir that held him. A quarter mile of fitted stone laid across the lower throat of the basin, stepped like a staircase for the water to descend, each course shaped so the flood passed over it without tearing it apart. The elders called it by a word that meant *the hand that holds*. Three hundred years old, maybe more. No mortar. No owner. Just hands, going back and back, laying stone on stone because the water needed holding and someone had held it.

Quentin had spent two days of the last week with Old Sef, the eldest of them, walking the weir's crest while the rain fell and fell, learning what the stones said. Where the water ran green and glassy, the foundation was sound. Where it ran white and worried, a course below had shifted. Where it made no sound at all — where the river went silent over a stretch of crest — that was the place to be afraid, because silence meant the water had found a way through and was eating the heart out of the structure from inside.

"The Company men will come up the lower channel," Sef had said, in the trade tongue, his voice pitched under the rain. "They will come when the water is high enough to float their shallow boats past the gorge, and low enough in their minds to think the valley is empty."

"And you'll want me to do what? Sink them?"

Sef had looked at him the way he looked at a young man who asked foolish questions about stone. "No one dies here," he said. "Not again. Not on our stone." Then he had crouched, laid his palm flat on the wet crest, and traced the line of an old sluice gate, long silted shut, half-hidden in the masonry. "But the hand that holds can also close."

* * *

They came on the twelfth day of the rains, in the middle of the afternoon, when the light was worst.

Quentin heard them before anyone saw them — a wrongness in the river's voice downstream, a rhythmic knocking he knew too well from twenty years of engines: the flat slap-slap of a shallow-draft screw working hard against current. He was on the weir with a repair crew when it reached him, and he stood up so fast the stone he'd been fitting rolled from his hands into the flood.

"Odessa!" His shout carried. She was fifty yards off, hauling brush, and she turned, and whatever was in his face told her everything. She dropped the brush and ran for the signal drum by the ferry house, and the valley began to speak to itself in the old way — short beats, long beats, the pattern Sera had spent ten days learning and Quentin still fumbled — and within minutes the gardens were emptying, the elders were moving to their stations, and every soul in the basin knew what the drum already knew.

*Boats. Coming up. Many.*

There were four of them, Quentin counted from the high bank, steel-hulled Meridian survey launches, the kind the Company used for charting shallow tributaries — flat-bottomed, armored at the bow, each carrying six or eight men in grey slickers. They came up the flooded lower channel in echelon, riding the pre-monsoon swell with the confidence of men who had bought their charts and believed them. Quentin could see the lead boat's coxswain standing at the wheel, consulting a board-mounted chart, and he felt the old contempt rise in him, familiar as his own pulse. *Chart men.* Men who believed the river was paper.

The trouble was, the river *had* been paper, once. Aldous Crane's paper. And Crane's charts showed the lower channel running wide and deep all the way to the valley mouth, because Crane had never once surveyed it in flood.

"They'll make the mouth in twenty minutes," Sera said at his shoulder. She had come up the bank at a run, hair plastered to her skull, the journal wrapped in oilskin inside her coat where it lived now, day and night. Her eyes were bright and terrified and furious, all three at once, and Quentin thought — not for the first time, and with a pang he didn't have time for — that she looked more like her father in these moments than she would ever have wanted to know.

"Then we've got twenty minutes to close a gate that's been shut for forty years," Quentin said.

* * *

The plan was Sef's, refined by Odessa, argued over for two nights around the fire, and it rested on a truth Quentin had spent his whole life respecting: water is heavier than men.

The old sluice gates — there were three of them, set into the weir's lower face where the original builders had let the river feed the mill channels and the ferry landing — had been sealed with silt and stone since the removal. Opening them would drop the level of the pool behind the weir by several feet in a matter of minutes, right where the lower channel met the valley mouth. Any boat caught in the shallows below the sill would ground, hard, on a bar that hadn't existed an hour before. And the flood coming down the gorge behind them — the monsoon-fed torrent that had nearly killed them all twice — would keep the grounded boats pinned there, broadside to the current, until the water chose otherwise.

"No blood," Odessa had said, and meant it as law. "We are not them. If they drown, we become them."

So the work was stone-work, and Quentin found himself, absurdly, in his element. Forty men and women hauled the sealing stones out of the sluice throats with ropes and levers while the drums talked and the rain hammered and the first launch's engine-note grew from a slap to a growl. Quentin worked the center gate himself, hip-deep in the sluice cut, wrestling a capstone the size of a coffin loose with a bar and a prayer, and when it finally shifted and the water behind it exhaled — a long brown sigh, then a rush, then a roar — he felt the whole weir shudder like a living thing waking up.

"Hold the side gates!" Sef shouted. "Center only! We shape the river, we don't unleash it!"

That was the difference, Quentin understood, between them and the Company. The Company would have blown the whole weir and damned the consequences. The valley opened one gate, precisely, the way a surgeon opens one vein.

* * *

The lead launch made the mouth at a quarter past four.

Quentin watched it happen from the crest of the weir, and it happened almost exactly as Sef had read it in the water. The coxswain brought his boat up the flooded channel at speed, riding the swell, following Crane's chart into water that Crane's chart promised was twelve feet deep — and the bow touched the bar with a grinding crunch that Quentin felt through the stone under his boots.

It was beautiful, in the way that disasters are beautiful when they happen to the right people. The launch hit the new shallows at eight knots and stopped as if it had struck a reef. The momentum carried the stern around, the current took the broadside hull and shoved it up onto the bar, heeling it, and the grey slickers went sprawling across the deck and into the scuppers. Behind it, the second launch's coxswain saw the grounding and rang his engine full astern — too late, too shallow, and his boat swung and pinned alongside the first, both of them locked on the emergent bar with the flood roaring past their exposed sides.

The third and fourth launches backed water frantically and withdrew down the channel, engines screaming, and took station in the rain a half-mile below, where the water was still honest.

On the bar, the stranded crews scrambled upright. Quentin counted heads. Eight men on the first boat, seven on the second. Rifles — he could see them now, slung, not shouldered. Nobody firing. Nobody quite sure yet what had happened to them, or who had done it.

A voice carried up through the rain, amplified and tinny — a hail-speaker, the same model Crane had used on the gorge. "This is a Meridian Company survey operation! You are obstructing licensed navigation! Stand off and allow us to refloat our vessels!"

Odessa stood beside Quentin on the crest, rain streaming off her, and she did not answer. None of them answered. That had been the plan too: no voices, no faces, nothing for the Company to put names to. Let the valley be weather. Let the grounding be the river's own doing, which, in fairness, it mostly was.

"They'll try to kedge off," Quentin murmured. "Anchor out astern, winch themselves back into the channel. They've got maybe an hour before the flood rises another foot and makes the bar unwinnable."

"Let them try," Sera said. "Every hour they spend on that bar is an hour Crane isn't getting word upriver."

And that was the true arithmetic of it, Quentin knew. This wasn't a battle; it was a clock. Every hour the scouts sat stranded was an hour Harrow's stay held, an hour the federal reviewers moved closer, an hour the journal and the deed copies sat safe in Fort Amity with Sera's sworn copies lodged in the circuit clerk's strongbox. The skirmish wasn't fought with rifles. It was fought with hours.

* * *

They kedged, as Quentin had known they would.

He watched the stranded crews work with a professional's unwilling admiration — anchors carried out in waist-deep floodwater by men wading against a current that wanted them dead, lines run to capstans, the launches groaning and tilting as the winches took the strain. Twice the first launch lifted her bow and slid six inches toward the channel, and twice the flood pushed her back. The bar was growing even as they fought it; the opened sluice was laying fresh silt across the shallows with every minute, building the trap higher around the boats that had sprung it.

By dusk the men on the bar had stopped trying. They gathered under a tarp amidships on the second launch, small and grey in the failing light, and someone lit a lantern, and the lantern's glow lay long and broken on the brown water, and Quentin felt something twist in his chest that he refused to examine closely.

"They're just men," Sera said quietly, beside him. She'd been watching the lantern too.

"They're men who came to take a valley," Quentin said. "Pity them tomorrow."

"I do pity them today. That's allowed."

He glanced at her. Rain ran off her chin. "Your father would've said something like that."

"My father said a great many things like that," Sera said, "and then he left. Pity's cheap from a distance. I'm working on the expensive kind." She turned away toward the fire-light of the camp. "Come eat something, Captain. The river will hold them. It's better at this than we are."

* * *

Night settled over the valley, and the stranded launches became two dim lanterns on a black bar, and the rain went on.

Quentin walked the weir one last time before turning in, checking the center sluice with a lantern, listening to the water run clean and full through the opened gate. Sef found him there, as Sef always seemed to find him, materializing out of the dark with the patience of stone.

"You closed nothing," Sef observed. "You killed no one. The valley holds."

"For tonight."

"The valley has held for three hundred years of nights."

"And the Company's been here for five years," Quentin said, "and they've got gunboats, and money, and a man named Crane who doesn't quit, and a judge in their pocket — well. Used to." He snapped the lantern shutter half-closed. "We won an afternoon, old man. Don't dress it up as more than it is."

Sef considered him for a long moment, rain dripping from the deep lines of his face. "You count hours like a man counting coins he owes," he said. "Why?"

Because the arithmetic never stops, Quentin thought. Because every hour gained is an hour Crane gains too, somewhere downriver, gathering boats and men and worse. Because a stranded scout patrol means Crane knows exactly where the resistance is thickest, and next time he won't send four survey launches and a hail-speaker. Next time he'll send what the Company actually keeps at its depots, and the valley will have to hold against something harder than a sandbar.

"Because it's a countdown," he said aloud. "That's all any of this is. We're not winning. We're spending time we don't have to buy time we need."

Sef nodded slowly, as if Quentin had finally said something worth hearing. "Yes," the old man said. "And the mountain counts faster than the Company. Have you seen the gauge stone?"

"What gauge stone?"

Sef led him along the crest to a squat pillar of rock near the eastern end, older than everything, carved with a ladder of notches worn smooth by centuries of reading. Water lapped at the fifth notch from the top. Sef laid his hand on the sixth.

"When the river takes this notch," he said, "the gorge closes. No boat comes up. No boat goes down. The valley belongs to itself until the dry season." He looked at Quentin, and the lantern-light caught something in his eyes that was not comfort. "Three days, perhaps four. After that, whatever happens here happens alone."

Quentin stood a long moment, watching the black water breathe against the ancient stone, feeling the countdown in his chest tick over another hour — no, less than an hour, less all the time — and far downstream, faint under the rain, he thought he heard the sound of engines. More than four. Many more.

Crane was coming, and the mountain was rising to meet him, and the valley had maybe three days of being reachable by anything except memory.

He turned back toward the fires. There were stones to haul, and a weir to watch, and whatever hours remained to spend.
