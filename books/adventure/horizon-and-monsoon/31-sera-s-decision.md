## 31. Sera's Decision

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the valley lay steaming in the grey light like something newly born.

Sera stood at the edge of the terraces above the camp, her boots sunk to the ankles in wet loam, and watched the river come down out of the mountains. It came the way it had come every morning since they'd arrived — brown with mountain clay, loud, certain of itself — and it went over the old stone weir in a long white sheet that sang against the silence of the basin. The elders called it *the voice*. She was beginning to understand why. You could stand there long enough and hear words in it, or almost words, the way you could hear your own name in wind through a doorway.

Behind her, the camp was waking. Smoke from three cookfires rose straight up through the still air before the wind found it and tore it apart. Someone was singing — one of Odessa's granddaughters, a thin high thread of a song about a fisherman who married the moon. The sound carried across the flooded paddies where the Riverkin were already wading, hip-deep, pulling silt from the channels their great-grandparents had cut into the hillsides with nothing but patience and stone.

Fifteen days of rain had done its work on all of them. Sera's hands were cracked at every knuckle, her hair a permanent damp rope down her back, her shoulders ridged with new muscle from hauling stone to the broken section of the weir. She had never been so tired in her life. She had also never slept so well.

And now she was going to ruin it. She knew that the way you know weather coming — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron at the back of the throat.

She turned and looked down at the Marguerite.

The old boat sat moored in the lee of the ferry landing — the landing Tomas had helped burn, twenty-two years ago, and which the whole camp had spent two weeks rebuilding around her hull as if the boat itself were a piece of the valley being put back. Her paddle wheel was half-dismantled; Quentin had been re-packing the bearings with tallow rendered from a pig the elders had traded them. Her rail was scarred where Crane's scouts' rifle fire had chewed through during the skirmish at the mouth. But she floated. She sat in the water with the stubborn dignity of a thing that has decided not to sink, and Sera had come to love her the way you love anything that has carried you through something terrible without complaint.

Quentin was under her stern, upside down in the machinery space, his legs sticking out into the mud, arguing quietly with a wrench.

Sera picked her way down the terrace steps — stone risers worn smooth by centuries of feet, each one slicker than it looked — and stood over his boots until he backed out into the light, blinking, a smear of grease across one cheekbone like war paint applied by a child.

"You're up early," he said.

"I didn't sleep."

He looked at her for a moment. He'd gotten good at looking at her these past weeks — good at reading the weather of her face the way she'd learned to read his. Whatever he saw made him set the wrench down carefully on a flat stone instead of tossing it, which was what he did when he expected bad news and wanted his hands free.

"Say it," he said.

* * *

They walked while she said it. He always thought better walking, she'd noticed, and she needed him thinking at his best, because what she had to say was going to land on him like a dropped anchor.

"The journal," she began. "And the deed copies. The ones we split after the weir — the real ones, the ones Tomas led Crane's men away from."

"What about them?"

"They're no good here."

He stopped walking. Above them the terraces climbed green and dripping toward the tree line; below, the river sang over the weir. "No good here," he repeated. "Sera, those papers are the only reason this valley is still standing. Crane came up here with forty men and a case of dynamite, and what stopped him wasn't our rifles — we barely have any. What stopped him was knowing that if he blew that weir, the documents proving his own signature on the forgeries go to a federal review board whether he likes it or not. Those papers are the wall."

"They're a wall nobody can see," Sera said. "That's my point. Right now they're oilskin bundles in a root cellar under Odessa's floor, and the only people who know they exist are us, the elders, and a judge eighty miles downriver who owes Meridian more money than this whole valley is worth." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I know. I've thought about Harrow. I haven't forgotten Fort Amity."

"Then don't do what it sounds like you're about to do."

"Judge Harrow issued the stay because four hundred people were standing in his square listening to my father's journal while the storm tried to blow the roof off his courthouse. That was fear, Quentin. Good fear, useful fear — but fear doesn't last. Fear evaporates the moment the rain stops. And the rain has stopped." She gestured upstream, toward the gorge, toward the drowned wreckage of Crane's stranded launch still visible at low water on the shingle bar. "Crane pulled back, but he didn't quit. Men like him don't quit. He's gone downriver to telegraph his directors, and his directors have lawyers, and the lawyers have time. The stay Harrow signed is paper. Meridian eats paper for breakfast. In six months — maybe less — there'll be a federal surveyor with a Company man riding beside him, and a new set of deeds with new signatures, and this valley will be a reservoir with a fence around it, and everything we've done here will be a story the Riverkin tell their children about the year the water almost stayed theirs."

"So what's your answer? Burn the papers too?" His voice had an edge on it, and she recognized the edge — it was the same one he got whenever the conversation bent toward staying anywhere, caring about anything, letting the world get a grip on him. "Throw them in the river with the map and call it justice?"

"No. Take them somewhere they can't be eaten."

"Where?"

"Fort Amity." She let it sit between them. "Not to Harrow. Past him. The circuit judge rides through Fort Amity twice a year — Judge Okafor, out of the territorial capital. She has no debts here, no history here, no reason to love or hate any of us. She'll hold court in the fort square on the first week of the dry season. Sixteen days from now, maybe seventeen." Sera had counted. She'd counted every day since the public reading, turning the idea over in the dark of the elder-house like a stone in her pocket, wearing its edges smooth. "We take the journal and the deed copies downriver ourselves, through the flood, and we put them directly into her hands. Not a petition. Not a letter that can be lost. Hands. Hers."

For a long moment Quentin said nothing. He walked to the edge of the bank and stood looking down at the water as though it might advise him.

"You've seen the river since the monsoon peaked," he said finally. "You were on deck when we took the supply skiff up to the north weir. You saw what the gorge looks like now."

"I saw."

"Then say it back to me. Say what running the gorge means right now."

"It means the channel's moved thirty yards east of where any chart puts it. It means the standing waves over the sunken barges are running twelve feet and folding back on themselves. It means the current through the narrows is faster than the Marguerite can make headway against if the wind comes upriver, and the wind comes upriver every afternoon." She recited it flatly, the way he'd taught her, the way he read water aloud to himself at night like scripture. "It means we could die."

"And that's just the river." He turned to face her, and the grease smear on his cheekbone made him look suddenly young and furious. "Now add the Company. Crane knows exactly what we're carrying — he's known since the weir. He couldn't take it from us up here because the valley watches every approach and his crews are demoralized and half of them think the flood was God taking sides. So what does he do? He goes home. He waits. And he knows — because men like him always know — that sooner or later the papers have to travel. There are exactly two ways those documents reach anyone who matters: by post, which he can intercept, or by boat, which he can sink. We would be carrying the single most dangerous cargo on this river through sixty miles of water he controls, past two checkpoints he staffs, into the one port town where he owns the judge, the harbormaster, and probably the church bell if it suited him." He shook his head slowly. "It's not a plan, Sera. It's a trap we'd be building for ourselves and then walking into it voluntarily, with the evidence in our arms."

"Or," Sera said, "it's the only door out of this valley that leads anywhere, and every day we don't walk through it, it gets narrower."

* * *

They argued for another hour. She gave him that much — she owed him that much, because she understood what she was asking.

She understood it in her body, actually, better than she could say it in words. Because Quentin Ashgrove had built his entire life into a shape designed to avoid exactly this: the stopping, the belonging, the wagering of something irreplaceable on something uncertain. He'd told her about his brother on the third night below the falls, when the engine lay open between them like a patient on a table and the stars came down close enough to touch. A brother he'd left behind in a river town because staying felt like drowning, and who was dead within the year, and whose name Quentin had not spoken aloud again for twenty years until he spoke it to her in the dark with his hands still black with grease. *Never stop, never belong.* It wasn't a creed. She saw that now. It was a scar shaped like a philosophy.

And she was asking him to run the worst water of the season, through hostile territory, carrying the thing that mattered most — not because the valley needed him to, but because she believed it was right. She was asking him to care out loud, in front of the river and the Company and God, in the one direction where caring could cost him everything.

So she argued gently, and she argued hard, and she used every tool she had.

She used the elders: "Odessa says the valley can't hold alone forever. The stay bought us a season, not a future. Ask her yourself — she told me last night the council is divided, and some of them want to melt into the hills again, scatter the way they scattered twenty-two years ago, and she's afraid that if they scatter this time they won't come back. The papers going to a real judge is the difference between a story and a verdict."

She used the law: "Harrow's stay is a bandage on a wound that needs stitches. Okafor can order a federal title review — a real one, with surveyors who don't sign for Meridian. Once that review opens, every forged deed Crane ever touched comes under a lamp. Not just ours. All of them. Every valley, every removal, every family. My father's journal stops being one dead prospector's grudge and becomes evidence in the largest land fraud case this territory has ever seen."

And finally — because he was silent too long, staring at the middle distance with the look of a man doing arithmetic he hates — she used the truth.

"This isn't really about the river, is it," she said quietly. "Or Crane. Or even the risk. You ran worse water than this before I ever met you, and you'll tell me so yourself if I wait long enough. This is about the fact that if we go downriver together, you're not ferrying a passenger anymore. There's no delivery at the end of it. No packet to hand off, no promise fulfilled, no dock where you drop me and steam away clean. If we do this, we're in it together, all the way to whatever's at the bottom of it, and you've spent your whole life making sure there was always a dock." She stepped closer, close enough to see the pulse move in his throat. "I'm asking you to burn the docks, Quentin. All of them. Mine included."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion around the eyes, and she hated herself a little for it and loved herself a little more for not taking it back.

"That's not fair," he said.

"No," she agreed. "None of this is fair. That's rather the point of the whole affair."

* * *

He didn't answer her that morning. He walked back to the Marguerite and disappeared into the machinery space, and the sound of the wrench started up again, rhythmic and furious, and Sera stood on the bank in the strengthening sun and let him have his silence, because she had learned that about him too: his silences weren't refusals. They were rivers finding their course.

The day went on. She hauled stone. She sat with the elders through the midday meal and listened to Odessa's oldest sister describe, for the fourth time, the wedding feast that had been planned on this very terrace the summer the Company came, and how the bride had worn a dress woven from river-silk, and how the dress had burned with everything else at the ferry landing, and how the bride had survived and lived to be ninety and was buried up on the ridge facing the water. Sera listened and carried stones and did not mention Fort Amity, because the elders' blessing would matter, but it would not decide anything. One person's decision hung over this valley like the weather, and he was currently lying under a paddle steamer swearing at a bearing race.

At dusk she found him on the roof of the pilothouse, sitting with his back against the chimney, watching the light die on the western peaks. She climbed the ladder and sat beside him without asking. The tin was still warm from the day's sun. Below them the camp fires were lighting one by one, and the smell of woodsmoke and cooking fish rose up soft as an apology.

"My brother's name was Daniel," Quentin said, to the mountains, not to her.

She kept still. She had learned that about him too.

"He wanted to stay places. Couldn't understand why I couldn't. Used to tell me I was going to wake up one day fifty miles downstream from my own life and not remember how to row back." A dry sound that was almost a laugh. "Smart kid. Annoyingly smart." He was quiet for a while. The first star came out over the ridge, then three more, quick as sparks off a grinder. "When you said burn the docks — I've been sitting up here trying to work out whether that terrified me because I'd lose everything, or because I wouldn't."

Sera turned to look at him. In the last blue light his face was all planes and shadow, and older than she'd ever allowed herself to see, and more frightened than he would ever admit, and more resolved than she had dared hope.

"Sixteen days," she said. "Okafor holds court on the sixteenth, if the roads are passable, which they won't be, which means the seventeenth. The Marguerite can make Fort Amity in four days if the gorge gives us a daylight window, and Odessa says her nephew reads the upper narrows better than any pilot alive and will run lead for us in a dugout if we ask. That leaves us twelve days to prepare the boat, copy the documents a second time in a second hiding place, and convince a council of elders to let their last hope leave the valley in the hands of a steamboat man and a salvage-yard girl." She paused. "And we leave before dawn on the ninth, because the river's lowest at dawn and Crane's patrols change shift at midnight."

Quentin was quiet so long she thought he hadn't heard. Then he reached inside his coat and took out the little brass compass he wore on a lanyard — the one thing she'd never seen him without, the needle that always pointed downriver, always pointed away — and held it out to her on his open palm.

"Steer us out, then," he said. "First watch is yours."

The compass lay in his hand between them, its needle trembling, swinging once, twice — and then settling, for the first time since she'd known him, pointing upriver. Toward the falls. Toward the source.

Toward home, said a voice in her that sounded terribly like her father's.

Down below, at the edge of the firelight, a boy from the camp came running along the bank waving both arms, shouting something she couldn't yet hear — and even before the words reached them, even before the word *gunboat* resolved out of the wind off the water, Sera felt the whole fragile plan tilt beneath her like a deck in a swell.
