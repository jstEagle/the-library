## 32. Downriver in the Storm

The rain came at them sideways before they cleared the valley mouth, and Quentin understood within the first mile that the river he had known all his life had been replaced overnight by something with no memory of him.

He stood at the wheel of the *Marguerite* with both hands locked on spokes gone slick as fish skin, reading water that refused to be read. The channel markers were gone — every one of them, swept or drowned — and the banks he'd steered by for twenty years had dissolved into a brown immensity that stretched from hill to hill like a second sea. Trees rode past in flotillas, roots skyward, their branches clawing at the current as if trying to climb back onto land. Somewhere beneath all that violence lay the shoals of Marrow Bend, the narrows at Stone Latch, the drowned fence posts of farms that would never be farmed again.

"Tell me you know where we are," Sera shouted from beside him. She was lashed to the rail with a length of cargo rope she'd tied herself, her oilskin hood streaming, her eyes fixed on the chaos ahead with a stubbornness he was beginning to recognize as inheritance. Vane's jaw. Vane's refusal to blink at weather.

"I know where we are," he said. "I just don't know where the river is."

Lightning split the sky off the western ridge and the thunder arrived almost instantly, close enough to feel in the deck plates. In the white after-flash he saw it: a whole orchard coming down the current, forty apple trees still chained together by their shared earth, tumbling end over end like something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

"Hard to starboard," he said, more to himself than to her, and threw his weight against the wheel.

* * *

They had left the valley at first light, when Odessa's people carried their packs down through the terraces to the hidden landing below the falls, and the elders had stood in the rain watching them go without waving. Odessa had pressed her forehead briefly to Sera's — some old Riverkin gesture Quentin didn't pretend to understand — and then said to him, quietly, in English flattened by years of disuse:

"The river will not forgive carelessness this week."

"No," he'd agreed. "It rarely does. This week least of all."

"You're taking our truth downriver in a tin boat." Her dark eyes had held his without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Bring it back true."

Now, six hours later, the truth was wrapped in oilskin inside Sera's coat, bound against her body the way a mother binds an infant, and the tin boat was fighting for its life.

The *Marguerite* was built for rivers, not for this. She was sixty feet of shallow-drafted stubbornness, a stern-wheeler Quentin had rebuilt twice and re-caulked three times, and she had never once been asked to run flooded water at full throttle through country this broken. Her paddles bit into water thick with silt and debris; every third revolution something struck the guards — a log, a fence rail, once what might have been a door, torn from somebody's house, spinning slowly downstream with its latch still turned.

Quentin had made a living out of never stopping. He had made a creed out of it, carved it into himself the way the Riverkin carved names into stone: keep moving, owe nothing, belong nowhere. And now here he was, stopped in every way that mattered except the one the river cared about, running a dead man's errand down a drowned river because a woman with her father's jaw had looked at him three nights ago across a lamplit table and said, *If we don't take it to Harrow, nobody ever will.*

He hadn't argued long. That was the thing that kept surprising him. Twenty years of arguing with everyone about everything, and with her he ran out of argument in a single evening.

"Quentin!" Sera's voice cut through the rain. "Ahead — bridge!"

He saw it in the next lightning flash and felt his stomach drop through the deck. The trestle at Cooper's Crossing had stood thirty feet above low water for as long as there had been a river. Now the flood had climbed to within eight feet of its deck, and the middle span was gone — collapsed, dragged under, only two black stumps and a snarl of trusswork marking where the channel used to be. The current funneled toward the gap like water down a drain.

"There's no room," Sera said.

"There's room."

"That gap is narrower than we are—"

"It's wider than it looks." It wasn't. "Get below. Tie yourself to the engine housing."

"I'm staying right here."

"Sera—"

"You'll need eyes aft," she said, "and I've got better ones than your deckhand, who is currently praying in the coal bin." And she planted herself at the stern rail, rope straining, and began calling depths to him over the storm like she'd been born to it.

Later — much later, in a dry room in Fort Amity with rain still hammering the shutters — he would try to remember the passage of Cooper's Crossing and find that memory had refused the job entirely. What remained were fragments: the roar of the gap filling the whole world. Sera's voice, steady as a surveyor's, calling *starboard, starboard, NOW*. The shriek of green timber scraping along the hull somewhere below the waterline, a sound like a giant dragging its fingernails down a chalkboard. The wheel kicking in his hands hard enough to tear skin. And then open water on the far side, gray and furious but alive, and the *Marguerite* wallowing upright, listing slightly to port, still moving.

He discovered he was laughing. He couldn't stop. It came out of him in great ragged gusts, half hysteria, and Sera untied herself and crossed the tilting deck and took the wheel beside him and laughed too, until the laughter turned into something else, something quieter, and they stood shoulder to shoulder steering through the ruin of the world while the rain came down like judgment.

* * *

They put in at dusk behind Widow's Island, in the one pocket of slack water between the main channel and the drowned willow flats. Quentin let the anchor chain run until it groaned, then killed the engine, and the silence that followed was so total it rang.

Not silence, exactly. The river was still roaring past the island's upstream point, a continuous freight-train sound. But compared to what they'd been inside of for twelve hours, it was silence, and it fell on the boat like a blessing.

Sera went below to check the bilge. Quentin stayed at the wheel a while longer, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all day. They had maybe forty miles to Fort Amity. At flood speed with the current helping, that was four hours of running — four hours of daylight, in water full of debris, past banks where anything could be waiting. Meridian knew they'd gone downriver; Tomas's warning had reached them the night before they left, delivered by a Riverkin runner who'd swum the cold side channels. Crane wouldn't have boats on this water. No boat Company men trusted could live in this. But Crane had patience the way other men had lungs, and there would be telegraph lines still standing, and Fort Amity was a Company town in everything but charter.

Forty miles. Four hours. One chance.

Below, he heard Sera swearing softly at the bilge pump, then the pump catching, then quiet. He went down the companionway into lamplight and the smell of wet iron and coal, and found her sitting on the engine housing with her boots off, her feet blistered raw from hours braced against the deck, wringing river water out of her braid.

"How bad?" he asked.

"We're taking water around the shaft packing. Slow. The pump's keeping up." She looked up at him, and in the yellow lamplight her face was hollowed with exhaustion and lit with something underneath it that hadn't burned out yet. "You got us through Cooper's Crossing."

"The river got us through. I just held on."

"Don't do that." Her voice sharpened. "Don't give it away. I watched you read that water, Quentin Ashgrove. You read it like my father read rock — like you could see the story underneath. Nobody holds on through a gap like that. You steered."

He crouched by the bilge, checked the packing nut, gave it a quarter turn, and found he couldn't look at her while he did it. There was a confession sitting in his chest that had been sitting there since the valley, since the night fishing below the falls, since longer than he wanted to count, and the storm had stripped away every excuse he usually used to keep it buried.

"My brother drowned in water like today's," he said.

The pump thumped. Rain drummed the deck above them. Sera went very still.

"Tommy. Younger by two years. We were running the shallows above Redding Bar, spring flood, same as this — smaller river, but the same animal. I was at the wheel. He was on the bow pole, sounding." Quentin turned the wrench another quarter turn, though it needed nothing. "A snag came out of the cut bank and took him off. Clean off, no sound, no splash I even heard over the engine. By the time I backed down he was a hundred yards behind us and going under for the third time, and the water was so fast I couldn't hold position, and every second I tried, we lost more ground." He set the wrench down. His hands were steady. They always were; that was the joke of it. "I ran the boat aground a half mile down and walked back along the bank all night calling his name. They found him three days later at the mouth of Miller's Creek."

Sera said nothing. She had learned, somewhere in the salvage yards of Port Calloway, when to let silence do the work.

"After that I made myself a rule. Keep moving. Never stop for anyone, never tie up anywhere, never owe anybody enough that leaving costs something. Because stopping is where people drown. Stopping is where you're standing still holding a rope that goes under." He finally looked at her. "I told myself that rule was wisdom for twenty years. It was just fear wearing wisdom's coat."

"And now?"

"Now I'm carrying a dead man's journal to a bought judge through a flood, in love with his daughter, and I can't remember why the rule ever seemed like a good idea."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft neither of them had felt arrive. Sera sat on the engine housing with her ruined feet and her father's letters bound against her ribs, and she looked at him the way she'd looked at the source valley when the rain lifted — awe braided with fury, fury mostly at herself for feeling the awe.

"Took you long enough," she said.

Then she slid off the housing, crossed the engine room, and kissed him, and it tasted of river water and coal smoke and salt that might have been rain, and outside the hull the flood roared on indifferent, carrying trees and fences and the wreckage of ordinary lives toward the sea, while inside the *Marguerite* two people who had spent their whole lives moving away from things finally, briefly, arrived.

* * *

They ran the last forty miles at first light, and it was the worst water of the day.

The storm had eased to a hard steady rain, but the river was still rising — fed now by the whole watershed upstream, every creek and coulee pouring its share into the main stem. Twice Quentin smelled mud before he saw it and swung wide around fresh slides that had taken entire sections of bank into the current. Once, near the old ferry landing at Harkness Point, they passed a house floating intact, roof ridgeline just breaking the surface, a rocking chair visible through a window, and Sera turned her face away and didn't speak for an hour.

At the mouth of Miller's Creek — he noted it without choosing to, the way a scar notes weather — the current split around a submerged island and formed a standing wave taller than the pilothouse. He took the northern channel against every instinct, because the southern had a debris dam building at its head, and for ten minutes the *Marguerite* climbed gray water at angles that sent every loose object on the boat sliding, and came down the far side smoking, literally smoking, the friction of the shaft packing heating the bilge until the pump ran brown.

And then, with the rain thinning and the hills opening out, Fort Amity appeared through the murk on its bluff above the river — flag drooping, chimneys smoking, the circuit courthouse squat and gray at the center of town like a toad on a stone. A Company town in everything but charter. A town where Judge Ambrose Harrow owed more money than his robes were worth.

Sera came up the ladder and stood beside him at the wheel as the town grew. She had the journal wrapped in fresh oilskin now, triple-bound, and she touched it once through her coat the way a gambler touches a card.

"He'll stall," she said. Not a question. She'd done her own arithmetic.

"He'll stall," Quentin agreed. "Harrow stalls the way other men breathe. But a stall means he listened. A stall means he's afraid — and a man who's afraid has something to lose, and a man with something to lose can be moved."

"And if Crane gets to him first?"

"Then we do what you did in the square at Port Calloway." He glanced at her. "We make the town listen instead."

The channel opened. The flood gentled as the valley widened to receive it, and the *Marguerite* came off the wild water and into the long slow reach below the fort, her paddles slapping a rhythm almost like calm. Quentin brought her alongside the public wharf — the one berth in forty miles no Company lease controlled — and threw the lines himself, and the moment the hawsers went taut and the engine died, he felt it again, that old alarm bell, the one that had rung for twenty years whenever he stopped anywhere: *you're moored, you're moored, you're caught.*

He let it ring. He stepped onto the planks and held out his hand to help Sera down, and she took it, and together they walked up the wharf road toward the courthouse through the thinning rain, two figures soaked to the bone carrying a dead prospector's truth in an oilskin packet, while behind them, at the far end of the levee, a telegraph clerk was already hurrying out of the Company office with a message flapping in his hand, headed for the courthouse steps at a pace that meant someone downriver had gotten word out first.
