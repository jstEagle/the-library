## 34. The Public Reading

The rain came to Fort Amity an hour before Sera did.

It arrived the way the old river pilots said a monsoon always arrived in the interior — not as weather but as a decision, a wall of grey moving up the valley with the deliberation of something that had already made up its mind. The first drops hit the fort's parade ground like thrown stones, raising little craters of dust that vanished as fast as they appeared. Then the sky simply opened, and the world became water and noise.

Sera Vane stood under the eave of the assay office across from the fort square, her father's journal wrapped in oilskin against her chest, and watched the crowd scatter.

That was the thing she would remember afterward — how fast they scattered. The market stalls collapsed their awnings in practiced motions, vendors sweeping goods into canvas with the economy of people who had done this every season of their lives. A mule train clattered for the covered arcade by the commissary. Within ten minutes the great dirt square, which at midday had held three hundred souls and smelled of tar and frying fish and horse sweat, was empty except for rain hammering mud into soup and the flag over the garrison snapping like something trying to tear itself free.

"Nobody gathers in this," Quentin said beside her. He had come out of the downpour without seeming to get wet — or rather, he was soaked through and wore it the way he wore everything, as if it were a choice he'd made and didn't regret. His hair was plastered flat; his eyes went from the empty square to her face and back. "You petition the judge tomorrow. When it clears. Harrow will see you in chambers—"

"Harrow will see us in chambers," Sera said, "and hear us in chambers, and file us in chambers, and we'll still be standing there when Crane's crews come down off the mountain." She turned to look at him. Rain ran off the eave between them in a beaded curtain, and through it his face was pale and set. "You said it yourself on the river. The law here is rented. You don't take a rented thing home and ask it nicely."

"I said that to talk you *out* of coming downriver."

"I know." She almost smiled. "It talked me into this instead."

Tomas came around the corner of the assay office with Odessa behind him, both of them carrying lanterns even though it was barely four o'clock and the day had gone the color of dusk. The old deckhand's shoulders were hunched against the rain, but his eyes, when they found hers, were steady in a way they hadn't been since the weir — since he'd stood in front of all of them with Crane's signal lantern burning a hole in his hands and confessed what he'd done, and what the Company did to witnesses, and why he'd turned the light the other way.

"They're saying the bridge road's washing out at Miller's Cut," Tomas reported. "Telegraph line's down past the second relay. Whatever happens here tonight, nobody outside this valley's going to hear about it till the water drops."

Sera felt Quentin go very still beside her. She understood why. That was the arithmetic of it, laid bare: whatever happened tonight would happen inside a bowl of rain, unrecorded, unwitnessed — unless the people in the square themselves became the record.

"That cuts both ways," Odessa said quietly. She was looking at the square too, at the flooded emptiness of it, and her voice carried the flat calm of a woman who had spent her life reading water. "Crane's men can't move either. Not tonight. Not tomorrow, maybe. The river owns the roads now."

"So it's ours," Sera said. "For one night, the square is ours."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. Rain drummed on the tin roof above them, so loud it was like standing inside a drum. She watched him do the thing he always did — run the shoals, sound the depths, calculate the cost of every channel — and she watched him arrive, as he had been arriving for weeks now, somewhere new.

"You'll freeze," he said finally. "Standing in that. All of you."

"Then bring blankets," Sera said. "Bring the Marguerite's lamps. Bring everything we have, Captain, because I'm only going to say this once, and after tonight no one in this territory gets to say they weren't told."

* * *

They gathered slowly, the way embers catch.

It began with the ferry families — the ones who lived year-round on the levee below the fort, whose barges and skiffs were already hauled up and lashed against the flood. They came because Odessa went to them, going door to door along the stilt row with her lantern held high, saying only: *A woman is going to read names in the square. Names of the removed.* The Riverkin did not need more explanation than that. They came wrapped in oiled canvas, old men and women with faces like river stone, and they stood where the market cross had been until the Company pulled it down, and they waited.

Then the others came, drawn by the strangeness of lights in an empty square in a storm. A woman who kept the boarding house brought her two boys because she could not leave them and would not miss whatever this was. Dock workers off the Meridian wharf — off-duty, some of them, and some, Sera noticed, who should have been on. A surveyor's assistant with ink stains on his fingers who stood at the edge of the lamplight and did not come closer. The blacksmith. The postmaster. A Chinaman who ran the laundry on Creek Street and who, when the reading began, closed his eyes and moved his lips along with certain passages as though he had heard this story before in another language, another country, another lifetime.

By full dark there were perhaps two hundred people in the square, standing in the rain because there was nowhere else large enough, and the rain had stopped being something that scattered people and become something that bound them — a shared ordeal, a common enemy overhead.

Quentin rigged the Marguerite's running lamps on poles dragged from the commissary fence, and their light made a rough circle in the downpour, gold and trembling. He stood at the edge of it with his arms crossed, watching the dark streets the way he watched narrows at flood stage, and Sera knew he was counting exits, counting silhouettes, listening for gunboats that couldn't possibly reach them tonight but might have eyes here anyway. Fort Amity was a Company town down to its foundations. There would be men in that crowd reporting everything they heard before the rain stopped.

She found she didn't care. That was the strangest gift of the whole terrible season — that somewhere between the salvage yard and the source valley, between the unopened packet and the burned map, she had lost the talent for caring what the Company thought of her.

She climbed onto the back of the flatbed wagon Quentin had borrowed, opened the oilskin, and began.

* * *

"My name is Sera Vane," she said, and her voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second, "and my father was Elias Vane, prospector, surveyor, and — for the last twenty years — a liar. Not to strangers. To me. He let me believe he was dead rather than tell me what he'd seen. This book is what he saw. It's in his hand. I never met the man who wrote it, and I read it like a stranger, and I'm going to read it to you now, all of it, because the man who wrote it asked me to carry it to the people it belongs to. I think that's you."

She opened the journal. The pages were soft as cloth from handling, warped by river damp, and the lamplight made his handwriting swim — that tight, careful engineer's script, letters like driven stakes.

"*June the ninth,*" she read. "*Came down from the divide at last light and saw the headwater basin entire for the first time. Terraces to the waterline. Stone weirs older than anything the Company has ever built, and the Company's own maps show this valley as vacant land. Vacant. Whoever drew those maps has never been here, or has been here and wished very hard otherwise.*"

Rain hammered the wagon bed. Somewhere in the crowd a child began to fuss and was hushed, and then, remarkably, was not hushed — the woman holding him simply shifted him to her other hip and stood listening, and the child listened too, wide-eyed at the lamps.

"*July the second. Met a man on the lower terraces fishing with a woven trap. Riverkin. He showed me the weir gates and how they read the water — told me his grandmother's grandmother helped lay these stones, and that the stones are the deed. I laughed and said paper is the deed. He looked at me the way you look at a man who has fallen in the river and doesn't know it yet.*"

A sound went through the crowd — not laughter, something quieter and harder, a collective exhale, and Sera saw old heads nodding among the Riverkin, saw a woman near the front close her eyes.

"*August the nineteenth. Crane came through camp today with the removal schedule. I asked him what schedule. He smiled at me like a partner and said the upper valley was being consolidated for development and the occupants were being compensated. I have seen the compensation ledger. It lists payments to families by name. I have also seen the families. They are living on the far bank at the mouth of the gorge with nothing, and the names in that ledger are names of the dead, or names invented whole, and every page carries Aldous Crane's signature and the territorial seal, and the seal is real and the signatures are real and none of it is true.*"

She paused. Her throat had closed. In the lamplight she could see Quentin's face at the edge of the circle, and behind him Tomas's, and Tomas was crying — openly, without shame, rain and tears indistinguishable down the seamed brown landscape of his cheeks — and she understood that for him these were not revelations but confessions being read back to him in public, and that he had chosen to stand in the light and hear them anyway.

She read on.

"*September third. My partners want to sell the find to Meridian and be done. I keep telling them about the ledger. They say ledgers can be fixed, deeds can be fixed, everything can be fixed if the price is right, and I say that's exactly the problem. Tonight they drew lots for who goes to Port Calloway to negotiate. I lost the lot and I have never been so glad to lose anything in my life.*"

Her voice caught on the next line and she had to stop, one hand flat on the journal, breathing. The crowd waited. The rain waited. Even the flag over the garrison seemed to stop snapping.

"*September eleventh. They are dead. Both of them. Found at the landing with the boat burned and the survey case gone. The Company man who told me said river pirates. There are no river pirates on this stretch. There never were. I am going to copy everything and hide the copies apart, and then I am going to disappear, because a man who knows what I know does not live long enough to testify. God forgive me. I am leaving my daughter nothing but a story she won't believe.*"

Sera looked up. Water ran off her chin. Two hundred faces looked back at her through the rain, and she said, not from the journal now, just plainly: "He was wrong about one thing. I believed it."

* * *

She read for two hours.

She read the survey notes and the measurements of the weirs, and the Riverkin elders murmured at the numbers like men hearing their own prayers recited in a foreign accent. She read the list of ledger names, all forty-one of them, slowly, letting each one stand alone in the air — *Marisol Ferro. Anselm Ferro. The twins, Ferro. Old Teodor. Teodor's daughter-in-law. The boy called Little Teodor* — and at the fourth name a woman in the front row made a sound like a branch breaking and was held up by the people on either side of her, and Sera realized with a jolt that went through her like cold water that some of these names belonged to people standing in this square, that the forged compensation ledger had paid dead relations of the living listeners, and that she was not telling these people their history. She was confirming it.

And that changed the square. She felt it turn, the way she'd felt the Marguerite turn in the flooded gorge — a slow gathering of force beneath her, something enormous deciding on a direction.

When she finished the ledger names, a dock worker called out from the middle of the crowd, a big man with a Company whistle still on a lanyard around his neck: "My brother's name is in that book. Anselm Ferro. They paid eleven dollars for my brother." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible part. It stayed level, conversational, the way men speak when they have passed through anger into something with longer legs. "Eleven dollars, and I loaded freight for them eleven years."

"The boarding house on Levee Street," the landlady said. "Company bought it in '81. Papers said it was abandoned. My husband died in it."

"Creek Street," said the laundryman, softly, in accented English. "Whole street. Paper says swamp."

Name after name after name. The square filled with them the way the gorge fills in flood — not all at once but everywhere at once, from every side, stories rising off the crowd like spray. Removals, buyouts, burnings, a ferry landing put to the torch, a village relocated to ground that flooded every spring because the survey that certified it dry had been signed by a man who had never walked it. And through it all the rain fell harder, as if the sky itself had joined the testimony, and the lamplight shook, and nobody left.

Sera stood on the wagon with the journal dripping in her hands and understood that something had happened here that could not be unhappened. Her father had written this book alone in camps and culverts, hiding copies apart like a man burying seed corn against famine, believing truth was a thing you smuggled. But truth wasn't cargo. It was water. It wanted downhill. All it had ever needed was a channel, and tonight, in a drowned square in a Company town, it had found one.

Quentin came to the wagon wheel and put his hand on it. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She could read him now the way he read water — the set of his shoulders had changed, the watchfulness gone from narrow calculation to something wider, something that included the crowd, the square, the future. The man who had built a life on never stopping, never belonging, was standing in the middle of a belonging so dense you could have moored a boat to it.

"There's a judge in that fort," she said, and her voice carried now without effort, borne on the hush. "Judge Harrow. Tomorrow morning I'm going to walk into his chambers with this book and the deed copies and ask him to stay every Meridian title in this territory pending federal review. Some of you are thinking that's a fool's errand, because Harrow banks with Meridian. I've heard it too." She let that sit. "So I'm asking you not to send me alone. Come stand in the square outside his window while I do it. Bring this book's brothers — your papers, your receipts, your grandmothers' names. Let him look out and see what his signature is worth."

For a moment, nothing. Rain on tin roofs, rain on canvas, rain on the river beyond the levee, going about its enormous business.

Then the ferry families began to move — not toward her, but toward each other, gathering at the edge of the lamplight, the old men speaking quickly in their own tongue, and Odessa among them translating, organizing, her lantern raised. The dock worker with the whistle pushed forward. "I'll stand," he said. "Me and anyone else off the wharf who's got a spine." Hands went up along the crowd like a tide coming in — dozens, then scores, then it stopped being countable.

And that was when Sera saw the two men at the corner of the commissary, under the eave, half in shadow. They weren't listening anymore. One had a notebook open against his chest, writing fast, sheltering the page with his body. The other had turned away and was walking quickly into the dark toward the Company offices on the hill, coat collar up, and even at that distance, even through rain, even in the bad light, Sera knew the walk — the precise, impatient stride of a man who measured everything, including other men, including her.

Aldous Crane had not waited for the storm to pass. He had come down off the mountain ahead of his crews, ahead of his own flood, and he had been standing in her audience.

Quentin had seen him too. She felt it rather than saw it — the way the whole crowd feels the pilot change course. He stepped up beside the wagon, and his voice, low, meant for her alone, was the voice he used in white water:

"He'll go straight to Harrow tonight. Wake him, buy him, whatever it takes. We've got until dawn, maybe less."

Sera closed the journal and held it against her heart, and looked up at the fort on its hill, where a single window had just lit yellow against the rain.

"Then we don't wait for dawn," she said.
