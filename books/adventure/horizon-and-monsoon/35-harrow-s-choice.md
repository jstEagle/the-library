## 35. Harrow's Choice

The rain had been falling on Fort Amity for three days when the ledger finally broke him.

Not the storm — the storm was only weather, and weather had never once moved Judge Ambrose Harrow to do anything but close his shutters and pour a second glass of cane spirit. It was the ledger itself, the small black book bound in calfskin that lived in the bottom drawer of his desk beneath a false layer of case notes, that did the breaking. He had taken it out that morning out of habit, the way a man touches a sore tooth, and he had sat with it open in the grey light while the rain stitched silver seams down his office window, and he had read his own name in it one more time.

*Harrow, A. — Meridian Land & Navigation Co., account current: 4,100 crowns, drawn against future consideration.*

Four thousand one hundred crowns. He knew every crown of it the way he knew the lines of his own face. The first thousand had come eleven years ago, when Meridian bought the timber rights along the lower Vessel and needed a magistrate who could be relied upon to find for survey over squatter. He had told himself then that the squatters were squatters, that law was law, that a man who took money for a lawful ruling was merely being paid what the territory was too poor to give him. The second thousand had come after the removals upriver — the ones nobody in Fort Amity spoke of above a whisper, the Riverkin ferry landing burned, the families scattered like chaff before a fan — and by then he had stopped telling himself anything at all. He simply signed. He was good at signing. It was, he sometimes thought, the only thing he had ever truly mastered: the application of ink to other people's ruin.

He was sixty-one years old. He had a wife buried on the hill behind the fort chapel and no children, which meant no one to disappoint except himself, and he had long since learned to live with that disappointment the way you learn to live with a bad hip — favoring it, planning around it, never quite admitting how much of your life it governed.

Then the girl had come into his chambers two days ago, soaked to the ribs, carrying an oilskin packet and a dead man's journal, and she had looked at him across his own desk with eyes the color of river water in flood, and she had said, "Judge Harrow, I'm asking you to do the one thing this office was made for," and something in his chest had shifted like ballast coming loose in a heavy sea.

He had stalled her. He remembered stalling her — remembered the sound of his own voice saying *these matters require verification, Miss Vane, the records must be examined, due process is not a faucet to be turned* — and she had listened politely, the way you listen to a man reciting a speech he clearly does not believe, and she had thanked him, and she had left, and he had known — known with the cold certainty of a drowning man knowing the surface is getting farther away — that she would not wait. That she would find some other door. And that when she found it, it would not open quietly.

Now, from his window, he could hear the square filling.

* * *

It began, as such things do, with almost nothing. A dozen figures under oilskins at the edge of the parade ground where the market stalls stood empty in the rain. Then thirty. Then the sound carried up to him through the drumming water — not shouting, nothing so useful as shouting, but a low continuous murmur, the sound of a crowd becoming a congregation. By midmorning there were two hundred people in the square, and by noon there were more than he could count from the window, and they were not dispersing when the rain redoubled. They were standing in it. River people, mostly — boatmen and fisherfolk and salvage crews in their yellow slickers, faces streaming, feet planted in six inches of standing water as though the flooded square were simply another stretch of deck.

And at the head of them, on the steps of the old customs house where the eave threw a ragged curtain of runoff, stood Sera Vane, holding a book above her head to keep it dry, reading aloud.

Harrow heard the words in fragments, carried up in pieces between gusts. He did not need the fragments. He had read the journal twice since she'd left it with him — or rather, she had left a copy, having understood even then that documents given to Ambrose Harrow had a way of catching fire — and he knew what the crowd was hearing. The names of the removed families. The dates of the forged signatures. His own court's case numbers, cited with terrible precision, each one a ruling he had rendered in Meridian's favor, listed like beads on a string.

He poured the cane spirit and did not drink it. He watched the glass tremble against the decanter and understood that his hands were shaking, and that the shaking was not fear, or not only fear. It was the specific terror of a man watching the last wall between himself and himself come down.

"Sir?" His clerk, Pell, stood in the doorway, dripping, hat in hand. "They're asking for you. Not — not demanding, sir. Asking. There's a woman here who lost her husband's boat in the removal year, and a family from Stone Latch, and—" Pell swallowed. "There's a lot of them, sir."

"I can see how many of them there are, Pell."

"Yes, sir." The boy hesitated. "There's also a gentleman from the Company arrived an hour ago. Mr. Crane's office sent him. He's waiting in the taproom of the Anchor and asking — politely — whether Your Honor requires any assistance with the crowd."

The words went through Harrow like a blade finding the gap in armor. *Assistance.* Eleven years of assistance, distilled into one courteous offer. He looked at the black ledger on his desk. Four thousand one hundred crowns. He thought about what the man in the taproom was authorized to make it worth if Harrow did the right thing today, and what it would cost him if he didn't — not in crowns, but in the currency Meridian actually traded in, the currency of quiet disappearances and burned annexes and partners who died of misadventure in the headwaters.

He thought about Elias Vane, whom he had never met, dying on a riverboat with his daughter's name as the last word in his mouth.

"Pell," Harrow said, "bring me my robes. And bring me the great seal."

"The — the seal, sir? For what instrument?"

"For whatever I decide to write before I go down those stairs." He closed the ledger. "And Pell — fetch the ledger from my desk. The black one. Bring it to me at the customs house steps."

Pell stared. In nine years of service the boy had never seen the bottom drawer opened, and both of them knew it. "Sir, are you certain—"

"No," Harrow said honestly. "But certainty is for men without ledgers. Go."

* * *

He wrote the order in twenty minutes, in the clear hand he had learned in law school on the coast, back when he had still believed the law was a lighthouse rather than a lantern that could be bought and carried wherever the buyer pleased. It was not elegant. It was not the sweeping judicial manifesto he might have composed at forty. It was four paragraphs of plain language:

*Whereas it has been shown to this court that land titles within the Vessel watershed, held or asserted by the Meridian Land & Navigation Company, rest upon instruments of doubtful provenance; whereas the original records supporting said titles have been destroyed under circumstances warranting investigation; and whereas the public interest demands that no title be perfected, transferred, or enforced upon potentially fraudulent foundations —*

He paused there, pen lifted, rain hammering the roof like applause from a vast indifferent audience.

*— it is therefore ordered that all Meridian titles within the watershed be stayed, absolutely and without exception, pending review by the federal circuit; and that notice of this stay be posted at every landing, crossing, and company office on the river.*

Below the signature line, after a moment's thought, he added a single sentence in his own voice rather than the court's: *The stay issues over the objection of this judge's own prior rulings, which are hereby acknowledged as compromised, and referred with these papers for whatever censure the federal review deems just.*

It was, he knew, professional suicide written in triplicate. Meridian's lawyers would have the stay enjoined within the month; the federal circuit was slow as a glacier and nearly as warm. But a stay was not a verdict. A stay was time — and time, in a monsoon season, on a river, with two hundred witnesses standing in the rain, was the one commodity the Company could not forge.

Pell returned with the robes and the seal and the ledger, and Harrow dressed in front of the window like a man arming for battle, which in every way that mattered he was. The black robe hung looser than it had five years ago. He took the ledger in his left hand and felt its weight — less than four pounds of calfskin and paper, heavy as an anchor.

"Sir," Pell said quietly, "whatever happens down there — my father ran cargo on the Vessel. Before. He never talks about it, but he used to sing the Riverkin work songs in his sleep, and he doesn't anymore." The boy's jaw worked. "I'll post the notices myself. Every landing. Tonight, if the rain lets me."

Harrow looked at him for a long moment. "Your father should hear what I say from the steps," he said. "Bring him."

* * *

The sound of the crowd changed when he came out.

It did not cheer — river people were too canny for cheering a judge — but the murmur dropped away all at once, replaced by that particular attentive silence he had only ever heard in courtrooms, the silence of people deciding whether to believe you. Two hundred faces turned toward the customs house steps through the rain. Sera Vane stepped aside to give him the top step, and he saw that she was exhausted, hoarse, her voice worn down to wire from hours of reading, and that she regarded him with no hope at all — only attention, the way one watches a card about to be turned.

Beside her stood the boat captain, Ashgrove — Harrow recognized him from the ferry registries, a name that appeared on every run upriver and belonged to no community anywhere, a man built entirely of motion — and behind them a knot of others: an old woman with Riverkin tattoos gone blue-grey with age, a deckhand with the ruined hands of a hauler, a young pilot whose bearing marked her as more than crew. Witnesses. The living archive the fire had failed to burn.

Harrow raised the ledger so the crowd could see it.

"This book," he said, and his voice cracked on the first word, and he let it crack, because a steady voice would have been one more forgery, "contains every payment the Meridian Company has made to me in eleven years on this bench. Four thousand one hundred crowns. I am going to read you the total, and then I am going to tear out the pages, and then I am going to tell you why tearing them out is not enough."

A ripple went through the square. Somewhere near the back a man swore softly, reverently.

He read the entries aloud. All of them. Dates, amounts, the cases they purchased, read in the flat grammar of accounting while the rain fell on two hundred upturned faces and no one moved. It took eleven minutes. When he finished, his voice was raw and the ledger was spotted dark with rain, and he tore the pages out — slowly, one by one, letting each torn page fall into the flooded square like a bird coming down — and the crowd made a sound he had never heard from a crowd before, low and collective, half grief and half release, the sound of people recognizing their own story spoken by the man who had profited from silencing it.

"The pages are nothing," he said, when the sound faded. "Paper burns. You know that better than anyone — you've seen what Meridian does with paper. So hear the part that isn't paper." He held up the order, the great seal hanging from it in green wax. "By authority of this court, every Meridian land title in the Vessel watershed is stayed — frozen — pending federal review. No sale. No transfer. No eviction. No dam. If a Company man shows you a deed after today, show him this, and tell him Judge Harrow says the ink on it is still wet."

The cheer, when it came, started small — the old tattooed woman first, a single high cry that might have been a word in the Riverkin tongue — and then it broke over the square like the river over a levee, and Harrow stood in it and felt nothing like triumph. He felt scoured. He felt, for the first time in eleven years, like a man standing in the correct place, and the feeling was so unfamiliar it was almost painful, the way weight is painful to a limb long asleep.

"One thing more." He waited for quiet. "My debts are mine. I will not resign this bench — you need a judge more than Meridian needs one, and the federal review will decide whether I keep it. But the debt itself ends today, publicly, before witnesses." He turned to Pell, who stood at the foot of the steps with his father beside him, a broad grey-bearded boatman with tears cutting channels through the rain on his face. "Pell, write it in the court record: *Ambrose Harrow acknowledges judgment against himself in the amount of four thousand one hundred crowns, owed not to the Meridian Company but to the people of the Vessel watershed, payable in service until discharged.*" He looked out at the crowd. "I don't expect forgiveness bought this cheaply. I expect work. Give me work."

Sera Vane was looking at him with an expression he would carry the rest of his life — not warmth, not yet, maybe never, but a kind of grim appraisal, the look of a woman checking a hull for seaworthiness before trusting it with cargo. She leaned close under the eave's runoff.

"They'll come for you," she said. "Crane won't leave a loose judge on the river. And the stay only holds until someone with bigger paper tears it up."

"Then we'd best make the paper bigger," Harrow said. "Miss Vane — the federal circuit sits at Fort Amity in six weeks. But there's a rider coming through ahead of the storm season, a commissioner out of the capital with authority to freeze corporate charters outright. He lands at—" 

He stopped. Through the thinning edge of the crowd, at the corner by the Anchor and Anchor's sign, a figure in a grey coat had detached itself from the doorway and was walking away at an unhurried pace, and even at that distance, even through rain, Harrow knew the walk. He had seen it across a dozen closing rooms.

"That man in the grey coat," he said quietly. "Miss Vane. Does the name Aldous Crane mean anything to you?"

Sera Vane went very still. Beside her, Captain Ashgrove's hand moved to the knife at his belt, and the old woman's cry rose again behind them, and somewhere upriver, Harrow knew, the monsoon was already reaching for the headwaters like a hand closing.

"He means," Sera said, "that we have less time than you think."

"Then we ride the next mail boat," Harrow said, folding the sealed order into his breast pocket, over his heart, where the ledger used to sit. "Commissioner Aldous arrives at Greywater Landing in four days. And gentlemen of the Meridian Company—" he raised his voice to the emptying square, to the rain, to the river beyond it, "—will find that this judge has stopped taking their calls."

Above the fort, the storm clouds stacked themselves against the horizon like testimony waiting to be read.
