## 36. Crane at the Source

The rain had been falling for three days when Aldous Crane finally saw the valley, and it did not look like anything his maps had promised.

He stood at the bow of the *Meridian Vigilant* with water sheeting off his oilskin hood and watched the gorge walls fall away on either side like a book opening. Below him the river went wide and slow and green, braiding itself around terraces that climbed the slopes in long, patient steps — stone steps, mortared with something older than mortar, furred now with moss and fern and small defiant trees growing straight out of walls that men had laid before any deed in Harrow's courthouse had a signature on it.

His survey notes said *unimproved basin, negligible structures.* His survey notes were in his breast pocket, wrapped in waxed canvas, and they were lying to him. They had been lying to him for eleven years, and he had signed them anyway, and every year the signature had grown a little easier to make until it was nothing at all — just ink, just habit, just the price of a corner office with a window that faced the harbor instead of the river.

"Mr. Crane." Brannick at his shoulder, rain running off the man's jaw. "Weirs ahead. Two of them, spanning the channel. Stone."

"I can see them."

"The pilots say we can't pass loaded. Not in this water."

Crane turned his face up into the rain and let it hit him, because he wanted to feel something and could not decide what. He had gambled the monsoon would not stop him. He had told himself the storm was an ally — that it would keep the valley's defenders huddled under their tarps, that it would wash away the traces of whatever he had to do here, that by the time the skies cleared the headwaters would be Meridian's and the story would be settled the way stories were always settled: by whoever held the paper.

But standing here, looking at what the Riverkin had built — not a camp, not a squatter's shambles, but a civilization written in stone across two hundred years of flood seasons — he felt the first cold thread of a feeling he refused to name.

It looked like the thing his own reports had erased.

* * *

They made camp on the gravel bar below the lower weir, forty men and three boats and enough dynamite to open the hills themselves. Crane walked the perimeter in the grey afternoon light while the crews pitched canvas and cursed the mud, and everywhere he looked the ground contradicted him.

A grinding stone half-buried in silt, its hollow worn smooth as a spoon. A row of foundation posts along the high bank, blackened but still true, still square. Carved rocks up the slope — he climbed to them without deciding to, boots slipping on wet grass — names cut deep into the granite, dozens of names, generations of them, some so old the letters had gone soft-edged as river stones.

And there, low on the third rock from the left, where the moss had been recently scraped away: **E. VANE**.

Crane stood in front of it for a long time.

He remembered Vane. Of course he remembered Vane. A surveyor's memory is supposed to be a professional instrument, and Crane's had never failed him, which was precisely the problem. Elias Vane, junior partner, the one who kept asking questions about the eastern parcels, the one who'd gone upriver with two other men to "verify boundaries" and come back down with a journal full of things that could not be allowed to exist. The fire at the survey office had been meant to solve that. The men on the levee had been meant to solve the rest.

Vane had lived another season after that. Long enough to carry the poison of the truth upriver like a seed. Long enough to have a daughter who was, at this moment, somewhere above these weirs, holding that same truth in her hands.

"Sir?" Brannick again. "The sappers are asking for placement orders."

Crane looked at the weir below him. It was beautiful, in the way old working things are beautiful — no ornament, no waste, every stone placed by someone who understood that the river is not an enemy to be defeated but a debtor to be managed. It had held through a hundred monsoons. It would hold through this one too, unless —

Unless someone broke it.

"Set the charges at the base of the central span," he heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's voice, calm and dry, the voice of a man giving an ordinary order about an ordinary Tuesday. "Breach it clean. We want the channel scoured down to bedrock. Once the pool drops, the upper terrace drains and we can bring the dredges in before the water comes back."

Brannick hesitated. "In this flow? If we breach it now, mid-monsoon—"

"It will be *fine*, Mr. Brannick." And there it was, the old tone, the boardroom tone, the tone that had carried him from a clerkship in Port Calloway to a seat beside the Company directors before his fortieth birthday. "The upper valley will absorb the release. That is what valleys do."

Even as he said it, he knew it was wrong. He had read the gauges at Stone Latch. He knew what nineteen days of rain had already put into these hills, knew the soil was saturated past holding, knew the tributaries were running brown and heavy as molten lead. A breached weir in a monsoon-fed gorge was not an engineering problem. It was a weapon with the safety off.

He gave the order anyway.

Later — much later, in the years when he still had years — he would try to understand why. Ambition was the easy answer and the false one. The truth was smaller and uglier: he gave the order because turning back had become impossible, because eleven years of signatures had built a wall behind him higher than any weir, because a man who has spent his whole life being right cannot stand in the rain in front of carved stones and admit he has been the villain in someone else's careful record since before some of those names' grandchildren were born.

The charges went in at dusk.

* * *

The blast came at 6:40 in the evening, and Aldous Crane felt it in his teeth before he heard it.

The lower weir did not explode so much as exhale — a white column of spray and shattered stone rising out of the darkening channel, then the groan, deep and geological, of two hundred years of masonry letting go all at once. The sound rolled up the valley and came back off the walls twice, three times, like the hills repeating it to each other in disbelief.

Then the water moved.

He had imagined a surge, a wave, something with a shape you could watch and measure. What came down the channel instead was the river itself changing its mind — the whole surface tilting, accelerating, going from green to brown to the color of wet iron in the space of a breath. The released pool met the monsoon current and the two of them became a single animal, and the animal was angry, and the animal was coming downstream toward his camp.

"Get them out!" He was shouting before he knew he was shouting, scrambling down the slope, oilskin flapping. "Get everyone to the boats — no — *away* from the boats — the high ground, the east bank, MOVE!"

The camp dissolved. Men dropped tools and ran. The first boat went over sideways against its mooring line and was gone, swallowed whole, and Crane watched forty thousand dollars of Company equipment turn end over end in brown water and felt nothing about it at all, which frightened him more than the flood did.

The water took the gravel bar in ninety seconds. He knew because he counted, some detached surveyor's part of his mind still counting, still measuring, even as he clawed his way up through wet brush with Brannick hauling his arm. Ninety seconds, and everything they had built on that bar — tents, stores, the powder magazine (empty, thank God, empty) — was simply no longer there. The river closed over it like a mouth.

By full dark, thirty-one of his forty men were accounted for on the east bluff, soaked and coughing and staring down at the water that had nearly been their graves. Nine were not.

Nine men, somewhere in the flooded dark, in a gorge whose walls offered no footing and whose current offered no mercy.

"We need lights," Crane said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Flares. Everything. Get flares burning on the bluff — if any of them are on the water, they need to see where the banks are."

Brannick struck the first flare, and red light bloomed over the gorge, and in its light Crane saw the flood for what it was: not a weapon aimed at Meridian, not an obstacle to a schedule, but a thing alive and indifferent, carrying whole trees past them at the speed of a galloping horse, wearing the valley's terraces like scars.

Somewhere downstream, faint under the roar, a whistle blew. Three short blasts. A riverman's signal.

*Man overboard.*

* * *

The lights came upriver twenty minutes later, and Crane's first thought, seeing them, was that Meridian's second launch had fought through from Stone Latch. Then he saw the boat itself — a battered river steamer riding low and stubborn, wheel churning, a searchlight mounted on her hurricane deck sweeping the flood lines — and he knew her the way you know a face from a wanted poster.

The *Marguerite*.

Quentin Ashgrove had come upstream into a flooded gorge at night, in the worst water of the season, to pull drowning men out of a disaster Crane himself had ordered.

Crane stood on the bluff with rain and flare-light on his face and watched it happen, and something in his chest came apart quietly, without drama, the way wet rope parts.

He watched Ashgrove's deckhands work — big men in slickers, throwing lines with the casual precision of surgeons, hauling half-drowned Company men out of the current two and three at a time. He watched a woman on the deck — Sera Vane, it had to be, she had her father's stubborn jaw even at this distance — wrapping blankets around shivering men whose paychecks bore the seal of the company that had burned her father's partners. He watched a grey-bearded deckhand go into the water himself with a line around his waist to reach a man tangled in a treetop, and come back with him, both of them bleeding, both of them alive.

Three short blasts again. Another man found. The Marguerite's searchlight swung, steadied, held.

Eight men recovered by midnight. The ninth — a young sapper named Pell, nineteen years old, Crane had signed his employment papers personally — was found at dawn, wedged in the wreckage of the lower weir, which had killed him with the same stones it had guarded the valley with for two centuries.

Crane sat on a wet rock above the channel and looked at his hands. They were steady. They were always steady. That was the horror of it; the hands never shook, no matter what they had signed.

"You should get below, sir," Brannick said softly. "You're soaked through."

"Mr. Brannick." Crane did not look up. "When we get back to Port Calloway — if we get back — there are going to be questions. About the breach order. About the gauges I ignored. About Pell."

"Yes, sir."

"I want you to know that I intend to answer them."

Brannick was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on oilskin. Down at the channel, the Marguerite's searchlight swept the flood one more time, patient as a lighthouse, and then began to withdraw upstream toward the valley mouth, taking Meridian's rescued men home to the people they had been sent to dispossess.

"That woman," Brannick said at last. "Vane's daughter. She stood on the deck and watched us pull our own men aboard, and she didn't say one word against any of us. Not one." He paused. "I don't know what to do with that, sir."

Neither did Crane. That was the truth of it. He had built his entire life on knowing what to do — with numbers, with men, with rivers, with inconvenient documents and inconvenient prospectors. And now he sat in the ruins of his own order, watching the consequences float past him in the brown water, and the only clear thought left in his head was the one he had been outrunning for eleven years:

*The deeds are forged. I forged them. And the land was never ours.*

He rose. His knees ached. Somewhere above the weirs, in the lifting pre-dawn grey, the source valley waited with its carved names and its elders and its stone testimony older than any Company charter, and the woman who held the journal was up there right now, deciding what the world would learn.

"Break camp," Aldous Crane said, and his voice, for the first time in his life, sounded tired all the way through. "We're going up. Not with dynamite." He looked once more at the broken weir, at young Pell's blanket-wrapped body laid respectfully on the bluff, at the flood still running wild with his signature on it. "With my survey notes. All of them. The real ones."

Brannick stared at him. "Sir?"

"She'll want proof," Crane said, and started climbing. "God help me — she already has it. But she should hear it from the man who wrote the lie."
