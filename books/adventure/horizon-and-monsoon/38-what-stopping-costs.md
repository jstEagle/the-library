## 38. What Stopping Costs

The rain quit sometime in the night, and Quentin woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath. He lay in his bunk beneath the wheelhouse and listened to the Marguerite settle around him — the hull ticking as the water dropped, the deck beams easing after weeks of strain, the small sounds of a boat that had been through something and was now, cautiously, telling him about it.

He climbed out into a morning rinsed clean. The valley sat under a sky scrubbed to a hard blue, the terraces steaming where the sun found them, the river running brown and fast but no longer furious. The flood had peaked three days ago and begun its long retreat, leaving its signature everywhere: silt on the lower steps of the elders' camp, weed wrapped around the pilings like bandages, a drowned canoe hung in the branches of a fig tree ten feet above the waterline.

And there was the Marguerite herself, aground at the edge of the old ferry landing, listing to port like a tired animal leaning against a fence.

Quentin walked her length with his hands in his pockets, doing what he had done after every hard passage of his life: counting damage. It was a ritual older than grief. You named what was broken, you priced it, and you decided whether the boat — or the man — could pay.

The starboard rub rail was gone entirely, torn away somewhere in the gorge, along with a section of planking he'd have to steam and bend new. The propeller had kissed rock; he could feel the vibration in his teeth just remembering it, and he'd need to haul her to check the shaft. The wheelhouse glass was cracked in a spiderweb from Crane's near miss with the tow cable. The engine — his engine, the one he'd rebuilt twice with his own hands — had taken water above the manifolds during the worst of it and would need a full teardown before she turned over again without complaint.

But she floated. She'd held. In thirty years of rivers, he had never asked more of a boat than he'd asked of this one, and she had answered every time he'd called.

He crouched at the waterline and ran his thumb along a gouge in the planking, deep enough to lodge a fingernail. Riverkin stonework, he realized. A weir stone, ancient and squared, that had scraped her hull when Odessa threaded the flooded gap. Even her wounds here were made of the valley's own bones.

"You're talking to the boat again," Sera said behind him.

"I'm assessing."

"You're talking to the boat." She came down the bank with two tin cups, steam rising off them, and pressed one into his hand. Her hair was still damp from the morning wash at the spring, and she had sawdust on her sleeve already, though the sun had barely cleared the ridge. "Odessa's people pulled three planks out of their stores for you. Old-growth cedar, seasoned twenty years. They wouldn't take payment. Odessa said the boat carried the journal upriver, so the boat has earned her boards."

Quentin looked at the cup, then at her. "I haven't agreed to stay."

"Nobody's asked you to." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "I'm telling you what's happening while you decide."

She left him there with the coffee and the boat and the arithmetic he couldn't finish.

* * *

The trouble with damage, Quentin thought that afternoon, hauling the first of the cedar planks up the ramp with two of Tomas's borrowed shoulders and a block and tackle rigged to a fig tree, is that it forces a man to imagine a future. A moving man never has to. A moving man counts damage the way he counts days — as things to be outrun, repaired cheaply or not at all, patched until the next horizon swallows the problem. There is a freedom in that. There is also, he was beginning to suspect, a kind of cowardice dressed in oilskins.

The ferry landing itself was the thing he kept coming back to. It had been burned once — Tomas had told them about that night, the firelight on the water, the children ferried across in the dark while the Meridian crews stood on the far bank with lanterns — and never rebuilt. For forty years the Riverkin had crossed by canoe, by hidden paths, by whatever means they could keep unmarked. The stone footings were still there under the silt, black with age, square as the day some mason had set them whose name no deed recorded.

Odessa found him at midday standing on those footings, ankle-deep in retreating water, looking at nothing.

"My grandmother landed here," she said, coming down beside him. She was a compact woman with grey in her braids and hands that looked like they had been carved for rope work. "Before the removal. She said the landing was the heart of the river road. Everything came through it — salt, news, brides, coffins. When it burned, the river forgot how to be a road."

"And when it's rebuilt?"

"Then the valley isn't a hiding place anymore. It's a place." She studied him with an expression he'd learned to read in Sera and no one else — the look of someone measuring whether a man's word has weight. "You've been watching the water drop all morning. I can hear you calculating. How long until the Marguerite floats free."

"Four days, if the weather holds. Six if it doesn't."

"And how long until she's sound again?"

Quentin shrugged. "Haul-out, new planking, shaft work, engine teardown. If I had a yard and help — three weeks. Working alone on a beach—" He stopped. He hadn't let himself finish the sentence, because finishing it meant staying three weeks, and three weeks became a season, and a season became—

"Three weeks," Odessa repeated, as if he'd said something else entirely. "The dry season is four months long. The elders are going to rebuild the landing. They voted on it last night, after your judge's stay came upriver. Forty years they waited for paper that said the ground under their feet belonged to them, and now the paper says maybe, and they're not waiting anymore." She picked up a flat stone and skipped it once across the slack water. "They'll need someone who knows boats. Someone who knows this river better than any chart does."

"I don't belong places," Quentin said. It came out flatter than he intended, an old plank worn smooth by handling.

"No," Odessa agreed. "That's what everyone says who does."

* * *

He worked on the engine that evening because working on engines required no decisions. He had her intake manifold off and laid out on a tarp on the foredeck, valves in a row like extracted teeth, when Sera came aboard with a lamp and a ledger book under her arm.

"Inventory," she said. "Odessa's putting me in charge of stores for the rebuild. I'm told you're the authority on what a boat needs."

"I'm told a lot of things lately."

She sat cross-legged beside the manifold and opened the ledger, and for a while they worked in the lamplight — she reading out quantities, he answering, both of them pretending this was administration and not negotiation. Caulk. Fastenings. A length of bronze shaft stock he'd have to send for downriver, which meant someone going downriver, which meant the river road opening again, which meant everything Odessa had said was already true in the future tense.

"The elders offered to feed the crew," Sera said. "All of us. Through the whole rebuild. There's a house by the spring that's been empty since the removal — Odessa says it's yours if you want it. Says nobody's slept in it since her grandfather died."

Quentin set down his wrench.

A house. Not a bunk, not a berth, not a bed rolled out in the wheelhouse because stopping anywhere longer than a tide invited questions. A house with walls that had waited forty years for someone to live in them.

"Sera." He wiped his hands slowly, buying time. "You've known me two months. You've seen me lie to Company men, run a gunboat onto a sandbar, and burn a map in front of a federal judge. What exactly do you think you know about me that makes you comfortable offering me a house?"

"I know you counted the damage this morning," she said. "I watched you do it. You walked her stem to stern with your hands in your pockets, and you touched every wound she has, and then you stood on the old footings for an hour staring at the water like it owed you money." She closed the ledger. "My father did the same thing. Counted instead of choosing. Kept moving because moving meant he never had to find out whether anything would hold him. And I spent my whole childhood learning what that costs the people who wait." She looked at him, and the lamp made her eyes very steady. "I'm not asking you to stay forever, Quentin. I'm asking you to stop pretending the leaving is free."

The river moved past them in the dark, carrying silt and branches and the whole indifferent world down toward the sea. Somewhere upstream an owl called, and the valley took the sound and gave it back softer.

"When my brother died," Quentin heard himself say, "I was eleven miles away making a delivery. If I'd stayed put that winter — if I'd ever once stayed put — I'd have been there. So I made a rule. Never stop, never owe, never belong. Thirty years I kept it, and I told myself it was discipline." He turned the wrench over in his fingers. "It was just the same drowning, slower."

Sera reached over and closed his hand around the wrench, folding his fingers shut. "Then put it down," she said. "The boat will still need you tomorrow."

* * *

He dreamed of the gorge that night, the way he always did after hard water — the green walls, the roar, the moment the Marguerite had gone weightless between two waves and he had understood, with total clarity, that he would rather die holding her wheel than abandon it. But in the dream the gorge opened, the way it had, into the basin, and the basin was full of people on the banks, and they weren't shouting at him to turn back. They were calling depths. *Two fathoms. Steer left of the white stone.* Voices from the shore, guiding him in.

He woke before dawn and went out and stood on the deck in the grey light, and the answer was simply there, the way a channel is there once you've finally seen it — obvious, and invisible for years, and impossible to unsee.

The price of stopping was everything he'd feared: the vulnerability, the debts, the grief you couldn't outrun because you'd finally stood still long enough for it to catch you. He'd known that price since he was nineteen years old. What he'd never let himself learn was what it bought.

It bought mornings when people brought you coffee without being asked. It bought a boat repaired with gifted cedar and a landing raised on stones your own hands helped dress. It bought a woman who read ledgers beside your engine and folded your fingers shut around the tools of your old life. It bought being guided home by voices on the bank.

He got the engine's first valve lapped and seated by full light, and when Odessa came down the path with a crew of six and a stack of hewn posts, he was waiting for them on the footings.

"Tell me what the elders want the landing to carry," he said. "Weight, draft, traffic. If we're rebuilding it, we build it to outlast us."

Odessa smiled like a woman who had won a bet with the river. "Canoes first," she said. "Then steamers. Then grandchildren."

* * *

They worked nine days before the Marguerite floated free.

Quentin ran the rebuild the way he ran rapids — total attention, no wasted motion, every man and woman on the landing knowing their task and their neighbor's. The Riverkin taught him to split cedar the old way, along the grain the wood wanted, and he taught them to caulk a seam tight enough to hold pressure. Tomas hauled stone until his hands bled and then hauled more, saying nothing, paying a debt in the only currency he had. Sera kept the ledger and the schedule and, though neither of them named it, kept him too — appearing at the edge of his exhaustion each evening with food and questions about nothing, anchoring him to the hours.

On the ninth morning, with the new planks bright as bone along her starboard side and the shaft trued and the engine turning over sweet and even, Quentin backed the Marguerite off the mud on her own power and swung her nose to the new landing — posts driven deep, decking laid, a ramp wide enough for two canoes abreast — and tied her off to a bollard he had set himself, in stone he had mixed himself, on a shore he had sworn for thirty years never to need.

The elders came down to watch. Nobody cheered. That wasn't the way of these people. But Odessa's grandmother's sister, a tiny woman nearly blind, walked the length of the new deck with her hand on the rail, and at the end of it she said, "Good wood. This will hold," and Quentin found he had to look at the river for a while before he could speak.

Sera came to stand beside him at the rail.

"So," she said. "What does a moving man do now that he's stopped?"

Quentin looked downriver, where the water ran clear and low in the dry season light, all the way to the horizon he had chased his whole life. Then he looked at the landing behind him — the smoke of the cook fires, the children already using the ramp as a diving platform despite Odessa's shouts, the valley rising green and terraced into the hills.

"He learns the cost," Quentin said. "And pays it."

He was still holding the rail when Odessa came up the ramp at a pace too quick for ceremony, a scrap of paper in her fist, her face arranged around news she clearly didn't want to deliver.

"That boy you sent downriver for shaft stock," she said. "Back already. Ran most of the way." She handed him the note. "There's a Meridian steamer at Stone Latch. Crane's flag on her. And she's not alone — the boy counted six barges behind her, heavy in the water."

Quentin read the note twice. Heavy barges, riding low. Equipment, or men, or both.

He looked at Sera. She was already reaching for the ledger.

"Well," he said, stepping away from the rail and feeling, for the first time in thirty years, no pull at all toward the open water. "Let's go see what we're defending."
