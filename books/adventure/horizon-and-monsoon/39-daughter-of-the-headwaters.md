## 39. Daughter of the Headwaters

The rain had stopped three days ago, but the valley still dripped.

Sera heard it everywhere — from the terraced slopes where water let go of leaf after leaf in slow percussion, from the great stone weirs where the river sorted itself into silver threads and braided itself back together, from the eaves of the longhouse where Odessa's aunts hung drying fish like banners of a country that had never stopped existing, no matter what the paper in Fort Amity had once claimed. The whole basin breathed moisture. It exhaled mist at dawn the way a sleeping animal exhales warmth, and Sera had learned, over these weeks, to read that breath the way she'd once read tide tables over a salvage yard's chain-link fence: not as decoration, but as information. The valley was telling them the dry season was coming. The river would drop. Boats would move again.

Which meant there were things that could not wait for boats.

She climbed the switchback path above the camp before the sun cleared the eastern rim, carrying her father's letter inside her coat, against her sternum, where she had carried it since the night she'd read it three times by lamplight on the *Marguerite*'s deck. The path was old — older than any of them, older than Meridian, older than the forged deeds Judge Harrow had stayed pending federal review. Riverkin feet had cut it, and then decades of rain had polished it, and now her boots found the hollows worn by people who had walked it when it was simply the way home.

The carved rocks stood where Odessa said they would, on the shoulder of the hill where the terraces began, a cluster of granite slabs tilted together like teeth, or like headstones, or — once you knelt among them and put your hand flat against the wet stone — like a guest book. Names. Hundreds of names. Some cut deep with iron tools, some scratched with knives, some pecked with such patience they must have taken days. Lichen had claimed the oldest ones, green-gold script over older script, so that the rock seemed to be writing its own commentary on the people who had written on it first.

She found his name on the third slab, low down, near the roots of a wild fig whose trunk had grown around the stone's edge as if trying to hold it.

**ELIAS VANE** — cut shallow, hurried, the letters leaning as though the boy who cut them had been looking over his shoulder even then. Beneath it, smaller, almost swallowed by lichen: a date. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. He had been fourteen years old.

Fourteen, standing here with a knife, carving himself into the record of a place he believed would always be his. Fourteen, twenty years before the Company men came with their papers and their lanterns and their fire at the ferry landing. Fourteen, twenty-five years before he died on a riverboat deck at dawn with his daughter's name in his mouth.

Sera knelt in the wet grass and did not cry. She had done her crying in the salvage yard, in the dark of the packet's oilskin, in the gorge when the white water tried to take them all. What she felt now was quieter and stranger — something like standing in an empty house and finding, behind the wallpaper, the pencil marks where someone had measured a child's height year after year. Evidence of a life she had never been shown. Her father had been a boy here. He had been *from* somewhere. All those years she'd spent hating him for having no origin, no anchor, nothing to explain why he couldn't stay — and all along the explanation was carved on this hillside, and it had been burned off the face of the valley along with the ferry landing.

"You came up early."

Odessa's voice, behind her. Sera didn't startle anymore; weeks in the valley had taught her that the Riverkin pilot moved the way herons moved, present one moment and arrived the next. Odessa came around the fig tree with a digging stick over one shoulder and a coil of cord at her hip, dressed for work, her grey-streaked hair bound back with red thread.

"I wanted to do it before anyone else was awake," Sera said. "It felt like it should be private."

"Nothing on this hill is private." Odessa said it without unkindness, setting down the digging stick. "That's the whole point of it. You carve your name where everyone who comes after can see you were here. That's how the valley keeps its people." She looked at the slab, at the shallow letters. "I remember him. Barely. He was older than me by some years, but I remember a Vane boy who could read water better than boys twice his size. My grandmother said he'd have made ferryman one day, if the world hadn't ended."

"The world didn't end," Sera said. "It just ended for us."

"For everyone it ends for," Odessa said, "someone else signed the order." She crouched, ran her thumb across the lichen-edged date. "You want to bury the letter beside his name?"

"How did you—"

"Quentin told me. Not the words in it. Just that you carried it, and that you'd know when the time came." Odessa picked up the digging stick and weighed it. "I brought this because I guessed today might be the day. The light's been wrong for three mornings — too clear, too still. Days like this, people come up here."

Sera took the letter out of her coat. The paper had gone soft at the folds, body-warm, faintly smelling of lamp oil and river. She had read it so many times she no longer needed to read it; the sentences lived behind her eyes now. *I was going to send for you when I had something worth sending for. Then I was going to send for you when I had nothing left to lose. Both times I waited too long, because waiting is a kind of cowardice that feels like prudence.* And then the last line, the one she'd read aloud to Quentin on the deck while the engine ticked and the hills listened: *If you are reading this, you are holding more than my apology. You are holding the account. Settle it however you see fit. That is the only inheritance I have that isn't stolen.*

Settle it however you see fit.

She had settled part of it already — the journal read aloud in the fort square, Harrow's stay, Crane's crews hauled half-drowned out of the flood they'd unleashed themselves. But accounts weren't closed by courts. Courts dealt in titles. This was a different ledger.

"Here?" she asked, indicating the ground beside the slab, where the fig's roots made a pocket of dark soil.

"There," Odessa agreed. "But not only the letter." She reached into the pouch at her belt and drew out something wrapped in waxed cloth, unfolding it with the care of someone handling a live coal. Inside lay a thin rectangle of slate, freshly split, its edges rubbed smooth. On it, in Odessa's own hand — and Odessa cut letters the way she piloted, economical and exact — were two lines:

**ELIAS VANE**
**HE CAME BACK BY HIS DAUGHTER**

Sera looked at it for a long time. The mist was burning off the terraces below, and the light came up gold through it, and she found she could not speak, so she didn't try.

"He never came back," she managed finally. "He died downriver."

"No," Odessa said. "His name came back. His account came back. His daughter came back carrying both." She set the slate down gently beside the carved name, aligning it with the slope of the stone as if seating a guest. "On this hill, that counts as returning. It's the only returning the valley recognizes."

* * *

They dug together. The soil was black and loose under the fig, threaded with roots fine as hair, and it smelled of everything alive — loam and fungus and the sweet rot of last season's leaves becoming this season's. Sera used her hands for the last inches, the way her father's letter deserved, working the earth until the hole was deep enough. She thought about what to put in with the letter. There was nothing else of his. No ring, no tool, no photograph. The journal had gone to the judge; the map had gone into Quentin's fire in front of elders and witnesses and a ruined surveyor, ash rising into monsoon rain. Everything Elias Vane had been was either public record or smoke.

Except this. Except the words meant for one person.

She laid the letter flat in the earth. For a moment she almost couldn't let go — the same reflex, she realized, that had kept her from opening the packet for a full day in Port Calloway, the same reflex that had kept her from reading his name without spitting for most of her adult life. Holding on had been the shape of her anger for so long that releasing it felt like losing her balance.

Then she remembered the height-marks behind the wallpaper, and she let go.

She filled the hole with her hands. Odessa pressed the slate into place at the head of it, wedging it firm against the slab so the rains of fifty years wouldn't shift it, and stepped back.

"Now yours," Odessa said.

Sera blinked. "Mine?"

"You think the valley lets you bury an inheritance and walk away unrecorded?" Odessa handed her a knife — not her own; a Riverkin knife, short-bladed, bone-handled, worn smooth by other hands across other generations. "Below his. Where you put your mark is where you're saying you stand."

Sera turned the knife over. Her palms were still black with grave-soil, and she wiped them uselessly on her trousers, and stood before the slab feeling the full strangeness of it: she had spent her whole life refusing to belong anywhere. Salvage yards, rented rooms, the transient towns downriver where everyone was between things. Belonging had looked, from the outside, like a trap her father had escaped from and she'd be a fool to enter. She had built her entire self on the creed that the safe place was the moving place.

And then a dying man had handed her a packet, and a stubborn boat captain had refused to leave it unopened, and a valley had risen out of the rain like something from a drowned story, and every single thing she'd built her life on had turned out to be scaffolding around an empty room.

She knelt. She set the knife's point below her father's name, below Odessa's slate, where the granite was sound and would hold a line for centuries, and she thought about what to write. Not her name alone — that felt insufficient, a signature without a statement. The names on these rocks carried their small testaments. *He came back by his daughter.* What was hers?

She cut slowly, carefully, tongue between her teeth like a child learning letters, each stroke deeper than her father's had been, because she had time and he hadn't, because she was carving in daylight with allies on the hillside below instead of at fourteen with the world already tilting.

**SERA VANE**

And beneath it, after a long moment, watching the river braid itself silver far below:

**KEEPER OF THE ACCOUNT**

"That's not how we'd say it," Odessa said quietly, when she finished. "But it's true. And true is what the rock wants." She studied the fresh-cut letters, then nodded once, the nod she gave when a channel checked out against her memory of it. "It will weather well. Your hand is honest."

Sera sat back on her heels. Below them, the camp was waking — smoke from cook fires, the knock of mallets on the weir stones where Tomas worked with the crew of men who had not yet forgiven him and probably never entirely would, but who had stopped turning away when he passed. Farther down, at the landing they were rebuilding, the *Marguerite* sat on the shallows with her hull stripped, and she could see Quentin's figure moving along her rail, small in the distance, doing his endless inventory of damage. A man deciding, whether he knew it yet or not, what staying cost and whether he'd pay it.

"Come down when you're ready," Odessa said. She shouldered the digging stick, then paused. "There's a thing that happens tonight, if the elders agree to it. They've been talking since the judge's stay came upriver. About records."

"Records?"

"This valley kept its own records for four hundred years. Weir ledgers. Planting calendars. Names, births, debts, the boundaries sung rather than written. When the Company drove us out, the elders scattered the archive — hid pieces with different families, different valleys, so no single raid could end it. Some of those pieces have been coming home these past weeks, now that it's safe." Odessa looked at the carved rocks, at the new slate, at Sera's soil-black hands. "An archive needs a keeper. Someone whose job is that nothing gets lost again. The elders want it to be someone young, someone literate in both worlds — ours and theirs, the paper world. Someone who has reason to hate forgetting." A pause. "Someone who chose to come back."

Sera's throat tightened. "I'm not Riverkin."

"No," Odessa agreed. "You're the daughter of a boy whose name is on this rock, which makes you more from here than most people born in the county seat. And you're the woman who read a dead man's journal aloud in a fort square until the law flinched. The elders don't need you to be one of us. They need you to be exactly what you are." She started down the path, then added, over her shoulder: "They'll ask you at the fire tonight. You don't have to answer yes. But think about what your father wrote. *Settle it however you see fit.*"

Sera stood alone among the carved rocks as Odessa's footsteps faded down the switchbacks. The sun had fully cleared the rim now, and the light moved across the granite like a slow hand, touching name after name after name — generations of them, the whole crowded testimony of a people who had refused, in the only language available to them, to be erased.

She touched her father's name. Then the slate. Then, last, the fresh grooves of her own cutting, still sharp-edged, still pale, waiting for the years to soften them into belonging.

*Keeper of the account,* she thought. *Keeper of the records.* Different words for the same vow: that nothing gets lost again.

Down in the valley, a conch horn sounded from the longhouse — the signal that the elders had gathered, earlier than expected, and that the fire tonight would not wait for darkness. Odessa stood at the bottom of the path, looking up, and raised one hand: *come.*

Sera took one breath of the dripping, breathing valley, brushed the grave-soil from her knees, and went down to meet her inheritance.
