## 1. Cold Water Work

The wreck had no name, which was how Ada Montrose liked her wrecks best.

Officially it did not exist at all. The charts showed clean water off the point where the hull lay in forty feet of gray-green cold, and the admiralty lists recorded no loss there, and the coast guard's patrol schedules — the ones Bram traded for with a fisherman who owed him money — never brought anyone close. A ship that had never existed could not be salvaged from, and so what Ada took from its bones belonged to no one, least of all to the government that had put it on the bottom and then erased the paperwork.

She went down at first light, when the water was flattest and the light came through in long slanted bars that turned the silt into smoke.

Forty feet down, the world narrowed to the sound of her own breathing and the tick of her regulator and the slow percussion of swell against a hull that had been taking that beating for eleven years without complaint. She moved along the starboard side with the economy of long habit, gloves trailing over plating furred green with weed, past a porthole crusted shut, past the place where a shell or a rock or simple fatigue had opened the deck like a tin lid. Her lamp found the interior beyond: a corridor of drifting sediment, the soft suggestion of a ladder, the geometry of things that had once been arranged by human hands.

Brass. That was the morning's work. Brass did not rot, did not rust, did not care about salt or time. Valves, fittings, gauge housings, the thick ring of a hawsepipe cover — all of it worth more per pound than the fish the *Horizon* used to chase, back when she'd been a fishing boat instead of what she was now, which was a barge with opinions.

Ada worked the way her father had taught her: hands first, eyes second, hurry never. *The sea gives you exactly as much as you take slowly,* he used to say, cinching her tank straps when she was fourteen and all elbows and impatience. *Rush it and it takes the difference out of your hide.* He'd said it the week before the dive that killed him, too, though he'd broken his own rule that day, and everyone aboard had known it, and nobody had said it, because you didn't tell a man like Elias Montrose — no, not Elias; that name belonged to another man entirely, one she hadn't met yet — because you didn't tell a man like Tomas Montrose he was getting old. You just watched him go down too deep and come up too slow, and you told yourself next time would be different, until there wasn't a next time.

She shook it off. Eleven years of shaking it off had made the motion almost automatic, like clearing an ear.

The first valve housing came free with a grunt and a twist. She clipped it to the lift bag's line and kept moving. There were six more along this stretch of machinery space if the survey she'd memorized last night held true, and six more after that deeper in, and every one of them was bread, diesel, Juno's dive certification course, the patch for the port pontoon that leaked worse every month. Poverty made a precise arithmetic of everything. Ada had gotten good at arithmetic.

Through the haze above her, filtered down through forty feet of water and twenty of air, the sky was going from pewter to a thin, watery gold. Somewhere up there, her crew was doing the other half of the job: watching the horizon.

* * *

On the surface, the *Horizon* rode her anchor with the patient discomfort of an old woman standing in line. She was sixty-one feet of patched steel and stubbornness, a former trawler converted for salvage work by a man who'd believed — loudly, often, at tavern volume — that the sea owed honest people a living. The sea had since offered several counterarguments. The boat bore them all in rust streaks and epoxy scars.

Bram Okafor stood at the wheelhouse rail with binoculars he'd repaired twice and refused to replace, sweeping the eastern horizon in slow, disciplined arcs. He was fifty-eight, built like something the dockyard had forgotten to dismantle, and he had been watching horizons for Tomas Montrose before Ada was born. He watched them now for her, and for the three others sleeping or pretending to sleep below.

"Anything?" Juno Reyes asked, materializing at his elbow with two mugs of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed from rope. She was nineteen, all collarbones and restless hands, three months certified and hungry for depth the way only the young and the broke can be hungry.

"Nothing." Bram drank anyway. "Fishing fleet's working the banks south of us. Coast guard cutter *Merrow* logged out of Harwick yesterday evening, heading west."

"You know their schedule better than they do."

"I pay attention." He said it without pride, the way you'd say *I breathe*. "That's the whole trade, girl. Everybody else looks at the water. We look at everything else."

Juno leaned on the rail and looked at the water anyway, at the pale rope of Ada's bubbles rising off the stern, breaking, rising. She counted them out of habit. Ada had taught her that too — count your diver's bubbles, learn their rhythm, and you'll know trouble before the line tells you. Ada's rhythm was metronome-steady. It always was. Juno privately suspected Ada's heart ran on the same schedule, that somewhere under all that competence there was a woman who simply refused to be hurried by anything, including grief.

Below decks, Wren Calloway was doing the books.

Wren did the books the way other people prayed: daily, seriously, and with a growing sense that no one was listening. She sat in the galley with the engine-room vibration humming up through the bench and a pencil worn to a stub, and she added the same numbers again hoping they'd improved. They had not. Fuel for the run out: gone. Food for the month: thin. The compressor's rebuild fund: a joke she told herself. The engine — the great groaning, wheezing, immortal diesel that had carried the *Horizon* through eleven years of salvage and thirty years of fish before that — needed work that cost more than the boat was worth, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, because saying it made it true.

They'd cleared four hundred and ten pounds from the last three wrecks. The engine wanted two thousand. Winter wanted more than either.

Wren wrote the numbers down again, smaller this time, as if smallness might make them kinder, and heard Juno's boots cross the deck overhead and felt, without looking up, the particular quality of silence that meant everyone aboard was thinking the same thing and waiting for someone else to say it.

* * *

Ada surfaced at nine-forty, blowing her fins off in the water and passing the lift bag's line up to Juno's waiting hands. The valve housings came over the gunwale dripping, each one catching the strengthening sun like a coin pulled from a giant's pocket.

"Seven," Ada said, spitting her reg and hauling herself up the ladder. "Two more in the machinery space, but they're behind a collapsed plate. Tomorrow's problem."

"Tomorrow we're supposed to be running north," Wren called from the galley hatch, not looking up from her ledger. "Tide window."

"Then tomorrow I'll be quick."

Bram took her arm as she stepped aboard — steadying her, ostensibly, though she hadn't wobbled in a decade — and studied her face with the frank, unhurried attention he gave to weather and nothing else. "You look tired."

"I look rich," Ada said. "Look at all this brass."

He didn't smile. Neither did she, really. It was the kind of exchange they'd worn smooth over eleven years, a groove in the wood of the day, and both of them understood what lived underneath it: that seven valve housings was a good morning's work and also nothing, that the numbers Wren kept were a tide going steadily out, that competent people were drowning slowly in full view of the horizon they never stopped watching.

They processed the haul on deck while the anchor watch turned. Juno scrubbed the worst of the weed off with a stiff brush, quick and reverent, handling each fitting like an artifact. In a way it was. Every piece had a serial number, and every serial number was a small act of larceny against a government that preferred its mistakes underwater. Ada checked each one against the little mental catalogue she kept — this pattern matched naval auxiliary work, 1970s vintage, consistent with the rest — and felt the familiar double edge of it. Pride in the craft. Shame at the necessity. And beneath both, the thing she never said aloud even to herself: the sense that she was picking coins off a corpse, and that the corpse had been left face-down on purpose.

Because that was the other rule. Not the one about diving slow. The other one.

Her father's rules had been few and absolute, carved into the *Horizon*'s culture so deeply that the crew observed them without remembering learning them. Never dive alone. Never trust a calm sea in autumn. Never sign a contract you haven't read twice. And the fourth, the one that mattered, the one that had ended conversations and partnerships and once, memorably, a friendship:

*There are wrecks we don't touch.*

Not dangerous ones — danger was just arithmetic with extra steps, and Ada had never been afraid of arithmetic. No. Certain specific wrecks. Navy vessels lost in circumstances the navy declined to discuss. Ships whose names had been removed from the record rather than merely misplaced. Her father had kept a list of them, handwritten, folded into the back of his logbook, and he had never once explained it, and he had never once broken it, and the one time a broker had come to the harbor offering triple rates for a job inside restricted water, Tomas Montrose had thrown him out of the Anchor & Chain hard enough to rearrange the furniture.

Ada had been sixteen. She remembered the sound the man made hitting the doorframe.

She thought about it sometimes, that list. Thought about it more lately, if she was honest, now that the arithmetic had stopped coming out in their favor. But thinking was as far as it ever went. Some inheritances aren't property. They're walls, and you live inside them your whole life without knowing why they were built, only that your father died believing they should stand.

"Ada." Juno was holding up the last fitting, scrubbed bright. "This one's got a stamp. Different from the others."

Ada took it. Turned it in the light. The maker's mark was standard enough, but below it, struck deep and deliberate, was a code she recognized from a hundred catalogued pieces and had never once seen on a civilian contract: a naval stores designation, followed by a batch number that began with a prefix reserved for classified procurement.

Same as the last three wrecks. Same as always. The navy's fingerprints, pressed into brass, sunk out of sight.

"Bag it separately," she said. "We'll move it through the dealer in Harwick. He doesn't ask questions."

"He never asks questions," Wren said from the hatch. "It's why we can afford him."

Ada almost smiled. Then she looked east, where the horizon ran flat and empty and innocent, and the feeling arrived that had been circling her all morning like a gull — the sense of being watched, not by anything present, but by the accumulated weight of everything absent. Her father. His list. The woman whose face she couldn't quite hold onto anymore, dead before Ada turned five, gone so long and so completely that her mother had become less a person than a shape cut out of the family's history, edges sharp, middle empty.

Twenty years this year. Twenty years since the Passage went down with thirty-one souls and a cargo manifest sealed by court order, and the navy called it weather, and the families got a ceremony and a wall, and her father got a list of wrecks he wouldn't touch, and Ada got a life spent harvesting the navy's discarded brass because it was the only honest dishonesty available to a woman with her skills and her name.

"Weigh anchor in an hour," she said. "North, like Wren says. Tide won't wait."

Nobody moved immediately. That was the thing about her crew — they were good, the best she'd ever worked with, and they loved her in the wordless way of people who have shared too much cold water — but they all knew the books, and they all knew the engine, and they all knew that seven valve housings bought October and not November. She saw it in Bram's level gaze, in Wren's pencil hovering over the ledger, in Juno's careful brightness, the eagerness of the young straining against the caution of the trained.

"What?" Ada said.

"Nothing," Bram said. Which, from Bram, meant *everything, and you know it, and I'll wait.*

They weighed anchor at eleven. The *Horizon* turned her scarred bow north, toward home harbor and the winter and the arithmetic, and Ada took the wheel for the first watch and let the sea do what it always did, which was say nothing, magnificently, forever.

It was nearly dusk when the harbor lights came up, and the Anchor & Chain's windows glowed yellow through the last of the fog, and Ada saw — even across the water, even at that distance — the figure standing outside the tavern door as if he'd been waiting for their boat specifically. Tall. Still. Coat too good for this town by about a decade.

She knew the silhouette before she knew the man. Some silhouettes you don't forget, not in eleven years, not ever: the set of those shoulders, the angle of that stillness, the particular way he held his hands like a man who had once carried things he shouldn't have.

Cassian Vey. On her dock. After eleven years.

Ada's hands went tight on the wheel, and beside her, quietly, Bram said, "Well," in the voice of a man watching a door open that he'd helped nail shut.

"*Horizon* to harbor," Ada said, to no one, to the fog, to the dead. "Coming in."

The engine answered, coughing, faithful, dying by inches. She steered toward the lights.
