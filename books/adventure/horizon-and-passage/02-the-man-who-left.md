## 2. The Man Who Left

The Saltglass filled up the way it always did on a Friday — slow at first, then all at once, like water finding its level. Ada Montrose sat in her usual corner with her back to the wall and her boots on the rail of an empty stool, counting the night's take in her head and coming up short by exactly the amount the engine manifold would cost them, if manifolds could be bought cheap, which they couldn't.

She was nursing her second beer and not drinking it when the door opened and the cold came in wearing a good coat.

She knew him before she saw his face. She knew him by the way he moved — that unhurried, deliberate walk, like a man who had learned somewhere expensive that haste was for people without leverage. Cassian Vey. Twenty years since she'd last seen him on dry land, and her body recognized him before her mind consented to.

He stood in the doorway a moment, letting the room take his measure. Tall, gray at the temples now, the sharpness of his youth worn down into something more careful. He'd been handsome once in the way knives are handsome. He still was, if you liked the risk.

The tavern noise dipped, then rose again, covering him. He crossed the floor toward her corner with the confidence of a man who had already asked someone where she'd be sitting.

"No," Ada said, before he reached the table.

Vey stopped. Smiled slightly. "You don't know what I'm going to say."

"I know what you're going to ask." She took her boots off the stool but didn't offer it. "And the answer's no. Sit somewhere else."

"Ada." He said her name like he had a right to it. That was the thing about Vey — he'd known her father, worked with him, eaten at their table in the years when there had been a table worth eating at, and he carried those years around like credentials. "Five minutes. Then I'll go wherever you point me."

"You'll go out the same door you came in." She turned back to her beer. Around them the tavern went on — darts thudding into cork, the jukebox grinding through something old, fishermen laughing too loud about fish that had gotten away honestly for once. Nobody here knew who Vey was. Nobody here knew what his face did to the inside of her chest. "We're not taking contracts. Not from you. Not from anyone who asks the way you're asking."

"And how am I asking?"

"Not asking at all. That's how."

* * *

Here is what Ada remembered, whether she wanted to or not:

A galley on the *Meridian*, her father's boat, steam rising off a pot of coffee while rain hammered the wheelhouse glass. Cassian Vey sitting across from her, twenty-two years old and already talking like a man reading from documents other people hadn't seen yet. Her father laughing at whatever scheme Vey was describing — deep-water salvage, restricted coordinates, triple rates, the kind of money that buys a new boat outright — and her mother's hand resting on Ada's shoulder, quiet, the way she got quiet when she was afraid.

Her mother had said: *Some wrecks aren't lost, Ada. Some are put there.*

Her father had said: *Not this one, love. This one we take.*

And then the *Meridian* had gone down with all hands but hers, and the job had never been taken, and the money had never mattered again.

Ada had been nineteen. Vey had sent flowers. She'd thrown them off the pier.

* * *

"Three times your going rate." Vey pulled out the stool anyway and sat, because he was the kind of man who treated refusals as opening positions. "I've done my homework. The *Horizon* is running on a borrowed engine and borrowed time. Your diver — Juno, is it? — she's good, but she's diving on gear older than she is. You're scraping brass off navy hulks at dawn so patrol boats don't catch you at it. That's not a business, Ada. That's a slow apology."

Ada set the beer down. "Careful."

"I'm being careful. That's the whole point of me." He leaned forward, elbows on the scarred wood, and for a moment the polish dropped and she saw the tiredness underneath, real and unglamorous. "One job. Deep water, north of the shelf. Six tides of work. And then you never see me again, if that's what you want, and I will consider that a fair price."

"The north shelf." Her voice came out flat. "Restricted water."

"I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to. There's nothing north of the shelf worth triple rates except one thing." She let the silence do its work. In the corner of her eye she saw Bram at the bar, ostensibly reading the shipping notices pinned above the taps, actually listening with the whole of his gray-bearded self. "No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard enough twenty years ago. My father heard the terms too." She stood, and the stool scraped back, and a couple of heads turned. She kept her voice low anyway; this wasn't tavern business, this was hers. "My father swore on his life that crew would never touch that wreck. His words. *Never.* And then he died proving how much his word weighed, and you—" the anger arrived all at once, hot and old, twenty years of it with nowhere new to go, "—you sent flowers."

Vey didn't flinch. That was the worst part. Most men would have flinched.

"I know what I owe," he said quietly.

"Do you? Because it looks to me like you've spent two decades getting comfortable and now you've come back to spend somebody else's grief on whatever it is you're really after." She picked up his coat from where he'd draped it over the neighboring chair and shoved it against his chest. "Get out, Cassian."

He took the coat. He stood. For a long moment he looked at her with an expression she couldn't read — regret, maybe, or arithmetic — and then he nodded once, like a man closing a ledger entry he intended to reopen later.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your father was right to swear the oath. And wrong about why."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

But he was already walking, threading between the tables, and the door swallowed him along with the cold. Through the salt-fogged window she watched him go down the wet street, unhurried even in defeat, and disappear under the broken lamp by the chandlery.

Ada sat back down. Her beer had gone warm. She drank it anyway.

* * *

Bram appeared ten minutes later with two fresh pints and the particular slowness of a man arranging furniture in a conversation. He set one in front of her and lowered himself onto the stool Vey had warmed, which she noted he did without hesitation, which told her something.

"He talked to you," she said.

"He talked at me. There's a difference." Bram was sixty-three and built like the anchor chain he used to tend, and he had loved her father in the wordless way old sailors love, and he had held the Montrose crew together through every bad year since. He turned the pint glass a quarter-turn on the wood. "Triple rates, Ada."

"No."

"I'm not saying yes. I'm saying the number out loud, because somebody has to, and it might as well be the man who counts the stores." He looked at her then, steady, no apology in it. "We've got six weeks of diesel. We've got a manifold cracked clean through, and Juno's regulator needs replacing before it kills her, and winter's coming the way winter comes. Triple rates for six tides buys us the year. Maybe two."

"Bram."

"I know what he is. I was there when your da made the oath. I helped write it, near enough." His voice roughened. "I also watched your da count stores the way I just counted ours, and I watched him take a bad contract anyway because the alternative was sending people home hungry. Men make oaths when they can afford them, girl. It's the affording that changes."

Ada stared into her beer. The tavern roared softly around them, oblivious.

"He wouldn't tell me what the cargo is," she said finally. "Twenty years, and he walks in here and won't say three words about what's down there that's worth what he's offering."

"Then either it's nothing," Bram said, "or it's something he thinks you'll refuse if you hear it named." He finished half his pint, set it down, and stood, joints cracking like line paying out. "Either way, I'd want to know which. But I'm an old man who likes knowing things. You're the captain."

He shuffled toward the bar to settle up, and Ada caught the small motion of his hand — quick, practiced, the sleight of a man who'd been palming things since before she was born — sliding something from his shirt pocket into the dark of his coat.

A card. White, heavy stock, the kind that costs money.

She said nothing. The anger was still in her chest, but underneath it now, unwelcome as a hook in the flesh, was another feeling entirely: the memory of her mother's voice saying *some wrecks aren't lost,* and the question that voice had left behind, unanswered for twenty years, turning over in the dark water of her like something rising.

Outside, the tide was turning. She could feel it in the harbor's breathing, in the groan of mooring lines, in the way the whole coast seemed to hold itself and let go.

Six tides, Vey had said. As if he already knew how many it would take. As if he'd measured the depth before he ever walked through that door.

Ada finished her beer, put money on the table, and went out into the cold to find out what her quartermaster was carrying in his pocket — and what, exactly, Cassian Vey thought was worth reopening a grave for.
