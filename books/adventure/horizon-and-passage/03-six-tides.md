## 3. Six Tides

Bram Oduya had counted money in the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel* for nineteen years, and he had never once enjoyed it. Tonight he hated it more than usual.

The lamp swung on its gimbal as the boat rolled at her moorings, and the light swept across the chart table where he'd laid out their winter in coins and crumpled notes and a single folded bank draft from a fish processor who paid late and complained about it. He counted it twice because the first count had been too grim to believe, and the second was worse, because now he believed it.

Four hundred and eleven marks. That was what remained of the season's salvage after fuel, after food, after the doctor in Port Callow who'd stitched Juno's forearm and refused payment until Bram pressed the notes into his coat pocket like contraband. Four hundred and eleven marks against an engine that needed a full rebuild — new bearings, new head gasket, a crankshaft that machinist Ilsa Voss had held up to the lamplight two months ago and pronounced *done, Bram, she's done, she's turning metal into gravel*.

A rebuilt engine would cost three thousand. A replacement, if they could find one used, might be had for eighteen hundred if the gods were drunk and generous.

Winter came to the northern coast the way debt came: slowly, then all at once. By the first week of Frostmonth the harbor would be rimmed with grey ice, the fish boats hauled out and shrink-wrapped, the taverns full of men telling stories about work they weren't doing. A salvage crew without a working engine wasn't a crew. It was four people sharing a cold room above a dead boat, watching their savings evaporate one meal at a time.

Bram put the money back in the tin box, latched it, and sat with his hands flat on the chart table the way he used to sit when Tomas Montrose was alive and the counting was Tomas's job and Bram's job was only to argue about it.

*You count it honest or you don't count it at all,* Tomas used to say. *Bad numbers are how good crews die.*

Tomas had been dead nine years, drowned off the shelf in water so shallow it should have been survivable, and Bram still heard him most clearly in this room, at this table, under this swinging lamp. He supposed that was what love did when the beloved was gone. It didn't leave. It just changed rooms.

He heard boots on the ladder well before the hatch opened — Ada's walk, quick and flat-heeled, no wasted motion even on her own deck. She came down into the wheelhouse with salt drying white on her shoulders and her hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a length of signal flag, and she stopped when she saw the tin box open and his face above it.

She was twenty-nine and she had her father's eyes and her mother's jaw, though she didn't know the second part, and Bram had spent twenty years making sure no one told her wrong about either parent.

"That bad?" she said.

"It's arithmetic," Bram said. "Arithmetic is always bad. The question is how bad."

"Give me the number."

"Three thousand for the rebuild. Eighteen hundred if we find a miracle in a scrapyard."

Ada pulled out the chair across from him and sat, elbows on the table, and looked at the tin box as if she could shame it into holding more. "And we have?"

"Enough to reach Port Callow and buy groceries. Not enough to reach spring."

She nodded slowly. He watched her do the same math he'd done, watch it land in her the way it landed in him — not as surprise but as confirmation of a thing they'd both been refusing to say aloud since the *Meridian* job went sideways in summer and ate their margin whole.

"So we're done," she said. Not a question. Testing whether saying it made it real.

"We're done by winter," Bram said. "Unless."

Her eyes came up. "Unless what."

"There's a card." He reached into his shirt pocket and laid it on the table between them, face up, because he'd decided on the walk over that he wasn't going to be sneaky about it. Sneaky was how crews rotted. Cassian Vey's card was heavy cream stock with black lettering, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than their fuel budget for a month. *C. VEY. SALVAGE & RECOVERY. PORT CALLOW.* A number beneath, and nothing else. No promise, no flourish. Vey had never needed flourish; he had money, and money did its own talking.

Ada looked at the card the way she'd look at a sea snake coiled on the deck plates.

"No," she said.

"He came to me after you threw him out of the Anchorline. Didn't ask for anything. Just said, when the vote comes, tell them all of it. Said your crew deserved to decide with the whole board showing."

"My crew isn't voting on anything from Cassian Vey."

"Then we're done by winter," Bram said quietly. "You want to hear the rest of it or do you want to throw the card in the stove now and we can start talking about which of us takes work on the herring boats and who stays ashore?"

The lamp swung. Outside, the harbor water slapped against the hull in that patient, indifferent rhythm the sea kept regardless of what happened on land, and Bram thought, as he thought every day, that the sea was the only honest creditor any of them had ever known. It took exactly what it took. It never pretended otherwise.

"Tomas swore it," Ada said. Her voice had gone low and careful, the voice she used when she was standing on ground she couldn't see the bottom of. "After the *Passage*. After Mom. He stood the whole crew down in this wheelhouse — I was fourteen, I was sitting right there — and he said, *we don't touch navy wrecks, we don't touch restricted water, and we especially don't touch whatever the Passage was carrying.* He said some wrecks aren't graves, Bram. Some wrecks are wounds, and digging in them just makes them bleed again."

"I remember," Bram said. "I was standing where you're sitting."

"Then you remember why the answer is no."

"I remember everything about that night, girl. I remember your father's hands shaking while he said it, and I remember that he wasn't shaking because he was afraid of the wreck." Bram turned the card over, once, and set it back down. "I've had nineteen years to think about why a man who loved salvage like Tom loved it would swear off the richest restricted site on the coast. And I've come to one conclusion I trust: he knew something. Something specific. And he was afraid of it in a way that had nothing to do with drowning."

Ada was silent a long moment. When she spoke again her voice was flatter, harder. "Vey wouldn't tell you what the cargo was."

"He wouldn't tell me what the job pays beyond triple rates. He wouldn't tell me the timeline except to say six tides. He said—" Bram paused, because this was the part he'd turned over all afternoon, the grain of sand in the boot of his mind. "He said, *tell Montrose's daughter the target is the Passage, and tell her I know what that word costs her, and tell her the price reflects it.*"

"The Passage," Ada repeated. The name came out of her like something she'd swallowed long ago and never fully got down. "Twenty years in restricted water. Nobody dives it. Nobody's allowed to."

"No."

"And Vey wants us to."

"Yes."

"Why us? He's got money. He's got contractors, fleets, half the licensed divers on the coast on speed-dial. Why crawl into the Anchorline and take abuse from a rival he hasn't spoken to in fifteen years?"

Bram leaned back. The question was fair, and he'd chewed it through three pipes' worth of tobacco without finding an answer that satisfied him, only answers that were true in the way uncomfortable things were true.

"Because of you," he said finally. "Or because of your father. Or because of something he did back when he wore a uniform instead of a diving suit. Men like Vey don't come back to places that hate them unless the place has something they need. And what he needs, Ada — my guess, and it's only a guess — is someone who can actually raise what's down there. And there's maybe three crews on this coast with the skill and the stomach. Two of them won't touch restricted water. The third is Harrow & Sons, and if Vey wanted Harrow he'd have hired Harrow."

"Harrow works for the navy."

"Harrow works for whoever pays. Which is mostly the navy." Bram tapped the card once. "So here's the whole board, like he asked. We have four hundred marks and a dying engine and six weeks of weather. Or we have a contract worth more than five good seasons, from a man we despise, on a wreck your father forbade, in water that killed your mother. Those are the choices. I don't like either one. But I'm fifty-eight years old and I've watched too many good people get poor honestly, and I'll tell you what I told your father once, God forgive me: poverty kills slower than the sea, but it kills just as sure, and it takes the young ones first."

Ada stared at the card. In the lamplight her face did something complicated — anger and hunger and grief all trying to occupy the same small space — and Bram recognized it because he'd seen it on Tomas's face the night before the *Passage* sank, when a navy liaison had come to the harbor asking quiet questions and Tomas had answered every one of them with his jaw set like a man biting down on broken glass.

"Call the crew," Ada said at last. "Full vote. Everyone hears everything, including what I think of it. And Bram—"

"Aye."

"If anyone votes no, we walk away. Even if it's one hand. Even if it's Juno. We are not a boat that drags people."

"I know what kind of boat we are," Bram said. "Your father built it. I've just been keeping it afloat since."

* * *

They gathered in the mess that evening, all four of them plus the cat, who considered herself crew and attended votes accordingly. Juno sat cross-legged on the bench with her stitched arm cradled against her chest, seventeen years old and vibrating with the effort of looking calm. Dessa, their engineer, arrived last and oiliest, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last year, and dropped onto the bench across from Bram with the expression of a woman who already knew what the meeting was about because she was the one who'd condemned the engine.

"Say the numbers," Dessa said. "Say them plain. I'm tired of pretending the noise she's making is normal."

Bram said the numbers plain. Three thousand for the rebuild. Four hundred and eleven in the box. Winter six weeks out, and the first hard blows usually early. He watched each face as the figures settled: Dessa grim and unsurprised, Juno's freckles standing out pale against skin gone suddenly tight, Ada standing by the galley door with her arms crossed, letting him lead because she'd promised a full hearing and she was a woman who kept her own rules even when they cut her.

Then he put the card in the middle of the table.

"Cassian Vey came to port today," he said. "Triple rates. Six tides. Target's the Passage."

The silence afterward had texture. Juno's mouth opened and closed. Dessa picked up the card, read it, and set it down with two fingers, the way you'd handle something that might be hot.

"The *Passage*," Dessa said. "The survey ship. The one that went down with everyone aboard, twenty years back, in the water nobody's allowed to dive."

"The same."

"That's a grave," Juno said quietly. "Isn't it? Thirty-one people. They never even found most of them."

"It's a grave," Bram agreed. "It's also, according to a man I have no reason to trust, a paying job. Triple rates means we rebuild the engine, we provision for spring, and we go into next season with money in the box instead of debts on the books. It means the *Kestrel* lives."

"And it means breaking the oath," Ada said from the doorway. Every head turned. She hadn't moved; her arms stayed crossed; but her voice carried the weight of a keel. "My father swore this crew would never take that job. I was here when he swore it. So before anyone votes, you should know where I stand. I think Cassian Vey is a liar with excellent manners. I think whatever's on that wreck, the navy sank it there on purpose and doesn't want it found — that's the only reason water stays restricted for twenty years. And I think my father knew it, and that's why he drew the line he drew." She paused, and Bram saw her throat work. "I also know we're done by winter if we say no. So I'm putting it to you anyway, because a captain who only calls votes she'll win isn't running a crew, she's running a cult. Vote your hearts. One no and we walk."

Dessa spoke first, because Dessa always spoke first when the subject was machinery. "I can keep her running for maybe three more weeks of easy work. After that I'm gambling with the crankshaft every time we turn her over. If we're going to gamble, I'd rather gamble on a payday." She looked around the table. "That's not a yes. That's a diagnosis."

"Juno?" Bram said.

Juno had been staring at the card. Now she looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "My mom's sick," she said. "You all know it. The treatments in Callow cost more than this boat. I've been doing the math since summer and the math says I can't help her and stay aboard past Frostmonth." She swiped at her eyes with her good arm. "I don't care about Vey. I don't care about oaths I wasn't there for. I care that triple rates buys my mother a winter that isn't a death sentence. So — yes. From me. And I'm sorry, Ada. I know that's not loyal."

"Juno." Ada crossed the mess and put her hand on the girl's head, briefly, the way you'd steady a mast in a swell. "Wanting your mother alive isn't disloyalty. It's the most loyal thing on this boat besides Bram's cooking. Vote stands."

Which left the vote at two yeses, one qualified, and the captain abstaining by her own rule — and every eye, including the cat's, swinging to Bram.

He had known this moment was coming since the Anchorline, since Vey's card sat warm in his pocket like a coal. He had rehearsed arguments on both sides all afternoon and discarded them all, because there was only one thing he actually believed, and it wasn't about money.

"Your father saved my life twice," Bram said. "Once off the Sable Shoals when I was green and stupid, and once later, in a way that had nothing to do with water, when I'd lost my family and didn't much want mine. I loved that man like a brother, and I have spent nineteen years keeping his boat and his rules and his memory, and I will spend nineteen more." He turned the card over between his fingers. "But I was there the night he made the oath, and I've told you what I saw. His hands shook. And Tomas Montrose's hands did not shake at graves — he dove war wrecks, he dove the ferry off Gull Point with bodies still aboard, and his hands were steady as davits every time. Whatever scared him about the Passage wasn't death. It was something living. Something that walks around on land and signs papers."

He set the card down and pushed it toward the middle of the table, equidistant from everyone, belonging to no one.

"I vote yes," he said. "Not for the money. Well — partly for the money, I'm not a saint, and Dessa needs parts. But mostly because I'm old enough to know that oaths made out of fear rot faster than hulls, and your father was protecting us from something, and I am tired — bone tired, Ada — of being protected from the truth. Twenty years is long enough. If the navy sank that ship and doesn't want it found, then I want to know why badly enough to risk winter for it. And if your mother died anywhere near that truth—" His voice caught, and he let it, because hiding it would have been the dishonorable thing. "Then I owe her more than I owe a dead man's caution. I loved her too, girl. In my fashion. We all did. She laughed at my jokes when they weren't funny."

Nobody spoke. The lamp swung. Somewhere below, the dying engine ticked as it cooled, like a clock counting down something.

Ada looked at each of them in turn — Dessa, Juno, Bram — and Bram watched her arrive at the arithmetic of it, three yeses however you weighted them, and watched her understand that the vote had already happened somewhere deeper than the mess table, in the place where a crew decides what it's willing to bleed for.

"Majority carries," she said finally. Her voice was steady. Her hands, Bram noticed, were not. "We take the contract. Six tides. We sail on tomorrow's ebb, north, and nobody radios anybody about where we're going. Dessa — I need the engine to make the run. Can you get her there?"

"I can get her there," Dessa said. "Whether she comes back is between her and God."

"Then we're committed." Ada picked up Vey's card and held it a moment, as if weighing it, then tucked it into her breast pocket — not thrown in the stove, Bram noted, and wondered if she noticed what that meant. "One more thing. If any of you learns what's really down there and wants to walk away, you walk with your share and my blessing. That's not sentiment. That's policy."

She left for the wheelhouse. Juno followed, chattering nervously about charts and tide tables, grateful for something to do with her hands. Dessa went below to begin the argument with the engine that would last all night.

Bram stayed a moment alone in the mess, and took out his pipe, and didn't light it.

*Tomas,* he thought, staring at the spot on the bench where a fourteen-year-old girl had once sat listening to her father draw a line in the water. *I've broken your oath. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me what you knew.*

The engine ticked. The harbor slapped the hull. And far to the north, under sixty fathoms of cold restricted water, the *Passage* waited with her dead and her secret, patient as arithmetic, certain as winter — and Bram Oduya, who had counted honestly all his life, felt for the first time in nineteen years that the numbers on the table were not the ones that mattered.
