## 4. What Her Father Swore

The logbook lived in the bottom of Ada's sea chest, wrapped in oilcloth the way her father had wrapped everything he meant to keep — tools, charts, the last photograph of her mother. She had not opened it in three years, and she told herself, as she knelt on the cabin floor of the *Kestrel's Mercy* with the lamp turned low, that she was opening it now only because Vey had made her angry, and anger was a kind of key.

The chest smelled of tar and cedar and old salt. Beneath it lay the things she never moved: a brass sounding weight worn smooth by her father's thumb, a coil of signal flags gone gray as ash, and the logbook itself, fat and cracked at the spine, its cover stamped with the name of a boat that no longer existed. The *Fair Passage*. Not the survey vessel. His first command, before any of it — before the wreck they didn't speak of, before the oath, before the night the harbor lights went out one by one behind him and did not come back on.

She carried it up to the galley table because the cabin felt too small to hold what she might find. Bram was on watch. Juno was asleep in the forepeak, breathing in the slow rhythm of someone young enough to still sleep deeply on a boat. The lamp hissed. Rain worked at the deck above her, patient as a debt collector.

Ada opened the book at random, somewhere in the middle years, and read her father's handwriting — that tight, disciplined script that slanted hard to starboard, as if even his letters were leaning into weather.

*Day 214. Water clear to forty feet on the shelf. Tomás says the cod are thick as rain this year. Marta made bread. We are rich and don't know it.*

We are rich and don't know it. He'd written some version of that line a dozen times across two decades, and Ada had always taken it for sentiment, the small philosophy of a man who owned a boat and nothing else. Reading it now, with Vey's card burning a hole in the pocket of her coat upstairs in her bunk — *CASSIAN VEY, SALVAGE & RECOVERY, TRIPLE RATES, DISCRETION ASSURED* — the line landed differently. It sounded like a man counting what he had against what he might be asked to lose.

She began at the beginning.

The early pages were ordinary and therefore unbearable: engine hours, tide tables, the price of diesel, a sketch of Juno at four years old asleep in a net pile with a pencil caption — *crew, youngest, best.* Ada's own name appeared as a child's scrawl copied beneath his, practicing. *A. Montrose, Master,* in letters an inch high. He had let her sign the log when she was six. She had forgotten that. She sat with her hand flat on the page for a long time, letting the forgetting end.

Then the entries changed.

It was gradual, the way rot is gradual. The prose tightened. The jokes thinned out. Somewhere around Day 1,900 the bread and the cod disappeared and the entries became all business — depths, coordinates redacted with heavy ink strokes, notes about navy traffic on the radio, notes about *questions asked in port*, notes about men in good coats coming aboard and leaving again without wetting their boots.

*Day 2,041. Refused a contract today. Told them the answer is the same as the one I gave five years ago and will give five years from now. Some water you don't work. That's not superstition. That's arithmetic.*

Some water you don't work. Ada knew the phrase. She'd grown up on it. It was the closest thing the crew had to scripture: there were wrecks you raised and wrecks you left, and the left ones were left not because they were dangerous but because they were *owned* — owned by grief, or by the government, or by both, and the two were often the same thing. Her father had drawn the line himself, in blood-ink, after whatever happened twenty-two years ago, and every diver since had inherited it like a debt.

Ada turned pages faster now, hunting. The entries grew sparser near the end. And then she found it — found the gap — the way you find a missing tooth with your tongue.

There was a page stub. Clean-cut, not torn ragged; cut with a blade, close to the binding, by someone who wanted the removal to look like damage rather than decision. The entry before it ended mid-thought:

*Day 2,047. They came again tonight. This time they named the ship. I told Vey—*

And the next surviving page began, three days later:

*Day 2,050. Done. All hands witnessed. No one speaks of it again, and no one takes a contract within sight of those coordinates, not for triple rates, not for ten times triple. My daughter will dive honest water or no water at all. Signed by every soul aboard.*

*I told Vey—*

*I told Vey.*

Ada read the fragment four times. Her father had been writing to Cassian Vey. Or writing *about* him. Or writing *to* him in the only way a man like Elias Montrose wrote to anyone — straight into the permanent record, where ink could testify. Whatever Vey had been told, whatever Vey had said or done or refused on that night twenty-two years ago, it had lived on this page until someone cut it out.

Her father would not have cut it. Her father kept everything; the man had preserved a child's pencil scribble for thirty years. Which meant someone else had opened this book. Someone who knew it existed. Someone who had come aboard the *Fair Passage* — or later, after — and taken the one page that mattered.

The rain picked up. Ada barely heard it.

She went through the rest of the book twice more, slower the second time, and found other absences once she knew how to look: a photograph removed from its corner mounts, the little triangles of paper still holding their shape around empty air. A receipt stub for a strongbox rental in a port town she'd never heard her father mention. And pressed flat between the final pages, so thin she almost missed it, a strip of newsprint yellowed to the color of old bone:

***SURVEY VESSEL LOST WITH ALL HANDS,*** the headline read. ***NAVY EXTENDS CONDOLENCES TO FAMILIES. INQUIRY FINDS SEAMANSHIP FAULTLESS, WEATHER FATAL.***

Weather fatal. Seamanship faultless. Twenty-two years of that sentence sitting in her father's book like a splinter working toward the heart.

Ada closed the logbook and held it against her sternum and breathed, the way Bram had taught her to breathe when the line went slack and the dark came up fast — four counts in, hold, four counts out — because panic was just a truth arriving faster than the body could pay for it.

Her mother had died on the *Passage*. Everyone said so. Drowned in bad weather on a survey run, one name among eleven, a woman Ada remembered only as a lap and a voice and a smell of lavender soap, gone before memory could set like concrete. Ada had built her whole life on the far side of that loss the way a shoreline is built on the far side of a wave. And now here was her father's own hand telling her the wave had been manufactured.

*They named the ship.*

Vey had stood in the tavern twelve hours ago and offered triple rates for a job he wouldn't describe, and she had thrown him out, and she had felt righteous doing it, clean as a struck bell. Now the bell had a crack in it. Because if Vey knew which page to take — and someone had known — then Vey hadn't come back after twenty-two years for salvage money. Triple rates bought divers. What Vey had actually come to buy, standing in the doorway of the Gull with his officer's posture and his careful eyes, was something else entirely.

Ada put the logbook back in its oilcloth. Then she took it out again. Then she put it back, and laughed at herself — a short, ugly sound in the empty galley — because she was guarding it from ghosts.

She wasn't going to sleep. She knew that by the second hour. So instead she did what her father had done, what the whole lineage of people who worked cold water did: she prepared. She laid out her dive gear and checked every seal. She pulled the charts of the northern shelf and spread them under the lamp, and there it was, the blank space the charts always showed, the rectangle of restricted water where the survey vessel had gone down — where *her mother* had gone down — and for the first time in her life Ada looked at that blankness and saw it not as a wall but as a door someone had locked from the other side.

Near dawn, boots on the dock. Heavy, deliberate, unhurried. Ada knew the walk before she reached the companionway, and that knowledge made her angrier than anything in the logbook: she had spent half the night hoping it was Vey.

He stood at the top of the gangway in the gray light, hatless, rain beading on the shoulders of a coat too good for this harbor, and he didn't ask to come aboard. He simply waited, the way a man waits when he knows he has already been invited by everything except courtesy.

"You cut it out," Ada said.

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. "May I?"

"No." She stayed between him and the gangway, the logbook's oilcloth bundle under her arm like a weapon. "You stand right there and you tell me why my father's oath page has a knife-cut in it, and why the man who took it is standing on my dock offering me triple rates."

Vey looked past her at the boat — at the rust streaks, the patched fender, the engine that needed replacing by winter or never — and when he spoke, his voice was lower than she'd ever heard it, stripped of the tavern charm down to bare planking.

"Because I was there when your father swore it," he said. "All hands witnessed, the log says. I was the last signature. And because the *Passage* wasn't carrying survey instruments, Ada. It was carrying cargo the families of those eleven dead were never told about — cargo worth killing a ship for. Your mother knew. Your mother was the only one who tried to do something about it." He paused, and the rain filled the pause with static. "I've spent twenty-two years paying interest on what happened out there. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to help me raise the proof before Harrow & Sons get to it first and it goes to the bottom of history along with everyone who died keeping it."

"The proof of what?"

"That the navy sank her," Vey said. "Deliberately. Holed from the inside, ballast opened, eleven souls written off as weather." His jaw worked. "Your father knew a piece of it. He refused the contract they offered him to stay quiet, and he made us all swear off the water, and I honored that oath for twenty years because I owed him that much. But the oath was a promise to leave the dead in peace, and the dead have not been left in peace. They've been left in *custody*."

Ada's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. Not fear — or not only fear. It was the feeling of a current shifting under a diver, the whole water column deciding to move at once.

"My mother," she said. "What was her name on the manifest?"

Vey flinched. Actually flinched, a small structural failure in that composed face, and Ada understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with depth, that the question was worse than anything he had prepared for.

"She wasn't listed as crew," he said quietly. "That's part of what's down there. Part of what nobody told you."

The rain came harder. Out in the harbor the fishing boats were waking, lights blooming one by one across the black water, and Ada thought of her father's line — *we are rich and don't know it* — and of the torn page, and of a woman with lavender soap in her hair whose name had been erased from a ship's papers by the same hand that had erased the ship.

Six tides, Vey had said. Six tides before the rival fleet reached the site, before the last of it was salvaged by the people who had sunk it in the first place.

Ada looked at Cassian Vey for a long moment, and what curdled in her chest was not forgiveness and not greed and not even grief anymore. It was resolve, cold and specific as a cutting torch.

"Get aboard," she said. "You'll tell Bram everything. All of it, from the beginning, while I listen. And Vey—" she stepped aside to let him pass, and her voice dropped to the register she used at sixty feet, where mistakes are final. "If I find out you cut that page to hide your own name in my father's story, the sea can have you. I mean that as a diving instruction."

Vey met her eyes, and for the first time since the tavern, he looked less like a man buying a job and more like a man reporting to one.

"I'd expect nothing less," he said, and came aboard.

By the time the sun cleared the headland, the *Kestrel's Mercy* had a course plotted north into restricted water, and Ada stood at the wheel with her father's logbook open on the chart table beside her, reading the surviving words of the oath over and over — *my daughter will dive honest water or no water at all* — knowing she was about to break it, and knowing, with a certainty that frightened her more than any depth, that it was exactly what her mother would have done.
