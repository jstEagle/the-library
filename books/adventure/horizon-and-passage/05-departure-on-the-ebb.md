## 5. Departure on the Ebb

Juno had never seen the *Kestrel* loaded for anything but scraps.

Brass fittings, copper pipe, the occasional porthole glass that still had its shine — that was the cargo she'd grown up around, crates of it stinking of brine and rust, worth barely enough to split four ways. This morning the deck held dive bottles racked in pairs, fresh line coiled like sleeping snakes, the recompression kit Bram had traded a season's share to get, and a steel case bolted under the wheelhouse bench that Ada wouldn't let anyone touch, not even to look at. Juno had looked. Everyone had looked. It was empty, as far as she could tell, except for a folded chart and something wrapped in oilcloth that clinked when the boat rolled.

They were going north. Nobody said where exactly, because nobody but Ada and Vey knew, and Vey wasn't aboard yet — he'd meet them past the point with his own tender, which suited Juno fine. She didn't trust the man's smile. It sat on his face like salvage on a wreck: valuable, maybe, but placed there by someone else.

The ebb was running when they cast off, gray water sliding out of the harbor mouth in long, patient ropes. Juno stood at the bow with her hands in her pockets and watched the town shrink behind them — the tavern with its crooked sign, the chandlery where she'd spent her first wages on a dive knife she couldn't yet afford to sharpen, the breakwater where her father used to fish before the sea took him too, because in their trade the sea took everyone eventually and you just hoped it took you old.

Ada came up beside her without sound. She always moved like that, like the deck was a wreck floor and she'd been walking it twenty years.

"You checked your bottle straps?"

"Twice."

"Check them again."

Juno grinned and went aft to do it, because being ordered around by Ada Montrose was, in Juno's private accounting, not an insult but an inheritance. Her father had dived with Ada's father. He used to say the Montroses had salt instead of blood, and he said it the way other men talked about saints. When he drowned off the Shelf — not on a job, just bad luck, bad weather, a bad minute — it was Ada who brought his knife back to Juno's mother, cleaned and oiled, and it was Ada who started letting Juno crew the following spring, fourteen years old and all elbows, because she said grief was lighter if you carried it working.

That was five years ago. Juno had learned everything since: how to read a tide table like scripture, how to hold her breath until her vision went to stars, how to stand a watch in fog and pretend her hands weren't shaking. What she hadn't learned was how to be afraid of Ada. Other people managed it. The harbor men went quiet when she passed; Harrow's crews gave the *Kestrel* a wide berth at the fuel dock. But Juno had seen her the night after her father died, sitting on the breakwater with her boots in the water, and there was nothing frightening about a woman who cried the way ordinary people did, quietly, facing out to sea so no one would have to see.

Now Ada stood at the wheel with the fog closing around them like a held breath, and Juno thought she looked less like a captain than a woman walking into a room where something had happened a long time ago and was still waiting.

* * *

The fog thickened through the morning until the world was thirty feet wide and made of milk. They ran on compass and clock, engine throttled down to a mutter, and the sounds of the boat became enormous — the tick of the cooling pipes, the creak of the mast step, Bram's boots on the deck plates overhead. Juno took the noon watch in the wheelhouse while Ada slept in the chair by the chart table, or lay there with her eyes closed, which amounted to the same thing; Juno had never once caught her actually asleep.

Bram sat across from her, mending a net he refused to admit was beyond mending. He was a mountain going soft at the edges, gray in his beard, hands like mooring line, and he hummed when he worked — old shanties, half-remembered, always the same three verses because those were the only ones he'd ever learned.

"You're humming," Juno said.

"I'm thinking."

"You're humming the one about the drowned girl."

"That's a working song." He pulled the needle through. "It keeps the hands busy so the head can do its business."

"What business is your head doing?"

He looked at her over the net, and his eyes were kind and tired and older than the boat. "Yours is doing the same business. You keep looking at the fog like it owes you money."

Juno turned back to the window. The truth was she felt wonderful and terrible in equal measure, the way she'd felt before her first deep dive, before her first storm, before every threshold she'd ever crossed. They were doing it. After years of scraping brass off wrecks the charts didn't name, after winters of counting coins on the galley table while the engine coughed itself toward death, they were finally on a job big enough to matter. Triple rates. A new engine. A winter where nobody had to choose between diesel and food.

And it was *the Passage*. She knew what that name meant the way she knew the shape of her own scarred knuckles — from overheard arguments, from the way old men in the tavern went silent when a drunk said it too loud, from the torn page in a logbook nobody talked about. Her father had been offered that job once. He'd come home white as paper and told her mother they were leaving the coast entirely, and then he hadn't left, and then he'd drowned, and Juno had spent half her life learning not to ask questions about things adults went quiet over.

Now she was sailing north to ask the biggest question of them all, and the asking felt like standing on the edge of a trench in full gear, looking down into water that had no bottom that anyone admitted to.

"Bram," she said. "Do you think we should have voted no?"

The needle stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the engine and the fog dripping off the wheelhouse roof.

"I voted yes," he said finally, "because a man who loved Tom Montrose doesn't get to vote no twice. I voted no the first time this job came around. Twenty years ago, in this same wheelhouse, with your father standing where you're standing." He tied off the mend and folded the ruined net over his knee. "Tom said the sea keeps what it keeps, and some of it it keeps for a reason. Then he died anyway, and the reason didn't save him, and I've had twenty years to wonder if keeping my word to a dead man was worth watching this crew eat cabbage all winter."

"And was it?"

"I'll tell you when I know why we're really going." He nodded at the fog. "Vey's paying triple rates for a wreck that's been down twenty years and will still be down in twenty more. Men don't pay triple rates for patience. They pay them for fear of somebody else getting there first."

Juno felt the words settle into her stomach like cold lead. She'd been so busy being excited — new gear, real depth, Ada trusting her with the deep watch — that she hadn't asked the simplest question, the one they taught you first: *who else wants this, and why?*

The foghorn sounded. One blast, long, from somewhere off the port beam — close, closer than anything should have been, and wrong. Not the low moan of a fishing boat. The flat, doubled note of a government vessel.

Bram was on his feet before the second blast came.

* * *

Ada took the wheel without a word being spoken to her. That was the thing about the *Kestrel*'s crew, Juno thought later — they communicated like a body communicates, pain traveling from hand to brain without anyone deciding anything.

Through the fog, maybe two hundred yards off the port quarter, a shape resolved: gray hull, orange stack band, the blunt superstructure of a coast guard cutter. Not a patrol skiff. A cutter, out here, in fog, on a heading that — Juno checked the compass, checked it again — matched theirs almost exactly.

"They're paralleling us," Ada said. Her voice was level, almost bored, and Juno marveled at it even as her own mouth went dry. "Been on our beam since the point, I think. Holding just outside hailing distance."

"Routine patrol?" Bram asked.

"In fog? Off the restricted shelf?" Ada shook her head slowly. "Cutters don't do routine in weather like this unless they're looking for something specific. And they don't find something specific unless somebody told them where to look."

The words hung in the wheelhouse. Juno watched them land on each face and saw three different calculations happen. On Bram's, the grim arithmetic of an old man who'd suspected as much. On Wren's — the engineer, leaning in the companionway with grease to her elbows — the quick defensive flicker of someone whose radio had been in her care for six years. And on Ada's, nothing at all, which frightened Juno more than any expression could have.

"We alter course?" Wren asked.

"No." Ada's eyes stayed on the cutter. "We alter course, we tell them we've got something to hide, and they've got every right to board a salvage vessel in restricted approaches. We hold course and speed like three idiots going north to fish the edge grounds, and we let them get bored." A pause. "But we change our destination. Wren, when we clear the fog bank, put us south of the planned track. We'll come at the site from the east, the long way. Lose us half a day."

"Half a day," Bram said quietly. "Against another fleet, if there is one—"

"If there is one, they already know where we're going, and half a day won't matter. If there isn't, half a day costs us nothing but fuel." Ada finally looked away from the cutter, and her gaze swept the wheelhouse and settled, briefly, on each of them — Juno last, longest. "Somebody talked. Maybe at the fuel dock, maybe at the tavern, maybe—" she didn't finish it, didn't have to. Maybe aboard. "From here on, nobody transmits. Nobody mentions the Passage, the contract, or Vey on any frequency. We're a fishing boat running north, and that's all we've ever been."

The cutter held its station for another hour, a ghost in the milk, its horn sounding at intervals like something breathing. Then, as the fog began to thin into a pale silvering, it turned — slow, deliberate, unhurried — and stood away to the northeast, back toward the shipping lanes, as if it had merely been out for the air.

Nobody aboard believed that for a second.

* * *

They cleared the fog at dusk and ran south of their true course through a long red evening, the sea gone calm and glassy, the horizon burning down to embers. Vey's tender found them after dark, a single running light rising out of the black, and Cassian Vey came up the ladder with a seabag over his shoulder and that salvaged smile fixed in place.

"You're late," Ada said.

"You're off your track," he answered, easy as anything, and Juno saw Ada go very still. "Coast guard cutter shadowed you from the point. I watched it from the headland before I sailed. Thought you might want to know your route was known before you ever weighed anchor."

"We know," Ada said. "We've known all day."

Something passed between them then, in the wheelhouse lamplight — not warmth, nothing like warmth, but the grim recognition of two people discovering they might be standing on the same small island. Vey set down his bag and looked around at the crew, at the racked bottles, at Juno, and his smile softened into something almost honest.

"Well then," he said. "I suppose we'd best talk about who else knows."

Juno went forward to take the midnight watch alone, because someone had to, and because she wanted the cold and the dark and the sound of the water. The stars were out hard and clean, and the sea ran black beneath them, and somewhere ahead, past the night and the next night, the Passage waited on the Graveyard Shelf with whatever it kept.

She had wanted this her whole life — the real work, the deep work, the work that mattered. Standing her watch with the compass light green on her face, she found the wanting had changed shape. It wasn't excitement anymore. It was closer to what a diver feels at the top of a descent, when the line runs down into blue-black and the only way to know what's down there is to go down and see.

Behind her, low voices in the wheelhouse: Ada, Vey, Bram, arguing in murmurs about who had talked, and when, and to whom.

Ahead, the sea lay open and dark and patient, and Juno could not shake the feeling — irrational, childish, impossible to dismiss — that it was watching them back.
