## 6. The Graveyard Shelf

The fog broke an hour after dawn and gave them the shelf.

Ada stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Mercy* with a mug of tea gone cold in her hands and watched the water change color beneath them — the deep green of open sound giving way to something darker, bluer, a blue that had no warmth in it at all. The chart called it the Graveyard Shelf because charts are honest even when people aren't: a plateau of drowned rock running out from the coast like a shelf of broken crockery, and past its edge the seafloor dropped away into the trench, down past two hundred fathoms into cold black nothing that no diver had ever come back from and no salvage company had ever bothered to insure against.

Three wrecks lay on the shelf. She could see one of them on the sonar screen in the wheelhouse behind her, a smear of hard return sitting proud of the bottom like a rib through skin. The other two were further out, closer to the edge, as if the sea had been arranging them for twenty years the way a child arranges toy soldiers before knocking them all down.

"Coming up on the mark," Bram said from the helm. He had the wheel in both hands though the water was glass, the way he always did when they were over dead ground. "Half a mile."

Ada drank the cold tea anyway. It tasted like iron and kelp. "Bring us over it slow. I want a clean pass before anyone touches gear."

Behind her, Juno was already at the sonar, headphones half off one ear, hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a length of signal line. The girl had not slept well — none of them had, the first night north — but she worked the equipment with the hungry competence of someone who had learned everything she knew from watching Ada and had decided, without ever saying so, that competence was a form of devotion.

"Contact," Juno said. Then, quieter, almost reverent: "There she is."

The return bloomed on the screen. Ada set down her mug and leaned in.

The Passage lay on her starboard side along the inner lip of the shelf, half sunk into the soft silt where the plateau began its long slide toward the trench. Even degraded by twenty years of cold water, the hull signature was unmistakable to anyone who knew survey vessels the way Ada knew them — the long lean lines, the heavy keel laid down for stability in heavy seas, the deck cranes folded against the superstructure like the wings of a bird that had landed once and never intended to fly again.

She was bigger than Ada had imagined. That surprised her. She had spent twenty years imagining.

"Depth to the hull," Ada said.

"One-forty to the upturned plates," Juno read. "Bottom at one-sixty-five." A pause. "That's — that's deep. That's deeper than the navy wreck we did in March."

"It is." Ada straightened. One-forty was past the comfortable line. Past the line her father had drawn on every chart he'd ever owned, in red ink, with his own hand: *past this you're borrowing time from a lender who doesn't forget.* But it wasn't past the impossible line. Not quite. Mixed gas, short bottoms, long decompression, and a sea state that held. They'd done worse for less money. They'd done worse for no money at all.

"Bram," she called. "Take us wide. I want a full circuit before we anchor."

The *Mercy* swung out in a slow arc, engine at idle, and Ada went back to the rail and watched the water and let herself feel what she felt, which was nothing she had a name for. It was not grief exactly. Grief was a thing with edges. This was more like standing in a house you'd been told burned down decades ago and finding the walls still standing, still holding the shape of rooms, and realizing you didn't know whether you wanted to go inside or burn it properly yourself.

Twenty years ago the Passage had gone down in restricted water with eleven souls aboard, and the navy had closed the file, and her father had torn a page out of his logbook, and her mother — her mother, who according to every record that existed had never set foot on that ship — was somewhere down there in the dark with the rest of them.

*Somewhere down there.* Ada made herself look at the water and not away from it.

"There." Juno's voice cracked slightly. "Off the port bow. Sonar contact, small, moving. It's — Ada, it's a boat."

Ada crossed to the screen. The contact sat at the far edge of the display, beyond the shelf, out where the trench began, hugging the boundary between their world and the deep water. It was moving parallel to them. Slowly. Keeping its distance the way a wolf keeps distance from something it hasn't decided about yet.

"Range?"

"Two miles. Maybe two and a half. No transponder showing."

"No transponder." Ada studied the return. Fishing boats ran transponders because fishery patrols fined them if they didn't. Salvage boats ran them because insurance demanded it. Navy boats ran them because they wanted to. A boat with no transponder, shadowing them at the edge of restricted water, two days after Vey had told them the Passage was theirs alone to raise — that was not a boat that had stumbled onto them.

"Could be a smuggler," Bram offered from the helm, without conviction. He didn't believe it either. Smugglers ran dark at night, not in broad daylight over a graveyard.

"Could be." Ada kept her voice level, because Juno was listening and because the crew took its weather from her face the way sailors take it from the sky. "Log it. Time, bearing, range. If it's still there in an hour, log it again. If it closes inside a mile, wake me wherever I am."

"You're diving?" Juno asked, and there it was — the eagerness fighting the fear in her voice, the same fight Ada remembered having at nineteen with her own voice, her own hands, her own father watching her with that look that meant *you'll do it wrong unless you do it scared.*

"I'm diving." Ada turned for the gear deck. "Vey with me. You and Bram stay topside. Nobody else goes down today."

* * *

The Passage rose out of the green gloom like a cathedral that had fallen over.

Ada hung at forty feet on the descent line and saw the whole of her at once — the hull lying on its beam ends across the shelf, masts and crane arms furred white with cold-water coral, the superstructure collapsed inward like a fist closing slowly over twenty years. Fish moved through the wreck's open wounds in silver rivers. Light came down from the surface in long pale columns and died against the hull plating, and below the wreck the shelf fell away into a darkness so complete it seemed less like absence of light than presence of something else.

Cassian Vey hung beside her on the line, regulator hissing, eyes moving over the wreck with the flat professional sweep of a man reading a document. Twenty years ago he had been a junior officer. Now he was a salvage man with gray in his beard and a debt he wouldn't name, and he swam like someone who had learned late and practiced obsessively, every movement deliberate, nothing wasted.

He caught her looking and tapped his wrist gauge. *Fine. Continue.*

They went down together along the hull, finning above the coral-crusted plates, and Ada let her gloves trail near the steel without touching it. Cold radiated off the wreck even through the neoprene. At sixty feet she found the first damage and stopped.

It was a hole. A large one, maybe eight feet across, punched through the hull plating just aft of midships, the edges bent outward.

She hung there and stared at it and made herself think it through, because thinking was the discipline that kept divers alive, and because the alternative to thinking was feeling, and she had twenty years of feeling banked against this moment and no intention of spending any of it at a hundred feet in water the temperature of a grave.

Outward. The edges were bent outward.

A shell hit bends metal inward. A mine under the keel lifts the plates up and folds them back like petals. Torpedoes blow holes that flare outward at the rim but carry blast scarring across the surrounding plating, paint stripped, rivets sheared in ranks. Ada had spent her whole adult life reading wounded ships. She knew what violence did to steel the way a doctor knows what knives do to flesh.

This hole had no blast scarring. The paint around it was intact, blistered only by age. The edges of the plating curled outward smooth and clean, pushed from within, the way a tin can opens when you press your thumb through it from the inside.

She looked at Vey. He was already looking at her. Behind his mask his face was unreadable, but he didn't pretend not to understand what she was seeing. He pointed: *inside. Look inside.*

They swam the length of the hull and found three more openings. All outward. All clean-edged. And near the stern, where the engine room would have been, a fourth breach larger than the others, its rim peeled back like the lid of something opened deliberately, methodically — and there, welded to the inner frame where the plating had curled away from it, Ada saw the stub of a fitting she recognized, because she'd seen its twin on the navy wreck she'd been stripping brass from since she was old enough to hold a wrench.

Scuttling charges. Placed inside. Set and fired by her own crew, or by men who came aboard, or —

Or by the ship that had been escorting her.

Ada's breathing sounded enormous in her own ears. She checked her gauge — ninety feet, pressure fine, gas fine, mind not fine — and made herself run the numbers again because the numbers had to be wrong. Ships sank. That was what the sea did; it took things and broke them and kept them. Storms sank ships. Mines sank ships. Guns sank ships. But this — this was a ship opened from within like a letter, like a confession, like a man slitting his own throat rather than say what he knew. Someone had decided the Passage should die and had arranged it from the inside, gently, efficiently, and then the navy had written *lost in adverse conditions* in a file and closed it, and her father had torn a page out of his logbook, and her mother had drowned in a murder dressed as an accident.

Her mother had drowned knowing. That was the thought that finally got through the professional calm, slid under it like a knife under armor. Whatever the Passage carried, whatever the sealed cargo was, her mother had known enough to matter. Had been aboard as a passenger no record acknowledged. Had died with the truth in her hands or in her head, down here, in the dark, while Ada was learning to walk.

Vey touched her arm. Pointed to his gauge, then upward. *Time.*

She nodded. But before she turned for the ascent line she did one thing more — unclipped the small slate from her harness, wrote four words on it, and held it up so he had to see it:

**HOLED FROM THE INSIDE.**

Vey read it. His shoulders moved — a breath, a flinch, she couldn't tell which through the gear. Then he took the slate, wiped it with his thumb, and wrote beneath her words:

**I KNOW. THAT'S WHY I'M HERE.**

They ascended in silence, hanging on the line through the long stops while the cold pressed in and the wreck sank away beneath them into the blue-black, and Ada watched the surface brighten overhead and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the lungs, that the job had changed. Vey had hired her to raise a ship. She was going to find out who killed it, and who was aboard when it died, and why her mother's name appeared nowhere except where it shouldn't.

And two miles east, a boat with no name was waiting at the edge of the dark to see what they brought up.

When Ada broke the surface, the first thing she heard was Juno's voice, tight and fast, coming from the wheelhouse rail:

"Ada — it's closing. It's been closing for the last ten minutes. It's inside a mile."
