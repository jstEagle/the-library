## 7. Names on the Manifest

The Passage's chart room sat at the heart of the wreck like a swallowed heart, and Ada had to pass through the body to reach it.

She went in through the cargo hatch on the port side, the one Vey had marked on her slate the night before with a grease pencil and a hand that did not quite shake. *Aft corridor, third bulkhead, mind the deck plates.* He had drawn the ship from memory — a ship he had never served aboard, sunk twenty years before he'd ever set foot on a survey vessel — and when she'd asked him how, he'd only said, *They were all built off the same bones. I've spent my life inside those bones.*

She hadn't liked the answer. She liked it less now, swimming down the corridor with her lamp beam carving a tunnel through water the color of old tea.

Twenty years of silt had settled over everything in soft grey drifts, and where the current reached it moved like breath, lifting veils of sediment and letting them fall again. Fish scattered ahead of her light — small silver shoals that turned as one animal and poured through a gap in the ceiling plating. A pipe organ of coral had grown up through the galley doorway as she passed, orange and white and absurdly beautiful, blooming in a place where men had once argued about coffee rations.

*Third bulkhead.* Her depth gauge read forty-one meters. Her computer ticked quietly against her wrist, patient as a debt collector. She had maybe twenty minutes of bottom time at this depth before the numbers started turning ugly, and she intended to use every second of them on the chart room, because Vey had told her one more thing before she went over the side, standing at the rail with the fog beading in his grey hair:

*If there's anything left of the official record, it'll be in the chart room safe. Survey vessels kept their manifests sealed aboard. The navy's copy would have burned with the inquiry files — if there ever was an inquiry.*

*And if there's nothing?*

He had looked at her then with an expression she was beginning to recognize, a kind of flinching honesty, like a man showing her a wound on purpose. *Then you'll have dived forty meters for nothing, and we'll both have learned something about hope.*

The bulkhead door stood open, warped in its frame by whatever pressure wave had rolled through the ship when she went down. Ada pulled herself through sideways, careful of her line, careful of the jagged lip of steel that could open a suit like a letter.

Her lamp found paper first.

It was everywhere. Charts, rotted to pulp and fused into the silt like lichen. Logbook pages gone soft as leaves, their ink long dissolved into the water that had taken the ship. The chart room had been aft and low, below the waterline, and whatever crew had been stationed here when the flooding came —

She stopped that thought. Professional calm. Her father's voice in her head: *You can grieve on the surface, girl. Down here you work.* Except her father had never worked this wreck, had sworn his crew off it so absolutely that even his logbook barely named it, and the voice in her head now was her own, and it sounded tired.

The safe was where Vey said it would be: bolted behind the plotting table, a naval-standard strongbox the size of a sea chest, its dial fused with corrosion. But the table above it had buckled when the ship struck the shelf, and the impact had sprung the safe's door half open — a hand's width of darkness, a gap just wide enough for a gloved forearm.

Ada wedged herself against the plotting table's leg, checked her gauges — fourteen minutes — and reached into the gap.

Her fingers found sodden bundles. She drew them out one at a time, working them free of the safe's mouth with the patience of a woman defusing something, and stowed them in the mesh bag clipped at her hip. A sealed document case, wax-stamped, the navy's anchor-and-crown pressed deep enough into the wax to survive the water. A folio bound in oilcloth. And beneath them, wedged in the back corner where someone had hidden it rather than filed it, a slim ledger with a brass clasp, its cover stamped with a number instead of a name.

She was reaching back for a last flat packet when her lamp caught the wall beside the safe.

A board. Not a notice board exactly — a crew board, the kind ships mounted near the mess for duty rosters and mail lists and, on survey vessels, the passenger manifest for each leg of a commission. The paper on it had rotted away entirely except for one sheet, laminated against the steel under a strip of clear celluloid that some tidy warrant officer had applied against salt air, and which the sea, for once, had respected.

PASSENGER MANIFEST — FINAL LEG, the header read. The ink beneath had faded to the brown of dried blood but held its lines. Eleven names. Crew berthed forward; passengers berthed aft, cabin 4.

Ada read the list the way she read sonar returns — top to bottom, fast, hunting the anomaly.

She found it in the fourth line from the bottom.

MONTROSE, ELENA R. — PASSENGER — CABIN 4 — MANIFESTED AT PORT CAIRN, 14 MARCH.

The water in the chart room did not change temperature. Her suit did not fail. Her regulator delivered air exactly as it had a moment before. And yet the whole ocean seemed to tilt, the way the horizon tilts when a swell passes under a boat too large to feel, and Ada hung in the flooded room holding a dead navy's sealed papers and understood that every fact she had ever stood on had a hole in it the size of her mother.

*Passenger.* Not crew. Every record — the memorial plaque at Port Cairn, the compensation file Bram had fought for three years, the story her father told and never elaborated, the story the whole harbor told — *lost with the ship, serving aboard her.* Elena Montrose had died a crew member in every telling. She had died a passenger in the only telling that mattered, the one written down while she was alive, in her own ship, in her own hand or the hand of whoever signed her aboard.

Manifested at Port Cairn. Fourteen March. Ada did the arithmetic without wanting to. Fourteen March was eleven days before the Passage went down. Eleven days before her mother boarded a ship her husband's crew had sworn never to touch, as a *passenger*, in a cabin aft, carrying something worth sealing in wax.

Her computer chirped. Nine minutes.

Ada's hands were steady. That was the strange thing, the thing she would remember afterward — that her hands went on working, clipping the manifest board free of its bolts with her pry tool, folding the laminated sheet with a care she usually reserved for live ordnance, stowing it against her chest where the mesh bag wouldn't chafe it. Grief happened somewhere else, in some other part of her, muffled like machinery through a bulkhead. Down here you work.

She took one last look around the chart room with her lamp, sweeping the beam across the pulp of charts, the dead plotting table, the sprung safe. Somewhere in this room, eleven days before the end, a clerk had written her mother's name on a list and screwed it to a wall, and no one had ever come back to read it.

*I'm reading it,* she thought, and pushed off for the corridor, and the sea closed behind her like a mouth.

* * *

Her mother's hands smelled of oranges.

That was the memory that came, unasked, as Ada hung at fifteen meters watching her ascent needle crawl, deco obligation stacking up in amber numbers on her wrist. Not her mother's face — she had photographs for that, a whole album of a woman squinting into harbor sunlight — but her hands, peeling an orange at the galley table of another boat, another life, the rind coming off in one long unbroken spiral because she'd learned it somewhere, from someone, and little Ada had demanded she do it again and again until the whole bag was gone.

*Where'd you learn that?*

*From a man on a ship,* her mother had said, and laughed, and given her the last segment, and Ada had been four years old and satisfied with that answer forever.

Twenty years later, hanging in cold green water with a dead navy's secrets against her chest, the answer finally opened like the orange. From a man on a ship. Which ship? What ship carried a woman who peeled fruit in one spiral and spoke to sailors easily enough that they taught her things? What kind of passenger learns a ship's habits in eleven days?

Other fragments surfaced as she rose, unmoored now, each one suddenly evidence. Her mother singing in a language nobody in Port Cairn spoke, and when Ada asked what it was, being told *an old song* and nothing more. Her mother's trunk, which her father had kept locked and which Bram had shipped to Ada on her eighteenth birthday still locked, because opening it felt like a theft neither of them could name. Her mother arguing once, low and fierce, through the cabin door of the house on the hill — not with her father; the other voice had been a stranger's — saying words Ada had been too young to keep and old enough now to reconstruct from rhythm alone: *you promised me it would go ashore. You promised me it would reach the light.*

She had thought, all her childhood, that memory of that night was a dream. Children dreamed of voices through doors. Children dreamed of mothers who smelled of oranges and sang in foreign tongues and vanished into the sea leaving behind a locked trunk and a husband who aged ten years in a season.

But passengers don't argue about cargo going ashore. Crew don't promise. Couriers do.

The word arrived in her head at twelve meters, unbidden, unwelcome, and fit itself to the shape of everything with a click she felt in her teeth. Her mother had been carrying something. Had promised someone it would reach the light. Had boarded the Passage as a passenger because passengers don't appear on crew rolls, don't stand musters, can slip aboard and off again without a signature — except someone had signed her aboard anyway, had put her name on a manifest and screwed it to a wall, and that single act of clerical tidiness had outlived the lie built over it, outlived the inquiry that never happened, outlived her father's silence, and waited twenty years in the dark for a woman with a lamp to come read it.

At nine meters Ada made her safety stop and hung in the featureless green, breathing slow, watching her bubbles climb and flatten against the surface far above like a ceiling of mercury.

Her mother had been on that ship on purpose. Her mother had died trying to get off it with whatever was in the wax-sealed case now riding against Ada's ribs. And her father — her father, who swore his crews off certain wrecks, who tore pages from his own logbook, who drank himself quiet every fourteenth of March without ever saying why — her father had known. Had known and buried it deeper than the Passage lay, and taken the truth to his grave rather than let his daughter drown in it.

*You promised me it would go ashore.*

The stop released her. Ada kicked for the light.

* * *

Bram met her at the ladder, as he always did, big hands ready on the rail, and the look on his face told her he'd read something in her eyes before she'd said a word. Twenty years of hauling her out of water had made him fluent in her.

"Talk," he said, peeling her mask for her, an intimacy neither of them remarked on anymore.

"Inside." She climbed over the rail on legs that felt borrowed. "Not on deck."

The wheelhouse was warm and close and smelled of diesel and Bram's terrible coffee. Juno was there, coiling line with the restless energy of the young, and Vey stood at the chart table pretending to study a course he already knew. Ada set the mesh bag on the table and unpacked it piece by piece: the document case, wax seal intact. The oilcloth folio. The numbered ledger with its brass clasp. And last, unfolded flat, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug and two brass fittings, the manifest.

Nobody touched anything. Nobody breathed, almost.

"Chart room safe," Ada said. Her voice came out level, which cost her more than shouting would have. "Sprung open when she hit the shelf. Papers are wet but whole. And this—" she turned the manifest toward them, "—was bolted to the wall."

Vey leaned in first. She watched him read, watched his lips move slightly along the names, and saw the exact moment he found hers — saw him go still in a way that had nothing to do with surprise. He had known. Some part of him had known what she would find, and had sent her down anyway, because it was hers to find.

"Elena Montrose," Juno read aloud, frowning. "Passenger?" She looked up, young face open, the question innocent and enormous. "But she was crew. Everyone says—"

"Everyone says wrong." Ada kept her eyes on the page. "Or everyone was told wrong. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and I think we're going to learn it."

Bram hadn't spoken. He stood with his knuckles resting on the table's edge, staring at the manifest the way men stare at graves, and when he finally lifted his head his eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all three at once, and Ada understood that he had loved her mother too — that everyone who had ever sailed with her father had loved Elena Montrose, quietly, the way you love a lighthouse.

"Fourteen March," Bram said hoarsely. "She boarded eleven days before she died. Your father was in port that week. He never—" His jaw worked. "He never said she was going. We'd have known. We'd have—"

"You'd have what?" Ada asked gently. "Stopped her?"

Bram had no answer. Outside, the fog pressed against the wheelhouse glass, and beyond the fog, somewhere past the edge of the shelf, the sonar's ghost of a shadowing vessel sat motionless in Juno's last sweep, patient as the sea itself.

Vey straightened up from the manifest. For the first time since the tavern, since the card Bram had pocketed, since any of it, he looked older than his years and certain of something.

"The wax seal's intact," he said quietly. "Whatever your mother died carrying, it's still in that case. And Ada—" he met her eyes, and did not flinch from what he found there, "—the people who sank this ship to bury that case will come for it. They came once already. They're out there now."

Ada looked down at the manifest, at her mother's name riding the fourth line from the bottom, manifested at Port Cairn, fourteen March, cabin 4 — a woman recorded plainly in her own handwriting-shaped truth after twenty years of erasure — and felt the vertigo settle into something harder, something with edges.

"Then they'd better bring a boat that dives," she said.
