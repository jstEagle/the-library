## 8. Vey's Confession

Cassian Vey had spent twenty years learning how to be alone in rooms full of people, and he was good at it. He could stand at a bar with a glass of something he didn't drink and become furniture. He could sit at a captain's table and be a contract, a signature, a number on a ledger. He had built an entire second self out of that talent — the salvage broker, the man with triple rates and half-answers — and for six days aboard the *Kestrel's Fortune* it had held.

It broke on the seventh night, somewhere past the Graveyard Shelf, in the hour after Ada came up from the chart room with the manifest crushed in her fist and her mother's name written on it in a dead clerk's hand.

He heard her before he saw her. Not shouting — she wasn't a woman who shouted. It was the sound of her boots on the deck plates, too even, too measured, the walk of someone holding themselves together with rigging line and will. She crossed the aft deck toward him where he stood at the rail with the fog coming down off the water like a held breath, and she stopped three feet away, close enough that he could see the salt dried white on her collar, and she said:

"You knew."

Not a question. He made himself meet her eyes anyway.

"I need you to say it," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it to my face or get off my boat."

*My boat.* Her father's boat, once. Vey had stood on this same deck plating twenty-two years ago, in a different life, wearing a different uniform underneath his civilian coat, and watched Silas Montrose say almost the same words to a navy liaison who'd come to buy his silence. Some things came back around like tide.

"Below," he said quietly. "Not here."

"There's no one here but your ghosts and mine."

"There's Juno," he said. "There's Bram. There are four people behind that wheelhouse door who signed on for a salvage job, not for this." He turned his glass in his fingers — tea now; he hadn't touched alcohol since the Passage entered his dreams every night instead of only some nights. "What I have to tell you doesn't belong to the whole deck."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then she jerked her chin toward the forward hold, where they kept the dive gear, because it was the only room on the boat with a door that locked from the inside, and because, he suspected, she wanted him standing in a room full of the equipment that had brought them all here, breathing air that smelled of rubber and brine and money spent.

* * *

The hold lamp swung gently on its hook, throwing their shadows up the bulkhead in slow arcs. Ada sat on an ammo crate that predated both of them, arms folded, and waited. She had always been good at waiting. It was one of the things he'd hated about her when she was nineteen and beating him to every wreck on the coast — that patience, that refusal to be rushed into error. She'd gotten it from Silas.

Vey didn't sit. He found that if he sat while saying certain things, his body decided they were over, and they were never going to be over.

"You want to know how I knew the Passage's hull numbers," he said. "How I knew the strongroom was portside amidships, below the waterline, behind a pressure door most survey vessels don't carry. How I knew the chart room would flood last." He swallowed. His mouth tasted like the inside of a diving helmet. "You've been asking yourself those questions since the first dive. You're too disciplined to ask me out loud. So I'll answer the question you haven't asked."

"Vey—"

"My name isn't Cassian Vey."

The lamp swung. Her shadow leaned across the deck and righted itself.

"It was," he said. "Twenty years ago. Before the court records were sealed and the service record was burned and a very patient man in a very gray office explained to me what a new name could buy. Cassian Vey is the man they let me become. The man I was is buried at forty-one degrees north, ninety fathoms down, along with thirty-six other people." He made himself keep going, because if he stopped now he would never start again. "I was a junior signals officer aboard the *HMS Corvane*, corvette, coastal patrol. The night the Passage went down, I had the midwatch. I was the one who logged the contact. I was the one who relayed the order to fire."

Ada had gone very still. Stillness, on her, was louder than screaming.

"She was running dark," Vey said. "No lights, no transponder, hugging the shelf to stay out of the shipping lanes. Standard procedure for a vessel carrying something she shouldn't. We were told she was smuggling. Arms, gold, defectors — the story changed depending on which officer was talking, which should have been the first warning. A real intelligence picture doesn't shift like weather. But I was twenty-three years old and I had a lieutenant's voice in my ear telling me the contact was authorized, and I did what I was trained to do. I confirmed the firing solution." His hands had gone cold. He looked at them as though they belonged to the boy he'd been. "Two rounds. The first missed. The second didn't. And then we closed to visual, and we watched her go down for eleven minutes, and we did not launch boats."

"Why?" Ada said. One word, flat as a depth charge.

"Because she wasn't smuggling anything. She was *carrying* something. A ledger. Records of arms shipments moving through coastal waters under navy escort flags — sold to buyers we were officially blockading, paid for through accounts that led back up the chain so high that when the first inquiry opened, it didn't climb, it was drowned. The Passage's captain was going to surface it. Someone above him decided thirty-seven deaths was cheaper than one document." Vey finally sat, because his legs had made the decision without consulting him, and the crate creaked under him. "I didn't know any of that that night. I knew it eight months later, when the survivors' fund paid out too fast, when the wreck site got designated restricted before the bodies were recovered, when a man from the Admiralty sat across from me in a room with no windows and told me that my testimony — my *logbook* — had been handled, and that my career, or my silence, was my choice. I took the silence. They gave me the new name and a broker's license and twenty years of being comfortable, and I have spent every one of those years finding out that comfort is just guilt with better upholstery."

The lamp swung. Somewhere above them, the hull ticked as the temperature dropped, the boat settling into the night like an animal folding its legs.

Ada hadn't moved. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet enough that he had to lean toward it, and quiet, on her, was worse than loud.

"The manifest," she said. "Elena Montrose. Passenger, not crew. Every record says she died ashore. Every record says she was never aboard."

"Yes."

"She was the courier." It wasn't a guess. He watched her arrive at it the way you watch someone touch a live rail — the whole body seizing around the knowledge. "She was carrying the ledger. That's why she was listed as crew on the real manifest and passenger on the fake one. That's why—" Her breath caught, and for a moment the discipline cracked and he saw the nine-year-old underneath, the girl who'd been told her mother died of fever in a port town two hundred miles from where the Passage actually sank. "That's why my father tore the page out of his logbook. That's why he swore the crew would never take this job. He knew. He knew what she was doing, and he knew why the navy sank her, and he spent the rest of his life keeping me away from it because he thought the truth would get me killed the way it got her killed."

"Yes," Vey said again. It was all he had. Yes, yes, yes — twenty years of yes, stacked up like ballast.

"And you knew she was aboard." Ada stood. She didn't raise her voice. She walked toward him slowly, and he understood that he was being approached the way a diver approaches something in deep water — carefully, because panic kills faster than pressure. "When you walked into that tavern. When you offered triple rates. When you let us vote, let Bram argue for the engine, let Juno pack her kit like it was an adventure. You knew my mother was on that ship, and you knew what she was carrying, and you hired her daughter to raise it."

"I needed a diver who could reach the strongroom," Vey said. "And I needed—" He stopped. Lies came easily to him; that was the poison gift of the new name. He refused it. "I told myself I needed the best. That was true. It wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth is that I couldn't do it alone. Twenty years I've tried to do it alone — petitions, inquiries, letters to newspapers that never ran. The sea keeps what it keeps unless someone goes down and takes it back, and I am fifty-three years old and I can't dive past sixty feet anymore. So I went looking for Silas Montrose's daughter, because she's the best salvage diver on this coast, and because—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "Because her mother died trying to bring that ledger up, and I fired the round that stopped her. If anyone has the right to finish it, it's her blood. If anyone should hate me for needing it done, it's also her blood. Both things are true. I've made peace with carrying both."

"Have you." Ada stood over him, and the swinging light put her face alternately in amber and shadow. "Made peace."

"No," he said. "God, no. Peace is for people whose hands are clean. I'm not asking for peace. I'm asking for work."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. In the hold, the gear hung in its racks like sleeping things — harnesses, helmets, the spare tanks lashed against the roll. Vey counted his own heartbeats and lost count twice.

Then Ada turned away from him. She walked to the rack where the dive knives hung, and she took one down — not threateningly, just to hold it, the way her father used to hold a tool while thinking — and she turned it over in her hands, blade catching the lamplight.

"Do you know what I remember about her?" she said, not looking at him. "My mother. I remember a yellow scarf. I remember she sang off-key, and my father pretended it was beautiful. I remember being lifted onto her shoulders once, at the harbor, and being taller than everyone." Her thumb moved along the knife's spine. "Four memories. That's what twenty years of careful lying left me. Four memories and a grave marker with the wrong date on it, in a town she never visited." She set the knife back in its rack, precisely, aligning it with the others. "And you want to know the worst part? The worst part is that everything makes sense now. The restricted water. The sealed file. My father drinking himself blind every year on the anniversary and never saying why. I spent my whole life thinking my family was just unlucky. We weren't unlucky. We were *managed*. Somebody in a gray office decided what my childhood got to know about my own mother, and you were the pen they wrote it with."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes to me again." She turned, and her eyes were dry, which somehow was more terrible than tears. "You don't get the small words. You gave up the small words the night you confirmed that firing solution. What you get is the big ones. So say the big one. Say what you're actually asking me for."

Vey stood up. His knees ached; he was suddenly aware of every one of his fifty-three years, of the distance between the boy at the sonar screen and the man in the hold, and how little of it was distance at all.

"I'm asking you to finish what she started," he said. "Bring the ledger up. Put it in daylight. Let the names in it burn whoever they burn, including me — my signature is in it, Ada. The Corvane's firing log cross-references the shipments. I'm in that book. I'm not asking you to save me from it. I'm asking you to make sure it gets read."

"And then?"

"And then whatever you decide I deserve."

Ada looked at him for a long, long moment. Outside, faint through the hull, he could hear Juno laughing at something Bram had said, the ordinary music of a crew that trusted its charter, and the sound of it made the hold feel colder.

"I don't forgive you," she said at last. Each word placed like a footing stone in current. "I want to be clear about that, so you never mistake anything I do next for absolution. I don't forgive you tonight, and I might not forgive you ever, and forgiveness isn't mine to give anyway — it belongs to thirty-six other people, and most of them are past giving anything." She stepped closer. "But my mother died trying to get that ledger to the surface, and you're the only man alive who knows exactly what's between it and daylight. So tomorrow morning you're going to stand in that wheelhouse and you're going to tell me every bolt, every bulkhead, every inch of the Corvane's gunnery and the Passage's construction, until I know that ship better than the people who sank her. That's your penance. Not my forgiveness. *Work.*"

"That's more than I hoped for," Vey said, and meant it.

"It's less than you deserve." She moved past him toward the door, then stopped with her hand on the latch, her back to him. "One more thing. My father. Did he know what you were? When the liaison came to buy his silence — did he know he was talking to the ship that fired?"

Vey closed his eyes. "He figured it out. Halfway through the meeting. I saw him understand." He opened them. "He never reported me. He never told you. He carried it the way I carry it — except he carried it honestly, and I bought my way out of the weight. That's the difference between us, and it's the difference between him and me, and I've had twenty years to learn it's the only thing that matters."

Ada was silent. When she spoke, her voice had gone rough at the edges.

"He'd have thrown you off this boat."

"Yes."

Instead she opened the door and left him alone in the hold with the swinging lamp and the hanging gear and the sound of the sea pressing patiently against the hull, and Vey sat back down on the crate and put his face in his hands and did not pray, because he had stopped believing he was owed an answer, but he said her name anyway — Elena, Elena — into his palms, like a man testing whether a wound still bleeds.

It did. Good. He'd earned the bleeding. What he hadn't earned, and what terrified him more than Harrow's fleet or the restricted water or the fathoms below, was the possibility that Ada Montrose would spend it wisely.

Above him, boots crossed the deck. A knock at the hold door — Bram's knuckles, heavy and unhurried.

"Cassian," the old quartermaster said through the steel, and there was something in his voice Vey had never heard there before, something that had been waiting twenty years to be said. "You and me. Now. There's a light on the horizon that's been sitting there for an hour, and it's not fishing."
