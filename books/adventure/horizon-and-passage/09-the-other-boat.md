## 9. The Other Boat

Juno heard it before anyone else — a low thrumming through the hull, felt in the soles of her boots rather than heard with her ears, like the heartbeat of something enormous swimming just beneath the threshold of sound. She was in the galley at the time, scrubbing a pot that didn't need scrubbing because her hands needed something to do while Ada and Vey argued in the chart room about decompression tables, and she froze with the rag dripping in her fist and said, to no one, *that's not weather.*

The engine noise of the *Kestrel's Grace* had a texture Juno had learned the way other children learned lullabies: the cough of the old diesel on a cold start, the steady four-stroke hum at cruising, the thin whine when Bram pushed her harder than she liked. This was none of those. This was deeper, smoother, the sound of many engines turning in time, and it was getting louder, and it was coming from the north, from the direction of the shelf, from the direction of the Passage.

She dropped the pot. It rang against the deck plating, a small bright sound, absurdly loud, and she was already moving, out the galley door and up the companionway ladder two rungs at a time, out into the gray afternoon light where the fog had thinned to gauze and the whole world smelled of cold iron and salt.

Ada was already at the rail, binoculars up. Vey stood beside her, and Juno saw — for the first time since he'd come aboard — that the man's hands were shaking.

"What is it?" Juno asked, and her voice came out younger than she wanted it to. "What's out there?"

Ada didn't answer. She lowered the binoculars, raised them again, as if the first look had been a mistake her eyes were trying to correct. Then she passed them to Juno without a word.

The fog was peeling back off the water in long rags, and through the gap, maybe two miles out, Juno saw them.

Three vessels. The lead ship was a converted trawler the size of a small cathedral, black-hulled, with a crane on her aft deck that could lift a bus and a superstructure bristling with antennas. Painted on her bow in letters that even at this distance Juno could read without the glasses: *HARROW & SONS — MARINE SALVAGE & RECOVERY*. Behind her came a work barge and a fast patrol launch, white and mean, cutting a wake like a drawn knife.

"Contractors," Vey said. The word came out of him flat and dead, like a coin dropped on stone. "That's Harrow's flagship. The *Iron Meridian*. I've seen her in drydock at Corvane. She's got a saturation dive system aboard, a hyperbaric complex, a full crew of navy-trained divers on rotation." He swallowed. Juno watched his throat move. "They're not here to compete with us. They're here to erase us."

"Then they know what's down there," Ada said. Her voice was very quiet, very level, the voice she used on the boat when the weather turned and everyone aboard needed to believe she wasn't afraid. Juno had heard that voice a hundred times and had believed in it completely for years, and only now, watching the tendons stand out in Ada's neck like cable under load, did she understand it was not the absence of fear but the decision to make fear useful. "They know exactly what's down there. Which means someone told them where we were going."

The silence that followed was worse than the engines. Juno looked from face to face: Ada at the rail, Vey with his shaking hands, Bram emerging from below with a wrench still in his fist and a look on his face like a man arriving at a fire he already knew was his own house. Sef and Marlow came up behind him, and then Odile, and the whole crew of the *Kestrel's Grace* — seven souls and a dying engine and a contract signed in blood-quiet desperation — stood at the rail and watched the other boat come for them.

"They've been shadowing us since the graveyard," Vey said. "Sonar contact beyond the shelf. I thought it was a fishing boat riding the thermocline. I was wrong."

"You were wrong," Ada repeated, and there was something in the way she said it that Juno had never heard before — not anger, exactly, but the sound a person makes when they discover that the ground they've been standing on was never ground at all.

* * *

They gathered in the chart room, and the chart room had never felt so small.

Juno took the bench by the bulkhead, the one that put her back against steel and let her see the whole room, because that was where Bram had taught her to sit in any confined space: back to something solid, eyes on the exits. She was nineteen and had been diving since she was fifteen and had grown up on this boat the way other children grew up in houses, but she had never been in a room where the air itself seemed to have changed weight. Fear did that. Fear was a gas, Bram said. It displaced everything else.

Ada stood over the chart table with both hands flat on it, and Vey stood across from her, and between them the chart of the Graveyard Shelf lay under a scatter of brass weights like a body under a shroud.

"How did they find us?" Ada asked. "Think. Every possibility. The harbor master at Ternholt. The fuel dock. The weather service. Anyone who could have sold our heading."

"Or someone aboard," Sef said, and the words dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Juno felt every head turn — not toward Sef, but toward the space in the middle of the room, the empty space where a traitor would stand if there was one.

"Don't," Bram said. One word, quiet, and it landed harder than a shout. The old man hadn't sat down. He stood with his back to the door, arms crossed over a chest like a sea chest, and Juno had known him her whole life and had never seen him look old until this moment. "We don't do that. Not on this boat. Your father—" and here he looked at Ada, and his voice caught on something and cleared itself, "—your father had a rule. You can suspect the whole world, but you don't suspect your own crew. The day you start suspecting your own crew is the day you don't have one."

"And if the whole world bought one of them?" Sef said. He wasn't being cruel. Juno could see that. He was being frightened, and frightened men do arithmetic out loud. "Harrow's paying divers triple rates on the coast. Everyone knows it. Double for silence. We're poor people on a poor boat sitting on top of something worth more than all of us put together. Do the sums, Bram. Do them honestly."

"I've done them," Bram said. "I've done them every night since we signed Vey's paper. And I'll tell you what they say. They say we're seven people who've got nothing left to sell but each other, and I'll drown before I let this boat become the kind of place where we start pricing one another."

Nobody spoke. The engine hummed beneath them, steady, faithful, the one thing aboard with no opinion about any of it.

Ada straightened. "Nobody's a traitor until somebody's a traitor," she said. "Until then, we work. Vey. How long before they're on top of us?"

"Forty minutes. Less, if they're not being careful, and they won't be. They don't have to be." Vey pulled a pencil from behind his ear and began, without seeming to decide to, marking positions on the chart. Juno watched his hands. They had stopped shaking. There was, she thought, a particular kind of calm that came over certain men when the disaster actually arrived — the waiting was the unbearable part, and the event itself was almost a relief. "They'll come alongside. They'll make an offer. When we refuse it — and we will refuse it, Ada, because I know you, and because the offer will be designed to be refused — they'll do something to remind us they can. A warning. Nothing that leaves a mark they'd have to explain to an insurer. And then they'll wait for us to make a mistake."

"And we have how long?"

"Three tides." Vey's pencil stopped. "Three tides before their saturation system is rigged and they can work the wreck around the clock without surfacing. After that, whatever's down there belongs to them, and we become a story they tell each other at dinner."

Ada looked down at the chart for a long moment. When she raised her head, Juno saw something in her face that frightened her more than the ships outside — not fear, not resolve, but a kind of cold, gathering certainty, like the pressure change before a storm front.

"Then we don't wait three tides," Ada said. "We dive tomorrow. Deeper than we planned."

* * *

Juno couldn't sleep, so she went up.

It was near midnight and the fog had come back thick as wool, and the *Kestrel's Grace* rode at anchor in the lee of the shelf with a single riding light burning, and out in the fog, maybe six hundred yards off, the Harrow fleet had anchored too — close, deliberately close, close enough that Juno could see the blur of their deck lights through the murk like the eyes of something watching from inside a wall.

She climbed to the foredeck and sat with her back against the windlass and pulled her coat around her and tried to be brave in private, the way she always did, because Ada never let anyone see her afraid and Juno had decided years ago that she would learn to be the same kind of person.

She was failing at it. Her hands wouldn't stop moving. She kept flexing them, opening and closing, feeling the ghost of the dive glove, the regulator, the line — all the small familiar tools of a trade that had never once, in five years, felt like this. Diving was dangerous, but it was a danger with rules, a danger you could study and beat, a danger that behaved like a gentleman if you behaved like a professional. This was different. This was men in a lit ship in the fog deciding what to do with you, and there was no table for that, no depth, no bottom time, no decompression schedule for what Harrow & Sons might decide to do to a salvage crew that wouldn't sell.

The deck creaked. She didn't startle — she'd learned that much — but her heart slammed anyway, and then Bram lowered himself onto the windlass housing beside her with the groan of a man twice his age and handed her a mug of tea without asking if she wanted it. He always knew. He had always known, her whole life, when the fear got too big to hold alone.

"Can't sleep either?" she said, though she knew the answer.

"Sleep's for people with nothing on their minds." He blew on his tea. "Or too much."

"Bram." She wrapped both hands around the mug because they needed the work. "Are we going to be all right?"

The old man was quiet for a while. Out in the fog, the Harrow lights shifted — someone walking a deck, maybe, or just the motion of the ship at anchor, it was impossible to tell, and that was the whole terror of it, that everything out there could be anything.

"No," he said at last, and Juno's stomach dropped, and then he went on, in that gravel voice that had taught her knots and tables and how to read water color from a masthead: "No, I don't expect we will. Not all right the way we were. That's not how this works, girl. You go through a thing like this and you come out different on the other side, and the only question worth asking is what kind of different."

"What kind are we going to be?"

He took his time. He always took his time; it was one of the first things she'd loved about him, back when she was small and Ada's father was alive and the boat had been a warmer place, that Bram never gave you an answer until the answer was true.

"Harder," he said finally. "Harder and closer. Fear does that, if you let it. It either scatters a crew like sparrows or it packs them down like coal in a hold. And this crew—" he knocked his knuckles against the deck, twice, the way he did when he meant the boat herself, "—this crew packs down. I've seen it already. Did you see Sef tonight, standing up to me? Sef, who's never contradicted a living soul? And Odile checking everyone's gear twice over without being asked? And Marlow sitting up with the radio all night listening for coast guard traffic nobody asked him to listen for?" He shook his head slowly. "That's not a crew getting ready to break, Juno. That's a crew getting ready to hold."

Juno looked down into her tea. "I'm scared," she said, because it was Bram, and with Bram you could say it.

"Good," he said.

"Good?"

"Scared means you understand what's at stake. The ones I worry about are the ones who aren't." He nodded toward the aft cabin, where Ada's light still burned. "She's scared too. She's just decided the fear is fuel. That girl's been burning her fear for years. I sometimes wonder what's left underneath." He drained his tea and stood, joints cracking like line over a sheave, and put his huge hand on Juno's head for a moment, the way he'd done since she was eight. "Get some sleep if you can. Tomorrow we work. And tomorrow night—" he paused, and Juno saw him look out at the fog, at the lights, at the shape of the trouble they were in, "—tomorrow night, Ada's going down past the safe depth, and she'll need every one of us watching her line. That includes you. So sleep."

He went below. Juno stayed.

She was still there an hour later, cold through and too wired to move, when the sound came — a single flat *crack* rolling across the water from the direction of the Harrow fleet, sharp as a door slamming in an empty house, followed by a long silence in which the fog itself seemed to hold its breath.

Then the flare went up. It climbed out of the murk trailing red sparks, hung at the top of its arc directly over the *Kestrel's Grace*, and burst — a bloom of burning crimson light that turned the fog to embers and threw the whole deck in blood color and lit Ada Montrose standing in the companionway, already awake, already dressed, already looking north with an expression Juno would carry for the rest of her life.

"A warning shot," Ada said, to the crew now stumbling up behind her, to the fog, to the ships in it, to the drowned ship beneath them all. Her voice was calm. Her face in the falling red light was not. "They want us to know they can reach us whenever they want."

She turned, and the flare died, and the dark came back in like a tide.

"Then they'd better understand something," Ada said. "We dive at first light. Three tides is enough."

Juno sat in the dark with her heart going like a hammer on a hull, and understood that the family she'd grown up in had just changed into something else — something harder, something that would hold or break under load, and that there was no longer any way to know which until the load came.
