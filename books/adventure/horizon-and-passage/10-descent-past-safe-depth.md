## 10. Descent Past Safe Depth

The strongroom was forty feet below the deck of the *Passage*, and the *Passage* was a hundred and ninety feet down, and Ada Montrose had spent the whole night doing arithmetic that kept coming out the same way: too deep, too far, too long.

She did it anyway.

She sat on the gunwale of the *Kestrel's* dive deck in the grey hour before dawn, working the numbers again because the numbers were the only thing she could argue with. Bottom time at that depth: eleven minutes, maybe twelve if she burned her reserve. Descent at thirty meters a minute on the shot line. Ascent with two mandatory decompression stops that would leave her hanging in open water like bait on a hook. Surface interval: none, because there wouldn't be a second dive. One breath of the ship's dead air, one look inside the strongroom, one chance.

"You could take the rebreather," Juno said behind her, and Ada heard the hope in it, the young diver's faith that there was always a cleverer way, a better rig, a trick that would make the impossible merely difficult.

"The rebreather's scrubber canisters are spent. We've been through this."

"Then take me down as your standby."

"No." Ada said it without turning around. "You're the only one besides me who can run the backup line from the surface if this goes wrong, and if it goes wrong, someone has to be able to think clearly. That's you. Congratulations."

"That's a terrible thing to congratulate me for."

"It's the only promotion I have to give."

Bram came up from the engine room smelling of diesel and sweat, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last month. He didn't say anything at first. He just stood at the rail beside her and looked down at the water, the way he'd looked at it for thirty years, the way her father had looked at it before him.

"You slept four hours," he said.

"You counted?"

"I always count. You dive past a hundred and eighty feet on air, your judgment's already gone before your fins get wet. Four hours of sleep makes it worse." He folded the rag into a square with the precision of a man who had once kept a Navy ship's stores and never unlearned it. "I'm not telling you not to go. I want that on the record. I'm telling you I know you're going, and I hate every second of it, and I'll be on the line."

"I know."

"Your father would've gone too."

"That's supposed to help?"

"No." Bram's voice was gravel over something softer. "It's supposed to be true."

* * *

Vey was waiting at the shot line when she finished kitting up, and he had drawn something on the back of a chart with a grease pencil: the *Passage*'s aft section in cross-section, corridors marked, hatches marked, and one small square amidships labeled in his tight officer's hand — **STRONGRM. ACCESS: COMPARTMENT 2-ALPHA.**

"Survey vessels don't usually carry strongrooms," Ada said, taking the chart. "That's what I kept coming back to. She was a survey ship. Charts, samples, instruments. Why does a ship like that have a vault?"

"Because she wasn't only a survey ship." Vey stood with his hands clasped behind his back, and Ada recognized the posture — parade rest, the stance of a man who had spent years being inspected and never fully unlearned it. "The navy converted her hull in drydock before her last deployment. I've seen the refit schematics. Compartment 2-Alpha was added between the frame ribs. Steel plate, not bulkhead standard — vault standard. One hatch, dogged from inside, with a pressure seal rated for depth. Whatever's in there, they built the ship around it."

"And the hatch?"

"Opens inward. Six-dog wheel. The seal will have set like concrete after twenty years, but the dogs are the problem." He tapped the diagram. "Naval strongroom hatches are designed so they can be opened from outside in an emergency — but the mechanism is behind a cover plate, and the cover plate screws are soft brass, and they'll be fouled. You'll need the pry bar and the hammer, and you'll need to do it fast, because every minute on that wheel is a minute your body is spending air it doesn't have."

"You've opened one before."

"I've drilled on them. In a pool, at forty feet, with an instructor counting my mistakes." He looked at her, and for once there was no performance in his face, no charm worn like armor. Just a man in his sixties telling the truth because he'd run out of anything else to spend. "Ada. The thing that will kill you down there isn't the depth. It's the arithmetic. Nitrogen narcosis at that pressure feels like being right. Everything will feel reasonable. When it starts feeling reasonable, that's the alarm."

"Anything else, Professor?"

"Yes." He hesitated, and Ada watched him decide to say it. "Your line is your life. Not your air — your air has gauges, warnings, redundancy. The line has nothing. It's just rope and your trust in it. When it fouls, and on a wreck that age it will foul, do not fight it. Fighting a fouled line at depth is how divers die with half a tank left. You signal, you wait, and you trust the man on the other end."

"And if the man on the other end is Bram?"

"Then you are the luckiest diver in this water." He stepped back and gave her the chart. "Go. And come back. That's an order."

"You don't get to order me."

"No," Vey said quietly. "I get to want to."

* * *

The water took her the way it always did, cold closing over her like a hand, and then the descent, the shot line humming through her gloved grip, the light dying above her in stages — green, then grey, then the blue-black that isn't a color at all but an absence wearing a color's clothes.

At sixty feet the *Passage* rose out of the dark, and Ada felt the old vertigo, the one she'd never said out loud to anyone: that a wreck at depth doesn't look like a ship. It looks like a country. Hills of hull plate, valleys of torn decking, and somewhere in it all, small as a coin dropped in a field, the thing she'd come for.

She ran the line down the port side, past the gash she'd found on the first dive — the inward-folded steel, the wound made from within — and in through the breach into compartment one. Her light carved a tunnel through the black. Silt hung everywhere, patient as fog. Twenty years of the sea's slow digestion, and the ship was mostly bones now, but the bones were naval bones, and naval bones are built to a standard, and Vey's chart was right: the corridor to 2-Alpha ran true, hatches dogged open by the sinking, the way marked by the same numbered stencils that had faded but not surrendered.

At a hundred and sixty feet the narcosis arrived, and it arrived the way Vey had promised: not as confusion but as comfort. The cold stopped mattering. Her breathing sounded musical. She caught herself admiring the way her bubbles rose, silver chains climbing toward a sky that wasn't there, and she thought, with a warm and distant clarity: *this is fine, this is all fine, I could stay down here all day.*

*That's the alarm,* she told herself, and the thought came through the warmth like a nail through silk. *You feel fine. Move.*

Compartment 2-Alpha. The strongroom hatch sat in the bulkhead like a closed eye, and the cover plate over the dogging mechanism was furred with corrosion, brass gone the color of an old bruise. She got the pry bar under it. Worked it. The first screw sheared with a sound she felt in her teeth. The second held, then gave, and the plate swung down on its fouled hinge and there was the mechanism, the six-dog wheel, crusted but whole.

She checked her gauge. Eight minutes of bottom time gone. She had perhaps four left before she had to start up or start dying, and the wheel needed both hands, and the wheel was going to fight her.

She put her hands on it and pulled.

It moved a quarter turn. Rust screamed somewhere inside the mechanism. She braced her fins against the bulkhead and hauled, and the wheel gave another quarter turn, and another, and her arms were burning and the narcosis was whispering that she could rest, just rest a moment, the ship wasn't going anywhere — and then the fourth dog seized and would not move and she was pulling with everything she had and the wheel turned and something else turned with it.

Her line.

She heard it more than felt it at first — a whisper of tension off her left hip — and then the whisper became a pull, and the pull became a lock. The line had run through her own working path, around a stanchion she'd passed on the way in, and when she'd braced against the bulkhead she'd wrapped herself into her own tether like a fly in a web. She was fouled. She was fouled at a hundred and ninety feet, in a dead ship, with a seized hatch and eleven minutes of air and a body that was already half-drunk on the pressure, and the wilderness — her father's word for it, the word he'd used for the sea when he was being honest — the wilderness forgave nothing. Not skill. Not courage. Not need. It forgave nothing, and it forgot no one, and she had come anyway.

*Don't fight it.* Vey's voice, calm as a chart. *You signal, you wait, you trust the man on the other end.*

But waiting was air, and air was time, and the hatch was one dog from open, and her mother had died somewhere inside this steel, and Ada pulled the line once, twice, the emergency signal — three sharp tugs for fouled — and felt nothing come back.

Nothing.

She pulled again. The line was taut as a harp string and answered with silence, and the cold thought arrived with perfect narcotic calm: *the line is cut, or the tender is gone, or the tender is dead, and you are alone in the country of the dead, and no one is coming.*

She pulled a third time, and her light swung with the motion, and in the swing she saw it — her line, her beautiful lifeline, running not to the stanchion she'd thought but through a collapsed shelf bracket, wrapped twice around a jagged spur of steel that had waited twenty years for exactly this. Not fouled on the ship. Fouled on the ship's teeth.

She had a knife. She had a knife and four minutes and one arm, because the other was holding the line taut to keep it from cinching, and she got the knife out and started sawing at the bracket's wrappings, and the line fibers parted one at a time like slow gunshots, and her gauge said what her gauge said, and the narcosis said *rest*, and she said *no* — out loud, into her regulator, a word that was just a shape and a burst of bubbles — and sawed.

The line came free. The world tilted. She was through the hatch gap — she'd gotten the last dog open, she realized, somewhere in the middle of dying, she'd actually gotten it open — and the strongroom yawned before her, a steel box the size of a chapel, and her light found shelves, and on the shelves, cases. Sealed cases, naval grey, stenciled with numbers and one word she couldn't read because her vision was going soft at the edges, going soft the way dusk goes soft, and she knew with the last clean part of her mind that she could not open them, could not even try, that the case she needed might be any of them or none and she had no time to learn.

She grabbed the nearest one. It was heavier than it should be. She clipped it to her harness, clipped it like it was her mother's hand, and turned for the line.

The line was gone.

Not cut — dropped. In the dark, in the fight, in the sawing, she'd lost her grip on it, and it had drifted off into the silt like a snake into grass, and she was in a steel chapel at the bottom of the sea with a case on her chest and no road home.

She did the only thing left. She went up. Blind, off the line, into the black above the strongroom, following her bubbles — the oldest rule, the last rule, the rule beneath all the other rules: *your bubbles go to the surface, and so do you.* She rose through the ship's broken ribs, through the breach, into open water, watching the silver chains climb and climbing after them, and her gauge was a red whisper, and the decompression stops she owed the sea went unpaid, and she knew she was going to pay for it later, in her blood, in her joints, in the bends' slow arithmetic — pay it later, pay anything, just rise.

Something took the line.

She felt it before she understood it: a tension at her harness, a steadiness, a hand the size of the whole surface. Then the line went taut in a rhythm she knew the way she knew her own pulse — two pulls, pause, two pulls — *hold on, hold on, hold on* — and she held, and the line hauled her up through the dying light, through the green, through the grey, and the surface broke over her face like a slap and the sky was there, the actual sky, grey and cold and the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

Bram had her under the arms before she'd finished coughing. He hauled her onto the deck of the *Kestrel* like she weighed nothing, which she knew was a lie, because she weighed everything, the case, the ledger, the twenty years, all of it. His face was grey. His hands were shaking. His voice, when it came, was the quietest she had ever heard it.

"Your line went slack," he said. "Eleven minutes, it went slack. I've been hauling on nothing for six minutes, Ada. Six minutes of hauling on the memory of my best friend's daughter."

"I got it," she said. Her teeth were chattering. Her whole body was a struck bell. She fumbled at the clip on her harness with fingers that wouldn't obey, and Bram's hands took over, and the case came free, grey and sealed and stenciled, and they both looked at it lying on the deck between them, dripping seawater, breathing the first air it had touched in twenty years.

Ada started to laugh. It came out wrong, high and cracked, halfway to the other thing. "One dog," she managed. "The hatch was one dog from open when the line fouled. I got it open while I was — while I was —"

"Shut up," Bram said gently, wrapping the blanket around her. "Breathe. Whatever you've got to say, it'll keep. It's kept twenty years."

Juno was suddenly there, and Vey, and the whole crew in a half-circle, and Ada looked up at them from inside the blanket, at the faces of the people she'd have died for and nearly had, and then Vey crouched down beside the case. He read the stencil. She watched his face as he read it, watched twenty years of guilt rearrange itself into something worse.

"Ada," he said. "This isn't the ledger."

The cold that went through her had nothing to do with the sea.

"It's the ship's paymaster's safe. This stencil — this is the *Passage*'s accounting section." He looked up at her, and his eyes were old and sorry. "The ledger was the courier's cargo. Personal custody. Which means your mother didn't lock it in a vault."

She followed it with him, the logic arriving like cold water in the blood. *Not in the strongroom. On her body. In her quarters. Wherever she kept the thing she was willing to die for.*

"Her effects," Ada said. "The personal effects. That's the manifest listing — the passenger manifest, the effects inventory. It's all in the chart room. It's all been in the chart room since the first dive."

She tried to stand. Bram held her down with one hand, easy as holding a child, and his face was terrible with love and refusal.

"You're not going back down," he said. "Not today, not on air, not after what you just —"

Above them, at the rail, Juno made a sound. Ada turned. The young diver was pointing north, toward the shelf's edge, where the fog was doing what fog does — and through it, low and black and patient, came the silhouette of a ship with a crane on its bow.

The Harrow fleet had found them.
