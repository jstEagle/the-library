## 11. The Ledger

The strongroom door was a mouth of rusted steel, and Ada had been feeding it her air for forty minutes.

She hung in the corridor outside it, breathing slow and shallow the way Vey had drilled into her over two sleepless nights with blueprints spread across the chart table — *naval construction, 2040s refit, the strongrooms sat aft of the magazine bulkhead because they didn't trust their own crews* — and she let the numbers steady her. Depth: one hundred and twelve meters. Bottom time remaining before she crossed into the red: eleven minutes. Nitrogen load climbing like water in a flooding compartment. Her dive computer ticked against her wrist, patient as a debt collector.

*Eleven minutes to open a door that was built to stay shut.*

The Passage lay on its port side at an angle that had turned every horizontal surface into a slope and every slope into a trap. Silt hung in the corridors like brown fog, stirred by her fins, settling only when she went still. Her lamp cut a cone through it, and in the cone the strongroom door waited — a wheel-lock hatch, brass fittings gone green, the navy's stamp still legible beneath twenty years of growth. She could read it if she tilted her head. *Property of His Majesty's Admiralty. Unauthorized access—*

Unauthorized access what? The rest had been eaten.

Ada pressed her glove against the steel. Cold came through it like a voice. Somewhere behind that door, sealed in a case designed to survive depth charges, was the reason her mother had died. The reason the Passage had been holed from the inside by men who then reported her lost with all hands. The reason her father had torn a page out of his own logbook rather than let his daughter learn what he knew.

*Her mother.* The words still didn't fit right in her head. Twenty years of mourning a woman who had supposedly been a cook aboard a survey vessel, and instead — a courier. A passenger under a false name on a manifest that officially did not exist, carrying something worth killing a shipload of people to bury.

Ada checked her gauges again. Nine minutes.

She unclipped the hydraulic spreader from her harness — Bram's idea, salvaged from a decommissioned rescue cutter and rebuilt twice on their own deck with parts that had no business working together. It weighed almost nothing down here but it fought like something alive as she wedged its jaws into the gap between door and frame. Her hands were clumsy in the cold; she'd been down long enough that her fingers had gone past numb into a strange distant ache, as though they belonged to someone else, someone she was merely supervising.

*Steady.* Her father's voice, or her memory of it. *The sea doesn't reward hurry. It only forgives patience, and not always.*

She pumped the handle. Once. Twice. On the third stroke something deep inside the hull gave way — not the door itself but the dogs behind it, the locking pins surrendering to corrosion and leverage with a groan she felt in her teeth more than heard. Bubbles streamed from her regulator in a silver ribbon and vanished upward into the dark, toward a surface she tried very hard not to think about.

The door swung inward six inches and stopped.

Enough.

Ada killed her spreader light and worked by lamplight alone, sliding sideways through the gap the way she'd slid through a hundred wrecks, shoulders turning, hips finding the angle, the silt swallowing her sound. Inside, the strongroom was a small square chamber, shelved along one wall, most of its contents rotted to anonymous sludge. Paper had become paste. Cloth had become shadow. Everything the sea touched, the sea eventually took.

Except what it had been built to keep.

The case sat in a floor bracket bolted through the keel-side plating — a hard-shell transit case, olive drab, stenciled with a classification code Vey had made her memorize until she could recite it in her sleep. It was the size of a sea chest, too large for her harness, and it was lashed down with webbing that had held for two decades in water that dissolved everything else.

Ada's heart was going now. She could feel it hammering up through her throat, feel her breathing rate climbing, and she made herself stop. Made herself hang motionless in the black water inside a dead ship at a depth where the pressure would fold an unprotected body like paper, and she breathed. In for four. Out for six. Juno's voice in her ear through the comm line, thin and stretched by distance and relay: *"Ada? Your time. You're at eight."*

"I see it," she said. Her voice sounded foreign to her, flattened by the helmet. "It's here."

A pause on the line. Then Bram, gravel-rough: *"Bring it home, girl."*

*Girl.* She was thirty-one years old and he'd called her that since she was nine, since the day her father brought her aboard the *Horizon* for the first time and put her small hand on the rail and said, *this is yours now, someday, if you want it.* Bram had been there. Bram had rowed the boat that found nothing after the Passage went down. Bram had watched her father carry whatever he carried to his grave.

Ada drew out her knife and sawed through the webbing. Each strand parted with a soft resistance, like sinew. When the last one gave, the case shifted in its bracket with a hollow knock that seemed impossibly loud, and she caught it against her chest and felt — absurdly, irrationally — it catch her back. Solid. Intact. Sealed against everything except her.

Seven minutes. Getting the case through the door gap would take three of them if she was careful and all of them if she wasn't.

She was careful. She fed it through sideways, rotating it in the silt-clouded dark, guiding it with her fingertips because her lamp angle was wrong and her arms were lead. Twice it jammed. Twice she stopped, breathed, and found the new angle. The third time it came free and drifted out into the corridor, and she followed it through the gap, scraping her tank across the doorframe with a shriek of metal that rang down the drowned passageway like a bell tolling in a church with no congregation left.

Then she clipped the case to her lift line, thumbed the release on the inflatable bag Bram had rigged, and watched sixty pounds of dead weight become forty pounds of buoyant cargo rising slow and deliberate into the dark above her, trailing its guide line like a kite string.

Ada looked back once at the strongroom. At the shelving gone to sludge. At the bracket holding nothing now. Somewhere in this ship — in this grave — were the bunks where people had slept, the mess where they'd eaten, the chart table where someone had plotted a course none of them finished. Her mother had walked these corridors. Had maybe stood in that doorway. Had known, in the last minutes, what was coming through the hull.

*I'm sorry I'm late,* Ada thought, and did not know if she was speaking to the ship or the woman or both.

*"Six,"* said Juno in her ear.

Ada turned for the line and began the long climb back toward the light.

* * *

Decompression was a purgatory of hanging in cold green water while her body slowly gave back what the depth had lent her. They'd rigged a trapeze bar off the stern at fifteen meters, then ten, then five, and Ada hung from it with the case tethered beside her like a coffin being lowered in reverse, watching her computer count down intervals that felt longer than her whole life.

Vey met her at the ladder.

He didn't say anything. He just took the case's weight as she came over the gunwale, and for a moment they stood with it between them, this man who had fired on this ship and this woman whose mother had died aboard it, both of them dripping, both of them holding the same thing. His face was gray. Not seasick-gray. Something older than that.

"You opened it?" he asked.

"No."

"Good." He set it on the deck with a care that bordered on reverence. "Not on deck. Below. And Ada—" He stopped. Started again. "Whatever's in there, look at it once before you decide anything. Because after you've looked, you can't unlook. I've lived twenty years knowing what's in that case without seeing it, and I'm telling you, the not-knowing was a kind of mercy I didn't deserve."

"That's not comforting, Cassian."

"It's not meant to be."

Bram appeared with a towel and thrust it at her, and the ritual of drying off, of peeling gloves, of the small ordinary mechanics of a body returning from the deep, carried her through the next few minutes without thinking. Juno hovered at the edge of things, vibrating with questions she was too disciplined to ask. Old Wren had the watch. The *Horizon* rode a long swell under a sky the color of unpainted zinc, three tides left, Harrow's fleet somewhere beyond the shelf edge like wolves keeping just outside lantern range.

They gathered in the cabin. All of them. Nobody suggested otherwise; nobody suggested otherwise because nobody wanted to be the one who didn't know, and because some part of them understood — the way crews understand things without voting on them — that whatever happened next, they were about to stop being salvage divers and start being something else.

Ada knelt over the case. The latches were military issue, simple, built to be opened by gloved hands in bad conditions. They opened the way such things do: with a flat, final click.

Inside, packed in foam that had stayed dry for twenty years, lay a ledger.

Not a book, exactly. A bound sheaf of waterproof polymer pages, spiral-spined, stamped with the same classification code as the case. Beside it, a smaller envelope marked in handwriting so faded it was nearly ghost — and Ada's breath stopped, because she knew that handwriting. Not from memory. From the single letter her mother had supposedly written before shipping out, the one artifact in the tin box on Ada's shelf, the one her father had never let her reread aloud.

The same slanted capitals. The same way the R hooked backward, like a person writing fast against a deadline.

"Open it," Bram said quietly. "Or don't. But either way, do it now."

Ada opened the ledger.

The first page was a shipping manifest. Dates, vessel names, tonnage, ports of origin that were not ports of origin — transshipment points dressed up with legitimate names. Cargo descriptions that meant nothing until you noticed the weights, the crating dimensions, the escort arrangements. Ada had spent her life around ships and manifests and the paperwork of moving things across water, and it took her less than a minute to understand what she was looking at.

Arms. Tonnage upon tonnage of arms, moved under survey and hydrographic cover, signed off at every stage by officers whose signatures she recognized from history lessons and news archives and the names painted on buildings in the capital. Shipments that had never existed. Deliveries to conflicts the navy swore it wasn't in. A parallel fleet, running inside the real one, documented in meticulous, damning, bureaucratic detail.

Page after page. Her eyes moved down columns of figures and the figures resolved into a single sentence written in the language of ledgers everywhere: *this happened, and these men knew.*

"Oh," Juno said softly, from behind her. Just that. Just *oh*, the way you speak at a graveside.

Ada turned pages faster now, hunting — she didn't know for what until she found it. Near the back, a section apart from the rest, in a different hand: annotations. Corrections. A record within the record, kept by someone who had copied key entries and added dates, locations, and a running tally headed with a single word: *EVIDENCE.*

Her mother's hand. The backward-hooking R. Page after page in that hand, each entry initialed with two letters: *M.P.* Mara Pascale Montrose. The name Ada had carried in her bones for twenty years, attached to a woman who cooked meals and hummed off-key and died in an accident. And underneath the initials, on the final annotated page, a note in smaller script, hurried, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the polymer:

*If this reaches shore, it reaches the press, not the Admiralty. They will call it treason. It is the opposite.*

Below that, a date. Eleven days before the Passage sank.

Ada became aware that she was shaking. Not from cold — the cabin was warm, the stove lit, Bram's tea going in the pot. She was shaking from the inside, from something with no thermal explanation. Triumph arrived first, huge and bright and completely useless: *she has it, they have it, the thing is real, it exists, it can be proven.* And horror arrived half a breath behind, wearing triumph's face, because the proof was real which meant the sinking was real which meant her mother had known she was carrying something worth murder and had stayed aboard anyway, had kept her records anyway, had written *it is the opposite* in a steady hand eleven days before the navy put a torpedo — or an internal charge, or whatever truth Vey carried — through the hull.

"She tried to save it," Ada heard herself say. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Level was all she had. "That's why she was aboard. That's why she was listed as crew when she was a passenger. They gave her a berth in the manifest to hide her, and she used the hiding to work." She looked up at the faces around her — Bram gray as ash, Juno white, Wren in the doorway with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral. "She wasn't cargo. She was the one person on that ship who knew exactly what it was, and she stayed."

Nobody spoke. The swell rocked the *Horizon* with the patience of something that had already seen how this story ended once.

"The money," Wren said finally, gently, the way you remind a drunk friend of the time. "Ada. There are buyers who'd pay — this is worth more than any wreck we'll ever raise. More than ten wrecks. We could—"

"I know what it's worth."

"And Harrow knows it's aboard," Bram said. "You can bet your life he does. Why else is a contractor fleet sitting on a restricted site burning fuel for three days?"

Because Harrow & Sons cleaned up after the navy. Because somewhere in Harrow's ledgers there was probably a contract, twenty years old, line item: *site remediation, Passage, complete.* Because they hadn't come for brass or copper. They'd come to finish a job.

Ada closed the ledger. Her hands steadied as she did it, the shaking draining out through her palms into the cover, into the case, into the deck, as though the object itself absorbed what she couldn't hold. She looked at the envelope still lying in the foam — the smaller one, the one in her mother's hand, unopened.

Not yet. She wasn't ready for that yet. Some doors you only get to walk through once.

"Three tides," she said. "Harrow's got three tides' worth of patience left, and we've got a crippled engine and a hold full of proof that could hang admirals. So here's what happens next." She stood, and felt the cabin rearrange itself around her, the way it always did, the way her father's cabin must have rearranged around him. "We get this boat and this ledger to shore in one piece. And nobody — nobody — sells it before I decide who it belongs to."

"It belongs to the families," Juno said. Quiet. Certain.

Ada looked at her — the young diver who idolized her, who had no idea yet what idolatry cost — and nodded once.

Above them, the wind shifted. Wren's boots crossed the deck overhead, quick, and then his voice came down the companionway, stripped of everything casual:

"Ada. You'd better come up. Lights on the shelf edge. Three of them, running dark — and the lead one just turned toward us."
