## 12. Bram's Bargain

Bram Halloway had kept the middle watch for thirty years, and in thirty years he had learned that the sea told you everything if you listened. Wind shifting a quarter-point meant weather coming down from the north. A change in the slap of water against the hull meant current, which meant tide, which meant time. And silence where there should have been sound — that was the one that mattered tonight.

The *Elsbeth* lay at anchor in the lee of the shelf, riding a gentle swell under a sky the color of wet slate. Three tides left. Ada was below, sleeping the sleep of a woman who had been to the bottom of herself and back, and Juno had the wheel-side watch with his collar up against the drizzle, and Vey — Vey sat in the stern like a man waiting for a verdict, smoking nothing because there was nothing to smoke, just holding an unlit pipe between his teeth the way he'd held it since Ada had refused him forgiveness.

Bram stood at the rail and listened.

There it was again. An engine, throttled down to almost nothing, somewhere beyond the line of rocks to seaward. Not the deep-throated churn of Harrow's flagship — that vessel announced itself the way a cathedral announces itself. This was smaller. Quieter. The kind of boat men used when they didn't want to be counted arriving.

He didn't wake Ada. That was the first decision he made, and he made it the way her father would have made it: you don't wake the diver until you know what's waking her for. Fear is a solvent; pour it into a crew too soon and it eats through everything, welds and loyalties alike. Bram had watched fear do that once before, twenty years ago, on another deck, in another lifetime, and he had sworn on a dead man's name never to let it happen twice.

So he moved quietly. He woke no one. He took the lantern from its hook by the companionway, turned the wick low, and went out onto the aft deck to meet whatever the dark had brought them.

* * *

They came alongside at half past two, three of them in a rubber skiff painted flat black, oars muffled with cloth. Professional work. Bram had seen navy contractors board vessels in the Gulf, in the straits, in places whose names never made it into reports, and this had the same texture: economy, silence, the particular courtesy of men who were paid well enough not to need violence but carried it anyway, folded up small inside their jackets.

The one who climbed aboard first was broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples, wearing a slicker worth more than the *Elsbeth*'s entire rigging. He smiled when he saw Bram, and it was a good smile, practiced, warm as a banker's handshake.

"Bram Halloway," the man said. "You won't remember me. I was a boy the last time our paths crossed. My father sends his regards."

"Your father," Bram said, "should have sent a letter instead."

"He did." The man — Harrow's son, then, the eldest, the one they said ran the contracting side while Elias handled the money — reached inside his coat, and Bram's hand drifted toward the knife at his belt, but what came out was an envelope. Heavy paper. Heavy everything about these people. "He wrote it twenty years ago. You're the second man he ever offered it to."

Bram didn't take it. "First was Tom Montrose."

Something flickered behind the son's eyes — surprise, maybe, or respect. "You do remember."

"I remember everything about that year." Bram leaned back against the rail, arms crossed, putting the lantern between himself and the visitor the way you'd put a fire between yourself and something in the night. "I remember your father sitting in my galley drinking my coffee and telling Tom that some wrecks are graves and some are ledgers, and only God knows which is which until you open them. And I remember Tom saying no. And I remember what happened after."

"What happened after," the son said gently, "was that your captain died penniless, and his daughter grew up diving other people's trash for brass fittings, and every winter your crew came within a month of eating their boots. My father has admired your loyalty for twenty years, Mr. Halloway. He simply never understood why it had to be so expensive."

Bram said nothing. The rain ticked against the deck. Somewhere below, Ada turned over in her bunk, and the sound of it went through Bram like a fishhook.

"The offer is simple." The envelope stayed extended between them, pale as a dead man's hand. "Double what Vey is paying. Double, plus a completion bonus, plus passage for your crew anywhere they care to go and enough left over that none of them ever have to dive again unless they want to. All you have to do is put the case in my hands before dawn. No one gets hurt. No one even has to know. Vey takes the blame for losing it — and frankly, Mr. Halloway, no one will weep for Cassian Vey. The girl keeps her crew, her boat, and her innocence. Everyone walks away richer than they came. It is, I'm told, the same offer your captain refused. We've kept it warm all these years. Inflation adjusted."

"You talk like your father," Bram said.

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"Don't." Bram looked at the envelope for a long moment. He thought about the engine — the dying, coughing, rust-eaten heart of the *Elsbeth*, the thing that had driven the vote, the thing that had put them all on this course. He thought about Juno's cracked dive gloves, patched so many times they were more tape than leather. He thought about Marta's list of debts pinned inside the galley cabinet door, each name a small quiet shame. He thought about all of it honestly, the way a man owes honesty to temptation before he refuses it.

Then he thought about a woman at the bottom of a trench, twenty years dead, who had tried to carry the truth ashore in her own two hands.

"No," Bram said.

The son's smile didn't falter, but something behind it cooled, the way water cools with depth. "May I ask why?"

"Because I've seen what happens to men who take your father's envelopes." Bram pushed off the rail and stepped forward, and though he was sixty-one years old and the other man was armed and young, the son stepped back — one instinctive half-step, boot scraping the deck, and Bram marked it and let him see that he'd marked it. "Tom Montrose took nothing and died clean. I intend to do the same. Now get off my deck."

"It's not your deck, Mr. Halloway. It's Montrose's daughter's."

"Aye," Bram said. "And she's more her father than she knows. Off."

For a moment the son studied him, head tilted, and Bram understood that he was being measured — weighed as a variable, assessed as an obstacle or an asset, filed away in whatever ledger the Harrows kept that was worse than the one in the strongroom. Then the envelope vanished back into the coat, the smile returned to its full warmth, and the man inclined his head like a dancer finishing a figure.

"My father will be disappointed. He liked Tom, you know. Genuinely. It's why he made him the offer at all — respect, in his way." He swung a leg over the rail, paused. "Offers don't expire, Mr. Halloway. They wait. Ask anyone who's dealt with us."

"I'll pass the message to the fishes," Bram said. "They're the only ones listening out here."

The skiff pulled away into the rain, black on black, and the muffled engine note faded toward the shelf, and Bram stood alone on the aft deck with his heart going hard and his hands, when he finally looked at them, perfectly steady.

He had refused. Clean, whole, without a tremor. He would carry that refusal to his grave like a coin sewn into a coat lining.

What he could not know — what no man can ever know, standing alone in the dark having done the right thing — was who else had been awake to see it.

* * *

Juno had come down from the wheel watch ten minutes after the skiff arrived, drawn by the wrongness of the silence, and had stopped dead in the shadow of the deckhouse when he heard voices. He knew Bram's voice, gravel and iron. The other voice he didn't know, but he knew its kind — smooth, moneyed, the voice of men who had come aboard the *Elsbeth* once before in Juno's short memory and left warning shots hanging in the air like smoke.

He couldn't hear the words. Not most of them. The rain ate the consonants and gave back only rhythm — the visitor's easy cadence, long and unctuous, and Bram's answers clipped short, shorter, shortest, until the last one was barely a word at all.

But Juno saw the envelope. Pale rectangle, passed across the space between the two men, held out, held out, held out — and taken back. And he saw Bram step forward and the smooth man step back, and he saw the exchange end not with a thrown punch or a thrown man but with something worse: with calm. With the visitor swinging over the rail unhurried, almost satisfied, like a merchant who had gotten what he came for.

And Juno — nineteen years old, running on four hours' sleep and the residue of terror from the standoff, in love with the idea of this crew the way only the young and the orphaned can be in love with anything — Juno did the arithmetic of fear.

Bram had loved Ada's father. Bram had argued, quietly, reasonably, in the first days, that the Passage job was madness. Bram had been the one who suggested they anchor here, in this cove, out of the shipping lanes — *his* anchorage, *his* choice. Bram had been awake. Bram had met the enemy in the dark. And the enemy had left smiling.

Juno pressed himself against the cold planking of the deckhouse and watched Bram stand alone at the rail for a long, long time, and the sight that should have read as vigilance read instead as a man counting money in his head.

He didn't tell anyone. Not that night. He went back to the wheel, put his hood up, and stared into the rain, and the poison did its slow work in him, drop by drop, the way bilge water rises — unnoticed, level, inevitable. Because that was the thing about suspicion: it never needed proof. It only needed a seed and a tired mind, and Harrow's people, whether they knew it or not, had planted both.

By dawn the rain had thickened to a steady gray roar out of the northeast, and the barometer on the bulkhead had begun its slow, sick fall, and Marta stuck her head out of the galley and said, "Weather's turning. Real weather. Twelve hours, maybe less."

Ada came up on deck last, hair still damp from sleep, and stood looking at the falling glass and the bruised horizon, and Bram watched her do the same arithmetic he had done a hundred times in thirty years — sea against steel, greed against grace — and he felt the old pride move in his chest, the pride that had survived Tom Montrose and poverty and age itself.

He didn't know that Juno was watching him watch her.

He didn't know that across the water, beyond the shelf, Harrow's flagship had begun to move.

And he didn't know — couldn't have known, no matter how long he lived — that the storm coming down on them was the least of what the next two tides would bring.
