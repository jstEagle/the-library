## 13. Mutiny Weather

Juno felt the storm before she saw it, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck in a dark room.

She was on deck coiling line when the barometer needle began its slow crawl toward the red, and by the time Bram called her to look at the western horizon, the sky had gone the color of old pewter — not clouded so much as *lowered*, as if the whole weight of it had been set down on the water and left there. The wind had backed around too, coming now out of the northwest with a wet iron smell that meant it had traveled far over open sea and picked up nothing but appetite along the way.

"That's not weather," Juno said. "That's a decision."

Bram grunted, which from him was agreement. He stood at the rail with his hands folded behind his back, watching the horizon the way he watched everything — patiently, as if the world were a slow student who would eventually get the lesson right. Twenty years of diving with Ada's father had taught him that storms were never really about the sky. They were about what people did when the sky turned against them.

Below them, forty fathoms down, lay the Passage. And somewhere aboard her, sealed in the strongroom Ada had nearly died reaching, was the ledger — the case of oilcloth and steel that had already cost them their engine's good health, their head start, and any illusion that Harrow & Sons would let this go.

"Go below," Bram said. "Get warm. We'll want everyone thinking straight tonight."

But nobody was thinking straight, and everybody knew it. The storm was only the excuse. The argument had been waiting for days, coiled under the deck plates like something drowned but not dead, and the falling glass was going to let it out.

* * *

It started in the mess, which was where everything on the boat either started or ended, because there was nowhere else big enough to hold all of them and their opinions at once.

Ada stood at the chart table with her back to the bulkhead, arms crossed, still damp from the last dive, hair drying in salt-stiff ropes around her face. The ledger case sat between them on the table like a guest nobody had invited — squat, black, patient. Juno had caught herself looking at it three times an hour since they'd brought it up, half expecting it to do something. It didn't. It just sat there being worth more than all their lives put together, or worth nothing at all, depending entirely on what happened next.

"We run for port," said Odile, who ran the winch and had run it through two wrecks and one near-mutiny before this. She wasn't shouting. That was the thing about Odile — she never shouted, which made her harder to argue with than anyone who did. "We run now, while we've got weather room. We put in at Corvess, we find a lawyer, we find a reporter, we let people with better boats than ours handle what comes next."

"And give up the site," Ada said. Not a question. A translation.

"Give up the *wreck*." Odile spread her hands. "The ledger's out. That was the job. That was Vey's whole bloody contract, and we've done it, and I'd like very much to be alive when we get paid."

"The wreck isn't empty." Ada's voice stayed level, but Juno saw her thumb press white against her own arm. "There are things down there that belong to the families. Belong to—"

"To *you*," said Pell, the engineer, wiping grease off his hands with a rag that had given up on cleanliness sometime last year. "That's what this is. Say it plain, Ada. You want one more dive for a dead woman's letters."

The silence that followed had weight. Juno sat on the bench by the galley door with her knees drawn up, twenty-two years old and trying to make herself small enough to disappear into the woodwork, because this was the moment she'd been dreading since the vote six tides ago — the moment when the crew stopped being a crew and became eleven separate people with eleven separate fears wearing the same oilskins.

Ada didn't flinch. Juno had never once seen her flinch, and part of her loved her for it and part of her understood, in a way she couldn't have explained, that it was the flinching that made people human.

"Yes," Ada said. "I want one more dive."

"For *effects*." Pell threw the rag down. "For photographs and a hairbrush. While a storm comes down our throats and Harrow's flagship sits out there past the shelf like a dog that's decided we're dinner. One more dive, and then what? We sail into port with the ledger and a keepsake box? You think Harrow cares about the difference?"

"I think," Ada said quietly, "that my mother spent her last hour on this earth trying to get off that ship alive, and she left things behind, and every record in the country says she was never on it at all. I think the navy erased her, and I am not going to help them finish the job because the barometer dropped."

"It's not the barometer," Odile said. "It's the engine. It's the steering. It's the fact that we're a salvage boat, not a warship, and we've got maybe three hours of weather window and a hundred miles of open water between us and anywhere safe."

"Then take the boat," Ada said.

Juno's stomach dropped.

"Take the ledger, take the tender if you want it, run for Corvess, and tell them whatever you need to tell them. I'll stay. I'll dive the Passage alone if I have to. But I'm going down one more time, and I'm coming back up with what she left."

"You'll dive *alone*?" Pell's voice cracked upward. "In building weather, on a wreck you've already blacked out on once?"

"I blacked out because my line fouled, and Bram hauled me back, and I'm here. I'm always here, Pell. That's the one thing you can count on about me."

"No," Odile said, and for the first time her voice bent. "No, Ada, that's not fair. You don't get to make it easy for us to leave you. You don't get to stand there being brave so the rest of us look like cowards for wanting to live."

And that landed — Juno saw it land, saw it go in under Ada's ribs the way only true things can — and for a long moment the captain of the boat said nothing at all, just looked at the ledger case as if it might speak for her.

It was Cassian Vey who broke the silence.

He'd been sitting apart the whole time, in the corner by the lamp, the way he'd sat apart since the night he'd confessed what he'd done aboard the firing ship. He looked older in lamplight, Juno thought. Older and more tired, like a man carrying something heavy uphill in the dark.

"She's right," Vey said. "Both of them are. That's what makes it a real choice." He rose, joints cracking. "Here's what I know about naval construction that you don't. The Passage has a lee side. When this system hits, the swell will come out of the northwest, and the wreck's superstructure will break it on the north face. There's a dive window on the south side — short, ugly, but real. Six hours, maybe eight, after the front passes through. If Ada dives then, she dives in the calmest water within fifty miles, because the storm that's killing the surface is doing her work for her down below."

"You're insane," Pell said.

"I'm a man who's read a great many ship schematics," Vey said, "for reasons I'm not proud of. The window exists. Whether we take it is another question."

"And if the window doesn't hold?" Odile asked. "If the sea decides otherwise?"

Vey met her eyes. "Then the sea decides, and we live with it. That's the trade we've been making since the day we took this contract."

Nobody answered. The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead, the first rain came across the deck like thrown gravel, and every head in the mess turned toward the sound as if the sky itself had entered the argument.

* * *

They voted in the end, because they always voted; Ada's father had built it into the bones of the boat, and even now, with the wind screaming through the rigging, no one suggested doing otherwise.

Run for port: five hands. Stay for the dive: four — Ada, Vey, Bram, and, after a silence that lasted three full breaths, Juno herself.

She heard her own voice say it before she'd fully decided to, which was how most of her bravest moments happened. "I'm staying."

Ada looked at her across the table, and something moved through her face — not gratitude exactly, something rawer and less comfortable. "You don't have to."

"I know." Juno made herself sit up straight, made her voice come out steady. "You taught me the south-face approach on the Graveyard Shelf. You said I read current better than anyone you'd trained. If there's a window, you'll want a second diver on the line, and Bram's hands aren't what they were, and Vey—" she glanced at him, and softened it, barely, "—Vey shouldn't be in the water at all."

Vey inclined his head, accepting the cut without flinching. He hadn't dived since the confession. Juno suspected he never would again, that the water had become a country he'd exiled himself from, and some mean little part of her had wanted him to hear it said aloud.

The five who voted to run didn't celebrate. That was the worst part, Juno thought later. Pell wouldn't meet her eyes. Odile squeezed her shoulder once, hard, on the way out — *be smart, girl, be smart* — and then they were gone to their stations, and the boat was divided down its own keel, five souls pointed toward safety and four toward the bottom of the sea, sharing the same hull, the same rain, the same shrinking piece of sky.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, required splitting the boat itself.

The tender could carry five in a pinch, six if nobody minded dying a little. They'd rig it with the spare fuel, the emergency radio, and — over Ada's flat refusal and Bram's quiet insistence — the ledger case itself.

"You carry it out," Bram told her, in the wheelhouse, while the rain hammered the glass like it wanted in. "Not the tender. You. After the dive, you take the case and you go with them."

"Bram—"

"Listen to me, girl." His voice was low and gravelly and absolutely without anger, which was how she knew it mattered. "I've buried your father. I've pulled you out of the water twice. I am not burying you *and* the truth in the same hole. If the window closes while you're down there, if the sea turns, then the ledger goes ashore regardless. The story survives you. That's the whole point of it — that's why she carried it, that's why you went down for it. It was never meant to die with the person holding it."

Ada stared at him. Outside, the world had gone almost fully dark though it was barely midafternoon, the storm eating the light hours ahead of schedule.

"And you?" she said finally. "If I go with the tender, who brings the boat home?"

"I do," Bram said. "Me and whoever's fool enough to stay with me. Someone should bring your father's boat home, Ada. That was always going to be the job."

Juno, standing in the wheelhouse doorway with a coil of signal line in her arms, understood suddenly that she was watching two people who loved each other arrange each other's survival like pieces on a board, and that neither of them would call it love, because calling it would break it.

* * *

The front hit at dusk.

Juno lashed down the deck gear with Odile's youngest boy, Tomas, both of them soaked through in thirty seconds, the rain coming sideways and cold enough to ache. The boat pitched and rolled at anchor, and beyond the shelf, far off through the murk, Juno caught the running lights of Harrow's flagship — riding it out too, hove to, waiting. Even predators respected this weather. Even predators knew the sea didn't take sides.

Inside, wrapped in a blanket with tea she couldn't taste, she found Ada in the passageway checking dive gear by lamplight — tanks, regulators, the cutting tool, line reels laid out with a precision that was almost prayer.

"Second tank's yours," Ada said without looking up. "South-face descent, sixty feet to the boat deck, then down the number three hatch. In and out in forty minutes. If the current picks up past half a knot, we abort, no argument."

"No argument," Juno agreed.

Ada finally looked at her. In the lamplight her face was hollowed out, the exhaustion showing through the competence like bones through skin, and for a moment she wasn't the captain or the legend or the woman Juno had followed into the water for three years. She was just tired, and grieving, and about to do something dangerous for reasons that had nothing to do with money.

"Why'd you vote to stay?" Ada asked. "Honestly."

Juno considered lying, considered something loyal and simple. What came out instead was: "Because I watched you read that manifest. Your mother's name. And I thought — if it were my mother, I'd tear this boat apart with my hands. And if I ever got like that, I'd want someone beside me who wasn't there to talk me out of it. Just to keep me alive while I did what I had to do."

Ada held her gaze for a long moment. Then she reached out and gripped the back of Juno's neck, brief and hard, the way her father used to grip Bram's, according to the old stories — the diver's blessing, half affection and half promise.

"Forty minutes," Ada said. "You pull me at thirty-five whether I'm done or not."

"Thirty-five," Juno promised.

From the wheelhouse, Bram's voice came through the speaking tube, roughened by wind: "Barometer's still falling. Window opens tomorrow, oh-six-hundred, if it opens at all. Get sleep, all of you. Tomorrow we find out what kind of fools we are."

Juno lay in her bunk that night listening to the storm work the hull, and understood that whatever happened at dawn, the boat she'd boarded six tides ago was already gone. Crews split and came back together or they didn't; that was the sea's oldest arithmetic. She thought of Odile's hand on her shoulder, of Pell's averted eyes, of Bram arranging the future like a man dealing cards he'd already counted.

Love against duty, she thought, drifting. Except it didn't look like anything grand. It looked like ordinary faces, tired and scared, arguing over tea in a rolling mess.

She woke before dawn to silence — the strange, pressurized hush at a storm's center — and to Ada shaking her shoulder gently in the dark.

"Window's open," Ada whispered. "Get your kit. We dive in twenty minutes."

And through the porthole, as Juno fumbled for her suit, she saw the eastern sky cracked open with thin gray light — and beneath it, moving fast and low through the gap where the storm had briefly stepped aside, a launch flying Harrow's colors, running straight for the cove.

They weren't the only ones who'd seen the window.
