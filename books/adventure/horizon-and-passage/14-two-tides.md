## 14. Two Tides

Cassian Vey had spent twenty years learning to read the sea the way other men read faces, and this morning it was telling him a lie.

The water lay flat as poured pewter, the fog burned off clean, the light came down gold and harmless over the Graveyard Shelf. A beautiful day. The kind of day that made small boats feel safe and made old men forget what the ocean kept in its pockets. Vey stood at the rail of the *Corvus* with his coffee going cold in his hand and watched the flagship come around the eastern point of the shelf like a thought a man tries not to think.

She was called the *Meridian*, though nobody aboard her said the name aloud. Hundred and forty feet of navy-surplus hull sold cheap to Harrow & Sons and refitted with everything the navy hadn't been allowed to keep: a crane that could lift a locomotive, sonar that could count the fish in a school, and — if the rumors held — a pair of deck guns that had never been properly decommissioned. She moved with the unhurried authority of something that had never once needed to hurry.

Vey counted seconds between her bow and the nearest of the three wrecks below. Did the arithmetic without wanting to. She wasn't cruising. She wasn't searching.

She was parking.

"Montrose." He didn't raise his voice. Ada Montrose had a sailor's ears; she heard her own name the way she heard weather changes. "You'll want to see this."

She came up from the deck house wiping grease from her hands, Juno a half-step behind her with the dive log hugged against her chest like a shield. Ada looked where he pointed and Vey watched her face do what it always did — nothing, or almost nothing, just a tightening at the corners of her eyes that most people would have missed and that he had learned, over these hard weeks, to read like scripture.

"That's not a search pattern," she said.

"No."

"She's sitting on the site."

"She's sitting on the *channel*," Vey corrected quietly. "Look again. She's not over the Passage. She's two hundred yards east, square across the mouth of the reef cut. That's not an accident, Montrose. Elias Harrow doesn't make accidents that convenient."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Below them, forty fathoms down, the Passage waited with its dead and its ledger and the letter Vey knew — because he'd seen Ada's face when she came up from the strongroom — that she still meant to go back for. Her mother's effects. A sealed locker in the officers' country that no salvage contract covered and no money could buy.

Two tides. That was all the window they had before the storm front crawling down from the north closed the shelf entirely. Two tides, and now a hundred and forty feet of armed steel lying across the only practical approach.

"He's not trying to stop us diving," Vey said. "He could have done that last night, boarded us in the dark, taken the case off Bram's bunk while we slept. He wants us boxed. He wants us to watch him sit there until the weather comes, and then he wants us to run for port with our tails down and let the sea swallow the rest of it. Clean. No bodies, no lawyers, no story. Just another boat that bit off more than it could chew."

"And you know this how?" Bram asked from the doorway, arms crossed, his gray beard moving in the wind. There was no hostility in it anymore — that had burned off somewhere around the reef run — but there was caution, the deep kind, the kind a man earned by burying a friend.

"Because I've sat on the other end of that maneuver," Vey said. "Twenty years ago I stood a watch on a ship doing exactly this to exactly this spot." He let that land. He'd stopped flinching from it weeks ago, mostly. "Harrow is running my old playbook. And I know where the pages are missing."

Juno spoke for the first time, her voice thin. "There's another way in?"

"There's a way in," Vey said. "It isn't another way so much as a wound. The reef channel on the west side — the locals call it the Throat. It runs through the coral shelf for better than a mile, narrow enough in places that you could shake hands with both walls, and it bends twice where the charts show straight water. The navy surveyed it thirty years ago and then buried the survey, because a channel that secret is worth more than a channel that's published. I've been through it twice. Once in daylight. Once not."

"And once was enough to remember?" Ada asked.

"Once was enough to dream about," Vey said. "That's different, and worse."

* * *

They argued about it in the deck house for two hours, and Vey let them argue, because a man proposing to steer other people's lives through a hole in the reef had no right to hurry their consent.

Bram laid it out plain: the *Corvus* was crippled. The reef run two nights ago had cost them their steering gear and very nearly cost them everything else; the jury-rigged tiller linkage would hold for slow work in open water and would fold like wet paper under any real strain. Their engine was a resurrection already. They had fuel for one serious maneuver and not two. If the Throat ate them, nobody was coming out of that trench to help, and Harrow's flagship would pick through the wreckage at leisure.

"If we stay," Bram finished, "we lose the window and probably the ledger. If we run for port now, we keep the crew alive and we keep the case, and Harrow has to take us in front of witnesses or not at all."

"If we run," Ada said, "he follows us, and he takes us somewhere with no witnesses. You know it. I know it. He offered me a deal in that cove and I told him no, and Elias Harrow doesn't hear *no*, he hears *not yet*."

Vey said nothing. This part belonged to them.

It was Juno who turned it, in the end, the way the young sometimes turn things without meaning to — simply by standing in the doorway with her chin up and saying, "Her mother died trying to get that letter out. We're not leaving it down there."

The silence after that was long. Vey watched Ada look at the girl — watched her weigh it, the way you weigh a line about to part, deciding whether to trust it with your whole weight — and then Ada turned to him.

"The Throat," she said. "Convince me."

So he did. He spread the old survey tracings he'd carried folded in oilskin for twenty years, the ones that had cost him a career to copy and a conscience to keep, and he walked them through it: the false shoal at the entrance that wasn't a shoal, the first bend where the current set south-southeast at three-quarters flood, the gut where the channel narrowed to less than the *Corvus*'s beam plus a boat's length on either side, the second bend hidden behind a coral head that looked, from seaward, like solid reef all the way through.

"It's a decoy run," he said. "We don't go through to dive from inside — the water's wrong, too shallow over the inner shelf. We go through loud, lights on, running like a boat that's found a way out. Harrow's sonar will track us into the channel and his captain will have a choice: follow us into water he doesn't know, or stay on the site and gamble that we're bluffing. Either way, half his attention goes with us. And while his eyes are west—" he tapped the chart, the deep water east of the shelf, "—the *Corvus* comes around the far side slow and dark, and Montrose dives her mother's locker with the whole fleet looking the wrong direction."

"And who pilots the decoy?" Bram asked, though Vey could see he already knew.

"I do," Vey said. "Alone. It has to be alone — the Throat needs hands on the wheel every second and a voice in the engine room who trusts the wheel, and I'm not asking anyone else to learn it in one tide."

"You'll be alone on a crippled boat in a channel that's killed better sailors than you," Ada said, "with Harrow's guns watching."

"Yes," Vey said.

"Why?"

And there it was — the question he'd been waiting for since the tavern, since the harbor, since the night twenty years ago when a survey vessel went down burning and a junior officer stood his watch and said nothing, and kept saying nothing, for two decades, until saying nothing became the shape of his whole life.

Vey looked at the chart instead of at her. "Because I fired on this ship," he said. "Because your mother was on it. Because I have spent twenty years being the man who did nothing, and I have discovered, late, that I can't stand him anymore." He folded the tracings along their worn creases. "You don't have to forgive me, Montrose. You never will, and you shouldn't. But let me be useful. Let me spend what I've got left on something true."

Ada studied him for a long time. Outside, the *Meridian* sat fat and patient on the horizon, blocking the light.

"One condition," she said finally. "You carry a radio, and you answer it. Every call. If you go silent in that channel, I'm coming in after you, and I'll probably kill us both, and it'll be your fault forever. Those are the terms."

"Those are fair terms," Vey said.

* * *

They made ready through the afternoon, and Vey worked with a strange lightness he didn't trust.

He'd forgotten, over the years, what it felt like to be believed. Not forgiven — Ada had made the distinction clear enough, and he honored her for it — but believed. Trusted with a helm and a plan and the lives of a crew that had every reason to feed him to the trench. Bram checked his rigging twice and then, wordlessly, checked it a third time and pronounced it good, which Vey understood was the closest thing to a blessing the old man gave. Juno pressed a spare radio battery into his hand and said, "Come back," with the fierce simplicity of the young, and Vey found he couldn't answer, and that she understood that too.

Ada came to him at dusk, at the rail, while the sky went the color of a bruise healing.

"The tide turns at 0300," she said. "Flood sets through the Throat at three-quarters. You'll have current helping you in and fighting you at the second bend — that's where it gets honest. Don't fight the bend. Let it take you and correct late."

"You've memorized my briefing."

"I memorize everything that might kill my crew." She looked out at the darkening water. "My father used to say the sea forgives nothing, but I've decided that's not quite true. I think it forgives nothing *except* preparation. You prepare, it lets you live. You don't, it keeps you." She glanced at him sideways. "Prepare, Cassian."

It was the first time she'd used his given name. He held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank — knowing it wouldn't save him, needing it anyway.

At 0230 they cast off from the cove. The *Corvus* slipped away eastward into the dark, running dark herself, low, quiet, Ada at her rail watching him go with an expression he refused to interpret, because interpreting it would either break his nerve or break his heart, and he needed both intact for the next hour.

Vey pointed his battered tender's nose at the western reef and turned on every light he had.

The Throat took him the way it had taken him in his dreams — hungry, intimate, close enough to touch. Coral walls rose black on either side, streaming with the flood tide, and the channel bent when he knew it would bend and narrowed when he knew it would narrow, and he steered with his wrists and his memory and twenty years of guilt converted, at last, into something with a use. Behind him, faint on the southern horizon, he saw the *Meridian*'s searchlights swing west. Saw them find him. Saw them follow.

His radio crackled. Ada's voice, tight: "*Decoy, report.*"

"Throat's open," he said, and put the wheel over for the second bend, letting the current take him the way he'd promised, correcting late, the coral head sliding past close enough to read the starfish on it. "Coming out the far side. Tell Montrose the door's unlocked."

But even as he said it, even as the channel spat him out into the open water beyond the reef, Vey was reading the *Meridian*'s lights with a sailor's eye, and what he saw there turned his blood cold.

She wasn't following him.

She was turning — slow, deliberate, all her lights swinging east, back toward the shelf, back toward the *Corvus* — and on her foredeck, in the wash of her searchlights, men were moving with purpose around something long and dark that was beginning, unmistakably, to elevate.

Elias Harrow had never intended to chase the decoy. He'd known about the Throat all along.

Which meant he'd known, all along, exactly where the real boat would be.
