## 15. The Reef Run

The storm found them before the reef did.

Ada felt it first in her teeth, a pressure change like a hand pressing against the inside of her skull, and then in the water itself — the long, wrong swell that came out of the northeast with no wind behind it, rolling under the *Meridian* like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Juno stood at the bow rail with the night glasses pressed to her eyes, and Ada watched the girl's shoulders go rigid as she picked out the white line of breakers ahead, faint as a scar in the dark.

"Channel's there," Juno called back. "Maybe. The chart says maybe."

"The chart is twenty years old and drawn by a man who was drunk half the time he surveyed it," Bram said from the wheelhouse door. He had the engine manual open on his knee even now, a pencil tucked behind his ear, as if reading it one more time might make the old diesel behave. "Vey. You're sure about this."

Cassian Vey stood at the chart table with his hands flat on the paper, and in the red glow of the binnacle light his face looked older than it had that morning, older than it had twenty years ago, when he'd been a junior officer with a clean jaw and a career ahead of him. He'd rolled his sleeves to the elbow. His forearms were mapped with old scars Ada had never asked about, and she wasn't asking now.

"I ran this channel twice," he said. "Nineteen years ago. Escort duty, before they closed the water. It's narrow, but it's straight if you trust the leading lights — the two beacons on the headland. When they line up vertical, you hold that course until the water goes green-black over the sand bar, then you come hard to starboard around the pillar rock. After that it opens up into the lagoon behind the shelf." He looked up at Ada. "Harrow doesn't know it exists. His charts won't show it. That buys us the night."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're a reef wreck instead of a blockade victim," Vey said, "and honestly, Montrose, I've been both, and the reef is quicker."

Ada almost smiled. She didn't. She went forward instead, past Juno, past the coiled lines and the dive tanks lashed under canvas, to the bow where the spray was already coming aboard cold and tasting of iron. Behind them, visible only as a smear of running lights against the black, Harrow's flagship held its station across the mouth of the open channel — a wall of steel and floodlights sitting fat and patient on the water, close enough that Ada could hear the grind of its generators carrying over the swells. Every few minutes a searchlight swept the sea in a slow arc, and every time the beam swung toward them the whole crew dropped below the rail line and waited for the light to pass like children hiding from a lighthouse.

The decoy had gone out an hour after full dark: the *Meridian*'s skiff, rigged with a lantern mast and a jury-rigged echo of their running lights, set loose on a long line toward the southern passage with the tide. From Harrow's bridge it would look exactly like what it was meant to look like — a salvage boat making a run for it, alone, panicked, easy to run down or herd. Let them chase a lantern on a rope. Let them spend their fuel and their patience on a ghost.

It would work until it didn't. Everything worked until it didn't.

"Ada." Juno had come up beside her without a sound, the way she did, all that young muscle moving quiet. Her hair was plastered to her forehead and her knuckles were white on the rail. "The swell's building. That's not storm swell yet, but it will be. If we're inside the reef when it comes—"

"Then we're inside the reef when it comes," Ada said. "Better than being outside it."

Juno didn't answer, which was how Ada knew she agreed. The girl had learned that much from her, at least: that the sea forgave nothing, planned nothing, and offered no second drafts. You took the water you were given and you made your work fit it.

She raised her voice. "Bram. Lights down. Vey — take the bow and call the marks. We go on my word."

* * *

They went in dark.

Every lamp on the *Meridian* was hooded or killed; the compass light was the only fire aboard, a coin of red floating in the blackness of the wheelhouse. Ada took the wheel herself because she could not bear not to, and because her hands were steadier than anyone's at three in the morning with a storm coming and a reef under the keel. The engine muttered beneath her feet, that tired old diesel rhythm she'd known since she was nine years old, since her father used to lift her onto the engine box so she could feel it breathe. *Listen,* he'd say. *A boat tells you the truth before anything else does.*

The boat was telling the truth now. It said: *I am old, and I am overloaded, and the water ahead is lying about its depth.*

"Leading lights bearing!" Vey's voice came forward from the bow, pitched flat against the wind. He stood braced against the pulpit with one arm around the forestay, pointing with his free hand toward the headland where — barely, barely — two dull points of light floated in the rain-haze. "Stand on that heading. Hold it. Hold it—"

The first of the big swells took them.

It came out of the dark with no warning color to it, no white water ahead, just a hill of black sea rising under the stern and lifting the *Meridian* as though she weighed nothing, as though she were a leaf and not forty years of other people's labor welded together. Ada's stomach dropped as the bow plunged down the far side, and the propeller came half out of the water and screamed, and for three seconds — three seconds that lasted a year — there was nothing under her feet but air and the memory of the sea.

Then the hull slammed down and the world became noise.

"MARK!" Vey roared from the bow. "Green water! Green water — COME TO STARBOARD!"

Ada spun the wheel. The rudder answered sluggish and heavy, the boat wallowing in the trough between swells, and ahead of them — suddenly, horribly close — the pillar rock reared up out of the sea like a black tooth, spray exploding off its seaward face. Fifty yards. Thirty. She could see the individual streams of water pouring off it, could hear the boom of the surge in its caves.

"Starboard! STARBOARD!"

"I'M STARBOARDING!" She put the wheel hard over and the *Meridian* leaned, groaned, and turned — turned too slowly, the current grabbing her stern and slewing her sideways toward the rock, and for one endless moment Ada Montrose looked at the reef dead ahead and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, exactly how her father's last minutes had begun.

Then the tide lifted them. A pulse of green water came through the gap and shouldered the hull sideways, graceless, ungainly, a shove from a drunk friend, and the pillar rock slid past her port side close enough that she could have spat on it, and then — open water. Black, heaving, but open. The lagoon spread out beyond the reef line like a held breath, sheltered from the worst of the sea, ringed by the drowned shapes of shoals.

Behind them, the storm arrived.

* * *

It hit the outer reef like a siege.

Even inside the lagoon, even with the coral wall breaking the worst of it, the sound was tremendous — a continuous deep-throated roar, the sound of an ocean spending itself against stone, and above it the wind came shrieking through the gaps in the heads with a note that made the rigging sing. Rain came sideways. Ada got the boat turned into the wind and held there while lightning walked along the horizon in silent white sheets, lighting up the lagoon in strobing flashes: the broken water over the shoals, the low black hump of an island in the middle distance, the rain like a curtain of nails.

And under her feet, the engine changed its voice.

She heard it the way you hear a wrong note in a song you've known all your life. A hitch in the rhythm. A metallic knock, once, twice — and then a grinding shriek from aft that went through her spine like a blade, and the deck shuddered, and the propeller shaft's steady thrum wound down into silence.

"No," she said. She said it quietly, to the engine, the way her father would have. "No, no, don't you do this to me now."

Bram was already below. She heard him in the engine room, shouting over the storm, heard the clang of the tool locker, heard words she was glad Juno couldn't make out. She threw the hook — there was no point pretending otherwise; without steerage they'd drift onto a shoal inside an hour — and the anchor chain rattled out into black water, and the *Meridian* swung her head to the wind and rode it, bucking, alive, going nowhere.

Vey came aft, soaked through, hauling himself hand over hand along the rail. He dropped into the wheelhouse beside her, dripping, breathing hard, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Through the salt-fogged glass they watched the lightning stitch the sky together and pull it apart again.

"The decoy?" Ada asked.

"Gone." Vey's voice was hoarse. "I watched the flagship turn south after it before we made the gap. They'll find a skiff and a lantern bobbing in the middle of nowhere, and by the time they work out there's no crew aboard—" He shook his head. "We bought the night. Maybe most of tomorrow."

"And the engine?"

He didn't answer, because Bram answered for her, coming up the companionway with a rag in one hand and grease to his elbows and a look on his face that Ada had seen twice before in her life, both times on boats that never sailed again.

"Crankshaft bearing's gone," Bram said. "Not damaged. *Gone.* Shattered, and it chewed the journal on its way out. I can't fix it here. I can't fix it anywhere short of a machine shop with a lathe and a week and parts that stopped being manufactured in nineteen eighty-something." He wiped his hands slowly, methodically, the way men do when the work is done and done badly. "We're anchored, Ada. That's what we are. We're anchored."

The word sat in the wheelhouse like a third person.

Anchored. Inside the reef, hidden from Harrow, yes — hidden from everyone — and also unable to run, unable to chase, unable to do anything but sit in this bowl of black water while the storm spent itself and the tide clock ran down. Two tides left. She could feel them passing already, could feel them the way you feel blood leaving a wound.

Juno appeared in the doorway, gray-faced, holding a kettle nobody had asked for. Steam rose off it and vanished instantly in the wet air. "I made tea," she said, and then, hearing herself, "I don't know why I made tea."

"Because you're a sailor," Bram said, taking the kettle, "and sailors make tea when the world ends. It's in the regulations."

Nobody laughed. But something in the wheelhouse loosened, a single degree, the way a knot loosens.

Ada looked at the chart spread on the table, at the little penciled cross marking their position inside the reef, at the wider circle beyond it where Harrow's flagship would be turning back north at dawn, patient and certain, closing on a site where a crippled boat sat at anchor with nowhere to go. She thought about the ledger wrapped in oilcloth in the strongbox under her bunk. She thought about the letter she hadn't opened yet, waiting in the strongroom of a drowned ship two miles north, addressed to a daughter who had taken twenty years to arrive.

One more dive. She still owed the dead one more dive, and now she would have to make it from an anchored boat in a cove with a hostile fleet converging and a storm still screaming at the reef, and when it was done there would be no engine to carry them anywhere at all.

"We lost our head start," she said aloud, to all of them, because they deserved the truth the way the sea gave it — plain and all at once. "The decoy bought us tonight, and the engine took it back. Come morning, Harrow knows where we are or he'll guess, and we can't run. So we don't run." She looked around the wheelhouse at their faces — Bram's grim and steady, Juno's frightened and refusing to be, Vey's unreadable and burning. "We finish the work. Both dives. What the sea kept, we take back, and then we deal with whatever's waiting outside this reef when the weather lifts."

Lightning lit the lagoon white. In that frozen instant Ada saw the whole of it — the ringed water, the broken reef, the small stubborn shape of her father's boat riding at anchor in the middle of a graveyard — and then the dark came back and the rain hammered the roof and the engine room smelled of burnt metal and salt.

From the bow, faint under the storm, came the sound of the anchor chain checking, holding.

Outside the reef, far off to the northeast, a searchlight swept the sea once, twice — and went dark, saving its fuel, waiting for morning like everything else.
