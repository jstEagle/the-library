## 16. What the Sea Kept

The cove held them like a cupped hand through the worst of the storm, and when the wind finally backed off toward morning, the water inside the reef was green glass, so still that Ada could see the shadow of the *Merrow's Debt* lying on her own anchor line.

They had paid for the shelter dearly. The steering gear was dead — a sheared quadrant pin they had no spare for, no port within reach to machine one — and the engine had swallowed seawater during the reef run and would need to come apart bolt by bolt before it turned again. Bram had spent the night below decks with a lamp and a wrench, cursing in the low, patient way he cursed at engines and at weather and at gods, and by dawn he emerged with salt-crusted knuckles and a verdict: two days, maybe three, if nothing else broke.

Three days in a cove that Harrow's flagship knew how to find.

Ada stood at the rail with a mug of tea going cold in her hands and watched the light come up over the reef wall. Beyond it, out on open water, the storm swell still ran long and gray, but here the surface barely wrinkled. It was the kind of calm that felt borrowed. Rented. Something would come to collect it.

"You're thinking about diving," Juno said from the cockpit doorway. She had a blanket around her shoulders and shadows under her eyes, and she said it not as a question but as an accusation dressed as observation.

"I'm thinking about a lot of things."

"The site's four miles north. Harrow's boat is sitting on top of it like a gull on a fish head." Juno came down the steps and stood beside her at the rail. "You can't get there."

"Small boats have gotten past big boats before."

"Not with no steering and half an engine."

Ada drank the cold tea anyway. Below them, through the hull, she could feel the cove breathing — the slow tidal pulse of water moving through the reef channels, filling and emptying like lungs. Her mother had been down there once, in water very much like this, holding something worth killing for, and the sea had kept her. Kept everything. The manifest, the ledger, the strongroom, and now, somewhere in the wreck of the Passage, in the cabin that had been hers, whatever a woman carries when she knows she might not come home.

The letter Vey had described — the sealed effects locker, the personal kit every survey crew member was required to file before a deep run — was still down there. And with two tides left before Harrow's deadline, before whatever bargain or bullet was coming, Ada wanted one thing that had nothing to do with ledgers or proof or money.

She wanted what the sea had kept of her mother.

* * *

She told the crew at breakfast, standing at the head of the table with her hands flat on the wood because sitting down would have made it feel negotiable.

"One dive. Today, while the weather holds and their boats are still working the main site. I go down to the Passage, I go to the aft cabins, I get the effects locker, and I come back. Nobody else goes in the water. Nobody fires a shot. If we're quiet, we're a fishing boat taking shelter, nothing more."

"And if you're not quiet?" Sefa asked. She was mending a net that didn't need mending, the way she always did when she was frightened. "If they've left a picket on the back channel?"

"Then I'm a diver who got lost." Ada met each of their eyes in turn. "I'm not asking anyone to like this. I'm telling you what I'm doing, and I'm telling you why. What's in that locker isn't cargo. It's hers. Twenty years she's been down there, and everyone who ever claimed to love her let her stay. I'm not doing that anymore."

Silence around the table. Rain ticked against the deck above.

It was Bram who spoke last, as he always did, after weighing everything twice. "Tide turns at eleven. Slack water's short this time of year — forty minutes of honest bottom time, maybe less if the surge is running in the channel." He looked at her the way he'd looked at her father across twenty years of tables like this one. "You take the redundant line. You take the spare bottle clipped off at thirty meters. And you take Vey."

Ada's jaw tightened. "Vey stays on the boat."

"Vey was a naval officer. He knows the Passage's layout better than your father's drawings do, and if something goes wrong down there, you'll want his voice in your ear more than mine." Bram's voice was gravel and iron. "I know what he did. I know what he was. But I also know you're going whether I say yes or no, and if you're going, you're going with every advantage we can put on your body. That's not forgiveness. That's arithmetic."

Juno was already pulling the dive bags from the locker. Sefa put down her net. The vote, such as it was, had happened without a show of hands, the way the best and worst decisions aboard the *Debt* always happened.

* * *

They ran the back channel at slack water with the engine at half throttle, steering by tiller — Juno lashed a temporary arm to the rudder stock overnight, ugly and effective — and Ada lay on the foredeck in her suit watching the reef walls slide past close enough to touch. Vey sat beside her, suited up too, checking her gauges without being asked, and neither of them spoke until the wreck's shadow rose out of the green below.

Twenty years underwater had softened the Passage's lines but not erased them. She lay on her starboard side on the shelf above the trench, masts gone, superstructure furred with white anemones, the scuttle hole in her hull — the hole made from inside, by her own crew's charges — now a cathedral door hung with drifting curtains of tunicates. Fish moved through the openings like thoughts moving through a sleeping mind.

"Effects lockers are aft," Vey said into the comm, his voice thin and close in her ear. "Below the officer country. Compartment C-2. There should be a placard — EFFECTS — stenciled on the frame. Your mother's cabin would have been..." A pause; she heard him swallowing something old and bitter. "Berth fourteen. Port side."

"Berth fourteen," Ada repeated, and let herself fall.

The descent was a slow sinking through water the color of old bottles. At twenty meters the light went from green to blue-gray. At thirty the wreck resolved fully, and Ada leveled off and swam along its flank with her lamp cutting a cone through the particulate, feeling the particular loneliness of being the only warm, breathing thing in a landscape built of rust and silence.

She had dived wrecks her whole life. She knew their grammar: the way a ship dies and becomes a place, how cargo holds become caves, how corridors become the ribs of some enormous animal. But swimming the Passage was different, and she understood only partway down why. Every other wreck she'd worked had been a stranger. This one was family.

She found the breach and entered through it, fins kicking gently, and the interior closed around her like a throat. Silt hung in the water, stirred by her own passage, and her lamp beam shrank to a few meters of usable vision. Compartment numbers were still legible on some frames, the navy's white paint stubborn under a generation of slime. C-4. C-3.

C-2.

The effects locker was a steel cabinet bolted to the bulkhead, its door sprung halfway open by corrosion, and Ada's heart stumbled when her lamp found it — because it wasn't empty. Most crews' lockers were stripped in any salvage, picked clean decades ago by divers with fewer scruples. But this cabinet sat in a flooded compartment behind a jammed watertight door, in a ship nobody official admitted existed, and the sea had guarded it better than any navy could.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had fused into a single black mass, were the personal kits of the lost. Ada could see the shapes of them through the rot: a camera in a leather case. A chess set. Someone's letters bundled with string. Names she didn't know, lives she didn't know, all of it waiting here in the dark while the world above decided they had never existed.

And at the back, smaller than the rest, a canvas bag with a brass tag.

She worked it free with fingers gone clumsy inside her gloves. The tag was corroded but legible, stamped in the navy's indifferent capitals:

**MONTROSE, E. — SURVEY SPECIALIST — BERTH 14**

Her mother's initials were the same as hers. Elara Montrose. Ada Elara Montrose. She had spent her whole childhood thinking she'd been named for a grandmother; now, weightless in a drowned corridor, she understood she'd been named for a ghost the family never spoke of.

The bag was heavy. Heavier than a kit should be. She clipped it to her harness, checked her gauges — nine minutes of bottom time left, and the swim back through the breach would eat five of them — and then, instead of turning for the exit, she kicked twice down the port-side passageway.

Berth fourteen.

The curtain was gone; the berth was just a steel coffer in the bulkhead, silted to the sill. But someone had left things. A hairbrush, its bristles dissolved. A paperback book swollen into a brick of pulp, its title gone. And pinned above the berth, impossibly, a photograph, its image bleached to sepia ghosts — but Ada knew the shape of it anyway, because an identical copy had hung in her father's wheelhouse her entire childhood. Three people on the deck of a small boat. A man laughing. A girl of six gap-toothed and squinting. A woman with dark hair and her daughter's exact face, looking not at the camera but at the girl, with an expression Ada had spent twenty years trying to find in anyone's eyes.

Her lamp flickered. Her air gauge read seven minutes. Somewhere above, a rival fleet was hunting them, and a deadline was closing like a fist, and none of it — none of it — mattered as much as the four seconds she spent reaching out through the water, through the glove, through everything, to touch the edge of that photograph.

Then she turned and swam for the light.

* * *

The ascent was clean. The decompression stop was clean. When Ada broke the surface beside the *Debt*'s hull, the first thing she saw was Juno's face over the rail, tight with a fear she was trying to make professional, and the second thing was Vey's hand hauling her up the ladder with a grip that shook slightly and pretended not to.

"You were late," he said.

"I found what I was looking for."

In the wheelhouse, with the engineless boat rocking gently at anchor and the rain starting again against the windows, Ada cut the oilcloth away from the canvas bag with Sefa's sharpest knife. The contents came out onto the chart table one at a time, dripping, transformed by twenty years of salt into relics: a wristwatch, stopped. A wedding band on a chain — her father's; he'd worn its twin until the day he died. A service pistol, fused solid with corrosion. A small tin that rattled.

And an envelope. Oilskin-wrapped, wax-sealed, dry inside its wrapping the way only things meant to survive can be dry. On its face, in a woman's careful hand, ink faded but unbroken:

**For my daughter, if she ever comes looking.**

Nobody in the wheelhouse breathed. Juno had both hands pressed over her mouth. Bram stood by the door with his cap in his fists. Vey had turned away, toward the window, toward the rain, as though he had no right to witness what came next — and perhaps, Ada thought, he was right.

She opened it with the knife point, the way you open something you've waited your whole life to open and are suddenly terrified of.

*My darling girl.*

*If you are reading this, then you came looking, which means you are grown, which means your father kept his promise to raise you angry enough to search and gentle enough to survive it. I am sorry for the way I had to leave you. I want you to know it was a choice, and I made it with my eyes open, and I would make it again, and I hate that those three sentences are all true at once.*

*The men who own this ship's cargo think paper sinks. It doesn't. Paper waits. I have done what I can to make sure it waits patiently. Whatever happens to me tonight, know that I was not afraid of the water. I was afraid of you growing up believing a lie about me, and I have written the truth in three places, and hidden it in two, and trusted it to one person I should not have trusted, and that is the whole of my strategy, and it is not much, but it is what a courier has when the couriers are hunted.*

*Be brave in the ordinary ways. Eat well. Keep good people close. Dive shallow.*

*I love you more than the sea, which is saying everything, because I love the sea the way other women love God.*

*Your mother,*

*Elara*

Ada read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, in a voice that started steady and did not stay that way, because some meetings cannot be postponed forever without cost, and the cost came due right there at the chart table, twenty years late, paid in full.

Bram put his hand on her shoulder and left it there. Juno cried openly and without shame. Sefa lit the lamp against the graying afternoon, and its warm circle made the little wheelhouse feel, for a few minutes, like the safest room in the world.

It was Vey who broke the spell — Vey, who had watched from the window with his back straight as a mast and his face gray as the water outside. He turned, and Ada saw that he had heard every word, and that hearing it had changed something in him, settled some account, closed some ledger of his own.

"There's a boat coming into the cove," he said quietly. "Single launch, no lights. Flying a white cloth at the masthead." He looked at Ada, and his voice was flat and careful. "It's Harrow. He's come alone, and he's asking to parley."

Ada folded her mother's letter along its original creases and slid it inside her jacket, over her heart, where it fit as though it had been measured for the space.

"Then let's hear what mercy costs," she said.
