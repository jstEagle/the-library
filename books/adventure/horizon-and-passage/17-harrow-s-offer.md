## 17. Harrow's Offer

The storm spent itself against the cliffs through the small hours, and by gray morning the cove had gone quiet in the way that frightened Ada more than the wind had — the hush of a held breath, the sea lying flat as poured pewter between the black walls of rock. The *Ebbtide's Grace* sat at anchor with her steering gear stripped open on deck, Bram elbow-deep in the hydraulics, and beyond the headland, somewhere out on that flat pewter water, the flagship of Harrow & Sons waited like a patient animal.

Ada had not slept. She had sat in the wheelhouse with the ledger case under her coat and her mother's letter unread in her pocket, because she could not bring herself to open it while the crew was still awake, still watching her, still waiting to learn whether she had drowned them all for a dead woman's paper.

She was on deck when the launch came around the point.

It came alone — no escort visible, no weapons showing, just a white-hulled tender cutting a clean line across the mirror-flat water, and standing at its bow a broad man in an oilskin the color of wet slate. Elias Harrow did not come to his meetings the way other men came to threats. He came the way a banker came to a foreclosure: punctual, unhurried, certain of the outcome.

"Captain." The man at the tiller called up when they were ten meters off, voice carrying easily across the still water. "Mr. Harrow requests a word. Alone, if you please. He'll come aboard."

Bram rose from the hydraulic housing, wrench in hand, grease to his elbows, and looked at Ada the way he had looked at her father a hundred times — asking permission without asking it, ready to refuse whatever she decided. Juno stood at the rail with her arms crossed tight, and behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the deckhouse, Vey watched with an expression Ada couldn't read at all.

"Let him come," Ada said.

* * *

Elias Harrow came up the ladder like a man boarding his own property. He was sixty or thereabouts, thick through the shoulders, with a face weathered into something almost gentle — the face of a grandfather, a man who coached children's sailing on weekends. That was the first thing Ada understood about him, standing there with salt crusted in her hair and no sleep behind her eyes: everything about him was designed to be underestimated. His hands were soft. His eyes were not.

"Captain Montrose." He said her name carefully, tasting it. "I've wanted to make your acquaintance for some years. Your father and I did business once. I regretted the way it ended."

"My father didn't do business with you," Ada said. "He took money from you. There's a difference, and I think you know what it is."

Something moved behind the gentle face — not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man finding the family resemblance he'd been promised.

"May I?" He gestured at the deckhouse. "I'd rather not conduct this conversation shouting across a rail. It insults us both."

Ada considered refusing. She considered the wrench in Bram's hand, the flare gun in the wheelhouse, the fact that Harrow's flagship carried men who had already fired a warning shot across their bow and boarded them once in the dark. Then she considered the ledger against her ribs, and the letter in her pocket, and the two tides — one tide now, maybe less — before Harrow's deadline ran out and patience became something else entirely.

"The wheelhouse," she said. "Bram stays within earshot."

"Of course." He smiled as if she'd offered him tea.

* * *

Inside the wheelhouse, the glass was fogged at the corners and the chart table still held Juno's half-erased pencil marks from the reef run. Harrow looked at none of it. He stood with his cap in his hands — actually held it, turned it over, a prop of humility — and when he spoke, his voice was low enough that Bram, leaning against the doorframe three feet away, had to lean harder to catch it.

"I'm going to speak plainly, Captain, because I think plainness is a currency you respect. You have something my clients want. I don't know precisely what it is — I've been given a description and a location, nothing more — but I know what it cost to send four boats and thirty men north for it, and I know what my clients will pay to see it stay lost." He set the cap down on the chart table, neatly, aligned with the edge. "So here is what mercy looks like, from a man like me."

He named a number.

Ada had heard large numbers before. She'd heard them in taverns, in salvage offices, in the mouths of men describing wrecks they'd never dive. This number was different. This number was not a price for cargo. It was a price for a life — for the *Grace*, refitted, re-engined, insured; for wages for six people through ten winters; for a house ashore with a roof that didn't leak; for Bram's bad knee and Juno's future and every debt the sea had written against them since her father died. It was a number built to fit exactly inside the shape of everything she was afraid of.

"And in exchange," Harrow continued, "the case comes to me, unopened by anyone but you. My clients' names never surface. No hearings, no newspapers, no investigators. And this—" he spread one soft hand toward the porthole, toward the cove, toward the flagship waiting beyond the headland, "—all of it goes away. The blockade lifts. The charges, if any were ever going to be filed, evaporate. Your crew sails home rich, and no one ever troubles the Montrose name again." He paused, and let the silence do its work, and then he added, softly, the knife going in where the silk had been: "Your father understood this arithmetic, Captain. He was offered less, and he took it, and his crew ate that winter. Whatever else you believe about him, believe that he did it for them."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Somewhere below, water lapped against the hull with a sound like slow applause.

"You're lying about my father," Ada said.

"I rarely need to lie." Harrow picked up his cap again. "The truth is usually better built. Ask yourself, Captain — why do you think a man like your father, a man with your own scruples about certain wrecks, agreed to run one last job in restricted water? Why do you think the *Passage*'s coordinates were in his logbook at all? Someone paid him to find her and say nothing. Someone made the same offer I'm making you. And someone—" Harrow's voice gentled further, became almost kind, which was the cruelest thing he'd done yet, "—someone watched him spend the rest of his short life unable to look at his own daughter without wondering if she knew. Grief is expensive, Captain. I'm offering to spare you his bill."

And there it was. Not the offer itself — she had expected the offer since the warning shot cracked across their bow — but the shape underneath it, the architecture of temptation, laid bare with a craftsman's honesty. Elias Harrow was not threatening her. Threats hardened people. He was *releasing* her — offering to lift the weight off her shoulders, to absolve her of the choice by making the choice pay, so that selling the truth would feel not like betrayal but like providence. Mercy dressed in a banker's coat. Her father had stood in exactly this wheelhouse-shaped silence twenty years ago, with a crew to feed and a daughter to raise, and he had done the arithmetic Harrow was doing now, out loud, in that reasonable voice — and he had taken the money, and he had died with the coordinates still hidden in his logbook like a swallowed stone.

*Oh,* Ada thought. *Oh, Da.* Not forgiveness — understanding, sudden and total and terrible, the way you understand drowning only underwater. He hadn't been weak. He'd been *offered mercy*, and mercy is the hardest thing in the world to refuse when everyone you love is standing behind you counting on your refusal to cost them nothing.

She thought of her mother then — the stranger on the manifest, the courier, the woman who had chosen the opposite arithmetic in a flooding chart room and paid in full. One parent had sold the truth to keep a child fed. One had died to carry it ashore. And Ada Montrose stood between them in a fogged wheelhouse with the case against her ribs, and understood, finally, that she had been making this choice her whole life without knowing it.

"No," she said.

Harrow sighed, and for just a moment the grandfather fell away and she saw the thing beneath — old, patient, and genuinely dangerous, a man who had sunk ships with paperwork and would sink this one the same way if it came to it. "Captain—"

"It's not a negotiation. You can stop building the sentence." She stepped closer, close enough to smell his cologne under the salt, absurd and civilized. "You want to know what I learned from my father? He took your deal and it killed him slower than the sea would have. Every winter after, he got smaller. I used to think it was the diving. It wasn't. It was you. It was knowing what he'd traded." She glanced at Bram in the doorway — at the old man who had loved her father, who had eaten that bought winter too and never known the price of the bread. "My crew eats honest bread or they go hungry. Those are the only terms I've got."

Harrow studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he nodded — a small, almost respectful motion, the nod of one craftsman acknowledging another's work.

"Elias told me you'd say that," said a voice from the ladder.

Cassian Vey climbed into the wheelhouse, soaked to the waist, having swum the last stretch from the launch — Ada realized only now that the launch had drifted back, that Harrow had come aboard with a second passenger hidden low in the tender's bow. Vey stood dripping on the deck plates, gray-faced, and Ada saw that his hands were shaking.

"Vey." Harrow's voice cooled by ten degrees. "You're supposed to be confined."

"I was confined. Your men are bad at it." Vey looked at Ada, and there was something in his face she had never seen there in twenty years of rivalry and one week of shared ruin: fear. Not for himself. "Captain, listen to me. The offer is real but it isn't the play. Harrow doesn't need you to sell. He needs you to *refuse loudly*, in front of witnesses, so that when the *Grace* sinks in this cove tonight — accident, storm damage, tragic — no court in the country will look twice at a salvage crew that turned down rescue money out of stubbornness." He turned to Harrow. "Tell her. Tell her what happened to the *Meridian*. Tell her what happened to the last captain who said no."

The silence stretched. Harrow put his cap on, unhurried, and smiled his grandfather's smile at Ada over Vey's dripping head.

"Mr. Vey has an imagination," he said. "It's why we let him go, all those years ago." He stepped toward the ladder, then paused, and his voice dropped to something almost private. "One tide, Captain. When it turns, my boats come in regardless. Sell, and live rich. Refuse, and live however long the sea allows." At the ladder's head he stopped again, and without turning around, added: "For what it's worth — your mother was offered the same terms once. Aboard the Passage, the night before she sailed. She gave the same answer you just did." 

He went down the ladder and away across the flat pewter water, and the cove closed behind his wake like a mouth.

* * *

Ada stood at the rail long after the launch vanished around the headland, the letter burning in her pocket, the tide running out beneath the hull.

"He knew her," she said quietly. Vey, dripping beside her, didn't answer, and didn't have to. "Vey. He knew my mother. Which means he knows what's in that case — and he just told me my deadline is a lie."

Below her feet, the tide began to turn.
