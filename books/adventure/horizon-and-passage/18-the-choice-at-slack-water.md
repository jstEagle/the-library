## 18. The Choice at Slack Water

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Ada Montrose was awake to watch it.

She stood in the stern of the crippled *Kestrel's Grace* with a mug of Bram's terrible coffee going cold in her hands, watching the water in the cove go slack — that brief, breathless hour when the sea neither rose nor fell, when the current forgot which way it meant to run. The storm had spent itself against the headland overnight and left the cove washed clean, rinsed of its fury, holding its water like a cupped palm. Beyond the reef, out where the channel opened into open sound, she could see the running lights of Harrow's flagship, patient as a spider on a web. It hadn't moved all night. It didn't need to.

Two tides ago it had been three. Now there was one.

Ada drank the cold coffee anyway, because wasting anything had been bred into her the way other girls were bred into manners, and she thought about Elias Harrow's voice. *I can protect them,* he'd said, sitting across from her in the cove's thin morning light like a man offering a blanket to someone drowning. *Your father understood. Your father took the same deal.* He'd said it gently, which was the cruelest thing about it. He'd said it as if he were doing her a kindness, and the worst part — the part that had kept her awake through the whole black night — was that some exhausted, salt-scoured corner of her had wanted to say yes.

That corner of her had done the arithmetic while she lay in her bunk listening to the bilge pump work. The ledger for the crew's lives. The truth for their survival. A fair trade, by any salvage man's counting. Her father had made it, or something like it, twenty years ago, and died regretting it, and Harrow had said that too, softly, like a knife slid in under the ribs: *He regretted it every day until the sea took him.*

But lying in the dark, Ada had found herself thinking not about her father. She'd found herself thinking about a woman she couldn't remember.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse at first light because there was nowhere else to gather and nothing else to do. Juno sat on the chart bench with her knees drawn up, looking younger than her twenty-three years and older than the sea. Bram filled the doorway the way he always did, arms crossed, gray beard still damp from the night watch. Vey stood apart by the window, his wounded shoulder bound and strapped, his face the color of old rope. He hadn't slept either. She could tell by the way he held himself very still, the way men do when they're rationing what's left of them.

Harrow's deadline was slack water before noon. His launch would come into the cove at eleven. After that, his flagship would come in too, and the *Grace* — engine dead, steering wrecked, riding low on burned fuel and flooded hope — would have no answer for it.

"So," Bram said. "We deciding?"

Ada set down the mug. On the chart table between them, wrapped in an oilcloth and looking far too small for everything it contained, lay the waterproof case.

"I've been thinking about my mother," she said.

Nobody moved. It wasn't the sentence any of them expected.

"Twenty years," Ada went on, "and I don't remember her face. I've tried. I've sat up nights trying, and what I get is pieces. A hand on my hair. Someone singing in a kitchen I can't place. A coat that smelled of diesel and lavender." Her voice held steady, mostly, the way a hull holds steady in a swell — you could hear the strain underneath. "Every record says she was nobody. Crew list says she never existed. My father's logbook has pages torn out. And the one woman who could have told me who she was is down there under forty fathoms with her name filed off."

Juno's eyes had gone bright. Vey looked at the floor.

"Harrow wants me to sell this case," Ada said. "Or sink it. Those are the two things he'll accept. Sell it, and we walk away rich enough to refit three times over, and the Passage stays a story the navy tells, and my mother stays a lie. Sink it, and maybe he lets us limp home poor but breathing." She turned the oilcloth back from the case, and the dull steel of it caught the gray light. "Those are his choices. I've been lying awake letting him make me think they're mine."

"And they're not?" Bram said quietly.

"No." She looked around the wheelhouse, at each of them in turn, and found she wasn't afraid anymore — or rather, that the fear was still there, huge and cold, but it had stopped being in charge. "There's a third thing. There's always a third thing. You just have to be willing to lose more than the other man can afford to take."

* * *

It took them two hours to build the machine.

Vey produced it from the bottom of his seabag, because of course he did; a man who'd spent twenty years carrying guilt also carried tools, and a navy-trained officer knew exactly what a field-document camera looked like. It was a military issue copy unit, compact as a bread tin, designed for photographing charts and orders in bad light aboard pitching decks. He laid it on the chart table and assembled it with his good hand, piece by piece, saying nothing, and when it was done he set a single sheet of test paper under the glass and shot it, and the little lamp flashed, and there it was — perfect, legible, damning.

"Where did you get that?" Juno asked.

"Requisitioned," Vey said, and for the first time in days something almost like a smile touched his mouth. "Twenty years ago. Along with a great many other things I've been meaning to return."

They worked through the morning in shifts. Ada handled the pages — cotton gloves borrowed from the medical kit, turning each leaf with the care she'd give a live charge, though half of her wanted to tear through them like a woman reading letters. The ledger was worse than she remembered. In the flat white flash of the copy lamp, page after page, the handwriting of dead clerks marched past: dates, tonnages, port codes, payments routed through shell companies with names like hymns. Arms moving south under survey flags. Bribes entered as maintenance. And on the final pages, in a different hand — smaller, hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — her mother's notes. Cargo weights. Times. A heading in a language of abbreviations Ada didn't fully understand but understood completely: *if this reaches anyone, they will kill us for it.*

Her mother had known. Had carried it anyway. Had gone into the water with it strapped to her body, and the navy had put holes in the ship she was aboard, and the sea had closed over both of them, and for twenty years the truth had sat in the dark being patient.

"You're certain about the newspapers?" Bram asked, feeding pages while Ada turned them. His big hands were surprisingly gentle with the fragile leaves. "Three of them?"

"Three," Ada said. "Different cities. Different owners. If one sits on it, or gets bought, the other two won't. My father taught me that much about dealing with powerful people — never trust a secret to a single pair of hands." She paused over a page, smoothed a curled corner. "And copies go somewhere else too. Somewhere Harrow can't reach even if he takes the boat, takes the case, takes all of us."

"The mail drop at Port Ellery," Juno said. "The one your father used."

"The one my father used." Ada allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "He always said a good mail drop outlives the man who built it. Turns out he was right about that too."

By ten o'clock it was done. Every page photographed. Three complete sets of prints, still warm from the developing tray Vey had rigged in the galley, sealed in oilskin inside three plain padded envelopes addressed in Juno's careful schoolroom hand. The original stayed in its case, because an accusation without evidence was just noise, and Ada Montrose had not come forty fathoms deep into her own grief to be dismissed as noise.

"What happens now?" Juno asked. She was holding one of the envelopes like it might go off.

"Now somebody mails them," Ada said. "Which is the problem."

* * *

The problem had a shape, and the shape was the reef channel and the dead engine and the fact that Harrow's launch would be entering the cove within the hour.

They argued it standing around the chart table, voices low, as if the water outside might be listening. The *Grace* couldn't run. The dinghy was a joke against the swell beyond the reef. The only working radio was the emergency set, and using it was a flare lit over their own position. Every solution ended the same way: someone had to get off this boat, cross open water, and reach Port Ellery before Harrow's lawyers reached the post office.

"I'll go," Vey said.

"No," said Ada, and then, because he opened his mouth, "you can barely lift that arm, and every man on Harrow's flagship knows your face better than mine. If they board us, you're the one they'll want alive. You stay."

"Bram, then—"

"Bram stays because if it goes wrong on this boat, I need one person aboard Harrow can't intimidate." She looked at Juno, and Juno looked back, and Ada hated what she saw in the girl's face, which was willingness. "Not you either. Don't argue. You're the best swimmer aboard and I may need that before tonight."

"Then who?" Juno demanded. "We're five people and a dead boat, Ada. Who's left?"

Ada looked out the wheelhouse window at the cove, at the slack water lying smooth as poured pewter, at the headland where the fog was burning off, and the answer had been sitting there since dawn, so obvious she'd been refusing to see it.

"The mail doesn't need a swimmer," she said slowly. "It needs a current. Slack water ends in—" she checked her father's battered tide table, "—forty minutes. When it turns, the ebb runs out through the northern gap and straight down the coast. Six knots, maybe seven, all the way past the point. Anything floating free rides it to the shallows off Ellery Head by nightfall."

Bram's eyebrows climbed. "You want to throw the envelopes in the sea."

"I want to give them to the tide." She was already moving, pulling the oilskin roll from under her bunk — the same roll she'd used diving, waxed canvas, weighted at one end, built to survive pressure that would crush a man. "Waterproof container, neutral buoyancy, dropped in the ebb stream at the gap. It drifts down-coast, grounds in the shallows, and whoever finds it finds three envelopes and a note worth more money than they've ever seen. Fishermen work those shallows every evening. It'll be ashore before Harrow's launch finishes its tea."

"And if it drifts the wrong way?" Vey asked. "If it grounds on the reef, or snags, or sinks?"

Ada tied off the last knot and weighed the bundle in her hands. It was small. It was absurdly small. Twenty years of lies, three newspapers' worth of proof, her mother's handwriting, her father's silence, all of it no bigger than a loaf of bread.

"Then I dive for it tomorrow," she said, "and I try again. That's the whole job, Cassian. That's always been the whole job. You don't get certainty in this trade. You get another attempt."

* * *

They ran the dinghy to the northern gap with muffled oars, because the tide table didn't lie and neither did the water: the ebb was already beginning to whisper through the rocks, a long low exhale, the sea clearing its throat. Ada knelt in the bow with the bundle between her knees. Behind her, Juno rowed, and ahead the gap waited — a slot of churning green between black rock, narrow enough to touch both walls, loud enough to swallow speech.

At the mouth of the gap, Juno shipped oars and let the dinghy hover on the edge of the stream, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"If this works," Juno said, "they'll print it. And then everything changes. And then—" She stopped, chewing on it. "Is that what you want? Everything changing?"

Ada watched the water run. Somewhere under that green rush, forty fathoms down, a broken ship held a woman who had chosen exactly this — chosen truth over safety, passage over harbor — and been drowned for it, and erased for it, and waited twenty years in the cold for her daughter to finish the work.

"She wrote *if this reaches anyone*," Ada said. "That was her whole plan. Get it to someone. Anyone. She trusted strangers with the most dangerous thing she ever held." She lifted the bundle. "Seems rude to trust them less than she did."

She leaned out over the gunwale and set the bundle into the stream.

For one heartbeat it hung there, turning, the waxed canvas shining wet — and then the ebb took it, quick and gentle and absolute, the way the sea takes everything it's given, and it was gone. Down the gap, out past the point, away along the coast toward the fishing shallows and the evening boats and the world.

Ada sat back. Her hands were shaking. Not from cold.

"That's it," Juno whispered. "It's done. We can't take it back."

"No," Ada agreed. Her heart was slamming against her ribs, terror and relief fused into one hot wire, and underneath it, rising slow as slack water turning, something that felt almost like peace. "We can't."

Behind them, from the mouth of the cove, came the low diesel cough of an approaching launch. Harrow, punctual to the minute, coming to collect his answer.

Ada turned in the bow to face him, and found she was smiling.

"He's late," she said. "The truth left twenty minutes ago."
