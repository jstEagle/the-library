## 19. Running the Blockade

Juno heard the launch before she saw it — the diesel cough echoing off the cove walls, flattening into something colder as it rounded the headland. She was on deck with a wrench in her hand and salt crusted in her eyelashes, and for one stupid second she thought: *maybe he'll just talk.* Maybe mercy came in diesel form, punctual to the minute, and all they had to do was hold out their hands.

Then Ada stood up in the bow, smiling, and said, "He's late. The truth left twenty minutes ago," and Juno understood that the world had already tilted past the point where talking would save anyone.

"Get below," Ada said, without turning her head. "Both of you."

"I'm not—" Juno started.

"*Below,*" Ada said, and there was something in her voice Juno had only heard once before, the night the *Kestrel*'s old engine let go in open water and Ada had gone down the engine room ladder into rising black water without hesitating. It wasn't anger. It was the voice of someone who had already done all her being afraid, somewhere private, and come out the other side with nothing left to spend on it.

Juno went below. Bram was already there, wedged against the bulkhead beside the engine housing, his big hands flat on his knees like a man waiting out weather. The crippled engine ticked as it cooled. Through the hull Juno could hear the launch's wake slapping the cove rocks, then voices — Harrow's voice, smooth as oiled rope, and Ada answering it, level and clear.

She couldn't make out the words. She didn't need to. She'd watched Ada write those letters last night by lamplight, watched her photograph every page of the ledger with hands that shook and didn't stop shaking, watched her seal three envelopes and hand them to a fisherman running supplies down the coast who owed Bram a favor and asked no questions. The truth was on its way to three newspapers, and whatever happened next, it was already too late to matter what Harrow paid or threatened.

Except it wasn't too late. That was the thing nobody said out loud. The truth could reach the papers and still never see print, if the people who carried it drowned in this cove, or vanished between here and port, or turned up in some navy report as smugglers lost in bad weather. Dead women couldn't testify. Dead crews made tidy stories.

Above her head, boots moved across the deck. Harrow boarding. The creak of the gangway plank. A long silence, and then Ada's voice again, louder now, pitched to carry:

"You can tell your lawyers I said thank you for the offer. And you can tell whoever signs your checks that my father took your deal once. He died regretting it. I'd rather die clean."

A sound from Harrow — not anger. Something worse. Laughter, low and unhurried, like a man closing a ledger he already knew the balance of.

"Miss Montrose," he said, "you have until slack water tomorrow. After that, my flagship moves to the mouth of this channel, and no boat leaves this cove. Not crippled ones. Not ones carrying stolen naval property." A pause. "Think about your crew. They didn't choose this."

"They did," Ada said. "That's rather the point."

The gangway creaked again. Boots receded. The launch coughed away through the cove mouth, and the silence it left behind was worse than gunfire, because at least gunfire told you where you stood.

* * *

They held a council in the galley, all five of them crowded around a table built for two, the lamp swinging on its gimbal with the swell. Vey sat with a chart spread out before him, weighted at the corners with mugs, and his finger rested on a line drawn in grease pencil through the reef channel north of the cove.

"Harrow will block the main channel," he said. "He won't bother with this." His finger traced the pencil line through a maze of shoals marked in red. "Nobody bothers with this. The charts call it the Throat. Half a mile of reef with maybe forty feet of navigable water in three places, none of them wider than this table. You steer it by eye and by memory, at speed, because if you slow down the current sets you onto the teeth."

"By *whose* memory?" Bram asked.

"Mine." Vey didn't look up. "I ran it four times, twenty years ago, running dispatches nobody asked questions about. The navy doesn't chart it because the navy doesn't want anyone using it. Which is exactly why we're going to."

Juno watched Ada study the chart. Watched her look at the red-marked shoals, then at the *Kestrel* beyond the galley door — her steering gear cracked, her rudder answering slow and drunken, her engine held together with prayer and welding rod — and Juno saw her do the arithmetic every diver does before a descent past safe depth. Air against bottom time. Odds against lives.

"It'll be daylight," Ada said. "He'll see us come out the far side."

"He'll see us," Vey agreed. "And he'll have maybe six minutes to get under way and cut us off before we reach open water. Six minutes is a long time, if you've got guns and short ones if you don't." He finally looked up, and his eyes found Ada's across the lamp. "I'm not going to lie to you. This might kill all of us. Staying might kill all of us slower. Those are the choices. I've made worse ones."

"When?" Ada asked.

"Slack water tomorrow morning. The current through the Throat runs eight knots at flood; at slack it drops to two. We go at slack, wide open, and pray the welds hold."

Bram grunted. "Prayer's free. Everything else on this boat cost triple."

Ada looked around the table — at Bram, at Juno, at old Sef who'd been engineer since before Juno was born, at Vey with his ruined face and his twenty years of penance — and Juno saw her decide, the way she decided everything, all at once and forever.

"Slack water," Ada said. "We run."

* * *

They worked through the night, and Juno learned things about fear she hadn't known before: that fear has stages, like tide, and that after enough hours of it you stop feeling it as terror and start feeling it as fuel. She spliced new line onto the steering cables while Sef welded below and sparks lit the bilge like lightning trapped underwater. Bram reloaded the flare gun and set out the signal mirror and said nothing about either. Ada stood watch at the cove mouth with binoculars, counting Harrow's lights.

At some hour past midnight, Vey appeared beside Juno at the rail with two mugs of tea that tasted mostly of salt, and they drank them in silence for a while.

"You're scared," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Good. Fear keeps your hands honest." He turned the mug in his fingers. "When I was a junior officer, I was scared all the time. Every day for two years. And I told myself it meant I was decent — that a man who felt fear couldn't do real harm." His laugh was barely a breath. "Fear doesn't stop anything, girl. It just watches. Remember that, tomorrow, when you're afraid and doing the right thing anyway. The fear isn't the test. The doing is."

Juno looked at him — this man she'd hated on sight when he walked into the harbor tavern, this ghost from the wreck that killed Ada's mother — and found, to her own fury, that she didn't hate him anymore. That was the trap of him. He kept standing in front of things.

"Get some sleep," he said. "You'll want your hands steady."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and windless, the sea lying flat as poured pewter, which was wrong — Juno knew it was wrong, knew a sea that calm was holding its breath — but there was nothing to be done about weather, only about course and speed and nerve.

They cast off at first light. The *Kestrel* answered her helm like a drunk uncle, wallowing, the cracked steering gear groaning through every turn, but Sef had gotten thirty more RPM out of the wounded engine overnight and she made way, ugly and stubborn, out of the cove and north toward the reef.

Vey stood at the wheel.

Ada had argued about it — Juno had heard the argument, low and fierce, in the dark before dawn — and Vey had ended it the way he seemed to end everything, by simply stating the truth and letting it lie there. *"I ran this channel when you were learning to swim. If we lose the wheel, I lose the wheel. You're worth more on deck than I am at anything else."* So Vey had the wheel, and Ada stood at the bow reading the water, and Juno crouched amidships with a boathook and a life ring and the useless, electric feeling of being nineteen years old inside something much larger than herself.

The reef rose around them like the bones of a drowned country. Black coral heads broke the surface in scattered fangs; between them the water shifted color from deep slate to pale green over sand to a brown-green murk that meant rock just under the keel. Vey steered by eye, calling depths from memory — "forty feet, bear right, right *now*" — and the *Kestrel* leaned and groaned and obeyed.

For eleven minutes it was almost beautiful. Juno would remember that afterward, how ashamed she felt remembering it: the channel unspooling ahead, the light coming up gold behind them, spray coming over the bow cold as ice water, and Vey at the wheel with his ruined face lifted into the wind looking, for the first time since she'd known him, like a man exactly where he belonged.

Then the swell came.

It came out of the northeast with no warning — a rogue, a freak, a single long green hill of water that shouldn't have existed in that windless gray — and it picked the *Kestrel* up like a toy and set her down hard, and Juno heard the steering gear let go with a crack like a rifle shot.

"Wheel's loose!" Vey shouted. "Cable's parted — Juno! The emergency tiller, aft, *move!*"

She moved. She didn't remember deciding to move; her body simply went, sliding down the wet deck as the boat yawed toward the coral, and she got the tiller pin seated and threw her weight against the bar and the *Kestrel* swung her head back toward the channel with maybe ten feet to spare off a coral head that would have opened her hull like a tin can.

"Tiller answers!" she screamed.

"Steer two points to starboard!" Vey called back, both hands still on the dead wheel, feeding her the course by eye. "Hold it — hold — hard to port on my mark—"

Behind them, over the reef flats, Juno heard it: engines. More than one. She twisted around and saw them coming — two fast launches out of the main channel, white wakes knifing across the flat water, and beyond them, sitting fat and black at the cove mouth where it had been waiting all night, Harrow's flagship getting under way at last.

"They're coming!" she yelled.

"I know!" Vey's voice didn't change at all. "Mark! Hard to port!"

She hauled the tiller. The boat leaned. Coral slid past beneath them, close enough to count the branches, and then the channel bent north and the launches behind them fell out of sight behind the reef wall, and for ninety seconds there was only water and rock and Vey's voice calling marks like a man reciting scripture.

The second swell hit them in the bend.

It wasn't as big as the first. It didn't need to be. It caught them beam-on in the narrowest part of the Throat, and the *Kestrel* rolled hard to starboard, and Juno went down on the tiller bar with her whole weight and heard something tear — not metal, not rigging. Cloth. Flesh.

Vey had left the wheel.

She saw it in pieces, the way you see a wreck from above the waterline: Vey crossing the deck in two strides, Vey reaching her, Vey turning his back to the rail as one of the launches came around the reef wall close aboard with a man standing in the bow with a rifle. The shot cracked flat across the water. Once. Twice.

The third one found him.

He folded over her, his weight driving her down behind the tiller housing, and she felt the heat of blood soaking through his shirt against her shoulder, and she screamed — she didn't know what, words or just sound — and above her Ada's voice cut across everything, raw and furious: "*Bram!*"

Bram was already moving. The flare gun went off twice, three times, burning white stars across the morning, and the launch flinched away behind the reef, and then — through the ringing in her ears — Juno heard a different engine note. Deeper. Slower. Official.

The coast guard cutter came around the northern point with its boards lit and its name blazing white on the hull, and it put itself directly between the *Kestrel* and everything else, and a loudhailer voice said words Juno would replay for weeks: *"All vessels in this area are instructed to stand down. This is now a federal investigation. Anyone firing on any vessel will be boarded and arrested."*

The launches turned. The flagship, far off, hung in the water like a decision being reconsidered.

Juno knelt on the deck with Vey's head in her lap and blood on her hands and her whole body shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and Vey opened his eyes and looked up at her and managed, through the gray of his face, an actual smile.

"Told you," he rasped. "Worth more on deck."

"Shut up," Juno said, and pressed harder on the wound, and cried, and steered nothing, and steered everything.

* * *

The cutter came alongside them in the open water beyond the reef, gentle as a pilot boat, and the officer who stepped aboard was young and serious and looked at the blood on the deck and at the waterproof case lashed under Ada's arm and said, quietly, "Are you Ada Montrose?"

Ada's chin came up. "Who's asking?"

"A man named Bram sent a letter to our district office nine days ago," the officer said. "Anonymous tip, except he signed it. Said a salvage vessel would be leaving restricted waters carrying evidence in the *Passage* sinking, and that contractors would try to stop her by force. He gave dates. He gave coordinates." The officer glanced at Bram, who stood at the rail with his hands visible and his jaw set like a man awaiting sentence. "We've been shadowing Harrow's fleet for a week. We were waiting for you to make your move."

Juno looked at Bram. Bram looked at the horizon. Twenty years of loyalty, tested and proven and poisoned and proven again, and the old man had spent it all on a letter written in secret, betting the crew's lives on the hope that somewhere in the coast guard there was one honest officer.

"You could have told us," Ada said. Her voice was strange — not angry, not yet, working toward it or away from it, Juno couldn't tell.

"And one of you would've talked in your sleep, or Harrow's ears are better than mine think, and the letter dies with the plan," Bram said. "Some debts you pay quiet."

Medics came over the rail for Vey. As they lifted him onto the stretcher, his hand found Juno's wrist and held it, brief and hard.

"The ledger," he said. "Guard it. It's not yours and it's not mine. It's hers." His eyes went to Ada. "All of it was always hers."

Then the cutter's deck rose between them, and the *Kestrel* fell in under escort, limping home through the morning light with the flagship watching them go and doing nothing, because doing nothing was suddenly the only move Harrow had left.

Juno stood at the rail with Vey's blood drying stiff on her sleeve, and watched the reef fall astern, and thought about what he'd said in the dark with the tea: *the fear isn't the test. The doing is.*

Ahead, the coast was coming up out of the haze — harbor cranes, breakwater, the ordinary world — and somewhere ahead of it, three newspapers were opening three envelopes.

She just hoped they'd be fast enough.
