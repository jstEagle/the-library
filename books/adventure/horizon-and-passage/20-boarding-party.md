## 20. Boarding Party

The cutter's name was *Meridian*, and she took them aboard the way a hospital takes in the injured — efficiently, without warmth, hands everywhere at once.

Ada came up the scramble net with Juno behind her and Vey bleeding through a field dressing on his shoulder, and the first thing she felt was not relief but the peculiar nakedness of stepping out of her own boat onto someone else's deck. The *Kestrel* had been theirs for eleven years, scarred and underpowered and smelling of diesel and Bram's terrible coffee, and every plank of her was known. The *Meridian*'s deck was grey non-slip, scrubbed anonymous, and the sailors moving across it moved like men who had never once had to think about whether the sea would let them come home.

They were separated immediately. That was procedure, a petty officer explained, and his voice had the flatness of a man reciting something he did not believe but would enforce anyway. Ada went one way with two escorts. Juno went another, white-faced, looking back until a bulkhead cut the line of sight. Bram went a third way, jaw set, hands loose at his sides in the old way he stood when he expected to be searched.

"They'll want statements," Ada said to no one in particular.

"Ma'am," said the younger of her escorts, "we just want you belowdecks where it's warm."

It wasn't warmth she wanted. It was the case.

She had carried it against her body since dawn — since before dawn, since the grey hour when they'd made their run past Harrow's flagship with the wind screaming and Vey hauling the wheel through gunfire that sang off the superstructure like wasps in a jam jar. A hard-sided case the size of a ship's logbook, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped under her coat, pressed to her ribs by a harness she'd rigged from dive weights webbing. It rode against her sternum now like a second heart, and when the escort patted her down at the companionway — thorough, impersonal — she watched his hands stop on the strap and then move on, because to him it was just another piece of salvage gear on a salvage diver, and salvage divers were always strapping things to themselves.

Belowdecks, the cutter was all pipes and hum and the smell of hot metal. They put her in a small mess compartment with a bolted table and a porthole showing nothing but grey water, and a steward brought tea she didn't touch, and for forty minutes she sat with the case against her ribs and listened to the ship breathe around her and thought about her mother.

Twenty years of thinking about her mother had been thinking about an absence. A photograph on the mantel, a woman laughing with her head tipped back; a name spoken carefully, the way you speak over a grave. But since the chart room — since the manifest with *ELENA MONTROSE, PASSENGER* typed in a clerk's indifferent hand — the absence had acquired weight and shape and, worst of all, intention. Her mother hadn't drowned. Her mother had *chosen*, had boarded a ship carrying proof of what the navy had done, had tried to bring it home, and had gone into the water with it rather than let it stay buried. Ada had spent twenty years mourning a woman who had never existed, while the real woman — the courier, the stubborn one, the one who'd married a salvage man and taught him which wrecks were never to be touched — waited in the dark with a letter addressed to a daughter who might someday come looking.

Ada had read the letter once, in the cove, by lamplight. She had not read it again. She would read it again when this was over, when there was time enough to weep properly, when the words could be hers alone instead of ammunition.

Because that was what they were now. Ammunition. Every line of Elena Montrose's careful hand was a round chambered against the navy, against Harrow & Sons, against every man in a clean office who had signed the order to sink a survey vessel full of witnesses and call it weather.

A knock. Two knocks. The door opened without waiting for an answer, which told her everything about who held power here.

The man who entered was maybe fifty, civilian clothes under an unzipped jacket that fooled no one, hair going grey at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than styled. He carried a legal pad and nothing else, and he closed the door behind him and stood with his hand still on it for a moment, as if deciding whether to leave again.

"Ms. Montrose." Not a question. "My name is Daniel Okafor. I'm an investigator attached to the Maritime Casualty Review Board." A pause, precise as a depth gauge. "Which is a polite fiction for an office that exists to make the navy look at things it would prefer not to."

"And which side of that are you on?"

He almost smiled. "I've spent nine years finding out." He sat down across from her, uninvited, and laid the legal pad flat. "Your quartermaster sent a signal three days ago from a relay buoy off the Sable Reaches. Anonymous, unencrypted, addressed to my office and two others. It named the *Passage*, gave coordinates, and said, quote, 'the cargo is not cargo.' Do you know anything about that?"

Ada kept her face still. Bram. Three days ago — before the reef run, before the storm, before any of them knew if they'd live long enough to use the ledger. He'd hedged the bet the only way left to him: by making sure that if the *Kestrel* went down, the truth went up. She filed it away next to the image of Juno misreading that meeting with Harrow's men, another debt the old man had paid quietly and would never mention.

"I know my quartermaster is smarter than most people give him credit for," she said.

Okafor studied her for a long moment. Outside the porthole, the grey water slid past, and somewhere above them Harrow's lawyers were presumably already climbing aboard whatever fast launch money could charter.

"Here's the situation as I understand it," Okafor said. "At 0640 this morning your vessel was intercepted inside restricted waters following what appears to have been a running gun engagement with a private vessel registered to Harrow Maritime Group. Two of your crew are being treated for injuries. My board has jurisdiction over casualty investigations, which means I can sit in this room and take your statement. What I cannot do is arrest anyone, seize anything, or protect you from what happens when the navy's own counsel arrives — and they're coming, Ms. Montrose. They were underway before you cleared the headland. Whatever you're carrying, you have perhaps an hour to decide who holds it."

"The story breaks either way," Ada said. "That's the point of it."

"The story breaks," Okafor agreed, "if it survives the hour. There are ways a document becomes evidence and ways it becomes lost property. I've seen both." He turned the legal pad around and wrote four names on it, unhurried. "These are the newspapers I trust. Not because they're honest — none of them are honest — but because they hate each other too much to bury the same story at the same time. If copies reach all four within the day, no one can sit on it. If it reaches one, it dies in an editor's drawer by Friday."

Ada looked at the names. Then at him. "You're telling me how to do this."

"I'm telling you what I'd do," Okafor said, "in the unlikely event I found myself aboard a naval cutter holding documentary evidence of a war crime, with the responsible party's lawyers inbound." He capped his pen. "I'm also telling you that I will formally request custody of the original, as evidence, the moment you show it to me. And that if you show it to me, I can put it under seal tonight — which means locked, logged, and witnessed, in a vault that requires three signatures to open. Including mine. Which means that from the moment I sign for it, losing it costs me my career and possibly my freedom. I am offering you my signature, Ms. Montrose. It's the only thing I have that's worth anything."

The compartment hummed. The tea had gone cold. Ada became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking — not from fear, she realized, but from the sheer strangeness of being offered something straight. Twenty years of salvage, of men who wanted things and dressed the wanting up as friendship or duty or love, and here was a tired civil servant laying his one asset on a bolted table like a man putting his last coin on a bar.

Her father had taken a deal once. She understood that now, fully, the way she hadn't in the cove when Harrow had offered her the same shape of mercy. Her father had sold silence for the crew's survival, and it had eaten him alive from the inside, and the torn page in his logbook was the wound the eating had left. She would not make her mother's death a bargaining chip. But she would not be naive, either. Naive got divers killed at sixty meters.

"If I give you the original," Ada said slowly, "I keep copies. Photographs of every page, plus the manifest, plus my mother's letter. Those go to your four papers today, by whatever means fastest, and not one word of it passes through navy communications."

"Agreed."

"And my crew walks free. No charges for the restricted water — the coordinates were given to us under contract by a party who claimed authorization, and you can pull that thread yourself. And Cassian Vey gets medical treatment, real treatment, not a bandage and a form."

"Vey." Okafor's pen paused. "Cassian Vey. Junior weapons officer, HMS *Corvidae*, the vessel that—" He stopped himself. Looked at her differently then, recalibrating, the way a diver recalibrates when the bottom turns out deeper than the tables said. "You know what he was."

"He told me himself. On my deck, in the dark, like a man coughing up glass." Ada unbuckled the harness. The case came out from under her coat and she set it on the table between them, and the sound it made — small, solid, final — seemed louder than the whole engine room. "Twenty years he's carried it. I haven't forgiven him. I may never. But he got me to the strongroom and he took a bullet keeping Juno alive on the run out, so he's earned the right to face this standing up, in daylight, with his name on the record. That's not forgiveness. That's accounting."

Okafor wrote for a moment. When he looked up, something in his face had shifted — the professional mask thinning over something older and wearier and, improbably, kind.

"My sister was on a ferry that went down off Corren Point when I was nineteen," he said. "Navy investigation called it ballast failure. Took me fourteen years to prove it was a hull weld they'd flagged twice and ignored." He tapped the case once, lightly, like knocking on a door. "I know what it is to want the paper more than the apology. Open it."

She opened it.

The ledger lay inside in its waterproof sleeve, pages browned at the edges but legible, her mother's handwriting marching down columns of dates, tonnages, destinations — arms moving under cover of aid shipments, payments routed through offices whose numbers meant nothing to Ada and would mean everything to a prosecutor. Beside it, the manifest. Beside that, the letter, and Ada found she couldn't look at the letter while a stranger's eyes were in the room, so she turned its face down gently, the way you'd close a book someone had fallen asleep reading.

Okafor read for a long time. The compartment hummed. Once, far off, a door banged and boots went along a passageway, and both of them glanced up like animals scenting weather, then back down.

When he finished, he sat back, and his breath came out slow and controlled, the breath of a man managing himself.

"This is real," he said. "This is — Ms. Montrose, this is the whole thing. Dates, signatures, routing codes. This isn't a leak. This is a confession written in the navy's own hand." He pulled the legal pad toward him and began, rapidly, to write. "I'm signing for custody now. You'll get a receipt. Your photographs — you have them developed or printed or however you've got them, and they go out from a civilian post office in Corren Harbor, not from this ship, not from any port facility with a naval presence. Can your boat make Corren?"

"Barely. She's steering with her engine room."

"Then she makes Corren." He tore the receipt off the pad — carbon copy, absurdly official, the bureaucratic sacrament — and signed both halves, and pushed one across to her, and Ada took it and found her eyes stinging and didn't entirely understand why. A piece of paper. Just a piece of paper. But it was the first piece of paper in twenty years that said, in effect: *this happened, and I saw it, and I am willing to be counted.*

"Statement first," Okafor said, gentler now. "Yours, in your own words, while it's fresh. Then I'll take Vey's, if he's able. And then—"

The door opened without a knock.

Three of them, and even Ada, who had never set foot in a courtroom, knew them instantly for what they were: lawyers. Grey suits, grey faces, the lead one carrying a leather folio the way a clergyman carries scripture. Behind them, filling the passageway, a lieutenant commander with the particular stiffness of a man following orders he wished he'd never received.

"Mister Okafor." The lead lawyer's voice was pleasant the way ice is pleasant. "You'll forgive the interruption. We understand you've taken possession of materials recovered from a restricted site. Under National Security Directive Eleven, those materials are now subject to immediate military jurisdiction, and we'll require—"

"You'll require nothing," Okafor said, and did not stand up. "This material is under seal with the Maritime Casualty Review Board as of seventeen hundred hours. Witnessed, logged, receipted." He nodded at the copy in Ada's hand. "There's the counterfoil. Directive Eleven applies to classified material. This isn't classified — it's *suppressed*, which is a different animal entirely, and one my board was chartered specifically to dig out of holes like the one you're digging. You're welcome to file a challenge. In the meantime, the seal holds, and if that case leaves this compartment by any route other than the documented one, there are four newspaper offices expecting wire copy by morning explaining exactly why."

The silence that followed had texture. The lead lawyer's eyes moved to Ada — weighed her, catalogued her, salt-stained jacket and dive-scarred hands and a dead mother's handwriting in a waterproof sleeve — and Ada met the look with the same level patience she used on men who tried to lowball a salvage claim.

"You must be Miss Montrose," the lawyer said. "We represent parties with a substantial interest in the disposition of certain recovered property. If you'd like to discuss compensation—"

"She wouldn't," Okafor said.

But Ada raised a hand, and the lawyer stopped, because people always stopped, because there was something in her voice when she chose to spend it.

"Twenty years ago," she said quietly, "a woman named Elena Montrose went into the water carrying that case, because the alternative was letting it sink with everyone's lies on top of it. My father spent the rest of his life paying for the deal you're about to offer me. So no. There's no number. There never was a number. There's just the truth, and it's already on its way to four places you can't reach, and by tomorrow morning every name in that ledger belongs to the public." She leaned forward. "Including yours, if you're in it. Check the index. People usually are."

The lawyer's composure held, barely, the way a bulkhead holds — visibly, expensively, at its rated limit. He gathered his folio. At the door he paused.

"You understand," he said, almost kindly, "that this destroys you. Salvage rights, contracts, your standing in every harbor from here to the capes. Harrow & Sons does not forget."

"Harrow & Sons," Ada said, "is about to learn what forgetting actually looks like."

They left. The door closed. Okafor exhaled through his nose, and for a moment the two of them sat in the humming compartment like survivors of the same wreck, which, Ada supposed, they now were.

"That was either very brave or very stupid," Okafor said.

"On my boat we call that Tuesday."

Something cracked in his face that might have become a laugh in a better-lit life. He gathered the case, cradled it against his chest the way she had carried it, and stood. At the door he turned back.

"Your statement. Tonight, before you sleep. And Ms. Montrose — the wire services pick up at six. If your copies reach Corren by the last post, the first bulletins break at six-oh-one tomorrow morning." His hand rested on the door handle. "Whatever happens after that — hearings, inquiries, the whole ugly machinery — happens in daylight. That's all any of us ever get. Daylight, and each other."

He was gone. Ada sat alone in the humming compartment with a receipt in her hand and the ghost of a case's weight still pressed against her ribs, lighter now and somehow heavier both at once.

Through the porthole, the grey water ran north toward Corren Harbor, where a crippled boat limped behind them carrying her crew, her rival-turned-ally bleeding in a bunk, and a young diver who had held the wheel steady through gunfire and wept afterward and denied it forever after. Somewhere ahead, a post office waited with its ordinary rubber stamps and its extraordinary power. Somewhere behind, Harrow's fleet turned for home to burn paper.

Tomorrow at six-oh-one, her mother's name would cross a wire and land on ten thousand breakfast tables, and strangers who had never heard of the *Passage* would say *Elena Montrose* aloud, and the woman in the photograph with her head tipped back laughing would stop being a secret and start being a story — Ada's grief, printed in columns, shared with a nation.

Private for twenty years. Public by breakfast.

Ada pressed the receipt flat on the table, smoothed it once with her palm, and went to find Juno, because whatever came at six-oh-one, her crew would hear it first from her — together, on their own deck, under their own flag, which was no flag at all except the one you owed the people beside you.

Above, on the *Meridian*'s bridge, a radio crackled to life with the evening traffic report, and among the shipping news, unnoticed, a single line moved down the wire toward Corren Harbor faster than any boat could sail:

*UNCONFIRMED: DOCUMENTS RECOVERED FROM WRECK OF SURVEY VESSEL PASSAGE — DETAILS TO FOLLOW.*
