## 21. Horizon

The hearings were announced on a Tuesday, in the way that governments announce things — quietly, on the third page of the shipping news, between a notice about dredging schedules and an advertisement for outboard motors. *Royal Commission of Inquiry into the Loss of the Survey Vessel Passage and Related Matters.* Related matters. As if her mother's whole life could be folded into two words and tucked behind a comma.

Ada read it standing at the galley counter of the *Kestrel's Wake*, coffee going cold in her hand, while Bram read over her shoulder and said nothing at all. There was nothing to say. The words had been twenty years coming; they could stand another hour without commentary.

"They'll bury it," Bram said finally. "Committees. Subcommittees. They'll call witnesses until everyone who remembers anything is dead or senile."

"Maybe." Ada set down the cup. "But they called it *the loss* of the Passage. Not the sinking. Not the accident. Somebody wrote *loss* into the title, and somebody above them let it stand." She tapped the paper once, lightly, the way you'd touch a bruise to see if it still hurt. It did. It always would. But there was a difference now between a wound and a scar, and she was learning to tell them apart.

The story had broken eleven days ago, on a Thursday evening, when the wire services picked up what three newspapers had been sitting on since her photographs reached their desks. She'd mailed the copies from a village post office two days' sail south of the cove, ordinary brown envelopes, ordinary stamps, no return address. Then she'd sat in the cockpit all night watching the harbor lights and waiting to feel afraid.

What she'd felt instead, around three in the morning, was something closer to hunger ending. Twenty years of not knowing, and then six weeks of knowing and carrying it alone, and then — nothing. Just the envelopes sliding into the dark mouth of the postbox, and the sea doing what the sea always did beyond the breakwater, which was continue, indifferent and enormous, as if truth were just another thing it swallowed and gave back on its own schedule.

Harrow had been arrested the following Monday. Not for the Passage — not yet, that would take the commission months — but for customs fraud stretching back a decade, the kind of charge prosecutors reach for when they can't yet prove the thing they actually want. Elias Harrow, photographed on the courthouse steps in a coat worth more than the *Kestrel's Wake*, face composed into something between dignity and disbelief. Ada had looked at the photograph for a long time. She'd expected satisfaction. What she found instead was a strange, tired pity, because she understood now what he'd offered her in the cove, and she understood that he'd probably offered it to her father in exactly the same voice, with exactly the same mercy in it, and that her father had taken it because mercy is very hard to refuse when your crew is starving.

Her father had died regretting it. She knew that the way you know things you can't source — the way Juno knew the sound of a fouled line before it fouled, the way Bram knew weather three days out. Some knowledge lives in the body.

"You're going to the harbor," Bram said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to the harbor."

He nodded slowly, and his eyes went soft in the way they only ever went soft when he was thinking about her father, which was most of the time, though he'd deny it under oath. "Your mother's things?"

"Already aboard."

* * *

Vey was at the harbor.

She saw him before she was close enough to pretend otherwise — a lean figure standing at the end of the north pier where the fishing boats tied up, hands in the pockets of a coat too good for the salt air, looking out at the water like a man reading a document written in a language he'd only half learned. He'd been shot through the shoulder running the blockade, a rifle round that had gone through meat and missed everything that mattered, and the doctors had told him six weeks of rest, and by the set of his shoulders she could see he'd been resting the way a held breath rests.

Nobody had invited him. That was the thing. The crew had come back from the cutter, from the hearings' first procedural sessions, from the strange bright weeks of being briefly famous in a small way — divers who found the ledger, the girl who mailed the photographs, the old man who took bullets — and nobody had said *tell Vey*. And yet here he stood, uninvited, present, the way a scar is present.

Bram grunted beside her. "Should I run him off?"

"No." Ada shifted the canvas bag on her shoulder. Inside it, wrapped in her mother's own scarf, were the effects she'd brought up from the chart room on that last dive: a hairbrush with silver hairs still caught in the bristles. A wedding band on a chain. A pocket watch, stopped. And the letter, which she had read by lamplight in the cove and had not been able to put down since, though she knew every line of it now the way she knew the sound of her own engine.

She walked down the pier toward him. He heard her boots on the planking and turned, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the gulls screamed overhead the way they always did, indifferent and enormous.

"Montrose," he said.

"Vey." She stopped three paces off. Close enough to talk. Far enough to leave. "You look terrible."

"I've been told I'm recovering." He almost smiled. It cost him something; she watched him pay it. "I read the commission notice. I wanted to say—" He stopped. Started again, and this time the polish came off his voice entirely, and underneath it was the junior officer who had fired on a survey vessel twenty years ago and had been drowning slowly ever since. "I won't ask to come. I know what I am to you. I know what I was on that ship. I just needed to be near the water today, and this seemed like the right water."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. The wind came off the harbor and moved the hair at her temples, and somewhere behind her Bram made a low sound that might have been a cough or might have been a warning.

"My mother," Ada said, "was a courier. She carried other people's secrets because she believed they belonged to the people they were taken from." She lifted the canvas bag slightly, so he could see it. "I'm taking her out to the coordinates this morning. To give back what the sea kept, and keep what belongs to the living."

Vey's face went carefully, deliberately still.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "I want that clear. What you did doesn't wash out. But my mother spent her last hours trying to get the truth off that ship, and you're the only man alive who knows those waters as well as I do, and if you want to pay something back—" She turned and started walking toward the boat. "You can take a line. We're short-handed."

Behind her, after a moment, came the sound of his footsteps following. It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even trust. But it was a berth on a boat, and in her family that had always meant more than either.

* * *

They ran north through a clean morning, the kind of autumn day that felt borrowed from summer and would have to be paid back with interest. The *Kestrel's Wake* rode easy with her refitted engine — jury-rigged still, half the parts scavenged, but hers, paid for with the settlement the newspapers' lawyers had wrung out of Harrow & Sons before the arrest froze the accounts. Not salvage money. Something better: money that hadn't required her to sell anything.

Juno ran the bow watch, young and straight-backed, scanning the water out of habit though there was nothing left to hide from. The rival fleet was impounded. The coast guard cutter that had intercepted them — called, it turned out, by Bram's anonymous tip, sent the night before the blockade run, a betrayal that had saved them all and that Juno had forgiven only after three weeks of pointed silence — was a friendly shape on the horizon now, assigned, unofficially, to make sure no one bothered the little dive boat heading out to the Graveyard Shelf.

Bram had the wheel. Vey stood at the rail with his arm still in its sling, saying nothing, watching the water with the expression of a man attending a service for someone whose funeral he'd helped arrange.

At the coordinates, Ada killed the engine and let the boat drift.

The sea below them was two hundred fathoms down and dark as closed eyes. Somewhere under all that black water lay the Passage, holed from the inside, holding her dead. They weren't going down. This wasn't a dive. This was something older than diving — something people had done on shorelines since before there were ships, standing at the edge of the water and giving things back to it.

She unwrapped the scarf.

The hairbrush she kept. The wedding band she kept. Those belonged to the living; her mother would have said so, she was sure of it, sure in the way the letter had made her sure of a hundred small things about a woman she'd known for less than four years of waking life. *My daughter, if she ever comes looking* — the letter began, and then it was all the things: don't carry other people's guilt, the sea forgives nothing so stop asking it to, love your crew like family because family is a choice you make daily or not at all. Her mother had written it in the chart room with the ship already dying beneath her, ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page, and every word of it had crossed twenty years of cold water to land exactly where it was needed.

What she gave back was ash — her mother's own request, in the letter's final lines. *If they burn me, take me where I went down. If they don't, take me anyway. I want to finish the passage.*

The urn was small. Government-issue, returned with the file the investigator had quietly copied for her, one of the few kindnesses in a season of paperwork. Ada stood at the rail with it in both hands and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't crying. She'd done her crying in the cove, by lamplight, alone. This was something past that. This was work, and she was good at work.

"For the record," she said aloud, to the water, to the woman in it, "I came looking."

She opened the urn and tipped it over the rail.

The ash went out in a pale ribbon, caught by the wind, scattered across the swell, and the sea took it the way the sea took everything — completely, without ceremony, without malice. Ada watched until the last of it was gone, and then she watched a while longer, because some things deserve to be watched longer than they need to be.

When she turned, Vey had removed his cap. Bram stood bareheaded at the wheel. Juno had her fist pressed to her mouth, and when Ada met her eyes the girl didn't look away, and that was its own kind of gift — being seen at the moment you're most undone, and finding the seeing gentle.

"She finished it," Juno said quietly. "The passage. Didn't she? Just took her twenty years."

Ada looked at the water one more time. Flat and gray-green and endless, running to the horizon where the sky came down to meet it in a line no one had ever touched.

"Yeah," she said. "She finished it."

Bram rang the bell twice — the old signal, all clear, make way — and the *Kestrel's Wake* came about and ran south for home, and Ada stood at the stern watching the coordinates fall away behind them, lighter than she had been in twenty years, her name feeling for the first time like something she'd chosen rather than inherited.

It was Vey who broke the quiet, near dusk, as the harbor lights came up ahead of them. He'd been working up to it for miles; she'd watched him do it, and let him.

"The commission will want to interview the crew," he said. "Under oath. All of it — the ledger, the blockade, Harrow's offer. It'll be ugly. They'll dig into your father too, Montrose. What he took. What he agreed to keep quiet."

Ada watched the lights grow. Somewhere in that town was a tavern with bad coffee and a harbor office with a wall of charts, and somewhere in her cabin was her father's logbook with its torn-out page, and she had decided weeks ago what she was going to write in the space where the oath used to be.

"Let them dig," she said. "We'll be sailing by spring."

And she went forward to tell Bram to log the day's position — the last entry she'd need from these waters, and the first line of whatever came next.
