## 22. Passage

Spring came to the harbor the way forgiveness came — slowly, and then all at once.

Ada Montrose stood on the deck of the *Horizon* in the first week of April and watched the yard crew swing her new engine over the gunwale on a chain too bright with grease to be anything but new. The crane groaned. Bram stood at the rail with his hands folded over his belly, supervising with the particular ferocity of a man who had spent thirty years making do and could not quite believe he no longer had to. Juno perched on the winch housing with a mug of coffee she was letting go cold, because watching a two-ton diesel descend toward the hull of your boat was better entertainment than any tavern in the district.

"Mind the flange," Bram called up. "MIND THE FLANGE."

The yard boss waved. The engine swung, cleared the rail by a hand's width, and settled into the open engine room like a heart being lowered back into a chest.

Ada felt it through her boots — the small shudder as the weight came home. Twenty years of diving other people's wrecks, and this was the first time she'd watched something go *into* a ship instead of out of one.

The settlement money had arrived in February, wired in an amount that made Bram sit down twice before he could read the figure aloud. It wasn't wealth. It wasn't even close to what the truth had been worth to the newspapers that ran it, or to the inquiry that ground on in the capital like a glacier with subpoena power. But it was enough. Enough for the engine, enough to recaulk every seam, enough to pay off the winter's debts to the chandlery and the fuel dock and old Marta who ran the bait shack and had never once charged them interest no matter how long they were late. Enough, Ada thought, to make survival paid for instead of borrowed.

Harrow's arrest had come in March. Elias Harrow himself, led out of his own offices in handcuffs while the cameras ate him alive, charged under statutes whose names Ada had learned only so she could watch them be used. The navy men above him were harder to reach — they had lawyers like armor plate — but the hearings were announced, public, scheduled for summer, and three separate journalists had already found the manifest listing Maren Montrose as passenger aboard the *Passage*. Her mother's name was in print now. Not in a casualty annex. In headlines.

Ada still didn't know how to feel about that. She suspected she would spend years learning.

"You're doing the thing," Juno said beside her.

"What thing."

"The face where you're here but you're also twenty fathoms down thinking about dead people."

"I'm thinking about the engine."

"Liar." Juno sipped her cold coffee. "You're allowed, you know. To think about her. Bram says you've been sleeping again. Real sleep, not diver sleep."

"Diver sleep is real sleep."

"Diver sleep is passing out with salt in your hair and waking up angry." Juno grinned, and Ada let herself be grinned at, because some debts between a mentor and the girl who'd pulled her line fouled at depth were paid in exactly this currency — small insolences, freely given.

Below them, the engine settled onto its mounts, and the yard crew began to bolt it home.

* * *

Cassian Vey came to the harbor on a Tuesday, which was somehow typical of him — arriving on the most unremarkable day of the week as though he could slip past their defenses disguised as ordinary time.

He looked different. The wound from the blockade run had healed crooked along his ribs, and he carried it without hiding it, a slight list to starboard when he walked, as if he'd been permanently re-rigged by the sea and had decided not to argue. He wore civilian clothes that fit badly, as if he'd bought them in a hurry after giving away whatever uniforms he'd owned, and he stopped at the foot of the *Horizon*'s gangway rather than coming aboard uninvited, which was also new.

Bram met him there. Ada watched from the wheelhouse window, arms crossed, saying nothing, letting the ritual happen.

"I'm not here about the money," Vey said. His voice carried across the water the way it always did, trained to cut through engine noise and panic alike. "I know you kept the settlement. Good. You earned it twice over."

"We're not hiring," Bram said.

"I'm not asking to be hired."

"Then what are you asking?"

Vey looked past Bram, up at the wheelhouse, and found Ada's eyes through the glass. He didn't look away when she came down the steps and out onto the deck. That was another change. The old Cassian Vey had negotiated everything through intermediaries and angles; this one stood in the open and took the weather.

"I have an offer," he said. "A funded expedition. Full outfitting, a proper survey vessel, salvage rights to three sites I know are worth more than everything Harrow ever touched. My money. Your boat, your crew, your name on the charter."

"No," Ada said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I heard 'your money.' That's the whole offer, Cassian. Everything after that is just decoration on the hook." She leaned on the rail. "You sat in my father's galley once. Do you remember?"

Something moved behind his eyes. "I remember."

"He told you we don't take contracts we can't walk away from. And money like yours can't be walked away from. It comes with strings, and the strings come with hands, and the hands belong to people who think they own the direction we point our bow." She shook her head. "We just spent eight months digging ourselves out from under other people's ownership. We're not climbing back in because the view from inside is nicer this time."

Vey nodded slowly, and Ada realized he'd expected this answer — maybe had come hoping for it, the way a man hopes for a verdict he dreads and needs in equal measure.

"Then I'll make you a different one," he said. "No money. No funding. No contract." He reached into his jacket and produced a folded paper, held it out flat on his palm the way you'd offer feed to a horse you weren't sure wouldn't bite. "My knowledge. Naval construction, restricted charts, the reef channels, the wreck registries nobody outside the service has ever seen. Twenty years of it. I'll work. Deckhand, diver, cook if it comes to that. Equal share, equal say, equal risk. And when I screw up — and I will, because I've spent my whole life giving orders instead of taking them — you get to tell me so, and I don't get to pull rank, because I won't have any."

Bram took the paper before Ada could. He read it, his lips moving slightly, then read it again. "It's a partnership agreement," he said. "Drafted proper. Witness lines and everything."

"I had time in the hospital."

Ada looked at this man who had fired on the *Passage*, who had betrayed her, who had taken the wheel through gunfire and caught a bullet meant for Juno, who had confessed his guilt like a wound offered and been refused like charity — and who had, apparently, spent his recovery drafting legal documents in which he gave away every advantage he had left.

"Why?" she asked. Not cruelly. She genuinely wanted to know, and she'd learned — from her mother's letter, from the ledger, from all of it — that the questions you actually wanted answered were worth asking plainly.

Vey considered the question longer than any officer she'd ever known would have.

"Because I spent twenty years paying a debt to the navy," he said, "and it turned out the navy never wanted it paid. They wanted it buried. And because I spent those same months with your crew learning what debt actually looks like when it's honest." He glanced at Bram. "Your father — your *father*, Bram, and you, Ada — your family built something on this boat that I helped sink once, in a way, and I'd like to help float it. Not own it. Float it."

Ada looked at Bram. Bram looked at Ada. Neither of them said anything, because they didn't need to; twenty years of shared grief and one winter of shared survival had worn the channels deep enough that decisions flowed between them like tide.

"It goes to a vote," Ada said. "Everything does, now. Crew rule."

"I'd expect nothing less."

The vote was four to zero, and Juno raised her hand so fast she nearly left her seat.

* * *

They sailed on the last ebb of April, on a morning so clear it felt almost indecent after the fog and storms of the autumn past.

There was no contract. That was the strangest part, the part Ada kept turning over like a coin she couldn't quite believe was real. No client waiting at the end of the run, no deadline measured in tides, no rival fleet shadowing them beyond the shelf. Just a chart case full of sites Vey had marked in his precise officer's hand — a freighter gone down off the Sable light with her cargo intact, a fishing vessel lost with all hands whose families had never been able to afford a search, a dozen others — and a crew of five, and open water running to the edge of the world.

Juno stood at the bow with the wind in her face, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the sheer animal fact of forward motion. Bram had the engine room singing — the new diesel ran smooth as poured cream, and he checked on it every twenty minutes anyway, the way a man visits a grave he's proud of. Vey worked the nav station with a competence that no longer needed to announce itself, and when he called a heading correction his voice was just a voice, no rank in it at all.

Ada had the wheel.

She'd had the wheel a thousand times before, but this was different, and she knew it was different, and she let herself know it. Behind her in the wheelhouse, lashed to the chart table where it had lived for as long as she could remember, lay her father's logbook — the real one, the one with the oath page torn out, the one she'd finished reading in the cove by lamplight the night before the blockade run. She'd added pages to it since. The reef passage. The storm. The boarding party. The hearing, described in her flattest salvage-report prose because some things were too large for adjectives. And on the last written page, dated yesterday:

*Crew of six. No contract. Course: north by east, first site the Sable freighter. Weather fair. Everyone fed. Engine sound.*

*For the first time since I took this book up, I am writing the log of a ship that belongs to no one but the people aboard her.*

She'd stared at that sentence a long time before closing the cover. It sounded like something a person wrote in a novel. Then she'd remembered that she was the sort of person who dove wrecks at dawn and hauled brass by hand, and decided it was true enough to keep.

"Montrose." Vey's voice from the nav table. "Sable light bears west-northwest, four miles. Site's forty minutes out at this speed. Water's two hundred feet — deeper than anything you've worked."

"Deeper than the strongroom?"

"Not even close."

"Then it's a holiday." She eased the wheel a degree, feeling the *Horizon* answer, alive under her hands in a way the old engine had never let her feel. "Juno dives first. I'll spot. Vey — you're on the line for your first working dive with us, so you follow Bram's rules like scripture, and if he says surface, you surface, and if you argue I'll personally tie you to the mooring buoy until you apologize."

"Aye," Vey said, and there was — she heard it clearly, and let it pass unremarked — something in his voice that might have been relief. The relief of a man finally being given orders he was glad to take.

The Sable light slid past to port, white against blue. Ahead, the water darkened where the shelf fell away, and somewhere down in that darkness a ship had waited twenty years for someone to come find her, not for her cargo, not for her secrets, but for her — for the names of her dead, for the story of her ending, for the simple dignity of being located and remembered by people who had no reason to be there except that the families had asked.

That was the work now. That was what the settlement money bought, and what the ledger had cost, and what her mother had died trying to carry ashore: not treasure, exactly. Truth, hauled up hand over hand, one dive at a time, by a crew that answered to no flag and no purse.

Ada rang down for two-thirds ahead and felt the new engine take the load without complaint.

"Bram," she called aft. "Log entry. Time, position, first dive of the season."

"Writing it now," he called back. "What do I put for conditions?"

Ada looked out over the water — the long swells rolling in from the open Atlantic, unhurried, ancient, keeping their dead and offering up their living, the horizon drawn clean and far and utterly unconcerned with anything human beings intended to do upon it.

"Put *fair*," she said. "And put *passage*."

She closed her father's logbook, opened the new one beside it — blank pages, good paper, bought with settlement money — and wrote the date at the top of the first page as the *Horizon* ran north by east, into open water, with no contract but their own.
