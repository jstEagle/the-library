## 1. Fuel, Rumors, Debt

The river gave up its fog the way it always did at dawn — reluctantly, in long gray ropes that clung to the water and let go of the banks first. Ulyana Ishikawa stood on the strip at Kettle Landing with her hands around a mug of coffee that had gone bitter an hour ago, watching the fog thin, waiting for the light to reach the numbers she'd painted on the gravel herself: **7-ISHIKAWA**, flaking now, half-swallowed by weeds.

Her plane sat at the top of the slope like a tired animal that had learned not to complain. The Otter was older than she was, patched in six places with metal that didn't quite match, and it drank fuel the way a grieving man drinks whiskey — steadily, without pleasure, because stopping would mean thinking.

She'd named it once, early on. *Provision*. Her father had laughed at the name and said you couldn't provision a horizon, only chase it. She'd stopped naming things after he died.

The first drum of avgas went up into the wing tank at 5:40, hand-pumped, each stroke of the lever a small argument between her shoulder and the morning. By the second drum her arms had settled into the rhythm and her mind could go where it always went at this hour: nowhere useful. The ledger lived in the plane's map case, wrapped in oilcloth, and she didn't need to open it to know what it said. She knew the number the way she knew her own pulse. **Forty-one thousand, two hundred and sixty credits**, carried forward from a man who'd never owed anyone anything honestly, accruing interest at a rate Mr. Ansel's clerks described as *standard* and everyone else described as nothing at all, because describing it out loud was how you ended up owing more.

She pumped the third drum and thought about nothing.

This was the skill she'd cultivated, the one nobody put on a manifest: the ability to be nowhere in particular while doing something precisely. Load the drums. Check the tie-downs. Wipe the dew off the pitot tube with her sleeve. Count the crates against the manifest without reading the labels, because reading the labels meant knowing what she carried, and not-knowing was one of the few luxuries the syndicate still allowed her.

Mostly.

* * *

The passengers came up from the dock at first true light, three of them, wet to the knee and loud about it. Ulyana watched them climb the slope and sorted them the way she sorted everything — by weight, by mood, by how much trouble they'd be at altitude.

The big one was a trader named Ferro who ran dried fish downriver and came back with bolts of cloth and opinions. He'd flown with her eleven times and tipped like a man apologizing for something. Behind him came two geologists from the survey office in Corvane, young enough that their boots were still new, carrying instrument cases they treated like infants.

"Morning, Ishikawa." Ferro dropped his duffel at the cargo door. "Fog on the middle bend yet?"

"Burned off by six. It'll be back tonight."

"You always know." He grinned. "You're the almanac with wings."

She didn't answer. She was checking the geologists' cases for balance points, sliding them forward until the load sheet told her the center of gravity was happy. The younger one — freckles, a chin still learning what beards were — watched her work with the frank curiosity of someone who hadn't yet learned that pilots in river country were furniture.

"You're Ishikawa? The Ishikawa?" he said. "My supervisor flew with your father. Said he could land on a sandbar the size of a dinner table."

"Your supervisor exaggerates."

"He said your father knew the upper river better than the charts did. Said there were channels he used that aren't on any map—"

"I wouldn't know," Ulyana said, and closed the cargo door on whatever came next.

It wasn't entirely a lie. She knew the stories about her father the way she knew the shape of a key she'd lost — by its absence, by the doors it no longer opened. Tomas Ishikawa had flown this river for nineteen years, first for the mission boats, then for whoever paid, and in his last four years for the Meridian Consolidated Mining and Provision Syndicate, whose name had *provision* in it the way a trap has cheese. When he died — engine failure over the Saddlebacks, so they said; the wreck was never recovered, which so they also said — the syndicate presented her with a ledger showing he'd been borrowing against his runs for years. Emergency repairs. Fuel advances. A hospital bill in Corvane she'd never heard of, dated three weeks before his death, signed in handwriting that looked almost like his.

She'd been twenty-two. She'd signed the assumption of debt because the alternative was losing the hangar, and the hangar was the last thing he'd built with his own hands, and grief makes terrible accountants.

Eight years later she was still paying. Not toward zero — the number never went toward zero — but paying, which was the arrangement. Ansel called it *a working relationship*. Ansel called most cages doors.

* * *

They lifted off at 6:15 into air so clean it felt rinsed. The Otter climbed heavily — she was carrying drums of diesel for the mission station at Two Rivers, medical crates for the clinic there, mail sacks, and the geologists' instruments — and then leveled off at nine hundred feet, below the cloud deck, above the water, in the corridor she'd flown so many times she could have done it asleep, and sometimes suspected she had.

Ulyana flew with one hand loose on the yoke and let the country come to her. The river unspooled below, braided and brown, splitting around islands that moved every flood season, so that any pilot who trusted last year's chart was a pilot writing next year's accident report. Settlements appeared as smoke threads and tin roofs. A dugout canoe waved at her — they always waved; she always dipped a wing, because it cost nothing and because her father had done it, though she'd have denied that under oath.

"Ferro says the mission's got fever at Two Rivers," the freckled geologist shouted over the engines. He'd wedged himself up behind her seat, past the point where politeness should have stopped him. "That true?"

"The clinic asked for antibiotics. That's all I know."

"And the fish run's dead below the falls? Ferro says the water's wrong. Says it's coming down too warm."

"Ask the fish."

He laughed like she'd been funny instead of finished. Up front, Ferro turned around with the expression of a man settling in for the thing he actually loved, which was talking.

"It's the glacier," Ferro said. "That's what I heard at Corvane. Water comes down warm out of the high valley, means the ice is going. Means somebody's buying up water rights before anybody downstream figures out what's happening."

"Somebody's always buying something," Ulyana said.

"Not like this." Ferro lowered his voice, which in the Otter's cabin meant he merely spoke at normal volume and leaned in. "You hear about Meridian?"

The word landed somewhere under her ribs, small and cold, like a stone through a window.

Meridian. Not the syndicate — the other thing. The expedition. She'd hauled crates marked MERIDIAN SURVEY twice in the spring, up to the last usable strip below the gorge, crates heavier than survey gear had any right to be, sealed, cold to the touch even in July. She hadn't asked. Asking was how you became a person who knew things.

"What about them?" she said, and kept her eyes on the river.

"Gone quiet." Ferro counted the facts off on thick fingers. "Six weeks now. Their supply contract got pulled — syndicate cancelled it, my cousin works the freight desk, he saw the paperwork. No radio checks since the new moon. And here's the part—" he paused, savoring it, a man buttering bread, "—the syndicate's asking around for charter pilots. Quiet-like. For a search party. But they're not hiring anybody local."

"Why not local?"

"Because locals talk." Ferro spread his hands. "And you're not flying it either, are you? So maybe they figure you'd talk too."

The geologist had gone quiet behind her. Even the engines seemed to lean in.

"They haven't asked me," Ulyana said.

"They will," Ferro said comfortably. "Ansel doesn't leave assets idle. Everybody knows that."

*Everybody knows that.* She banked around a flock of ibis rising off a mudflat and thought about the word *asset*, and about how a man could die owing forty-one thousand credits he'd never spent, and about how the syndicate had cancelled a supply contract to an expedition it had itself equipped, and how none of those facts wanted to sit next to each other politely.

Below, the river bent north toward the gorge country, toward the white smear of the high valley visible on clear days from thirty miles — a scar of blue-white that the maps marked with a surveyor's abbreviation and the river people marked with nothing at all, because you didn't name a thing you had no business visiting.

She dipped the wing for the canoe. Habit. Inheritance. Whatever it was.

* * *

Two Rivers materialized out of the heat shimmer at 8:40 — a mission dock, a clinic with a rusted red roof, a football pitch gone to grass, forty people already on the bank because the plane's arrival was the day's weather. Ulyana brought it in across the trees, sideslipped off the extra height, and set it down on the strip in a bounce she felt professionally obligated to hate and privately considered adequate.

Unloading took an hour. The clinic's sister-in-charge, a broad Samoan woman named Fa'avae who'd known her father, pressed a bag of oranges through the cargo door and refused payment. "For the girl who flies," she said, which was what she'd called Ulyana since Ulyana was fourteen and riding shotgun, and which Ulyana tolerated because correcting it would have required a conversation about being thirty and alone, and she'd rather eat glass.

The geologists transferred to a waiting boat. Ferro stayed aboard — his cloth was for the settlements further down — and as they lifted out again he was already asleep in the second row, mouth open, trusting her completely in the way of men who had never once considered what trust cost the person holding it.

She flew the afternoon leg alone with her thoughts, which was the arrangement she preferred and, today, the one that wasn't working.

*Gone quiet.* Expeditions went quiet all the time in this country. Radios drowned. Batteries soured in the damp. Men decided the valley was better than the report due date. Six weeks was nothing. Six weeks was a long weekend, upriver.

But the syndicate had pulled the supply contract. And the syndicate didn't pull contracts; it strangled them slowly, with lawyers, or it ignored them forever. Pulling one clean, all at once, meant a decision had been made upstairs in the lowland office, made by men with good shoes, and decisions made by men with good shoes always arrived downriver eventually, wearing boots.

At the fuel stop at Halfway she checked the manifest board out of habit and found a new notice pinned under glass, typed, official:

**MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED MINING & PROVISION SYNDICATE — NOTICE OF SUSPENDED OPERATIONS, UPPER RIVER CONCESSION PENDING REVIEW. ALL CHARTER TRAFFIC ABOVE KM 340 REQUIRES WRITTEN AUTHORIZATION.**

Pending review. Written authorization. The syndicate had never needed written authorization for anything in its life; it owned the river's paperwork the way it owned her ledger. Which meant the authorization requirement wasn't for them. It was for everyone else. A fence going up around a piece of country, posted in the language of administration.

She stood in the heat with her hand flat on the glass and let herself do the arithmetic she usually refused.

Crates sealed and cold. A concession pending review. A search party that wouldn't hire locals. And her father's old route — the one the freckled geologist had mentioned, the channels not on any chart — running straight up the throat of the high valley, where the water came down warm.

"You're not flying it either, are you?"

She bought a cup of tea she didn't drink, walked back to the plane, and opened the map case to check her fuel figures. The ledger was there under the charts, oilcloth-wrapped, patient as a stone. She didn't touch it. She knew its weight the way she knew the compass in the same case — her father's brass compass, needle worn pale, the one thing of his the syndicate hadn't itemized, because to them it was worth nothing, and to her it was worth the whole sky.

The needle lay under its glass pointing north, steady, indifferent, pointing at everything she'd spent eight years not looking at.

*Assets don't stay idle,* Ferro had said. *Everybody knows that.*

She latched the case, climbed in, and flew south into the falling dark, dipping her wing for the last canoe of the day, and if her hands were not quite steady on the yoke, there was no one up there to see it — which was, she told herself, exactly how she liked it.

By the time she set down at Kettle Landing the fog was coming back off the water in ropes, and there was a lamp burning in the syndicate agent's hut at the bottom of the strip, where no lamp burned at night unless someone had traveled far to burn it.

Mr. Ansel, she thought. Or someone sent by Mr. Ansel.

She cut the engines and sat in the sudden quiet, listening to the metal tick as it cooled, and understood — with the calm of a woman who has been swimming in deep water for years and has finally seen the bottom drop away — that the rumor had not been a rumor at all.

It had been a delivery. And she had signed for it.
