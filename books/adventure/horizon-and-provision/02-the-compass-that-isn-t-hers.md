## 2. The Compass That Isn't Hers

The magneto came apart in her hands like a secret she already knew.

Ulyana worked by the light of two lanterns and a headlamp with a dying battery, cross-legged on a tarp spread over the gravel of her strip, because the hangar's one workbench was buried under spare parts and a decade of good intentions. The night air off the river smelled of wet clay and kerosene. Somewhere across the water a frog chorus argued about the moon.

She laid the magneto's guts out in order — points, condenser, coil, the little felt wick that wanted oiling — and let her hands do the thinking while the rest of her went where it always went on nights like this: backward.

* * *

Her father had kept the compass in his left breast pocket, always, even sleeping. A brass surveyor's compass, older than either of them, its lid worn to a soft gold shine where his thumb had rubbed it for thirty years. The needle didn't just point north; it pointed north with an opinion, swinging wide when he turned toward the interior, as if the thing had its own ideas about where they ought to be going.

"You don't steer by it," he told her once, when she was nine or ten, sitting on the float of the old Beaver while he checked the water for debris. "You steer by the river. The compass is so you remember which way home is when the river lies to you."

"Does the river lie?"

"All rivers lie," he said. "That's how they get you to follow them."

He'd laughed then, that low laugh that lived mostly in his chest, and she had thought — the way children think, absolutely and without evidence — that nothing bad could happen to a man who owned a compass like that.

The syndicate's plane found him in weather no sane pilot would have entered, according to the report. Engine failure, according to the syndicate. Debt outstanding, according to the ledger Mr. Ansel slid across the desk to her three weeks later, along with a brass compass wrapped in a chamois cloth.

"We recovered this from the site," Ansel said. "I thought you should have it."

She'd been twenty-three, still wearing the same shirt she'd flown to the syndicate office in, still smelling of avgas and panic. She took the compass because taking it was the only thing in the room she could do without asking permission. It was warm from his pocket. She never asked which pocket, and hated herself for wondering.

"The debt," Ansel continued, "is regrettable. Your father was a valued contractor. But valued contractors still owe what they owe." He tapped the ledger once, gently, the way you'd tap a sleeping animal. "I'm not in the business of ruining people, Ms. Ishikawa. I'm in the business of keeping accounts. You'll find those are very different things."

She hadn't known then how different. She knew now. Ruin was quick. Accounts were forever.

* * *

Back in the present, the condenser tested dead. Of course it did. She dug through the parts bin for the spare, found instead a spark plug clogged with carbon, half a roll of safety wire, and a note in her own handwriting from some exhausted past self: *buy condensers*. She wrote notes like that all the time and then read them months later like messages from a stranger who had more money than she did.

The strip was hers, technically. Six hundred meters of graded gravel on a bluff above the river, a lean-to hangar, a fuel cache she paid for in installments. Her name was on the lease the way her father's name was on the debt — technically, and under someone else's terms. Every month Ansel's collector flew out or sent word, and every month she paid down a number that never seemed to move, interest accruing with the patience of water working at stone.

Four years of runs. Medicine to the delta settlements, mail to the mission, bolts of cloth, outboard parts, sacks of rice, and gossip — gossip both ways, because in river country information was cargo too, and she carried it free of charge. She knew which families were feuding, which elder was dying, which trader was watering his fuel. She knew everything and belonged nowhere, and she had decided somewhere in the last four years that this was not loneliness but freedom, and she repeated that decision to herself often enough that most mornings she believed it.

The new condenser went in. She gapped the points by feel, by lamplight, by the memory of her father's hands over hers guiding a feeler gauge. *Tolerance isn't perfection,* he used to say. *It's agreement.*

She was torquing the last screw when the frog chorus stopped.

All at once, like a door closing. She sat still in the lantern light, wrench in hand, listening. Frogs stopped for owls, for snakes, for men walking heavily. The river said nothing useful. Then, faint, from down the bluff where the landing met the water trail: an engine. Not an aircraft. A boat, running slow, running quiet, the way boats run when they want to arrive before they're announced.

She looked at the compass sitting on the tarp beside her, needle steady, pointing north with its usual opinion, and thought: *not yet.* Whatever was coming up the water trail in the dark, she wasn't ready for it, and she suspected — with the flat certainty of long experience — that readiness wouldn't matter.

* * *

Ansel had come to the strip twice in four years, and both times she remembered afterward with the specific discomfort of having been handled well.

The first time was eighteen months after the funeral. He arrived unannounced on the monthly supply barge, stepped off in polished boots that immediately betrayed him in the mud, and surveyed her operation with the expression of a man appraising a painting he already owned. He admired the hangar. He admired the drainage. He complimented her father — "a careful man, a man who understood obligation" — in a tone that made the words sound like accusations filed politely in advance.

Then he sat in her single chair, declined her coffee, and restructured her payment schedule. Not reduced it. Restructured. Smaller payments, longer term, a grace period through the flood season. Kindness, on its face. She signed because the alternative was losing the strip, and only after the barge pulled away did she do the arithmetic and understand: the new schedule meant the debt would outlive her. It wasn't forgiveness. It was tenure.

"He wants me comfortable," she told the empty hangar that night. "Comfortable and owing."

The second visit was a year ago. He brought a gift — a crate of proper aviation-grade hardware, gaskets and hoses and a starter motor she'd been doing without. No invoice. "Consider it maintenance of an asset," he said, and smiled, and the smile was genuinely warm, which was the worst part. Men who menace you openly, you can fight. Ansel never menaced. He invested.

She'd stood there holding the crate and understood, finally, why his courtesy felt like a leash: because a leash is also what keeps a dog from wandering into traffic. Every kindness had a length. Every favor defined the radius of her life. She could fly anywhere within it. She could even be happy within it, probably, if she never tested the edge.

She had spent four years not testing the edge, and calling that peace.

* * *

The boat engine died below the bluff. Oars next, or a pole — she heard the knock of wood against a hull, unhurried. Whoever it was knew the landing. Knew the landing meant knowing her, or knowing about her, and the list of people who made night visits to her strip was short enough to count without taking off her gloves.

She closed the cowling on the rebuilt magneto, wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than rag, and hung the lantern on its nail. The compass she picked up last, thumb finding the worn spot on the lid without her permission, the way her thumb always did. Her father's ghost lived in that wear mark, and she resented it, and she carried it anyway. That was inheritance in river country: you didn't choose what washed downstream to you.

The footsteps came up the trail, gravel crunching, measured. Unhurried men with business were the dangerous ones; hurrying meant passion, and passion could be redirected.

She stepped out into the dark and waited, arms crossed against the river damp, the compass heavy in her pocket like a small brass heart beating against her hip.

A lantern kindled at the edge of the strip, and Mr. Ansel's voice, mild and courteous as ever, said her name.
