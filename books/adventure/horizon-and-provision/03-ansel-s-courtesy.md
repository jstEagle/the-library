## 3. Ansel's Courtesy

The rain came in the night and left the strip smelling of rust and green things, and Ulyana spent the morning walking the length of it with her boots squelching, looking for the soft spots that would eat a wheel. There were three of them, all new since last week. She shoveled gravel into the worst one from the pile by the fuel drums, tamped it down with the flat of the shovel, and was starting on the second when she heard the engine.

Not a plane. A boat motor, upriver, running against the current at a steady unhurried pace, the way engines sound when the man behind the throttle isn't worried about anything. She stood with the shovel in her hands and listened to it come, and she knew before the bend gave up its view who would be standing in the bow.

Mr. Ansel traveled like weather. He arrived when he arrived, he changed everything slightly, and then he left, and you were left measuring what had shifted. The launch was one of the syndicate's — grey hull, no name painted on, just a registration number stenciled small enough to be deniable. Two crew in matching oilskins. Ansel himself sat amidships under an umbrella that someone else held, wearing a dry tweed coat that had no business within four hundred kilometers of this river, and he looked up at her on the bank with the expression of a man arriving slightly early for lunch.

"Miss Ishikawa." He raised his voice just enough to carry over the water. "Your strip is better than I remembered."

"It's worse than I remembered," she said. "That's why I'm shoveling gravel into it."

He smiled at that as if she'd said something charming, and the launch nosed in against the bank where the gravel was firmest, which meant he'd been here before recently enough to know, or had asked someone, or simply read the river the way she did. That was the thing about Ansel that kept her off balance: he did his homework without ever appearing to work.

She dropped the shovel and walked down as the crew made fast. Up close Ansel smelled of wool and something faintly chemical, a preservative smell, like documents kept in good condition. His hair was silver at the temples and nowhere else, arranged rather than grown. He did not offer to shake hands. He never had. With Ansel, courtesy was a complete system; there was nothing left over for contact.

"I've brought you a contract," he said.

"You've brought me a boat."

"And a contract." He produced it from an oilskin wallet, and even that was characteristic — the wallet was better than hers, the paper inside was heavy stock, and the pen he offered was gold-capped and warm from riding in his inside pocket. Everything about him suggested that whatever he wanted from her, he had already decided she would provide it, and had budgeted accordingly. "May we walk? I find business goes easier in motion."

They walked the strip together while the crew stayed with the boat. He admired her hangar — "You've kept the doors true; most men let them sag" — and asked after the engine — "The old Wright still turning over?" — and each question landed precisely on something she'd mentioned once, months ago, to a syndicate clerk filling out a form. He remembered. Being remembered by Mr. Ansel felt less like being known than like being filed.

"The Meridian expedition," he said, when they were halfway down the strip and out of earshot of the water. "You've heard the rumors. Everyone on this river trades them like small change."

"They've gone quiet," Ulyana said. "That's what I heard."

"Quiet," Ansel agreed, tasting the word. "Eleven days now. No radio schedule met, no resupply acknowledged. Meridian is a well-provisioned scientific survey with excellent equipment and eleven experienced people, and eleven experienced people do not simply stop answering their radios unless something has happened to them." He paused at the soft spot she'd filled, looked down at the fresh gravel with approval, and stepped over it. "The company is naturally concerned. Families are concerned. Insurers are concerned. So the company is mounting a search."

"That sounds reasonable," she said, because it did, and because the reasonableness was exactly what made her want to put the shovel between herself and him.

"We need gear moved upriver. Radios, medical stores, cold-weather kit, a collapsible boat. Some hundreds of kilos, and it needs to go to the last coordinates Meridian reported, which sit above the second gorge where no fixed-wing strip exists and no boat has yet been willing to gamble the water level." He turned to her then, and his face did something rare: it became frank. Or wore frankness so well she couldn't tell the difference, which amounted to the same thing in practice. "There is one pilot on this river with the aircraft, the range, and the judgment to put that load down on a sandbar short of the gorge and not kill anyone doing it. You'll be surprised to hear I don't consider that flattery. It's arithmetic."

"What's the pay?"

"Generous."

"How generous?"

"Enough to retire a meaningful portion of your account." He said *your account* the way a priest says *your sins* — gently, without judgment, with total familiarity. "I know the number, Miss Ishikawa. I've always known the number. It isn't my habit to embarrass people with it."

They had reached the end of the strip, where the grass took over and the forest started its slow annual argument with the runway's edge. Ulyana stopped and looked upriver, where the water ran high and brown with the early monsoon, carrying whole trees past her strip like matchsticks. Somewhere up there, eleven people weren't answering their radios. Somewhere up there was the second gorge, which she'd overflown twice in her life and both times had felt the air coming off the ice like a hand pushing back.

"Why me?" she said. "You have boats. You have other planes further south. You could charter—"

"I could," Ansel said. "And every one of those options involves men who don't know the upper river, landing on ground they haven't read, in aircraft I can't vouch for, in a season that punishes strangers." He folded his hands. "You know the river because it's yours. Your father flew it before you. There is no one whose failure I can predict less and whose success I trust more. Also—" and here the courtesy tightened, so gently she almost missed it, the way you miss a snare closing until the weight comes up on the line, "—there is the matter of the account itself. It has stood for six years with remarkable patience. The company has been patient because your father was valued, and because you have paid faithfully, cargo by cargo. But patience is not infinite, Miss Ishikawa. If you decline this contract, I'm instructed to begin recovery proceedings. I would hate that. I think you would hate it more."

"There it is," she said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The leash. You keep it very clean. You polish it. But it's still a leash."

Ansel considered this with what appeared to be genuine interest, the way a chess player considers an interesting but doomed move. "I prefer to think of it as a relationship of long standing," he said. "One with obligations on both sides. The company honored its side — the loan, the forbearance, the years of quiet. Now the company asks. And asking, Miss Ishikawa, is what creditors do instead of taking." He held out the contract and the pen. "Read it. Take your time. The boat will wait; the season won't."

She read it. She read it twice, standing in the wet grass while the river carried trees past and the monsoon stacked itself on the southern horizon in towers the color of bruise. The terms were clear, which surprised her — Ansel's contracts were usually foggy, and clarity meant he wanted her to understand exactly what she was agreeing to, which meant the trap wasn't hidden. It was displayed. Fly the search party's equipment to the last known coordinates of Meridian. Land where feasible. Cooperate fully with the search personnel provided by the company. Payment against debt upon completion or upon confirmation of loss of aircraft, whichever occurs first.

She read that clause three times. *Upon confirmation of loss of aircraft.* If the mountain ate her plane, the debt died with it. It was almost kind. It was the kindest sentence in the document, and it was also the one that told her how much the company wanted this done — they'd priced her death into the deal and called it generosity.

"And the search personnel?" she said. "Who are they?"

"A hydrologist, for the technical assessment. A company representative to manage logistics." A pause so small it might have been breathing. "You'll meet them tomorrow at the syndicate dock, oh-six-hundred. Crates will be waiting. They are sealed, Miss Ishikawa, and I'll ask you not to open them — manifest weights are certified, and the seals are insurance requirements. I trust that won't trouble a professional."

It troubled her enormously. She said nothing.

"One more thing." Ansel took back the pen when she didn't move to sign, capped it, and held the contract lightly between two fingers, letting the wind worry the corner of the page. "Whatever happened to Meridian — and I want to be clear that I hope it was nothing, nothing at all — you may find things up there. Equipment. Papers. The river takes things and gives them back in its own time. If you find anything belonging to the expedition, it comes back with you. All of it. Unexamined, ideally, but returned regardless." He smiled again, and this smile had a different weight than the others, denser, like ballast. "A company likes to close its accounts. Even its sad ones."

Especially its sad ones, she thought. Especially the ones with papers in them.

She thought about her father then, standing in this same spot twenty years ago with the brass compass in his hand, pointing it not north but upriver, toward the ice, saying *the maps lie, little one, the maps always lie, that's how you know who drew them.* She thought about the ledger in her footlocker with his signature on page after page, borrowing against the plane, against the strip, against his own life, and dying with the number still growing. She thought about six years of dawn runs and invisible landings and being nobody to anybody, which had been the only freedom the debt left her, and which she had somehow learned to love.

If she signed, she flew upriver into whatever silence had swallowed eleven people, carrying sealed crates she couldn't inspect, for a company that priced her coffin into the fare.

If she refused, she lost the strip, the plane, the hangar, the only shape her life had held since she was nineteen years old. Recovery proceedings. She knew what those looked like downriver — the auctions on the syndicate dock, men carrying her father's tools out of her father's hangar while she stood on the bank with her hands empty.

The rain began again, fine and warm, beading on the contract's heavy paper without soaking in. Good paper. Paper built to survive rivers.

Ulyana held out her hand for the pen.

Ansel passed it back to her with both hands, formally, the way you hand over something precious, and watched her sign her name on the line with the small satisfied stillness of a man watching a lock accept its key. He did not gloat. Gloating would have required admitting there had been a contest. He simply blotted the signature, folded the contract along its crease, and restored it to the oilskin wallet with the tenderness of a man putting away a photograph.

"Oh-six-hundred," he said. "The crates will be marked. Load the aft first — the hydrologist will tell you why, and he'll be right." He turned toward the boat, then paused, half-turned back, and added, almost as an afterthought, in a voice pitched only for her: "Your father once told me this river forgives nothing and forgets everything. I've found him right on both counts. Do try to come back, Miss Ishikawa. Accounts closed by death are so much harder to reconcile."

The launch pulled away against the current, umbrella furled now, tweed coat shrinking into the grey of the water and the grey of the sky until she couldn't have said which grey it had been. The engine note faded upriver, toward the gorge, toward the ice, toward the coordinates already printed in her mind's eye from a single glance at the contract's annex map.

Ulyana stood on the bank a long time after the sound was gone, holding the pen he'd forgotten to take back, gold cap warming in her fist like something stolen. Then she walked to the hangar, opened the footlocker, and took out the ledger — her father's ledger, the debt's gospel, six years of numbers in a dead man's handwriting — and set it on the workbench beside the brass compass, where the two objects sat together like question and answer, though she couldn't yet have said which was which.

Tomorrow, oh-six-hundred. Sealed crates. A hydrologist, a fixer, and the last known coordinates of eleven silent people.

She picked up the compass. The needle swung, settled, and pointed upriver, faithful as ever, toward whatever the maps were lying about this time.
