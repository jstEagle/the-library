## 4. Passengers

They came up the strip at first light, three of them and a stack of crates that weighed like sin.

Ulyana watched from the cockpit while she ran her preflight, one eye on the gauges and one on the men walking the grass toward her plane through the ground mist. The tall one carried nothing. That told her something already. Men who carry nothing expect other people to carry things for them.

The short one — round-faced, sweating in the cool of the morning, a canvas satchel strapped across his chest like a bandolier — was talking the whole way. She couldn't hear the words over her engine warm-up, but she could see his mouth going, going, going, and she could see the tall man not listening, which meant the tall man had already decided whatever needed deciding.

The third man did the carrying. Two trips. He loaded the crates into her hold without asking where, which meant he'd been told, which meant someone had measured her hold before she'd ever seen these men. She filed that away with the other small cold facts she kept.

Ansel's contract had named them: Okonkwo, hydrologist. Reyes, logistics. No surname for the carrier. He'd signed the manifest as *T.* and left it at that.

"Fuel's topped," she said when they reached the wing. "Six hours' range with this load, four if we fight headwind past the bend. Weather window closes tomorrow night. We fly today or we don't."

Reyes smiled at her. It was a good smile, practiced, the kind that arrived a half-second after the eyes finished assessing you. "Ms. Ishikawa. Pleasure." His handshake was dry and brief, a man who touched people the way he touched paperwork — lightly, so nothing could be traced back to him.

"Okonkwo," said the talker, thrusting out a hand that was soft and ink-stained. "Adaeze Okonkwo — no, wait, that's my sister. I'm Chidi. Chidi Okonkwo. You can call me Doc, everyone does, though I'm not that kind of doctor, I'm the water kind." He laughed at his own joke before it landed, then looked embarrassed, then talked again to cover it. "You've flown the upper river? How far does the fog usually burn off? Ansel said noon but Ansel says a lot of things—"

"Noon if we're lucky," Ulyana said. "Get in. Front seat's yours. Reyes rides behind me."

Something flickered across Reyes's face — fast, gone. She pretended not to see it. She was good at pretending not to see things; it was most of what twenty years of flying other people's cargo had taught her.

The carrier — T. — climbed in last without being asked twice, folded himself into the remaining seat among the crates, and closed his eyes before the wheels left the grass.

* * *

She read people by how they sat in turbulence. Everyone has a tell, and the air finds it faster than any interrogation. Her father taught her that, or maybe she learned it watching him watch passengers, back when she was small enough to ride the jump seat and pretend to sleep.

Okonkwo gripped the sides of his seat at the first bump and narrated. "That's a thermal off the shingle bar, right? Air rising off the rocks?" He wasn't scared, exactly. He was a man who needed the world explained, and turbulence was just the world refusing. When she banked around the first line of storm cloud he leaned into the turn like a boy at a fair, and she caught him mouthing numbers — counting seconds, probably, doing some hydrologist's arithmetic about drift and distance. A man in love with data. Dangerous only to himself, she guessed. For now.

Reyes sat perfectly still. That was the tell. Nobody sits perfectly still in chop unless they've trained themselves to, and nobody trains themselves to sit still unless stillness is part of the job. He didn't grip anything. He didn't brace. When the plane dropped through a hole in the air and her stomach floated, his head didn't move a centimeter, and she understood she was carrying a professional. Not a thug — worse. Thugs were honest about what they were.

T., in the back, slept through all of it. Or performed sleeping. She checked him once in the mirror and his breathing was too even, too rhythmic, a metronome set by someone who wanted to be underestimated. Fine. Underestimate him. But count the exits.

And the crates. Four of them, stenciled with a shipping code she didn't recognize and a weight class that made no sense for survey gear. Survey instruments came in long thin cases. These were cubes, dense as ballast, and when she'd tried to shift one during loading T.'s hand had come down on it — gently, no threat in it at all, just finality, like a door closing.

"What's in them?" she'd asked.

"Equipment," Reyes had said.

"For weighing water?"

"For measuring it very precisely."

She'd let it go. You learned to let things go in this business, the way you learned to eat standing up and sleep sitting down. But letting go wasn't forgetting. In the air now, with the river unspooling below them brown and patient as a rope, she found herself doing the math she shouldn't do: crate weight, fuel burn, center of gravity. Whatever was in those boxes, it rode heavy and low, and it shifted nothing in the turns because it was packed by someone who knew mass the way she knew weather.

Somebody had planned this flight before she'd agreed to fly it.

* * *

*The river, from above, is a sentence written in a language of mud.*

*Chidi Okonkwo knows this the way other men know scripture. Every oxbow is a word the water wrote and then regretted. Every sandbar is a comma. He presses his forehead near the glass and reads, and what he reads frightens him, because the river is running lower than it should for this season, and the tributaries feeding it are running lower than that, and somewhere upstream there is a tap being turned that no map shows.*

*He thinks about the crates. He has signed things he shouldn't have signed. He tells himself the data will be used for mapping, for science, for the public record — and then he remembers the clause in his contract, the one about proprietary findings, and the word* proprietary *sits in his stomach like swallowed ballast.*

*The pilot watches him in the mirror. He pretends not to notice her noticing. Everyone on this plane is pretending something. At least, he thinks, I'm pretending toward the truth instead of away from it.*

*Probably.*

*He grips the seat as the plane bucks and starts explaining thermals to no one.*

* * *

Past the second bend the weather found its voice.

It came the way monsoon weather always came — not as clouds arriving but as a decision already made, somewhere over the coast, that the sky would close today and everything beneath it would simply have to accept the terms. Ulyana saw the front stacked on the western horizon like a wall built by something with an opinion about her plans, and she turned south of the direct line, trading ten minutes of range for smoother air.

"We're deviating," Reyes said. Not a question. A notation.

"I don't file a flight plan with you."

"Ansel expects us at the coordinates by dark."

"Ansel isn't flying this plane." She kept her voice level, hands light on the yoke. "I am. And this plane doesn't go into that front, because planes that go into fronts stop being planes and start being debris, and debris doesn't deliver anyone anywhere by dark."

Silence behind her. She watched him in the mirror and saw him recalibrating — not angry, she realized. Recalculating. Filing her response somewhere, cross-referencing it against something. A man building a dossier in real time.

Okonkwo, bless him, filled the silence. "Is it true about Meridian?" he asked, leaning forward between the seats. His breath smelled of coffee and nerves. "The expedition. That they went quiet."

"You know more than I do, Doc. They hired you, didn't they?"

"They hired*someone*. I got a retainer and a list of instruments and a rendezvous I wasn't supposed to ask about." He laughed, too quick. "Which I asked about. Obviously."

"And?"

"And nobody answered." He said it lightly, but his hands had gone still in his lap, and stillness, Ulyana had learned, was where fear lived when it got tired of fidgeting. "Three weeks of silence. Ansel says radio failure. Equipment. The plateau eats signals, he says." He paused. "He said it like a man rehearsing."

Ahead, the river bent north into the gorge country, and the mist lay along the water in long white ropes. Below them, a fisher's canoe traced the current, absurdly small, a seed on a vein. Ulyana felt the old familiar feeling rise in her — the one she usually enjoyed, the godlike loneliness of altitude, the whole world reduced to pattern. Today it curdled. Patterns were how lies hid.

"Meridian was nine people," she said. "Survey crew, two boats, a satellite rig. My father flew their first reconnaissance eight years ago." She hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out anyway, the way true things do when the cabin gets quiet.

"Your father knew the expedition?" Okonkwo, sharp despite the chatter. She gave him credit.

"My father knew every expedition. He flew this river before the maps did." Before the syndicate bought the maps, she didn't say. Before the debt. Before the compass came to her in a padded envelope with no note, just the brass worn bright on one side where his thumb had lived for forty years.

Reyes stirred behind her. "The coordinates are marked on your chart," he said. "A cove below the terminal moraine. There's a beach — gray shingle, hard to see from altitude until you're on it. We'll need two passes."

"I know the cove."

"You've been there?"

"I've flown over it. Everyone's flown over it. It's where the glacier meets the river and the river goes cold enough to kill a swimmer in four minutes." She glanced at the mirror. "Why do you ask questions you already know the answers to, Mr. Reyes?"

The smile again, half a second late. "Habit. I like to hear how people say things."

"And how do I say things?"

"Like a woman who's already decided what she believes." He settled back. "Makes my job easier."

She wanted to ask what his job was. She didn't, because asking would tell him she cared about the answer, and caring was currency, and she was done spending. Instead she flew. She flew the way she always flew — precisely, economically, invisibly — and let the miles do the talking.

* * *

*T. opens his eyes when the engine note changes. He doesn't move otherwise. This is the discipline: you learn the machine's voice, and you learn the voices of the people around the machine, and you keep your own voice silent.*

*The pilot is good. Better than the file suggested. The file said competent, indebted, controllable. The file did not say that she reads turbulence like a page, or that she deviates around weather without asking permission, or that she mentioned her father unprompted — which means the father is load-bearing inside her, which means the debt is load-bearing, which means Mr. Ansel's architecture holds.*

*He catalogues: hydrologist, nervous, honest-leaning, will require management. Fixer, smooth, loyal to payroll, will break under real pressure. Pilot —*

*The pilot banks around a rain shaft with a touch so light the crates don't shift, and T. revises his entry, and revises it again, and finally writes what he should have written from the beginning:* unknown. *Unknown is the worst entry a man like him can make. Unknown means the plan has a hole in it shaped exactly like her.*

*He closes his eyes and resumes his breathing exercise, four counts in, six counts out, and waits for the cove.*

* * *

They made the gorge mouth at midafternoon, walls rising on either side like the covers of a book closing, and the air went rough where the cold drainage fell off the ice and collided with the warm valley flow. Turbulence, real turbulence now, the kind that argues with the airframe. Ulyana's hands moved constantly, small corrections, a conversation with the sky conducted entirely in verbs.

Okonkwo stopped narrating. He gripped the seat frame and stared straight ahead, and she heard him breathing in deliberate fours, and thought: *he's frightened, and he's managing it, and he hasn't asked me to turn around.* Whatever had brought a soft-handed academic into this gorge, it outweighed his fear. She wondered what they were paying him. She wondered if it was enough.

Behind her, Reyes leaned forward, and for the first time since the strip his stillness cracked — not with fear but with intent, a man leaning toward a destination.

"There," he said. "The shingle. One pass to mark it, then we set down."

She saw it on the second pass: a gray crescent of beach wedged between the moraine and the water, and on it —

Her hands stayed steady. Her voice almost didn't.

"That's not a camp," she said. "That's a burn scar."

The beach below was black in a crescent maybe forty meters wide. Structures flattened. Metal glinting where the fire had stripped paint. And at the waterline, tied to a rock, the ghost of something — a mooring line, cut clean, trailing into the current.

No boats. No people. No movement at all except the river, moving the way it always moved, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Set down," Reyes said quietly. All the warmth had gone out of his voice, and what remained underneath was the actual man, the one the smile was built over. "Set down, Ms. Ishikawa. Mr. Ansel will want a full inventory."

Ulyana looked at the black beach, at the cut line trailing in the current like a loose thread pulled from the hem of the world, and she brought the plane around, and she began to descend — because the contract was signed, and the debt was real, and the fuel was spent, and the sky behind them was already closing like a hand.

But some part of her, the part that read passengers by how they sat in turbulence, had already understood what the crates were for, and what the silence upriver meant, and that she had not flown here as a pilot.

She had flown here as evidence.
