## 5. Last Known Coordinates

The river bent north at a place the charts called only Mile 212, and Meridian's coordinates put their camp two miles past the bend, on the east bank where the floodplain widened into gravel bars and stunted willow. Ulyana had flown over it a dozen times in three years of supply runs — a smudge of canvas and solar panels beside the water, smoke rising straight up on calm mornings like a signature.

She flew over it now at four hundred feet, banking to look, and there was no signature. No smoke. No canvas. Just a black rectangle printed onto the earth, precise as a stamp.

"That's not right," she said, mostly to herself.

Reyes leaned forward between the seats. "What's not right?"

"Camp's burned." She leveled out. "Burned clean."

"Lightning," Reyes said. "Dry season lightning happens."

"It's been raining for six days." She glanced back at him. He had that stillness he did when he was deciding what to tell her, which was most of his stillness. Okonkwo, wedged behind the crates they'd never let her open, had gone very quiet in the way of a man doing arithmetic.

"How far to a landing?" Okonkwo asked.

"There's a shingle bar half a mile downstream. Water's low enough this time of year." She didn't like it. The bar was soft at the upstream end and the current ran crooked through the middle, and landing a floatplane on crooked water with two passengers who weren't pilots and a cargo manifest she hadn't seen was the kind of thing she'd spent three years learning not to do. But the contract said *assess and report*, and Ansel's voice had said it too, in that courteous register that made refusal sound like bad manners.

She brought the plane around and set it down on the bar with more skill than the situation deserved, and the water took them with a long hiss, and then they were stopped, engines ticking, in a silence so complete she could hear the willows working against each other in the wind.

* * *

They walked in single file along the bank because the gravel near the water was quick with silt. Reyes carried the satellite phone in a waterproof case slung across his chest like a pacemaker. Okonkwo carried nothing but a notebook and a look of professional unhappiness. Ulyana carried the flare pistol she kept under the pilot's seat, which she told herself was standard procedure and knew was not.

The smell reached them before the sight did. Burned nylon has a sweetness to it, chemical and wrong, and under it something else — old ash rained on by days of monsoon, a cold hearth smell, wet char. Then the trees opened and the camp was there.

It had been a proper expedition camp: two large dome tents, a cook shelter, a gear line, a work table under tarp, a latrine screened off downwind. Someone had built it with competence. Someone had also destroyed it with the same quality. The tents weren't just burned; they were burned *down*, collapsed inward, and the aluminum poles had been pulled from the wreckage and laid out parallel on the gravel like specimens. The radio mast was down. The solar panels were smashed — not cracked, smashed, stomped into glittering fragments that the rain had scattered across fifty meters of mud.

Ulyana crouched at the edge of it and let the place talk.

Fire moves in patterns. Lightning comes down and goes sideways and up; it's chaotic, it skips, it leaves a tree scarred next to a tent untouched. This fire had started in at least three places at once and burned evenly outward, fed. Accelerant, or fuel drums opened and poured, or both. And the cleanup afterward — the poles aligned, the wreckage consolidated, the ash raked flat in places — that wasn't panic. Panic doesn't rake.

"Reyes," she said. "Come look at this."

He came. He looked. His face did the thing where it became a door closing.

"Lightning," he said again, without conviction.

"Lightning didn't stomp the solar panels. Lightning doesn't align tent poles."

"Animals, then. Scavengers drag things—"

"Bears don't rake ash." She stood. "Reyes. I've flown this river for three years. I know what a wreck looks like and I know what a message looks like. This is a message."

Okonkwo had drifted toward the work table, or what remained of it. He was standing very still over a scatter of metal on the ground, and when Ulyana came closer she saw why. Radios. Four of them, maybe five — handhelds and a base unit, all of them broken, and broken specifically. Not burned with everything else, though fire would have taken them easily enough. They'd been set aside and destroyed by hand. A rock lay nearby with radio-colored paint on it.

"They wanted these dead," Okonkwo said quietly. "Not damaged. Dead. You burn a camp, the radios go with it. Unless you want to be sure no one ever pulls one out of the ash and finds it works." He turned one over with the toe of his boot, careful not to touch it. "This is methodical. This is someone making certain."

"Or someone angry," Reyes offered.

"Angry people throw radios in the river." Okonkwo looked up. "Patient people smash them on rocks."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river went on being the river, brown and muscular and indifferent, sliding past the burned camp as if camps burned here every season.

"Eight people," Ulyana said. "Meridian fielded eight. I hauled four of them out here myself in April — Dr. Voss-Adjani, two techs, a kid named Pell who got airsick every ten minutes and apologized for it the whole way." She heard her own voice go flat, which was how she knew it mattered. "Where are eight people?"

"Evacuated," Reyes said. "Downriver, ahead of the weather. That's the sensible read."

"Sensibly, you'd take the radios."

"Sensibly, you'd leave a note." Okonkwo was walking the perimeter now, slow, hands clasped behind him like a man touring a ruin. "There's no note. There's no cache marker. Expedition protocol — I've read Meridian's field manual, it came in the contract annex — is that any departure from basecamp gets logged on the whiteboard in the cook shelter, date, destination, expected return." He stopped. "Is there a whiteboard?"

Ulyana found it. Or found its frame: a sheet of white plastic screwed to two posts, scorched at the edges, wiped clean. Not burned blank — wiped, with a rag or a sleeve, recently enough that faint ghosting remained where dry-erase ink resists total erasure. She tilted it against the light. Lines. Columns. The shadow of handwriting.

"You can't recover that," Reyes said behind her.

"I'm not trying to." She lowered the board. "I'm noting that someone bothered to wipe it before they burned everything else. You torch a camp in five minutes or you erase the schedule first. They had time. They weren't running."

"Maybe they erased it themselves before evacuating."

"Then they'd have taken the radios," Okonkwo said, "and left a note, and not smashed the panels." He said it without heat, almost apologetically, the way he seemed to say everything, and somehow that made it worse. Reyes turned and walked toward the latrine screen with his jaw set, needing to be somewhere else.

* * *

The lifeboat was the thing that settled it, though none of them said so for an hour.

Meridian's gear manifest — Ulyana had signed for part of it in April, crates stenciled MERIDIAN EXP / 7 OF 9 — had included an inflatable rescue boat, hard-shell bottom, outboard motor. Standard for glacier country, where the water eats people. She found the cradle for it at the camp's downstream edge: two logs notched to hold the hull, the tie-downs cut clean, the motor's mounting bracket empty. Footprints in the drying mud around it, weeks old, hardened into ridges by rain. Boot prints, several sets, heading toward the water.

"One boat," she said. "Nine-person capacity, if it's the model I shipped. Eight people, one boat, and they left from here going—" she pointed downriver, then upriver, then let her hand fall. "Going somewhere."

"Could have ferried in shifts," Reyes said.

"To where? There's no camp downstream inside a hundred miles that isn't on our route. We'd have seen them. We'd have *heard* them." She looked at the cut tie-downs again. Clean cuts, single stroke each. "And whoever left didn't leave in a hurry. Hurried people saw halfway through a tie-down and tear the rest."

Okonkwo crouched and studied the boot prints without touching them. "At least four distinct tread patterns," he murmured. "Possibly five. All adult. All walking, not running — stride length is consistent." He glanced up at Ulyana. "You notice a lot, Ms. Ishikawa."

"I notice cargo," she said. "People are just cargo that complains."

But that wasn't true anymore, and the camp was making it less true by the minute. She kept seeing the kid, Pell, gray-faced in the right-hand seat somewhere over the gorge, apologizing for being sick into the bag she'd handed him, and her saying *kid, everyone pukes on this run, it's basically a tradition.* Eight people. One boat. No note.

"Here's what I think," she said, and heard herself decide to say it, which was itself new. "I think somebody came upriver after Meridian got here. I think there was a conversation those people didn't survive as a team, whatever happened to them as bodies. I think the ones who could leave, left in the boat, and the camp was burned to close the account. And I think we're standing in the middle of the reason your syndicate wants this stretch of river assessed so badly that you're paying my debt to get me to fly you into it."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Rain misted between them. When he spoke, his voice was level and almost gentle, which from Reyes was its own kind of alarm.

"What I log," he said, "is lightning strike, accidental fire, camp destroyed, no remains located, party presumed evacuated downriver prior to onset of monsoon. That's what the report says. That's what keeps this simple."

"Simple for who?"

"For everyone." He held her eyes. "Ms. Ishikawa. You're contracted for transport and assessment. Assessment means observable facts. Observable fact: camp burned. Cause undetermined but consistent with natural ignition. Everything else is story, and stories don't pay debts."

"There it is," she said quietly.

"There what is?"

"The debt. You keep it in your pocket like a tool. Every time I look at something too long, out it comes." She should have stopped. Some old instinct, three years deep, told her to stop — smile, shrug, fly the man home, keep the leash slack. She didn't stop. "I'll sign whatever factual report you write about the fire, Reyes. I'll even agree it might've been lightning, because I can't prove otherwise and neither can you. But I'm not agreeing it was an accident, and I'm not forgetting the radios, and if anyone asks me directly I'll say exactly what I saw."

Something moved behind his face — not anger. Recalculation. "That's unwise."

"Probably."

"Mr. Reyes." Okonkwo had come up silently, notebook closed against the damp under his arm. "A word about the report, when you draft it. I'll need to append my own observations as the expedition's consulting hydrologist. My contract specifies independent findings." He smiled, mild as milk. "Independent is the operative word. I observed deliberate destruction of communications equipment. I observed evidence of orderly departure rather than flight. I'll note those. If your report differs materially from mine, the discrepancy will itself become a finding, and discrepancies travel upward much faster than either of our reports do."

Reyes stared at him. For the first time since the strip, Ulyana saw the fixer have to actually work — see him weigh, visibly, the cost of pushing against a man whose name carried institutional weight, versus the cost of letting the record split. It was like watching a man test ice.

"You'd put that in writing," Reyes said.

"I put everything in writing. It's why I'm expensive."

* * *

They walked back to the plane along the waterline, single file again, and nobody talked until the shingle bar came into view and the plane sat rocking gently at its mooring, white hull streaked brown, looking suddenly small and far from anywhere.

Ulyana stopped at the top of the bar.

"One more thing," she said. "Before we get in the air and start pretending."

Reyes waited. Okonkwo waited. Downstream, the river bent away north toward the mountains, toward the pale wall of ice visible on clear days from eleven thousand feet, the valley the maps forgot and the syndicate remembered.

"The lifeboat went somewhere," she said. "Eight people don't fit in it comfortably, but they fit. Which means some of Meridian might be alive, downriver or upriver or holed up in the valley waiting out the weather. And whoever burned this camp — whether it was Meridian's own people or someone else — knew exactly what they were doing, and they did it within the last three weeks, which means they may still be on this river." She looked at Reyes. "So when we file your nice clean report, I want it filed from somewhere with a radio tower between us and this bend. Because if the people who smashed those radios learn that a floatplane full of strangers came asking questions at Mile 212—"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The mist moved through the willows, and somewhere upstream a branch cracked — probably a deer, certainly a deer, almost certainly — and all three of them turned toward the sound at once, and stood listening to the river lie to them about being quiet.

"We leave at first light," Reyes said finally. "Report files from the relay station. Nobody's asking questions past that." He paused, and for just a second the door in his face opened a crack, and what was behind it looked a lot like fear. "Ansel will want to push farther upriver anyway. Weather window or not."

Ulyana looked north, at the water bending toward the ice, and felt curiosity die in her chest and something colder take its seat.

"Of course he will," she said.
