## 6. Weather Window Closing

The sky told her first.

Ulyana had learned to read weather the way other people read faces — early, out of necessity, before she'd ever trusted a forecast or a man. And the face the sky was making now was not a good one. She stood on the shingle at the edge of Meridian's burned basecamp, coffee can in hand, and watched the western horizon stack itself like plates for a dinner no one wanted to attend.

Three fronts. Maybe four. They came up the river valley in gray-blue shelves, each one taller than the last, their tops boiling white where the sun still found them, their bases dragging curtains of rain that hadn't yet decided where to fall. Between them ran lanes of hard blue light — windows, a pilot would call them, and every pilot knew that windows closed.

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. The nearest front was two hours out, maybe three if it dawdled over the ridge country. The second would be on top of the first by nightfall. The third by morning. After that, the monsoon proper would settle over the upper river like a lid on a pot, and nothing with wings would move for weeks. Nothing with wings was supposed to move now, honestly — this far into the season, this far upriver, she was already flying on borrowed atmosphere.

"Reyes." She said it loud enough to carry across the camp without shouting. He was crouched at the tree line doing something fussy with a satellite phone, his back to the water. "We're leaving."

He didn't turn around right away. That was its own kind of answer.

"We're leaving," she said again, and set down the coffee can, and walked toward him because some conversations you have to have standing up.

* * *

The argument lasted forty minutes, which was longer than it should have, because Reyes was good at his job and his job was making other people feel unreasonable.

"The window holds," he said, standing beside the plane with a clipboard he never wrote on. "Ansel's meteorologist says—"

"Ansel's meteorologist is sitting in an office in the lowlands looking at satellite imagery from six hours ago." Ulyana pointed west with her whole arm, the way her father used to point at storms, as if you could shame them. "I'm standing under the actual sky. Those fronts are stacking. When they merge, the valley fills with cloud from the water to eleven thousand feet, and there is no flying through that, around that, or above that. Not in this plane. Not in any plane I'd put my body in."

"So we wait it out here." Reyes said it lightly, testing. "A few days. The gear's already ashore."

"Here?" She looked at the burned basecamp — the blackened tent frames, the smashed radio crates, the fire pit full of things that had been radios once and were now just melted shapes. "In what? On what? We've got maybe ten days of food between us, and the river's rising already. You can hear it."

You could. The river had come up half a meter since dawn, brown and muscular, hauling branches past the camp like it was clearing furniture out of a room it owned.

Okonkwo stood a little apart, near the waterline, watching the current with the particular attention of a man who measured rivers for a living. He hadn't said much all morning. When he finally spoke, it was quiet, and it landed harder than anything Reyes had managed.

"She's right about the sky," he said. "And I think she's right about something else too. This camp wasn't burned in a hurry. Look at the arrangement of it. Someone took their time destroying the radios specifically." He turned a fragment of green circuit board over in his long fingers. "Accidents burn tents. People burn communications."

Reyes's jaw did something small and controlled. "The syndicate's assessment is accident."

"I'm sure it is," Okonkwo said, mild as milk.

Ulyana watched the two men not look at each other, and felt the old familiar tightening in her chest — the sense of being inside a machine whose purpose she could only guess at. She'd spent nine years keeping herself outside machines. Now she was cargo in one.

"My call is this," she said. "We load now, we fly south ahead of the first front, we land at the syndicate strip with an hour to spare, and Ansel can send his next expedition next season with better radios and worse luck. That's the sensible call. That's the only call."

"That's your call," Reyes said. "It's not mine to make. And it's not yours either, Ms. Ishikawa. You're charter."

The words went in clean, the way cheap things go in deep. *You're charter.* Nine years of owning her own aircraft, her own route, her own name on her own manifest — reduced to a woman who flew where she was pointed.

"I'm the one who decides whether we all die in a valley with no strip," she said.

"And I'm the one who decides whether you spend the rest of your life paying off a debt you've got nineteen years left on." He said it without heat, almost kindly, which was somehow worse. "I don't want that. Genuinely. Fly us out tomorrow if the window holds. But tonight, we call it in. Protocol."

* * *

They called it in at dusk, huddled under a salvaged tarp strung between two poles, the radio Reyes had brought — his own, military-green, the kind that didn't advertise itself — hissing static into the wet air.

Ulyana stood where she could watch both the radio and the sky. The first front had arrived while they argued; it sat over the western ridges now like a sleeping animal, and behind it the second front had closed most of the gap. The lane of blue between them had narrowed to a slit.

"Meridian recovery party, Meridian recovery party, this is Reyes for Ansel, do you copy?"

Static. Then a voice she knew came through, thin and tinny and unmistakable, and Ulyana felt it in her sternum before her brain caught up: Mr. Ansel, sounding exactly as he always sounded, courteous as a bank lobby.

"Loud and clear, Reyes. Report."

"Basecamp confirmed destroyed. Radios smashed, equipment burned. No bodies. One lifeboat missing from inventory. Okonkwo believes deliberate. The pilot wants to extract ahead of the weather."

A pause, filled with rain beginning to tick on the tarp.

"The pilot," Ansel said, "is of course free to make her own professional assessment."

Ulyana leaned toward the handset. "Mr. Ansel. It's Ulyana. The window closes tonight. If I'm not airborne within—" she checked the sky, checked her gut, split the difference— "two hours, I'm grounded until the monsoon breaks. Weeks. Maybe more."

"Yes," Ansel said, and she could hear him considering it, actually weighing it, the courtesy in his voice like a gloved hand turning something over. "That presents a difficulty, Ulyana. The Meridian situation has become time-sensitive in ways I won't burden you with. We cannot wait out a monsoon. Therefore: you will proceed upriver by boat to the coordinates in the expedition file. The aircraft remains where it is. My people will secure it."

Every part of her body that understood airplanes went cold at once.

"Secure it how? Where? There's no strip, no hangar, no—"

"There's a flat stretch of shingle above the flood line two hundred meters inland. My surveyors used it last season. It will hold. A ground crew will reach it when the weather permits and ferry it home. You'll be compensated for the layover, naturally, at the standard rate."

"It's not about compensation." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "That plane is collateral. It's my father's debt made metal. If it sits on a shingle through a monsoon season, under trees, in flood country—"

"—then it will be exactly as safe as my word that it will be recovered intact," Ansel said, "which I would remind you has been good for nine years."

The rain thickened. Through the gap in the trees she could see the plane tied down where they'd left it that morning, nose into the wind, wings level, looking patient and stupid and hers. Water was already sheeting off the high wing. Somewhere under that cowling were cylinders she'd have covered, plugs she'd have installed, a tail she'd have weighted — and none of it, none of it, because she was about to walk away from it in a flood valley because a voice on a radio told her to.

"You're stranding me," she said.

"I'm re-tasking you," Ansel said. "There's a difference, and I suspect you know it. The boat is chartered and waiting eight kilometers downstream — a river man named Deng, whom I'm told is reliable. Reyes has the coordinates. Okonkwo goes with you; his expertise is required upstream. These are the arrangements, Ulyana. I'm sorry the weather hasn't cooperated with anyone's preferences, including mine."

And that was the thing about Ansel, she thought, listening to the rain hammer the tarp. He never gave you an order you could hate cleanly. He wrapped every leash in apology and compensation and the mild regret of a man forced by circumstances to do what he had plainly planned to do all along.

"Two hours ago you told me to assess and report," she said. "I assessed. I reported. You're overriding the assessment."

"I'm incorporating additional information you don't have. That's not an insult to your competence. It's a description of hierarchy." A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle: "Your father chafed at the same thing, near the end. Did you know that? He also believed the sky was always telling him the truth. Sometimes it was. But the sky doesn't sign contracts, Ulyana. People do. Goodnight."

The channel clicked dead.

* * *

She walked down to the plane in the dark with a flashlight in her teeth and did everything she could do, which was almost nothing.

Covered the intakes. Bagged the pitot. Installed the plugs she carried for exactly this indignity, though never once in nine years had she expected to use them on her own ship, on her own trip, by order of someone else. Weighted the tail with sandbags Reyes produced from somewhere, because of course Reyes had sandbags, because of course Reyes had prepared for the outcome where the plane stayed. He'd known. Standing in the rain beside her, holding the flashlight, he'd known since the lowlands.

"When did you find out the plan changed?" she asked him.

"Find out?" He kept the beam steady on the tail tie-down while she worked the knot. "Plans evolve. Ansel evolves them."

"Don't do that. Don't give me the fixer's shrug. When."

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for him. "There was a contingency in the file from the start," he said finally. "Air extraction was preferred. River continuation was authorized. I didn't choose it. I just didn't argue against it, because arguing against Ansel's contingencies is a hobby for people with spare lives."

She straightened up and looked at him across the wet flashlight beam. "You let me fly us four hundred kilometers upriver knowing the plane might stay."

"I let you fly us upriver knowing the mission might continue," Reyes said. "Same sentence. Different emphasis."

She turned away before she said something the debt couldn't survive, and stood instead with her hand flat on the cold engine cowling, the way she'd done since she was a girl, the way you'd calm a horse. Rain ran off the leading edge in a beaded curtain. Behind the plane, the forest stood black and dripping, and beyond it, invisible, the river climbed and climbed.

Nine years she'd kept the sky as her one open border. Debt on the ground, ledgers in offices, Ansel's courtesy pressing down like weather — but up there, above the cloud deck, the world had always been wide and hers and unowned. That was the real payment she'd taken for every run, every debt installment, every year of competent loneliness: altitude. Distance. The ability to leave.

Now the monsoon was closing that border too, and the man on the radio had simply picked the moment, the way he picked everything, patiently, with paperwork.

* * *

They left at first light, because Ansel's schedule allowed no better hour and because waiting longer meant watching the river take the shingle.

The water had come up another meter overnight. The plane sat above it still, on its sandbags and its stakes, nose into the wind that was now coming steadily out of the southwest, warm and wet and endless, the breath of the whole monsoon funneling up the valley. Ulyana looked back at it from the boat for as long as she could see it — a gray shape shrinking under gray sky, getting smaller, getting less hers.

Deng turned out to be a compact, unsmiling man of sixty-odd with hands like rope and eyes that never stopped moving along the water. His boat was long, shallow-drafted, powered by a diesel engine that sounded older than he was, and loaded — Ulyana noticed as they boarded — with crates she hadn't seen loaded, crates with stenciling she couldn't read from where she sat, crates that Reyes positioned aft and then sat on, casually, for the entire first hour.

Okonkwo sat amidships with his instrument cases strapped around him like a life vest, watching the banks slide past. The banks were drowning quietly. Villages' worth of forest stood knee-deep and rising, and every eddy had a new appetite.

"The gauge at the confluence," Okonkwo said, mostly to himself, "will break its record by Thursday."

"You sound pleased," Ulyana said.

"I sound like a hydrologist." He smiled faintly. "Pleased and appalled are adjacent professions."

Ahead, the valley narrowed. The river quickened between walls of green, and the clouds came down to meet it, lowering by the hour, until the ceiling that had been eleven thousand feet at dawn was a thousand feet by noon and five hundred by mid-afternoon, pressing the whole world down into a corridor of brown water and dripping leaves. There was no up anymore. There was no out. There was the river going where it went, and the boat going where it was told, and the rain arriving not in curtains now but in sheets, in walls, in the whole gray weight of a season settling in to stay.

Ulyana sat in the bow with the spray in her face and thought about ceilings. Five hundred feet. Three hundred. She had flown under ceilings her whole life — read them, respected them, slipped beneath them with inches to spare when the cargo mattered. But a ceiling you could climb through, wait out, outrun — that was weather. This was architecture. The monsoon wasn't passing over the valley; it was becoming the valley, roof and walls and locked door, and somewhere behind them her airplane sat under that roof like a pawned thing, and somewhere ahead of them was whatever Ansel wanted so badly that he'd trade a plane and a pilot's judgment to get it.

Reyes touched her shoulder and pointed forward, shouting over the engine: lights, faint, swinging at the water's edge. A fuel cache, a mooring, a syndicate waypoint — the river, she realized, was stitched with them, marked and provisioned like a company road, and she had flown over these waters for nine years without ever seeing the stitches.

That was the claustrophobia arriving all at once: not the clouds, not the rain, but the understanding that the country she'd thought of as empty — her escape, her horizon — had been mapped and claimed and furnished the whole time, and the map had never included her except as a line item.

The boat swung toward the lights. Behind them the sky finished closing like a door.

Somewhere upriver, in the drowned dark, someone was watching the same water — and the first sign of them came an hour later, when Deng cut the engine without being asked and held up one rope-scarred hand for silence, and out of the rain, thin and wrong and human, came the sound of someone calling for help.
