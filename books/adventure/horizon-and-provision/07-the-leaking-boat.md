## 7. The Leaking Boat

The boat was called *Providencia*, which Ulyana took as a bad joke the moment she saw it. She'd chartered worse, but not by much — a flat-bottomed river freighter gone soft at the seams, its paint peeling in long gray ribbons, its single diesel engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs. The owner, a wiry man named Tavares with three days of stubble and the eyes of someone who had been paid to ask no questions before, met them at the landing below the burned-out airstrip and said only: "Fuel's aboard. River's high. Don't argue with her."

Ulyana had spent eleven years arguing with machines. She knew exactly how that went.

They loaded at first light, working in a silence that had curdled overnight. Okonkwo stacked his sample cases with the care of a man handling eggs he'd personally counted. Reyes moved the syndicate crates — the ones she still hadn't been allowed to open — with a hand truck and a face like a closed ledger. Sable sat apart on the pilings, wrapped in a tarp, eating nothing, watching the water move north with an expression Ulyana couldn't file under anything useful.

The monsoon had arrived ahead of schedule. The sky over the western ridge was the color of wet slate, and the air tasted the way a storm cellar tastes — mineral, old, patient.

"River's running fast," Ulyana told Tavares as they cast off. "How's she handle in current?"

"Like a drunk woman," Tavares said. "But she floats."

He said it the way men say things they want to be true.

* * *

The first six hours were almost tolerable. The river here ran wide and brown-green, braided around gravel bars, and Ulyana stood at the wheel while Tavares dozed against the rail and the country unspooled — drowned forest standing hip-deep in floodwater, egrets lifting off in white flurries, the occasional roof of a house that had lost its argument with the dam failure decades ago, now just a shingled island.

Okonkwo came up beside her and pointed at a cutbank where the water ran milky with silt. "Glacial flour," he said. "That's coming down from the upper valley already. The melt's starting early this year."

"That means what?"

"It means the glacier is awake." He said it mildly, but his eyes stayed on the water a long time.

Reyes stayed below most of the day, wedged among his crates, doing something quiet with paper. Ulyana noticed he never came up when the boat rolled hard. She filed that away with everything else she was filing — the crates, the coded weight of his silences, the way he checked the fuel gauge twice an hour as if the numbers might change their minds.

Sable slept in the bow like a cat that had decided the safest place was the one farthest from everyone else. Once, passing a drowned church whose bell tower leaned out of the water at forty degrees, Ulyana saw her eyes were open, tracking the tower until it slid behind them.

"You know this river?" Ulyana asked her, once, keeping her voice low.

"I know pieces of it," Sable said. "Meridian came up by boat too. Before the plane runs started." A pause. "It was higher then."

That was all. But the way she said *higher* made Ulyana look at the waterline marks on the banks — old scars in the clay, two meters above the current — and feel the river as something with a memory and a temper.

* * *

The leak announced itself sometime after midnight, politely, the way bad news does when it wants to seem manageable.

Ulyana woke to a sound she couldn't place — a rhythmic sloshing beneath the deck plates, too regular to be the river against the hull. She lay in the narrow bunk behind the wheelhouse listening to it for a full minute before her body understood what her mind refused: the sound was inside the boat.

She found Tavares already down in the hold with a lantern, standing ankle-deep in water that hadn't been there when they anchored.

"Seam," he said. "Aft of the cargo well. She opened up on a bar somewhere today — I felt us touch twice, but she's taken hits like that for years." He said it without drama, which frightened Ulyana more than panic would have. Panic meant surprise. This was a man describing weather.

"How bad?"

Tavares crouched and put his palm flat on the water, reading it like a page. "Bilge pump buys us hours. Not days. We're two days from the gorge mouth at this speed, and the current's rising with every hour of rain upstream." He looked up at her. "You wanted to argue with the river. Now's your chance."

By dawn the water was mid-shin and the bilge pump — a temperamental thing that sounded like a dying animal — was cycling every twenty minutes instead of every two hours. They bailed in shifts. Okonkwo worked with the methodical endurance of a field researcher, bucket after measured bucket, counting strokes under his breath. Sable bailed like someone settling a personal score with the water. Reyes bailed badly, splashing more than moving, his hands shaking.

It was during the third shift, gray light coming through the hatches, that Ulyana learned the thing that rearranged the whole geometry of the boat.

She came down from the wheelhouse to relieve Sable and found Reyes sitting on a crate with his feet out of the water, arms locked around his knees, breathing in short controlled pulls. Not seasick. Not exhausted. Something else.

"We should beach her," he said. "Run her aground on one of those bars and wait out the rain."

"And walk into the valley? In monsoon?" Ulyana crouched so she could see his face. It was the first time she'd seen him look young. "We'd lose the supplies. We'd lose the boat."

"The boat is losing itself."

"Not yet." She studied him. "What are you actually afraid of, Reyes?"

He didn't answer for a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its fixer's polish: "I can't swim."

Ulyana blinked. "You work rivers for a living."

"I work *boats*," he said. "There's a difference. I've been on water my whole career and I have never once been in it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? If this hull opens up in the current—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "If we go down, I don't get to the surface. That's the whole equation. There is no surface for me."

The water sloshed against their shins, indifferent. Somewhere above, the bilge pump groaned through another cycle.

Ulyana thought about all the ways she'd read this man since the airstrip — the closed face, the guarded crates, the careful weight of him. She'd catalogued him as a threat. She'd never once catalogued him as a passenger. And now here he was, the syndicate's instrument, more afraid than any of them, because the river he'd been sent to master had never agreed to carry him safely.

"Okay," she said. "New arrangement. You bail. You bail better than anyone on this boat, and you don't stop, and in exchange nobody asks you to go near the rail. Deal?"

Something in his shoulders let go — not trust, exactly, but the temporary relief of a plan. "Deal," he said.

From the ladder, Sable watched them with an expression Ulyana couldn't name. Later, hauling buckets, Sable said, without looking up: "You just made yourself responsible for him."

"I made myself responsible for the boat."

"No," Sable said. "You did the other thing."

* * *

By noon the arithmetic had turned ugly.

The rain came in earnest — not the theatrical downpour of the coast but a fine, relentless, horizontal drizzle that soaked everything and made the river swell visibly, new debris riding the current: branches, half a fence post, a plastic drum turning slowly like a drowned man's toy. The bilge pump began cycling every ten minutes. Then every seven. Tavares ran the engine harder to keep steerage way, and the engine, in protest, began to smoke faintly from the exhaust elbow.

Ulyana did the math at the wheel with rain running down her collar. Pump capacity versus ingress rate. Fuel burn versus distance remaining. Current speed versus hull integrity. Three curves, all bending the wrong way, all intersecting somewhere in the dark about thirty hours out.

Thirty hours was generous. Rivers didn't honor estimates.

She called a council in the wheelhouse — Tavares, Okonkwo, herself. She left Reyes bailing below, because someone had to, and left Sable at the bow because Sable had appointed herself lookout and no one had argued.

"She beaches or she sinks," Tavares said flatly. "Those are the options. Beach her high on a bar, patch the seam with what's aboard, wait for the rain to slack. Or we keep going and we find out how deep this channel really is."

"If we beach, we lose a day. Maybe two," Ulyana said. "And the current's still rising. If she settles wrong on the bar, the river rolls her."

"If we don't beach, the river does it for us. Less politely."

Okonkwo cleared his throat. "There's a third option. The side channel at the old ferry crossing — twelve kilometers up. It's narrower, but it's cut into clay bank, sheltered from the main current. Meridian used it as a staging point. There may even be gear cached there." He glanced at Ulyana. "Your father used it too, if the stories are right."

Ulyana kept her eyes on the channel ahead. People kept doing that — offering her father up like a coin they assumed she'd want to spend. "Twelve kilometers," she said. "Can she make twelve kilometers?"

"With the pump running and everyone bailing," Tavares said. "Probably."

"Probably," Ulyana repeated. "Great. That's my favorite word."

* * *

They made nine of the twelve kilometers.

The tenth arrived as a grinding shudder through the hull — not the soft kiss of a gravel bar but the hard scrape of submerged timber, a deadfall swept down from some flooded tributary, invisible under the brown water. Tavares swore in three languages and fought the wheel. The engine screamed. For five seconds the boat wallowed broadside in the current, shipping water over the gunwale in green sheets, and Ulyana heard — clearly, unmistakably — the sound of the old seam giving up its long argument.

Then Tavares brought the nose around and they were moving again, listing hard to port, the bilge pump screaming through a cycle it would never finish.

"Beach her," Ulyana said. "Now. Anywhere."

Tavares put the *Providencia* into the clay bank of the side channel with the last of her steering, and the hull ground up onto the mud with a sound like a whale settling into shallows. The list eased. The river kept going past them, high and brown and utterly unconcerned.

They got the pump ashore, or rather Ulyana did, wading shin-deep to rig it on the bank with a tarp shelter, pumping the worst of the water out of the grounded hull so she wouldn't settle and split. Okonkwo inventoried the crates — dry, barely, thanks to Reyes, who had spent the entire crisis standing waist-deep in the hold moving them to high ground without once being asked, without once looking at the water any longer than he had to.

Now they were four strangers and a sleeping boat captain on a mud shelf in the rain, twelve kilometers short of shelter, with a holed boat, a failing pump, and a valley full of answers waiting upstream.

Ulyana stood at the edge of the bank watching the current carry the rain away northward. Behind her she heard Reyes say, very quietly, to no one in particular:

"Thank you. For the arrangement."

"Don't thank me," Ulyana said, not turning around. "Thank the boat. She's the one who held."

But that night, wrapping herself in a tarp under the overturned hull of the ship's dinghy, she thought about what Sable had said. *You just made yourself responsible for him.* And about the way the power on this river had shifted without anyone voting — the fixer who couldn't swim, the pilot with no plane, the defector who lied beautifully, the scientist counting bucket-strokes like prayers.

None of them could do this alone. The river had seen to that.

She was almost asleep when Sable's voice came through the rain, low, from the next tarp.

"There's a light," Sable said. "Upstream. On the bank. Someone's camped at the old ferry crossing."

Ulyana sat up. Through the drizzle, maybe two kilometers distant, a yellow point flickered where no camp should be — where, according to every map she'd ever flown, nothing had existed for thirty years.

"Meridian's people?" Okonkwo whispered, crawling closer.

"No," Sable said, and her voice had gone strange and flat. "Meridian's people are all deeper in the valley. I'd know." A pause, long enough for the rain to fill it. "That's not a campfire. Campfires don't blink."

Ulyana watched the light pulse — steady, deliberate, three flashes, pause, three flashes — and felt the last comfortable assumption of the trip dissolve. Somebody upriver knew they were coming.
