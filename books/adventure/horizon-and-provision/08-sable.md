## 8. Sable

I smelled the boat before I saw it — diesel and bilgewater and strangers — and I had maybe four seconds to decide whether to be found or to stay a rumor.

Four seconds is generous. Starvation makes time slow. I'd been living on snails and one unlucky duck for eleven days, and my body had started making decisions without consulting me. My legs were already moving toward the waterline before my mind finished the arithmetic: three people in a leaking longboat, one of them rowing like she hated the river personally, one sitting very still the way fixers sit when they're counting exits, and one hunched over a crate with his arms wrapped around it like a child around a dog.

Meridian's boats were all accounted for. Burned, mostly. So this was either rescue or the syndicate, and after what I'd done to the radios, I wasn't sure which of those frightened me more.

I stepped out from behind the driftwood stack with my hands visible and my voice scraped down to its last coat of paint.

"Boat," I said. "You're taking on water. Port side, aft of the second rib."

The rower — small woman, sunburned forearms, hands that knew engines even if they were on oars today — didn't startle. That was the first thing I noticed about Ulyana Ishikawa, though I didn't have her name yet. She didn't startle. She just looked at the waterline where I'd pointed, confirmed it with a glance, and said, "We know."

"We've known for an hour," said the still one.

"Then you know you'll sink before dark." I kept my hands up. Old habit from surveying country where the wrong gesture meant bear or border patrol. "There's a camp. Mine. You can beach there, patch the hull, wait out the front. It's coming." I nodded at the west, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise going bad. "Monsoon's stacking. You don't want to be on open water when it lands."

The fixer looked at the pilot. The pilot looked at the sky. The hydrologist — I guessed hydrologist because he was holding a crate of sample vials the way other men hold Bibles — said nothing at all, which told me plenty.

"You alone out here?" the fixer asked.

"Yes," I lied.

It was a stupid lie. There was one driftwood shelter behind me, one fire ring, one set of footprints in the mud, and I was thin enough that my ribs had their own shadows. But lying is a muscle and mine had atrophied into something strange over the past months — I could lie about enormous things with total calm and fumble the smallest ones. Ask me whether Meridian's team went deeper into the valley and I'd tell you yes, voluntarily, with a steady eye. Ask me if anyone else was at my camp and my voice would crack like river ice in March.

The pilot's eyes went from my face to my footprints to the single shelter and back. She didn't call the lie. She just filed it somewhere, the way pilots file weather.

"Beach us, then," she said.

* * *

My camp was three walls of driftwood and a tarp that had been a Meridian supply cover in a former life, and I watched them take it in with varying degrees of horror. The fixer — Reyes, I'd learn, though for the first hour he was only *the man who checked the perimeter twice* — looked at the shelter like it owed him money. The hydrologist, Okonkwo, looked at my survey stakes and his whole face changed, the way a musician's does hearing another instrument tune.

"These are Meridian's marks," he said quietly.

"They were," I said.

"And you are?"

"Sable." I let the name sit there without a surname, because the surname belonged to a version of me I was trying to drown. "Surveyor. Meridian expedition."

The pilot set down the bail bucket she'd been working and looked at me for a long moment. She had the kind of face that didn't perform anything — no welcome, no suspicion, just attention, which is rarer and more unnerving than either. "Ulyana Ishikawa," she said. "I flew your resupply runs. Twice."

"I know," I said. "You brought the core drill bits in April. And a crate of antibiotics we didn't order."

"Somebody ordered them. Somebody paid."

"Somebody wanted our medical logs to show we were healthy," I said, and then heard myself, and stopped, because that was already more truth than I'd planned to spend on strangers.

Reyes's head came around slowly. "What do you mean by that?"

"I mean," I said, choosing each word off a shelf while the rest of me screamed to stop, "that the expedition went deeper into the valley. Voluntarily. All of them. I stayed behind to hold the equipment, and then the equipment burned, and then I stayed anyway."

Every word of that was true except the load-bearing ones.

Okonkwo was watching me the way he'd watched the survey stakes. Reyes was watching me the way a man watches a scale that might be rigged. And Ulyana Ishikawa was watching the fire, feeding it one piece of driftwood at a time, and I understood suddenly that she was the dangerous one, because she wasn't watching me at all yet — she was waiting until I forgot she would be.

* * *

They fed me. I want to record that honestly, because everything else I told them that night was bent, but the rice and the dried fish were real, and I ate like an animal and hated myself and kept eating. Okonkwo gave me half his portion without comment. Reyes watched me eat with an expression I couldn't read until later, when I understood it was calculation: how long could this asset survive, what was she worth, could she walk.

"So," Reyes said, when I'd slowed down enough to talk. "Meridian. We were sent to find them. Radio silence for six weeks. The syndicate's concerned."

"The syndicate," I repeated.

"Funding partners," he amended smoothly.

"Funding partners don't send a fixer upriver in monsoon season." I wiped my bowl with two fingers. "They send hydrologists. They send pilots. Fixers come when they think something needs fixing."

"You don't know what I am."

"You check sightlines before you sit down. You sleep facing the water. You asked me three questions about the team and none of them were *are they alive*." I shrugged, and my shoulder cracked audibly, and everyone pretended not to hear. "I'm a surveyor. Reading terrain is the job. People are terrain."

That landed harder than I intended. Ulyana looked up from the fire.

"Are they?" she asked. "Alive."

And here is where I should tell you what happened to Meridian, or at least the shape of it, and here is where I did the thing I do — the thing that had kept me breathing through eleven days alone and eight months before that — I told the truth sideways.

"They went deeper into the valley," I said. "Voluntarily. After the basecamp burned, they decided the work mattered more than the risk. The glacier route opens at calving season. They wanted to be through the gorge before it closed again." I turned my bowl in my hands. "I disagreed. About the going. So I stayed."

"Why?" Ulyana asked.

"Because someone has to hold the way back open."

It was the truest sentence I'd said all night and it was still a lie, because nobody holds a way back open by hiding in driftwood eating snails. But Okonkwo nodded slowly, and Reyes wrote something in a waterproof notebook, and Ulyana put another stick on the fire, and the lie held its shape, the way a hull holds — until the water finds the rib you didn't caulk.

* * *

I slept badly, which was normal, but I slept badly near other humans for the first time in weeks, which changed the flavor of it. Around what I guessed was three in the morning I woke to a sound I knew too well: the click of a radio's transmit key, soft as a beetle in a wall.

Reyes sat apart from the embers, cupped around a handset, transmitting in bursts too short to carry words to me — numbers, probably, or code. Reporting. *Asset located. Asset cooperative. Team status per subject: proceeded upvalley voluntarily.* He'd write it exactly like that. Voluntarily. His handwriting would be beautiful.

I lay still and breathed through my mouth and thought about the glacier.

Here is what I could not say, not yet, not to these three: that *voluntarily* was the word Voss — no, that's my own name getting tangled; the expedition lead, a man named Corben, had used it in the last message any of us sent south. *Team proceeding upvalley voluntarily.* He'd said it while three of the five were too sick from the water to argue and the fourth had been bought with a promise about his daughter's school fees. Voluntary is a word that gets used on people the way brands get used on cattle. I had walked out rather than sign my name under it, and Corben had let me go, because a surveyor who objects is less useful than a surveyor gone quiet, and because — I believed this then, I need to believe it now — he thought the valley itself would decide the question. That the ice would make the argument I couldn't win in camp.

Eleven days of snails had not settled whether I'd abandoned my team or escaped them.

And here is the other thing I could not say: the syndicate hadn't sent a search party. It had sent a confirmation. Search parties hope. Confirmations verify. Reyes's radio was proof that somebody south already knew what answer they wanted, and my continued existence was a line item that could be closed either way.

So when dawn came up gray and dripping, and Ulyana handed me tea made from pine needles and asked, casually, looking at the river and not at me, "Which way did they go? Exactly," I gave her bearings.

Real ones. That was the strange mercy of my condition: I couldn't lie about coordinates. Numbers were the one language where my tongue wouldn't bend. I gave her the bearing up the valley, the elevation of the pass, the distance to the calving face, and I watched her translate them instantly into fuel and hours and weather margins, because she was that kind of pilot, the kind whose head is a flight computer wrapped in scar tissue.

"That's four days' travel by water," she said. "Five, with your boat's leak."

"It's not my boat."

"It's the boat we have." She looked at me then, finally, directly, and her eyes were the flat gray of the river before rain. "You're coming with us."

"I—"

"You know the route. You know where they were going. You're the only person on this river who can tell the difference between a survey cairn and a rock pile." She sipped her terrible tea. "Also, you lie badly, and I'd rather have you where I can hear it."

Heat climbed my neck. "I haven't lied to you."

"No," she agreed, mild as milk. "You haven't."

* * *

We broke camp in a drizzle that meant business. I watched the three of them work and took their measure the way I'd take a slope's: Reyes carried everything heavy and complained about nothing, which meant he was being paid very well or threatened very well. Okonkwo catalogued the river as we loaded — flow rate, turbidity, the color of the clay banks — muttering figures like prayers. And Ulyana ran the loading like a preflight, touching each crate, each drum, each lashing, her hands reading the boat the way mine read contour lines.

At the last moment she paused over my pack. It was falling apart. Everything I owned was in it: a broken theodolite, a tin of pencils, a waterproof notebook with most of its pages razored out, and a photograph I kept in the lining that I would have burned before showing anyone.

"What happened to your notes?" she asked.

"Fire," I said.

Some of them, anyway. The pages I'd cut out were sewn into the hem of my shirt, and they contained the aquifer survey — the real one, the one Corben's reports falsified, the reason the word *voluntarily* existed at all. Eleven days I'd slept on those pages. I'd stopped feeling them as paper. They were part of my body now, ribs inside ribs.

Ulyana looked at my ruined notebook, then at my shirt hem, which hung unevenly where I'd stitched it with fishing line, and I watched her see it — actually see it — and watch her decide, visibly, not to ask. Not yet. She tucked the information away next to my cracked lie about being alone, and my too-precise bearings, and whatever else she'd collected, a ledger of me that I could feel growing heavier by the hour.

"Get in," she said. "Bow. Call the water for me. There's deadfall below the bend that isn't on any chart."

"How do you know there's deadfall?"

"Because I've run this river since I was nine years old, and charts forget rivers." She stepped to the stern and took the yoke of the little outboard. "People too."

We pushed off into the current with the bruised sky pressing down and the leak ticking away under the floorboards like a slow clock. Ahead of us the valley narrowed toward the glacier, white and patient, holding everything I'd buried and everything I'd stolen and every person I'd failed to save.

*Voluntarily*, I thought, and put my hand in the cold spray, and called the first deadfall before it came.
