## 9. What Meridian Was Looking For

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the driftwood, and the pilot woman — Ishikawa, though she hadn't offered the name yet, Sable had read it off the plane's tail two days ago through binoculars and had been sitting on it like a stolen egg — was asleep with her back against a drum. The fixer, Reyes, slept like a man who expected to be woken badly. Only the hydrologist still watched her across the flames, patient as water finding its level.

"You said they went deeper," Okonkwo said quietly. "Voluntarily."

"I said that."

"Is it true?"

Sable turned her hands over the fire's warmth. Her fingers were still wrong — too thin, nails split, the surveyor's calluses gone soft from weeks of not holding anything but fish and river stones. She thought about lying again. She'd been lying for so long it had started to feel like navigation: you picked a heading, you held it, you didn't look down.

"It's true," she said. "That's the worst part. Nobody made them go. I'm the only one who walked."

* * *

Six months earlier, and the valley had been a different country.

Meridian had come upriver in three boats and a mood of cheerful secrecy. Twelve of them: drillers, geophysicists, a camp cook who sang hymns off-key, and Dr. Verrone, the expedition lead, who wore his idealism like a pressed shirt — crisp every morning no matter what the weather did to it. Sable had signed on as surveyor because the pay cleared her sister's medical debt in one season, and because the contract said *hydrological mapping, upper basin*, which sounded like science, which sounded clean.

The first month was honest work. They ran seismic lines along the glacier's toe. They dropped sensors into meltwater channels that rang like struck glass. Sable walked the moraines with a theodolite on her back and mapped the gorge walls where the ice pressed against stone, and at night she drew the valley in ink, and it was beautiful, and she believed in it.

The second month, the crates marked AGRI-EQUIPMENT arrived, and Verrone stopped letting anyone inventory them.

She remembered the exact evening the shape of the thing came clear to her. It was cold enough to see your breath indoors; the mess tent smelled of diesel and the cook's burnt rice. Verrone spread the new charts on the folding table and weighted the corners with mugs, and he was glowing, actually glowing, the way men glow when they think they've been chosen by history.

"Do you know what we're sitting on?" he asked the room.

"Aquifer," said Okafor, the senior driller. "We've known since week two."

"We've *confirmed* since week two." Verrone tapped the chart, and Sable saw what he was tapping: her own contour lines, the ones she'd drawn showing how the glacier's meltwater sank into the gravel beds under the valley floor and traveled — this was the part that had taken her breath away when the data first came in — traveled *downriver*, a slow underground river sixty kilometers long, feeding springs, feeding wells, feeding every settlement in the delta without anyone above ground ever knowing why the water kept coming.

"The syndicate's lawyers can file exclusive extraction rights before calving season opens the gorge," Verrone said. "Surface claims are contested. Subsurface claims, with a full survey? There's no precedent against it. We own the water under half the territory, and nobody downstream will even know their wells are drinking our property until the meters go in."

Nobody in the tent moved. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the glacier groaned — that long tectonic complaint it made at dusk, like a door in an enormous house.

Okafor said, "And the settlements?"

"What settlements?" Verrone smiled. "They're using it. We'll be *managing* it. That's a kindness, if you look at it right. Unmanaged water is wasted water."

Sable looked at the chart. Her lines. Her beautiful, careful lines, drawn at dawn with frozen fingers because she took pride in her work, because precision was the one form of honesty she'd never doubted. And now every contour was a fence post. Every elevation figure was a clause. She had spent six weeks building the cage, and she'd built it well, and Verrone was praising her for it.

"That's a remarkable survey," he said, meeting her eyes. "Best field work I've seen in twenty years."

"Thank you," Sable said, and hated herself with a completeness that felt almost restful, the way pain is restful once it stops pretending to be something else.

* * *

Here is what she learned in the weeks that followed, and what she could not stop learning, the way you can't stop learning the words to a song you hate:

That the data would be used exactly as Verrone said. That the syndicate's man — a quiet, courteous visitor who flew in twice by floatplane and called everyone by their first names — reviewed her maps each time and left tips for the cook. That the crates were drilling casings and pump housings, pre-positioned for the season after next. That two of the team had figured it out before her and decided the money was still the money. That the cook sang his hymns louder when he was uneasy, which meant he knew too, in the wordless way cooks know everything.

And that she stayed anyway. Three more weeks. She told herself she was gathering evidence. She told herself she needed to see the full extent of the aquifer before she could judge. She told herself her sister's debt had a face and a name and a hospital bill, and the aquifer did not.

Then the courier came upriver with the mail, and with it a syndicate memorandum that someone had left, deliberately or carelessly, in the map case. It was a schedule. Not for drilling — for *filing*. Dates, registry offices, the names of clerks already retained. And one line item that Sable read four times because the first three readings refused to be true:

*Phase II contingent upon seasonal access via gorge (est. open window 8–12 days). Contingency: acquisition of downstream surface rights at distressed valuation following Phase I metering.*

Distressed valuation. Meaning: turn off the free water, wait for the settlements to panic, buy their land cheap. Meaning: her contours weren't just fences. They were the instrument itself. The map was the weapon. She was the weapon.

That night she walked out of camp with a pack, a rifle she didn't take, and the one thing she did take: the master survey folio, her own working copy, because some animal part of her understood that whoever held the original data held the truth, and truth was about to become the scarcest resource in the valley.

Verrone sent two men after her. She heard them crashing around in the alder thickets below the moraine, calling her name in friendly voices, saying *Sable, come back, you're not thinking straight, the weather's turning.* The weather was always turning. That was the whole point of the valley. She climbed instead of running level, let them exhaust themselves on the scree, and by full dark she was alone on a ridge with the icefield silvering under the moon and the whole drowned country spread out below her like a secret she finally understood the price of.

She sat up there until sunrise. Not deciding — she'd already decided, somewhere back in the mess tent, maybe back at the first seismic line. Just grieving. Because she had loved the work. Because she had been good at it. Because there is a particular vertigo in discovering that your competence has a body count, that skill is morally neutral, that the same hand that draws a hospital can draw a trap, and the hand doesn't get to choose unless it chooses early.

She had not chosen early. She chose late. Late was what she had to live with.

* * *

"So you stole the survey," Okonkwo said, in the present, in the firelight. His voice carried no accusation. That was somehow worse than accusation.

"I took my own work. It was mine before it was theirs."

"And then you hid."

"I survived. Hiding was incidental." She fed a stick to the fire. "I've been living on the river since. Watching the gorge. Waiting for the window to close for good."

"Why not go south? Report it. Territorial bureau, newspapers, anything."

"Because the syndicate owns the registry clerk and half the newspapers, and because—" She stopped. The true answer sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Because I led them to it.* Because Verrone had needed a surveyor who could read the gravel beds, and she had been the best, and without her the expedition might have taken years, might have failed, might have—

"Because I'm the one who mapped it," she said instead. "If it gets filed, it's my signature on the chain of custody. I wanted it to stay lost. I wanted the whole valley to forget me and the data together."

Across the fire, the pilot shifted in her sleep and murmured something — a word, or half of one. Sable caught herself leaning toward the sound and made herself stop.

Okonkwo was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The data. Your copy. Do you still have it?"

"No." True. "It went into the river in the third week, with most of my kit, when the boat swamped at the falls." Also true. What she did not say — what she had buried so deep she sometimes lost it herself — was that the *originals*, the sealed cores and the field notebooks, the irreplaceable primary record, had never been hers to carry. Those had gone into a cache before she defected. A cliff fissure above the old drowned village site, sealed in wax and oilcloth, because she had not been able to destroy them and had not been able to keep them either. Evidence, waiting. A debt of her own, accruing interest in the dark.

She had told herself she'd hidden them so the syndicate couldn't file. She had told herself a great many things.

"They're still up there," Okonkwo said. Not a question. He was a hydrologist; he understood aquifers, and apparently also people — how things seeped, how pressure found its way through whatever you packed against it. "Aren't they."

Sable looked at him across the coals and felt the vertigo return, the old familiar tilt of the world, except now it wasn't six months ago and a tent full of complicit warmth. Now it was here, and the people on the other side of the fire were strangers, and one of them worked for the syndicate and was sleeping ten feet away, and the woman whose name she'd been carrying like contraband was dreaming in the cold with a brass compass somewhere in her kit that Sable had seen the outline of and not yet understood.

"If it is," she said carefully, "then everyone in this camp is standing on top of the reason Meridian disappeared. And the reason they'll send more."

Okonkwo nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed a reading he'd already taken.

Behind them, far up the dark throat of the valley, the glacier spoke again — that long, patient groan, ice shifting against ice, the sound of a clock the size of a mountain beginning, very slowly, to count.
