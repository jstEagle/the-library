## 10. Monsoon

**Ulyana**

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, like a tenant who had signed papers on the whole sky.

They had found the cave on the second day of the storm — a limestone mouth in the valley wall above the river, dry-floored, big enough for four people and their miseries. Ulyana had flown weather like this for eleven years and never once been inside it. From the air, monsoon was texture: gray curtains, updrafts like fists, the river going the color of old tin. From inside it, it was simply weight. It pressed on the cave entrance in a solid sheet, and after three days she stopped being able to imagine anything else.

She inventoried the food because inventory was something her hands knew how to do when her mind wanted to run.

"Say it again," Sable said from across the fire ring. She'd built the ring herself, out of river stones, with the competence of someone who had camped badly for a long time and gotten good at it.

"Twelve days of rice if we eat like monks," Ulyana said. "Eight if we eat like people. Six cans of that mystery meat Reyes swears is chicken. Tea for nine days. Salt. Half a jar of oil." She looked up. "And whatever's in the crates you two wouldn't let me open."

Okonkwo didn't look up from his notebook. He hadn't looked up much since the burned camp. He wrote in small, dense columns, numbers mostly, and when he spoke it was to the page. "The crates are instruments. You can open them if it matters."

"It doesn't matter."

"Then we won't."

Reyes sat nearest the entrance, which Ulyana had decided was either bravado or a man keeping an eye on the way out. He'd wedged his duffel between himself and the wall, and he slept with one arm through its strap, and he woke at every sound the rain made against the stone, which was all of them.

Four strangers in a hole in a mountain, waiting for the sky to lose interest. Ulyana had been alone in worse places. She had never been crowded in one before.

* * *

**Sable**

Sable counted the exits the way other people said prayers.

There were two: the cave mouth, and a chimney crack at the back that went up maybe forty meters to a ledge where nothing could live but moss and one stubborn stunted pine. She'd climbed it on the first afternoon, in a break between fronts, and stood on the ledge looking down at the river running high and brown and fast enough to swallow a boat whole. Their boat was still tied below, taking water on a schedule they couldn't argue with. Going anywhere by river now was a way of dying with extra steps.

So: two exits, neither good. She'd lived with worse arithmetic. She'd lived with worse for years.

What she hadn't lived with was being known. Or half-known. Ulyana watched her the way surveyors watch terrain — not hostile, just measuring, filing things away. Sable caught her at it constantly. Over the rice. Over the fire. Once, unbearably, over the moment Sable pulled her boots off and flexed her toes and made a sound she'd have paid money to take back.

"You were Meridian's surveyor," Ulyana said that evening, apropos of nothing, stirring the pot. "You read country for a living."

"I read it."

"So read this one. How long?"

Sable took her time answering, because the honest answer was a number she didn't want to own. "The front sitting on us now breaks tomorrow night. Then we get maybe thirty-six hours of thinner rain. Then the next system comes in behind it, bigger. After that—" she shrugged— "the monsoon settles in for weeks. We're not moving by boat until it does. Not safely."

"And by then?"

By then the calving season would be opening the gorge, and everything Sable had walked away from would be walking toward them. "By then we'll be very tired of rice," she said.

Ulyana's eyes stayed on her a beat too long. Sable held the look, because holding looks was the one lie she'd never learned to tell well — or rather, the one place lying showed. Her face gave things away. It always had. That was why she'd gotten so good at lying with everything else.

* * *

**Ulyana**

On the fifth day, the rice got shorter and the silences got longer, and Ulyana found the radio.

It wasn't a search. She was looking for the spare fuel filter she'd packed in the boat's kit, back when the boat was supposed to be a means and not a coffin, and instead her fingers found a shape wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Reyes's gear bag — no. Not his bag. The communal pile, where everyone's overflow had migrated as the days compressed them. That was what she told herself later. The bag was gray. All the bags were gray.

The oilcloth came away and there it was: a compact transceiver, syndicate issue, the kind Ansel's field men carried. Water-stained case, fresh batteries. A pencil stub and a folded sheet of paper covered in Reyes's cramped hand — groups of letters, five at a time, the kind of cipher a man memorizes in a training room and prays he never has to use.

She should have put it back.

She sat with it in her lap for a long time while the rain hammered its one note, and she thought about Mr. Ansel's courtesy, the soft voice on the phone, the contract signed in a hangar that smelled of kerosene and her father's ghost. *Fly the search party's gear.* She had flown the leash in herself. She had carried it in her own cargo hold, strapped down and tied off, and thanked the tower on the way out.

The paper had dates. Today's date was on it, top line, with a group of letters beside it.

She copied the letters into her head — she had a pilot's memory for strings of characters; frequencies, call signs, squawk codes, a career of numbers that kept you alive — and she wrapped the radio back in its cloth and put it exactly where it had been, smoothing the bag flap the way she'd found it.

Then she went and sat by the fire and let the knowledge sit in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

**Reyes**

Reyes transmitted at dawn, when the others slept deepest and the rain made the most noise.

He'd found the rhythm of it by the third day. The cave mouth faced north, and the transmission window Ansel's people had given him was short — ninety seconds, low power, burst-coded, aimed at a relay station downriver that might or might not be listening through this weather. He crouched at the back of the chimney crack with the antenna snaked up toward the ledge, and he sent his groups, and he packed the set away, and his hands shook less each time, which he chose to read as competence rather than conscience wearing out.

The message was simple. *Party alive. Sheltered. Subject Sable alive, cooperative. Moving when weather permits. Estimate exit via gorge at calving.*

He left out the part about the burned camp. He left out the lifeboat. He left out the way Okonkwo looked at him sometimes, mild and terrible, like a man doing long division on your soul. And he left out Ulyana Ishikawa entirely, except as *pilot present*, because Ansel had asked about her twice on the pre-mission brief and twice was enough for a man like Reyes to understand that the pilot was not cargo.

He was loyal to the syndicate the way a man is loyal to a roof in a storm. It was over his head. It kept the rain off. Nobody asked whether he loved it.

But crouched there in the wet dark with the key under his thumb, he thought — briefly, unwelcome — about the pilot's face when she'd signed the contract. About how she'd looked at the debt ledger like it was a diagnosis. About his own daughter, in a city he hadn't seen in two years, who thought he worked in logistics.

Logistics. Well. Wasn't this logistics? Things moving from one place to another. Information. People.

He sent the burst. He packed the radio. He went back down into the cave and ate his ration of rice and lied with his face, which was the only kind of lying he was any good at, and even that was getting harder in the damp.

* * *

**Sable**

The dreams started on the sixth night.

In them she was back in the Meridian tent, and Dr. Haas was spreading the aquifer charts on the table, and everyone was smiling the way people smile when they think they've found a vein of gold, and Sable was smiling too, smiling and nodding and saying nothing, saying nothing for eleven more days, and in the dream she could feel those days accumulating in her body like silt. Eleven days of knowing. Eleven days of eating Meridian's food and taking Meridian's pay and watching the data get organized into a claim filing that would put a gate and a meter on the only fresh water between here and the coast.

She woke with her heart going and the fire down to coals and Ulyana awake across the cave, watching her. Of course awake. The woman slept like a pilot on standby — lightly, ready, one ear on the engines.

"Bad dream?" Ulyana asked. Quiet. Not mocking.

"Inventory," Sable said. "I dream in inventory sometimes. Lists of things I should have done."

"What was on the list?"

Sable looked at the coals. The honest answer was *telling someone sooner*. Burning the data myself. Walking out of the tent on day one instead of day twelve. Warning the settlements downriver whose names she'd never learned because she'd never bothered to learn them, because she'd told herself the work was just work, the map was just a map, and maps don't hurt anyone — people do, and she wasn't a person, she was an instrument, and instruments aren't responsible for what gets built with them.

"A lot of rice," she said. "I dreamed we were out of rice."

Ulyana didn't smile. "We're not out yet."

"No," Sable agreed. "Not yet."

It was the closest they'd come to talking about it — the thing that sat between them like a third passenger, the thing Sable had walked out of the valley carrying and Ulyana had flown in without knowing. Some nights Sable almost told her. Almost. But telling Ulyana meant telling her about the cache site, and the cache site meant the duress, and the duress meant admitting that Meridian had partial data already, that her defection had come with a receipt, that she'd handed the syndicate half the map before she'd grown the spine to leave.

Two liars in a cave. The rain approved of them both.

* * *

**Ulyana**

On the eighth day the rations forced a decision, and the decision forced a meeting, and the meeting showed Ulyana exactly how thin the ice under all of them had gotten.

"We go down to the boat tomorrow," she said. "Salvage what we can, haul the rest up here. If the hull's going to sink anyway, I'd rather it sink empty."

"The river's up two meters since we landed," Okonkwo said. "The bank trail may be gone."

"Then we find out from the top, not the bottom."

Reyes was quiet a moment too long. "I'll stay with the camp," he said. "Somebody holds the site."

"Nobody's going to steal our rice, Reyes."

"Somebody should hold the site," he repeated, and smiled his logistics smile, and Ulyana felt the swallowed stone turn over in her chest.

That night she lay listening to the rain and ran the cipher groups in her head against everything she knew. She wasn't a cryptographer. But she'd grown up around pilots' shorthand, and her father had taught her one rule above all others: *when a man tells you where he'll be, believe him, and ask why he needed to say it.* Reyes volunteering to hold the camp was a man telling her where he'd be. The letters in the notebook had today's date beside them. Dawn transmissions needed dawn privacy. Everything fit with the ugly neatness of a load properly balanced.

She didn't confront him. Not yet. Confrontation was a commodity and she had exactly one unit of it, and she intended to spend it when she understood what the messages said — or better, when she could prove it, because a man like Reyes would deny a suspicion and destroy a certainty.

Instead she lay in the dark and listened to him breathe near the entrance, steady, untroubled, a man sleeping under a roof he trusted.

The rain kept its one note. Somewhere above the valley, under all that gray, a glacier was grinding toward the sea, patient as arithmetic, and when it calved it would open the gorge, and whatever came up that gorge would come for them.

She closed her eyes and saw the brass compass on her father's dashboard, needle steady, pointing at nothing she'd been willing to name.

Tomorrow she'd salvage the boat. Tomorrow night, if the front broke the way Sable promised, she'd climb the chimney to the ledge and look south and count the hours. And the morning after that, when Reyes crouched at the back of the crack with his antenna and his ninety seconds, she would be standing behind him in the wet dark, and the cave would get very small indeed.

The rain hammered on. Trust, she thought, erodes faster than limestone. She'd watched it happen to whole valleys.

She watched it happen in the dark, drip by drip, and said nothing, and kept the stone.
