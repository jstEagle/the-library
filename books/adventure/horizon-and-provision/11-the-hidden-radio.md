## 11. The Hidden Radio

The rain found the cave's throat at midnight and began to speak in it.

Ulyana lay awake listening to the acoustics of water — the way the overhang turned the downpour into a low organ note that filled the whole chamber, the way drips from a seam in the ceiling kept their own time, patient as a metronome in an empty practice room. Okonkwo slept with his back to the fire pit, arms crossed over his chest like a man conserving heat and dignity in equal measure. Sable had taken the outer ledge, where the wind came through, because she said she couldn't sleep without weather on her face.

Reyes slept closest to the packs.

She'd noticed it three days ago and told herself it meant nothing. A man guarding his own gear was just a man with few possessions. But Reyes owned almost nothing — a change of clothes gone soft with damp, a folding knife, a tin of tobacco he rationed like medicine — and still he arranged himself each night between the fire and the duffels they wouldn't let her open at the airstrip, weeks ago now, in another country of dry air and clear sky.

Tonight he'd done something new. He'd moved the packs.

Not far. A meter, maybe less, shifting them toward the wall behind his bedroll, into the shadow of a fallen slab. Anyone not watching would have called it tidying. Ulyana had spent eleven years reading passengers by how they sat in turbulence, and she knew what tidying looked like, and this wasn't it. This was a man building himself a small country with defensible borders.

She waited until his breathing went long and even. Then she got up.

* * *

The cave floor was cold through her socks. She moved along the wall where the stone was dry, past the fire pit's banked coals breathing orange under ash, past Okonkwo's motionless shape. At the packs she knelt and put her hands on the canvas and simply held them for a moment, feeling foolish, feeling certain.

The first two were what they'd always been: rope, tarpaulin, the expedition's survey crates with syndicate stenciling she'd hauled upriver without ever being allowed to inventory. The third was heavier than its size. She unzipped it by inches, working the teeth slow so the sound stayed under the rain.

Blankets. A medical kit she'd packed herself. And beneath the blankets, wrapped in oilcloth, a shape with edges.

She lifted it out. It had the density of something built to survive being dropped, and when she peeled the cloth back far enough, the firelight caught a dial face, a frequency scale, a stub antenna folded flat against a casing scuffed gray-green. Military surplus, or something dressed to look like it. A radio. Not the expedition set they'd all mourned at the burned basecamp — a second one, carried secretly from the beginning, through her cabin, past her cockpit, upriver in her hold.

Her hands went very still around it.

There was a protocol for this, she thought distantly. There was probably a whole etiquette. You woke the others. You laid it out in the light. You asked questions whose answers you already had. But what she actually did was turn the thing over in her lap looking for the tell — the modification, the extra band, whatever made it worth hiding — and found instead a notebook, small, rubber-banded, tucked into the oilcloth fold.

She shouldn't have opened it. She opened it.

Columns of figures. Dates going back to the airstrip, to before the airstrip, to the charter negotiation she'd watched Ansel conduct across his desk with such unbearable courtesy. Times paired with frequencies. Phrases she didn't understand — *package stable*, *route nominal*, *asset cooperative* — each initialed R. And near the end, more recent, the handwriting tightening:

*Subject S — location confirmed within 4 km of grid 77. Will confirm closer when weather lifts.*

*Ansel wants confirmation of status. Alive preferred. Not required.*

*Not required.*

Ulyana read the phrase four times. The rain filled the silence the way water fills a hull — steadily, from below, while everyone above deck keeps talking about the view.

Alive preferred. Not required.

She looked across the fire at Reyes, asleep on his side with one hand curled near his chest, and felt something in her chest perform a maneuver she recognized from bad flying: not panic exactly, but the moment the instruments stop agreeing with each other and you understand that one of them has been lying to you since takeoff, and you have to decide which one, right now, in cloud, with no horizon.

She'd flown him here. She'd signed the contract, loaded the crates, argued about fuel weights and never once about cargo manifests, because Ansel had smiled at her and called her *the most reliable operator on this river* and she had been so hungry for the word reliable that she hadn't asked reliable for whom.

She flew the leash in herself. She'd carried it in her own hold, fueled it with her own hands, and every kilometer of river between here and the delta was a length of line paid out from her own drum.

The needle steadied. Cold clarity, her father used to call it — the state after fear burns off, when the situation stops being a story about you and becomes a problem with inputs. She'd lived inside that state for years. She knew its temperature.

She closed the notebook. She stood, radio in both arms, and said, quietly, in the voice she used for passengers who needed to hear something once and only once:

"Reyes."

Nothing. Then his breathing changed — a hitch, a fraction too fast — and she understood he'd been awake the whole time, or nearly, the way men sleep who sleep beside secrets.

"Reyes." Louder now. "Get up. Bring your conscience if you can find it."

* * *

He came up off the bedroll fast, hand going to the knife, then stopping when he saw what she was holding and how she was holding it — out from her body, away from the firelight, the way you carry something that could be dropped or thrown.

Okonkwo was sitting up already, glasses on, saying nothing, watching with the terrible patience of a man who took measurements for a living. From the ledge, Sable's voice came down through the rain-hum: "What is it?"

"A radio," Ulyana said. "One we weren't told about. One I carried." She set it on the flat slab between the fire and the packs, deliberate as landing gear touching down. "And a logbook of everything it's been used to say."

Sable came off the ledge in three movements, silent even on wet stone, and stopped when she saw the notebook. Something crossed her face that wasn't surprise. That was the worst part, Ulyana thought later — how little surprise there was anywhere in the cave. Everyone had known a piece of it. Nobody had assembled the whole.

"Read it aloud," Sable said. Her voice was level. Her hands weren't; they'd closed into fists at her sides, white-knuckled, and she seemed to notice and force them open finger by finger.

"No," Reyes said.

Everyone looked at him. He was standing now, barefoot on the cold rock, hair flattened on one side, and he looked smaller than he had at the airstrip, smaller than he had on the boat, smaller than he had any right to look given what he'd been carrying. But his chin was up. Whatever else Reyes was, he wasn't a man who denied things well, and some stubborn professional pride in the performance of honesty seemed to be holding him upright.

"I'll read it myself, then," Ulyana said, and picked up the notebook, and read.

She read the dates. She read the frequencies. She read *package stable* and *route nominal* and let the phrases sit in the cave air like specimens. And then she reached the last pages and read, slowly, giving each word its full weight: "*Subject S — location confirmed within four kilometers. Will confirm closer when weather lifts.*"

Sable didn't move. Rain ran somewhere far back in the cave, a thin silver thread of sound.

"*Ansel wants confirmation of status,*" Ulyana read. "*Alive preferred.*"

She stopped. Looked up. Made herself say the rest to Reyes's face rather than to the page.

"*Not required.*"

The words came out flat, stripped of everything, and in the quiet after them the rain seemed suddenly enormous, the whole mountain talking over them.

"Say it isn't mine," Sable said. "Say Ansel wrote that line himself and you just transcribed it. Say anything, Reyes. Lie to me. You're good enough at it that I might believe you."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Then at Ulyana. Then at the radio, as if it might intervene.

"It's mine," he said. "The handwriting. The transmissions. All of it." He dragged a hand down his face. "You want the rest? Fine. The rest is short. Ansel hired me before Meridian ever launched. My job wasn't logistics. Logistics was my cover. My job was to keep track of the survey team and report position weekly, and when Sable walked out—" he gestured at her, an ugly little flick of the fingers— "my job became finding her. Confirming location. Confirming status."

"Status," Sable repeated.

"Whether you were alive." His jaw worked. "Preferred alive, because a live defector can be negotiated with, tried, made an example of. Dead is messier. Dead raises questions." He shrugged, and the shrug was the most honest thing he'd done, because it was helpless. "But Ansel doesn't spend money on messy if clean is cheaper. If you'd been in the way — really in the way, holding data he needed buried — the order would have changed. We both know it. I know it. I've known it since the day I took the job."

"And you took it anyway," Ulyana said.

"I take jobs," Reyes said. "That's what I am. I'm the man who takes jobs." He laughed, short and joyless. "You think I'm the monster in this? I'm the leash. Somebody else holds it."

"You're the leash I flew in," Ulyana said.

It came out of her before she'd decided to say it, and once it was out she couldn't take it back and didn't want to. She heard her own voice shake on the second half and hated that, and let it shake anyway.

"I signed his paper. I loaded your crates. I sat in that cockpit and did preflight on a hold full of spy gear and a hunt warrant and I never once opened a zipper, because a man was polite to me and called me reliable, and I wanted—" She stopped. Breathed. The cold clarity held, barely, like ice holding a man's weight. "I wanted the debt to keep being manageable. That's all. Manageable. I've been paying on my father's ghost for eleven years and Ansel lets me pay in installments of obedience and I thanked him for it. I flew the instrument of my own captivity upriver and called it work."

Nobody spoke. Okonkwo took his glasses off, cleaned them on his shirt with great care, and put them back on, which Ulyana had learned was his version of shouting.

"For the record," Okonkwo said mildly, "I also suspected the third crate. Weight was wrong for blankets. I assumed money."

"Money," Reyes said, "would have been better."

"How long," Sable said. Her voice had gone very quiet, which Ulyana was learning was worse than loud. "How long have you known where I am?"

"Since the driftwood camp. Since the night I hailed you." Reyes met her eyes and, to his credit, didn't flinch. "Four kilometers was generous. It was two hundred meters. I could have walked down there in ten minutes."

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because confirming and delivering are different jobs." He said it fast, like pulling a tooth. "Because transmission range from the camp was marginal and I wanted a better fix. Because—" he faltered, and Ulyana watched him reach for the professional answer and find it empty, and stand there holding nothing. "Because you gave me soup," he said finally. "At the camp. You were starving and you split your soup with me and I sat there holding a transmitter in my pack and I couldn't—" He cut himself off. Pressed his lips together. "The last entry says *will confirm closer when weather lifts*. The weather hasn't lifted. I haven't transmitted in nine days. That's the truth. Make of it what you want."

Ulyana made of it what she wanted, which was nothing yet. Nine days of silence could be a conscience. It could also be a man waiting for a better position from which to sell. She'd flown enough passengers to know that people were rarely one thing, but they were usually one thing *when it counted*, and the counting hadn't started.

"What happens now?" Okonkwo asked. Practical as ever. "Options. Let's hear options."

"He doesn't transmit again," Sable said immediately. "We take the radio apart. We take the batteries. When the weather breaks, he walks out with us or he doesn't walk out."

"That's not an option," Reyes said. "That's a hostage scenario with worse food."

"It's an option."

"It's a delay. You understand that, don't you?" For the first time Reyes sounded genuinely urgent, and Ulyana watched him choose, visibly, to spend his remaining credibility on a warning. "You break the radio, Ansel goes quiet on his end. Quiet makes him nervous. Nervous makes him move. There are boats on this river with engines we can't outrun and men who get paid by the result. The only reason none of that has come upriver yet is that my reports have kept saying *everything normal, everything on schedule*. Kill the signal and you kill the one thing keeping the dogs home."

"He's right," Ulyana said.

They all looked at her. She was surprised at herself too, but the logic was already running, smooth and cold, the way engine math runs when you're in trouble and can't afford to feel anything.

"Think about it like weather," she said. "Right now there's a system parked over us and we're in its eye because Reyes's reports are the pressure gradient. Destroy the station and the gradient collapses. Everything moves." She turned to Reyes. "So here's what happens instead. You transmit. On schedule. Same format, same frequencies, same initials. And every word of it gets approved by this cave first. You become our transmitter. Our channel. Ansel thinks he's reading his asset; he's reading us."

Silence. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"You want me to double agent," Reyes said.

"I want you to do the job you've already been doing," Ulyana said, "except for the person who's actually holding the leash. That's the offer. Here's the alternative." She nudged the radio with her toe, gently. "Sable's option. Disassembly. And when the gunboat comes — and it'll come, Reyes, you know the arithmetic better than any of us — you're standing next to us when it does, with no signal and no value and no soup."

Reyes looked at the radio. At Sable, whose expression offered him nothing at all. At Okonkwo, whose expression offered him a kind of clinical pity that was somehow worse. Last, longest, at Ulyana.

"You'd trust my transmissions," he said. "After this."

"No," Ulyana said. "I'd verify them. There's a difference, and the difference is that I check my instruments now. Every flight, every gauge, every reading. I learned that today, and the lesson cost me the last comfortable thing I believed about myself, so forgive me if I don't hand the controls straight back." She crouched, pulled the batteries from the casing, and set them on the slab in plain view, separate, like evidence. "You'll transmit tomorrow at your scheduled window. In front of all of us. Text approved beforehand. Afterward the power cells stay with me, and the set stays disassembled between windows. You'll hate it. Good. Hate is honest. Trust me to build around honest."

Reyes exhaled through his nose. Somewhere in it was a laugh.

"And what do I tell him?" he asked. "Tomorrow's report. What's the content?"

Ulyana looked at Sable. Sable looked back, and for a moment the two of them hung on the same hook, defector and debt-pilot, liar and liar, each holding a piece of the truth the other needed.

"We tell him the truth," Sable said slowly, and a strange light was coming into her face, the light of a surveyor seeing a route nobody had mapped. "Everything normal. Everything on schedule. Subject located. Subject cooperative." Her mouth curved, and it wasn't quite a smile. "Let Ansel believe his leash is working. Leashes make people lazy. And while he's lazy—"

"—we find out what my father left in those flight logs," Ulyana finished.

She said it and felt the sentence land in the cave like a dropped tool, ringing. She hadn't meant to say it yet. But it was true, and it was hers, and the compass in her jacket pocket pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, its brass case warm from her body, its needle pointing — it was always pointing — at something under the mountains that the syndicate would burn the river country to own.

Okonkwo was watching her with that measuring look. "Your father," he said carefully, "has been dead eleven years."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "And he's still the best pilot this river ever produced, and Ansel is afraid of his paperwork. Tomorrow we start finding out why."

Outside, the rain kept falling on the drowned valley, steady as a telegraph, sending a message none of them could read yet — except that Reyes, gathering his bedroll with the defeated grace of a man reassigned rather than forgiven, glanced at the storm-dark mouth of the cave and said, mostly to himself:

"She's going to hate what's in those logs."

Ulyana picked up the batteries and held them against her chest, cold as river stones.

"Then she'll finally know how I feel," she said, "about everything I've been carrying."
