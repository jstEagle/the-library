## 12. Okonkwo's Maps

The rain had a rhythm by the fourth day in the cave camp, and Okonkwo had learned to work inside it — not against it, but inside it, the way you learned to work inside any large indifferent system. The monsoon was a system. The syndicate was a system. Grief, he had found, was also a system, with its own maintenance schedule and its own failure modes.

He spread his maps on the flattest stone he could find, weighted the corners with four river stones and a tin of tea leaves, and waited for Ulyana to notice.

She was at the cave mouth, mending something — she was always mending something, a strap, a net, a seam in the world's fabric that only she could see. Her hands moved with the economy of a woman who had decided long ago that wasted motion was a form of debt. He had watched her for eleven days now, ever since Meridian's burned basecamp, ever since Sable came out of the treeline like something the river had coughed up, and he had formed his assessment the way he formed all his assessments: slowly, on paper, in pencil, with room for revision.

Ulyana Ishikawa. Pilot. Debtor. Daughter of a man whose name appeared in three of Okonkwo's source documents under two different spellings.

She noticed the maps because she noticed everything; her eyes flicked from the cave mouth to the stone and back twice before she let herself look properly. Curiosity, in her, seemed to be a thing she rationed.

"What are those?" she asked, though she already knew they were maps. The question was really *why are you showing me*.

"Come sit," Okonkwo said. "Your feet will thank you. Mine have filed a formal complaint."

She didn't smile — he'd catalogued perhaps four smiles total, all small, all quickly retired — but she came and sat cross-legged on the dry side of the stone, tucking her hands into her armpits against the damp cold. Outside, the rain kept its rhythm on the rocks, a sound like static, like a radio tuned between stations.

"Reyes is asleep?" she asked.

"Snoring. He snores like a man defending himself."

That earned him one of the four smiles. It vanished fast.

Okonkwo turned the top map over. He'd drawn it himself, in the field, over three seasons — the kind of map no one commissions, which meant it was the kind of map no one pays you to falsify. It showed the upper river watershed in blue ink: the glacier at the head of the valley, the meltwater channels braiding down through the gorge, and then — this was the part he needed her to see — lines continuing past the gorge, past the drowned village sites, past every bend where the syndicate's surveys simply stopped, all the way down to the delta.

"Meridian wasn't looking for minerals," he said. "You've probably worked that out."

"Sable says water."

"Sable is right, and I can tell you how right." He tapped the glacier. "This ice feeds an aquifer. Not a river — an aquifer. A gravel bed under the valley floor, saturated, pressurized, holding maybe forty years of meltwater even if the glacier vanished tomorrow. It discharges through springs along the lower river. Every settlement in the delta drinks from it. Every field. Every fish run depends on the temperature of the spring-fed channels." He drew his finger down the blue lines. "It is, functionally, the region's only fresh water that doesn't turn to soup every monsoon."

Ulyana looked at the map the way she looked at weather — reading it, not admiring it. "And the syndicate wants to own it."

"The syndicate wants to *gate* it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole story." Okonkwo pulled the second sheet from beneath the first. This one was older, photocopied, its edges soft from handling. A survey abstract. A corporate letterhead he recognized the way you recognize a predator's tracks. "If they file exclusive claims on the recharge zone — the glacier valley, the gorge, the upper aquifer — they don't have to pump a single liter to control everyone downstream. They just have to own the ground the water comes through. Then every settlement negotiates. Or doesn't get water."

"They'd hold a gun made of thirst."

"Yes." He said it plainly because there was no other way to say it. "I've spent nine years mapping discharge rates in deltas. I've seen what happens when upstream interests close a tap. It isn't dramatic. That's what people don't understand. It's not a war, it's a price list. People don't die in battles. They die in ledgers."

He watched that word land. *Ledgers.* Ulyana's jaw did something small and controlled, a muscle moving once beneath the skin, and he understood he had chosen the correct word for this particular audience.

"How many people downstream?" she asked.

"Counting the delta settlements, the fishing camps, the seasonal villages — call it eleven thousand in a bad year. More when the sardine runs bring people up from the coast." He paused. "Your med runs. Where do you deliver?"

Her stillness changed quality. "Twelve landings," she said. "Thirteen counting the mission at Kessu Point."

"All of them on this map?"

She leaned forward despite herself, traced the delta with one finger, and he watched her find each of her landing strips on his ink lines — watched her realize that the geography of her debt and the geography of the aquifer were the same geography, had always been the same geography, that she had been flying supply lines over the body of the thing the syndicate intended to capture.

"That's why Ansel needs Meridian's data before calving season," she said. Her voice had gone flat, pilot-flat, the voice she used for turbulence. "The route through the gorge opens when the glacier calves. It's the only window to survey the recharge zone on the ground. File the claims while the window's open, and by the time anyone downstream understands what happened—"

"—the paperwork is law. Yes." Okonkwo sat back. His knees ached; he was fifty-four and the cave floor was not designed for fifty-four. "Which brings me to the part I've been rehearsing for two days, and I'll thank you to let me finish the rehearsal, because I'm better at data than persuasion."

She folded her arms. Waiting. He liked that about her too — she didn't fill silences with noise.

"I know about your father's debt," he said.

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere deeper in the cave, Reyes rolled over and muttered at a dream.

"Not the number," Okonkwo continued. "The structure. I spent six years in territorial archives before I went into hydrology — I was going to be a lawyer, God help everyone, and I failed out of it because I kept wanting to introduce evidence instead of arguments. But I learned to read contracts. And the instrument the syndicate holds against your family — I've seen its shape before. It's a standard production-debt conversion. Cargo advances capitalized at punitive interest, secured against equipment, callable at the holder's discretion." He met her eyes. "It's designed never to be paid off. It's designed to be a leash. Your father wasn't a debtor, Ms. Ishikawa. He was infrastructure."

Something moved behind her face — he thought of water finding a new channel, sudden and subterranean. "You don't know that."

"I know that Ansel has used the same instrument four times that I can document. River pilots, mostly. People who move freely through country the syndicate can't easily watch. You don't leash a man who owes you money. You leash a man who sees things." He tapped the burned-basecamp direction, upriver, though the basecamp was far away now behind rain and ridge. "Meridian's team saw things. Ask Sable why she really walked. And your father flew this river for twenty years. Whatever he saw, the debt was how they kept him flying it anyway."

"You're guessing."

"I'm a scientist. Guessing is the polite word for hypothesis, and hypotheses are testable." He turned the third sheet. This one he had been saving. It was his own work, recent, done in the first days of the expedition before the burning — discharge measurements from the spring line, matched against settlement census data he'd collected on earlier trips, cross-referenced with the syndicate's public filings. Three columns of figures. He'd written a fourth column last night by lamplight, and the fourth column was the one that mattered: projected household water access under syndicate gate scenarios, year one, year five, year ten.

He turned it so she could read it upside down, which she did, fluently, because pilots read instruments upside down and sideways and in the dark.

"Eleven thousand people," he said. "Year five, under full gate: sixty percent lose reliable dry-season access. Year ten—" he stopped. The numbers were the numbers. He didn't perform them.

Ulyana read for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was quiet. "My medicine goes to those landings. Half my cargo is antibiotics and rehydration salts. You know what cholera does to a settlement without clean water."

"I know exactly what it does. I've published on it."

"So this isn't abstract for you either."

"No," Okonkwo said. "Nothing about this is abstract for anyone in this cave except possibly Reyes, and Reyes's abstraction is a survival strategy, and I almost respect it."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, immediately suppressed, as if laughter were also a debt she was trying to service. Then she looked at the maps again, and he watched her do the arithmetic he had brought her here to do. Not the numbers on the page. The other arithmetic. The one where the columns were *debt* and *people*, and the exchange rate between them was the only question left.

"If I fly out when the weather breaks," she said slowly, "Ansel wins. Meridian's data gets confirmed, the claims get filed, and I go back to running cargo over a captive river until I die or the debt eats me."

"If you fly out, yes."

"And if I stay, I'm in breach of a contract signed under duress, my plane sits at their strip getting rained on, and the debt comes due against equipment I can't defend." She exhaled through her nose. "Neither column balances."

"No," Okonkwo agreed. "They don't. That's what makes it a real decision instead of a calculation. If one column balanced, you wouldn't be choosing anything." He gathered the corner stones off the maps, one by one, deliberately, giving her time. "But here is what I actually came to say, and then I'll let you sleep, or not sleep, whichever this night intends. I'm not asking you to fight them. I'm not asking you to be brave — bravery is overrated and usually fatal. I'm asking you for something smaller and harder."

"Which is?"

"Don't fly out. Even if the sky opens tomorrow. Even if the window comes. Don't carry Ansel's confirmation upriver, and don't carry yourself back down to him. Just — be absent. Be unfindable. Weather happens. Planes fail. Pilots get lost. You of all people know how a rumor works; become one." He held her gaze. "Every day this data stays unconfirmed is a day eleven thousand people keep drinking. That's all. That's the ask. Not heroism. Absence."

Ulyana was silent for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, then eased, then thickened again, breathing.

"You think I can just disappear," she said finally. "You've seen what he holds."

"I've seen paper," Okonkwo said. "Paper is powerful. But I've also seen what happens when enough people stop agreeing to be governed by a piece of paper. It's called history, and it's the only force larger than hydrology that I know of." He began rolling the maps, careful, square, the way he did everything. "Besides. Your father left something in this valley besides a debt. I've read enough fragments in the archives to believe that. And I don't think a man hides evidence for twenty years intending his daughter to spend her life paying for the privilege of not finding it."

That landed somewhere deep — he saw it hit, saw her decide not to show how hard. She stood, brushed cave grit from her knees, and looked toward the cave mouth where the rain came down silver in the last grey light.

"Sable knows more than she's told us," she said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't agreement. It was a heading. A bearing.

"I suspect everyone here knows more than they've told us," Okonkwo said mildly. "Including me. Including you. That's what makes a crew."

From the back of the cave, Reyes snorted awake, sat up, and said groggily, "What crew? Nobody said anything about a crew," and the moment broke the way moments do, irreversibly, into whatever came next.

Ulyana looked at Okonkwo one last time. "Show me the spring-line figures again," she said. "All of them. Slowly."

He smiled, unrolled the maps, and began — and neither of them noticed, until much later, the shape of Sable standing silent at the cave's dark inner edge, listening, with wet footprints leading away from a place no one had seen her come from.

* * *

That night Okonkwo couldn't sleep, and around the hour when the rain briefly stopped altogether — a silence so complete it felt structural, like the mountain holding its breath — he heard low voices at the cave mouth. Ulyana's, and Sable's, too quiet to parse, going on and on in the dark like two instruments tuning toward the same note.

He lay on his back and did what he always did with unsolvable systems: he broke them into parts, labeled the parts, and waited for the parts to tell him something true.

Part one: the aquifer, mapped, real, eleven thousand lives hanging off its discharge curve. Part two: the syndicate, patient as geology, already filing its intentions in triplicate. Part three: a pilot who read his projections upside down and asked to see the numbers twice. Part four: a surveyor who lied badly about small things and perfectly about large ones, and whose lies, he was increasingly certain, were load-bearing.

And part five, unlabeled, the variable he couldn't yet quantify: whatever her father had hidden in this valley, and whether it would surface before the ice opened and the gunboats — because there would be gunboats; institutions like Ansel's did not send surveyors to do what soldiers were priced for — came upriver to collect.

The silence ended. The rain resumed its rhythm, and Okonkwo slept at last, and dreamed, as he often did, of water finding its level.
