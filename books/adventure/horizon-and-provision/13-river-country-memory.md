## 13. River Country Memory

The river gave the village back to them on the third day of falling water.

Sable saw it first from the bow — a dark geometry under the surface, too straight to be geology. She called out and Ulyana brought the raft of salvaged drums alongside a shelf of shingle that hadn't existed a month ago, and there it was: rooflines. A whole street of them, standing in two feet of clear, cold water like a congregation waiting for a service that had been cancelled forty years ago.

"The dam," Sable said. "When it failed."

Ulyana said nothing. Okonkwo stood at the edge with his notebook out, already sketching, already measuring, because that was how he metabolized wonder — into data. But Ulyana just looked, and Sable watched her look, and understood that this woman was seeing something older than water here. Something personal she hadn't named yet.

They made camp on the high bank above the drowned street, where a stand of alders had kept the ground dry enough for a fire. The monsoon had thinned to a steady grey drizzle, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang, and for the first time in days the air felt breathable. Sable got a line in the water off the point and pulled three fat river char before the light went. Protein changed the mood of a camp faster than any speech. Even Reyes, marooned and sullen at the far end of the fire, ate what he was given without commentary.

It was after dark, with the fire down to coals and the drowned village a black suggestion below them, that Ulyana asked.

"You've been up here before."

Not a question. Sable turned her char on its stick. "Twice. Once with Meridian, once before that, when I was younger and dumber and working for a mapping outfit that paid by the kilometer." She nodded toward the water. "That's Kesh-Tara down there. Or was."

"Kesh-Tara," Ulyana repeated. The name sat strangely in her mouth, like a word from a language she'd stopped speaking on purpose.

"Four hundred people, give or take. Fishers, mostly, and gardeners on the terraces above. They'd run this stretch of river since before anyone thought to write it down." Sable pointed with her chin at a shape breaking the surface fifty meters out — a leaning post crowned with tangled net. "That was the drying rack for the whole village. Salmon season, you could smell it a kilometer downstream. Every family had its own rack, but that one belonged to the river itself. First catch went on it, every year, no matter who caught it. For the water."

"For the water," Okonkwo murmured, not looking up from his notebook.

"They had a whole relationship with it. Not ownership — they'd have laughed at the idea. Stewardship, maybe, if you want an English word, but even that's too legal. The river fed them; they kept its memory. That was the arrangement." Sable let the char skin crackle over the coals. "There were keepers. Old women, usually, who memorized the flood years, the lean years, which channels shifted after which winter. Living archives. When the syndicate's surveyors came through thirty years ago asking about water, the keepers told them exactly what they tell everyone: the mountain holds more than it shows. There's a lake under the glacier that never freezes through, and it feeds everything from here to the delta. The surveyors wrote it all down as folklore."

"And then built their numbers on it anyway," Okonkwo said quietly.

"Of course they did. You don't file a claim on folklore. But you can fly a hundred passes over a valley and call it your own data." She shrugged. "The dam was supposed to be small. Run-of-river, they said. Power for the mines. The keepers warned them about the spring melt — told them the gorge chokes with ice every calving season and the backwater would drown the terraces. The engineers had models. Models don't listen to old women."

She paused. Below them, the current moved through the drowned street with a sound almost like breathing.

"When it failed, it took eleven minutes to empty. Eleven minutes. The wave came down this reach at — I don't know, forty kilometers an hour, maybe faster. Kesh-Tara didn't drown slowly. It drowned all at once. Most of the people were downstream at the fish camps when it came, which is the only reason anyone survived. They came back upriver and found their houses under four meters of green water, and the syndicate's lawyers found them before the grief did. Waivers. Settlements. Clauses nobody could read. By the time it was done, the survivors owned nothing, the water rights reverted to the company, and the maps were redrawn so the village officially never existed." She looked around the fire. "That's why your charts show blank space here, pilot. It's not that nobody surveyed it. It's that somebody paid for the blankness."

Ulyana was staring into the fire. "You know a lot of detail for someone who wasn't there."

"I wasn't." Sable hesitated, and then decided — the way you decide to step off a ledge — "But my teacher was. The woman who taught me survey work, who taught me to read ice and read rivers, she was born on those terraces. Her name was Maren. Maren Oduya-Kesh. Last of the keeper line." She smiled at something private. "She took me on when I was nineteen because I asked too many questions and she liked that in a person. Everything I know about water, I learned from a woman whose entire inheritance was a drowned village and a memory nobody wanted to buy."

* * *

The rain thickened near midnight and they retreated under a lean-to of drum-lids and tarp. Sleep came in pieces. Sable woke once to find Ulyana sitting up, knees drawn in, turning something over in her hands — the brass compass, catching the last orange of the buried coals.

"My father flew this river," Ulyana said, to the compass or to Sable, hard to tell.

"I know."

Ulyana's head came up sharply. "How do you know that?"

"Because Maren talked about him." Sable sat up too, pulling the tarp closer against the drip. "Not by name, at first. She called him the lowland pilot. The one who ran mail and medicine up the gorge when the syndicate still pretended the upper river was open. He knew the channels better than their own bargemen. He'd land on the shingle below the terraces — right where we put in today, actually — and Maren used to say you could set a clock by his engine. The children would run down to meet him. He always came with something. Insulin for old Teodor. Books. Once, a whole crate of oranges, which Maren swore was the greatest event in the history of Kesh-Tara."

Ulyana had gone very still. In the dark, Sable couldn't read her face, only hear the change in her breathing.

"He flew relief runs after the dam failed," Sable went on, gently now. "Before the lawyers closed the valley. He carried the survivors' testimony downriver — statements, photographs, the keepers' flood records. Maren trusted him with the village's memory because he was the only one with wings who'd come when he wasn't paid to." She paused. "He tried to get it filed. The territorial registry rejected it twice — insufficient standing, the paperwork said. Then the syndicate bought his debt, and after that he stopped flying the upper river, and Maren said—" She stopped herself.

"Say it."

"Maren said he didn't stop flying because he was afraid. He stopped because he was being watched, and he was smart enough to know that some cargo you don't carry until the sky is safe." Sable met her eyes across the dead fire. "I think he was waiting for the sky to be safe, Ulyana. I think he was waiting a long time."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out on the water, a piece of the drowned village settled with a soft wooden groan, like the past shifting in its sleep.

"He never told me any of this," Ulyana said finally. Her voice was flat in the way Sable had learned meant the opposite of calm. "Debt. That's all I ever got. Debt and the compass and *fly straight*. Twenty years I thought he was just — a man who owed money. A cautionary tale." A short, bitter breath. "And the whole time he was carrying a drowned village in his plane."

"People carry what they're allowed to carry," Sable said. "Maren used to say the syndicate's real genius wasn't taking the water. It was convincing everyone downstream that their own history was somebody else's property. You don't fight for what you've been taught to forget." She reached over and tapped the brass casing lightly, once. "Your father never forgot. He just couldn't say it out loud. So he said it the only way left to him."

"In the logs," Ulyana whispered.

Sable said nothing. Some doors you open; some you stand beside while the other person opens them herself.

Okonkwo stirred in his sleep and murmured something about gradient and discharge, and the rain kept its patient rhythm on the tarp, and out in the black water the village of Kesh-Tara held its roofs up against forty years of forgetting.

* * *

Morning came thin and silver. The river had dropped another hand's width overnight, and more of the drowned street stood exposed — doorframes now, and the corner of a stone hearth, and half a staircase climbing out of the water toward a house that no longer existed.

Sable waded out at first light while the others broke camp. The water was snowmelt-cold and honest about it. She stood in the middle of the old street, boots on cobbles that Maren's grandmother had laid, and let herself feel the full strange weight of it: that she was standing inside a story she'd been telling for twenty years, and that the daughter of the man in the story was camped ten meters behind her, alive, armed with a brass compass and a grievance, and completely unaware of what she was carrying.

Because Sable hadn't told her everything. Not yet.

Maren hadn't only talked about the lowland pilot. In her last winter, coughing and clear-eyed in equal measure, she'd given Sable a message with instructions sharper than any survey bearing: *If you ever meet his child, tell her the keeper's records weren't lost in the flood. Tell her the copies went somewhere dry. And tell her the pilot knew where.* Sable had carried those sentences up and down this river for six years like a stone in her boot, waiting for a child who might never come.

Now the child was here, and the sky was not yet safe, and Reyes was watching them both from the bank with the flat calculating patience of a man composing a report.

Sable waded back to shore, wrung out her sleeves, and found Ulyana coiling rope with the fierce concentration of someone avoiding conversation.

"There's more," Sable said quietly. "About your father. About what Maren left with him. But not here." She glanced, deliberately, at Reyes. "And not until we're moving."

Ulyana's eyes came up. Whatever she saw in Sable's face made her coil the rope slower.

"How much more?"

"Enough to explain why Ansel is willing to lose men over paper." Sable shouldered her pack and looked upriver, where the gorge waited with its stacked grey weather and its ten-day clock. "Your father didn't just carry the village's memory, Ulyana. He kept a copy of it. And I think I know where."

Behind them, unnoticed, Reyes had stopped packing. He was looking at the water, at the drowned street, at the two women on the bank — and his hand had drifted, casually, toward the canvas bag where the hidden radio slept.
