## 14. My Father's River

The compass wouldn't sit still.

Ulyana had it flat on her palm, the brass case warm from her pocket, and the needle was doing something needles weren't supposed to do — swinging in slow, deliberate arcs, settling, then swinging again, as if it were reading the valley and finding it interesting.

She'd owned the thing for eleven years. She knew its moods. It pointed north with a stubbornness that had gotten her through whiteouts and one memorable night over the salt flats when every other instrument lied. It did not wander.

"Stop that," she told it.

It didn't stop.

They'd made camp on a gravel shelf above the drowned village site, where the river ran wide and shallow over the bones of a town the dam had swallowed forty years ago. In low water you could still see the church roofline breaking the current like a whale's back. Sable had spent the evening there, walking the shingle, touching things. Okonkwo was asleep under the tarp, his maps weighted down with stones against a wind that smelled of ice.

Ulyana sat apart, the way she always sat apart, and watched the needle lie to her.

Except it wasn't lying. That was the thought that kept circling back, patient as the rain. Needles didn't lie. They responded. Something nearby was pulling at it — something magnetic, or something massive enough to bend the local field, or—

Or the compass wasn't pointing north at all anymore. It was pointing upriver. Into the gorge. Toward the glacier.

She turned it slowly. The needle held its bearing relative to the case, dragging after her hand like a dog on a short leash, then swung back the moment she let it rest. Pointing. Insisting.

Her father had carried this compass for thirty years. He'd flown this river before she was born, before the dam failed, before the syndicate's name meant anything but fuel invoices. He'd died with it in his jacket pocket, and Ansel had been almost gentle about returning it to her — *your father would want you to have it, Ulyana* — courtesy like a leash, kindness with a ledger attached.

She'd never once asked herself why a man like Ansel bothered.

The fire crackled. Somewhere across the water, Sable was still moving among the ruins, a dark shape against darker stone.

Ulyana opened her father's flight log.

She'd brought it without deciding to bring it — pulled it from the lockbox under her bunk the night before they left the burned basecamp, the way you grab a photograph in a fire. She'd told herself it was sentiment. She'd read it a hundred times over eleven years: fuel loads, headings, wind corrections, the dry arithmetic of a working pilot's days. Page after page of a man counting his own life in nautical miles.

But she'd never read it *with* the compass in her hand.

She started at the beginning, because that was where he'd taught her to start everything. The early entries were ordinary. Then, three years before the dam failed — before the flood, before the debt, before any of it — the entries changed.

Not obviously. Her father hadn't been a man who hid things dramatically. He'd been a man who hid things in plain sight, in the margins, in the spaces between columns where a surveyor's eye might catch what an auditor's wouldn't.

*Dev. 4° W at 0930, self-correcting by 1100.* Deviation notes. Pilots logged compass deviation all the time; it was housekeeping. But her father's deviations were too consistent, too localized. Always the same stretch of river. Always the same bearing correction. And once, in the margin beside a heading into the gorge, in handwriting so small she'd needed the firelight and half her patience to make it out:

*Magnetic anomaly confirmed. Not ore. Water moving under stone.*

She read it four times.

Water moving under stone.

Okonkwo's maps. The aquifer. The thing Meridian had died — or vanished — mapping. The syndicate's prize, locked under glacier ice, feeding every settlement downriver. Her father had found it decades ago, flying supply runs for a company that hadn't yet learned what it wanted, and he had written it down in the only place no one would ever think to look: the boring parts of a dead pilot's logbook.

Her hands were not steady. She set the log on her knees and made them be steady, because this next part mattered.

If he'd written it down, he'd hidden the proof somewhere. A man that careful didn't leave evidence in a logbook he flew with every day. The logbook was the *index*. The compass —

She held it up again. The needle strained toward the gorge, toward the anomaly, and she understood, with the particular vertigo of a floor giving way beneath a familiar room, that the compass had never been a keepsake. It had been altered. Weighted, or magnetized, or built around a core that answered not to the pole but to whatever iron-bearing strata ran beneath the glacier. Her father hadn't given her a memento.

He'd given her a key. And Ansel had handed it back to her with both hands and a smile, because Ansel believed the key was useless without the map — and the map, as far as the syndicate knew, had gone into the river with the man.

*You've been wearing the leash and the treasure around your neck for eleven years,* she thought. *And you charged yourself rent for the privilege.*

*

Sable came back across the shingle an hour past full dark, boots wet to the knee, and found Ulyana still sitting by the fire with the log open and the compass balanced on her knee like a small insistent heart.

"You look," Sable said, "like someone who just remembered something."

"I found my father."

Sable went very still. It was one of the things Ulyana had learned about her in the weeks since the driftwood camp — how completely she could stop, mid-motion, mid-breath, the way animals do when the sound in the grass turns out to matter.

"He's dead," Ulyana said. "I mean I found what he was. Sit down."

Sable sat. Firelight moved on her face, and Ulyana watched her listen — really listen, no bad lies anywhere in her — while Ulyana laid it out: the deviation notes, the marginalia, the needle that wouldn't point north. When she got to *water moving under stone*, Sable closed her eyes briefly, the way people do when a pain they've been carrying gets named by someone else.

"The anomaly," Sable said quietly. "Meridian picked it up on the gravimetric survey. We thought it was new data. A discovery." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Your father beat us by thirty years. With a compass and a pencil."

"He didn't just find it. He hid the proof." Ulyana turned the compass so the needle's insistence was visible between them. "This isn't pointing north. It's keyed to the strata under the glacier — I'd bet the plane on it. He built himself a way back to wherever he cached the originals. Survey stamps, copies, whatever a registry would actually need." She paused. "There's a fissure system in the east wall of the gorge. I've flown past it a hundred times. Basalt dikes run through it — that's your magnetic pull, that's why the needle drags. He used to land on the shingle below it, back when the strip existed. Before the dam."

"And the cache is in the fissure."

"If the compass is what I think it is. If the margins mean what I think they mean." Ulyana looked into the fire. "Eleven years I thought he was a man who owed money. That he'd flown badly, borrowed badly, left me holding the bill. I built my whole life on top of that. Every run I flew for Ansel, every cargo, every time I signed my name — I was paying for his shame."

"And now?"

"Now I think there was never any debt." The words came out flat and strange, like cargo shifting in a hold. "I think Ansel invented it. Or inherited the invention. A pilot who finds out the syndicate's water surveys are forged — who has the field notes to prove it — doesn't get a bill. He gets a leash. And when the leash stops working—" She stopped. She didn't finish it. She didn't have to; they both knew how her father had died, alone, in weather he was too good to die in.

Sable reached across the fire and put her hand over the compass, not taking it, just resting her fingers on the brass as if steadying it. Or steadying Ulyana.

"My whole career," Sable said, "I told myself Meridian was science that got captured. That the sin happened at the end, when the data met the lawyers." Her voice was low and even. "But your father's log says the sin was there first. The falsified surveys. The claim architecture. They built the machine before they had anything to feed it. I walked away from the expedition. You've been flying for it for a decade." She looked up. "We're not so different, you and I. We both worked for the thing that killed someone we—"

"Don't," Ulyana said. Not harshly. Just final, like closing a throttle. "Not tonight."

Sable nodded and withdrew her hand, and the space where it had been felt colder than the night air.

*

Ulyana didn't sleep. She sat with the log until the fire burned down to coals, cross-referencing, and the past reorganized itself around her entry by entry, the way a river reorganizes around a fallen tree — patiently, totally, without asking permission.

Every supply run she'd ever flown took on a second shadow. The year Ansel raised her fuel rates and she'd swallowed it. The season he'd offered to buy her plane outright and she'd refused without knowing why, some animal instinct in her throat. The courtesy, always the courtesy — a man being kind to the daughter of the one witness who could unmake him, waiting to see if the blood carried the same caution as the father.

And the compass. God, the compass. She'd navigated by it for eleven years, trusted it in weather that ate lesser instruments, and the whole time it had been pointing at the truth every time she crossed the gorge — and she'd corrected for it like a good pilot, noted the deviation like her father taught her, and flown on.

*Self-correcting,* he'd written. She almost laughed. So was she. Eleven years of correcting for a lie and calling it navigation.

Near dawn, Okonkwo emerged from under the tarp, blinking, gray-bearded, immediately perceptive in the way of men who had spent their lives noticing water. He looked at the log, the compass, Ulyana's face, and said nothing for a long moment.

"You've found something."

"My father found it. I just found him." She slid the log across the gravel to him. "Read the margins. Tell me if this matches your data."

Okonkwo read the way he did everything — thoroughly, silently, with occasional small sounds of recognition, like a man greeting old friends in unexpected places. When he reached the last marginal note, his hands stopped moving entirely.

"This is field-verified," he said. "These aren't estimates. Flow rates, seasonal variation, recharge zones — he measured this. Over years." He looked up, and behind the exhaustion his eyes were bright with something between grief and fury. "Do you understand what this means? My survey confirms his. Two independent datasets, thirty years apart. That's not evidence, Ulyana. That's *proof*. No registry on earth dismisses corroboration like this — if the originals exist."

"They exist." She turned the compass in the light of the rebuilt fire. The needle leaned hard toward the gorge, toward the basalt and the ice, patient as it had presumably been patient for eleven years. "He left me the way to them. I just never knew I was holding it."

Okonkwo closed the log carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"Then we have a decision to make," he said. "Because data like this doesn't stay secret. Reyes may be gone, but Ansel knows Meridian found *something*. If he learns there are original documents — documents that predate and contradict his falsified surveys — he won't send lawyers first this time."

Ulyana looked upriver. The gorge waited in the gray light, walls going up and up into cloud, the glacier somewhere above it all, calving season coming whether they were ready or not. Somewhere in that stone was a fissure, and in the fissure, her father's hands had placed the weight of paper that could give a river back to the people who lived on it.

For eleven years she had flown to pay a debt she now understood was a fiction, wearing a key she'd mistaken for a relic, correcting for a truth she'd mistaken for error.

"All right," she said, and heard her father's calm in her own voice, and didn't mind. "Then we go get what he left. Before anyone else remembers this river exists."

Behind them, downstream, faint as a rumor, the first thunder of the calving season rolled up the valley like a door being unlocked.
