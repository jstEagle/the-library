## 15. Reyes Deserts

Reyes had learned to sleep in thirds. A third of the night for exhaustion, a third for listening, and a third for deciding which lie he would tell in the morning. It was a system that had kept him alive through two syndicate audits, one cartel negotiation, and eleven years of being the man Ansel sent when the message needed to arrive with weight behind it.

Tonight the third he'd allotted to deciding was running long.

He lay on his bedroll under the boat's canopy, rain drumming the tarpaulin above him like fingers testing a lock, and listened to the river work at the hull. The bilge pump coughed every ninety seconds — a wet, asthmatic sound that had gotten worse over three days. He'd timed it. Ninety seconds, then the cough, then the gurgle of water finding somewhere new to go. The boat was losing an argument with the river, and everyone aboard knew it, and nobody said it, because saying it would make it true.

Ulyana Ishikawa slept with her back against the fuel crates, one hand resting on her father's compass like it was a rosary. Sable was farther up the bank, in the driftwood lean-to she'd built before they found her, and Okonkwo — the hydrologist, the quiet one, the one Reyes had privately filed as *harmless* and was now re-filing as *unknown* — sat the middle watch, hunched under a poncho, writing numbers in a waterproof notebook by the light of a candle stub.

Reyes watched him write and felt the old familiar machinery of assessment turn over. *What does he know. Who does he report to. What is he worth.* Eleven years of habit. And underneath it, for the first time, a different question, arriving unwelcome and staying anyway:

*What happens to me.*

* * *

He had not always been this man. That was the thing nobody understood about fixers — that there had been a first day, an original version, some younger self who would have been appalled. Reyes's original version had joined the syndicate's logistics division at twenty-three because it offered dental coverage and a clear ladder, and he had told himself the stories all fixers tell: *I'm just moving paper. I'm just the messenger. The weight is in the cargo, not the hands.*

Then came the year of the evictions along the middle river, and Reyes discovered that paper moves people. That a claim filed correctly can do what a gun does, slower and cleaner, and leaves no blood to wash off the deck. He had carried those papers. He had watched families read them. He had learned to stand very still while they did, because stillness read as authority, and authority kept things from becoming physical.

He was good at his job. That was the whole catastrophe of it. Ansel valued him precisely because Reyes never flinched, never moralized, never confused the mission with his feelings about the mission. *You're my best instrument,* Ansel had told him once, in the lowland office, pouring coffee like a priest distributing communion. *Instruments don't bend.*

Reyes had accepted the compliment the way you accept a diagnosis.

* * *

The radio sat in its hiding place beneath the false bottom of the tackle locker, and Reyes thought about it the way a drunk thinks about a bottle behind the flour tin. He'd transmitted twice since the burned camp — short coded bursts, position and status, nothing Ansel couldn't have guessed. But the third transmission was due, and the third transmission was different, because the third one required an answer to a question Ansel had asked in his last reply, in that flat institutional shorthand they used:

*CONFIRM SUBJECT SABLE STATUS. LOCATION AND CONDITION. DEAD OR ALIVE SATISFIES.*

*Dead or alive satisfies.*

Reyes turned the phrase over in the dark. He knew what it meant operationally: Ansel needed certainty about what Sable knew before the calving window opened, because everything downstream — the lawyers, the filing dates, the gunboat that Reyes suspected existed even though nobody had said so — depended on whether one half-starved surveyor was going to walk out of this valley talking.

But he also knew what it meant humanly, which was that a woman who had shared their fire for nine days, who had given them her dried fish when rations ran thin, who had taught Okonkwo a card game using bottle caps for chips — that woman was now a line item with two acceptable outcomes.

And Ulyana Ishikawa had found the radio. Two nights ago. She hadn't confronted him yet — he'd watched her decide not to, watched her fold the knowledge away behind those pilot's eyes — but she knew. Which meant the clock on his usefulness here was already running, and when it stopped, three people on this river were going to look at him the way you look at something you've decided is weather rather than a person. Something to be endured until it passes.

* * *

He got up quietly, or as quietly as a hundred-and-sixty pounds of guilty conscience can move on a wooden deck, and went to check the fuel.

It was a ritual disguised as diligence. Every night he checked the reserve drums lashed amidships — forty liters of treated gasoline, the boat's margin, the difference between drifting and choosing where you went. He checked the seals, checked the siphon hose coiled beside them, checked the small hand pump. And every night he told himself he was doing it because he was thorough.

Tonight he lifted the tarp and looked at the drums for a long time.

Forty liters. Downriver from here, if the charts in his head were right, maybe sixty kilometers of winding channel to the syndicate's relay station at the mouth of the gorge approach — a shack, an antenna, a diesel generator, and a man named Corvo who owed Reyes money and asked no questions. From the relay station, a working transmitter. From the transmitter, Ansel. And from Ansel —

From Ansel, extraction. A seat on the gunboat. A return to the world of coffee poured like communion, of being an instrument instead of a man adrift on a drowning river with three people who had every reason to leave him on a sandbar.

That was the arithmetic. It was clean. It was the kind of arithmetic he was good at.

The other column was shorter. In it: Sable's face when she talked about the aquifer, lit by firelight, animated for the first time since he'd known her — not with ambition but with grief. Okonkwo's notebook, full of numbers that described every well and cistern and rain-catchment from here to the delta, thousands of lives expressed as flow rates. Ulyana's hand on the brass compass, and the way she'd said, once, not to anyone in particular: *My father flew this river before your syndicate had a name for it.*

Reyes closed the tarp.

He went back to his bedroll. He did not sleep in any of his thirds.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping, and with it the decision arrived the way decisions always arrived for Reyes — not as a lightning strike but as a slow flood, seeping up through the floorboards until one morning you simply stepped into the boat and it was already full.

The trigger was small. It usually was.

Sable caught a fish — actually caught it, bare-handed, in the shallows below the camp, holding it up grinning like a girl, and Okonkwo laughed, a real laugh, and Ulyana smiled from the boat, and for about four seconds there on the gray river it was almost a crew, almost a company, almost the kind of belonging that Reyes had spent eleven years telling himself he didn't want because wanting it was how instruments got bent.

He stood in the shallows watching them and understood, with total clarity, that he could not be part of it. Not because they wouldn't have him — that was the humiliating part; he suspected Ulyana, cold clarity and all, would have let him try — but because everything he was had been built to prevent exactly this. Belonging was a liability. Ansel had taught him that without ever saying it. You don't bend, so you don't attach, so you don't break.

Except he was breaking anyway. He could feel it in the way he flinched when Sable looked at him. In the way he'd started rehearsing lies and getting them wrong. Last night Okonkwo had asked him a simple question about current speed and Reyes had heard himself answer with a deflection so smooth and so pointless that he'd wanted to punch himself.

An instrument that flinches is worse than useless. It's dangerous. To itself most of all.

* * *

He made his move at the change of watch, in the dead hours before dawn, when the rain softened to mist and even the river seemed to hold its breath.

He'd prepared the story during his second third: he was taking the dinghy — the little patched rubber thing they used for shore runs — downriver to scout the channel ahead. Standard caution. Nobody would question it for six hours, and by then he'd have the drums transferred to the dinghy's tow line and be past the first bend, running the outboard quiet, hugging the shadowed bank.

The fuel drums were heavier than he remembered. Of course they were. Everything was heavier at four in the morning. He rolled the first one across the deck on its rim, wincing at every click of the rim against planking, and lowered it into the dinghy with a rope, breathing through his teeth. The second drum followed. Forty liters, riding low in the rubber hull, the tow rope taut between the boats.

Sable's lean-to was dark. Okonkwo slept sitting up, the notebook on his chest, rising and falling. Ulyana —

Ulyana was awake.

She was sitting exactly where she'd been when he'd last seen her, back against the fuel crates, and in the pre-dawn murk her eyes caught what little light there was and held it. She didn't move. Didn't speak. Just watched him stand there with a tow rope in his fist and guilt coming off him like heat off engine metal.

For a long moment neither of them said anything. The bilge pump coughed. Somewhere upriver, ice shifted in the glacier with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"You checked those drums every night," she said finally. Her voice was low, uninflected. "Nine nights. I thought it was thoroughness."

"It was," Reyes said. And then, because lying to her suddenly cost more than the truth: "It started that way."

"Ansel's waiting for your transmission."

"He's waiting for confirmation on Sable. Dead or alive." He heard himself say it and watched it land — watched Ulyana's jaw tighten, watched her hand close around the compass until the knuckles whitened. "I never sent it. The location. I gave him positions, distances, garbage precision. I bought us days."

"Why are you leaving, then?"

Because the honest answer — *because I can't watch you people be decent to me anymore, it's corroding something I need to survive* — was too naked to say out loud. So he said the operational truth instead, which was also, in its way, a confession:

"Because if I stay, I'm a liability. I'm the man who knows where the relay station is, and Ansel knows I know, and when the gunboat comes up this river it will come looking for its instrument. Better it finds me gone." He swallowed. "Better it finds me reporting."

"You're going to report."

"I'm going to survive." The words came out flat and final. "It's the only thing I've ever been good at. You should understand that better than anyone — you've been paying a debt for ten years just to stay alive. I've been doing the same thing. We just signed different contracts."

Ulyana stood then, slow, and for one absurd moment Reyes thought she was going to hit him, and he found he wouldn't have blamed her. Instead she looked at him the way you look at weather. Something to be endured until it passes.

"The fuel gets us through the gorge," she said. "You know that. You're not stealing our margin, you're stealing our route home."

"I know."

"And you're taking it anyway."

"I'm taking it anyway."

She nodded slowly, once, and there was no forgiveness in it and no fury either, and somehow that was worse than both. "Corvo's station," she said. "You'll run to Corvo's station."

He didn't ask how she knew. Pilots knew things. It was in the job description.

"When you get there," she went on, "and you make your transmission, and Ansel sends whatever he sends — remember that you chose this. Remember that there were four seconds on a river yesterday when you could have been something else, and you did the math instead."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is for people who aren't instruments."

* * *

He pushed off into the mist with the drums riding low behind him and the outboard at quarter throttle, and the river took him the way it took everything — without comment, without judgment, folding him into its gray current as if he'd always been bound downriver.

The channel narrowed. Mist hung in ropes off the water. Behind him, the camp vanished — the lean-to, the boat, the three people who had shared their fish and their fire with a man who was robbing them — and Reyes found himself doing the thing he despised in others, which was narrating his own betrayal as necessity.

*I bought them days,* he told the mist. *I never sent the coordinates. When Ansel asks about Sable I'll say lost track, conditions deteriorated, subject mobile. I can slow the machine from inside. I can be useful and harmless at once.*

But he knew the shape of that lie too. He'd written enough of them. Once the transmission went out, the machine would do what machines did — extrapolate, dispatch, escalate — and Reyes's fine distinctions would matter exactly as much as they always had, which was nothing. Instruments don't get to qualify their readings.

Sixty kilometers. The dinghy wallowed under the drums' weight, shipping water over the bow in small spiteful gulps, and Reyes bailed with a plastic scoop and steered by memory and by the deepening roar ahead — the gorge, the glacier, the whole frozen reservoir of the valley's memory beginning to groan toward its calving season.

Twice he looked back. The first time, mist. The second time, mist, and the faintest smudge of smoke where a cookfire had been restarted, and someone — he told himself it was imagination, knowing it wasn't — someone standing on the bank, watching him go, making no move to follow.

Pity. He recognized it even at a distance, even through rain. They weren't chasing him because they pitied him, and pity, he understood, was the one thing worse than pursuit. Pursuit meant you mattered. Pity meant you were a sad fact about the world, like weather, like the leaking boat, like the debt.

He ran the rest of the channel without looking back again.

* * *

The relay station appeared near noon as a brown smudge against the tree line: the shack, the leaning antenna, the generator shed, and Corvo's skiff tied at the rotting dock. Reyes grounded the dinghy on the shingle and sat for a moment without moving, hands still curled around the tiller as if it might steer him somewhere else if he held on long enough.

Then he climbed out, dragged the drums above the waterline, and walked to the shack, and the door opened before he knocked, because Corvo had seen him coming for an hour through binoculars and had the kettle already on, because that was the kind of place this was — a place where men arrived with wet feet and secrets and were fed before they were questioned.

"Rough season," Corvo said, which was not a question.

"Bad one." Reyes set his pack down. His hands, he noticed distantly, were shaking — not from cold. "I need the transmitter."

"Ansel's frequency?"

"Ansel's frequency."

Corvo looked at him for a long moment, and whatever he saw in Reyes's face made him nod slowly and reach under the counter for the key. "You want tea first?"

Reyes thought about the camp upriver. The fish. The laugh. Four seconds of belonging, traded for forty liters and a head start.

"Tea first," he said.

While the water heated, he keyed the transmitter's standby light and watched it glow amber in the dim shack, and began composing the message in his head — position, status, the careful garbage precision that might buy three more days, or might buy none. Outside, far upriver, the glacier spoke again, long and low, a sound like the valley clearing its throat before it said something irreversible.

Reyes drank his tea, picked up the key, and began to tap.
