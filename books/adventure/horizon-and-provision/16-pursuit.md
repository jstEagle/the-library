## 16. Pursuit

The fuel was gone before the sun cleared the ridge.

I stood at the water's edge and did the arithmetic twice, because the first time it came out impossible and I didn't want to believe myself. The boat sat on its mooring line with its stern low in the current, and when I waded out and pulled the cap off the reserve tank, the smell that came up was river water — brown, cold, faintly green with rot. Reyes had siphoned it clean. He'd even left the hose coiled neatly on the deck, which was somehow worse than if he'd thrown it overboard. Tidiness meant he'd had time. Time meant he'd had a head start.

"He took the jerricans too," Sable said from the bank. She was crouched by the cold fire ring, reading the ash like a page. "Two of them. He carried them down to the water — you can see where he dragged his heels."

Okonkwo limped to the edge of the shingle and looked at the river as if it might explain itself. It wouldn't. The river never explained itself; that was the whole of my education in this country.

"How long?" I asked.

Sable considered. "The fire's still warm under the crust. Two hours. Maybe three."

Three hours. In a boat with a full reserve and a man who knew exactly where he was going, three hours was a country. Downstream the channel bent south past the drowned village, then straightened for a long gray run toward the relay station — Ansel's ears on the upper river, the place where everything we'd bled for could be turned into a radio burst and a set of coordinates. Reyes had been listening to us for weeks through that hidden transmitter. Now he was running to tell what he'd heard.

"We can't catch him," Okonkwo said. Not cruelly. Just the way a hydrologist states flow rates. "Not in anything we have."

I looked at what we had. A dead boat. A cache of salvaged fuel drums — forty-five gallons each, empty since we'd drained them for the sabotage run, stacked under a tarp above the flood line. Rope. Three knives. My father's compass, riding in my breast pocket like a small hot coal.

"Empty drums float," I said.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Sable started pulling the tarp off the stack.

* * *

We built the raft the way you build anything in this country: badly, quickly, and with total commitment. Eight drums lashed two-by-two into a spine of rope, cross-poles lashed over the top, our packs and the wrapped logs lashed down center where they'd stay driest. I doubled every knot twice and hated every knot anyway, because knots are promises made to a river, and rivers collect on promises.

Okonkwo tested the flex of the poles and shook his head. "This will not survive the gorge water."

"We're not going through the gorge," I said. "Reyes won't either. The gorge runs east toward the ice. He's going south, down the old channel." I pointed with my chin. "Past the drowned village. Past the shallows where the dam failure dumped all that gravel. If he knows the river, he'll shoot those rapids standing up. If he doesn't—"

"He does," Sable said quietly. "He's been on this water longer than any of us. Meridian hired him as a runner before he was a fixer."

Of course he had. Reyes was many bad things but he was never careless, and a careless man doesn't steal your fuel and leave the hose coiled. We were chasing competence. That was the part that sat in my stomach like ballast.

We shoved off at mid-morning into a river running high and fast with monsoon melt, and the drums bit the current and went, and I'll be honest about what it felt like: it felt like flying without wings. No engine noise, no instrument panel, nothing between me and the water but rope and nerve. The current took us at a speed that made conversation useless and thought nearly so. Spray came off the wave trains in sheets. Sable knelt at the front with a paddle cut from driftwood, reading the water the way I read weather — a boil here meaning a rock below, a smooth glassy tongue there meaning the fast lane.

"Hard right!" she shouted once, and we all threw our weight starboard, and the raft swung wide around a sweeper willow whose branches would have stripped us off the drums like fruit off a branch.

I steered from the back with a second paddle and my father's compass open on the thwart beside me, though I barely needed it. South. Everything was south now. The needle lay steady along the channel and I kept glancing at it anyway, the way you touch a bruise.

Futility rode with us. I want to be precise about that, because adrenaline is a liar and I've spent enough of my life believing liars. Even at full current, even with the drums singing under us, we were gaining nothing. A boat with an outboard makes four times our speed against any water. Every minute that passed, Reyes got further from us and closer to the relay, and the relay was a fixed point, and fixed points don't move no matter how hard you paddle. We were not catching him. What we were doing was arriving slightly less late.

You do it anyway. That's the whole secret of this river country, maybe of every country. You do it anyway, because the alternative is sitting on the bank doing arithmetic until the arithmetic eats you.

* * *

We passed the drowned village at noon.

Or rather, the river passed it for us, and we held on. The rooftops were under again after all these decades — just rooflines breaking the surface like the backs of animals, and the church spire leaning at its permanent angle, and the top two feet of the bell tower showing black above the brown water. Sable had told us their story there, weeks ago, in the cave camp: the families who lived on this bend, the dam that failed upstream one spring night, the water that came down the valley and took the town in eleven minutes. My father had flown relief runs over this exact stretch afterward. I hadn't known that then. I knew it now, and knowing it changed the way the water sounded.

"Keep your eyes on the channel," I said, mostly to myself.

But it was Sable who saw him first. She stood up on the front drum — actually stood, knees bent, riding the pitch like she'd been born on floating things — and pointed downstream-left.

"Smoke!"

A thread of it, thin and blue-gray, rising off the far bank two bends down. A cook fire, banked low, smoke kept deliberately small. Reyes was being careful even about his lunch.

Okonkwo shielded his eyes. "He's stopped? Why would he stop?"

Because the rapids were ahead, I realized. The shallows where the dam failure had dumped half a mountain of gravel into the channel — Sable had described them, and I'd flown over them a hundred times without ever seeing them properly, a mile of braided water braiding around gravel bars, some channels deep, most of them ankle-deep lies. A loaded boat could run them. Could also open its hull on the first bar and spend the rest of the day stranded in the middle of a gravel field with a syndicate gunboat eventually coming upriver behind it. A careful man scouts first. Reyes was a careful man.

"He has to pick his line," I said. "And he has to wait for the light to be right — glare off the water hides the bars. He'll wait for the sun to come around."

Sable looked up at the sky, gauging. "An hour. Maybe more."

An hour. For the first time since the cold fire ring, the arithmetic moved. We couldn't beat him to the rapids, but we could arrive while he was still staring at them, and a man alone with a boat and a decision is a man who can be argued with.

Or shot at, said a voice in my head that sounded unpleasantly like mine. He has a rifle. You have paddles.

"Then we stop short," I said. "Take out above the bend and come at him on foot through the trees. If he sees a raft coming he'll just run the rapids blind, and if he runs them blind he either dies or makes it, and both of those are worse than talking."

"Worse how?" Okonkwo asked.

"If he dies, we're killers by pursuit. If he makes it, he reaches the relay having seen us coming, and he'll transmit everything including our faces." I shipped my paddle. "Talking is the only option where somebody gets to keep being a person afterward."

Sable sat back down slowly. She was looking at me with an expression I couldn't file — something between surprise and reassessment, like I'd changed shape in front of her.

"When did you become the one who thinks like this?" she asked.

"I've always thought like this. I just used to do it alone, in a cockpit, where it didn't matter to anyone." I jabbed the paddle toward the bank. "There. Gravel bar, behind the alders. Get us in."

* * *

Beaching a drum raft is a controlled disaster. We came in too hot, as you must, and the drums ground up the shingle with a sound like a whale dying, and we spilled into knee-deep water hauling the lashings before the current could take the whole stupid contraption back. We dragged it above the flood line, tied it to an alder, and stood dripping in the trees, listening.

The river talked. Wind in the alders talked. Somewhere downstream, faintly, a kettle lid rattling — or I imagined it did.

We went downstream along the bank in single file, Sable leading because she moved quietest, Okonkwo behind her favoring the leg he'd hurt in the narrows ambush, me last with the compass in my pocket and a boat hook in my hands because it was the closest thing to a weapon I was willing to carry. I don't know what I intended to do with a boat hook. That was the honest truth of it. I had walked out of the relay station weeks ago having helped wreck a transmitter rather than point a gun at a man, and I hadn't gotten braver since, only more tired, and tired people do strange arithmetic about violence.

We found him at the head of the rapids.

He'd beached the boat on a gravel spit below the last bend, and he stood at the water's edge with his back to us, studying the braided channels through a pair of binoculars. The jerricans sat in the boat. His rifle leaned against a driftwood log six feet away, which told me he expected no one — or wanted his hands free for the glasses. The cook fire was dead, drowned with water, careful to the last.

Sable raised a hand and we stopped. The wind was at our backs, carrying our scent away downstream, and the river covered whatever sound we made. For a long moment the three of us stood in the alder shade watching a man plan his escape, and I felt the strangest thing, which was recognition. I knew that stance. Feet planted, weight forward, jaw set — the posture of a person trying to control the only thing left controllable. I'd worn it in a cockpit over burning country. I'd worn it signing Ansel's contract on my own airstrip while courtesy closed around my throat like a leash going soft.

Reyes wasn't a monster. That was the trap of him. He was a man with a debt, same as me, and his debt had a different name on it, and he'd chosen differently at every fork and arrived here, alone on a gravel spit, about to gamble his life on a channel he couldn't fully see because the alternative was a syndicate that ate its runners.

I stepped out of the trees.

"Don't," I said, loud enough to carry over the water.

He spun. His hand went for the rifle and stopped — Sable had drifted wide during the approach and now stood ten feet from the driftwood log, one boot resting on the stock, arms folded. Okonkwo came out of the brush behind me with his hands visible and empty, which was its own kind of argument. Reyes looked at the three of us, and at the rifle he couldn't reach, and did the math men like him are always doing, and let his hand fall.

"Ishikawa." He almost smiled. Almost. "You built a raft out of fuel drums. That's the most pilot thing I've ever seen."

"You left the hose coiled," I said. "Neat. Considerate. Like you wanted me to know how much time you'd had."

"I wanted you to know it wasn't personal." He wiped his face with his sleeve. His eyes kept flicking to the rapids, to the boat, to the jerricans — measuring exits even now. "You think I wanted any of this? Ansel had my sister's medical bills the way he had your father's ledger. Same leash, different collar. When the radio died at the station I figured my collar was cut, so I ran. Can you honestly tell me you'd have done different?"

Yes, I wanted to say. I'd have done different. But I remembered chapter after chapter of my own life — the contract signed, the crates I hadn't inspected, the years of cargo flown quietly for a syndicate because the alternative was losing everything my father left me — and the word stuck in my throat like a fishbone.

"The transmitter's wrecked," I said instead. "Even if you reach the relay, there's nothing to send on. We killed it."

"There's a backup set in the strongbox under the floor plates. I installed it." He shrugged, miserable and defiant at once. "Ansel doesn't pay for single points of failure. Neither would you, in his boots."

Behind me, Okonkwo exhaled slowly. So the sabotage had bought us days, not safety. Somewhere downriver, a hidden radio waited under a floor for a loyal voice.

"Then don't send anything," Sable said. She hadn't moved from the rifle. Her voice was flat and very quiet, the voice she used for the truest things. "Go south on foot. Disappear. There are settlements in the delta that owe Ansel nothing — that hate him, if anything. You know the river families remember Ulyana's father. They'd take you in. People get forgiven out here for less than what you've done."

"And when the gunboat comes upriver asking questions? Because it will come, Sable. Ansel doesn't lose cargo and shrug." Reyes laughed, and there was nothing in it. "You're offering me a slower version of drowning."

"I'm offering you a choice," she said. "You haven't had one in years. I recognize the look."

The river ran loud through the silence. Out on the braids the light was changing, the glare sliding off the water as the sun came around, and I watched Reyes watch it — watched him understand that his window was opening whether he took it or not, and that we were the only thing standing between him and it.

"Here's my offer," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and river-cold, the voice I used on passengers who lied about their cargo weight. "You beach the boat here. You walk into the trees and keep walking, south, and we don't follow. The jerricans stay — we need them more than you need speed you can't use. And you forget the backup radio exists. Not for us. For your sister, whoever she is, wherever she is — because if Ansel gates that aquifer, every settlement downriver goes thirsty, and sick people with no clean water is a list your sister is probably already on. You've seen Okonkwo's maps. You know I'm not lying."

Reyes stared at me. Something worked behind his face — fear and loyalty and arithmetic, grinding against each other like gravel in a rapid.

"And if I say no?" he said. "If I run the rapids right now?"

"Then we'll be behind you," I said. "On drums. All the way to the relay. And you'll spend every mile of it looking over your shoulder instead of at your channel, and these shallows eat distracted boats. Ask the river. It's been collecting on this bend for forty years."

He looked at the water a long time. The braided channels glittered, treacherous and beautiful, half of them deep enough to float a boat and half of them deep enough to drown one, with no way to tell from here except patience and good light and a stillness he no longer had in him.

His shoulders dropped half an inch. That was all. But I'd spent my life reading how people sit in turbulence, and I knew a surrender forming before it finished forming.

"The strongbox code is Meridian's founding date," he said quietly. "Backwards. Don't tell Ansel I told you."

He picked up his pack. He didn't look at any of us — looked at the river instead, the way you look at a thing you're leaving — and walked up the gravel spit into the alders, and the brush closed behind him, and he was gone.

Sable let out a breath she'd been holding since the trees. Okonkwo sat down abruptly on a driftwood log, as if his leg had finally sent its invoice.

"That was—" he began.

"Don't," I said. "It's not done. A man walking south changes his mind every hundred yards. And even if he doesn't — Ansel already knows we're on the river. Reyes transmitted for weeks. The gunboat isn't coming because of what Reyes reports. It's coming because of what he already reported."

I waded out to the boat and pulled the floor plate, and there it was: the backup set in its steel box, squat and patient, antenna coiled beside it like a sleeping snake. I lifted it out and carried it to the deepest hole in the gravel bar and pushed it under the water and held it down until the bubbles stopped.

When I stood up, Sable was beside me, and she was looking downriver — past the rapids, past the drowned village, toward the long gray run where the relay station sat with its wrecked antenna and its floor full of secrets.

"Ulyana," she said. "Look at the water level."

I looked. The shallows should have been dropping with the morning's melt passing through. They weren't. The braids were running fuller than an hour ago, and the main channel had climbed another finger-width up the gravel spit, and far downstream a haze hung over the valley that wasn't rain haze — it was the kind of standing mist that forms when a big body of moving water meets a wall.

Something upstream had opened. The calving had begun early, and the gorge was letting the glacier's winter loose all at once, and a flood pulse was coming down the old channel with the relay station — and whatever was left of Ansel's plans — directly in its path.

"Get the drums," I said. "We're not chasing anyone anymore. We're racing the river."
