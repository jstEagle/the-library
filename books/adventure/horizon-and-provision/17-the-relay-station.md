## 17. The Relay Station

The rain had a rhythm by then, and I'd stopped hearing it as weather and started hearing it as time. Each hour of it was an hour Reyes was ahead of us on the river, an hour closer to the syndicate's relay station on the bluff above the last bend before the lowlands.

We passed the burned remains of his theft sometime after dawn — the fuel drums he'd taken from our raft cache, two of them, wedged in a strainer of fallen timber where the current had thrown them. One still held fuel. The other had split along its seam and bled itself into the water, a rainbow skin of it spreading downstream like something wounded.

"He's paddling heavy," Sable said. She crouched at the front of our raft of salvaged drums, lashed together with vine rope and prayer, reading the water the way Okonkwo read strata. "Two drums of fuel, a pack, himself. He'll hug the north bank where the current runs slack. He'll stop before dark."

"He won't stop," I said. "He's terrified."

"Terrified men stop," she said. "Terrified men make camp early and build big fires and sit up all night listening for us." She looked back at me, rain sheeting off her hood. "You don't know him the way we do."

I did, actually. I knew him better than either of them, though I hadn't said so out loud. I knew how he sat in turbulence — braced, always braced, hands flat on his knees like a man waiting to be told to jump. Men who sat that way weren't brave and weren't stupid. They were survivors, and survivors didn't push through a flooded gorge at night with a raft they didn't trust.

But I didn't argue. Arguing cost breath, and breath cost speed, and speed was the only currency we had left.

Okonkwo sat amidships with his arms around the one dry crate — the radio parts, the tools, the coil of det cord Sable had carried out of Meridian's stores months ago like a woman who kept her exits open. His shoulder wound had gone quiet under the poultice, which worried me more than if it had throbbed. Quiet wounds went septic. But that was a problem for a night with walls around it, and this day had none.

The relay station showed itself at midafternoon, when the rain thinned to a gray gauze: a tin-roofed shack on a shelf of rock above the river, a forty-foot mast beside it guyed with wire, a landing below with a fuel cache and a signal mirror bolted to a post. Syndicate property. Syndicate paint — the same institutional green as everything Ansel touched, chosen by someone who had never seen the river and understood that green meant nothing here except algae.

No smoke from the chimney. No boat at the landing.

"We're first," Sable said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before — not relief exactly. Appetite.

* * *

We beached below the bluff and climbed through wet fern, and I will say this for fear: it makes you light. I hauled myself up that slope on roots and handholds without once thinking about my hands, which is not how I usually climb.

The station door wasn't locked. It was never locked — that was the point of relay stations, they existed for whoever the syndicate sent, and no one else came this far upriver except people the syndicate had already broken. Inside: a bunk, a stove, a shelf of tinned food gone dusty, and the transmitter — a proper set, bench-mounted, with a hand-crank generator for when the solar panel failed, and an antenna lead running out through a porcelain grommet to the mast.

It was a good radio. It was, in fact, the same model as the hidden one Reyes had been whispering into during the monsoon, and seeing it made my jaw tighten in a way I felt all the way up into my ears.

"Ulyana." Okonkwo stood in the doorway, dripping. "Think about what you're doing."

"I'm thinking about it right now."

"Say it aloud. All three of us should hear the plan before anyone touches anything."

So I said it. "Reyes reaches this station, or he reaches the next one downriver, and he reports. Position, names, the fact that we have the logs. Ansel sends a gunboat through the gorge at calving season — that was always the plan, Reyes just accelerates it. If this transmitter doesn't work, Reyes has to travel every kilometer of river himself, in the wet season, alone, on foot past settlements that remember my father's face better than mine." I turned to look at them both. "That buys us days. Maybe a week."

"And after the week?" Okonkwo asked. He has a way of asking questions that isn't hostile, which is worse. "After the week, someone comes to fix the station. And they find it broken deliberately, and they know who broke it, because there are exactly four people on this river with reason to."

"Then let them know."

"That's not strategy, Ulyana. That's temper."

"It's both," Sable said, from the corner where she'd been examining the antenna lead. "She's right and she's angry and those aren't opposites." She pulled a multitool from her jacket. "Question isn't whether. Question is how. A smashed radio gets repaired in a day by any tech with spares. A radio that looks fine but doesn't transmit — that gets repaired never, because nobody checks what appears to work."

I stared at her. "You want to sabotage it so it fails quietly."

"I want to sabotage it so it fails *confidently.*" She was already opening the access panel. "Reyes arrives, cranks the generator, keys the mic, hears carrier hiss in his headphones, and reports back to Ansel that he's in contact. Except he isn't. Nothing leaves this mast but noise." Her hands moved inside the chassis with the sureness of someone who had spent years maintaining survey equipment in worse conditions than this. "I'll pull the driver tube and crack the filament. Looks perfect. Dead as stone."

"You could have done this to your own expedition's radios," I said, before I could stop myself.

Something crossed her face — not guilt, exactly. Recognition. "I thought about it," she said. "For about nine days. Then I walked instead, because breaking radios only works if someone's willing to walk away from everything the radios connect to." She glanced up at me. "Are you?"

I didn't answer. I took the crank handle and started turning, because the generator needed testing and because turning a crank is easier than answering questions.

The set powered up clean. Carrier hum, meter needle swinging, everything beautiful and functional and about to lie for us. Sable swapped the tube, cracked the filament against the bench edge with a small precise tap, reseated it, and closed the panel. From outside, from the air, from any inspection short of disassembly, the station was ready for traffic.

Then Okonkwo said, "Boat."

* * *

He'd seen it through the window — or rather, seen the absence resolved into presence: a shape on the north bank two bends up, moving. Reyes, making better time than I'd credited, or more desperate than I'd credited, which amounted to the same thing.

We had perhaps twenty minutes.

"Down to the landing," Sable said. "He'll come up the path. We take the high ground behind the woodpile."

"No," Okonkwo said.

Both of us looked at him.

"He's armed," Okonkwo said. "He took the flare pistol and I'd bet the settlement rifle from the cave camp too. You put three people on a hillside above a frightened man with a gun, you get shooting, and the first person shot will be whichever of you he sees move first." He pushed past us, out into the rain, toward the front of the shack. "He knows me. He trusts me the way a man trusts a doctor — not warmly, but completely. Let me stand in the doorway."

"He'll think we ambushed him," I said.

"He'll think that anyway. Better he thinks it looking at a man holding a kettle than a woman holding a crowbar." Okonkwo was already filling the kettle from the rain barrel. Of course he was. "Put the tea things out. Make the shack look like what it is — a place someone stopped. Not a trap. A kitchen."

And that is what we did, in the strangest fifteen minutes of my life: three fugitives setting a table for the man hunting them. Sable found cups. I lit the stove — dry wood cached under the eave, blessedly — and put water on, because Okonkwo said steam and smell would do what weapons couldn't, would tell Reyes before he reached the door that the people inside had chosen cooking over killing.

I heard him coming up the path while the kettle was still warming. Heavy footfall, uneven, the gait of a man who'd slept badly and paddled worse. Then the shadow across the window, and the pause — long, long enough that I could hear the rain ticking on the tin roof and my own pulse in my wrists.

The door opened. Reyes stood in it with the rifle at his hip, soaked through, gray-faced, eyes going from Okonkwo at the stove to me at the table to Sable by the bunk, cataloguing us, counting exits.

"Don't," he said. Just that. His voice was wrecked.

"Sit down, Mr. Reyes," Okonkwo said. "You're shivering. There's tea."

"I'm not—" The rifle came up six inches and stopped, as if the arm lifting it had run out of conviction halfway. "Don't do the hospitality thing. Don't. I know what you are. All of you. Traitors, saboteurs—"

"Saboteurs of what?" Sable asked mildly, from the bunk. "Your sleep schedule?"

"Shut up." But his voice cracked on it, and the muzzle dipped again, and I saw the truth underneath the anger, which was exhaustion so complete it had eaten through to terror. He hadn't slept. He'd paddled all night through flooded water he couldn't swim in, a man who couldn't swim alone on a river, and whatever Ansel held over him — money, family, the simple animal promise that loyalty buys safety — it had brought him here to a door with warm light behind it and enemies inside.

"The radio works," he said suddenly, and it came out like a confession. "I checked the mast from the bank. I'm reporting in, and then the gunboat comes, and none of this—" the rifle gestured at the shack, at us, at the whole drowned green world outside it, "—none of it matters. So sit down. All of you. Sit down and wait."

"Reyes." Okonkwo poured the tea. Steam rose between them. "Key the set."

"What?"

"Key it. Transmit. Call your check-in now, while we watch. I'm asking you to prove yourself right."

I saw it land on him a half-second before it landed on me — the trap inside the courtesy, the same trick Ansel used, turned around. Because Reyes wanted to key the set. Wanted it desperately. Wanted the hiss in the headphones and the acknowledgment code and the enormous relief of being, finally, in contact with the machine that would save him.

He set the rifle against the doorframe — carefully, the way exhausted men do, telling themselves it's temporary — and crossed to the transmitter, and cranked the generator, and put the headphones on, and keyed the mic.

Hiss. Beautiful, full, healthy carrier hiss. He keyed again, longer, sent his prefix, waited. Sent it again. Waited.

The rain ticked on the roof. The kettle sang softly.

"Band conditions," he said. Nobody answered him. "Storm interference. It's — the front's still overhead, propagation's garbage, I'll get through on the scheduled window tonight." He was talking faster now. "Tonight's window. Ansel monitors the window. It's fine. It's fine."

"Take off the headphones, Mr. Reyes," Okonkwo said gently.

Reyes didn't move. Then his hand came up and pulled the driver tube — I watched him do it, watched him find the fault in ten seconds flat because he was syndicate-trained and good at his job — and he held the cracked glass up to the light and looked at it for a long moment.

"You broke it," he said.

"Yes," Sable said.

"You broke it and you *waited for me.*" He turned around slowly, and I braced for the rifle, but his hands were empty and shaking. "You sat here and made tea and waited. Why? Why not just leave? Why not run?"

"Because you can't swim," I said.

He stared at me.

"And the river's up," I said. "And the settlements downriver have been watching the banks since the storm started, because that's what river people do. You wouldn't have made the delta. You'd have made the first rapid, and no one would ever have known which side of it you went over." I stood up. My legs were unsteady and I hated that. "I flew you upriver, Reyes. I read you in the cabin the whole way — you brace in turbulence, you count the exits, you survive. So survive *this.* Sit down and drink the tea and listen, because Okonkwo has something to say and you're going to hear all of it before you decide anything."

He sat. He drank the tea. His hands steadied by degrees, the way a fever breaks — not all at once, but measurably, minute over minute.

* * *

What Okonkwo said took an hour, and I won't reproduce all of it, partly because much of it was maps — he spread his waterproof sheets on the bunk boards and walked Reyes through the aquifer the way a teacher walks a student through anatomy, every settlement marked, every well, every dry-season spring, the whole living circulatory system of the river country that Ansel's claim would put behind a gate and a meter.

What I remember instead is the moment Reyes stopped arguing. It wasn't a surrender. It was smaller and sadder than that. He was looking at the map, at the cluster of marks downriver, and he said, "My mother lives at Second Landing," in a voice like a man reading his own diagnosis, and after that he didn't say much at all.

He asked what we intended to do with him. Sable said maroon him here — the station had food for weeks, a roof, a stove, and no working radio. He'd be found eventually, or he wouldn't; either way he'd miss the calving window, and the gunboat would come upriver blind, if it came at all.

"I'll report the sabotage," Reyes said dully. "When they find me. I'll tell them everything."

"Tell them whatever keeps you alive," Sable said. "That's the only instruction I have for you."

We left him the rifle — Okonkwo insisted, and I understood why: a man alone on a river needs it for food and for the particular courage it lends an empty room. We left him the tins, the stove, the blankets, a week of our own rations on top of the station's stock. We left him the tea.

At the door, he caught my sleeve. Up close his eyes were bloodshot and young, younger than I'd ever let myself see.

"Ishikawa. Your father—" He stopped. Started again. "Ansel talks about him. In the office. Not the way you'd think. There's a photograph on his desk, old, river survey stamp on the corner. He kept it." He let go of my sleeve. "I never understood why until this week."

I walked out into the rain without answering, because there was nothing in me yet equal to that, and because the raft was waiting and the current was waiting and somewhere behind us, up the drowned gorge, a glacier was getting ready to let go of itself.

We pushed off into the gray water. Once, looking back, I saw a small figure standing on the bluff under the dead mast, watching us go — not raising a hand, not calling out. Just watching. A man marooned with his survival and his mother's village and a broken tube of glass, violence averted between us and nothing resolved, nothing that rain could wash clean.

Sable put her paddle in and matched my stroke.

"Ten days," she said. "Maybe less."

The river took us down.
