## 18. Intermission of Rain

**Ulyana**

The rain did not fall so much as settle in, the way a debt settles in — patiently, without malice, certain of its claim. For the first three days Ulyana counted it. After that she stopped counting and started living inside it.

The relay station sat on a shelf of gravel above the river bend, a squat concrete box the syndicate had built to whisper coded traffic downriver. Its transmitter was dead now — Okonkwo had pulled its heart out with the calm of a man defusing something — but the building itself was sound. Roof intact. A woodstove. Bunk shelves that smelled of creosote and old paper. There was even a window, glazed with river fog, through which the world arrived in shades of gray.

Ulyana organized it the way she organized her cockpit: everything with a place, everything in reach. Rations on the second shelf, counted nightly. Fuel drums lashed under the lean-to. The salvaged radio parts Reyes hadn't taken locked in a crate with a padlock she'd brought from the plane, because trust was a thing you built hardware around when you couldn't build it any other way.

"You inventory like someone who's been robbed," Sable observed from the doorway, watching her count matches for the third time that evening.

"I've been robbed."

"By Ansel?"

"By arithmetic." She closed the matchbox. "Debt is just robbery with better manners."

Sable laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped custody. Ulyana found herself wanting to hear it again and made herself not want that, the way you make yourself not look at a fuel gauge on a long crossing. Some needles you didn't need to watch.

The weeks arranged themselves into a shape. Mornings: Okonkwo checked his instruments, logged rainfall, took readings of the river's rise with a stick and a notebook, because a hydrologist without instruments was still a hydrologist, the way a pilot without a plane was still — she tested this thought and put it away. Afternoons: work. There was always work. The station's dock had half-collapsed; they rebuilt it with driftwood and salvaged cable. The woodpile grew. Nets they'd found rotting in an outbuilding got mended, though none of them fished at first, mending them anyway because hands needed occupation or they started thinking.

It was during the nets that Ulyana learned Sable had been a surveyor and not, as Ulyana had privately assumed, a soldier. Surveyors, it turned out, could tie knots no one else could name.

"What do you call that one?" Ulyana asked, watching Sable's fingers walk a net line into a flat, impossible braid.

"A sheet bend's cousin." Sable held it up to the gray light. "My grandmother taught me. She fished the coast before the syndicate bought it."

"Everyone's grandmother fished something before the syndicate bought it."

"Yes," Sable said, and there was no bitterness in it, which was somehow worse. "That's how they win. They buy things people have already stopped guarding."

Ulyana looked at her own hands, stained with pitch, holding a stranger's net. She had spent nine years guarding exactly one thing — a ledger balance, a number that shrank by fractions every season — and she was beginning to suspect she'd guarded the wrong object entirely.

* * *

**Okonkwo**

Okonkwo kept two diaries. One was official: rainfall, river stage, barometric guesses based on how his knee felt, all entered in a surveyor's hand he'd learned decades ago and never lost. The other was unofficial, kept in his head, and it recorded different data entirely.

*Day nine.* Reyes gone eleven days now, downstream with the stolen fuel, and the river running high enough that Okonkwo gave him decent odds of reaching the lowlands alive, which was more charity than the man deserved and less than he'd need if Ansel read his garbled reports the way Okonkwo feared.

*Day twelve.* The pilot — Ishikawa — had reorganized the food stores twice. He recognized the behavior. Grief does housekeeping. So does fear. In her case they were probably the same filing system.

*Day fourteen.* Sable taught cards.

That was worth recording. The game was called Kessel, a surveyor's game, played with a deck missing three cards that had gone wherever lost things go, and its rules were vicious: you won by making your opponent believe a lie about what you held. Okonkwo was terrible at it. He had spent forty years being paid to tell the truth in numbers, and bluffing sat in his mouth like river mud.

Ishikawa was good at it. Better than good — she lied with her whole body, shoulders slumping in theatrical defeat while her hand quietly assembled a killing sequence, and then she'd sweep the pile with a face of pure apology. But when Sable bluffed, Ishikawa folded every time. Every time. It was statistically impossible unless the folding was voluntary, and Okonkwo, who had spent a career noticing what rivers did under their surfaces, noticed this too.

He said nothing. He wrote it in the second diary, in the space where he kept things he wasn't ready to say aloud:

*The pilot folds to the surveyor and no one else. Note for later. Possibly note for never.*

On day sixteen the rain eased for six hours — the first break since they'd arrived — and the whole valley exhaled. Mist lifted off the water in ropes. Somewhere upstream a glacier shifted and the sound came down the gorge like a door closing in a very large house. All three of them stood on the repaired dock looking at mountains that had appeared out of nothing, white and enormous and indifferent.

"We'll fly out of here someday," Ishikawa said, "and forget this place exists."

"No, we won't," said Sable.

Okonkwo entered it in the diary without comment. Some entries annotated themselves.

* * *

**Sable**

Sable had been alone before — properly alone, weeks alone, the kind of alone where your own voice starts sounding like a stranger's — and she had believed she preferred it. The intermission corrected her. Not dramatically. Corrected her the way water corrects a compass: slowly, inevitably, until one day you looked down and the needle pointed somewhere new.

She learned the station's rhythms and then she learned theirs. Okonkwo woke before dawn and stood at the window doing something he claimed was stretching and which was clearly prayer, though he'd deny it under oath. Ulyana slept badly and rose late and worked furious, as if labor were a debt collector she could outrun. And between them, Sable discovered, there was a current — not spoken, barely visible, the kind of current that will capsize you precisely because you refuse to acknowledge it.

She caught herself cataloguing the pilot. The scar on the left forearm, burn-shaped, never explained. The way she talked to machines — full sentences, patient, kinder than anything she offered people. The way she'd rebuilt the stovepipe with a coffee can and then stood back with an expression Sable could only call proprietary tenderness, as if the stove were a child who'd done well at school.

Stop, Sable told herself. You walked away from Meridian because you understood what wanting things costs in country like this.

But the rain kept falling, and the cards kept coming out after supper, and the circle kept happening — the three of them around the woodstove, lamplight, the river talking to itself below — and wanting things turned out to be less like a decision and more like weather. You didn't choose it. You only chose whether to carry an umbrella.

One night Ulyana lost spectacularly — threw the game, Sable was nearly sure, though she couldn't prove it — and laughed, actually laughed, head back, and Sable looked at her across the cards and thought: *oh.* Just that. Just the small detonation of it.

"You're staring," Ulyana said.

"You're cheating."

"I'm *losing.*"

"That's what I mean."

Okonkwo gathered the cards with the dignity of a man leaving a room before furniture starts flying. "I'll check the rain gauge," he announced, to no one's belief, and went out into the dark.

The silence he left behind had weight. Sable turned her last card over and over between her fingers. Outside, the river ran high and cold toward a sea neither of them could see, carrying everything it carried, keeping nothing.

"My father flew this river," Ulyana said suddenly, to the stove. "Before the dam failed. Before all of it. I keep finding out more about him from strangers than I ever knew from him."

"He was probably protecting you."

"From what?"

"From being someone who belongs somewhere," Sable said. "It makes you targetable."

Ulyana looked at her then — a long look, unguarded, the first fully honest instrument reading Sable had ever gotten off her — and whatever passed between them in the lamplight was real and terrifying and completely unactionable, and both of them knew it, and both of them let it sit there, circling, circling, like birds over a drowned village.

"Your turn to deal," Ulyana said finally, voice rougher than the words required.

Sable dealt.

* * *

**Ulyana**

Week three, and Ulyana caught herself doing something unforgivable: humming.

She was mending the same section of net she'd already mended twice — her knots were cockpit knots, functional and ugly — and she was humming her father's landing checklist set to a tune he'd never used, and she stopped mid-bar when she realized Sable was listening from the doorway with an expression of open delight.

"Don't," Ulyana warned.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to compose a ballad."

"It would've been brief." Sable came and sat across the net from her, taking the opposite end without asking, and they worked toward each other in silence for a while, meshing, closing the gap. This was the other thing about the intermission: it kept arranging them like this. Facing each other over small work. Hands almost meeting in the middle.

"Tell me something true," Sable said. "Since we're trapped."

"The stove draws better since I fixed it."

"Larger."

Ulyana considered the net, the rain, the crate with the padlock, the whole gray economy of the waiting. "I don't know how to be here," she said. "In a place. With people. I know how to arrive and leave. That's my entire skill set. Arriving and leaving."

"So stay badly," Sable said. "Nobody stays well at first. My grandmother said staying is a muscle."

"Did your grandmother say anything that wasn't a proverb?"

"She said syndicate men tip badly. Also a proverb, if you think about it."

Ulyana laughed despite herself, and the laugh turned into something quieter, and the quiet stretched between them, warm instead of awkward, and she understood with a pilot's clarity that they were approaching something — a fix, a waypoint, a point of no return drawn in lamplight — and that neither of them had filed a flight plan for it.

"Sable."

"Mm."

"When the weather breaks—"

The question hung unfinished, because outside, cutting through the rain-hush, came a sound Ulyana's body knew before her mind did: an engine. Not the river. Not ice. An aircraft engine, throttled low, somewhere south beyond the fog bank, circling.

All three of them were at the window before the lamp finished swinging. Gray sky, gray water, gray curtain of rain — and threaded through it, faint and wrong, the sound of wings hunting for a place to land.

Or hunting for a place to look.

Okonkwo spoke first, very evenly. "Reyes reached the lowlands."

Ulyana watched the fog, listening to the engine draw its slow circles, and felt the intermission end the way all intermissions end: not with a bell, but with the lights coming up on whatever you'd been hiding from in the dark.
