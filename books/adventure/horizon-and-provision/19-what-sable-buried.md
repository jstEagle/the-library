## 19. What Sable Buried

The rain had found a new rhythm by the third week at the relay station — not the assault of the first days but a long, patient drumming on the corrugated roof, like someone tapping a finger on a table, waiting for an answer.

Sable had learned to hear it as a kind of clock. Morning: rain steady, wind from the southwest. Midday: rain heavier, wind swinging. Evening: rain softening just enough to remind you it could stop, and then not stopping. She'd spent eleven months in the valley learning to read ice and light, and now she was learning weather against her will, pinned inside a building that smelled of diesel, mildew, and Okonkwo's boiled bandages.

He was sleeping. The wound in his shoulder had turned ugly before Ulyana's improvised surgery, and though the fever broke two nights ago, he still drifted through most of each day in a shallow, sweating sleep. Ulyana sat beside him now, mending a net with the fierce concentration of a woman who needed her hands busy or they would start doing something else — wringing, maybe, or shaking.

Sable stood at the window, watching the river run brown and swollen past the station's rotting dock.

She had been putting this off for nineteen days. She knew the number because she counted everything now, the way she'd once counted survey points: distance to the cache site, days since Meridian went up the valley, hours since Reyes had tried to transmit her location on the hidden radio. Nineteen days since Ulyana had found the radio and looked at her across the cave camp with an expression Sable couldn't file under any category she trusted. Not betrayal exactly. Something worse. Recalculation.

*She flew you out here,* Sable reminded herself. *She owes you nothing.*

But the thing in her chest didn't care about ledgers. It had been sitting there since the night she walked away from Meridian's fire, and every day Ulyana patched Okonkwo's dressings or rationed the rice with scrupulous fairness or laughed — actually laughed, once, at Sable's terrible card playing — the thing got heavier.

Tonight, she decided. Tonight she would tell her.

* * *

She waited until Ulyana came to the window herself, drawn by the same restlessness. They stood shoulder to shoulder in the grey light, watching the water carry whole trees downstream like matchsticks.

"The gorge will open soon," Ulyana said. "Calving season. Ten days, maybe less if the melt comes early."

"I know."

"Okonkwo can't walk far yet. And we still have to move the logs." She said *the logs* the way other people said a child's name — carefully, with weight. Her father's flight logs, pulled from the cliff fissure three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth, sitting in a ammunition box under Okonkwo's cot like a second patient.

"They'll keep," Sable said. "They've kept thirty years."

Ulyana glanced at her. "You sound like you're stalling."

Sable almost laughed. "I'm always stalling. You haven't noticed? I'm very good at it. I stalled Meridian for six weeks before I ran."

"You told us you walked when you understood what the data would be used for."

"I did." Sable turned from the window. Her reflection ghosted in the wet glass, thin and rain-blurred, and she thought: *this is what you look like when you're about to stop lying.* "But I didn't tell you everything. And you're about to stake your life — all our lives — on getting those logs downriver, so you should know what I buried before you decide whether I'm worth the trouble."

Ulyana set down the netting needle. Slowly. The way she did everything when she was paying real attention.

"Sit down," Sable said. "This takes a while."

* * *

"When Meridian hired me, I thought it was a hydrographic survey. That's what the contract said. Glacial mass balance, meltwater timing, aquifer mapping. Good science. I'd been doing contract work in Patagonia and Svalbard, and the money was nearly double, and I told myself the syndicate's name on the letterhead was just money being money." Sable sat on the upturned crate across from Ulyana and laced her fingers together to keep them honest. "Ansel interviewed me himself. That should have been the first warning. Principals don't interview surveyors. He asked me strange questions. Not about ice. About people. Whether I was good at keeping teams moving when morale failed. Whether I'd ever had to make a decision alone that affected everyone's safety. Whether I could tell when someone was lying to me."

"What did you say?"

"That I was excellent at all of it." Sable's mouth twisted. "Which was true, which is how he knew he could use me. You see it now? He wasn't hiring a scientist. He was hiring a handler."

Ulyana said nothing, but she'd gone very still, the way she did in turbulence — reading the shape of what was coming by how it moved.

"The first month was legitimate. We surveyed the lower glacier, established basecamp, ran the seismic lines. Then Ansel flew in with a second team — security, he called them, though they carried more than survey equipment — and the mission changed. We weren't mapping the aquifer anymore. We were racing it. There's a route through the gorge that only opens at calving season, ten days a year, and everything above it is locked in ice the rest of the time. The syndicate wanted the full extent of the aquifer mapped and documented *before* calving season opened the route to anyone else. Exclusive data. Exclusive claim. They weren't studying the water. They were fencing it."

"And the settlements downstream?"

"Don't exist, as far as Meridian's paperwork is concerned. Ansel's surveys show the upper valley as uninhabited wilderness. Dozens of pages of falsified land-use reports. I saw them. I helped compile some of them, God forgive me, because I told myself the data itself was neutral and someone else would decide what it meant." She shook her head. "That's the lie scientists tell themselves. Data is never neutral. Data is a loaded gun on a table, and whoever pays for the survey picks it up first."

The rain filled the pause. Down the hall, Okonkwo murmured in his sleep and went quiet again.

"So I started dragging my feet," Sable said. "Bad weather calls. Equipment failures. I recalibrated instruments that didn't need it. I lost a week arguing about datum corrections that would have taken a day. And it worked, for six weeks, because Ansel is patient and he assumed I was incompetent rather than hostile." She looked up. "Then he stopped assuming."

"How?"

"He brought in the flight logs."

Ulyana's breath caught — Sable heard it, small and sharp, in the drumming quiet.

"Not your father's," Sable said quickly. "Older ones. From the dam company, before the failure. Surveys of the valley floor, the drowned villages, the old channels. Because here's the thing nobody talks about: the aquifer isn't natural, not entirely. When the dam failed, the floodwater didn't all drain to the sea. A huge volume of it went underground, into the fractured rock under the valley, and it's been there ever since — cold, clean, pressurized behind the glacier like water behind a plug. Your father must have known. Anyone who flew this river long enough would have seen the springs that don't freeze, the green patches in January. But knowing and proving are different things, and proof is what Ansel wanted. Proof, and a witness who could lead his teams to the physical evidence before calving season made the valley accessible to anyone else."

"You're saying he needed a guide into the ice."

"I'm saying he needed me." Sable's voice came out flat, stripped. "Because I'd already found something. In week five, running a ground-penetrating radar line along the east wall, I found a fissure — deep, dry, stable, opening onto a cavity behind the icefall. And in the cavity, cached in waterproof drums, someone had left supplies. Old ones. Pre-dam-failure markings. A pilot's cache. Fuel, parts, documents sealed in oilcloth." She met Ulyana's eyes. "Your father's cache. Or one of them. I didn't know whose it was then. I reported it as a geological feature with anomalous contents, and Ansel's security team went up the next morning, and they came back with the drums, and Ansel read the documents for four hours behind a closed flap, and after that he stopped being patient with me."

The crate creaked under Sable as she leaned forward.

"He didn't threaten me. That's the thing you have to understand about Ansel — he never threatens. He *arranges*. He showed me photographs of my sister's house in Tromsø. New roof, he noted. Recently installed. Expensive. He mentioned that Meridian's insurance covered accidents in the field but that contractors who abandoned their obligations forfeited certain protections. He restructured my contract so that my completion bonus became a completion *penalty* — owed by me, payable immediately, if I failed to deliver the final survey package. He built me a debt, Ulyana. Out of nothing. Out of paper. And then he held it over me the way—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The way he holds yours over you."

Ulyana hadn't moved. But something in her face had shifted, tectonic and slow, like the ice they'd both spent months studying.

"So I led them there," Sable said. "That's what I buried. You asked me once why I ran, and I told you it was conscience, and that was true, but it wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth is that I guided Meridian's security team back to your father's cache site and watched them take what was in it. Survey stamps. Charts. Correspondence. Thirty years of documentation proving the aquifer's extent and the falsified reports that hid it. I stood on the ice and gave them bearings, and I told myself I was buying time, buying safety, buying my sister's roof, and none of it was a lie except the part where I pretended I had no choice."

"Did they get everything?"

"No." Sable held up one hand, counting off fingers. "Three reasons. First, the cache was bigger than they expected and the security team was lazy — they took the drums that were easiest to reach and logged the rest as 'inaccessible secondary material.' Second, I misreported the cavity's depth by forty meters on the official survey, so anything they file later won't match the terrain. Third—" her hand closed, "—your father was smarter than any of us. The cache I found was the decoy. I'm nearly sure of it now. Real caches don't sit in convenient fissures with labeled drums. Real caches are where a man hides things he expects professionals to hunt for. Which means somewhere above the icefall, there's another one, and Ansel has been looking for it for a year, and he thinks I know where it is."

"Do you?"

"No." And this, at least, Sable could say cleanly. "I never knew. I found the decoy by accident. Whatever your father hid deeper in, he hid it well enough that a year of Meridian's searching hasn't found it either."

"But they have partial data," Ulyana said. "From the decoy cache. Enough to—"

"To start a claim, yes. Maybe enough to win one, if no one contests it with better originals." Sable spread her hands. "Which is why your father's flight logs matter more than you know. They're not corroboration, Ulyana. They're the only complete record left. Everything else is fragments Ansel controls."

* * *

Silence. The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere under the cot, the ammunition box sat with its oilcloth burden, and Sable thought she could feel it listening.

Ulyana spoke at last, and her voice was careful, level, the voice of a woman landing a plane in crosswind. "When you told me you walked away on principle — in the cave, the first night — you let me believe you'd taken nothing from them. That you were clean."

"I let you believe that."

"Why?"

"Because you were flying me away from men who wanted me dead, and because—" Sable stopped. The honest answer sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed months ago. "Because you looked at me like I was someone worth saving, and I didn't want to change your mind. That's not a reason. That's vanity. But it's the truth."

Ulyana nodded slowly. Then she did something Sable didn't expect. She reached under the cot, drew out the ammunition box, and set it on the table between them — not opening it, just placing it there, heavy and present, like a third person joining the conversation.

"My turn," Ulyana said. "Since we're trading."

"Sable—"

"No. Fair is fair." She sat back down. "I told you my father died fleeing debt. Shame. That's what Ansel let everyone believe, and I believed it because it was easier than the alternative. But I've been reading his logs every night since we pulled them from the fissure, and my father wasn't running from anything. He was carrying something. Page after page of observations — spring temperatures, flow rates, the syndicate's survey markers photographed next to his own readings. He was building a case, and he died before he could file it, and I spent fifteen years being ashamed of a man who was braver than I'll ever be." Her jaw tightened. "And here's the part I buried: Ansel didn't come to my strip last month because the debt came due. The debt was always fiction — I understand that now. He came because Meridian went silent, and he suspected someone had found the valley's secret, and he needed a pilot who knew the river. Me. He's been grooming me for years. The courtesy, the patience, the leash — it was never about money. It was about making sure that when the moment came, I'd fly exactly where he pointed."

Sable stared at her. "You're telling me we're the same."

"I'm telling you I've spent my whole life paying a debt that was designed to own me, and you spent six months paying one that was manufactured to use you, and neither of us chose it, and both of us let it define what we thought we deserved." Ulyana's hand came to rest on the ammunition box, flat and steady. "So. Two liars, one table. Anything else buried, or do we start digging from here?"

Sable laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her, that hurt a little coming out. "One more thing."

"Of course there is."

"The decoy cache. When the security team hauled the drums out, one of them split on the ice. Documents spilled. I gathered them up to repack them, and I kept one page. Slipped it inside my jacket. Survey stamp impressions — your father's registry marks, clear enough to authenticate originals against." She reached into her shirt pocket and produced a folded square of paper, soft as cloth from months against her skin, and laid it on the ammunition box. "I told myself I stole it to protect myself. Leverage. Insurance. But I think I stole it because it was the first honest thing anyone on that expedition had touched, and I couldn't stand to watch them carry it away."

Ulyana picked up the page. Unfolded it. Her thumb moved over the stamped impressions, and Sable watched her face do something complicated — grief and recognition and a fierce, unguarded tenderness, all at once, like weather changing over a valley.

"These are his," Ulyana said quietly. "These are definitely his."

"Yes."

"They'll authenticate the logs at the registry."

"If we get them there." Sable nodded toward the window, toward the brown river and the white silence beyond it, where the glacier waited with its ten-day door. "Which brings us to the problem I've been stalling around. If Ansel already has partial data, he doesn't need to find us anymore. He needs to stop us. And he doesn't send one man for that. He sends a boat."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain kept its patient rhythm, but underneath it — faint, distant, felt in the floorboards more than heard — came a low rolling report from upriver. Like thunder. Like something enormous shifting its weight.

Both women went still.

"That's not weather," Sable said.

"No," Ulyana agreed, already reaching for her boots. "That's the ice. Calving season's early."
