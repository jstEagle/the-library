## 20. The Flight Logs

The compass needle didn't waver, and that was the problem.

Ulyana had spent three days trying to make it waver. She'd held it level on the relay station's porch while Sable read the bearing aloud, then checked it against Okonkwo's maps, then checked again, because a needle that steady felt less like navigation and more like accusation. Two hundred and sixty-one degrees. The same every time. Her father's brass compass pointing upriver into the gorge country with the certainty of something that had been waiting years to say its piece.

"That's not a bearing," Sable said, watching her turn it over for the fourth time. "That's an address."

"It's a rock face," Ulyana said. "Two hundred and sixty-one degrees from here is forty kilometers of cliff."

"Then we walk until it stops being a rock face and starts being a door."

Okonkwo spread his field maps across the station's one table, weighting the corners with cartridge cases and a tin of tea leaves. His arm was still bound to his chest — the wound in his shoulder had closed but not forgiven him — and he worked one-handed with the economy of a man who'd decided not to be an invalid. He traced the river's course with a pencil stub.

"The old channel," he said. "Before the dam failed, before the flood rearranged everything. Your father flew this river when the maps were honest." He tapped a stretch of the north bank where his own survey lines went thin and speculative. "There's a fissure system through here. I mapped part of it in '09. Limestone, water-cut, deep enough to swallow a man and quiet enough to keep him."

"You think he hid paper in a cave."

"I think a pilot who understood what he was carrying wouldn't trust a safe. Safes get opened. Caves get forgotten." Okonkwo looked at her over the lamplight, and there was nothing gentle in it. "He trusted you'd come looking."

"He never told me anything."

"No," Okonkwo agreed. "He told you a compass. That was the telling."

* * *

They left at first light with three days of food, the rifle, and the compass riding in Ulyana's breast pocket where she could feel its weight against her sternum like a second heartbeat. Sable led. She moved through the monsoon-soaked forest the way water moved — finding the low lines, the game trails, the places where the ground remembered being walked on. Twice she stopped without explanation and stood listening, and twice Ulyana heard it a moment later: the far-off cough of the gunboat's engine, or maybe just the river. They'd stopped being able to tell the difference, which was its own kind of information.

The gorge narrowed through the morning. The walls came down close and gray, streaked black with runoff, hung with vines that dripped even between rains. The air smelled of wet stone and cold iron. Somewhere above them, out of sight, the glacier spoke — long groans, like furniture being dragged across a floor by someone patient and enormous.

Ulyana found herself talking, because silence had gotten too heavy to carry alone.

"He used to fly this stretch low. Lower than made sense. I asked him once why, and he said the river tells you things from a hundred feet you can't hear from a thousand." She stepped over a fallen trunk furred green as velvet. "I thought it was just something pilots said."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was reading it. Taking its temperature. Learning where the water came from and where it went, so that when the syndicate's surveys said *dry*, he'd know they were lying."

Sable stopped at the head of a scree slope and looked back at her. Rain beaded in her cropped hair; her face was still thinner than it should have been, the Meridian weeks carved into her cheekbones. "You knew about the falsified surveys," she said. "From my confession. But you didn't know your father had proof."

"I knew he owed them money. That's what I was told. That's what I believed for eleven years." Ulyana's voice came out flat, and she let it. "Every cargo run, every dawn takeoff, every time Ansel smiled at me across a desk — I thought I was paying for a dead man's weakness. If these logs say what Okonkwo thinks they say—"

"They'll say he was the strongest man on this river," Sable said. "And they'll say you've been paying interest on a lie."

The scree took another hour. By the time they reached the base of the north wall, the light had gone the color of dishwater and the rain had settled in for the duration, fine and persistent, more climate than weather. The cliff rose sheer above them, two hundred meters of limestone seamed with dark vertical cuts, any of which might be the fissure Okonkwo remembered.

Ulyana took out the compass.

Two hundred and sixty-one degrees had brought them here, but now the needle did something new: as she turned slowly, holding it level, watching, the needle trembled — once, twice — and swung off true north by a degree, then steadied, pointing not at magnetic north but at a seam in the cliff face perhaps eighty meters along the wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots.

"Lodestone," Okonkwo said quietly, when she told him that night. Or would have said — actually he only nodded when she described it, because they were standing in the rain and he was watching the seam with the expression of a man confirming a hypothesis he'd been carrying like a stone. "Iron-bearing strata. It pulls the needle off true. Your father found it flying low, same as he found everything else. And he used it — a landmark no map records, visible only to a compass. You couldn't find this cache by accident. You could barely find it on purpose."

The seam was wide enough to admit a body turned sideways. Sable went first, because Sable always went first, her headlamp cutting a cone through the dripping dark. The fissure swallowed sound. Their breathing came back to them flattened, immediate, intimate. The walls pressed close enough that Ulyana's shoulders brushed both sides at once, and the cold coming off the stone was the glacier's own cold, filtered down through a hundred meters of rock.

Ten meters in, the passage opened.

Not much. A chamber the size of her plane's cabin, dry — genuinely dry, the first dry air Ulyana had breathed in weeks — floored with sand and gravel, and against the far wall, wrapped in waxed canvas and tarred cord, a shape.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. That was her first thought, absurdly: all this, for something the size of a duffel bag. Her second thought arrived with her knees, which decided independently to stop working, and she sat down hard on the sand and put her hand flat against the stone floor because the chamber seemed to be turning.

Sable knelt beside the bundle without touching it. "Ulyana."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, and that's allowed. Touch it or don't. Either way it's real."

She touched it. The canvas was stiff with age, the cord tight, the knots — she recognized them with a jolt that went through her like current — the knots her father tied, the ones he'd taught her on the dock at Kessel Landing when she was nine, saying *a knot should hold forever or fail loudly, nothing in between*. She cut the cord with her belt knife because untying it felt like something she hadn't earned yet.

Inside: three flight logbooks, bound in oilcloth. A sealed tin canister, military surplus, stenciled with faded lettering. And beneath both, folded into a square no bigger than a hymnal, a chart of the upper river in her father's own drafting hand, annotated in pencil so dense the margins had grown a second skin of writing.

Her hands shook. She hated that they shook, and opened the oldest logbook anyway.

* * *

The lamp threw their shadows huge against the wet stone outside. Inside the fissure they didn't need it; Sable's headlamp lay on a rock, aimed at the ceiling, filling the chamber with a pale underwater glow. Okonkwo sat with the tin canister open on his knees, lifting out glass-plate negatives wrapped in cotton, each one numbered in pencil. Survey plates. Originals.

"These are pre-syndicate," he said, and his voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was frightened and refusing to show it. "These are *his own* flights. Look — dates, altitudes, flow readings. He surveyed the aquifer himself, over years. This isn't a snapshot. This is a longitudinal record." He looked up. "Do you understand what that means legally? Anyone can claim they found water. Almost no one can prove they tracked it for a decade."

Ulyana turned pages. Her father's handwriting marched across the columns — fuel, heading, wind, remarks — and in the remarks column, growing year by year, a second kind of entry. *Meltwater turbidity high, glacial source confirmed.* *Spring discharge at km 114 unchanged despite drought upstream — source must be artesian.* *Syndicate survey team passed downstream, reporting 'no significant aquifer' — see attached plate 14.*

Plate 14 was in the tin. She held it up to the headlamp: a photograph of a river gauge, dry and rusted, with her father's penciled note beneath: *Gauge installed by syndicate contractors at site chosen to miss the spring line by 3 km. Deliberate. Witnessed. Dated.*

"They didn't just lie," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, like radio traffic from another aircraft. "They built the lie. They placed instruments to manufacture ignorance. And then they filed claims based on manufactured ignorance, and called my father a debtor because he—" She stopped. Because the next page had stopped her.

The final logbook's last entry was dated nine days before his plane went down. The remarks column, usually terse, ran long:

*Ansel knows I have copies. Offered to forgive the debt for the originals. Told him the debt is his, not mine. He laughed. These people laugh easily. If I go down, the cache holds everything: logs, plates, chart. Compass points the way for whoever I trust to look. U. — if it's you, I'm sorry for the weight. Carry it as far as you can, then carry it farther.*

Ulyana read it three times. The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far above, the glacier groaned and settled, and a fine sift of dust came down through the lamplight like slow snow.

"He wrote to me," she said. "He knew it might be me."

"He hoped it would be you," Sable said. "There's a difference."

"There's a difference," Ulyana agreed, and found she couldn't say anything else for a while, because her throat had closed around something eleven years old.

Okonkwo repacked the negatives with hands that weren't quite steady either. "This is enough," he said finally. "This is more than enough. With these plates and his stamped surveys, we don't file a claim — we file a *correction*. The registry will have to hear it. Ansel's whole edifice stands on those falsified gauges. One clerk with a conscience and these originals, and it comes down."

"And Ansel knows it," Sable said. "Which means somewhere between here and the registry, this becomes the most hunted paper on the river."

Ulyana lifted the chart — her father's chart, the river drawn in his sure draftsman's lines, the aquifer's extent shaded in blue pencil like a second river running beneath the first, vast and branching, feeding every settlement from the gorge to the delta. Eleven years she'd flown over it, carried medicine to the children who drank from it, and never known she was flying the length of her inheritance.

Paper, she thought. Everyone feared guns, floods, ice. Nobody warned you about paper. Three logbooks and a tin of glass, and they weighed more than any cargo she'd ever hauled — more than fuel, more than ore, more than the debt itself. Because cargo stayed in the hold. This would change what everything weighed, forever after.

She wrapped the logbooks in the waxed canvas exactly as she'd found it, retied her father's knots — *hold forever or fail loudly* — and slung the bundle across her chest where the compass rode, so the two of them knocked together faintly with each step, brass and paper, key and lock, finally in the same room.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. Sable scanned the cliff line and went very still.

"Smoke," she said.

Ulyana followed her gaze. Far downriver, past the gorge mouth, a thread of gray rose against the dusk — too steady, too deliberate, too large for a campfire.

"Campfire smoke doesn't hold position," Sable said. "That's a boat under power, banked for the night." She turned, and in the last light her eyes were grim and clear. "The gunboat's entered the gorge. We have the logs, Ulyana. Now we find out how fast we can run with them."
