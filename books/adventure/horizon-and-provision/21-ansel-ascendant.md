## 21. Ansel Ascendant

The office smelled of cedar polish and old paper, which was how Ansel liked it. Institutions should smell like something that outlasts the people inside them.

He sat at his desk with the window open a hand's width — enough to hear the lowland rain ticking against the awning, not enough to let it curl the corners of his correspondence. On the blotter before him lay three items arranged with the precision of a man who believed order was a form of prayer: a cup of coffee gone lukewarm because he refused to drink while working; a letter from the territorial survey office, stamped and filed and therefore already dead to him; and a map of the upper river, creased along the fold so many times that the valley itself had become a scar in the paper.

He was reading the letter again. Not because he needed to. Because it pleased him.

*Application for exclusive extraction rights, glacial aquifer sector seven, deferred pending verification of hydrological survey data.*

Deferred. Ansel had spent thirty years learning what that word meant when a government used it. It meant: *we are waiting for someone braver or richer to decide for us.* It meant the door was closed but the lock was broken. It meant everything.

The clock on the mantel — an heirloom, brass, older than the syndicate itself — ticked with the patience of a thing that had never once been in a hurry. Ansel checked it out of habit. Four minutes past three. Reyes's scheduled transmission was due at half past.

He poured two fingers of the coffee anyway, cold as river water, and drank it standing up. A small penance. He'd found early in his career that men who allowed themselves comfort before results tended to mistake comfort for results.

Outside, the compound went about its afternoon. The fuel drums got rolled onto the loading ramp. The mechanics argued over a carburetor in voices that carried. Somewhere a radio played music from the coast, thin and tinny through the static, a woman singing about a town she'd left. Ansel listened for a moment without sentiment. He had left towns too. That was all such songs were ever about — the leaving, never the arriving, because nobody wrote songs about sitting still in an office waiting on a radio.

At twenty-six minutes past, the set in the corner crackled.

Ansel crossed the room and stood over it the way other men stood over fires in winter. The operator on duty — a boy named Ferro with soft hands and good ears — glanced up, then adjusted his headset and began writing.

The transmission came in fragments. That was normal this far upriver; the relay at the gorge bounced signals off weather as much as anything else. But fragments were information too, if you knew how to read absence. Ansel had built a career on reading absence.

Ferro transcribed as it came:

*MERIDIAN SITE CONFIRMED BURNED STOP NO SURVIVORS AT BASECAMP STOP ONE BOAT MISSING STOP*

A pause. Static like sand poured over glass.

*PROCEEDING UPRIVER BY WATER STOP WEATHER DETERIORATING STOP SUBJECT FEMALE POSSIBLY PRESENT STOP WILL CONFIRM STOP*

Then nothing. The carrier tone held for another minute, then died.

Ansel read the flimsy twice. Ferro watched him with the careful blankness of a young man who had learned that watching Mr. Ansel think was like watching weather form — interesting, and best done from indoors.

"Confirmed burned," Ansel said. "No survivors at basecamp."

"Yes, sir."

"And *possibly present.*" Ansel tapped the phrase once with one finger. "Reyes is being vague. Reyes is never vague by accident."

"He might have lost the—"

"He has a codebook, Ferro. Vagueness is a choice." Ansel took the flimsy. "Log the time of receipt. Then get me Wexler at the marine yard, and after that the meteorological desk. In that order."

* * *

Wexler answered on the second ring, which told Ansel the man had been waiting by the phone, which told Ansel the man was nervous, which was useful.

"The *Corvus,*" Ansel said. "How soon can she make way?"

A pause long enough to measure a heartbeat. "She's fueled. Crew's aboard. But Mr. Ansel — the monsoon front's stacked up over the middle river. My skipper says the gorge won't be passable until—"

"I know when the gorge will be passable, Wexler. I've been reading ice reports since your skipper was in short trousers. The calving window opens in nine days, perhaps ten. I want the *Corvus* timed to enter the gorge on the first day of navigable water. Not the day after. The first day."

"You understand what you're asking. First-day ice is—"

"I understand precisely what I'm asking." Ansel kept his voice level, almost gentle. Gentleness, deployed correctly, was more coercive than shouting. Shouting told a man where the boundaries were. Gentleness let him discover them himself. "I'm asking you to put a boat into moving water at the exact moment the mountain begins shedding itself into the river. Yes. That is what I'm asking."

Another silence. Then, smaller: "And if they refuse?"

"They won't. Your skippers are paid above scale for exactly this reason. Remind him of the reason."

He rang off before Wexler could develop a conscience. Consciences were like leaks — manageable if caught early, ruinous if ignored.

The meteorological desk took longer. Ansel spoke to a junior forecaster whose name he made a point of remembering — people performed better for a man who remembered their names — and extracted what he needed: the front would stall over the middle river for six, possibly seven days, then break apart from the south. Which meant clear water behind it. Which meant the *Corvus* could ride the breaking weather upriver and reach the gorge mouth as the ice began to go.

Nine days. Ansel wrote the number on the corner of the map and looked at it for a while.

Nine days was either a great deal of time or none at all, depending on who moved first.

* * *

He worked through the evening the way he did most evenings: standing at the map table, sleeves rolled, a pencil behind his ear he never used. The office lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, and he was aware of it, distantly, the way a man is aware of his own breathing. His shadow reached the door. If anyone came in, they would walk through him first. He liked that arrangement.

The problem, laid out plainly, was this: Meridian had gone silent. Reyes reported the site destroyed and was proceeding upriver toward the valley with a pilot, a hydrologist, and — the phrase still bothered him — a *subject female possibly present.*

Ansel did not deal in possibilities. Possibilities were what other men traded in. He dealt in facts, and where facts were absent, he manufactured conditions under which facts became unavoidable.

So: assume Sable was alive. Assume she had walked away from Meridian rather than deeper into it — the missing lifeboat suggested departure, not pursuit. Assume she knew what the surveys contained, because she had drawn them. A surveyor who defects is worth ten times a surveyor who stays; she carries the whole map in her head, and heads cannot be subpoenaed or bought back.

Assume also — and here Ansel permitted himself the luxury of assuming, because he had earned it — that the girl Ishikawa was involved. She had flown the search party up. Her father had run that river before the dam failed, and old Ishikawa had been a problem in his day. Quiet problems were the worst kind. They compounded.

Ansel had inherited the debt the way he'd inherited everything: cleanly, on paper, from men who preferred not to look too closely at origins. A fabricated ledger entry, a loan against a plane, interest compounding annually like moss on stone. Old Ishikawa had died before settling it, and the debt had passed to the daughter like a surname. Ansel had never once threatened her. Threats were crude. He had simply maintained the debt, punctually, courteously, the way one maintains a fence around a field — visible, well-kept, unremarkable. The courtesy was the leash. He had explained this to no one, because explaining it would have weakened it.

Now the leash had carried her upriver, directly into the one place on earth Ansel needed eyes and could not trust his own.

He turned to the window. The rain had thickened. Down in the yard, the floodlamp caught the *Corvus* at her mooring — a gunboat in the honest sense of the word, armored wheelhouse, forward mount, a hull designed to argue with rivers. She rode the black water impatiently, straining at her lines like a dog that smells the hunt before the hunter does.

Nine days, and she would go up that river, and whatever was in that valley would come down it in her hold: data, witnesses, or silence. Ansel had learned long ago that all three were acceptable outcomes, provided you were the one holding the manifest.

* * *

There was a visitor that night, though Ansel would not have called him that. Visitors were announced. This man simply appeared at the outer office at half past eight, dripping, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a subordinate delivering news he hoped would be received as reassurance.

His name was Corben. Legal department. The syndicate's memory, in the flesh — a heavy, careful man who quoted statutes the way priests quoted scripture, selectively and with total conviction.

"Mr. Ansel. Thought you'd want this tonight rather than morning." He laid a folder on the desk. "Registry clerk sent word down the wire. There's been an inquiry. Informal. Someone asking after filing procedures for collective water claims."

Ansel opened the folder. Inside, a single telegram, its language bureaucratic and therefore terrified: *RECEIVED VERBAL INQUIRY RE COUNTER-CLAIM PROCEDURES DELTA SETTLEMENTS STOP ADVISE.*

"Who asked?"

"Clerk wouldn't say. Said a river family. Secondhand." Corben shifted his weight. "Could be nothing. Could be fishing."

"It's never fishing," Ansel said. "Downriver, everything is nets." He read the telegram again. Collective claims required original documentation. Original documentation required surveys. Surveys of sector seven existed in exactly three places: the syndicate's vault, Okonkwo's instruments, and — if old Ishikawa had been as thorough in death as he'd been in life — somewhere along the river his daughter now floated on.

Ansel closed the folder. "File a response. Standard language. No counter-claim may be lodged without certified originals, chain of custody, registered surveyor's stamp." He paused. "Add a line about penalties for filing fraudulent documentation. Make the penalties vivid. Clerks respond to vividness."

"And if they produce originals?"

"Then we will know where the originals came from," Ansel said, "and knowing is most of winning." He handed the folder back. "Thank you, Corben. Go home dry."

When the man had gone, Ansel stood at the map again. The valley. The glacier. The buried water — millions of liters moving under stone, cold and patient, feeding every settlement from the delta to the coast whether anyone held title to it or not. The syndicate's entire position rested on a simple proposition: that ownership could be established faster than community. That paper beat memory. That a claim filed in an office outweighed a need lived with daily for generations.

Ansel believed this. It was not cynicism; it was observation. Paper always won, eventually — not because paper was stronger, but because paper never slept, never flooded, never starved, and could be copied.

But paper needed couriers. And couriers, lately, kept going quiet.

* * *

He slept badly, which was rare, and woke before dawn, which was usual. The house he kept near the compound was spare — a bed, books, a window facing the river. He ate breakfast standing. He had never understood men who lingered over meals; food was provisioning, and provisioning was logistics, and logistics was war conducted politely.

By seven he was back in the office. The overnight traffic log waited on his desk. One entry mattered:

*0407 HRS — PARTIAL REPEAT FROM RELAY GORGE — SOURCE UNCERTAIN — TEXT GARBLED — ARCHIVED.*

Ferro had flagged it. Ansel pulled the raw tape and had it played twice. Beneath the static, a voice — Reyes's voice, he was nearly certain, though compressed and broken — saying words that resolved only partially:

*...complication... subject... do not... repeat, do not...*

Then the carrier folded and did not return.

Ansel sat very still for a long moment. Outside, the rain had stopped; the light coming through the window had that washed, rinsed quality of air scrubbed by storm. Somewhere a bird was making an idiot of itself in the wet trees.

*Do not.* Do not what? Transmit? Trust? Proceed? A truncated order was worse than no order — it was a mirror, and every man who heard it saw his own fear in it.

He pressed the intercom. "Ferro. Two things. First, keep the gorge relay manned around the clock — wake shifts, I don't care how. Second, signal the *Corvus.* Move her departure up forty-eight hours. She'll stage at the lower anchorage and wait there instead of here. When the window opens, I want zero transit time between the order and the gorge mouth."

"Sir, the crew—"

"Will be compensated. Add it to my discretionary account." He released the switch, then considered, and pressed it again. "One more item. Prepare the aircraft charter file. Not to dispatch — just to have current. Pilot options, range figures, weather minimums. If the river fails me, I'll take the sky."

Ferro's voice came back carefully neutral. "You're expecting complications, sir."

Ansel looked at the map. At the valley, crease-scarred, patient. At the penciled number in the corner — nine days, now seven.

"I'm expecting arithmetic," he said. "Everything else is commentary."

* * *

That afternoon he walked the compound, as he did most days, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back. The mechanics nodded. The clerks straightened. Men gave way to him slightly, unconsciously, the way water gives way to a hull — and Ansel noted, with the detached satisfaction of long practice, that this deference cost him nothing and purchased everything.

At the mooring, the *Corvus* took on her last stores. Her skipper, a leathery man called Dray, met him at the gangway with a report he'd clearly rehearsed.

"We'll be ready to make way on your word, Mr. Ansel."

"You'll make way on the schedule, Dray. Stage at the lower anchorage tonight. Hold there. When the ice report confirms the gorge is open, you go in at full steam, and you don't stop for anything floating."

"Anything?"

"Bodies, boats, or appeals to your humanity. The river will be full of all three this season." Ansel looked up at the armored wheelhouse, at the forward mount under its tarpaulin. "Dray. Do you know what's up that valley?"

"Water, they say."

"Everyone says water." Ansel smiled — a real smile, rare, reserved for moments when the truth and the advantage happened to align. "What's up that valley is the only fresh water between here and the coast, and in eleven days the law will say it belongs to whoever holds the best documents. We are going to be the ones holding them. Whatever it costs."

Dray said nothing. Good men under pressure rarely did; they saved their words for the moment they'd be needed, which was a quality Ansel respected and intended to use.

He walked back along the quay as the light went copper over the water. Behind him, the *Corvus* cast off her lines, engines turning over with a sound like a large animal clearing its throat, and slid out into the current, downriver first, toward the staging anchorage — patient, armed, and timed to the mountain.

Ansel stood and watched until she was gone.

Seven days. The clock on his mantel ticked. The glacier, far upstream and utterly indifferent to him, groaned through its slow calendar, preparing to open a door that stayed shut eleven months of the year.

He intended to be standing in the doorway when it did.

In his breast pocket, against his heart out of pure theater he permitted no one to see, he carried the original ledger page — the one with Ishikawa's name on it, the debt that had outlived its debtor and grown teeth. He touched it once, lightly, the way a gambler touches a card he hasn't looked at yet.

Then he went back inside, out of the wet, and began drafting the orders that would follow the gunboat upriver like a shadow with a signature.
