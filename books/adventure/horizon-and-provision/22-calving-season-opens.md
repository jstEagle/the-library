## 22. Calving Season Opens

The glacier spoke first.

Not a word — nothing so kind. A groan, deep and structural, the sound a house makes when it settles, if the house were made of compressed centuries and the settling were measured in megatons. Ulyana heard it through the rock of the relay station before she heard it with her ears, a vibration in the floorboards, in the tin cups, in her own back teeth. She was mending a net by the window and the net went still in her hands as though the whole valley had drawn one breath and was holding it.

Sable looked up from the card game she'd been losing to Okonkwo for an hour. "There it is."

"There what is?" Okonkwo asked, but he already knew. He'd been predicting this for three days, watching the meltwater rise in the gauge he'd rigged from a fuel drum and a notched stick. The water had come up eleven centimeters since yesterday. The river had turned from green to gray, thick with flour — rock ground fine beneath the ice — and it moved now with a purpose it hadn't had all the weeks they'd waited.

"The calving," Sable said. She set down her cards. "It's starting."

Ulyana put down the net. Outside, the rain had finally thinned to a drifting mist after twenty-odd days of the monsoon's full attention, and through that mist the head of the glacier was visible at the far end of the lake — a wall of blue-white ice two hundred meters high, dirty at the base, cracked into towers and seracs that looked from here like a city skyline built by something with no interest in human architecture. For weeks it had been silent. Dams do that. They hold.

Now the dam was opening its hands.

The first collapse came an hour later, and no description Ulyana had ever read prepared her for the sound. It wasn't thunder. Thunder comes from above, from weather, from something indifferent. This came from across the water and it came *for* them, a crack like the sky splitting along a seam, then a roar that went on and on, thirty seconds, forty, while a section of the ice face the size of a church sheared away and fell into the lake. The wave it threw traveled the length of the water in under a minute and slapped against the shingle below the station hard enough to shift driftwood the size of rowboats. The station windows rattled. Okonkwo's instruments jumped on their shelf.

When it was over, the three of them stood at the door and watched the bergs spread out across the lake, white and patient, some of them taller than the plane Ulyana had left behind at the syndicate strip. The water around them steamed faintly where the ice chilled the air.

"Ten days," Okonkwo said quietly. "Maybe twelve. After that the gorge chokes."

"You're sure?"

He pointed with his chin at the glacier face. "See how it's retreating? Every summer it pulls back, and every summer the melt cuts the channel deeper for a few weeks before the new icefall dams it again. That channel is our road. It's open now." He turned. "It will not be open long."

* * *

They spent the evening doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic was ugly.

Okonkwo laid it out on the station table with his maps weighted down by tin cups: the gorge ran eleven kilometers between the glacier's flanking walls, narrow in places to less than thirty meters, and the current through it during calving season ran fast enough to sweep a loaded raft sideways into the ice. The raft of salvaged drums they'd ridden downstream from the cave camp was still lashed below the station, but it was built for the lower river, not for water that moved like a stampede.

"The logs go first," Ulyana said. "Whatever happens to us, the logs go first."

"They don't float," Sable said.

"I know they don't float. I'm saying the priority—"

"I know what you're saying." Sable's voice was flat, and Ulyana heard the old argument underneath it, the one they'd been circling since the cliff fissure: *burn them, bury them, knowledge this dangerous gets people killed.* Sable hadn't raised it in days. She didn't need to. It lived in the pauses now.

"We take them together or not at all," Ulyana said. "That's the whole point of them. Proof doesn't mean anything if there's nobody left to carry it."

Okonkwo cleared his throat — the diplomat's cough, the one he used when two people needed to stop looking at each other. "Our real problem isn't the ice. It's the gunboat."

Nobody said anything. They'd seen the smoke once, days ago, far downlake — a smudge on the horizon that might have been a campfire and almost certainly wasn't. Reyes had reached the syndicate. Whatever garbled report he'd transmitted from wherever he'd ended up, Ansel had received enough of it to act. Men like Ansel didn't send gunboats for missing surveyors. They sent them for paper.

"He'll wait for the channel too," Okonkwo said. "He has to. No boat comes through that gorge before calving opens it, and none comes after it closes. Which means we have the same ten days he does, and we have a head start of—" he calculated, lips moving, "—two days, maybe three, depending on where he's staging."

"Then we leave tomorrow," Ulyana said. "First light."

"And go where?" Sable asked. "Downriver? To the delta? You want to run eleven kilometers of gorge on a drum raft carrying a hundred kilos of paper, then what — paddle to the settlements and hope the river families remember your father fondly enough to hide us?"

"Yes," Ulyana said.

Sable stared at her.

"That's the plan," Ulyana said. "That's the entire plan. It's a bad plan. I've flown cargo on worse."

Something moved across Sable's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if it could afford one. "You're serious."

"I'm always serious about bad plans. It's the good ones I doubt."

* * *

They worked until dark and past it, by lamplight, because there was no more time to be careful.

The logs came out of their oilcloth wrapping for the last inspection — her father's flight books, four decades of them, pages dense with his small square handwriting and the survey stamps that gave them legal weight, plus the folded charts and the falsified syndicate surveys he'd annotated in red. Ulyana had carried them so long now that their weight had become part of her body's map of itself. Tonight she rewrapped each volume in fresh oilcloth, sealed the seams with pitch from the station stores, and packed them into a waterproof duffel that had once held flare cartridges.

Okonkwo broke down his instruments and data into duplicate sets — one to travel with them, one wrapped in canvas and cached high on the valley wall behind the station, marked only by a cairn he built with stones from the shingle. "If we lose everything else," he said, "the numbers survive. Numbers are patient."

Sable did the raft. She knew boats in a way she'd never explained and Ulyana had never asked; she re-lashed the drums with wire and stripped rope from the station's mooring lines, built a deck frame from salvaged planking, rigged a steering oar and two paddles and a mast step for a improvised sail of stitched tarpaulin. By lamplight her hands moved with a certainty that made Ulyana watch longer than was useful.

"You've done this before," Ulyana said.

"Everyone's done everything before," Sable said, not looking up. "It's just that most of us lie about which things."

* * *

She woke before dawn without needing to. Some part of her had been standing watch all night, the way it had stood watch through her father's debt, through the monsoon, through every year of flying other people's cargoes over other people's rivers. She dressed in the dark, checked the duffel straps twice, and stepped outside into air so cold and clean it felt newly minted.

The glacier was roaring.

Not continuously — in bursts, in salvos, like artillery from a battery that never quite ran dry. Crack, boom, the long grinding roar, the wave. Crack, boom. Between the reports the silence rang. Mist stood off the ice face in plumes, and the morning light coming down the valley turned those plumes gold, and it would have been beautiful if beauty were the right category for it. It was more like witnessing. Like being present at something that did not care whether you approved.

Okonkwo joined her, breath smoking. He looked better than he had any right to — the wound in his side long closed now, though he still favored it climbing. He stood beside her and listened to the glacier work.

"My grandmother had a saying," he said. "*The river keeps the time.* Downriver we say it when the floods come early. Up here I think it means something else." He nodded at the ice. "That's the clock. Not Ansel. Not the registry deadline. That. Everything else is just weather."

Ulyana found Sable at the raft, making a final adjustment to the steering oar's lashings, checking them with a violence that suggested she'd checked them four times already. She didn't look up when Ulyana stopped at the edge of the shingle.

"If you've changed your mind," Sable said, "say it now. Once we're in the gorge there's no walking out. There's barely swimming out."

"I haven't changed my mind."

"You should know I lied to Meridian for six months before I walked. I'm good at lying. If I tell you this raft will hold, that could be one of the good ones."

"Is it?"

Sable pulled the last lashing tight and stood. She looked at the raft — ugly, overloaded, riding low — and then at Ulyana, and whatever passed across her face she let stay there, unhidden, which from Sable was worth more than testimony.

"It'll hold," she said. "I built it to hold."

Ulyana believed her. That was the strange thing, the thing she turned over as they loaded the duffel amidships and ballasted it with the instrument cases and pushed off into gray, flour-thick water that pulled at the hull like a living hand. Somewhere in the last weeks — she couldn't name the day, couldn't find the seam — she had stopped auditing Sable's words and started trusting them, and trust, for a woman who had spent her adult life being invisible on purpose, felt less like relief than like exposure. Like standing in an open field.

The current took them.

The mouth of the gorge opened ahead like a gate — granite walls rising sheer on both sides, the river compressed between them into a single muscular channel of gray water, and the sound coming out of it was the sound of the whole valley draining. Sable took the steering oar. Okonkwo braced the duffel. Ulyana knelt forward with a paddle and did the only thing she had ever truly been good at, the thing her father had put in her hands before he put anything else: reading moving water.

Behind them, uplake, another section of the ice face let go. The crack rolled down the gorge like a door slamming in a very large house, and the first push of the displacement wave came surging up the channel beneath them, lifting the raft, accelerating it, hurling them through the gate and into the gorge itself, where the walls closed overhead and the light went pewter-gray and the clock — audible now, unmistakable, a detonation every few minutes echoing off stone — began counting down the only days that mattered.

Ten days. Maybe fewer.

Ulyana dug her paddle in and steered for the tongue of smooth water on the left, where her father's old notes said the current ran kindest, and the gorge swallowed the sound of the glacier behind them and gave back only its own noise: water, stone, and the thin high whistle of wind through a crack in the world.

Somewhere downriver, a gunboat was waiting for the same gate to open.

She paddled.
