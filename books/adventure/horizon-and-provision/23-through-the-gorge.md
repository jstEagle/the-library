## 23. Through the Gorge

The ice told us before the sky did.

We'd been camped at the gorge mouth for two days, listening to it — a deep, irregular groaning, like a giant shifting in its sleep, like something structural giving way somewhere far above the tree line. Sable said that was the sound of a glacier deciding. Okonkwo said that was unscientific. Then he lay awake all night listening to it, and in the morning he said, "We go today. While it's deciding."

The raft was three drums lashed with aircraft cable and a deck of salvaged planking, ugly as sin and heavier than it looked, which was the whole point — weight was ballast, ballast was stability, and we were going to need both. We loaded it in the grey half-light: the waterproof duffel with the flight logs wrapped in three layers of oilcloth, the medical kit, the last of the food, the paddles we'd cut from green timber and shaped with Okonkwo's knife. Sable rigged the tarp as a spray hood. I checked the lashings twice, then a third time, and Okonkwo watched me do it without comment, which was its own kind of comment.

"Ulyana," he said finally. "You've checked it."

"I know."

"You've checked it three times."

"I know that too."

He let it go. He was good at that — knowing when a person's fear needed work more than it needed comfort. Mine needed something to do with its hands.

The logs sat in the center of the raft where nothing could fall on them. I'd put them there myself. I told myself it was because the center was the driest place, the safest place, the place least likely to take water if we shipped a wave over the gunwales. That was even true. It just wasn't the whole truth, and I knew it wasn't, and I put them there anyway.

Sable crouched at the bow, reading the water the way I read weather — the way the current braided around the first rocks, where the eddy lines curled, where the river went glassy and flat over something deep and hungry. She'd run this water once before, coming out. Once, in daylight, in a proper boat, with a full crew.

"How bad?" I asked her.

She was quiet a moment. "The first three kilometers are fast but honest. The river tells you what it's doing. After the second bend it narrows, and that's where the calving face comes down to the water on the left side." She looked up at me. "That's where Meridian lost their boat."

"Lost it how?"

"Wave." She said it flat. "Not the ice itself. The wave. The ice doesn't have to touch you. It just has to fall in the water near you."

* * *

We pushed off at first light, and for the first hour I almost let myself believe it would be easy.

The river ran high with melt, brown-green and muscular, but the current was smooth, the channels wide, and the drums rode the water with a low, confident hiss. Sable called the lines from the bow — "left of the rock, hard left, now ease" — and I steered from the stern with a paddle that was too short and a sweep oar that was too long, and between the two of them we threaded it. Okonkwo sat amidships with the logs in his lap, bailing with a cut-down fuel can whenever we took spray, and he did it with the methodical calm of a man taking measurements, which, knowing him, he probably was.

The gorge walls rose on either side. Not gradually — they just went up, grey granite streaked with old waterlines, close enough in places that I could have thrown a line to the rock. The air coming down off the ice was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season, a cold that fell straight from the glacier tongue visible now between the peaks ahead, blue-white and ancient, hanging over the river like a held breath.

And the sound. I'd been listening to it for two days and I still wasn't ready for it up close. Every few minutes the groan came again, deeper now, felt in the drums under my feet more than heard, and between the groans there were smaller sounds — cracks, ticks, the occasional sharp report like a rifle going off somewhere high in the ice. Okonkwo flinched at each one. He tried to hide it. He was bad at it.

"They're just settling," Sable called back. "The big failures happen at the waterline. Stay away from the waterline."

"The waterline," Okonkwo said, "is where the water is."

"Yes," Sable said.

* * *

The second bend announced itself before we saw it — a change in the sound of the river, the smooth hiss breaking up into something rougher, and then the bend itself, and then the narrows.

The channel pinched to perhaps thirty meters between a rock shelf on the right and — there it was — the calving face on the left. It came down to the water like a wall built by something with no interest in human architecture: sheer, fractured, glowing that deep glacial blue in the crevasses, streaked grey with moraine. It was perhaps forty meters high where it met the river, and it was not still. Even as I looked, a seam near the waterline spat a chunk of ice the size of a truck into the current, and the splash reached us as a lazy swell, four seconds later, rocking the drums.

"That's small," Sable said. "That's it shrugging."

"Can we go wide?" I asked. "Right side?"

"Right side has the shelf. Water's shallow, rocks under the surface." She pointed with her paddle. "We hug the face. Fast as we can through the fall zone. The face is most active where the crevasse meets the water — see the blue seam? We pass it at speed, we're in the shadow of it maybe twenty seconds."

"Twenty seconds," Okonkwo repeated.

"At speed," Sable said.

I put the sweep oar in the water and we went.

The current took us and the narrows took the current and suddenly the river was moving faster than I'd ever run water in my life — the banks blurring, spray coming off the wave trains, the drums groaning against their lashings as they surfed down the channel. The calving face slid past on my left, close enough that the cold rolling off it raised the hair on my arms, and I was watching it, I couldn't not watch it, the way you can't not watch a snake, and the blue seam was coming up, the crevasse like a wound in the ice, and I dug the sweep oar in and hauled and —

The glacier exhaled.

I will hear that sound for the rest of my life. Not a crack. Not a groan. A *report* — a detonation, flat and enormous, and the whole face moved. Not a chunk. A section. Ice the size of a house, the size of several houses, sheared off along that blue seam and went into the river all at once, and the river *disappeared*. Where the water had been there was a white eruption, a standing wave of spray and shattered ice climbing, climbing, and then the displacement wave came at us across the channel, a brown wall of water two meters high and moving faster than we were.

"BRACE — " Sable screamed, and the wave hit.

I've capsized before. I've been thrown from a plane on the ground and walked away. I have been in weather that tore the fabric off a wing. Nothing prepared me for the wave. The raft stood up on its edge — I remember the sky where the water should have been, the calving face swinging up into view like the whole world tipping — and then it came down and we were inside the wave, inside the roar, and the river had us.

The sweep oar was gone. I didn't see it go. One moment it was in my hands and the next the water had taken it the way the river takes everything, without malice, without effort. The drums were awash, the deck planks under brown water, and I was on my knees on the spray hood frame with my hands full of rope that had been a rigging line a moment ago. Somewhere Okonkwo was shouting — not words, just sound. Somewhere Sable was shouting back. The channel was full of ice, small bergs the size of tables bobbing in the turbulence, and one of them the size of a car was turning slowly in the eddy directly downstream of us, and we were going to hit it.

"LEFT!" Sable's voice, raw. "PADDLE LEFT OR WE HIT THE BERG —"

I found a paddle — someone's paddle, the green timber cold in my hands — and I dug with everything I had, and Okonkwo dug on the other side, and the raft yawed, sluggish, half-flooded, and the berg slid past close enough that I could have touched it, close enough that the meltwater streaming off it hit my face like a hand.

And then we were through. Past the seam, past the face, the channel opening, the current easing, the roar of the calving falling behind us into the ordinary thunder of rapids. The drums were riding low. Very low. We'd taken on half a ton of the river and she wasn't giving it back.

"Bail," I said. My voice came out in pieces. "Bail now, bail everything."

Okonkwo was already bailing. The cut-down fuel can went in and came out, went in and came out, brown water arcing over the side, and I got the spare paddle and paddled us toward the right bank where an eddy curled behind a rock spine, and we slid into stillness, and for a long moment nobody said anything.

That's when I counted heads and came up one short of where I'd filed everyone.

"Where's the logs," Okonkwo said.

They were gone. The duffel — the center of the raft, the safe place, the place I'd put them — was gone. The lashings had parted under the wave, or the water had simply reached in and taken them; either way, the river had the flight logs, my father's logs, the aquifer surveys, the falsified reports, everything we'd come through the gorge to carry, and it was carrying them downstream at four meters per second through water full of ice.

I was moving before I decided to move. I don't remember deciding. I remember the water hitting me like a door — cold past cold, the kind of cold that isn't temperature anymore but a fist closing around your chest — and the brown roar, and my hand closing on oilcloth.

* * *

Sable told me afterward what it looked like from the raft. I'm glad she waited, because at the time I experienced none of it as a story.

What I experienced: cold. The river pulling me the way it pulls everything, patient and total. My boots filling. The oilcloth strap wrapped twice around my fist, the duffel's weight dragging my arm straight, my shoulder screaming, my face downstream and the ice coming at me — a table-sized berg, then another — and me kicking sideways, gracelessly, the duffel swinging me like a pendulum on a hook. I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like someone else's voice in my head: *you cannot let go, and you cannot hold on, and one of those is going to kill you.*

I remember choosing. That's the part that stays. There was a moment — half a second, maybe less — when the strap started to tear out of my grip, finger by finger, and I could have let it. Every rational part of me was voting to let it. The water was death, the duffel was an anchor, the current was winning, and the vote was unanimous except for my hand.

My hand won.

Then there was a second hand. Sable's — I knew it by the grip, hard and sure, a climber's grip, closing on my collar, then my arm, and she was shouting something I couldn't hear over the river, and the raft was there, and Okonkwo was there, and between the three of them and the last of my own stubbornness they hauled me and the duffel over the gunwale and onto the deck, and I lay there on the planks coughing up the river while the cold shook me like a dog shakes a rag.

The duffel was in my hand the whole time. I never let go of it. I remember that too, with a shame that arrived later, fully grown, like a bill.

Because here is what Sable told me, later, in the flat voice she used for things that mattered: when the wave hit and the raft stood up, Okonkwo went in. He went in on the upstream side, in the fall zone, in water full of ice, a hydrologist in his fifties who could not have survived thirty seconds in that current. And Sable went in after him. She went in after him without a rope, without a plan, without hesitation — dove off the downstream rail into the calving turbulence of a gorge that had just eaten an expedition — and caught him by the pack straps as he surfaced, and held him against the raft while I, ten meters away and equally drowning, held a bag of paper.

She saved a man. I saved the logs. And I would do it again. That was the worst of it — lying there shaking on the deck, watching Okonkwo's lips go blue while Sable stripped his soaked outer layers and got the emergency blanket around him, I ran the moment back and checked my own choices the way I'd check a rigging line, three times, and all three times I found the same answer: I'd do it again. The paper was the settlements' water. The paper was thousands of people downriver who would never know this river existed. The paper was my father.

And Okonkwo was a man, shivering, alive because Sable was braver and faster than I was, and I hadn't even looked. The wave hit and I went for the bag and I never even *looked* to see who'd gone in.

"Fear shows you who you are," Sable said that evening, when we'd made camp on the gravel bar below the narrows and Okonkwo was wrapped in every dry thing we owned, drinking the broth I'd made from the last of the stock cubes. She said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "It doesn't make you anything. It just shows you."

"And what did it show you?" I asked.

She looked at the fire. "That I dive after people," she said. "Even when it's stupid. Especially when it's stupid." A pause. "Meridian taught me that. The part of it worth keeping."

Okonkwo, wrapped and steaming, raised his head. "For the record," he said, "I would like it noted that I was bailing at the time, and that the logs were in my lap, and that when the raft stood up I was given a choice between the bag and the rail, and I chose the rail, because I am a scientist and not an idiot, and because I knew — " he looked at me, and his eyes behind the wet hair were kind and terrible — "I knew you'd go for the bag. I knew it before you did. That's why I let go."

I didn't have anything to say to that. I still don't, mostly. What I did was sit down next to him and hold the broth where he could reach it, and after a while he drank it, and the fire cracked, and upriver the glacier went on deciding, its reports fading behind the bend like distant artillery from a war that had already passed us through.

* * *

We slept in shifts. I took first watch, which was a mercy, because I didn't want to be inside my own head with my eyes closed.

The gorge below the narrows ran quieter, the river spreading, the walls stepping back, and the stars came out hard and cold between the peaks. The duffel sat by my feet. I'd checked it — twice, then a third time — the oilcloth dry, the seals intact, the paper inside safe. My father's paper. My father's river. I put my boot on it and watched the water and tried to feel like someone worth what Sable had done, and couldn't, and decided that maybe the feeling wasn't the point. Maybe the point was just the next kilometer of river, and the one after that.

It was near dawn when I heard it.

Not the glacier. Not the river. Something mechanical, far downstream, around the bends we hadn't run yet — a low, rhythmic thudding under the water sound, an engine under load, laboring against the current.

Coming up.

I stood very still and listened until there was no doubt left, and then I woke Sable, and put my hand on her shoulder, and said the only thing that needed saying:

"Smoke on the water. We're not alone in the gorge."
