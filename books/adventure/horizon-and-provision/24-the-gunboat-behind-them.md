## 24. The Gunboat Behind Them

**Ulyana**

The sound came again at first light, and this time there was no mistaking it.

We stood on the shingle above the raft — three drums lashed to a frame of salvaged planking, the logs wrapped in oilcloth and strapped down like a patient — and watched the bend downstream where the river narrowed before the second icefall. The morning fog was lifting off the water in rags. Through one of the gaps in it, I saw it: a smudge of gray smoke, moving. Not drifting. Moving *upstream*.

"Nobody runs a boat up the gorge during calving," Sable said. She said it the way you'd say *nobody walks into a fire*, flat, factual, already past arguing with it.

"Somebody is," Okonkwo said.

I counted what I knew about the boat without seeing it. A gunboat draws too much water for the shallows below the narrows; Ansel's people would know that as well as I did. The current through the gorge runs eight knots where it funnels, twelve where it squeezes. Ice comes off those walls in blocks the size of houses, and when a block goes into the channel it doesn't make waves so much as it makes weather. Any pilot, any boatman, any sane human being looks at that gorge during calving season and says *wait ten days*. Ten days and the route opens clean and wide and you walk your cargo through like a Sunday.

Which meant whoever was driving that boat wasn't being sane. He was being arithmetic.

Ansel had run the numbers. I could almost see him running them, back in his lowland office, dry and warm, pen moving across paper. Value of claim: everything. Cost of a boat and crew: nothing he'd miss. Probability of losing the ledger's contents if he waited for the ice to open: high, because we'd be gone, filed, witnessed, safe behind law he couldn't buy. Probability of catching us in the gorge before the registry closed for the season: low but not zero.

Low but not zero was enough. It was always enough for men like him. That was the whole trick of them — they never gambled, they just spent things that weren't theirs. Money. Rivers. People.

"He'll lose the boat," Sable said. "Maybe the crew."

"He knows that." I picked up my pole. "That's why he sent it anyway."

Okonkwo's shoulder was still bound tight under his jacket, the wound three days old and angry-looking even through the dressing. He'd insisted on coming aboard, and I hadn't argued, because leaving him alone on the shingle with no boat and no radio was not a plan, it was a eulogy with extra steps. But I watched him lower himself onto the drum seat and I did the arithmetic too, mine: how far could we push him, how fast could we go, what would the rocking do to the fever that was starting again behind his eyes.

"We beat them through the narrows or we don't beat them at all," I said. "After the narrows the river braids out for six kilometers. Shallows, channels, a hundred places to hide a raft and lose a gunboat. Before the narrows, we're meat on a stick."

"The narrows are two hours downstream," Sable said. "If they're making four knots against current—"

"They're not making four knots. They're making whatever speed gets them here." I pushed off. "Ansel didn't send a boat. He sent a schedule."

The raft answered the current the way she always did, sullen and true, and we went downstream toward the sound of the engine, racing the thing that was chasing us toward the only ground where the race could still be won.

* * *

**Ansel**

The office had been quiet since Reyes's transmission came in — half of it static, half of it panic, all of it useless except for the coordinates and the single word *logs*. Voss had found her father's cache. Of course she had. The compass. He should have taken the compass from her years ago, should have taken everything, should have understood that sentiment left unconfiscated always compounds.

He stood at the window of the relay station annex and watched the river come down out of the hills, brown and swollen with melt, carrying ice the way a dog carries sticks — proudly, destructively.

"You understand the gorge is closed," said the boatmaster, a broad man named Ferro who had run this stretch for eleven years and had the missing fingers to prove it. "Calving season. I've seen it take a barge whole."

"I have read your report on the gorge, Mr. Ferro. Twice."

"Then you know—"

"What I know is that in nine days a territorial clerk files a counter-claim with original survey stamps, and every drop of water in this valley stops belonging to us. What I know is that the expedition cost more than your boat, your crew, and your career put together, and it returned nothing because a surveyor grew a conscience and a pilot grew an opinion." Ansel turned from the window. "What I know, Mr. Ferro, is that you are going upstream through the gorge today."

Ferro looked at him for a long moment. Men usually looked away first. This one didn't, which was interesting, and irrelevant.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I pay the next man double, and I note in your file that you refused a direct contract in the field, and your file follows you the way files follow men in this business. You have a daughter in school in the capital, I believe."

It wasn't a threat. Threats were crude and created martyrs. It was simply information, offered the way you'd offer weather. Ferro took it the same way, jaw working once, twice, and then he spat over the rail into the current and went to wake his crew.

Ansel watched the gunboat pull out — forty feet of riveted steel, a forward mount, six men, and enough fuel to make the passage if the passage allowed fuel to matter. He had done the calculation honestly, which was more than most men managed. If the boat came through, he recovered the logs and ended three problems permanently. If the boat didn't come through, he lost equipment he'd written down years ago and men whose names he would learn only if it became necessary to write letters about them.

Either way, the claim survived or died on what happened in the gorge in the next thirty-six hours, and Ansel had never in his life left an outcome like that to other people's caution.

He opened his ledger — the real one, the private one — and made an entry. *Gorge transit attempted. Asset recovery probable.* Then, after a moment, in the smaller hand he used for truths: *Probable.*

**Ulyana**

By midmorning we could hear them clearly whenever the wind died — that laboring diesel knock, gaining, gaining, patient as tide. The gorge walls rose on either side, green-black rock streaked white with old ice scars, and between them the river ran fast and glassy and full of floating shards that ticked against the drums like fingernails.

Sable navigated from the front, reading the water the way I read sky. "Fork ahead. Left channel's deeper but it bends toward the icefall. Right is shallow but straight."

"Straight," I said. "Shallow we can feel our way through. Icefall we can't argue with."

We took the right channel and scraped twice, a grinding shudder through the frame that made Okonkwo close his eyes and breathe through it, and then we were through and the channel merged back and the engine sound was louder, and I made myself stop listening to it and start listening to the glacier instead.

That was the thing nobody who hadn't flown this country understood: the glacier talked. All day, all night, cracks and groans and the occasional deep boom like a door closing in a very large house. And when a wall was getting ready to calve, it got *chatty* first — a rising creak, a series of sharp reports, the ice telling you what it intended to do if you were polite enough to listen.

The left-hand wall was talking now. Loudly.

"Faster," Sable said.

"I'm poling a raft of fuel drums. Faster is a rumor."

She glanced back at me, and for a second — the first time since the cave camp — something almost like humor crossed her face, exhausted and crooked. "You sound like me lying to Meridian."

"I'm not lying. I'm negotiating."

Behind us, around the last bend, the smoke showed again, closer, and beneath the diesel I heard something new: voices. Shouting, faint, in the flat cadence of men being ordered to do something they didn't want to do. Ferro's crew, maybe, or what was left of their enthusiasm.

Then the glacier stopped talking and started screaming.

I've heard calving my whole life. I'd never heard it directly overhead. The whole left wall let go at once — not a block, a *face*, hundreds of tons of blue-white ice peeling off the rock like bark — and the sound wasn't a crash, it was a detonation, and then the river stood up.

I drove the raft hard right, into the shallows, poles down, both of us leaning into it, and the wave hit us broadside and lifted the whole frame like a toy and slammed it down and we shipped half the river and kept moving, which was the only prayer I had time for. When the water settled, the channel behind us was gone. Filled end to end with brash ice, churning, impassable — for a day, maybe two.

The diesel sound kept coming. Around the ice. Somehow.

They'd find a way through or die trying, because somewhere behind that wheel was a man doing arithmetic, and arithmetic doesn't hear the glacier scream.

"Narrows," Sable said quietly, pointing. Ahead the walls pinched together, fifty meters of water between cliffs, the current through it smooth and terrifying, like a throat.

"If we're going to do something about that boat," I said, "we do it there."

**Ansel**

At noon the relay station picked up Ferro's scheduled burst. Position, speed, condition. Condition: *one man injured by falling ice, non-critical. Hull intact. Proceeding.*

Ansel read it twice and felt the small cold satisfaction he permitted himself instead of relief. The gorge was passable. He had known it would be — known it the way he knew most things, by refusing to accept the alternative — and now it was proven, and the proof had cost one bruised crewman and some paint.

He drafted the reply himself. *Narrows within reach of target. Do not board. Do not fire unless fired upon. Recover documents intact. Persons secondary.*

*Persons secondary.* He weighed the phrase as he wrote it, turned it over like a coin. It was accurate. It was also, he supposed, the kind of sentence that a different sort of man would flinch from writing. Ansel had stopped flinching at sentences decades ago; that was what the sentences had been for. Every one of them was a small payment against the large debt of the syndicate's position, and debts, unlike people, could always be refinanced.

He sealed the transmission and stood again at the window. Somewhere upriver, in a gorge he would never see, a woman who flew medicine to settlements that didn't appear on maps was poling a raft of fuel drums toward a fight she couldn't win with poles.

He felt nothing about that, he told himself, which was nearly true, and in Ansel's accounting nearly true had always been sufficient.

* * *

**Ulyana**

We ran the narrows at midafternoon, and it was the worst two minutes of my life on that river, and I have landed a plane on a sandbar in a crosswind with a bleeding passenger, so I keep a competitive list.

The current took us the moment we entered, and after that we weren't steering, we were choosing which mistake to make slightly less. The walls blurred. The water made a sound like the whole world tearing cloth. Sable lay flat with her arms around the logs, and Okonkwo braced against the drum with his good arm, and I poled until the pole snapped and then just held on, and then it was over and we shot out the bottom side into the braided water beyond, spinning, drenched, alive.

And behind us, growing, the diesel.

But now I could see them. Around the upper bend, wedging through the ice-choked channel with their bow wave standing up white — a steel boat, gun mount forward, six figures aboard, one of them holding a rifle across his chest not aiming it, just holding it, the way you hold a tool you've been told you might need.

Fifty meters of narrows between us and them. Six kilometers of braids ahead of us.

Sable stood up in the swaying raft, soaked to the skin, watching the boat come on, and I saw her do the same math I was doing, and I saw her arrive somewhere I hadn't yet.

"There's scree on the south wall above the narrows," she said. "Loose. A lot of it. I surveyed that bank for Meridian — the freeze-thaw has been eating it apart for years. It wouldn't take much."

"How much is 'much'?"

Her eyes came to mine, gray and steady and asking permission without asking it.

"It would take us," she said. "Climbing. Above it. With hands that haven't stopped shaking since the cave."

Okonkwo spoke from the drum seat, fever-bright and perfectly clear. "The narrows are the only place the river forces them into single file. If the narrows close, they turn back or they beach, and either way we're gone into the braids. If the narrows stay open—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

I looked back once at the gunboat, at the smoke, at the small stubborn machine of other people's money coming up the throat of the river to collect us, and I thought about my father running this same water ahead of men exactly like these, and I thought: *he never outran them. He outthought them, or he hid, or he died.*

I picked up the broken pole and turned the raft toward the south bank.

"Then we'd better climb fast," I said. "Because that boat isn't slowing down, and neither is the ice."

Above the narrows, the scree field waited — acres of it, silent, loose as sand, holding its breath the way the whole valley seemed to hold its breath when the glacier spoke.

We started up.
