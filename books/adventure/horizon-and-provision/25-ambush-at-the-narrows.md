## 25. Ambush at the Narrows

The narrows announced themselves an hour before we saw them, the way bad places do. The river quit its lazy braiding and funneled between walls of gray granite, and the sound changed — water going from a murmur to a conversation to an argument. I'd been through here with Meridian in the spring, when the channel was wide enough for two boats abreast and the current was a rumor instead of a threat. Now, in calving season, the river had climbed six meters up the rock and turned mean.

I stood at the bow of our raft of salvaged drums and watched Ulyana read the water. She did it with her whole body — shoulders tilting before her hands moved, weight shifting on the sweep oar like she was dancing with something that wanted to lead. Behind her, Okonkwo sat among the fuel drums and the oilcloth-wrapped bundle of flight logs, one hand resting on the logs the way other men rest a hand on a sleeping child.

Behind us, faint but persistent as a headache, came the sound of the gunboat's engine.

"They'll reach the gorge mouth by dark," Reyes had told them before they marooned him, and he'd been right about everything else so far. Ansel didn't send boats; he sent arguments. This one was diesel-powered and carried men who were paid by the outcome.

"We can't outrun it," Ulyana said. She wasn't asking me. She was confirming what we both already knew, because saying it out loud made it a fact you could work against instead of a fear you worked under.

"No," I agreed. "But we don't have to outrun it. We have to make the river wider than it is."

Okonkwo looked up from his maps. "Meaning?"

"Meaning there's a place ahead where the canyon pinches down to maybe thirty meters across, with a talus slope on the north wall — old rockfall, loose scree, sitting right above the channel. Meridian's geologists flagged it as unstable in their first survey. Nobody ever went back to check." I let that sit. "We go back to check."

Ulyana's oar stroke didn't falter. "You want to bring a hillside down into the river."

"I want to *invite* it," I said. "There's a difference. A polite difference."

* * *

We put ashore two kilometers above the pinch point, on a gravel shelf that Meridian had used as a cache site once upon a more innocent expedition. The rain had eased to a fine drifting mist, which was the monsoon's version of mercy — it never stopped, it just occasionally pretended to.

I led them up the goat track I remembered, switchbacking through alder scrub gone wild, until we stood on the shoulder of the talus slope itself. It was worse than I'd described, which was good. Thousands of tons of shattered granite, frost-cracked over centuries, stacked at an angle that gravity had been patiently auditing for decades. All it needed was an auditor with a pry bar.

"Here's the arithmetic," I said, crouching to show them the seam I'd chosen. "The gunboat draws too much water to run the rapids below the narrows even if the channel is clear. But if the channel is clear, they can anchor above it, land men, and come after us on foot along the bank trail. That takes them a day, maybe less. If the channel is *blocked*, they have to turn around, run back down the gorge against the current, find another route — three days minimum, probably four. By then the upper gorge starts icing and their own boat becomes a liability."

"And if it doesn't block?" Okonkwo asked. He always asked the question everyone else was thinking and nobody wanted voiced.

"Then we've made noise for nothing and we're still running." I shrugged. "I've done stupider things for worse reasons. Ask anyone from Meridian."

Ulyana hadn't spoken since the shelf. She stood looking down at the talus with her arms crossed, wind pushing her hair sideways, and I recognized the expression because I'd worn it myself standing in front of Meridian's data, deciding whether to walk away from my own name. It was the face of someone doing math with parts of themselves.

"The rocks won't care about our reasons," she said finally. "If any of us is on that slope when it goes—"

"Nobody will be on the slope when it goes. That's the entire discipline of it." I met her eyes. "I've rigged collapses before. Mining country. You learn or you don't survive the learning."

Something passed over her face — not quite belief, but the decision to act as if belief were available. "Show me where you need hands."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon in the failing light, and I'll say this for both of them: neither treated me like a woman describing lunacy. Okonkwo pried at seams with a wrecking bar salvaged from the boat kit, opening channels in the scree the way a surgeon opens a line of approach — methodical, unhurried, asking questions about load paths that told me he understood more about physical systems than his hydrologist's title suggested. Ulyana hauled rocks. Not arranging them, not fussing — hauling, moving tonnage up the slope with a patience that made me revise my estimate of her again. There are people who are strong the way machinery is strong, and people who are strong the way rivers are strong, and she was the second kind.

The rigging itself was simple in concept and unforgiving in execution. A key stone — a flat slab the size of a door — wedged at the head of the slope, holding back perhaps two hundred tons of looser material behind it. Underneath it, packed into the seam, we set the drums. Not full ones — we'd emptied three during the raft build — but each charged with a cup of the fuel we could not afford to spare, soaked rags for wicks, fuses made from waxed cord measured out in arm-lengths.

"Two hundred meters of burn time per length," I said, tying off the last of them. "We light all three, we walk, we don't run. Running means tripping, and tripping means being on this slope when it decides to become a valley floor."

"And the timing?" Okonkwo asked.

"We light it when we hear the engine close. Not before. If we drop the slide early, they stop, assess, and find another way around while we're still in sight. We drop it as they commit to the narrows — when they're inside the pinch and can't reverse easily. Then the rock comes down ahead of them, behind them doesn't matter, and their choice is made for them."

Ulyana crouched beside the nearest fuse, testing the cord between her fingers. "How long have you known you'd be doing something like this?"

I thought about lying. I was, by my own honest accounting, a very good liar — that had been my function on Meridian, the surveyor whose maps said what the syndicate needed them to say, whose reports smoothed over what the syndicate needed smoothed. Lying was my trade the way flying was hers.

"Since the day I realized maps are weapons," I said. "Somebody has to draw the line where the fight happens. Might as well be someone who knows the ground."

She looked at me a moment longer than the answer required. Then she nodded and went back to checking knots.

* * *

Dusk came down the canyon like water filling a basin. We ate cold rice and dried fish on the gravel shelf below, watching the narrows through a gap in the alders, and the waiting did what waiting does — it got inside your clothes, your joints, your thoughts. Okonkwo cleaned his glasses twice. Ulyana coiled and uncoiled a length of rope with hands that needed occupation. I sat still, because stillness was the only skill I'd ever fully mastered, and listened.

The engine sound grew teeth around full dark.

It came up the gorge the way I'd predicted — steady, unhesitating, the sound of an institution that had never once been asked to hurry. Searchlight beams began strobing off the canyon walls upstream, sweeping the water, the banks, the cliff faces. We killed our small fire an hour earlier and pressed back into the alder shadow, and I felt rather than saw Ulyana go motionless beside me — a different quality of stillness than mine, hers the stillness of prey that has decided to be mistaken for stone.

The beam swept past us twice. Held. Moved on.

"They're running the narrows tonight," I breathed. "They think we're hours ahead, panicking. They're not expecting the ground itself to file a complaint."

The searchlight found the pinch point and stopped there, playing across the far wall, measuring. A voice crackled over a loudhailer, words shredded by echo, but the tone was unmistakable: confidence, orders, the flat institutional certainty of men who had been promised an easy night.

"Now," I whispered. "Walk, don't run."

We went up the goat track single file, Okonkwo first with the lantern shuttered to a coin of light, then Ulyana, then me with the matches. Two hundred meters of climbing in the dark, feet finding the path by memory and faith, the engine note swelling below us like a tide coming in. At the head of the slope I knelt at the first fuse, struck the match, touched flame to waxed cord.

It caught. Orange spark crawling away into the dark, patient as anything.

Second fuse. Third. Three orange threads stitching downslope toward the key stone, and then we turned and walked — walked, I tell you, though every nerve in my body screamed otherwise — back down the track as fast as walking allows, which is not fast enough, which is never fast enough.

We were fifty meters from the slope when the world ended politely.

Not an explosion, exactly. A *detonation* of silence followed by sound — the drums going up in three flat cracks, and then the mountain clearing its throat. The talus moved the way glaciers move, which is to say impossibly fast for something so large, a whole hillside deciding in one long grinding roar to relocate. Dust boiled up white in the searchlight beams. The narrows disappeared.

The sound went on and on. Rock striking rock striking water, the river's argument suddenly drowned out by a larger one, and beneath it all — faintly, horribly — the gunboat's horn sounding three short blasts. Danger. Reversing engines. They'd committed to the pinch just as the mountain committed to the channel, and now forty tons of steel was backing out of a throat that was filling with stone.

"It worked," Ulyana said. She was staring down through the thinning dust at the wall of rubble damming the narrows, the river already beginning to pool behind it, brown and confused. "It actually—"

That was when the second collapse started.

Nobody had audited the slope above our working area. Two hundred tons of scree, destabilized by the vibration of the main slide, chose that moment to follow its neighbor's example — directly above the goat track, directly above Okonkwo.

He heard it before we did. I saw him hear it — head turning, glasses flashing in the last of the searchlight glow — and then he was moving, and I want to be precise about this because precision is all I have left of the man's dignity that night: he didn't shout a warning. He didn't save himself. He calculated, in whatever fraction of a second hydrologists calculate, that the falling rock was tracking toward *me*, and he crossed the distance between us in three strides and hit me square in the chest with both hands, knocking me off the track and down the embankment into the alders.

The rock took him across the shoulder and back.

I heard it. I will always hear it — a wet, dense sound, nothing like the movies, nothing dramatic at all, just matter meeting matter with the mountain's indifference. He made no cry. He folded onto the track like a coat dropped from a hook, and the smaller stones rattled down around him and past him and were done, and the canyon went quiet except for the river pooling behind our dam and the gunboat's horn, farther now, retreating.

"Sable." Ulyana's voice came from somewhere above me, stripped down to wire. "Sable, get up. Get *up*."

I got up. My hands were shaking so badly I couldn't tell if I was hurt. I scrambled back up the embankment and knelt beside Okonkwo, and Ulyana was there a half-second later, and together we turned him over with the tenderness you use on something you're afraid of breaking.

His eyes were open. His glasses were gone. Without them his face looked younger and more frightened, and he was trying to focus on us, and he said — of all the things, of all the sentences available to him — "The fuses. Did you measure the cord correctly? Because if the secondary charge—"

"Shut up," Ulyana said, and her voice cracked clean through. "Just — shut up and breathe."

"He pushed me," I said. I was saying it to no one, to the canyon, to the ledger of things I owed. "The rock was coming at me and he — I was the target and he—"

"You were both targets," Ulyana said, tearing open his jacket with hands that had gone suddenly, terribly steady — pilot's hands, emergency-checklist hands. "Help me roll him. Gently. On my count."

We rolled him. The shoulder was wrong — sat wrong, moved wrong — and there was blood soaking the back of his shirt in a bloom that spread as we watched. But he was breathing, ragged and shallow, and when Ulyana pressed her ear to his chest he swore at her weakly in three languages, which she informed him later was the best possible sign.

We made a litter of the wrecking bar and the oilcloth and rope, and we carried him down to the raft through a canyon that had gone strange and new — the narrows dammed, the river backed up into a brown lake, the gunboat's engine sound fading downstream into defeat. Our gamble had paid. The mountain had taken our side, and it had sent a bill.

All the way down, Okonkwo drifted in and out, and each time he surfaced he asked a different question — about the dam's stability, about the pool level, about whether the backup of water would flood the lower cave camp — professional to the last, mapping even his own disaster. And each time he surfaced, I found myself unable to look at him, because I kept seeing the geometry of it: the rock's arc, my position, his three strides. He had done the calculation I would not have done for myself. He had decided, in the space of a heartbeat, that my life was worth his shoulder, his back, his months of recovery, maybe worse.

"Why?" I finally asked him, as we loaded him onto the drums and Ulyana checked the lashings with hands that trembled only when they thought I wasn't watching. "Why did you—"

He found my face without his glasses, squinting, and gave me a smile that was mostly pain and partly apology. "Surveyor," he said. "You should know better than anyone. Sometimes the map has to be redrawn around the person, not the terrain."

Then the fever-talk took him, and Ulyana pushed off from the shore, and we slid out onto the flooded narrows with our dammed river and our broken friend and the flight logs wrapped in oilcloth between us, and somewhere downstream, in the dark, the gunboat limped home to report that the mountain had voted against the syndicate.

Ulyana poled us into the current. Her face was set in the shape it took when she was refusing to feel something yet, filing it for later, and I knew — with the terrible clarity of a woman who had spent years reading what people hid — exactly which drawer she was putting it in.

"He needs medicine," she said. "Real medicine. Antibiotics, splints, the works. I have some in my med stores from the old runs — if any of it's still good."

"And if it isn't?" I asked.

She didn't answer. She put her shoulder into the pole and drove us into the current, and the flooded narrows fell away behind us, and the fever burned in Okonkwo like a small fire in wet wood — stubborn, smoky, not yet out.

I sat with his head in my lap and watched the canyon walls slide past, black against a sky just beginning to bruise gray, and I thought about maps again. About lines drawn where fights happen. About a man who redrew himself around another person in half a second, without hesitation, without martyrdom — just arithmetic, just a friend doing the math.

And I thought: whatever comes next — the fever, the gunboat regrouping, Ansel's next argument — we carry him now. All of us. The way he carried me.

The river carried us toward morning.
