## 26. Fever

The wound had been clean for two days. That was the lie Ulyana kept telling herself as she knelt beside Okonkwo in the relay station's back room and watched the skin around the sutures go from angry pink to a red that had opinions.

She'd stitched him herself after the narrows — eleven stitches, interrupted technique, the way her mother had taught her on a pig carcass behind a mission clinic when Ulyana was fourteen and thought she'd never use it. The rock fragment had gone into his flank below the ribs, torn through muscle, missed anything that mattered. She'd told him he was lucky. He'd laughed, which she'd taken as a good sign.

Now he was hot to the touch and talking to someone who wasn't in the room.

"Ulyana." Sable stood in the doorway with a lamp, and the light did her no favors; her face was gray with the particular exhaustion of someone who'd been watching a man die by increments for six hours. "His fever's at — I don't know. High. He's not making sense anymore."

"I know."

"He keeps asking about his samples. The water tables. He thinks we're still at the cave camp."

"I know." Ulyana pressed the back of her hand flat against Okonkwo's forehead, then his sternum, then the hinge of his jaw, reading him the way she read weather. Heat everywhere, but worst at the core. His skin was dry, which was worse than sweat. Sweat meant the body was still fighting on its own terms. Dry meant it had stopped negotiating.

The wound itself was the confession. A thin line of pus at the lowest suture, the tissue around it swollen tight and shiny, and when she eased the dressing back the smell reached her before the sight did — sweetish, wrong, the smell of a body hosting something it couldn't evict.

"Cellulitis," she said. "Maybe deeper. Maybe in the muscle."

"Can you fix it?"

She didn't answer immediately, because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and because the other honest answer — the one rising in her chest like water finding its level — was *I used to know*. There had been a version of Ulyana Ishikawa, ten years and one father's death ago, who could have answered without flinching. Who had run a med circuit out of a Cessna 180 along three hundred kilometers of river before the syndicate bought her debt and turned every flight into arithmetic. She'd carried penicillin then. Carried it in foam-lined crates, kept it cool with chemical packs, delivered it to settlements whose names weren't on any map worth printing. She'd known the dosing tables by heart. She'd known which children were allergic, which grandmothers lied about their symptoms, which headmen hoarded supplies against imagined sieges.

Then her father died, and the debt arrived, and medicine didn't pay what ore runs paid, and she'd told herself the story pilots tell: *I fly things now. I move cargo. What happens at either end of the runway isn't mine.*

She'd believed it for years. Believing it had been easy, the way most comfortable lies are easy.

"Ulyana," Sable said again.

"There's a med crate," Ulyana said. "In the plane's aft bay. Left side, under the tarp, under the spare oil. I never unpacked it."

Sable stared at her. "You've had antibiotics this whole time?"

"I've had *cargo* this whole time. It's different." She was already moving, pulling the tarp back, dragging the crate into the lamplight. "It was supposed to be a delivery run after the search contract closed. I kept meaning to offload it. I kept — " She stopped. Pried the lid. "It doesn't matter why I kept it."

But it did matter, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so. She'd kept it the way you keep a door unlocked in a house you've stopped living in.

* * *

The crate held a small civilization: ampoules of crystalline penicillin in wax-sealed tubes, sulfadiazine tablets in brown bottles, a scalpel, forceps, curved needles already threaded, gauze rolled tight, a bottle of iodine gone amber-dark with age, and a thermometer she'd forgotten existed. Everything dated. Everything questionable.

Ulyana sat on the floor of the relay station and inventoried it like a woman counting coins in a currency that might be counterfeit.

"This penicillin is four years past date," she said. "Possibly five. Crystalline penicillin degrades slowly if it stays dry and cool, and this crate has lived in a cold aft bay its whole life, but slow isn't never. The potency could be sixty percent. Could be forty. Could be nothing."

"So do we give it or not?" Sable crouched across from her.

"If we don't, he dies of the infection. If we do and it's dead powder, he dies of the infection anyway, just later, and we've lost the time we could have spent doing something else." Ulyana turned an ampoule in her fingers. "If we do and it's alive, he probably lives. The math only works one way, but the math requires me to guess right about chemistry I can't test."

"You flew this stuff for years. You'd know if it looked wrong."

"It looks fine. Fine is not a guarantee. Fine is what everything looks like right up until it isn't." She set the ampoule down and rubbed her eyes. "There's a test. Sort of. Old bush trick. You dissolve a bit in water and inject it under the skin of someone healthy and see if they welt. If the drug's potent enough to cause a histamine response, it's potent enough to kill bacteria. Probably."

"Someone healthy," Sable repeated. "Meaning you."

"Meaning me."

"No."

"Sable — "

"I said no." Her voice cracked on it, and Ulyana looked up and saw that Sable's hands were shaking, had maybe been shaking since the narrows, since Okonkwo went down shielding her from a rockfall that had been aimed at no one in particular and hit exactly what mattered. "You're the only one who can do whatever comes next. If you welt up, or worse — you said allergic reactions happen. You told me that on the raft. You said penicillin allergies kill faster than the infections."

"That's why we test first. Small dose. If I react, we stop, and we use the sulfa instead, and the sulfa is tablets, and tablets are more honest about their age than powders are."

"And if the small dose puts your throat shut?"

"Then Okonkwo dies and you bury us both and walk out in spring, and I'll have died knowing exactly how much you care, which will at least settle something between us." She heard her own voice go light and hated it — the old deflection, humor as a tourniquet. Sable's face said she hated it too.

They compromised the way exhausted people compromise: Ulyana would take the test dose, but Sable would draw it, and Sable would sit with the adrenaline ampoule uncapped in her lap for twenty minutes afterward like a woman holding a lit fuse.

The solution went into Ulyana's forearm at a quarter to midnight. The station's main room smelled of kerosene and river damp and the ghost of the transmitter Reyes had wrecked and they'd half-rebuilt. Rain worked the roof in long slow sheets. Somewhere beyond the walls the gorge muttered to itself, ice shifting in the dark, calving season winding down toward closure.

Ulyana watched her own forearm the way she watched cloud banks — for the change that means everything changes. A mosquito-bite welt rose at the injection site, pink and modest, and her chest stayed loose, and her throat stayed open, and after twenty minutes Sable exhaled like a diver surfacing.

"It's alive," Ulyana said. "The drug. I'm alive too, incidentally."

"I noticed." Sable's voice was unsteady. She capped the adrenaline with exaggerated care. "Don't do that again."

"Do what?"

"Volunteer to be the experiment. Not while I'm — " She stopped. Recalibrated. "Just don't."

* * *

The dosing was the next argument, and Ulyana won it by refusing to have it. Intramuscular penicillin, every four hours, through the night, plus opening the wound to drain it — because antibiotics alone wouldn't save a pocket of pus any more than a new engine saves a flooded carburetor. You had to get the bad fluid out and let the good blood in.

Boiling the instruments was almost ceremonial. She found the station's dented kettle, filled it from the rain barrel, and set the forceps, the scalpel, the retractor she improvised from a bent tent stake filed smooth on a whetstone, and the second needle into a rolling boil for thirty minutes while Sable fed the stove and Okonkwo murmured hydrology to the ceiling.

"The recharge zone," he said clearly, to no one. "It's not the glacier. Everyone thinks it's the glacier. It's the gravel terraces. The glacier is just the bank."

"I know," Ulyana told him, because delirious men deserve answers.

"Your father knew." His eyes opened partway, unfocused, fixed somewhere above her head. "He flew the transects. Before anyone paid him to forget them."

Her hands went still on the kettle handle.

That was the trouble with fevers. They pried lids off things. She set the kettle aside, let the instruments cool in their boiled water, and told herself she'd think about what he'd said later, when there was room. There was no room. There was a man dying on a cot and a night ahead of her shaped like a throat.

* * *

Draining a wound on a conscious man who trusts you is a specific kind of intimacy, and Ulyana had forgotten how much of medicine was simply permission. She explained every step before she took it — *this will hurt, this will hurt more, this is the hurt that helps* — and Okonkwo, lucid in the gaps between fever waves, nodded each time and made a joke about peer review.

She reopened the lowest inch of the incision with the boiled scalpel. Pus came first, then a wash of blood that was finally the right color, and she irrigated with boiled-and-cooled water laced with iodine until the return ran clear, pressing the tissue around the pocket with her thumbs, working it like dough, like bilge, like anything that yields to steady pressure. Okonkwo gripped Sable's hand hard enough to blanch it and swore in three languages, one of which Sable claimed afterward not to recognize.

"Done," Ulyana said, at some black hour. "Packed, dressed, dosed. Now we wait. Every four hours, another injection. If the fever breaks before dawn, we're winning. If it climbs, we're losing, and I don't have a second plan."

"What's the first plan's success rate?"

"On fresh wounds with drainage and live penicillin? Good. On four-year-old penicillin in a leaking building during monsoon?" She stripped her gloves — boiled rubber gloves, reused past all manufacturer's advice — and flexed her aching hands. "Ask me at sunrise."

* * *

The night organized itself into watches of the syringe.

Four hours is long when you're waiting and short when you're working, and Ulyana discovered that the work was the mercy. Drawing the dose, swabbing the site, driving the needle into the meat of his shoulder, massaging the depot in — each cycle was a task with edges, and tasks with edges kept the fear from pooling. Between doses she checked his pulse against her own wrist, sponged him down with tepid water, logged his temperature on the back of a cargo manifest in pencil: 39.8, 39.6, 40.1. The numbers climbed, plateaued, climbed. She watched them the way she'd once watched a fuel gauge over a mountain range with no strip on the far side.

Sable stayed awake the whole night, though Ulyana ordered her twice to sleep. She sat against the wall with the lamp trimmed low and the adrenaline ampoule within reach and watched Ulyana work with an expression Ulyana couldn't file anywhere — something between awe and grief, as if she were watching a door open onto a room that had been bricked over.

Around three, Okonkwo surfaced briefly, truly, and found Ulyana's face above him.

"You're doing the thing," he rasped.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you pretend you're just a pilot." He swallowed. His lips were cracked. "My grandmother was a nurse in the war. I know the hands. The hands remember even when the mouth denies it."

"Drink this." She held water to his mouth and he drank, obedient, and his eyes stayed on hers a moment longer than the water required.

"Your father's logs," he whispered. "Terrace seven. Ask the compass."

Then the fever took him back under, and Ulyana sat very still with the syringe in her lap and the words *terrace seven* ringing in her like a struck bell, and outside the rain went on falling on a country that apparently had been keeping her family's secrets longer than she'd been keeping its medicine.

* * *

Dose at four. Dose at eight. Between them, the vigil.

Somewhere past five the fever peaked — 40.3, the pencil shaking slightly as she wrote it — and Ulyana did the thing she hadn't done in front of another person since her father's funeral: she put her head down on the edge of the cot and admitted, silently, in the dark behind her eyelids, that she might lose him. That competence was not sovereignty. That she could do everything right and the chemistry could still vote no.

Sable's hand came to rest on the back of her neck. Warm. Steady. Saying nothing, which was the correct dosage of comfort, and Ulyana leaned into it for exactly ten seconds, counted honestly, and then straightened up and went back to work, because the patient was still breathing and the watch wasn't over.

At half past six the sweat came.

It started at his hairline and spread, and his skin went from dry furnace to damp, and his breathing changed register, and when Ulyana took his temperature it read 38.9 and falling. She took it again because she didn't trust good news. Same number. She sat back on her heels and listened to the rain ease off the roof and felt something unlock in her chest that she'd welded shut so long she'd mistaken the weld for the metal.

"He's turning," Sable said.

"He's turning." Ulyana peeled her gloves, flexed her fingers — stiff, cramped, ink-stained, competent — and looked at them as if meeting a stranger she'd been introduced to years ago and never followed up with. "I need to redress the wound. And then I need coffee, and then I need to sleep for a week, in that order."

"You should sleep first."

"Coffee first. Some things are structural."

Okonkwo opened his eyes at seven, properly, tracking, present. He looked at the IV-less setup, the manifest covered in temperatures, the two women wrung out and rumpled in the lamplight going pale against daylight, and he managed, in a voice like gravel settling: "Told you. The hands."

"Drink your water," Ulyana said, and her voice betrayed her, cracking on the second word, and Sable pretended very carefully to be checking the dressing.

* * *

By midmorning the sun came through torn cloud for the first time in days, laying a hard white stripe across the station floor, and Ulyana stepped outside to stand in it like a lizard. The gorge glittered. Upstream, the ice walls held their silence; downstream, the river ran high and fast with the monsoon's leavings, carrying the season toward the delta.

Which was when she saw the smoke.

Not woodsmoke — woodsmoke rose lazy and blue from the settlement side of the river, and this was none of those. This was a single thin column, black-edged, standing up from the treeline perhaps two hours' float below them. From the bend. From the place where, according to the syndicate charts she'd memorized years ago and never managed to forget, Ansel's organization kept a fuel cache.

A cache that was supposed to be empty. A cache that was supposed to be abandoned until the calving window opened.

Someone was there now. Someone had a fire going and, if the syndicate's habits held, a radio.

Ulyana stood in the sun with the taste of penicillin dust still on her fingers and Okonkwo's fever breaking behind her, and understood that the night's victory had been a loan, and the interest was coming due downriver.
