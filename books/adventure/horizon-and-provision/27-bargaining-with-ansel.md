## 27. Bargaining with Ansel

The fuel cache sat in a bend of the river like a thing someone had forgotten to lie about better — two steel drums under a tarp, a hand-painted stencil of the syndicate's crane logo already bleeding rust, and beside them, absurd against the wilderness, a folding table with a white cloth and a thermos.

Ansel stood behind the table as if he'd been poured there.

Ulyana saw him from two hundred meters out, poling the raft through the shallows, and her first thought — before fear, before calculation — was that he had ironed his shirt. Somewhere upriver, in the teeth of the monsoon's tail, the man had found a way to press cotton. That was the whole man in one detail. The world could rot around him and Ansel would arrive creased where he intended to be creased.

"Easy," Sable said beside her, low. Her pole had stopped moving. "He doesn't travel alone."

He didn't. Two men stood back by the drums with the loose-armed patience of people paid to wait, and a third held a rifle across his chest the way a waiter holds a tray — professionally, without appetite. Behind them, half-hidden in the willow scrub, the gunboat. Not the one they'd blocked at the narrows. A smaller one, a patrol skiff, which meant Ansel had come around the gorge the long way, or had never gone through it at all. Which meant he'd known where they'd surface before they did.

Okonkwo shifted on the raft's center plank. His arm was bound tight against his chest, the fever finally broken but leaving him gray and slow, and when he spoke it was barely above the water sound. "You don't have to go ashore."

"Yes, I do." Ulyana shipped the pole. "He'll wait all day otherwise, and we've got maybe six hours of light and no fuel."

"That's rather the point of the table," Okonkwo said dryly.

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she swung the raft toward the shingle and felt the river let go of them, that small sick lurch when current releases a hull, and stepped onto land where the man who owned her father's ghost was waiting with coffee.

* * *

"Miss Ishikawa." Ansel didn't offer a hand. He never had; courtesy at that altitude of precision didn't touch. He poured from the thermos instead — three cups, then hesitated over the fourth, glancing at Sable, and poured it too. The gesture was so exactly calibrated it made her skin crawl. *I know who you are. I know what you did for us. I know what you are now.* All in a cup of coffee nobody would drink.

"You look tired," he said.

"We've been paddling a raft of fuel drums through a flood season," Ulyana said. "You look pressed."

"I do my best." He folded his hands on the white cloth. Up close the shirt wasn't quite perfect — a damp shadow at the collar, a day or two of stubble he hadn't bothered with because he knew he wouldn't be photographed. Small human leaks. She catalogued them the way she read passengers in turbulence: how a body sits tells you what it's braced for. Ansel was braced for nothing. That frightened her more than the rifleman did.

"The gunboat," she said. "The narrows didn't stop you."

"The narrows cost me eleven days and two good men's pride. I went downriver and came back up the coastal channel. Slower. Certain." He said it without apology, the way a man reports weather. "Which brings us to why I'm standing in the mud offering you coffee."

"Because you want the logs."

"I want," Ansel said, "to make a problem go away. The logs are simply the shape the problem currently takes."

Sable made a sound — not quite a laugh, bitten off at the root. One of the riflemen glanced at her. Ansel didn't.

"Here is what I can do." His voice settled into the register she remembered from her father's debt negotiations, that terrible gentle arithmetic. "The ledger entry. Your father's account. Two hundred and forty thousand in principal, interest compounded across nine years, the strip mortgaged, the hangar held as collateral. I have the authority, sitting here, to mark it paid in full. Not forgiven — *paid*. There's a difference. Forgiven leaves a smell. Paid leaves nothing at all. You walk away owning your airstrip outright, your father's name clean in every book the syndicate keeps, and a letter to that effect signed before witnesses." He turned the thermos cap a quarter-turn, aligning its handle with the cups. "In exchange, the flight logs. The cache contents. Everything Meridian failed to deliver and everything your father hid in the rock."

"And the counter-claim?" Okonkwo asked from the raft. He hadn't come ashore. His voice carried flat across the water, fever-thinned but level. "Filed yesterday morning at the territorial registry. Does your authority reach into the clerk's office?"

Something moved behind Ansel's face — the first crack, hairline, quickly plastered. "Paper can be contested. Contested paper costs years and money neither of you has. My offer costs you nothing but a walk into the cliff fissure and back."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the debt stands, the strip goes to auction within the month, and I will still get the logs — later, less politely, with more people hurt in between. Miss Ishikawa, I am not threatening you. I'm describing a schedule." He unfolded his hands, spread them slightly, a man showing empty pockets. "You've seen what this river does to certainty. Don't make me certain."

* * *

She looked at the coffee. Steam curled off it, bent sideways by the river wind, and for one disorienting second she was nine years old again, sitting on a crate at the strip while her father drank syndicate coffee and laughed at something a syndicate man said, and the laugh had sounded real, and she'd believed for years afterward that it was.

Temptation, she thought, doesn't wear a stranger's face. It wears yours. It sounds like your own voice saying *this is reasonable, this is fair, take the strip, take the clean name, you never asked to be anyone's witness.* It offers you back the life you had before everything got heavy. The plane with the patched wing. The dawn runs. The competent loneliness she'd told herself she preferred.

Two hundred and forty thousand dollars. Nine years of flying cargo for a debt that, she now knew, had been drawn out of thin air to put a leash on a pilot who'd seen too much. And here was the leash-holder offering to unclip it, gently, kindly, if she'd just hand over the only proof that the leash had ever existed.

"You fabricated it," she said. "The debt."

Ansel's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer.

"My father died running evidence down this river," she went on, "and you turned his death into an invoice. And now you're standing on his river offering to sell me back his good name for the price of his testimony." She picked up the coffee cup. Held it. Didn't drink. "That's quite a product."

"It's the only product I have." For a moment — a real moment, she'd swear to it later — something almost honest crossed his face. Exhaustion, maybe. Or the particular loneliness of men who do institutions' work with their own hands. "Miss Ishikawa. I have served this company for thirty-one years. I have filed claims on four continents and I have never once lied to a counterparty across a table. What I'm offering is genuine. The debt dies. The strip is yours. You can spend the rest of your life hauling medicine and gossip to settlements that will forget your name the day you stop coming, and no one will ever point a rifle at you again." He paused. "It's not nothing. I know it isn't nothing. I've watched you want it."

That landed harder than any threat. Because he was right. She *did* want it — wanted it with a hunger she'd kept in the same locked compartment as her grief, and hearing it spoken aloud by this pressed, patient man made her feel flayed.

Sable moved. Just a step, just closer, shoulder near enough that Ulyana felt the warmth of her through wet cloth. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. *We talked about this,* the silence said. *You already answered this.*

But that had been Sable arguing for burning the logs. This was different. This was Ulyana's own life being priced and offered, and the fact that accepting it would also mean handing over the aquifer data — meaning every settlement downriver drinking on syndicate sufferance — was almost secondary to the raw pull of it. *Take the deal. Take your name back.*

Almost secondary. Only almost. That was the thing worth noticing about herself, she thought. Even drowning in wanting, some part of her was doing arithmetic on other people's water.

"I need time," she said.

Ansel blinked. Of all the responses he'd war-gamed, she suspected, that one hadn't made the list. "Time."

"Until dark. Six hours. I'll give you an answer on this shingle before the light goes."

"Why?"

"Because you taught my family that signatures taken fast are worth more than signatures taken true, and I intend to return the lesson." She set the coffee down untasted. "And because if I say yes, I want to be able to live with it, and if I say no, I want to mean it. Neither happens in five minutes."

Ansel studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy some internal auditor, because he nodded once, precisely, and began reassembling the thermos with the care of a man packing surgical instruments.

"Six hours," he agreed. "The offer doesn't improve after dark. It also doesn't expire. I'll be at the skiff." He gathered the cups himself, stacking them, and paused with the last one in hand. "Your father once asked me for time, in this same bend of the river. Nineteen years ago. I gave him a week." The smallest pause. "He used it well. I've often wondered what he'd have done with more."

Then he walked back through the willow scrub toward the skiff, and the riflemen folded around him, and the white cloth came off the table and disappeared into a satchel as though the entire negotiation had been a conjuring trick that mustn't leave residue.

* * *

They didn't talk for the first hour.

Ulyana poled them back out into the current and took them upstream along the shaded bank, putting distance between themselves and the fuel cache without deciding to, hands doing what hands knew. Sable sat forward with the spare paddle. Okonkwo lay against the drum stack with his eyes closed, conserving whatever the fever had left him, though she doubted he slept.

The river ran high and brown with the monsoon's leavings, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks. Somewhere ahead, ten days of open gorge remained before the calving ice shut the route until next year. Somewhere behind, a territorial clerk held a counter-claim with their names on it. Somewhere overhead, eventually, a chartered aircraft with Ansel's money in its fuel tanks.

"He gave your father a week," Sable said at last. "What happened?"

"I don't know." Ulyana planted the pole, pushed. "Nobody ever told me what the week was *for*. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought my whole childhood was debts. That's the story I built. Man flies cargo, man owes money, man dies owing, daughter inherits the owing. It explained everything. It was even comfortable, in a way. Debts are simple. You pay them or you don't." The pole caught, slipped, caught again. "Now there's a week I never knew about, sitting nineteen years back, and I have to wonder if he spent it hiding those logs in the rock. If the compass bearings were already cut by then. If he knew."

"If he knew he was going to die?"

"If he knew what he was going to die *for*." She heard her own voice go rough and hated it. "There's a difference."

Sable reached back without looking and rested her hand on Ulyana's knee — brief, warm, gone again, the way she did everything she couldn't yet say aloud. "Six hours," she said. "What are you going to do with them?"

"I'm going to read the logs. All of them. Every page." Ulyana swung the raft toward the cliff shadow where they'd cached the oilskin bundle, where the brass compass in her jacket pocket pointed home to her father's handwriting. "Ansel offered me my old life back. Before I answer, I want to know exactly what I'd be trading for it."

The cliff rose ahead, black stone streaked white with bird lime, the fissure a vertical seam no wider than a coffin. Ulyana shipped the pole and let the current carry them the last few meters, and in the quiet she heard Okonkwo murmur, mostly to himself, mostly asleep:

"She's going to refuse. Write it down. She's going to refuse, and then it gets worse."

Ulyana looked at Sable. Sable looked back, and for once didn't smile.

"Then we'd better decide tonight," Sable said, "what we do with the paper — before he comes back for his answer, and before someone else decides for us."

The raft kissed the rocks below the fissure, and Ulyana climbed.
