## 28. The Weight of Paper

The cache came out of the cliff fissure wrapped in oilcloth and wax and forty years of river patience, and for three days after we found it, nobody wanted to be the one to say what we were all thinking.

I said it first. I always say the ugly thing first — it's a habit from survey work, from being the one who has to write *unstable slope* on a map everyone else wants to call scenic.

"We should burn them."

Ulyana didn't look up. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the relay station's back room with her father's flight logs open across her knees, and she had been sitting that way, in roughly that posture, since dawn. The station had been ours for weeks now — Reyes marooned upriver with his pride and half our supplies, Okonkwo's fever finally broken but leaving him thin as winter reeds, the transmitter gutted and its parts scattered into three separate hiding places because none of us trusted any single hiding place anymore. We had made a life inside the sabotage. Mended nets. Card games with house rules that changed every night depending on who was losing. And underneath all of it, like ballast, the logs.

Six of them. Waterproof ledger paper, survey stamps still crisp, her father's handwriting marching across every page in that narrow, disciplined script I'd come to know almost as well as my own by now. Aquifer extents. Pressure gradients. Seasonal melt tables going back decades. And interleaved through it all, the thing that made my hands go cold every time I touched the pages: copies of Meridian's own falsified surveys, annotated. *Depth exaggerated 40m to justify exclusive filing.* *Sample sites relocated to dry benchland.* Ansel's syndicate hadn't just been chasing water. They'd been manufacturing the paperwork to own it, and Ulyana's father had been quietly, methodically building the case that would undo them.

"You're not listening," I said.

"I'm listening." She turned a page. "I'm choosing not to agree."

"Ulyana." I crouched down so I'd be in her sightline, which felt like an intrusion even after everything — after the cave camp, after the gorge, after the night I held her hand while she stitched Okonkwo's shoulder by lamplight and neither of us pretended our hands were steady for medical reasons. "Look at what's happened to every person who carried this knowledge. Your father died running it. Meridian's crew went into that valley and most of them never came out, and the ones who did went *deeper*, voluntarily, because they'd already decided the data mattered more than they did. Reyes sold us all for a radio call. Ansel is sending a gunboat up a gorge full of calving ice because he'd rather lose men than lose a claim. This paper doesn't protect anyone. It paints targets."

She closed the log. That was worse than arguing. When Ulyana closed a book on you, you understood the conversation had been moved to a different room.

"Say the rest," she said.

"The rest?"

"You have more. You've been rehearsing this since yesterday, when you thought I was asleep. Say all of it."

I sat down across from her, on the crate of salvaged antibiotics, because if we were going to do this I wanted us at the same height. Outside, the rain had eased into the gray drizzle that meant the monsoon's spine was breaking, and somewhere beyond the ridge the glacier was talking to itself — that deep, structural groaning that wasn't sound so much as weather made of stone. Calving season. Our ten days, ticking.

"Fine," I said. "Here's all of it. I led Meridian to the cache site. Under duress, yes, but I led them there, and that means there may already be partial data in Ansel's hands, which means whatever protection these logs offer is already compromised. It means the only thing they're good for now is evidence, and evidence only works if it reaches a registry alive, and between here and the territorial office there is a gunboat, a bribed clerk, a fuel cache Ansel knows about, and approximately four hundred kilometers of river where any one of us can die very easily. I have watched what happens to people who carry dangerous documents. I watched Okafor drown holding a satchel he wouldn't let go of. I watched Voss—"

"Don't," Ulyana said quietly.

"—I watched your father choose the papers over the parachute," I finished, because I had decided, sometime in the last day, that we were done protecting each other from the truth, and once I decide a thing I am terrible at un-deciding it. "Everyone says he ran out of fuel. Okonkwo pulled the incident file years ago. There was fuel. He jettisoned the raft to keep the weight of the case manifest legal, and then the engine quit over open water, and he had time to jump, and he didn't, because the logs were in the plane and he thought they were worth more than he was."

The stove ticked. The glacier groaned. Ulyana sat very still with her hands flat on the closed log, and I watched her absorb it, and I hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me, and I kept going anyway, because the whole point of love — and I had finally admitted to myself, somewhere in the third week of mending nets beside her, that this was what it was — the whole point of it was that someone had to be willing to be wrong in front of you at volume.

"So burn them," I said. "Burn them and scatter the ash in the current and we walk away. We go south. I know people who owe me favors in three territories. You start flying cargo without a debt attached, which is the ending you've been dreaming about since before I met you. Nobody hunts ashes. Nobody dies for smoke. The settlements lose their proof, yes, and that's a tragedy, but it's a tragedy spread across thousands of people instead of concentrated in the three of us, and I have done that arithmetic before, Ulyana, and I am telling you as a woman who has buried a whole expedition: the arithmetic is a lie. It always feels like mercy from the outside. From the inside it's just a smaller number of graves."

She was quiet long enough that I started composing apologies.

Then she opened the log again, to a page near the front, and turned it around so I could see it, though I'd seen it a dozen times. A hand-drawn map of the delta settlements. Downriver families. Names in her father's hand next to little inked symbols — a fish, a well, a drying rack. He'd known them. Not as clients. As people whose wells he'd tested for free, whose kids he'd flown medicine to, who had taught him the river's memory of the drowned village.

"Do you know why I stopped doing med runs?" she asked.

"Because of the debt."

"Because of the debt, yes. But do you know why I *started*?" She tapped the map. "Him. I used to sit in the copilot seat with my feet not reaching the pedals, and he'd fly the delta low enough that I could count the roofs, and he'd tell me which family needed what, and he'd say — I can hear his voice right now, Sable, I have been hearing it for three weeks — he'd say, *the maps forgot them, so somebody has to remember out loud.* That was the whole philosophy. That was the man. He didn't hide this evidence in a cliff because he was brave. He hid it because he believed remembering was a job, and jobs get finished."

"And it killed him."

"It killed him," she agreed, and her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "And every year on the anniversary I flew his route and told myself I was different, I was careful, I was invisible, invisibility was safety. And then Ansel showed me how much protection invisibility buys — none, Sable, it bought me nothing, he had my debt and my strip and my leash the entire time, and the only reason I'm free of any of it is that I stopped being invisible. I walked into a valley. I read his logs. I let two strangers become—" she stopped, recalibrated, and I watched her decide not to flinch away from the word, "—become mine. So no. I'm not going back to the arithmetic. The settlements need proof, not ashes. They need a document with his survey stamps on it that a clerk can't lose in a drawer. They need the counter-claim filed before Ansel's lawyers land. Burning these doesn't make us safe. It makes us safe and complicit, and I have been complicit my whole life by staying quiet, and I am done."

"That's not fair," I said. "You weren't complicit. You were surviving."

"I was *comfortable*. There's a difference and I'm old enough to name it." She stood, stiff-legged, and carried the log to the table where Okonkwo's hydrology charts lay under river stones, and set it down among them like it belonged there, like it had always been headed for this table. "And here's the part you're not saying, so I'll say it for you. You want to burn them because burning them means we don't have to go back down the river. No registry run, no delta, no council, no risk. Just south, and quiet, and each other. And I understand that. God help me, I understand it better than you think. But if we run, I will spend the rest of my life listening for that gunboat, and you will spend the rest of yours waiting for me to regret it, and I will, Sable. I will regret it the way my father would have regretted jumping."

* * *

We fought for two more hours, and it was the best fight of my life, which sounds like a contradiction until you've spent years around people who fight like surveyors — carefully, coldly, marking boundaries — or like fixers, like Reyes, fighting to win rather than to understand. Ulyana fought like a pilot in weather. She didn't fight to destroy me. She fought to stay in the air, both of us, both engines turning, and when I threw the worst thing I had — *you're doing exactly what he did, trading your life for paper, and I refuse to watch it twice* — she took it, held it up, examined it honestly, and handed it back changed.

"He traded his life for paper because he was alone with it," she said. "That was the flaw. Not the principle. One witness, one copy, one plane, one river. So we fix the flaw. We don't abandon the principle. We make copies. We split the route. We give the delta the original and keep a duplicate in a place Ansel will never find, and if the registry run goes wrong, the truth survives us. That's not courage, that's engineering. You taught me that. Redundancy."

I laughed. It came out ragged and unwilling, halfway to something else. "You're using my own survey doctrine against me."

"I learned from a professional."

Okonkwo found us like that — hoarse, laughing badly, wrung out — standing over the table with the logs and the charts and the beginning, visible to both of us now, of a plan. He looked from her to me with the expression of a man reading instruments he trusts, and said only, "So we're not burning them," and when I said, "No. Apparently we're copying them," he nodded once and went to find candle stubs, because in the relay station we had learned that arguments end in tasks or they don't end at all.

We copied through the evening. Ulyana wrote; I checked figures against my own field notes, the ones I'd memorized and smuggled out in my head when I defected, the numbers I'd been too frightened to speak aloud for months. Page by page the duplicate grew, and with every line I felt the weight shift — not lighter, never lighter, but distributed now, shared, the way a load sits differently when two people carry it instead of one.

Near midnight she paused over the last page and said, without looking up, "You were wrong about one thing."

"Only one? I'll take it."

"You said I wanted to run south with you and forget the river." She blotted the final figure. "I did want that. For about a minute, when you first said it, some tired part of me wanted it so badly I could taste it. And then I realized the tired part wasn't me. It was just the debt, still talking, wearing my voice. It's been doing that my whole life." She looked up then, and the lamp caught her face, and she was smiling in the way I had learned meant she'd landed something difficult in crosswind conditions. "The voice is quieter now. You helped. Don't let it go to your head."

Outside, the glacier calved — a long report of thunder rolling down the gorge, ice meeting water, the season opening whether we were ready or not. Ten days. Maybe less.

I stacked the duplicate pages, squared their edges, and thought about waterproofing, and routes, and which of us should carry which copy, and I did not say the other thing I was thinking, which was that I had finally found a document worth the weight, and it wasn't paper at all.

By morning we'd need to decide who flies.
