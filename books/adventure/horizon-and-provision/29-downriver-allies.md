## 29. Downriver Allies

The delta announced itself before Ulyana saw it — a smell of mud and woodsmoke and drying fish riding up the river on the last of the flood current, so familiar it hurt. She had been flying over this country for eleven years and had never once landed here. Now, hollow-eyed and salt-rimed from the gorge, she stood at the stern of the patched raft with her father's logs wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, watching the channels braid apart ahead of them like a river trying to say too many things at once.

"You're sure about this," Sable said. It wasn't a question. Sable hadn't asked a real question in two days; she'd just kept checking Ulyana's face for an answer to one.

"No," Ulyana said. "But Ansel's gunboat is behind us somewhere, and Okonkwo's fever is down but not gone, and I don't have a better idea."

"Better ideas are overrated," Okonkwo said from under the tarp. His voice was still gravelly, still carrying that faint surprise of a man who'd expected to die of a shoulder wound in a rockslide and hadn't. "I've had three this month. All of them worse than this one."

The first settlement appeared around a bend of the main channel: stilt houses leaning over brown water, nets strung between poles like laundry, a fish-drying rack smoking gently in the drizzle. Children saw them first — children always did — and shouted something downstream, and by the time Ulyana steered the raft toward the landing planks there were a dozen adults waiting with the particular stillness of people who had learned to read every arrival as either opportunity or threat.

Ulyana killed the outboard and let the current carry them in. She raised both hands, empty.

"We're not armed," she said. "We need shelter. Maybe medicine. We can pay—"

She stopped, because an old woman at the end of the dock had gone very still, staring at her the way you stare at a photograph that has started moving.

"Ishikawa," the old woman said. Not a question either.

Ulyana's hands tightened on the tiller. "You know me?"

"I know your father." The woman's River dialect was thick, older than the syndicate's trade patois, and she used it deliberately, like laying down a card. "Kazimir Ishikawa. Flew the low water runs when nobody else would. Brought us penicillin the year the fever came up from the south. Landed on this exact plank when the strip at Kessel's Landing was flooded, because he said—" she paused, and something moved through the crowd, a ripple of recognition passing from face to face, "—he said the river remembers who lands on it."

Nobody spoke. The rain ticked on the water. Ulyana became aware that she was holding the oilcloth bundle against her chest like a shield, and made herself lower it.

"He never told me about any of you," she said finally. "He told me nothing. He flew cargo and he owed money and he died owing it."

"He told you nothing," the old woman repeated, "because telling is how men get followed home." She stepped forward and put a hand flat on the raft's gunwale, steadying it against the current, an old pilot's gesture, or a mother's. "My name is Marisol. You'll come up to the house. All three of you. And whatever you're carrying in that bundle, it can dry by my stove while you eat."

* * *

Sable had spent her whole life being welcomed into places under false pretenses, and the strangeness of being welcomed truthfully nearly undid her.

Marisol's house sat high on pilings above the channel, one large room smelling of smoke and dried catfish and some medicinal leaf boiled endlessly in a pot on the stove. Okonkwo was installed in the best chair, protesting weakly, and was ignored. A girl of maybe fourteen — Marisol's granddaughter, called Pilar — examined his bandages with the brisk competence of someone who had stitched fishing nets since she could hold a needle, then rewrapped the wound with a poultice that smelled fierce enough to fight infection on its own.

"That will draw it," Pilar said. "Or it won't, and we'll boil more leaves."

"It's already drawn," Okonkwo said, amazed. "I could feel it drawing."

Sable stood near the door where she could see the river — old habit, newer necessity — and watched the settlement absorb them. Word traveled fast here, faster than boats. Within an hour people were drifting past the house on the pretext of errands: a net-mender who looked too long at Ulyana's face; two brothers who stood in the rain under the eaves and spoke low; a man with a rifle slung casually who didn't come in but didn't leave either.

"They're deciding something," Sable murmured to Ulyana.

"They're deciding whether we're worth what sheltering us costs." Ulyana said it without bitterness, the way you'd state the weather. "Ansel's gunboat exists. Everyone on this river knows what happens to places that cross the syndicate."

"And they're sheltering us anyway?"

Ulyana didn't answer, because Marisol answered for her, coming back from the stove with four bowls of fish stew and handing them out like she was dealing cards.

"You want to know why," Marisol said, sitting down across from them with her own bowl. "Because eleven years, your father flew the winter runs and never once raised his price. Because when the syndicate bought the fuel depot at Kessel's and doubled the price of petrol, he carried our letters free. Because—" she set her spoon down, and her voice went quieter and harder at once, "—because we heard he died running from debt, and half this river never believed it, and now here comes his daughter downriver with strangers and a package she won't let out of arm's reach, and suddenly we believe it less than ever."

Sable felt Ulyana go rigid beside her. She reached over without looking and put her hand on Ulyana's knee under the table, and left it there.

"The package," Marisol said. "Is it why the syndicate's boat burned fuel coming down the gorge like a man late for his own funeral?"

"It's proof," Ulyana said. "Of what the aquifer really is. Of what the syndicate falsified to claim it. My father collected it. He hid it. He died for it, and I only found out three weeks ago, and I'm still—" she stopped, jaw working. "I'm still learning how to carry it."

Marisol studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way you nod at a landing you've decided to allow.

"Eat your stew," she said. "Tomorrow we call the landings together."

* * *

Okonkwo slept badly and woke at dawn feeling, for the first time since the rockslide, like a hydrologist instead of a patient.

He climbed down the ladder carefully — Pilar shadowing him with her arms out like a spotter, which he permitted because dignity was a luxury and she was usually right — and walked the boardwalks between the stilt houses while the delta woke around him. He had spent twenty years reading rivers for a living, and everything here confirmed what his own instruments had told him from inside the valley: this water was glacier water. Cold even in the warm season. Clear after the silt settled. Fed from a source no dam, no pump, no syndicate gate could replace.

And every family here drew their drinking water, their fish ponds, their rice paddies from it. He counted intake pipes as he walked. Eleven, twelve, fifteen. Each one a household. Each household a reason.

By the time he got back, Marisol's main room had filled. Not just this settlement — word had gone out overnight by boat and by that fastest of all delta networks, children sent running with messages. There were faces he didn't know from three other landings: a shrimp-farmer with forearms like rope, a schoolteacher, a woman in a syndicate uniform shirt worn defiantly unbuttoned at the collar, an ancient man who was introduced only as "the one who remembers the dam."

They arranged themselves on benches and crates and the floor, and Marisol stood, and the room quieted the way rooms only quiet for women who have fed everyone in them.

"These are the ones," she said simply. "Ishikawa's daughter, and two others. They carry paper that says the water is ours."

Okonkwo rose. His shoulder screamed and he ignored it. He had rehearsed nothing, which was, he'd learned long ago, how his best testimony happened.

"I'm going to show you maps," he said, "and I need you to understand that I helped make one of them, and I am ashamed of it." He unrolled his field sheets on the table, weighted the corners with stew bowls, and turned to face the room. "This is the aquifer. It sits under the glacier valley, and it drains into this river system through channels that took ten thousand years to form. Every settlement on this map — every one of yours — draws from it. If the Meridian Syndicate files exclusive rights, they don't just own the water in the valley. They own the recharge zone. They can meter your wells. They can close them. Legally."

Silence. Then the old man — the one who remembered the dam — spoke for the first time, in a voice like gravel poured slowly.

"My father watched a company do exactly that in the coast province. Metered the wells. Then closed them. Then sold the water back to us by the tin." He looked around the room. "I said then it would come here someday. Nobody listened. I'm an old man. Listen now."

"What do we do?" the schoolteacher asked. "We're fishermen. They're lawyers with gunboats."

"We file," Okonkwo said. "A counter-claim. Collective. Every landing on this river as co-claimant. The territorial registry has provisions for communal water rights — they're old, they're rarely used, and the syndicate has spent thirty years making sure nobody remembers them. But they exist. And to file, we need originals. Certified surveys. Not copies." He glanced at Ulyana. "Her father's logs are the originals. His survey stamps are still valid. His name still carries weight in that office — I checked, years ago, when I still worked for the other side."

"Then we file," Marisol said, as if it were already done, and the room — slowly, then all at once, like ice going out — agreed.

It was Ulyana who raised the practical objection, standing at the edge of the lamplight with her arms crossed.

"There's a gunboat behind us. There are syndicate agents in every port town between here and the registry. And the registry closes for the monsoon recess in nineteen days." She looked around the room full of faces that had, an hour ago, been strangers. "Someone has to carry these papers there. Fast. By air."

The silence that followed wasn't doubt. It was arithmetic — everyone doing the same sum and arriving at the same answer, and all of them looking, gently, apologetically, at her.

"You have a plane," the shrimp-farmer said.

"My plane is sitting on the syndicate strip at Kessel's Landing," Ulyana said, "under guard, because I stole myself away from them along with everything else."

"Then," said Marisol, folding her hands, "we had better talk about how to get it back."

* * *

That night Sable found Ulyana on the dock, sitting with her feet hanging over the black water, the brass compass open in her palm. The needle swung lazily, found north, held.

Sable sat down beside her. Close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them mentioned it.

"Eleven years," Ulyana said. "I flew over this place hundreds of times. Carried their medicine. Never landed. Never let myself land." She turned the compass over. Her father's initials were scratched inside the lid, worn almost smooth. "I thought belonging was something you earned by paying off what you owed. Debt first. Then maybe. Then someday."

"And now?"

"Now a woman I met yesterday fed me my father's history with my dinner, and a room full of strangers voted to make my father's war their own, and nobody asked me for anything." Her voice caught, and she let it. "It arrived unasked, Sable. I don't know what to do with that. I've been invisible so long I built my whole life on it."

Sable was quiet for a moment. Out on the channel, a night fisherman's lamp moved slowly, a small defiance of the dark.

"When I left Meridian," Sable said, "I thought I was running from what I'd done. Turns out I was running toward something I hadn't done yet." She looked at Ulyana sideways. "Same river. Different direction."

Ulyana laughed — short, surprised, real — and leaned into her, just slightly, just enough.

Behind them, up the bank, a door opened. Pilar came down the dock steps at a run, breathless, holding a scrap of paper.

"Boat came through from the coast landing," she panted. "Fish buyer. He says—" she swallowed, "—he says there's a syndicate aircraft chartered out of the capital. Twin-engine. Asking after a woman pilot and a stolen plane. He says it's already refueling at Kessel's."

Ulyana stood. The compass snapped shut in her hand like a decision.

"Nineteen days," she said. "Then we move first."
