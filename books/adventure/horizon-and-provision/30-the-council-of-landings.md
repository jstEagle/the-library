## 30. The Council of Landings

The shed smelled the way the delta smelled — salt, smoke, and ten thousand fish giving up their water to the sun. It was built on stilts over a tidal slough, its walls made of split bamboo lashed with wire, its roof a patchwork of tin and thatch that let the light through in stripes. Someone had swept it clean for us. That was the first thing I noticed: the floor was bare packed earth, raked smooth, and someone had laid out planks across two sawhorses to make a table.

Someone had decided we were worth preparing for.

I stood in the doorway while Sable helped Okonkwo down from the skiff, and I let myself feel it — just for a moment — what it was like to walk into a room where people had been expecting you. Not hunting you. Expecting you.

"Easy," Sable said, her shoulder under his arm. The fever had burned him down another few pounds since the narrows; his shirt hung off him like rigging after a storm. But his eyes were clear this morning. Clearer than they'd been in three days. Whatever the antibiotics were doing, they were doing it just barely, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it.

"I'm fine," Okonkwo said, which was what he'd been saying since the delta, and which had stopped being true somewhere around the second sandbar.

"You're lucid," Sable corrected. "There's a difference."

"There's always a difference with you."

They came up the ladder together, and I turned back into the shed, and the room turned toward me.

There were eleven of them. I counted without meaning to — an old habit from cargo runs, counting heads against manifest. Four women, seven men, ages spread from a boy of maybe sixteen to a woman so old her hands shook when she folded them, though her eyes didn't shake at all. They sat on crates and overturned buckets and one long bench worn silver by generations of other meetings. Behind them, strung along the walls, hung the season's catch — split and salted fish turning slowly in the draft, hundreds of pale bodies swaying like a congregation at prayer.

The oldest woman rose. She was small and square as a fuel drum, and she wore a man's old cardigan buttoned wrong, and when she spoke her voice carried the particular authority of someone who had buried more people than she'd outlived.

"You're Ishikawa's girl," she said.

Not a question. My father's name, arriving before mine did, the way it always did now.

"I'm Ulyana," I said.

"I know who you are." She looked me over the way you look over a boat before buying it — hull, seams, engine mounts, the places where rot hides. "I'm Marta Osei. This is the Landing at Tern Reach. There are nine landings in the delta. Six sent somebody. Three are represented by proxy." She gestured at the room. "You'll notice nobody came armed. You'll also notice we're not fools. We know who's behind you, or ahead of you, or whatever he is."

"He's behind us," Sable said flatly, easing Okonkwo onto the bench. "For now."

"And the gunboat?"

"Blocked at the narrows," I said. "Rockslide. It bought us days, not weeks. When they clear it — and they will, Ansel doesn't quit, he re-budgets — they'll come downriver looking for us and for anything we're carrying."

Marta Osei nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd already written down. "Then we shouldn't waste the days. Sit. All of you. The doctor looks like he's about to fall through himself, and whatever he's carrying in his head is heavier than any of us can lift alone."

* * *

They gave Okonkwo the table, and he took it the way he took everything — methodically, without flourish, spreading his maps and charts across the planks with hands that trembled only when he wasn't watching them.

He'd spent the boat ride down consolidating. While I ran the tiller and Sable kept watch and Reyes's absence sat in the stern like a third passenger, Okonkwo had lain in the bilge with a pencil stub and my father's flight logs open on his chest, cross-referencing thirty years of altitude readings and water-depth soundings against his own instrument data from the Meridian survey. What he'd produced overnight was four sheets of drafting paper covered in a handwriting so precise it looked printed.

"This," he said, tapping the first sheet, "is the aquifer."

The map showed the river country from the glacier to the sea — every settlement, every landing, every tributary — overlaid with contour lines that didn't follow the surface. They followed the water underneath it.

"The meltwater doesn't just run off," Okonkwo said. "It percolates. Through gravel beds laid down before the last glaciation, under the whole valley floor, and it travels — slowly, coldly, filtered clean — all the way down here." His finger traced the contours to the delta. "Every well in every settlement in this region draws from it. Every field. Every fish camp. The river itself is fed by it in the dry months. It is, functionally, the only fresh water between the gorge and the coast that doesn't go brackish in summer."

He looked up. Fever had put a bright, glassy shine on his eyes, but his voice held.

"The syndicate knows this. That's what Meridian was for. Not minerals — the mining story was cover. They commissioned a full hydrological survey of the aquifer's extent and recharge rate, timed to finish before calving season opens the upper gorge, because once the route is navigable their lawyers can physically reach the territorial registry and file exclusive extraction claims on the headwaters. Control the source, and every claim downstream becomes a negotiation. Every negotiation becomes a lease. Every lease becomes rent."

"Rent," Marta Osei repeated. The word came out of her like a bone.

"On water," Okonkwo said. "Water that falls on their glacier, drains under their valley, and has drunk from no one's permission."

Silence, except for the fish turning on their lines and the slough lapping at the pilings below.

A man near the back — young, a net-mender's calluses on his hands — said, "We heard stories. For years. Syndicate buying up land grants nobody thought were worth anything. Dry parcels, uphill parcels. Everyone laughed." He wasn't laughing now.

"They weren't buying land," Sable said quietly. "They were buying the lid of the jar."

Okonkwo lifted the second sheet. "Here's the problem, and I want to be honest about it because honesty is the only currency I have left." His finger found a cluster of annotations along the map's edge — dates, registry numbers, stamp marks. "These are Ulyana's father's original survey stamps. Thirty years ago, he flew sounding runs over this entire valley — before the syndicate existed, before anyone understood what was under the gravel. His logs contain depth readings, flow estimates, seasonal variation data. Independently gathered. Legibly stamped. Filed format, witnessed format — the whole legal architecture the registry requires."

"And that matters because?" the net-mender asked.

"Because a counter-claim needs provenance," Okonkwo said. "My data is better — newer instruments, denser sampling. But my data comes from a syndicate expedition. Every reading I took, they paid for. Their lawyers will stand up in the registry hearing and say my numbers are stolen work product, tainted, inadmissible. And they might win that argument." He set his palm flat on the logs, stacked beside him like ballast. "But these readings predate them. These readings belong to a dead pilot with no syndicate affiliation and a reputation in every landing on this river. If the originals are lodged with our filing, the provenance chain is unbroken. The claim stands on its own feet."

"They'll contest it anyway," I said.

"Of course they'll contest it. Ansel contests weather." Okonkwo almost smiled. "But contesting takes time, and time is the one thing a filed claim buys. Once it's lodged, the water is disputed property. No exclusive grant. No leases. Just a hearing, in public, with our names on one side and theirs on the other. That's a fight they can lose."

Marta Osei had come around the table without anyone noticing. She stood over the maps, studying the contour lines the way she probably studied tide tables — reading the future in them.

"How many people," she said. "Underneath all your lines. How many drink from this."

Okonkwo did the arithmetic he'd clearly done a hundred times. "Between the gorge and the coast? Eleven thousand. Call it twelve in a good fishing year."

"Twelve thousand people drinking out of a jar," Marta Osei said, "that a company in the lowlands wants to put a lock on." She straightened up and turned to face the room. "I've heard enough. Has anybody else?"

Nobody spoke, but everybody answered. It was in the way they leaned forward, the way the old woman with the steady eyes closed her hands into fists, the way the boy sat up straighter. I'd flown into enough landings to read a room by how it sat, and this room had decided.

"We file," Marta Osei said. "Collectively. All nine landings, named parties, one claim. If the syndicate wants the water, they can negotiate with twelve thousand people instead of buying twelve signatures." She looked at Okonkwo. "What do you need?"

"Three things." He held up fingers, and his hand shook, and he steadied it against the table. "One: the originals — the flight logs and the survey stamps — lodged with the filing itself. Copies won't hold. Two: signatures or marks from each landing, witnessed properly, before the registry closes for the monsoon recess. Three—" he paused, and I saw him choose the words carefully, "—all of it delivered to the territorial registry office in Port Meridian within six days. After that, the clerk's commission lapses until the rains end, and Ansel's lawyers will be first in line when it resumes."

"Six days," Marta Osei said.

"Five, if the weather argues."

"It always argues," she said dryly. Then: "How do we get it there? The river road is watched. Ansel has eyes on every landing with a radio, and half the landings without."

And then the room did the thing I'd been dreading since Okonkwo first said *registry*. Eleven faces turned, not toward Okonkwo, not toward Sable, not toward the fevered doctor or the defector with her badly-kept secrets.

Toward me.

"I have a plane," I said, before anyone could ask. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "At the syndicate strip. Under guard. With a clipped wingtip I haven't repaired and a fuel state I don't know and a debt ledger attached to it that says it isn't even mine."

"It's yours," Marta Osei said simply. "Whoever holds the paper, the flying was yours. Everybody on this river knows that."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to say, *you don't understand, the paper is the whole point, paper is the only thing Ansel respects, paper is what killed my father.* Instead I looked at the maps — at the thin blue lines threading under everything I'd ever flown over, connecting every landing I'd ever resupplied, every rumor I'd ever carried, every medicine run I'd made thinking I was just paying off a debt when I was actually, all along, stitching a region together one delivery at a time.

"When you say collectively," I said. "All nine landings. Even the ones that didn't send anybody?"

"Especially those," Marta Osei said. "The ones that stayed home are the ones most afraid. A claim that only protects the brave isn't worth the paper."

Sable made a sound beside me — small, involuntary. I glanced over. She was staring at the map, at the contour lines, and her jaw was tight, and I knew exactly what she was seeing: the survey she'd led Meridian along, line by line, under duress, the partial data she'd handed over before she walked. The syndicate already had pieces of this picture. Which meant Ansel already knew the clock we were racing.

"There's something else," Sable said, and told them. All of it — the duress, the cache site, the partial dataset, the possibility that Ansel's numbers were already good enough to file a claim of his own if he got to the registry first. Her voice never wavered, and that was how I knew how much it cost her. Sable lied badly about small things and perfectly about large ones, and this was the largest thing she'd ever said out loud, and she made it flawless.

When she finished, the room was quiet again, but it was a different quiet — not weighing her, but weighing the news.

"So it's a race," the net-mender said.

"It's always a race," Marta Osei said. "Everything worth having on this river is upstream of something trying to take it." She rolled the maps — carefully, the way you handle things that are now the most valuable objects in the delta — and set them in front of Okonkwo. "The landings will meet tonight by runner and radio-silence. Marks and witnesses by tomorrow noon. After that, captain—"

She stopped. Looked at me. Waited.

"After that," I said, "somebody steals my airplane back."

* * *

The council broke into clusters — signatures to organize, runners to dispatch, a boy sent sprinting down the stilts to carry word to the two landings that hadn't come. Marta Osei cornered Sable by the fish lines, and the two of them talked low and fast, two women conducting an audit of each other. I left them to it.

Okonkwo was still at the table, repacking the logs into their oilskin wrap, moving slower than he'd admit. I sat down across from him.

"You shouldn't be presenting while you're burning at a hundred and two," I said.

"A hundred and one this morning. Trending correctly." He tied the wrap off. "Ulyana. I need to say something before you decide anything."

"Don't ask me not to fly out. You already did that in the cave. I remember."

"That was about the data. This is about you." He looked up, and the fever-brightness in his eyes made the look more naked than either of us wanted. "The strip is guarded. Ansel expects you to try something — maybe not this, but something. If you go in there and it goes wrong, there's no delta big enough to hide in. And if it goes right—" he paused, "—you'll be flying the most important cargo this river has ever carried, through monsoon weather, in a damaged aircraft, with a gunboat somewhere behind you. I've done the risk math. I hate all of it. But I've also seen you fly, and I've seen you keep a leaking boat alive with a coffee can and spite, and I've concluded that the math is wrong because it keeps leaving you out of the variables."

"That's not very scientific."

"No," he agreed. "It's not. It's faith. I caught it off the river people. It's apparently endemic."

I almost smiled. Then the smile went, because through the doorway I saw Sable coming back across the shed floor, and behind her, out in the gray afternoon light, the tide was turning in the slough — the water beginning to run the wrong way, the way it does twice a day here, the whole delta reversing itself on schedule like a heart beating backwards.

Sable crouched by the table. She didn't sit. When she spoke, her voice was pitched for me alone.

"Marta's sending a runner to the strip," she said. "Tonight. To count guards, watch the rotation, find the gaps." A beat. "And I told her the diversion would need to happen from inside. Fuel store. Something loud, something that pulls every rifle to one corner of the fence line." She met my eyes. "I volunteered."

"No."

"You don't get a vote yet."

"Sable. If they catch you inside that wire, Ansel doesn't shoot you. He trades you. You're the one piece of Meridian he can still recover, and he knows exactly what you're worth—"

"Which is why I'm the only one who can do it." She said it without heat, the way you state a bearing. "Reyes talked. We have to assume he talked. Which means Ansel knows my face, knows my value, and knows I'd rather burn his operation than rejoin it. I'm the perfect arsonist, Ulyana. I have nothing left to protect except this." She touched the oilskin wrap over the logs, lightly, the way you touch a wound that's finally being treated.

I looked at her — this half-starved surveyor who'd walked out of a glacier valley with nothing but a lie and a compass heading, who'd lied to me badly and then told me truths that rearranged my whole life, who circled me the way the delta circled the tide, in and out, never landing — and I understood that arguing with her was going to cost us the six days we didn't have.

"One condition," I said.

"You're not in a position to—"

"One condition. You get out over the north fence, past the fuel store, within four minutes of the fire starting. Four minutes, Sable. If you're not at the tree line in four minutes, I take off anyway, and you spend the rest of the monsoon explaining yourself to Ansel."

Something moved through her face — surprise, then something warmer and more dangerous than surprise. "Four minutes," she said.

"Three and a half. I fly better angry."

Outside, the tide finished turning. Below the shed, the slough ran backward toward the sea, carrying everything on its surface — leaves, rain, the reflected gray sky — out toward the coast and the registry and the race we'd just entered.

Tomorrow noon, the marks. Tomorrow night, the runner's report. And the day after—

I put my hand flat on the oilskin wrap, on the paper heavier than any cargo I'd ever hauled, and started planning a theft.
