## 31. Who Flies

The fish-drying shed still smelled of the council — kerosene, tobacco, and the sweet rot of a season's catch — long after the last of the representatives had gone out into the rain. Ulyana sat on an upturned crate with the counter-claim's carbon copy in her lap and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since dawn.

The registry office closed to new filings in nine days. After that, Ansel's lawyers would walk in with their exclusive claim and a briefcase full of falsified surveys, and everything they'd bled for in the gorge would become a footnote in someone else's ledger.

Nine days. And her plane was sitting at the syndicate strip, ninety kilometers downriver, under guard.

She'd known it since the delta. She'd said it aloud once, at the council, and watched the hope drain out of the room like bilge water. The wingtip Ansel's rifleman had clipped could be patched — Sable had helped her splice the spar with epoxy and birch lashing, and it would hold, probably, if she didn't ask too much of it. Fuel wasn't the problem; there was a drum cached at the old mission landing, half a day's paddle from the strip. The problem was simpler and worse: between her and that airstrip stood four syndicate men with rifles, a radio mast, and standing orders — Reyes had told them this much before they marooned him — to shoot the pilot on sight.

"They won't shoot the plane," Okonkwo said now, from his cot against the shed wall. His fever had broken two days ago but he still spoke like a man conserving matches. "Ansel wants it intact. It's the only aircraft on this stretch of river. He'll have told them to disable it, not destroy it — pull a magneto, slash the tires, wait for the gunboat." He turned his head carefully. "Which means you can't just walk in and fly out. They'll be watching *for* you."

"I know."

"And you're going to tell me you have a plan anyway."

Ulyana looked at the carbon copy. Her father's survey stamp was pressed into its corner, the ink slightly blurred where her thumb had sweated on it. *R. ISHIKAWA, LICENSED SURVEYOR, TERRITORIAL REGISTRY NO. 4471.* A dead man's name doing live man's work.

"I have half a plan," she said. "I'm working on the other half."

"You've been working on it for three days."

"Good plans take time."

"Bad ones take less," Okonkwo said, and closed his eyes, which was how he ended arguments he didn't intend to win.

* * *

Sable found her at the waterline an hour before dark, where Ulyana was pretending to check the raft drums for seepage she already knew weren't there. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist that made the far bank look like a pencil sketch of itself. Sable came down the shingle without hurrying, the way she did everything now, as if she'd finally made peace with the speed of her own body.

"You've got the look," Sable said.

"What look."

"The one where you're flying somewhere in your head and the somewhere is a bad idea."

Ulyana kept her eyes on the drums. "The registry closes in nine days. My plane is at the syndicate strip with four men camped around it. That's not a bad idea, that's just the situation."

"So the plan is what? You paddle down alone, stroll past the guards, and ask them nicely to hold your coat?"

"The plan is I go at night. They're bored, they're wet, they're not expecting anyone stupid enough to come *toward* them. I cut through the treeline on the north side, get to the plane, and I'm airborne before they finish arguing about whose turn it is to make coffee."

Sable crouched beside her, forearms on her knees, and studied the river the way she studied contour lines — patiently, looking for what the surface was hiding. "And when one of them hears the engine start," she said, "what happens then?"

"Then I'm already moving."

"At night, off a wet grass strip, with a spliced wing, in the monsoon's tail. You told me yourself the tail winds shear off that bend. You'd be lifting into—" She stopped herself. Ulyana watched her do it, watched her fold the sentence away, and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite anger.

"Say it," Ulyana said. "Lifting into what?"

"You know what."

"I want to hear you say it."

Sable turned and looked at her straight on. She had gotten thinner in the weeks since the cave, and the thinness had pared her face down to something honest and severe. "You'd be lifting into a crosswind that has killed better pilots than you, over water cold enough to kill you in four minutes, carrying documents that four armed men have orders to keep grounded. And you'd be doing it alone because you've done everything alone your whole life and you think that's the same thing as being good at it."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere upriver a kingfisher made its dry rattling call, absurdly domestic.

"That's the situation," Ulyana said finally. "It doesn't change the math. Nobody else can fly. Okonkwo can't stand up straight. You can't—"

"I can walk."

Ulyana stopped.

"I can walk," Sable said again, slower, the way she laid out survey data, point by point. "I walked out of Meridian country with nothing. I know that strip — I surveyed it, Ulyana, I set those boundary markers myself two years ago. There's a game trail along the north treeline that comes out forty meters from the fuel store, and there's a culvert under the east fence that the guards don't know about because it wasn't on any map I ever gave them. I can be inside their perimeter before midnight. I can light something — the fuel store, the brush pile, anything — and every rifle on that strip will swing toward the fire. And while they're all looking at the fire, you start the engine on the south side, in the dark, and you're gone before they understand what they're looking at."

"No."

"It's the only version of this that works."

"No," Ulyana said again, harder. "You walk into a lit perimeter with four rifles and no weapon and no way out except back through the culvert, and if one of them decides to chase instead of watch the fire—"

"Then I run. I'm faster than any of them. I've been outrunning that syndicate since the day I left Meridian."

"And if you're not fast enough?" Ulyana heard her own voice crack on it and hated the crack, hated what it admitted. "If they put a bullet in you in the dark and I'm lifting off over your body, is that the version that works? Is that the arithmetic?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something gentler and more dangerous. "You burned a ledger into this river so that thousands of people keep their water. You flew through a calving gorge with ice coming down around you like the sky was falling in pieces. You held a man's life together with boiled instruments and salvaged penicillin. And now, when the last thing needs doing, you want to do it in the way most likely to get you killed, alone, because alone is the only way you've ever let yourself matter."

"That's not fair."

"No," Sable agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing about this is fair. But you asked me once why I walked out of Meridian instead of staying to sabotage the data from inside, and I told you it was because I couldn't trust myself not to become them. This is me trusting myself now. Let me do the thing I'm actually good at." She reached out and took Ulyana's hand, mud-cold fingers closing around hers. "You fly. That's yours. Let me draw the fire. That's mine."

Ulyana looked down at their hands. The river moved past them, patient, carrying everything eventually somewhere.

"If you're not at the mission landing by dawn," she said, "I'm turning around."

"You will not."

"I will absolutely turn the plane around and land on that strip with four rifles pointed at me, and we will both die stupidly together, and Okonkwo will be furious."

Something moved across Sable's face — surprise first, then the slow reluctant beginning of a smile, the first real one in weeks. "That's the worst threat anyone's ever made me."

"I mean every word of it."

"I know," Sable said. "That's what makes it terrible." She squeezed Ulyana's hand once, hard, and let go. "Dawn at the mission landing. Bring the plane. I'll bring the fire."

* * *

They spent the evening preparing, and the preparation had the quality of a ritual neither of them named. Okonkwo insisted on sitting up to help, propped against the shed wall with a lamp, talking them through it in his dry way — timing the paddle downriver, estimating the guard rotations from what Reyes had told them before they left him, marking the culvert on a scrap of chart with a grease pencil. Twice his voice slurred with exhaustion and twice he shook himself back. When Ulyana tried to make him lie down, he gave her a look that needed no translation.

"You'll need a distraction that burns long and bright," he said. "Diesel-soaked brush, not gasoline — gasoline flashes and dies. And light it upwind of the barracks, so the smoke does half the work for you."

"I surveyed fires too," Sable said. "Before Meridian. One season, forest service."

"There's nothing you haven't surveyed," Ulyana said.

"There's plenty." Sable rolled the fuse cord — cotton wicking from an oil lamp, waxed with candle stub — and didn't look up. "I never surveyed what it would feel like to stay somewhere."

Okonkwo's lamp flickered. Ulyana busied herself with the chart.

Later, near midnight, Sable packed: a knife, the fuse, a tin of diesel-soaked rags wrapped in oilcloth, a compass — Ulyana's spare, the dented aluminum one, not the brass. At the door of the shed she paused, silhouetted against the mist, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"When this is done," Sable said, "when the claim is filed and Ansel is finished — what happens to us? To this?" She gestured vaguely between them, at the shed, at the whole dripping green world.

Ulyana thought about the question honestly, which was the only way she knew how to answer anything anymore. "I don't know," she said. "I used to think I'd fly until the debt was paid and then find out who I was. But the debt was a fiction and I still don't entirely know who I am. So maybe — maybe we find out at the same time. If you want."

Sable smiled in the doorway, small and unguarded. "I walked three hundred kilometers out of a glacier valley," she said. "I'm not walking away from this."

Then she went out into the dark, and the mist closed behind her like water.

* * *

Ulyana didn't sleep. She sat in the shed with the counter-claim copies sealed in oilcloth against her chest, listening to the river and counting hours the way she'd once counted engine revolutions — steadily, mechanically, because the alternative was thinking. Okonkwo slept at last near three, his breathing rough but even. At four she woke him, and he pressed a folded paper into her hand without being asked: a letter, addressed to the territorial registrar, vouching for the data, signed with his name and title. In case, he said, the stamps weren't enough. In case they needed a living witness and not just a dead man's ink.

She paddled downriver alone in the last of the dark, the current helping, the mist thick enough to drink. By five-thirty she was at the old mission landing, six kilometers above the syndicate strip, tying the raft to a rotted piling with hands that wouldn't quite stay steady. From here she could see, when the mist thinned, the distant shape of the strip's wind sock hanging dead in the still air, and beyond it the gray suggestion of the hangar, and the tiny moving points that might have been men or might have been her imagination.

At 5:47, the eastern sky went the color of old brass.

At 5:52, a column of black smoke stood up from the syndicate strip, fat and sudden, exactly where Sable had promised it would be. Even at this distance she heard the shouting start — thin, ragged, human, four voices finding their panic in different keys. Figures ran toward the fuel store. A whistle blew. Someone fired a shot at nothing, the report rolling flat across the water.

Ulyana was already running for the plane.

It sat where Reyes had said it would, on the south apron, chocked, one tire slashed and sagging — they'd followed Ansel's orders, disabled rather than destroyed — and the second tire held air, barely, enough. The magneto leads hung cut beneath the cowling. She stripped the insulation with her teeth, twisted the copper together, taped it with the roll she'd carried in her jacket pocket for three days, and climbed into a cockpit that smelled of dust and old fuel and every flight she'd ever made in it.

The engine caught on the fourth swing. Behind her, the fire roared and the guards shouted and nobody, nobody, was looking south.

She taxied onto the grass with the slashed tire dragging, swung into the wind off the bend, and pushed the throttle forward into whatever the tail winds had decided to give her today. The spliced wing flexed and held. The ground fell away. And below her, shrinking, the strip burned bright against the mist — and at the edge of the smoke, one figure broke from the treeline and ran, waving both arms, alive, alive, *alive* —

Ulyana banked toward the mission landing, heart slamming, and began the turn that would bring her down to pick up the woman who had chosen the fire so she could choose the sky.
