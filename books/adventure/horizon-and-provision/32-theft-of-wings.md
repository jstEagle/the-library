## 32. Theft of Wings

### Sable

The syndicate strip sat in a bowl of cut grass at the river's elbow, two hangars, a fuel store, a guard hut with a tin roof, and four men who were paid to be bored. Sable had watched them for two days from the tree line, learning their boredom the way she'd once learned rock strata — by its layers, its faults, the places it would give way.

There was a rhythm to it. Two men on the strip at all times, one walking the perimeter at dusk with a rifle slung loose, one in the guard hut where a radio hissed and a kettle went on the coals every ninety minutes. The other two slept in the hangar office and came out when something happened, which was almost never. The last plane to land here had been Ulyana's, three weeks ago, and they'd put a chain through her propeller and posted a man beside it like a sentry over a tomb.

Tonight there would be wind. Sable could smell it coming down off the interior — that dry, mineral breath that meant the monsoon's tail was dragging itself apart over the high country. Wind was good. Wind carried smoke sideways, made eyes water, gave every fire an excuse.

"You're sure about the layout," Ulyana said behind her. Not a question. Ulyana didn't ask questions she'd already asked twice; she just restated them until the answer held still.

"Fuel store against the north fence. Drums stacked three high along the wall, spill tray underneath because nobody here respects gasoline." Sable kept her voice flat, surveyor's voice, the one that reported facts without feeling. "Guard hut forty meters south. The kettle man will smell it before he sees it, which buys us maybe four minutes of confusion."

"And after that?"

"After that I run east along the fence line into the canebrake, and they chase me, or they don't."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. In the dark under the trees her face was all planes and shadow, and Sable found herself cataloguing it again — the set of the jaw, the small scar over the eyebrow she'd never asked about — the way she used to catalogue contour lines, wanting to know everything and knowing the knowing was dangerous.

"If they catch you," Ulyana said, "Ansel doesn't need you alive anymore. You understand that. The data's filed. The claim's lodged. You're not an asset, you're a witness."

"I've been a witness since the day I walked out of Meridian's camp," Sable said. "It hasn't killed me yet. Statistically I'm overdue either way."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It was arithmetic." She turned to face her fully. "You fly at moonrise. I light it twenty minutes before, so the guards are still coughing and arguing about buckets when you roll out. Okonkwo waits at the river bend with the raft — no, don't argue, your leg is worse than you pretend and he knows it too. You take the logs, you take the compass, you get to the registry before Ansel's lawyers finish their breakfast. That's the whole plan. Everything else is weather."

Ulyana looked at her for a long time. Somewhere downriver a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

"When this is done," Ulyana said finally, "you'll tell me what you're running from. The real thing. Not the version you give strangers."

Sable felt the old reflex rise — the smooth deflection, the half-truth worn smooth as river stone — and let it die unspoken. "When this is done," she said, "I'll tell you anything you want to know."

"Don't promise that lightly."

"I never promise anything lightly. That's my whole problem."

Ulyana almost smiled. Then she did the thing Sable had learned to dread and admire in equal measure: she reached out and gripped Sable's forearm, hard, pilot's grip, the grip of someone who understood that hands were for holding on. "Twenty minutes," she said. "Make them count."

Then she was gone into the dark, moving low and quiet toward the river bend where the raft lay hidden, and Sable stood alone among the trees with a tin of kerosene, a bundle of oil rags, and the particular clarity that comes from having burned every bridge except the one you're standing on.

She checked the wind again. It was rising, as promised, bending the cane tops toward the east. Good. Fire loved a lie about direction.

She thought, absurdly, of her old life — the expedition tents, the clean instruments, the morning when she'd first understood what the aquifer maps would become in syndicate hands and had walked out past the pickets with nothing but a bedroll and a hatred she couldn't yet name. She'd told herself then that she was done being useful to bad men. Tonight she was going to be useful to a good one, and it felt exactly the same in the body: cold hands, fast heart, the world narrowed to a single next step.

She crossed the open ground in the last of the dusk, moving when the perimeter walker moved, freezing when he stopped. The fuel store's corrugated door was latched but not locked — why lock it, out here, where the only thieves were weather and rust? She worked the latch with a screwdriver, slipped inside, and stood in the dark among the drums, breathing gasoline fumes and thinking clearly.

Not a big fire. A big fire brought everyone running and burned the strip she needed intact. A *small* fire — a smoldering rag tucked in the spill tray, licking up the drum seams — made just enough smoke to be interesting and just enough threat to be urgent. Guards would form a bucket line between the store and the hangars. Guards would leave the plane unwatched. Guards would be looking south at the flames while a woman ran east along the fence with the whole night to hide her.

She soaked the rags, stacked them in the tray beneath the oldest drum — the one with the rusted seam, the one that would weep rather than burst — and struck the match.

For one second the flame stood perfectly still in her cupped hands, and she saw her own reflection in it, small and yellow and absolutely committed.

Then she set it down, walked out, closed the door gently behind her like a considerate guest, and began to count.

### Ulyana

Moonrise came up red through the smoke haze, which hadn't been in the plan but should have been, because plans were weather and weather was God's own improvisation.

Ulyana lay in the ditch at the edge of the strip with her chin in wet grass and watched the fuel store glow from within like a paper lantern. No flame yet — just that deep orange respiration, smoke curling from the eaves — and then the first shout went up from the guard hut, and the night organized itself around the wrong emergency.

The kettle man came out first, still buttoning his shirt. The perimeter walker broke into a trot. Someone kicked the store door open and the light spilled out across the grass, and Ulyana counted bodies the way she counted fuel gauges: two at the fire, one running for the hut radio, one — the sentry — still standing by her plane, rifle in hand, torn between orders and spectacle.

*Come on,* she thought at him. *Go be a hero. There's a fire.*

He lasted ninety seconds. Then the first drum seam let go with a soft whump and a column of black smoke stood up against the stars, and the sentry decided that chained propellers don't burn and ran for the bucket line.

Ulyana went over the ditch bank at a dead sprint.

The chain through the propeller was padlocked, and she'd spent two nights of the walk downriver practicing this with a borrowed bolt cutter that Okonkwo had produced from somewhere with the mild expression of a man who'd been a boy once. One cut. The lock dropped into the grass. She wound the chain off the blades, hand over hand, fast, the metal biting cold into her palms, and threw it into the ditch.

Preflight in the dark. She'd done it ten thousand times and she did it now the way she did everything that mattered — with the feeling switched off and the checklist running. Fuel: full; Reyes's stolen reserve, irony noted, filed, discarded. Control surfaces: free. Tires: soft left main, flyable. Wing fabric aft of the strut: patched with the delta women's canvas and stitched with fishing line, ugly as sin, holding. Logs in the map case, strapped to the seat beside her. Compass in her breast pocket, brass warm from her body, needle steady on a bearing that had nothing to do with north and everything to do with her father.

She climbed in. Magnets on. Fuel pump on. Throttle cracked.

The engine caught on the third swing and settled into that battered, beloved rhythm she knew better than any heartbeat of her own, and the whole airfield turned toward the sound.

She saw it happen the way you see things in emergencies — in bright, separate frames. The bucket line breaking apart. Men turning. The sentry dropping his bucket and grabbing for the rifle slung on his back. The radio man in the hut doorway, mouth open, shouting words the engine ate.

Rolling. Tail up. Grass hissing under the wheels. The strip was short and rough and she'd landed on it once, weeks ago, under escort, and now she took it back the way you take back anything worth having — at speed, in the dark, with no permission asked.

The rifle opened up when she was halfway down.

She heard it before she understood it — flat cracks stitching the night, muzzle flash strobing from beside the hangar — and then the windshield starred, one hole, two, and something hit the left wing with a sound like a hammer on a door. The plane yawed. She corrected with rudder and refused to look at the wing, because looking was for after, and after was a place you earned by getting airborne.

Tailwheel off. Ground falling away. The strip lights — there were no strip lights, only the burning fuel store sliding beneath her left side like a fallen sun — and then she was up, climbing into the red-lit smoke, gear fixed, throttle forward, the engine pulling hard and true.

She banked east, away from the firelight, and only then allowed herself the glance.

The left wingtip was gone. Not damaged — *gone*, a foot of fabric and rib torn away, the trailing edge frayed where the bullet had opened it like a letter. The aileron cable hung visible in the moonlight, slack on one side. She moved the stick and the plane answered sluggishly, rolling right eager, rolling left reluctant, a bird with a clipped feather trying to remember flight.

Flyable. Barely. In calm air, at light weight, with a pilot who didn't ask anything rude of it.

Behind her the fuel store burned properly now, flames standing off the roof, and tiny figures ran below with buckets that were theater, pure theater, because the fire had done its job and so had the woman who'd lit it, and Ulyana put both thoughts away in the compartment where she kept everything she couldn't afford to feel yet — Sable alone on the ground, Sable running east into the canebrake, Sable whose odds she had calculated and approved and would now carry like ballast until—

The radio crackled. Syndicate frequency, the guard hut's transmitter, bleeding weakly across the valley.

"—all units, all units, aircraft stolen from strip, single-engine, heading east, repeat heading east—"

And then another voice, farther away, thinner, relayed from downriver, and Ulyana's blood went cold in a way the rifle fire hadn't managed.

"—advise Ansel. Registry filing confirmed this evening. He wants the charter ready at dawn. He says—" static, a long tear of it, "—he says burn the valley records and bring him the woman's head if she resists."

Ulyana flew on into the dark with a clipped wing and a full heart and the beginning of a new arithmetic forming behind her eyes: whatever she carried in the map case was worth more than money now. It was worth killing for. Which meant it was worth finishing.

She checked the compass. Checked the wing. Checked the fuel.

Then she pointed the nose at the registry town, eighty miles east across the sleeping interior, and flew.

### Sable

The canebrake took her in like water takes a stone.

She ran bent double through the stalks, thorns opening her forearms in thin lines she wouldn't feel until morning, and behind her the strip glowed and shouted and organized its furies around a fire that was already dying of good management. Twice she stopped and listened. Once she heard dogs — no, not dogs, a man crashing through cane on the far side, cursing the thorns, chasing a noise she'd thrown like a decoy: her jacket, hung on a broken stalk fifty meters back, still carrying her warmth and her scent and none of her.

She waited until the crash faded. Then she turned west instead of east, because east was where they'd look tomorrow, and west was the river bend where a raft waited with a hydrologist who owed her nothing and had stayed anyway.

Okonkwo saw her come out of the cane and didn't ask questions. He just handed her the paddle he'd saved and said, "He got off?"

"He got off." Her voice came out scraped. "Wingtip's gone. He's flying anyway."

Okonkwo nodded slowly, the way he nodded at data that confirmed a hypothesis he'd been afraid to hold. "Of course he is," he said. "That man has never once let the condition of the aircraft decide whether he flies. Only the condition of the debt." He pushed the raft into the current. "Which is gone now, yes?"

"Gone." Sable took the paddle. The river took them both, smooth and black and going the same direction for once. "We're all flying on a clipped wing tonight, Doctor. The trick is the same as always."

"Which is?"

"Don't look at it until you're safely airborne."

Downstream, the fires of the strip shrank to a red coin on the horizon, and Sable paddled, and bled quietly into the river water, and let herself think — for the first time in three years, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own — about *after*.

By dawn they'd hear whether the registry clerk had done his job. By dawn they'd also learn what Ansel sent at dawn.

She paddled faster.
