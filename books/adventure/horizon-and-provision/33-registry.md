## 33. Registry

The territorial registry office sat at the end of a gravel street in Port Meridian — not named for the expedition, the expedition had been named for the port, which told you everything about how the syndicate thought of this place: a label first, a town second.

Ulyana had landed on the salt flat two kilometers out and walked in with the logs wrapped in oilcloth under her arm and Sable a half-step behind her, both of them dressed in the kind of clothes that made clerks look through you. That had been the plan for years, invisibility. Today she needed the opposite. She needed to be seen so hard it stuck.

The office smelled of mildew and carbon paper. A ceiling fan turned with the enthusiasm of a dying thing. Behind the counter, a man in a sweat-darkened shirt was stamping something with great ceremony, as if the stamp were the point of his life.

"Registry," Ulyana said, and set the oilcloth bundle on the counter.

He looked up. He looked at her face, then at the bundle, then at the door behind her, where the street stood empty and bright. His hand drifted toward a drawer.

"I wouldn't," Ulyana said quietly. "Whatever's in that drawer. Whatever they pay you. I'd wait five minutes before deciding."

The clerk's name, according to the brass plate, was T. Okafor, Deputy Registrar. According to the tremor in his hands, he was a man who had already been visited. She could see it in the way his eyes kept going to the window, cataloging exits that didn't exist.

"You're the Ishikawa girl," he said.

Not *the pilot*. Not *the debtor*. The Ishikawa *girl*. Something moved through her chest like water finding an old channel.

"My father flew for this district eleven years," she said. "His stamps are in this bundle. Originals. You'll want to verify them against your master register — 2041 through 2052, survey series Kilo. Go ahead. I'll wait."

Okafor didn't move. "You should understand my position—"

"I do understand your position." She unwrapped the oilcloth anyway, folding it back corner by corner, unhurried, letting him watch. The flight logs came into the light: canvas-bound, water-stained at the edges but dry at the heart, her father's block capitals marching across survey grids. And clipped inside the front cover, in a leather sleeve gone dark with age, the stamp itself — brass, worn smooth where a thumb had pressed it ten thousand times, the district seal cut deep enough to still bite paper.

She set it on the counter between them.

Okafor stared at it the way men stare at things that have come back from the dead.

* * *

"They told me you'd come," he said finally. "Three days ago. A lawyer from the syndicate. Very polite. Very expensive shoes. He left a memorandum." He pulled a folder from beneath the counter as if it might bite him too. "Any claim filed bearing the name Ishikawa is to be held pending verification of... authenticity. Indefinitely."

"Indefinitely," Ulyana repeated. "That's a word lawyers use when they mean never."

"Yes," Okafor said miserably.

Sable leaned on the counter beside her, arms crossed, watching the clerk with the flat patience of someone who had spent weeks eating fish and waiting out rain. "Mr. Okafor. How long have you worked here?"

"Twenty-two years."

"And in twenty-two years, has anyone ever brought you a document you couldn't file because of a memorandum?"

"No."

"Has anyone ever brought you a document with an original district seal on it that you refused to verify?"

A long silence. The fan creaked overhead.

"No," Okafor said again, softer.

"Then the question," Sable said, "is whether you work for the registry or for the memorandum."

Ulyana almost smiled. This was what Sable did — she didn't argue, she disassembled. She laid a person's life out on the counter in pieces and let them see which piece was load-bearing.

Okafor wiped his palms on his shirt. "If I file this and the syndicate challenges it, I lose my position. Possibly more than my position. Reyes came through here in the spring asking about records. About who had copied what. People remember that."

"People also remember my father," Ulyana said. "I've been flying medicine up and down this river for six years, Mr. Okafor. I've landed at every settlement from the delta to the falls. Do you know what they say about Kenji Ishikawa? They don't say he owed money. They say he mapped their water when nobody else would come. His name isn't just legal weight. It's the only weight in this district that the syndicate hasn't been able to buy."

She opened the log to the page she'd slept next to for three nights. Survey series Kilo, transect seven. Her father's handwriting, dense and exact, plotting aquifer pressure readings against seasonal melt. In the margin, small enough to miss: *Syndicate figures for sector 12 do not match field observation. Falsified upstream of gauge station. Note for record.*

"Your master register will show he filed these surveys contemporaneously," she said. "Stamped, dated, witnessed. Which means the syndicate's own claims — the ones they filed last year — rest on data my father documented as false twenty years before they filed it. You're not choosing between me and them, Mr. Okafor. You're choosing between the record and a lie someone asked you to hold open forever."

Okafor looked at the stamp. At the logs. At the window, one last time, hoping perhaps for a gunboat, a lawyer, anything to make the decision for him.

Then he took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirttail, put them back on, and picked up the stamp.

* * *

It took four hours.

That surprised her. She had imagined filing as a single thunderclap — ink, seal, done. Instead it was forms. Triplicate forms, carbon copies, a claims ledger with columns for parcel, basis, claimant, witness. Okafor worked slowly and precisely, and Ulyana understood after the first hour that the slowness was deliberate. Every form he completed was a fact he was making permanent. He wanted no gaps. No ambiguity a syndicate lawyer could drive a barge through.

"Claimant name," he said, pen hovering.

"Ulyana Kenjirovna Ishikawa."

"Basis of claim?"

She hesitated. This was the hinge. Okonkwo had argued all night at the delta landing about how to frame it — collective counter-claim on behalf of the settlements, or personal claim by inheritance of her father's prior survey rights. The council had split. Old Marta from the fish camp had ended it: *"File it her way. The river knows whose daughter she is. Let the paper catch up."*

"Inheritance of prior survey rights," Ulyana said, "established by original documentation, series Kilo, together with a collective counter-claim joined by the undersigned representatives of fourteen delta and river settlements." She slid the second bundle across — the council's signatures, gathered in the fish-drying shed, pages soft from being passed hand to hand along the river faster than any plane could fly them.

Okafor read the names. His lips moved over some of them. Halfway down the second page he stopped.

"These are my cousins," he said. "Lower delta. The Ferreira line."

"The whole river signed," Sable said. "Took them nine days. Boats mostly."

Okafor set down his pen. For a moment he looked older and younger at once — a small man in a mildewed office discovering that the mildewed office was load-bearing after all.

"Verification of seal," he said, and his voice had changed, steadier now, the voice of a man doing the job he'd been hired for twenty-two years ago. He pressed her father's stamp onto the test pad, then compared it against the master impression in the locked cabinet — the one he'd opened without hesitation once he'd decided. Brass met brass. The impressions matched to the worn edge, to the little crescent nick in the border that her father had never gotten around to repairing.

"Verified," Okafor said. "Original. Contemporary. Witnessed." He stamped the claim form. Once. Twice. The sound went through the room like a shot fired into water.

* * *

They walked out at four in the afternoon, the certified copy folded in oilcloth against Ulyana's ribs, and the light on the salt flat had gone the color of hammered tin. Monsoon tail clouds stacked along the southern horizon, but the air over the strip was still, holding its breath.

Sable counted the hours aloud as they walked. "Plane's fueled. Storm window opens tomorrow morning, closes in maybe thirty-six. Ansel's charter left the lowland field yesterday — we heard it on the salvaged radio. If he's coming, he's coming through that weather or around it."

"He'll come through it," Ulyana said. "He doesn't know how to go around."

At the plane she stopped with her hand on the propeller and made herself say it, because saying things out loud had become, somewhere on the river, a discipline she hadn't known she needed.

"It's filed. It can't be unfiled. Even if he burns this office down tonight, the record exists, there are witnesses, there are fourteen settlements with countersigned copies. He can't own the water anymore. All he can do now is try to destroy the people who stopped him."

Sable looked at her across the cowling. "And us."

"And us."

"So we don't be where he expects," Sable said simply, and climbed into the navigator's seat, and began unfolding the river charts with their pencil ghosts of gunboat routes and fuel caches and the gorge marked in red: *closed until calving ends.*

Ulyana ran the preflight in the last light. Fuel caps. Control surfaces. The patched wingtip from the syndicate strip — Sable's fire had drawn the guards, the rifle fire had cost her a wingtip's worth of aluminum and nothing else, and the repair was ugly and true. She touched the rivets once, the way other pilots touched talismans.

In the cockpit, the brass compass sat in its place on the dashboard where she'd mounted it that morning. The needle swung, settled, pointed north-northwest toward the interior — toward the valley, the glacier, the water that belonged, as of four o'clock this afternoon, to everyone whose names were on those pages.

Her father had flown these skies with that compass and never owned a meter of them. Debt had eaten his standing alive; she'd believed that since she was nineteen. But standing, it turned out, wasn't something the syndicate gave and took. It was something you carried — stamped, dated, witnessed — and handed to a frightened man in a mildewed office, and watched him press it into the permanent record of the world.

She started the engine. The salt flat blurred into motion. Ahead, the storm wall waited, and somewhere inside it or behind it, Ansel's charter was cutting a hole in the sky toward them.

Ulyana banked south, into the weather, and felt the needle swing with her, steady as a heartbeat, pointing home.
